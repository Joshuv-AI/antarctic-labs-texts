Return on investments in social determinants of health interventions: what is the evidence?
Authors: Sayeh Nikpay, Zhanji Zhang, Pinar Karaca-Mandic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere has been an increasing recognition of the importance and the value of addressing social determinants of health (SDOH) to improve population health outcomes, manage health care costs, and reduce health inequities. Despite the strong interest in investing in SDOH initiatives by various stakeholders, the literature on the return from such investments is scarce. The differences in study populations and methodologies, and the lack of data on SDOH intervention outcomes and/or costs, make it challenging to quantify and generalize outcomes for decision-making. We reviewed the literature on SDOH interventions focused on food and housing insecurity, and developed a methodology for estimating a key outcome: the return on investment (ROI), defined as the net returns from an intervention divided by its costs. The ROI estimates we report can be used by stakeholders to prioritize among alternative SDOH interventions for fundraising, investing, and implementing purposes. The average ROI for food-insecurity programs was 85% (ranging from 1% to 287%; except for 1 study‘s ROI, −31%) and for housing-insecurity programs was 50% (ranging from 5% to 224%; except for 1 ROI, −38%). In addition, these estimates can serve as key inputs for designing and employing innovative financing and policy solutions to increase the use of these interventions.Keywords: return on investment, social determinants of health

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are “the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age that affect a wide range of health functioning, and quality-of-life outcomes and risks.”1 In recent years there has been great interest in developing and implementing interventions to address SDoH and thereby improve health outcomes.2 Social determinants of health interventions have broad support across a variety of stakeholders in the health sector. Numerous government agencies such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,3 Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation,4 and a variety of other health agencies5 have recognized SDOH as drivers of health inequities. Provider groups, such as the American Hospital Association6 and the American Medical Association,7 also support the use of SDOH interventions. Furthermore, employer groups have also advocated for addressing SDOH through employer benefits.8

A group with a particular interest in implementing SDOH interventions are state Medicaid programs, where policymakers seek to improve health outcomes and achieve health equity with limited state resources. As of 2019, the majority of states were pursuing policies to address SDOH in the Medicaid population.9 In January of 2023, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) issued guidance that allows states to authorize Medicaid managed care plans to provide “in lieu of” services, which include SDOH interventions that may be offered instead of traditional Medicaid benefits.10 Combined with Section 1115 Demonstration waivers, states now have wide latitude to fund and implement SDOH interventions. Indeed, in 4 states, Medicaid Managed Care Organizations may offer specific evidence-based food- or housing-related interventions to specific high-need populations.11

Despite substantial interest in SDOH interventions, the literature on the costs and benefits of these interventions is relatively thin. In particular, few studies report the data necessary to recover a key outcome for decision-making: return on investment (ROI). Defined as the net returns from an intervention divided by its costs, SDOH interventions with small, zero, or negative ROIs may be unattractive to decision makers. Return on investment can also help decision makers choose among alternative SDOH interventions with positive ROI or identify innovative solutions to increase the use of these interventions.12

Although few papers report ROI, some studies contain changes in outcomes—such as reduced emergency department (ED) visits—that could be used to construct a measure of an intervention's benefits, so long as the costs are also reported or could be simulated. The resulting health care ROI captures the health care–specific returns to an intervention.

In this brief report, we review the literature on SDOH interventions focused on food and housing insecurity. We then extract data on changes in costs and benefits and describe a procedure to standardize the estimates in order to calculate estimated ROI. Finally, we present the estimates of calculated ROI.

Data and methods

Literature review

We began by conducting a literature review focused on 2 popular areas for SDOH interventions: food and housing instability. We selected these interventions because they appeared to have the largest peer-reviewed evidence base. We started our review with peer-reviewed studies that could be identified on these topics in the Social Interventions Research & Evaluation Network (SIREN) Evidence and Resource Library,13 PubMed, and reports by the National Academies of Sciences proceedings14 and the Commonwealth Fund.15 This initial review identified 65 studies related to food insecurity and 94 studies related to housing insecurity.

Of these candidate studies, we only included those that reported on utilization and cost measures as intervention outcomes for a particular, identifiable intervention. Given the small size of this literature, we did not limit studies based on design (eg, randomized controlled trials, interrupted time series), population (eg, children/youth, elderly, homeless, Medicaid-insured, Medicare-insured), or the time period of the study. We did, however, exclude studies that took place outside of the United States, studies that consisted of simulation exercises, or interim evaluations or implementation studies of emerging SDOH interventions. Finally, we only included studies that reported the costs of the program. For example, if a study reported a reduction in ED visits associated with a housing intervention, but did not report the cost of providing housing or other services that were part of the intervention, they were excluded from the study.

After all restrictions, we identified a total of 19 studies: 7 studies on food- and 12 studies on housing-instability interventions. These interventions included medically tailored meals, nontailored meals, and food-prescription programs for food, and interim housing after hospital discharge, emergency housing assistance, temporary or permanent supportive housing, affordable housing with supportive services, housing search, and eviction-prevention services for housing. (See Appendix Tables S1 and S2 for a list of food- and housing-related studies, respectively.)

Medical expenditure panel survey sample

To infer the economic value of changes in health outcomes in the studies, we used data from the 2019 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey–Household Component (MEPS-HC)16-18. (Details on the MEPS-HC are provided in the Appendix.) Using information in each study, we selected a subsample from the MEPS-HC that most closely matched the study population on medical service utilization, chronic conditions, health insurance coverage, socioeconomic status, and demographic characteristics. For example, a medically tailored meal program studied by Riviere et al19 targeted patients with heart failure with vulnerability in SDOH and either 2 ED visits or 2 hospitalizations for heart failure in the past year. To match this sample, we identified individuals in the MEPS-HC with heart disease (including coronary heart disease, heart condition, and heart attack) and at least 2 inpatient or 2 ED visits during the past year. We then identified the percentage change in outcomes associated with the intervention as reported in the study, and used the MEPS-HC data to find the proportional change in costs for the matched sample. (See the Appendix for more details on the treatment of outcomes.)

Calculating ROI

Each ROI estimate combined both estimates from the literature with data from the MEPS-HC. We combined these data to calculate the health care ROI in 2 ways. First, if the study reported costs and benefits directly, we used them to calculate ROI, which was defined as benefits, less costs, divided by costs.20 Second, if the study reported intervention costs but estimates of the economic benefits consisted of a change in utilization, we used the estimate of a reduction in costs associated with the utilization change in a sample of individuals in the MEPS-HC to calculate the dollar value of benefits. We expressed all costs and benefits in 2019 dollars. We calculated ROI for 14 studies directly and used the MEPS-HC to estimate benefits for another 5 studies.

Results

Figure 1 presents the distribution of estimated health care ROI by intervention type. Specifically, we present the distribution of estimated health care ROI in bins of 20 percentage points. Among food-insecurity programs, we found an average ROI of 85%. All estimated ROIs were positive except for 1 (−31%). Among the 6 studies with positive ROIs, they ranged from 1% to 287%. Among housing-insecurity programs, the average ROI was 50%. Only 1 estimated ROI was negative (−38%). Among the 11 studies with positive ROIs, they ranged from 5% to 224%.

Discussion

Identifying estimates of ROI for SDOH interventions is a crucial step to encourage health plans, as well as other private actors in the health care space, to invest in these interventions. Among those studies we found for which ROI could be calculated, ROIs were positive 85%, on average, for food interventions and 50% for housing interventions. Large variations in ROI for SDOH programs may be partially due to target populations. Some interventions targeted inpatient-admitted patients, while others were designed for people with certain health insurance types, like dual eligibles. In addition, the length of SDOH programs might also play a role in the large ROI differences. Some food-insecurity programs were 6 months compared with others lasting 24 months. However, the fact that we were only able to calculate ROI for 19 studies out of an initial 159 speaks to the importance of including ROI estimates, or the components necessary to calculate ROI, in studies of SDOH interventions.

Our approach has several limitations that must be noted. First, our study assumes that the reduction in costs associated with changes in outcomes can be well represented by the MEPS-HC data. This may not always hold true, although the MEPS-HC is a nationally representative population sample. Second, our estimates of ROI contain only data on health care outcomes—not on other factors that might have been positively (or negatively) affected by the intervention. As a result, the estimates may under- (or over-) estimate the true ROI. Third, we considered only 2 types of interventions: housing and food insecurity. These were selected because they have little overlap with medical benefits. Estimating the ROI for transportation-based interventions, by contrast, may be more difficult as the costs of transportation benefits are likely included in the patient's premium and benefit design. Finally, we did not judge studies on the strength of the research design used, as there were so few studies to consider. Nearly half of the studies that we used to calculate ROI were based on simple comparisons of data before and after the intervention. (Appendix Tables S1 and S2 briefly state the methodology of each study.) These evaluation designs cannot rule out the possibility that changes around the time of the intervention were simply due to secular trends in outcomes.

To move towards action on implementing SDOH interventions at scale, providers, advocates, and policymakers need actionable evidence. Similarly, long-term, sustainable financing of SDOH interventions needs innovative frameworks and financial instruments. Recent proposals to develop and implement variants of social impact bonds offer a promising approach to engage multiple stakeholders, such as managed care organizations and other public–private partnerships. A social bond, for example, would raise large amounts of capital from individual investors, and equitably distribute funds to SDOH interventions in the community.12 The SDOH interventions would be expected to improve health outcomes and reduce health care spending, and allow the issuers of the bond (eg, a group of Medicaid Managed Care Organizations) to pay back the investors. However, translating this concept into practice necessitates involving stakeholders, conducting thorough literature reviews, and using intricate modeling to refine the bond structure for optimal outcomes. The success of such a financing model critically depends on the ROI estimates of the individual SDOH interventions and, as such, could determine the optimal portfolio of SDOH interventions that the funds raised could support.

Our exercise demonstrated that, even with retroactive calculation, such estimates are elusive. Funders of evaluations of SDOH interventions should consider a uniform reporting standard for such studies in order to facilitate comparison and use in the realm of practice. At a minimum, future studies of SDOH interventions should consider reporting on not only benefits but also costs. However, estimating the true costs of such interventions is difficult. Frequently, SDOH interventions are administered by community-based organizations with limited capacity to model costs. Establishing best practices for cost models and standard frameworks that identify key inputs to SDOH interventions (eg, costs for labor and goods, number of implementation sites, number of beneficiaries per site) can facilitate comparing costs as well as the benefits across a range of SDOH interventions.
