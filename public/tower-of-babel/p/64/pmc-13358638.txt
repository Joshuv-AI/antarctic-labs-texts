Rapid disruption of pollination function by the invasive plant Impatiens glandulifera
Authors: R Pérez‐Barrales, L M Taylor, G Horn, S Ben‐Menni Schuler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Biological invasions can disrupt plant–pollinator interactions by altering pollinator behaviour and pollen transfer dynamics, yet the mechanisms and timing of these effects remain poorly understood. Most studies rely on observational comparisons or removal experiments in long‐established invasions, but little is known about changes in pollination function at the onset of invasion.We investigated the pollination of Stachys sylvatica by combining field comparisons between pristine and invaded sites with an experimental introduction of Impatiens glandulifera into a previously uninvaded site. We quantified pollinator visitation, pollen loads carried by bumblebees, and conspecific and invasive pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas.Across multiple field sites, stigmas of S. sylvatica in pristine habitats received approximately three times more conspecific pollen than those in invaded sites. Bumblebees dominated the pollinator assemblage across all sites, and in invaded habitats, they carried pollen loads strongly dominated by I. glandulifera. During the experimental introduction, bumblebees rapidly incorporated I. glandulifera into their foraging, while conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas declined sharply, with 81.5% reduction within 4 days.Our results demonstrate that invasion by I. glandulifera can rapidly impair pollination function of a co‐flowering native species through changes in pollen transport and transfer efficiency, even before strong shifts in visitation patterns become apparent. By capturing early invasion dynamics through experimental introduction, this study highlights the importance of direct pollen‐based metrics for understanding how plant invasions disrupt pollination processes. Keywords: biological invasions, Bombus, Himalayan balsam, plant–pollinator interactions, pollinator‐mediated competition

INTRODUCTION

Biological invasions are among the leading drivers of biodiversity loss worldwide, with over 900 non‐native plant taxa established in the United Kingdom alone (Mack et al. 2000; Sala et al. 2000; Willis & Hulme 2002; Bímová et al. 2004), compared with 1,692 native species (Stroh et al. 2023). Invasive plants often possess traits that promote rapid spread and dominance, including fast growth, high reproductive output and effective dispersal mechanisms (Van Kleunen et al. 2010; Tyler et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2023). Many also produce abundant floral rewards and conspicuous flowers, making them highly attractive to pollinators (Titze 2000; Nienhuis et al. 2009; Najberek et al. 2023). As a result, plant invasions can disrupt pollination interactions by altering pollinator behaviour, diverting visits from native species and modifying pollen transfer dynamics, potentially reducing native reproductive performance (Morales & Traveset 2008; Traveset & Richardson 2014; Parra‐Tabla & Arceo‐Gómez 2021; Tourbez et al. 2025).

Early work on invasive plants often reported negative effects on the pollination of native species, including reduced visitation, increased heterospecific pollen transfer and lower seed set. Classic experimental studies on Lythrum salicaria, for example, showed reduced visitation and seed production in the native L. alatum, as well as negative effects of mixed pollen loads on reproduction (Brown et al. 2002). Subsequent research, however, revealed that invasion impacts on native pollination are highly contextdependent. Neutral or even facilitative outcomes have been documented depending on floral trait similarity, invader abundance, pollinator assemblages and community structure (Lopezaraiza‐Mikel et al. 2007; Muñoz & Cavieres 2008; Morales & Traveset 2009). Comparative and network‐based studies further demonstrated that invasive plants can integrate into pollination webs without consistently reducing the pollen receipt or seed set of native species (Bjerknes et al. 2007; Vilà et al. 2009a). Together, these contrasting findings highlight the need for mechanistic approaches that move beyond visitation rates alone (King et al. 2013) and directly quantify pollen delivery to stigmas, a critical step in plant reproduction and a robust proxy for pollination function (Morales & Traveset 2008; Opedal et al. 2023).

Most empirical studies assessing the impacts of biological invasions on pollination rely on observational comparisons between invaded and uninvaded sites, or on experimental removal of invasive plants, often documenting partial restoration of pollinator visitation or interaction frequencies following removal (Brown & Mitchell 2001; Lopezaraiza‐Mikel et al. 2007; Jakobsson et al. 2008; Morales & Traveset 2008; Morales & Traveset 2009; Traveset & Richardson 2014; Kaiser‐Bunbury et al. 2017). These approaches have been instrumental in demonstrating that invasive plants can disrupt pollination interactions, but they necessarily focus on habitats or plant communities that have already experienced prolonged exposure to invasion. What remains poorly understood is how the pollination process changes at the onset of invasion, when pollinators first encounter a novel, highly rewarding floral resource. Experimental introductions of invasive plants into previously uninvaded sites offer a complementary, yet rarely applied, approach to address this gap, allowing direct assessment of the speed and mechanisms by which invasion effects on pollination function emerge.

Impatiens glandulifera (Himalayan balsam) is one of the most aggressive invasive plant species in Europe and the United Kingdom (Weber 2003). It produces large, nectar‐rich flowers that attract a wide range of generalist pollinators, particularly bumblebees and can dominate pollen transport networks (Lopezaraiza‐Mikel et al. 2007; Nienhuis & Stout 2009; Emer et al. 2015; Najberek et al. 2023; Tourbez et al. 2025). Studies have shown that a high proportion of flower visitors carry I. glandulifera pollen on their bodies, reflecting its strong magnet effect on pollinators (Tourbez et al. 2025) and that its presence can reduce visitation to neighbouring native plants and crops (Emer et al. 2015). Despite this, the effects on reproductive success of native species are not uniformly negative. Some studies report little or no reduction in conspecific pollen deposition on stigmas of native species despite high levels of alien pollen transport, suggesting that individual pollinator behaviour and pollen placement on the body may mediate invasion outcomes (Bartomeus et al. 2010; Emer et al. 2015). Recent work further highlights strong among‐individual variation in pollen transport, with a small subset of pollinators acting as ‘super‐spreaders’ of I. glandulifera pollen, potentially explaining heterogeneous effects across sites (Emer & Memmott 2023).

Here, we investigated how I. glandulifera affects the pollination of Stachys sylvatica (Lamiaceae), a widespread native perennial in the United Kingdom with zygomorphic flowers primarily pollinated by bumblebees (Brodin 1994; Taylor & Rowland 2010). Stachys sylvatica shares pollinators and key floral traits with I. glandulifera, including pink, bilaterally symmetrical flowers and a primary reliance on bumblebee pollinators, making this species pair well‐suited for assessing pollinator‐mediated competition. We combined correlative field observations across pristine and invaded sites with an experimental introduction of I. glandulifera into a pristine locality to address three questions: (1) does the presence of I. glandulifera reduce conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas? (2) does invasion alter pollinator visitation and the pollen loads carried by shared pollinators? and (3) how does the introduction of I. glandulifera into a pristine site modify pollinator visitation and consequent patterns of conspecific and invasive pollen deposition during the initial phase of an invasion? We predicted that both invaded conditions and experimental introduction would reduce conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica and alter pollen transport by pollinators. By integrating pollinator observations, pollen load analyses and direct measurements of stigmatic pollen deposition, our study provides a mechanistic assessment of how an invasive plant alters pollination function in a native species. Importantly, whereas most previous studies have focused on long‐established invasions or species removals, our experimental introduction captures the early stages of invasion, offering novel insight into how quickly invasive plants can disrupt pollination processes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area and study species

The study was conducted in July 2013 in the South Downs National Park (southern England, United Kingdom), along the River Rother and adjacent woodland habitats (Table 1, Fig. S1). Based on information from park authorities on the local distribution of I. glandulifera, we selected six sites: three invaded sites where I. glandulifera co‐occurred with S. sylvatica, and three pristine sites where I. glandulifera was absent. One pristine site was later used for the experimental introduction (see below).

Stachys sylvatica is a perennial plant forming patches through creeping rhizomes. It produces erect stems 30–120 cm tall, presenting inflorescences in terminal spikes with verticillasters, each bearing 4–8 reddish‐purple to purplish‐pink flowers, about 12–18 mm long, with a hooded upper lip 4–5 mm and a 3‐lobed lower lip 6–7 mm; stamens 4. Flowers are zygomorphic, hermaphrodite and hypogynous. Stachys sylvatica is commonly visited by Hymenoptera, including different Bombus species (Taylor & Rowland 2010, Fig. S2A,B).

Impatiens glandulifera is a tall annual herb that forms dense monospecific stands in riparian habitats. Inflorescences are axillary racemes bearing 3–12 large, pendulous flowers. Flowers are zygomorphic, hermaphrodite, hypogynous and 2.5–4 cm long; sepals 3, the lower one modified into a prominent, curved nectariferous spur 12–20 mm long; petals 5; the dorsal petal forms a characteristic hood above the androecium and the united lateral petals produce a prominent lip for alighting insects; corolla bright pink to purplish (occasionally white), glabrous; stamens 5 (Beerling & Perrins 1993). In the United Kingdom, I. glandulifera is commonly visited by species of Bombus (Bartomeus et al. 2010, Emer et al. 2015; Fig. S2C,D).

Natural pollination of S. sylvatica in invaded and pristine sites

To assess whether the two species share pollinators, pollinator observations were conducted in pristine and invaded sites. Pollinator observations were conducted under suitable weather conditions for insect activity. Hence, observations were restricted to sunny days without precipitation events or strong winds. Sampling was conducted within a restricted time window, and pristine and invaded sites were surveyed during overlapping dates to minimise temporal variation across all sites surveyed (Table 2). At each site, flowering patches (ca. 1 m2) of each species were observed for 5‐min periods, with observers rotating among patches. A total of 60 min of observation per plant species was accumulated at each site. Observations were conducted during both morning (09:00–11:30 AM) and afternoon (1:00–04:30 PM) between the 5th and the 28th of July (Table 2). At the invaded sites, the observations were done on patches containing only one species. All flower visitors were recorded and identified to the lowest taxonomic level possible in the field. Most visits were made by bumblebees, and visits by other insect groups were infrequent. Representatives of observed bumblebees were collected for identification in the laboratory and to quantify pollen deposition on the body (see below). Because Bombus terrestris and B. lucorum could not be reliably distinguished in the field, they were grouped as B. terrestris/lucorum.

To quantify natural pollen deposition on S. sylvatica, one to four flowers per plant from 7 to 20 plants were collected immediately after pollinator observations. Plants were exposed to open pollination; flowers were not bagged prior to sampling, as the objective was to quantify natural pollen deposition under field conditions. Sampling was conducted in both morning and afternoon periods to assess potential diurnal variation in pollen deposition. Flowers were stored in 70% ethanol until processing. In the laboratory, sampled flowers were inspected, and only those with fully mature stigmas (stigmatic lobes fully extended) were used for pollen counts. Flowers were dissected, and stigmas mounted in glycerol jelly stained with basic fuchsin and examined under a light microscope at ×10 magnification. To correctly identify S. sylvatica and I. glandulifera pollen, pollen samples were prepared in glycerol jelly and compared with the pollen grains observed on S. sylvatica stigmas. Pollen grains of both species are distinguishable under the microscope based on differences in size and morphology (PALDAT; Halbritter et al. 2018; entries 304183 and 304434). For this analysis, we focused on S. sylvatica conspecific pollen deposition quantified per stigma (i.e., per flower), which constitutes the unit of observation in all analyses. We did not include heterospecific pollen, which was generally rare (see Results).

Pollen loads carried by bumblebees

To study the pollen types and loads carried in the body of Bombus in pristine and invaded sites, the insect specimens sampled for taxonomic identification were placed into separate, sterilized containers to prevent pollen contamination among specimens. In the laboratory, specimens were pinned, and the pollen was collected from the surface of the bumblebee body using glycerol jelly cubes. Under the stereomicroscope, small cubes were gently rubbed over the entire body surface to remove pollen grains, allowing the quantification of total pollen load per individual. Pollen from the corbiculae was excluded, as it is not typically involved in pollination. The glycerol jelly samples were then heated to melt and mounted onto microscope slides for pollen identification and counting under light microscopy.

Experimental introduction of Impatiens glandulifera in a pristine site

To examine how rapidly invasion effects emerge, I. glandulifera plants in full bloom were experimentally introduced into Pristine Site 1 (PS1; Fig. S1). Flowering individuals were carefully excavated from a nearby invaded site with intact root balls and surrounding soil. Plants were transferred to containers and watered to minimize transplant stress. Containers were standard horticultural pots, approximately 20–25 cm in diameter and 35–40 cm deep (15 L volume). Three experimental patches were established in Pristine Site 1. Each patch consisted of three containers, each with 2–4 plants, totalling eight I. glandulifera plants per patch. Containers were placed near patches of S. sylvatica and buried at ground level to stabilize them and maintain natural floral display height (1.8–2 m tall). Stems were supported with stakes to preserve upright orientation. Only plants without mature fruits were used to prevent seed dispersal (Fig. S2E). The experiment lasted four consecutive days, between the 22nd and 25th of July 2013, after which I. glandulifera plants were removed. The scale of the experiment was constrained to one site to reduce any risks of establishment and expansion of I. glandulifera in pristine sites, as recommended by the South Downs National Trust. Each day, flowers of S. sylvatica located near the introduced I. glandulifera were sampled every 2 h between 09:00 AM and 04:30 PM and kept in vials with ethanol 70%. One flower per plant was collected at each sampling interval, and different plants were used over time. In the laboratory, S. sylvatica flowers were processed as described above to count pollen on the stigma, noting if the pollen belonged to S. sylvatica or I. glandulifera. During the experiment, pollinator observations were conducted on S. sylvatica and I. glandulifera. On the 22nd July, observations totalled 60 min (30 min per plant species recorded in 5‐min intervals). The remaining days included observations of 120 min per day (60 min per species in 5‐min intervals). Observations were conducted on S. sylvatica plants near the I. glandulifera containers.

Data analysis

Differences in conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas between pristine and invaded sites were analysed using generalised linear mixed‐effects models (GLMMs) with a negative binomial error distribution. Site type (pristine versus invaded), time of sampling (morning versus afternoon) and their interaction were included as fixed effects. Sampling site and plant identity (plants from which flowers were collected) were included as random effects.

Pollen loads carried by bumblebees sampled in pristine and invaded sites were analysed using GLMMs with a negative binomial distribution, with site type as a fixed effect, and the individual bumblebees sampled and sampling site as random effects. Within invaded sites, to assess the relative dominance of invasive versus native pollen, pollen loads of I. glandulifera and S. sylvatica carried by bumblebees were compared using a similar modelling framework, with plant species as a fixed effect.

Bumblebee visitation to I. glandulifera and S. sylvatica during the introduction experiment was sparse, with a strongly non‐normal residual distribution under a linear model (Shapiro–Wilk W = 0.916, P < 0.001). Attempts to model raw visitation counts between plant species using either Poisson or negative binomial distribution resulted in convergence instability of the models. Therefore, bumblebee visitation rate was analysed using a non‐parametric factorial approach based on the aligned rank transform (ART), implemented in the ARTool package (Elkin et al. 2021). This method allows factorial analyses without assuming normality or homoscedasticity. The full factorial model included plant species, day of introduction and the interaction effects. Significance of main effects and interactions was assessed using ANOVA on the transformed data.

Pollen deposition data from the experimental introduction of I. glandulifera were analysed using generalised linear models (GLMs) to the appropriate response variable. First, the probability that sampled S. sylvatica stigmas received I. glandulifera pollen (presence or absence) was analysed using binomial models. Then, changes in the number of conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas across the 4 days of the experiment were analysed using a negative binomial distribution, using pollen counts from the same site prior to the introduction (Day 0) as a reference. Finally, the same modelling approach was applied to assess changes in the amount of I. glandulifera pollen deposited on S. sylvatica stigmas during the experiment.

Model assumptions were evaluated using simulated residual diagnostics implemented in DHARMa (Hartig 2017). Across all negative binomial and binomial models, residual simulations indicated no evidence of overdispersion, zero inflation or systematic residual deviations (all tests P > 0.05). Where Poisson alternatives were evaluated, negative binomial models provided substantially better fit (ΔAIC >500).

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024) using RStudio (version 2024.04.2 + 764; Posit Team, 2024). All GLMMs and GLMs were performed using the packages glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017). Estimated marginal means and post hoc comparisons were obtained, where appropriate, using emmeans (Lenth et al. 2023). All figures were created using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016).

RESULTS

Natural pollination of S. sylvatica in invaded and pristine sites

Pollinator surveys revealed that bumblebees were the main visitors for both species (Table 2). In pristine sites, where only S. sylvatica was present, B. pascuorum was the most frequent visitor, accounting for between 5 and 17 visits per site, followed by B. hortorum and B. lucorum/B. terrestris. Less frequent visits were recorded for Apis mellifera, Anthophora furcata and small syrphid flies. In contrast, invaded sites showed a marked shift in visitation, with most observations representing visits to I. glandulifera by multiple Bombus species and A. mellifera. In two of the three invaded sites, no visits to S. sylvatica were recorded during the study period. Visits to S. sylvatica were observed in Pristine Site 3, where B. pascuorum and B. hortorum visited both S. sylvatica and I. glandulifera, indicating pollinator sharing. For several taxa (e.g., B. pascuorum and B. hortorum), visitation was higher on I. glandulifera than S. sylvatica. Additionally, A. mellifera and syrphid flies were recorded more frequently on I. glandulifera than on S. sylvatica. Some Bombus species (B. pascuorum, B. hortorum and B. lucorum/B. terrestris) were consistently observed in both pristine and invaded sites, while B. bohemicus and B. pratorum were exclusive to invaded sites (Table 2). Combined across all sites, S. sylvatica received 53 visits in pristine sites, but only 22 in invaded sites, corresponding to an approximate 58% reduction in observed visitation.

Across all sites, 136 flowers of S. sylvatica were sampled in pristine sites, of which 103 had fully mature stigmas suitable for pollen counts. In the invaded sites, 174 flowers were sampled, of which 110 could be used for pollen counts. In the pristine sites, 40 flowers (39%) presented pollen from other species (mean ± SD: 6.8 ± 9.1 pollen grains). In invaded sites, 42 flowers (38%) presented I. glandulifera pollen (3.4 ± 4.3), while only 12 flowers (11%) presented other pollen types (3.3 ± 3.1). Generalised linear mixed‐effects models revealed significant differences between pristine and invaded sites in the amount of S. sylvatica pollen deposited on stigmas, while neither the time of sampling nor the interaction between site type and sampling period had a significant effect (Fig. 1; Table 3). On average, stigmas of S. sylvatica flowers received 29.64 conspecific pollen grains in pristine sites and 8.33 conspecific pollen grains in invaded sites, indicating that flowers in pristine sites received approximately 3.5 times more conspecific pollen than flowers in invaded sites (Fig. 1).

Pollen loads carried by bumblebees

In total, 41 bumblebees were sampled (14 and 27 in pristine and invaded sites, respectively). The amount of S. sylvatica pollen carried by bumblebees differed significantly between pristine and invaded sites. Bumblebees collected in pristine sites carried higher loads of S. sylvatica pollen than those collected in invaded sites (χ2 = 4.32, df = 1, P = 0.038; Fig. 2a).

Within invaded sites, pollen loads carried by bumblebees differed strongly between plant species. Bumblebees carried significantly greater amounts of I. glandulifera pollen than S. sylvatica pollen (χ2 = 74.92, df = 1, P < 0.001; Fig. 2b). This pattern suggests a strong dominance of invasive pollen in the pollen loads of pollinators within invaded sites.

Experimental introduction of Impatiens glandulifera in a pristine site

During the introduction experiment, most of the visitors observed on S. sylvatica and I. glandulifera included bumblebees belonging to the species B. pascuorum, B. hortorum, B. pratorum and an unidentified bumblebee (two visits by Bombus cf. terrestris or Bombus cf. lucorum). Three visits by syrphid flies were recorded on S. sylvatica and I. glandulifera, and a single visit of an Andrena was seen on S. sylvatica. In all cases, nectar was the resource collected. In general, through the 4 days of observations, S. sylvatica received more visits by bumblebees than I. glandulifera (Table 4), which was reflected in significant differences in visitation rate (Table 5, Fig. 3a). Visitation rates varied across days, with generally lower visitation early in the experiment and higher visitation towards the end. For S. sylvatica, visitation rate was lower on the first 2 days of the introduction and increased on days 3 and 4. In contrast, bumblebees did not visit I. glandulifera flowers until the second day of introduction, with visitation peaking on the third day (Fig. 3a).

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate that the presence of I. glandulifera can rapidly disrupt the pollination function of the native S. sylvatica. Impatiens glandulifera produces large, nectar‐rich flowers that attract high levels of pollinator visitation, particularly bumblebees, a pattern widely reported across its invasive range (Nienhuis & Stout 2009; Bartomeus et al. 2010). In our study, we selected S. sylvatica as a focal native species because it shares key floral traits with I. glandulifera, including pink, bilaterally symmetrical flowers and a primary reliance on bumblebee pollinators (Taylor & Rowland 2010). Despite differences in flower size and amount of nectar, this trait overlap increases the potential for pollinator sharing, making S. sylvatica a sensitive model for detecting pollinator‐mediated competitive effects of invasion.

The observed reduction in conspecific pollen deposition between pristine and invaded sites is consistent with pollinator‐mediated competition likely driven by the strong attractiveness of I. glandulifera to bumblebees, a pattern consistent with other invasive plants that combine high reproductive investment and efficient dispersal (Van Kleunen et al. 2010; Tyler et al. 2015). Across multiple field sites, stigmas of S. sylvatica in pristine sites received approximately three times as much conspecific pollen as those in invaded sites. However, although 38% S. sylvatica stigmas presented I. glandulifera pollen, the average pollen deposition was low. Bumblebees dominated the pollinator assemblage across all sites, with substantial pollinator sharing, but visits to S. sylvatica were rare in invaded sites. Furthermore, bumblebees collected at invaded sites carried lower loads of S. sylvatica pollen while the pollen loads of I. glandulifera were substantially larger, leaving bumblebees covered by pollen after visits (L. Taylor and G. Horn, personal observation). This pattern is consistent with recent studies showing that I. glandulifera flowers can function as hubs that concentrate pollinator activity and pollen transport (Najberek et al. 2023; Tourbez et al. 2025). Consequently, the presence of I. glandulifera was associated with reduced transport of native pollen by shared pollinators. These shifts in pollen transport suggest that even when pollinators remain present, patterns of conspecific pollen deposition for native plants can be substantially reduced. Our results are consistent with a reallocation of pollinator foraging effort towards the invasive species and changes in pollen placement on the body of bumblebees, resulting in reduced pollen delivery to the native species. These findings align with pollination studies in the presence of invasive species, including I. glandulifera, with clear negative effects on visitation of native species and concomitant consequences in the patterns of conspecific and invasive pollen deposition (Larson et al. 2006; Bartomeus et al. 2008, 2010; Emer et al. 2015; Johnson & Ashman 2019; Parra‐Tabla & Arceo‐Gómez 2021).

Observational studies (i.e., comparison between invaded and pristine sites) have largely demonstrated the negative impact of biological invasions on the pollination of native species (Bartomeus et al. 2008; Morales & Traveset 2009; Padrón et al. 2009; Vilà et al. 2009; Emer et al. 2015). In addition, experimental removal of invasive plants has been widely used to infer impacts on native plant–pollinator interactions, often revealing partial restoration of visitation patterns or interaction frequencies following removal (Baskett et al. 2011; Hanula & Horn 2011; Hanna et al. 2013; Kaiser‐Bunbury et al. 2017). While these approaches have been invaluable for demonstrating that invasive species disrupt native pollination interactions, they necessarily examine communities that have already experienced prolonged exposure to invasion. By contrast, the experimental introduction of invasive plants into previously uninvaded sites remains largely unexplored in the context of pollination, despite its potential to reveal how invasion effects arise at their onset (Morales & Traveset 2009; Traveset & Richardson 2014). Our introduction experiment, therefore, complements removal‐based approaches by capturing the early stages of invasion, allowing us to isolate immediate behavioural and functional responses of pollinators before longer term community restructuring or local adaptation occurs.

Results from the introduction experiment illustrate how substantial changes in pollination function can arise even in the absence of dramatic shifts in visitation rates. Bumblebees rapidly incorporated I. glandulifera into their foraging repertoire following introduction, consistent with previous work showing that pollinators can respond quickly to highly rewarding invasive flowers (Lopezaraiza‐Mikel et al. 2007; Emer et al. 2015). Nevertheless, visitation rates remained higher on S. sylvatica throughout the short duration of the experiment, and we did not observe a complete shift of pollinator visitation away from the native species. Despite this, conspecific pollen deposition on S. sylvatica stigmas declined sharply, with an 81.5% reduction within 4 days of introduction. This decoupling between visitation frequency and pollen delivery is consistent with growing evidence that visitation alone is a poor predictor of pollination function and that changes in pollen transport can occur without obvious changes in visitation rates (King et al. 2013; Opedal et al. 2023). Because the reference for pollen deposition under pristine conditions and the introduction experiment were conducted within the same sampling period, but not immediately consecutively, short‐term variation in pollinator activity cannot be ruled out. However, the magnitude and rate of the decline in conspecific pollen deposition following the introduction strongly suggest that the presence of I. glandulifera was the primary driver of the observed changes.

Although the proportion of S. sylvatica flowers receiving I. glandulifera pollen increased steadily during the experiment, reaching approximately 75% by the final day, this was not accompanied by a comparable increase in the absolute amount of invasive pollen deposited on stigmas. Instead, the dominant effect of the introduction was a marked reduction in conspecific pollen deposition. One plausible explanation is that bumblebees rapidly became heavily coated with I. glandulifera pollen, as found in those sampled in invaded sites, reducing the availability or effective transfer of S. sylvatica pollen during subsequent visits, even when visitation to the native species continued. It is also plausible that the differences in flower size, or fit with flowers, prevented higher I. glandulifera pollen transfer to S. sylvatica stigmas, which deserves more work. Effects of pollen carryover and pollen placement on pollinator bodies have been shown to strongly influence pollen delivery and plant reproductive success (Harder & Barrett 1996; Muchhala & Thomson 2010). Together, these results suggest that early invasion effects on pollination may be driven less by complete pollinator displacement than by rapid changes in pollen loads and transfer efficiency. Such mechanisms highlight how invasive plants can alter the functional dynamics of pollination systems even before major restructuring of pollination visitation becomes apparent.

Several aspects of our study require cautious interpretation. First, while we quantified pollination function through direct measures of pollen delivery to stigmas, we did not follow through to seed production and therefore cannot directly assess downstream reproductive consequences. Nevertheless, conspecific pollen deposition is a well‐established proxy determinant of reproductive success in animal‐pollinated plants and represents an adequate metric for detecting early disruption of pollination processes (King et al. 2013; Opedal et al. 2023). We also did not systematically measure floral abundance or display across sites. Differences in floral abundance can influence pollinator foraging decisions through resource concentration effects, thereby affecting variation in visitation rate and pollen transfer dynamics (Heinrich 1979; Essenberg 2012). Hence, part of the variation detected between pristine and invaded sites might reflect differences in floral dominance in local communities in addition to species identity per se. Future studies incorporating explicit measures of floral abundance and spatial structure would help disentangle behavioural shifts driven by invasive species from those associated with resource density.

Additional considerations relate to the experimental introduction and its temporal scale. The manipulation was conducted at a single pristine site and involved potted individuals rather than in‐ground planting. This design was constrained by biosecurity considerations to prevent the establishment and spread of I. glandulifera in protected habitats. While containers were buried to maintain natural floral height and display, potted plants may differ from naturally established individuals in microhabitat integration or fine‐scale spatial distribution. No visible signs of stress associated with transplant or floral wilting were observed during the experiment; yet subtle physiological differences cannot be excluded.

The introduction involved a limited number of individuals at a single site over 4 days, limiting the generality of the inference across environmental contexts. However, this configuration intentionally reflects an early‐stage invasion scenario rather than a fully established population. This design allowed us to isolate immediate behavioural and functional responses of pollinators. Still, longer term experiments would be required to evaluate whether these effects persist, intensify or are modulated by changes in pollinator learning, plant phenology or community composition (Dellinger et al. 2025; Pérez‐Barrales et al. 2025). The apparent discrepancy in visitation patterns between established invaded sites and the experimental introduction likely reflects differences in invasion stage and temporal scale. In long‐established invaded sites, bumblebees showed a clear preference for I. glandulifera, whereas during the short‐term introduction experiment, visitation remained higher on S. sylvatica. Patterns observed in established invasions may therefore represent longer term behavioural adjustments and foraging optimization by pollinators, while the introduction experiment captures immediate responses to a novel floral resource before such behavioural shifts are fully consolidated. Similar distinctions between short‐term responses and longer term pollinator adjustment have been highlighted in recent work on co‐flowering species and pollination performance (Dellinger et al. 2025). Hence, the consistency between the cross‐site comparisons and the introduction experiment strengthens the inference that the observed decline in conspecific pollen deposition reflects a rapid and functionally meaningful invasion effect.

Finally, our study focused on a single native species, and responses of other co‐flowering plants may differ depending on floral traits, reward levels and pollinator assemblages. Despite these limitations, our results show that invasion by I. glandulifera can rapidly disrupt the pollination function of a co‐flowering native species by reducing conspecific pollen delivery, even before strong shifts in visitation rates become apparent. By combining site comparisons with an experimental introduction, our study demonstrates that early invasion effects can arise through rapid changes in pollen transport and transfer efficiency mediated by shared pollinators. These findings highlight the importance of moving beyond visitation metrics alone and of incorporating direct measures of pollen delivery when assessing the ecological consequences of plant invasions. Given the widespread distribution of I. glandulifera along riparian and woodland habitats in Europe, understanding how quickly pollination processes can be disrupted following invasion may be critical for informing timely management actions aimed at safeguarding pollination of vulnerable native species.
