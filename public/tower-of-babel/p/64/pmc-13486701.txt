Benchmarking criteria to determine latent linear dimensionality in neural data
Authors: Francesco Edoardo Vaccari, Stefano Diomedi, Edoardo Bettazzi, Matteo Filippini, Marina De Vitis, Kostas Hadjidimitrakis, Patrizia Fattori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dimensionality reduction is widely used in modern Neuroscience to process massive neural recordings data. Despite the development of complex non-linear techniques, linear algorithms, in particular principal component analysis (PCA), are still the gold standard. However, there is no consensus on how to estimate the optimal number of latent variables to retain. In this study, we addressed this issue by testing different criteria on simulated data. Parallel analysis, optimal singular value thresholding and cross-validation proved to be the best methods, being largely unaffected by the number of units and the amount of noise. Notably, among the CV schemes tested, cross-validating along either feature and observation dimensions simultaneously provided the best performance, whereas cross-validating only along one matrix dimension was suboptimal. In addition, we showed that given a data matrix with known properties (such as the size and the decay rate of the eigenvalues) the number of significant components that can be estimated is extremely limited. Finally, as an example application to real neural data, we analyzed the spiking activity of a publicly available dataset recorded from macaque primary visual cortex. We show that different criteria can lead to remarkably different results, whereas most of them still identify similar trends in the estimated dimensionality. Our findings suggest that the term ‘dimensionality’ needs to be defined carefully and, more importantly, that the most robust criteria for choosing the number of dimensions should be adopted in future works. To help other researchers with the implementation of such an approach on their data, we provide a simple software package, and we present the results of our simulations through a simple Web based app to guide the choice of latent variables in a variety of new studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55225-1.

Introduction

Over the past decade, state space and dimensionality reduction techniques have become fundamental to data analysis in the field of neuroscience, particularly for the study of population spiking activity1–5, and their utility has been supported by robust theoretical work6–10. In parallel, computational studies have introduced highly sophisticated non-linear methods, based primarily on autoencoder neural networks (e.g., LFADS11; pi-VAE12; PSID13; TNDM14; CEBRA15; d-VAE16). Although these complex tools can model neural activity and uncover latent dynamics related to behaviour, their adoption is limited, primarily due to the expertise required in machine learning and neural networks. Depending on the specific application, linear techniques often provide the best balance between interpretability, simplicity and practical relevance.

As a result, many studies continue to rely on Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) or their variants, providing important results1,2,17–25. Furthermore, PCA is the standard for determining the so-called ‘embedding dimensionality’ (6,8; see Discussion) and it is supported by strong theoretical background10.

A key challenge common to both linear and non-linear methods is determining the optimal number of dimensions to retain, which should ideally reflect the true dimensionality of the dataset. Whilst non-linear models are often cross-validated by default to reduce overfitting thus the number of dimensions is a hyper-parameter often investigated in this stage, this is not typically the case for linear models, making the choice of dimensionality particularly critical in the latter.

To address this issue, we focus here on PCA, as it is the simplest, most widely used and computationally efficient technique. Typically, researchers choose an arbitrary number of principal components (PCs)26–29 or the minimum number of PCs required to achieve a certain percentage (often 80% or 90%) of the explained variance30–32. More recently, a simple eigenvalue-based metric known as the participation ratio (PR) has been used10,33–35. Other criteria have been proposed for selecting the optimal number of PCs that might outperform these traditional approaches, but their adoption in neuroscience remains limited. Among these, several criteria based on eigenvalue thresholds appear promising, such as Kaiser’s rule36, parallel analysis (PA37) and the optimal threshold of Gavish and Donoho38. As mentioned, cross-validation (CV) is widely used in machine learning and can also be applied to dimensionality reduction algorithms, treating the number of dimensions to retain as a hyperparameter to be optimised. For a [time, neurons] matrix describing the activity of neural populations over time, various cross-validation schemes have been proposed (row-only cross-validation, column-only cross-validation, bidirectional cross-validation, and row-and-column cross-validation with imputation; see Methods), but there is still no consensus among neuroscientists as to which cross-validation approach should be considered the gold standard.

Our study builds upon the work by Altan and colleagues39 that focused on dimensionality estimation with linear and non-linear algorithms. They found that, as expected, for linear noise-free generated data both types of algorithms performed similarly well, whereas for non-linear data the linear algorithms performed poorly. However, when noise was intentionally introduced into the system, the non-linear methods were the most affected, overestimating the true dimensionality to the point that they performed worse than linear ones even on non-linear data. Thus, while the study found that noise can specifically disrupt some methods of dimensionality estimation, the authors did not investigate this crucial aspect in depth. Given the pervasiveness of noise in neural data and in order to fill this gap, we here focused on PCA as the most important linear algorithm, and we explored how it deals with properties of the neural data such as scarcity, noise and non-linearity to provide practical guidelines for future studies. We here compared different criteria for dimensionality estimation on simulated data. In this case, simulations are essential to have ground-truth data, both in terms of dimensionality and noise amount. In addition, simulations allowed us to manipulate several parameters of the data to mimic neural activity data under various scenarios. Finally, we applied the same battery of different criteria for dimensionality estimation to real spiking activity from a public dataset to highlight the differences in the results they may generate.

Our work is intended as a guide for neuroscientists using PCA in choosing the most appropriate number of components and proposes that the criteria that performed best in our simulations may be considered for broader adoption.

Whilst the time series in this example have been designed and scaled for ease of visualisation, in real-world situations it is extremely difficult to determine how many principal components are informative and how many contain noise, as the true underlying structure is unknown and noise can obscure meaningful signals. For the purpose of this study, we defined “noise” as the uncorrelated variance or “fluctuations unique to each neuron”6, disregarding any relationship to behaviour or other eventually relevant variables (see Discussion).

Accordingly, an ideal method for choosing the optimal number of latent components to retain should be able to estimate not only the number of relevant dimensions, but also the amount of noise independently. In addition, the dimensionality and the noise amount estimates should be invariant with respect to the number of units, given a stable underlying structure.

For testing different criteria, we first implemented a procedure to generate matrices with a known underlying structure composed of a defined number of latent variables. This process was designed to be flexible, allowing us to simulate various scenarios. Specifically, the parameters that could be varied were: final matrix size ([T, M] where T represents the number of observations, i.e. time bins, and M the number of variables, i.e. synthetic neurons), the number of latent variables N and the way they were generated (either with a random process or a dynamical system), the steepness of the explained variance decreasing curve (DF) which affects the shape of the eigenspectrum, the amount of noise (NF) and noise distribution (Poisson or Gaussian), the distribution of noise across the population of synthetic units (β), the degree of non-linearity of the responses (α) and the type of matrix normalization (z-scoring or soft-normalization; see Methods for more details). Before applying the criteria, a few quality checks ensured that the properties of the simulated data followed the tuning of the simulation parameters as expected. Specifically, the chosen noise factor (NF) (R > 0.90) with had a strong correlation (R > 0.90) with the noise variance in the final matrix (0.97 for linear NEURAL SET, 0.92 for non-linear NEURAL SET). The decay factor (DF) correlated (R > 0.60) with the τ estimated on the final matrices (0.92 for linear NEURAL SET vs 0.62 for non-linear NEURAL SET because non-linearities spread the variance across multiple components). Finally, the non-linear factor α correlated (R > 0.60) with the difference between the variance of X explained by the matrix U (containing responses after non-linear activations) and matrix L (with latent variables before any non-linear activation) that we used as a proxy for the non-linearity of each simulated dataset.

The results are organized into 4 main sections: 1) linear simulations aimed at comparing different criteria and identifying the most reliable for estimating the dimensionality of a linear dataset; 2) analysis of the effect of non-linearities on the performance; 3) simulations designed to estimate the upper bound on the number of identifiable components when the actual number of latent variables is unknown and potentially very high; 4) application of these methods to real, spiking activity data from a public dataset.

Criteria comparison on linear datasets

In this section, we compare the performance of various PCs selection criteria on matrices with a predefined number of significant latent variables (i.e. of known dimensionality). Please, refer to the Methods section for details about the tested criteria. During the data simulation procedure, the final matrices were obtained by adding a noise free data matrix derived from known latent variables to a noise matrix (uncorrelated and randomly generated). To ensure that the number of detectable PCs corresponded to the number of latent variables N, we adjusted the slope of the curve of decreasing explained variance by varying DF. Specifically, DF was reduced iteratively so that the variance explained by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}^{th}$$\end{document} latent variable (i.e. the last one when ordered by explained variance) was never lower than the highest noise component. This approach ensured that all N latent variables could be detected. To validate this, we calculated the ‘optimal’ number of PCs as the number that maximized reconstruction accuracy, which serves as a reference especially for non-linear datasets (see below).

In this section, we focus on a subset of parameters to simulate data that could mimic real neural activity, such as generating latent variables from a dynamical system, setting the noise to follow a Poisson distribution and to be normally distributed over the synthetic units, and soft-normalizing the final matrix (NEURAL FAKE SET). To generalize findings, we also ran another set of simulations generating latent variables via a random process, setting a uniform level of Gaussian noise across all observed variables, and z-scoring the final matrix (GENERAL PURPOSE SET), useful for datasets of other types, such as EMG (see Discussion). The results from the GENERAL PURPOSE SET are reported in the Supplementary Materials and led to very similar findings to the NEURAL FAKE SET.

The remaining simulation parameters were set according to the values listed in Table S1.

The performance metrics we calculated were: i) the dimension error as the difference between the estimated dimensionality and predefined number of latent variables N; ii) the information about the latent structure that can be retrieved from the denoised matrix expressed as a fraction of variance; iii) the estimated noise error as the difference between the estimated and the ground-truth noise amount (see Methods for further details). Note that the last two metrics are expected to be inversely correlated, as a method that can reliably discriminate noise (thus providing a low estimated noise error) will likely also be able to maximize the meaningful information in the denoised matrix. However, because their calculations reverse dependent and independent variables and are applied to slightly different matrices, the resulting values are not constrained to sum to 1 (or 100%).

To report some of the results, we computed the mean absolute error (MAE) which prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling each other out and provide an overall measure of each method performance. To better investigate the behavior of each criterion in different scenarios, for other figures, we used the delta, defined as the signed difference between the estimated and ground-truth dimensionality or noise. Additionally, to provide reference values, we computed an ‘optimal reconstruction’, an approach feasible in our simulations because the U matrix containing the noise-free correlated signals is known (see Methods).

Regarding the error in dimensionality estimates, the noise regime deeply impacted the performance of the variance thresholds, leading to an almost linear increase in the estimated dimensionality (Figs. 3A and 4A). Also, PR and K1 were affected, increasing their estimated dimensionality as the noise increased. The other criteria resulted more robust to noise variation. Secondly, the number of units strongly impacted the explained variance thresholds (orange and red bars in Fig. 3A), leading to a linear increase in the estimated dimensionality (see also Fig. 4B). It also affected the K1 and Opt. SVHT criteria, inflating their estimated dimensionality (Figs. 3A and 4B). The other criteria were less affected. The length of the time series had an effect of the variance thresholds and only marginally on Opt. SVHT (Figs. 3A and 4C). Finally, the decay rate of the matrix was slightly impactful on PR dimensionality estimation which is in accordance with its behaviour as a variance concentration estimator (see Discussion).

Investigating how the accuracy of the reconstruction was influenced by simulation parameters, the noise level was the most influential factor affecting (Fig. 3B), with a linear decrease in performance as the noise increased for most methods except for the variance thresholds which showed an even more rapid decrease as the noise regime moved away from the fixed threshold (Fig. 4D). Additional units were slightly beneficial for most criteria, ensuring an increase in the reconstruction of 5–10% (Fig. 4E).

Analysis of the error in system noise estimation revealed that the most influential parameter was again the noise regime itself. A priori variance-based thresholds (10% and 20%) produce accurate noise estimates only when the true noise level coincides with those preset values; as the actual noise falls below or rises above these thresholds, the estimator correspondingly over- or underestimates noise with a linear trend (Figs. 3C and 4G). PR and, to a slightly lesser degree, the K1 criteria exhibit the same regime-dependent bias, albeit with a reduced magnitude. The other criteria appeared to be much more robust to changes in the noise regime (Figs. 3C and 4G). The length of time series, making spurious correlations less frequent, tended to reduce the noise estimation error, in particular for K1, whereas the other parameters had much less influence on noise estimates (Figs. 3C and 4H-I). Counterintuitively, some of the CV schemes (especially rows only and columns only, but also bi-cross) seem to be negatively influenced by longer time series (Fig. 4C, I). This trend is not very strong (for example, see the small coefficient values in Fig. 3), but it should be considered when even longer time series are available.

Finally, we evaluated the distributions of computation time for the different criteria (Fig. S3). Criteria were implemented in MATLAB and computations were run on a Workstation with Intel Xeon Gold @2.10Ghz (Ubuntu 20.04 LTS). In a few words, the variance thresholds, PR, K1 and Opt. SVHT were the fastest methods (in the order of 10–3 – 10–1 s), then followed by the bi-crossvalidation and other CVs (except imputation) taking 10–1 – 100 s. The slowest were the PA and the imputation-based CV with median computation times of 0.87 and 2.28 s respectively (Fig. S3A), but with the 95th percentile that was about 7 s for PA and 15 s for CV. Predictably, larger data matrices led to longer computation times, and for CV the upper limit on the number of components considered further amplified processing demands (Fig. S3B-D).

The effect of non-linearities on latent structure recovery

The relationship between the population latent dynamics and the observed spiking activity is likely non-linear. To account for this, we extended our simulation procedure by incorporating a non-linear activation function (see Methods), with a tunable parameter that controls the degree of non-linearity in the responses. However, in datasets of this nature and applying the PCA-based linear methods evaluated here, increasing non-linearity amplifies the tradeoff between accurately estimating the true number of latent dimensions and achieving high reconstruction accuracy. Specifically, as non-linearity grows, a greater number of linear components, exceeding the dataset’s true dimensionality, are needed to maintain comparable reconstruction performance. Using the convenient terminology proposed by Jazayeri and Ostojic8, while the intrinsic dimensionality (i.e. the real number of independent latent variables) remains equal to N in our simulations, the embedding dimensionality (i.e., the linear dimensions needed to describe the neural spaces) increases as the non-linearity increases.

To quantify this mismatch, we identified an “optimal” number of PCs as the number that maximizes the reconstruction accuracy of the U matrix (the noise-free, correlated signals), as already done for linear datasets. Our results showed that the discrepancy between this optimal number of PCs and the true number of latent variables N increases almost linearly with the degree of non-linearity. The variance attributable to non-linearities in one of our simulated X matrices was estimated by comparing how much of the data matrix X was explained by the non-linear U responses versus the linearly generated L matrix. The variance explained by non-linearities was indirectly regulated by the α parameter (see Methods). Specifically, we observed a correlation > 0.60 between the dimensional mismatch (intrinsic vs embedding dimensionality) and the variance attributed to non-linearities. Based on this observation, we considered methods that overestimate the dimensionality N but still provide accurate reconstructions and good noise estimates (i.e. approximating the ‘optimality’) as more favorable in this context.

The computation time for non-linear datasets generally aligns with that of linear ones (Fig. S6A). However, it increases substantially (by a factor of 3 to 5) for CV schemes as the proportion of non-linear variance rises from 0 to 40% (Fig. S6B). This is likely due to the higher apparent dimensionality of the data, which necessitates testing a greater number of components (see Methods).

In summary, the non-linear mapping between latent dynamics and neural activity does inflate the apparent dimensionality of a dataset when applying PCA/SVD based methods, but it does not substantially degrade the capacity to recover the noise free activity or estimate the noise of the system when appropriate criteria are applied.

Simulations for estimating the range of identifiable components in high-dimensional datasets

The simulations described so far were intentionally low-dimensional, with the number of latent variables kept much smaller than the number of population units. However, the assumption that population activity is inherently low-dimensional remains to be extensively tested. Furthermore, it is also reasonable to expect an upper bound on the number of principal components (PCs) that can be reliably distinguished from noise, given a matrix with specific dimensions, a particular variance decay profile, and intrinsic noise characteristics. In other words, having limited available data, obtaining a structure with low-dimensional as a result could be just a methodological limitation. To provide guidelines for future applications, we conducted a set of simulations in which data were generated using a very high number of latent variables (N=1000). In these cases, DF was not constrained, and non-linearities could further inflate the apparent dimensionality. For computational efficiency, we used a random generative process instead of a dynamical system to generate such a high number of latent variables, with other parameters set as described in Table S2. As a result, we expected that, although the latent structure was highly complex, only a small subset of PCs (relative to the known N) could be reliably estimated after noise was added. The purpose of this analysis was to establish an upper bound for real datasets, where matrix dimensions are known and τ can be estimated directly from the real PCs’ explained variance, but the maximal number of PCs to retain could, in principle, equal the number of units (i.e., variables in the data matrix).

Furthermore, beside τ, the size of the matrix is determinant when estimating the dimensions to be retained. Indeed, in small matrices, more likely spurious correlations led to fewer significant PCs than in larger matrices. Given a physiological range of τ which is normally 0.5 – 0.8 (at least for motor cortices, based on data extrapolated from figures of25,41,42), the maximum number of dimensions that can be reliably estimated is approximately between 3 and 10 for small matrices (Fig. 7A) and 3–20 for larger ones (Fig. 7B; note that the variability depends also on other factors such τ, noise etc.). When dealing with small matrices, noise estimation is extremely unreliable, providing results largely scattered (Fig. 7E) whereas on larger ones it can be estimated with a few percent points of error (Fig. 7F). As already mentioned, the reconstruction accuracy depends much more on the noise level, rather than the mere size of the matrix (Fig. 7C,D). Very similar trends were observed also when analyzing data simulated with N=1000 and via the dynamical process as latent variable generator (data not shown).

These findings can have important consequences when interpreting the latent variables in real datasets (see Discussion). Results for other matrix dimensions or other criteria can be conveniently consulted via a Web app with a simple graphic interface, see ‘Code availability’.

The dimensionality of neural data depends on the criterion chosen

To show the impact of criterion on dimensionality analysis outcomes, we applied the criteria for component retention to data from a public repository, containing spiking activity from macaque visual cortex20,43. Neurons were recorded while anesthetized macaques were passively shown gratings with 8 different orientations interleaf with a blank screen. Here only the activity from the primary visual area (V1) was considered due to the much higher unit count than the secondary visual area (> 100 vs 30 neurons/session on average). We compared activity between each grating vs blank screen presentation. PR has been used previously on data recorded from the gustatory cortex of rats and showed that the dimensionality of neural population activity decreases in the presence of a stimulus35. Accordingly, in our case, one could expect a lower dimensionality during the presence of visual stimulation with respect to the resting activity. Moreover, we aimed at assessing how stable the dimensionality and noise estimation are when changing the size of the neural population because in real-world data this aspect can be strongly limited by the available recordings. Note that we maintained consistency with previous analyses and adhered to the implicit assumptions underlying PCA-based modeling of neural activity. Accordingly, we defined ‘noise’ as uncorrelated activity across neurons, irrespectively of whether it carried or not information about the stimulus. Briefly, we averaged the activity for each stimulus and for the baseline activity during blank screen visualization. We randomly subsampled the units to increase the population size in a controlled way, and we applied all the criteria for retaining the principal components. First, the decay rate (τ ) of the explained variance was calculated for each neural data matrix as a proxy of the concentration of variance in the first PCs which is also strongly related to the maximal number of components that can be distinguished from noise (Fig. 7A–C). We observed that the decay rate tended to be stable as the population size increased (1.17- 1.21) for the baseline activity and it slightly converged to around 0.60 with larger populations for the activity during the stimulus presentation. In both cases, the variability decreased as the population size became larger (Fig. 8A–C).

We then investigated how much the estimated dimensionality changes when varying population size and, more importantly, the method used to assess it. We observed a considerable amount of variability depending on which selection criterion was used and, for some of these, as seen in the simulated data, also depending on the size of the neural population analysed.

Indeed, many criteria tended to estimate higher dimensionality as the population size increased, especially for the baseline activity (Fig. 8D,E). This trend was particularly evident for variance threshold methods and the Kaiser Rule. This was also the case for the bi-cross validation applied on the stimulus activity, but only for small populations (< 20 units). When computing the relative difference between the dimensionality of the stimulus and the baseline activity, again we found different trends for the different criteria (Fig. 8F). Specifically, the variance thresholds and, to a lesser extent, also K1 and PR, provided negative values, indicating a lower dimensionality during stimulus driven with respect to spontaneous activity. In contrast, all the cross-validation schemes, PA and Opt. SVHT, provided positive values (100 to 250%), supporting an expanded dimensionality during stimulus presentation.

Finally, when considering the noise estimates provided by the different criteria (Fig. 8G, H), we found that most of the estimates reached a plateau when > 20 units were provided, but in correspondence of very different noise levels. Indeed, for the baseline activity, the K1 seemed to provide significantly lower noise estimates (around 25%) than all the other methods that in turn provided similar estimates in between 30 and 40%. When analyzing the stimulus activity, most criteria indicate the noise amount in the range 5–10% (for example, Opt. SVHT, bi-cross and imputation CVs, K1 etc.), while others slightly higher (for example, only rows or only columns CVs), around 15–17%. Interestingly, when computing the difference between the noise levels of the stimulus and baseline activity (Fig. 8I), all the methods provided negative values, strongly suggesting the amount of noise (i.e. uncorrelated activity) during the stimulus presentation is reduced. As mentioned previously, the variance thresholds cannot be used to estimate the amount of noise, since they are based on a priori arbitrarily chosen noise regime.

The reduction of the global noise level during stimulus is likely to be related to a reduction in the neural variability, that is expected to quench due to stimulus presentation as observed at single-cell level44,45. However, since this issue was beyond of the scope of the present work, we did not test further this hypothesis.

Overall, this apparent contrasting results obtained with different retention criteria can be reconciled considering the different assumptions behind their specific application (see Discussion).

Discussion

This study was intended as a benchmark of different criteria to define the optimal number of latent components when applying PCA/SVD to neural signals. Simulations allowed to manipulate noise, size of the data matrix, non-linearities etc. to generate a variety of scenarios plausible in Neuroscience.

We found that criteria such as variance thresholds and participation ratio can provide a measure of the concentration of variance in the first PCs, but, when used to identify data dimensionality, they are strongly biased by properties of the data (noise, the number of units in the population etc.). In contrast other criteria such as parallel analysis (PA), optimal SVHT and CV resulted much more robust, so they are preferrable in most cases. Our work investigated several important aspects that were not addressed in the study by Altan and colleagues39. For example, they only considered PA as a linear estimator of dimensionality without benchmarking it with other criteria on noisy data. Moreover, they did not vary the shape of the explained variance distribution across PCs which was found to have a significant impact, with higher decay values preventing a clear distinction between informative and noise components for many PCs. In addition, the possibility to use retention criteria to simultaneously evaluate the noise of the data was not explored previously. Here, we have shown that criteria that were robust enough to provide accurate estimates of dimensionality in a variety of scenarios were also able to estimate the overall level of noise in the system.

First, we aimed at identifying which methods were the most reliable to estimate dimensionality. Note that, when not stated otherwise, we define the dimensionality of a dataset as the number of components that effectively capture meaningful patterns of correlation across the population. To be considered significant, the strength of these correlations must surpass that of spurious or random correlations (see below). The commonly used fixed variance thresholds (80–90%) guaranteed good results only when the noise regime was close to the predefined threshold, with performance degrading rapidly with either increasing or decreasing noise. Moreover, these thresholds methods are extremely sensitive to the number of units in the dataset, often overestimating dimensionality as the unit count increases. The Participation Ratio behaved similarly to a 75–77% threshold, but offered more flexibility, mitigating some of the hard threshold limitations (e.g., it exhibited lower sensitivity to number of units / variables in the data matrix or noise). However, PR cannot provide a completely independent estimate of the dataset dimensionality from the amount of noise it contains, overestimating noise in low-noise conditions (similarly to the 80% threshold) and underestimating it in high-noise conditions. The Kaiser Rule performed quite well with long time series (i.e., sufficient rows in the data matrix, 1000+) where the impact of spurious correlations was reduced, but it was still sensitive to the amount of noise and to the number of units. Parallel analysis provided very robust results. This is in line with previous works that pointed out the superiority of PA compared to other criteria46,47, even when applied to neural data similarly to what reported by Altan and colleagues39.

However, it was prone to a statistical bias as it tended to underestimate dimensionality and overestimate noise especially when not many simulated neurons were available. This bias could be partially due to the use of the 95th percentile of the null distribution as threshold which is the standard in the field. For the sake of simplicity, we did not explore lower thresholds here, but they could have potentially mitigated this bias.

Cross-validation and optimal SVHT resulted to be the most reliable criteria. Regarding Optimal SVHT, the methodology was well-grounded on the random matrix theory48 and it has recently been generalised to noise distributions other than that zero-mean Gaussian assumed in the original paper49. It ensures very robust performance with a few simple calculations with respect to CV. In our study, we tested four different CV schemes to which most of the approaches proposed in the neuroscience literature can be traced back. Cross-validating leaving out one or more time points / trials (I, CV over rows) or alternatively one or more neurons (II, CV over columns) have been often used leading to intriguing results about the functions of neural populations (time/trials:50 | neurons:5,26,51,52). However, from a statistical perspective, in these CV schemes, predictions are not completely independent from the training set, possibly providing to suboptimal results53. Accordingly, when tested on simulated data exhibiting a well-defined low-dimensional structure, the cross-validated error (or the reconstruction R2) is not guaranteed to reach a clear minimum (or maximum). As a result, additional procedures must be employed, such as identifying the “elbow” point in the error curve (see Methods). When dealing with the complexity of real-world data, this phenomenon may be mitigated and may not necessarily introduce biases, but caution is still required. Bi-cross validation (III) and CV over rows and columns with imputation (IV), although not overtly used in neuroscience to our knowledge, demonstrated robust performance and yielded clear minima or maxima, particularly the imputation-based method. The latter offers good flexibility, being able also to handle missing values by design (missing trials for some neurons, for example), but at the cost of a much higher computational load with respect to the other criteria here tested.

Recently, shared variance component analysis (SVCA) was developed by Stringer and colleagues54. This is closely related to bi-cross validation because it is based on dividing the data into two parts, both along the neural dimension (dividing the neural population into two subpopulations) and along the temporal domain (dividing the recordings into two alternating groups). In a separate study from the same group, an alternative CV scheme for estimating signal versus noise in PCA was introduced55. This method involves computing eigenvectors from one set of stimulus repetitions and projecting activity from a distinct set of repetitions to isolate the true stimulus-related covariance. Although of extreme interest, it might be conceptually related to scheme of CV over rows, being possibly suboptimal compared to other schemes.

Finally, other promising approaches can be found in literature (for example: PCs selection after estimation of trial-to-trial noise through reshuffling, 23; Empirical Kaiser Criterion, 56). For reasons of simplicity and because these methods have not been widely adopted by the neuroscience community, we decided not the include them in our battery of retention criteria.

The choice of the correct number of dimensions can have important implications on data interpretation (e.g., when calculating measures such as the tangling index or the alignment between subspaces29,32), thus representing a critical step. To provide guidance for future applications with real data and provide upper bounds for dimensionality, we designed a subset of simulations with a complex underlying structure (i.e., with a high number of latent variables) to explore scenarios where not all latent variables would be detectable. Indeed, given a known decreasing curve in the explained variance for the PCs and a data matrix with specific dimensions, one can not reliably estimate an arbitrary large number of significant components. Accordingly, as the decay τ of the curve increases (indicating that the variance is more concentrated in the first PCs) and the matrix dimensions decrease (raising the likelihood of random spurious correlations), the number of significant components that can be estimated decreases. We found that in standard-sized datasets (with tens to hundreds of neurons recorded for hundreds to thousands of timepoints), we can distinguish from the noise at most a few dozen components, even when the actual dimensionality is much higher. In other words, in many cases, whenever a low-dimensional structure is identified, there is always the question of whether a true low-dimensional structure underlies the neural activity, or whether the true structure is high-dimensional but cannot be fully observed due to methodological limitations.

While applying the battery of PCA‐based retention criteria to macaque V1 recordings during baseline and visual stimulation, we found that both the choice of criterion and the number of neurons included strongly influenced dimensionality and noise estimates. Variance concentrated differently across principal components in baseline versus stimulus conditions, and criteria diverged in how they interpret that variance as signal or noise. As more units are added, dimensionality estimates vary in method-specific ways, underscoring that perceived neural dimensionality could be prone to analysis assumptions.

Overall, our findings point in the direction of a reduction in the noise (i.e., uncorrelated activity) following stimulus presentation and the emergence of more activation patterns that are consistent and can be captured by an increased number of PCs. In line with that, the variance is spread over more dimensions as suggested by the lower τ values for stimulus with respect to the spontaneous activity. Specifically, we found that the CV with imputation, Opt. SVHT and PA, that resulted the most robust on simulated data, tended to provide similar results (lower noise and higher number of identifiable dimensions during stimuli). The PR converged to a dimensionality (≈ 4) either during spontaneous or stimulus-driven activity and estimated a slight reduction in noise (from around 30% to 17%). In contrast, the variance thresholds assessed a lower dimensionality during stimulus (from -40 to -60%) which is in line with their behaviour in higher vs lower noise regimes (see previous results on simulated data).

Previously, the activity during drifting grating presentation was found to span 5–10 PCs in macaque V1, either with an 80 or a 90% explained variance threshold57. Given that the population size considered by the authors was approximately 60 neurons, the findings do align well with the dimensionality we here estimated from the real data, which is around 4 (6) for the 80% (90%) variance threshold (60 units, Fig. 8E). In a related study, the activity of the gustatory cortex in rat was found to have a lower of dimensions during stimulation than baseline 35. Dimensionality was assessed with PR and the unit count was significantly lower (up to 10 neurons) than ours, thus it is unknown whether these findings would persist with larger populations. This apparent discordance with our results could be to a different information processing in gustatory vs visual cortices or to methodological aspects (e.g. populations of different size, preprocessing, etc.).

The findings presented highlight the need for a careful, explicit definition of ‘dimensionality’ when it is applied to neural data. Indeed, ‘dimensionality’ is normally used to indicate the number of latent, independent patterns of activity that “capture a significant fraction of population covariance”9. However, in the term “significant” lies an ambiguity: it may simply refer to a large amount of variance or, alternatively, differentiated from noise. Our study shows that retention criteria such as variance thresholds and PR operate in accordance with the first definition, whereas other methods (such as PA, Opt. SVHT and cross-validation) do retain components based on the second one. In fact, adopting the convenient nomenclature already used by Jazayeri and Ostojic8, a neural dataset could be characterized by an ‘ambient dimensionality’ (equal to the number of observed variables, i.e. the single neurons), a lower ‘apparent embedding dimensionality’ (equal to the number of latent variables identified by thresholds/PR methods and mostly indicating the concentration of variance in the first components), an even lower ‘structural embedding dimensionality’ (equal to the number of latent variables identified by PA/Opt. SVHT/CV methods and providing hints on the real structure can be significantly discriminated in the dataset) and finally the ‘intrinsic dimensionality’ (determined with non-linear methods8,33).

We will now discuss the limitations and future perspectives of our study, focusing on three main aspects: our decision to concentrate exclusively on PCA (I), our definition of ‘noise’ (II), and the generalisability of our results (III).

In this work, we deliberately chose to focus only on PCA as it is the most common linear dimensionality reduction technique for its simplicity (see Introduction). We demonstrated that it is still capable of providing acceptable estimates of the underlying structure of the data even when noise and non-linearities are present, thus serving the scope for most Neuroscience applications.

Several extensions of PCA have been designed to handle noise more appropriately thus being more suitable especially in modelling single-trial variability and / or to maximize the interpretability of the loading vectors. Examples include probabilistic PCA (PPCA58), robust PCA (see59, for one of the many available implementations), sparse PCA60,61, their combinations (see for example62) or Factor Analysis and its variants5. The list is by no means exhaustive, and unfortunately these methods are still underused in the field of neural data analysis, thus they were not included in our benchmarking. We hope that future studies will examine in more depth their pros and cons when analysing neural dynamics.

In the present study, noise was intended as ‘uncorrelated’ activity across neurons, without any reference to behavior or any external variables. In real-world applications, there can be information about behavior/other variables encoded in components of neural activity capturing a very limited variance (i.e., not in the top PCs) that would be discarded by the unsupervised PCA-based criteria since they are agnostic about behavior. If interested in uncovering neural dynamics related to a specific behavior / external variable, one should rely on supervised dimensionality reduction techniques which basically maximize both the variance shared across neurons and the information about some external variable. Among these, important examples are dPCA25 or targeted dimensionality reduction17.

Notably, a part of our findings also extends to other related methods. For example, while parallel analysis / opt. SVHT can be adapted only for all those techniques involving a covariance matrix and/or eigenvalues, the CV can easily be applied to other types of analysis (see a similar suggestion in5). This could be the case, for example, with the Principal Component regression (PCR), the Partial Least Square (PLS) regression or the Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) which are gaining popularity for modelling inter-area communication subspaces or the relationship between muscle and neural activity63–66. In these cases, very complex procedures are required to identify the significant latent components (see for example63), while the imputation CV, for example, removing data along time and neuron dimensions simultaneously, could lead to more robust results in a single step. Similarly, non-negative matrix factorization 67 or its more efficient variants68 are often used to identify muscle synergies. However, determining the number of synergies to be retained is still problematic and the same CV scheme could be beneficial69. It should be noted that, although the focus of this study is on spiking activity, the results are generalisable to other types of data (such as kinematics or EMG), as long as that their distributions do not deviate too strongly from the Gaussian distribution (for example, in our case, the use of Poisson distribution and some non-linearity did not disrupt the results).

Finally, techniques such as SVD / PCA assume static correlations across neurons, which can obscure the dynamic structure in neural activity. To overcome this limitation in an unsupervised manner, methods like Hidden Markov Models19,70–73 offer a powerful alternative by segmenting neural time series into distinct temporal states. Within each hidden state, dimensionality reduction can be applied to reveal state-specific variations in correlation patterns and to capture the evolving dynamics of neural populations more effectively.

In conclusion, we systematically compared several criteria for estimating the number of principal components using a broad set of simulated datasets. While hard explained variance thresholds and the Participation Ratio are commonly applied in neuroscience, they seem highly sensitive to both noise regime and the number of observed variables (i.e., recorded neurons). Overall, cross-validation, Parallel Analysis and Opt. SVHT, emerged as the least affected by noise and sample size, making them promising tools for real-world applications.

Although cross-validation is increasingly adopted, we found that not all cross-validation schemes perform equally well. In this regard, cross validating simultaneously along both columns and rows of the data matrix outperforms other schemes.

Importantly, while our findings highlight the advantages of a few criteria for estimating data dimensionality and noise levels, we do not intend to undermine previous work that used other criteria. In fact, the results often differ by only a few percentage points, yet significant trends may still emerge. Rather, we emphasize the need for future studies to specify clearly what is meant by “dimensionality” (and “noise”) as different criteria entail distinct definitions. We also advocate for the implementation of more robust approaches, including the choice of retention criteria guided by theoretical assumptions and the use of bootstrapping to address varying population sizes, in order to improve reproducibility and comparability across studies. Our aim is to encourage discussion on the adoption of more reliable methods in a collaborative spirit and to open new perspectives on the interpretability of large neural datasets. To facilitate this process, we provide software alongside this manuscript, offering practical tools to support the routine application of the criteria examined here as part of preliminary analyses.

Methods

The primary scope of this work is to provide guidance for the analysis of neural data. However, the criteria used to estimate the dimensionality are not necessarily derived from the neuroscience literature and can therefore be applied to a wide range of datasets. Although, in the following sections, we refer to the columns of the synthetic data matrices as ‘units’ or ‘neurons’ for clarity, they are simply time series generated as linear combinations of a set of latent variables, thus they can be representative of different types of data (e.g., EMG recordings; see Discussion).

Finally, in the following paragraphs, when we refer to ‘the first PCs’ or similar terms, we mean the first PCs sorted according to their explained variance.

Simulations

To compare the performance of different criteria for choosing the optimal number of latent variables to retain we applied them on a variety of synthetic data. This step was unavoidable to have a ground truth underlying structure knowing a priori the number of components and their temporal dynamics as well as the amount of noise injected into the system. Moreover, by controlling the different parameters of the data generation procedure we have been able to simulate various scenarios in which the different criteria of interest were tested.

The simulation procedure, partially similar to that used in Altan and colleagues 39, followed a number of steps (see Fig. 1E for a flowchart of the entire procedure): 1) latent variables generation; 2) linear combination to create time series (and eventual non-linear activation); 3) noise addition; 4) normalization.

This step was motivated by the fact that latent variables (i.e., PCs) in natural, real data show a continuous, smooth decrease in variance, thus there is not a clearcut between those representing significant shared covariance vs those containing noise. We computed the scaling factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SF}_{i}$$\end{document} drawn from an exponential decay curve as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SF}_{i}= {e}^{-DF\cdot i}$$\end{document}where the decay factor DF can be regulated during the simulation.

For each simulation, the scaling factors were then normalized to ensure comparability across different simulations:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SF}_{i}= \frac{{SF}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{SF}_{i}}$$\end{document}where N indicates the number of generated latent variables.

During the step of scaling, the parameter DF is crucial for regulating the steepness of the decay curve and thus affecting the amount of variance explained by each of the generated latent variables in the final data matrix. Specifically, when DF increases, the first latent variables will have a much greater importance than the last ones. For even higher DF, the last latent variables will be irrelevant to the point that they will not be detectable in the final matrix and be confused with the noise. This scaling step resembles that by Owen and Perry77 (‘geometric pattern’ for the singular values).

(2)Linear combination to create time series.

(2)Linear combination to create time series.

Linear combination to create time series.

M random loading vectors W, each containing N random values drawn from a Gaussian distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, 1\right)$$\end{document} were generated and then normalized, to obtain isotropic vector distributions. Loading vectors W were used to create M synthetic neurons as linear combinations of the generated latent variables:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{{W}^{j}}_{i}*{l}_{i }(t)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is the value representing the activity of the jth synthetic neuron at time t. At the end of this step, the vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}$$\end{document} was z-scored to obtain the same variance (= 1) for all synthetic units. We stacked together the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}$$\end{document} vectors to generate the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U \left[T, M\right]$$\end{document} matrix containing the synthetic neurons noise-free activity. For non-linear simulations, we normalized the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}$$\end{document} vectors in the range [0, 1] and we then applied an exponential activation function to each U element39:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(U)= \frac{{e}^{\alpha U}-1}{{e}^{\alpha }-1}$$\end{document}where α regulates the degree of non-linearity of the simulated neural responses.

(3)Noise addition.

(3)Noise addition.

Noise addition.

In this step, a random noise matrix E, with the same size as U was generated. Two alternative distributions were used to generate the noise matrix E: either a Gaussian or a Poisson distribution. In cases in which noise followed a Gaussian distribution, E was simply generated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathrm{0,1}\right)$$\end{document}. Only in cases in which noise was Poisson distributed, each column of U was first normalized in the range [0 5] to approximate the physiological firing range [0 100] sp/s of a cortical neuron in a 50 ms bins. Then, we calculated E as was generated as a ‘shifted’ Poisson distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\sim (Pois\left(U\right) - U)$$\end{document} to obtain a matrix where noise variance depended on the time varying activity of each synthetic unit (as expected under the Poisson assumptions) and the mean was equal to 0. Subtracting the expected values (i.e. shifting the Poisson distribution to have 0 mean) enabled us to separate the pure noise matrix E from the noise-free activity U.

Then, irrespectively from the distribution used, both U and E matrices were zscored to ensure that all columns in both matrices had equal variance (= 1).

The final data matrix X was then obtained as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{t, j}=\left(1-{\beta}_{j}\right)*{U}_{t,j}+ NF* {\beta}_{j}*{E}_{t,j}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t\in \left[0, T\right]$$\end{document} and j∈ [1, M]

where U is the matrix with noise-free synthetic unit data, E is the matrix of noise, NF is a noise factor that scales the entire E matrix and β is a parameter that controls the amount of noise added to each synthetic unit independently.

NF controlled the total amount of noise to be added to the data matrix. Indeed, with NF ≈ 0, the amount of noise was insignificant, while for NF ≈ 1 the noise had the same variance as the signal (i.e., the generated data matrix U), representing approximately 50% of the total variance in the final matrix. Although such high noise values may seem unphysiological, our application on real data showed even higher values (see Results). Further reasons for this noise range are provided in the Discussion.

Since it is unlikely that all units are equally noisy in real situations, we introduced the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{j}$$\end{document} values to be able to control the noise to be added to each single unit. For a subset of simulations, we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{j}=0.5$$\end{document} for all units (similarly to the noise added in39), but in most of data simulations, we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{j} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0.5, 0.17\right)$$\end{document} (note that these mean and std values ensured that approximately 99% of the values fell between 0 and 1 since, for a normal distribution, the interval mean ± 3*std contains approx. 99.7% of the probability mass; more extreme values outside this range were set to 0 or 1, respectively).To ensure that X was completely positive matrix, as is typical in real spiking neural activity expected to lie in a range [0 + Inf] , and without affecting the latent variables’ temporal patterns, we performed the transformation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{t, j}= {X}_{t, j}+|{\mathit{min}}_{t,j}\left({X}_{t, j}\right)|$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t\in \left[0, T\right]$$\end{document} and j∈ [1, M]

At the end of the procedure, X was a [T, M] matrix, with each row representing a time bin and each column a synthetic neuron. Formatting the data as a matrix was necessary to subsequently apply PCA.

(4)Normalization.

(4)Normalization.

Normalization.

This step is intended as part of data preprocessing, more than the last step of the simulation procedure. Indeed, once the neural data was simulated as previously described, an additional step was taken to make sure that the common preprocessing follows that typically used for real spiking activity. Indeed, it is good practice to standardize (z-scoring) data before PCA, and this was our first choice. However, when dealing with real neural data, soft-normalization seems preferrable because it is a good trade-off between hard normalization (where each unit will have the same weight in determining PCs, regardless of spiking activity variance) and no normalization (which is biased towards the more active units in the population). Since soft-normalization is widely used in Neuroscience applications involving state-space methods and dimensionality reduction32,42,78, we used soft-normalization alternatively of z-scoring in a subset of simulations before applying PCA and criteria for selecting the PCs. Soft-normalization was performed as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{t,j}= \frac{{X}_{t,j}}{range\left({X}_{1:T,j}\right)+ const}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{\left(1:T,j\right)}$$\end{document} is the noisy data of the jth synthetic unit over T time points, and const is a constant set equal to half the mean of X (i.e., the mean activity of the simulated population). After soft-normalization, we subtracted from each jth column its mean. Note that soft-normalization might influence the dimensionality estimates since it has been proven that, keeping constant pair-wise correlations, dimensionality is maximal when all neurons have the same firing rate variance and it decreases as the distribution becomes more heterogeneous35. We thus ran simulations using soft-normalization (main results, NEURAL FAKE SET) and others with z-score (GENERAL PURPOSE SET).

In summary, we designed a procedure to build synthetic data matrices of size [T, M] containing M synthetic unit responses obtained by combining N latent variables generated by a dynamical process. Other adjustable parameters included the steepness of the latent variable variance decreasing curve (DF), the degree of non-linear activation (α ), the noise distribution (Gaussian or Poisson), the noise level (NF), the noise distribution across the population of synthetic units (β), the type of matrix normalization (z-scoring or soft-normalization).

Variance decay rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{\tau}}$$\end{document} of a matrix

As already mentioned, the decay rate of the variance explained by principal components of a matrix (strongly related to the eigenspectrum) is an important property when determining the dimensionality. Indeed, it provides an indication of how much the variation is concentrated in the first PCs or alternatively is more spread across a wide number of dimensions.

For a generic matrix, the decay rate τ of the explained variance associated with its PCs can be computed fitting the function:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ExplVar(k)= {e}^{-\tau *k}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\in \left[1, K\right]$$\end{document}

where ExplVar(k) is the explained variance associated to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}^{th}$$\end{document} PC and K is the number of PCs (normally equal to the number of neurons, in the case of neural data, or M, the number of synthetic units in the case of our simulations). Note that in the case of our simulated matrices, computing τ was done a posteriori, and it is obviously related to DF value set for a specific simulation. However, knowing DF is not sufficient to get an exact estimate of τ a priori due to the random steps involved (see Results). Since τ can be estimated on simulated as well as on real data (but not DF values, which are known only for simulations), we used the estimated τ on the simulated matrices to provide guidance about its impact on the number of components to retain in future, real world applications (see Results). As a final note, although the explained variance decrease has been modelled with a power law function in some extremely high-dimensional datasets55,79, we found that an exponential decay better fitted the variance of the first PCs (< 25) of our simulated matrices even when the scaling factor for the generated latent variables was calculated following a power law (data not shown). It seems that the power law provides a better description of the eigen spectrum of very high-dimensional matrices due to the thicker tail of this distribution. However, since the real dimensionality of the matrices we here generated was often limited (< 25) (I), in common datasets of spiking data only the first dozen of PCs is normally the most investigated (II), and the two distributions differed very slightly (II), we opted for exponential decay without loss of generalizability. Nevertheless, a function to fit the power law to matrix eigen spectra was included in the associated software package.

Criteria for dimensionality estimation

In our study, we compared multiple criteria to estimate the optimal number of PCs:

Explained variance threshold and participation ratio (PR).

Explained variance threshold and participation ratio (PR).

Explained variance threshold and participation ratio (PR).

First, since they are commonly used, we implemented two explained variance thresholds, one at 90% and another at 80%. Other percentages (such as 70%) are sometimes used, but much more rarely and the results are expected to generalize to those cases.

Second, we considered the Participation Ratio (PR) which is calculated as follows10,35,80:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PR= \frac{{(\sum_{k=1}^{K} {\lambda}_{k}) }^{2}}{(\sum_{k=1}^{K}{{\lambda}_{k}}^{2})}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{k}$$\end{document} is the eigenvalue associated to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}^{th}$$\end{document} PC, and K is the total number of PCs (normally equal to M, the number of synthetic units). A PR=1 indicates that all the variance is explained by the first PC (thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{k}=0$$\end{document} for k>1), while a PR=K=M implies that variance is equally distributed across all PCs (thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{1}={\lambda}_{2}=\dots {\lambda}_{K}$$\end{document}). Values within these lower and upper bounds indicate the concentration of the variance in the first PCs, providing a direct estimation of the number of PCs to retain. This criterion generally behaves similarly to a 75–77% variance threshold (10; see also our Results).

(2)Kaiser rule.

(2)Kaiser rule.

Kaiser rule.

According to Kaiser rule, all PCs with eigenvalues greater than one should be retained36,81. The reasoning behind this criterion is that eigenvalues greater than 1 correspond to PCs which explain more variance than each of the original variables. However, the relationship between eigenvalues and explained variance is straightforward when data are standardized before applying PCA (i.e., z-scored), but it is not when data have been soft-normalized (since different original variables can explain more variance than others). Since we used z-scored, but also soft-normalized data, we adapted the Kaiser rule to retain all the PCs explaining more variance of X than the average variance explained by each individual column (i.e., unit) in X.

(3)Parallel analysis (PA).

(3)Parallel analysis (PA).

Parallel analysis (PA).

Parallel analysis is a derivation of the Kaiser rule46,81,82 that instead of using a single, fixed threshold for the eigenvalues (= 1), it uses values derived from a random null distribution37. We generated a surrogate matrix by reshuffling the elements of each column (synthetic unit) of the matrix X, thus preserving individual columns statistics, but disrupting any significant correlation due to the latent structure except for the spurious ones. We sorted in descending order the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix of these surrogate data. This procedure was normally repeated 250 times when not otherwise specified (100 and 1000 iterations were also tested in a subset of simulations with negligible differences) obtaining a null distribution of eigenvalues from surrogate matrices. Finally, a PC was retained if its associated eigenvalue was higher than the 95th percentile of the eigenvalue null distribution.

(4)Optimal hard thresholding (optimal SVHT).

(4)Optimal hard thresholding (optimal SVHT).

Optimal hard thresholding (optimal SVHT).

The optimal hard threshold for singular values criterion was introduced by Gavish and Donoho38. The idea is to retain the components with eigenvalues greater than an optimal threshold. The threshold, in the case of a matrix with unknown noise, is obtained multiplying the coefficient ω and the median of the matrix eigenvalues. ω depends on the shape of the matrix (ratio between the number of rows and columns) and it can be conveniently computed with the MATLAB code provided by the Authors in the original publication38.

(5)Cross-validation (CV).

(5)Cross-validation (CV).

Cross-validation (CV).

Finally, cross-validation was considered as a method to choose the number of components. CV is not straightforward in the framework of dimensionality reduction because the need to cross-validate both rows and columns of the data matrix to obtain optimal values53,83. We here tested 4 different CV schemes: CV over neurons (columns), CV over time (rows), bi-cross validation and CV with imputation (see Fig. 1F-I). CV over neurons consists in holding out a random subset of neurons (for example, 10–20% of the population, test set), estimating the latent variables on the train set and computing the precision of the reconstruction of the test set as a function of the number of PCs used (scheme in Fig. 1F). This procedure can be seen as an extension of the leave-one-neuron-out scheme proposed by some Authors 5,52. In CV over time, a subset of time points or entire trials rather than neurons is held out as a test set (scheme in Fig. 1G). Bi-crossvalidation was introduced by Owen and Perry77 building on Gabriel’s intuition84. It seeks complete independence between the train and the test set and it is based on a double partition of the matrix over rows and columns (scheme in Fig. 1H). In principle different proportions of rows and columns can be held out. Accordingly, a bi-crossvalidation scheme is defined as (r, c) indicating r-fold CV over rows and c-fold over columns. For the sake of simplicity, we here compared the classic tenfold procedure for all other CV schemes with a (3, 3) bi-crossvalidation and the fivefold with (2, 2) bi-crossvalidation77. Finally, we implemented another CV scheme based on imputation. The core idea is to hold out random, speckled elements over rows and columns (scheme in Fig. 1I) following the pioneering work by Wold85. The drawback is that the train set results in a data matrix with missing values and the algorithms for PCA become non-efficient and complex when dealing with data with missing values (for example, the standard MATLAB PCA function, which uses the ALS algorithm to handle missing values, takes × 10–20 longer with [1000, 100] matrices than our implementation). Thus, we implemented an iterative algorithm based on imputation which is known to perform extremely well86,87. Our implementation resembles very closely to the SVDimpute algorithm presented by Troyanskaya and colleagues88 and perfected in Perry77. In our approach, randomly selected elements of X are removed and set apart as test set. These elements are replaced by 0 s (i.e., the mean of columns of X, since the matrix must be centered subtracting the mean), allowing us to use classic PCA algorithms without missing values. By replacing missing elements with other values (we also tried with random values, data not shown; the mean of each column excluding missing values77), very similar results are obtained. Then, the selected elements (but not all the other elements constituting the training set) are updated at the end of each iteration until convergence. The algorithm converged when the relative change in Δ between two consecutive iterations was below a tolerance threshold:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{\Delta}_{k-1}- {\Delta}_{k} }{{\Delta}_{k-1}}<tol$$\end{document}where tol is an arbitrary low threshold (tolerance). We considered the number of components as a hyperparameter to be tuned via cross-validation. Accordingly, we expected that the R2 of the test elements reconstruction would increase with the number of PCs peaking in correspondence with the optimal number of PCs. Beyond this point, adding more components leads to a decrease in the R2, meaning that additional PCs capture more noise than significant information. However, especially for row-only and column-only CVs this is not guaranteed since the not complete independence of train and predictions. Thus, in cases where it was not possible to identify a peak, the inflection (i.e., the elbow) in the performance curve was considered as the optimal point after which the increase in performance was only residual and depended on the training data leakage. This point was identified automatically as the point that maximised the fit of the two straight lines. More straightforward approaches, such as correcting the error by the number of degrees of freedom (as proposed elsewhere, e.g.53), did not resolve the issue completely. For most results (when not differently specified), we used a tenfold CV partition ((3,3) for bi-cross validation); for imputation, tol value was set equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-2}$$\end{document} and the maximum number of iterations to 50. Tests with a fivefold CV ((2,2) for bi-cross validation) scheme and tol equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-4}$$\end{document} or 75/100 iterations produced similar results (data not shown). Other CV partitions may also yield good results. In this regard, it should be noted that CV splits with smaller training sets tend to reduce the number of estimable components, which could pose a problem for very small datasets (see also the discussion by77).

(6)Optimality.

(6)Optimality.

Optimality.

Finally, to get reference values other than the dimensionality set during the simulations, we computed an ‘optimal reconstruction’ as the number k of components that maximized the variance of the noise-free U matrix containing the correlated signals explained by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{X}}^{k}$$\end{document}(i.e., the rank- k PCA reconstruction of X). In other words, here we want to find the number of components that are the most informative about the shared, underlying structure of the data. Note that this approach is feasible in our simulations (and not in real data) because the U matrix is known (it is basically what the application of PCA aims to estimate). The optimal reconstruction method is expected to match the number of dimensions used to generate the data on linear datasets. As non-linearities increase, it can be the case that significant shared covariance is spread in more dimensions than those nominally used to generate the data, thus we expected a mismatch between the dimensionality values (see Results). In the manuscript, we will refer to this method also as ‘optimality’.

Metrics for criteria comparison

We calculated three metrics to evaluate the performance of each criterion, considering three objectives that an optimal method should simultaneously aim to achieve: i) estimate the correct number of latent variables underlying the data, ii) provide a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{X}$$\end{document} denoised matrix reconstructed using only the first PCs according to the method selection, which retains as much information as possible related to the noise-free data matrix U, iii) estimate the correct amount of noise (i.e., uncorrelated variations across different units; see Discussion).

Specifically, we calculated: i) the dimension error, calculated as the difference between the estimated number of PCs and the number of latent variables N used to generate X; ii) how accurately the information about the latent variables can be retrieved from the reconstructed matrix, calculated as the variance of U (i.e., the matrix containing the noise-free data) that is explained by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{X}$$\end{document} (i.e., the approximation of X provided by the truncated PCA); iii) the estimated noise error, calculated as the difference between the proportion of variance of X unexplained by the selected PCs (noise estimated by the used criterion) and the proportion of variance in X unexplained by the generated latent variables l (ground-truth noise amount).

In order to quantify how each parameter influenced the performance metrics across simulated scenarios, we fitted a multiple linear regression model that included all main effects and pairwise interaction terms. The predictors comprised the number of fake units, time-series length, number of latent dimensions, noise variance, variance explained by non-linearities, variance decrease (τ), and matrix ratio, each z-scored to allow direct comparison of the fitted coefficients. Although a linear formulation cannot capture complex dependencies, it yields reliable first order estimates of each parameter’s impact. Visual inspection of residuals confirmed that the model captured the dominant trends in the data and accounted for approximately 70–80 percent of the variability (R2 ≈ 0.7–0.8) for predicting performance of most criteria, supporting its use for our sensitivity analysis.

Neural data pre-processing

To further validate the applicability of these methods to real world situations, we analyzed spiking data available online (20,43; see Data availability). During recording, macaques were anaesthetized and passively shown visual stimuli. In brief, animals were premedicated with atropine sulfate (0.05 mg/kg) and diazepam (1.5 mg/kg). Anesthesia was induced with ketamine (10 mg/kg). During recording, anesthesia was maintained by sufentanil citrate (typically 6–18 μg/kg/hr, adjusted as needed for each animal). Visual stimuli consisted of oriented gratings (1.28 s), with each presentation followed by a blank screen (1.5 s). Simultaneously, spikes were recorded from primary and secondary visual cortex, but here we only used activity for the primary visual cortex. For full details of the experimental procedures, drugs and equipment used, please refer to the original paper43. Following previous publications that analyzed on the same dataset, only 1 s of activity from 160 to 1160 ms after stimulus (or blank screen) presentation were considered20. Firing rates were calculated in 10 ms non-overlapping bins and condition averaged to obtain 1 matrix with baseline activity and 8 matrices with stimulus activity. All matrices were the same size [50 bins, num. of neurons]. Final matrices to be analyzed were obtained randomly subsampling an increasing number of units for each matrix. Final matrices were then soft-normalized (ε = mean activity over each couple of matched stimulus/blank matrices / 2, similarly to42)\varepsilonand mean-centered. Dimensionality was estimated with the different criteria on each final matrix. A bootstrapping procedure was performed to provide robustness to the analysis (100 random repetitions for each subpopulation size). Among the 5 recording sessions available in the online dataset, we here considered only 4 because they shared the exact same set of visual stimuli (approx. 100 recorded neurons each). The results reported are therefore average estimates across repetitions and sessions (also over stimuli in the case of stimulus-driven activities).
