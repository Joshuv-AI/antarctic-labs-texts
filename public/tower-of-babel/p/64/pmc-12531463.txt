Cage Amine Superbases: Synthesis and Studies of Nona(ethylene)Hexamine and Dodeka(ethylene)Octamine
Authors: Kel Vin Tan, Dat Quoc Duong, Keith F. White, Brian J. Smith, Peter J. Barnard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cage‐amine molecules nona(ethylene)hexamine and dodeka(ethylene)octamine, have been prepared by coupling 1,4,7‐triazacyclononane (tacn) or 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane (cyclen) to the corresponding α‐chloroacetamide derivatives of these macrocycles. The formed tri‐ or tetra‐amide cryptands were then reduced to the corresponding hexamine and octamine with borane. In all cases, the amide and amine cryptands were isolated as protonated ammonium salts, with the proton oriented to the inside of the cage molecules (i.e., endohedral). Deprotonation studies on the hexamine and octamine showed the encapsulated protons are highly inert, with no evidence of deprotonation or deuterium exchange. Detailed theoretical studies predict very high proton affinity and basicity of 1118.7 and 1086.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, for dodeka(ethylene)octamine, and 1108.3 and 1083.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, for nona(ethylene)hexamine, placing them among the strongest amine bases ever prepared and firmly within the realm of “superbases”.

Introduction

Proton transfer reactions are among the simplest yet most fundamental of chemical processes and are of great importance in both chemistry and biology.[ 1 ] Neutral nitrogen‐containing compounds such as 1,8‐diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec‐7‐ene (DBU) and 1,8‐bis(dimethylamino)naphthalene 1 (Figure 1) are commonly utilized as bases in chemical synthesis. Among these, 1 (also known as Proton Sponge) has received much attention, as it is nonnucleophilic and, for an aryl amine, highly basic with the proton constant and proton affinity being 12.34 and 1028.4 kJ mol−1, respectively.[ 2 ] For 1, the repulsive interaction between the nitrogen lone electron pairs is relieved by protonation and the formation of a strong NH+⋅⋅⋅N ionic hydrogen bond.[ 2 , 3 ] Significantly, the shielding caused by the methyl groups of 1 results in much slower rates of proton association and dissociation in comparison to ordinary amines.[ 4 ]

Selected examples of nitrogen‐containing bases: Proton Sponge 1, [1.1.1]cryptand 2, cage‐adamanzanes (3H⋅Br and 4H⋅Br) and dodeka(ethylene)octamine 5.

The protonated form of 1 resembles a traditional coordination complex, where a chelate ring is formed between 1 and the bound proton; this “proton complex” concept has been extended to a range of other systems, including protonated macrocyclic and macropolycyclic molecules.[ 1 , 5 ] In an early example of this phenomenon, Lehn reported the proton complexation properties of [1.1.1]cryptand 2 (Figure 1) and elucidated the potential for protonation on either the outer or inner face of the molecule, with the latter resulting in proton complexation and much stronger binding (proton constant = 17.8).[ 6 ]

Macrotricyclic adamanzanes are a highly symmetrical class of cage amine molecules composed of four nitrogen atoms and six alkyl linkers that display proton complexation properties.[ 7 ] Compounds 3 and 4 (Figure 1) show remarkably strong proton complexation properties and are known only as mono‐protonated cations ([3H]+ and [4H]+).[ 7c–e ] In both cases, the proton is encapsulated within the molecular cavities of these cage molecules and the four amine lone electron pairs are endocyclic and contribute to stabilizing the encapsulated proton via the formation of strong hydrogen bonds.[ 7 , 8 ] The proton affinity of 4H⋅Br is calculated to be 1067.3 kJ mol−1, which is significantly higher than that of 1 [ 9 ] and the inert nature of the encapsulated proton of [4H]+ has allowed it to be utilized in the synthesis of a crystalline “inverse sodium hydride” in which sodium acts as an anion (sodide) to balance the cationic charge of the protonated adamanzane.[ 10 , 11 ]

More recently, theoretical studies have predicted that the highly symmetrical cage molecule dodeka(ethylene)octamine 5 (Figure 1) will exhibit a remarkably high proton affinity of 1102.5 kJ mol−1, suggesting that this compound should possess even higher basicity than 4H⋅Br due to the presence of eight endohedral nitrogen lone electron pairs,[ 9 ] making 5 a highly attractive synthetic target.

Herein, the synthesis of the cage‐amine molecules, nona(ethylene)hexamine and dodeka(ethylene)octamine 5 from the macrocyclic precursors 1,4,7‐triazacyclononane and 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane, respectively, is described. Both the hexamine and octamine cryptands were isolated as mono‐protonated cations, with the ammonium proton encapsulated within the molecular cavity. The encapsulated proton of these compounds is highly inert, and the protonated compounds were not deprotonated by the bases NaOH, DBU, or NaH. Detailed theoretical studies on the geometries, proton affinities, and pK a values for these molecules have been undertaken.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Cryptates

The α‐chloroamide synthetic intermediates 6 and 7 (Scheme 1) were prepared by treating either tacn or cyclen with the appropriate molar ratios of 2‐chloroacetyl chloride in the presence of the bases KOH or Cs2CO3, respectively. The mono‐protonated amideamine cage molecules 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl were prepared by the reaction of 6 or 7 with tacn or cyclen, respectively, in equimolar mixtures with the base K2CO3 and CsCl added as a templating agent.[ 12 ] (Scheme 1). The addition of CsCl to the reaction mixtures significantly improved the yields of compounds 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl, for example, in the case of 9H .Cl, if CsCl was omitted, the product was obtained with a 38% lower yield than when it was present. The addition of CsCl had a clear effect on the solubility of the α‐chloroamide compounds 6 and 7 in acetonitrile, which were poorly soluble in the absence of CsCl. In these synthetic reactions, both 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl were obtained as mono‐protonated ammonium salts, and the free amine forms of these cryptands were not isolated. The structures of 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl were confirmed by mass spectrometry, 1H NMR spectroscopy and X‐ray crystallography (in the case of 9H, X‐ray data is of the bromide salt, 9H .Br, is presented) (see the Structural Studies section). These data clearly show the cryptands are mono‐protonated in both cases, and the X‐ray crystal structures show the proton is encapsulated within the cavity of the cryptand molecules. The positive‐ion ESMS spectra of 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl (Figure S3, Supporting Information) displayed one major ion in each case, located at m/z 379.3 for 8H .Cl and 505.3 for 9H .Cl, and the isotope distribution patterns are consistent with the formulations [C18H31N6O3]+ for 8H .Cl and [C24H41N8O4]+ for 9H .Cl.

Synthesis of a) 8H .Cl and b) 9H .Cl from the α‐chloroamide precursors 6 and 7 and macrocycles tacn or cyclen, respectively.

The tertiary cyclic amide (lactam) groups of cryptands 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl give rise to conformational isomers.[ 13 ] Schematic diagrams of the two possible isomeric forms of the triamidetriamine 8H .Cl (α and β) and the four possible isomeric forms of the tetraamidetetraamine 9H .Cl (α, β, γ, and δ) cryptands are shown in Figure 2. For the cryptands 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl, the number of resonances observed in 13C NMR spectra allows for a determination of the conformational isomeric forms of these compounds present in solution. A total of twenty‐four signals were observed in the 13C NMR spectrum of 8H .Cl (Figure S4, Supporting Information), with four characteristic downfield shifted resonances for the carbonyl groups, consistent with a mixture of the α‐8H .Cl and β‐8H .Cl isomers in solution. Additionally, the 1H NMR spectrum of 8H .Cl shows two distinct downfield shifted ammonium N ─ H signals (9.59 ppm and 9.93 ppm), and integration provides a 1:3 ratio of these isomeric forms. 1H‐15N HSQC analysis was used to confirm that these resonances correspond to N ─ H groups with the 15N chemical shifts being 40.5 and 30.3, respectively. The presence of the asymmetric isomer β‐8H .Cl is also consistent with the X‐ray crystal structure for this compound (see structural studies section).[ 14 ]

Schematic representations (top view) of the possible conformational isomers of amideamine cryptands a) 8H .Cl and b) 9H .Cl. The second unit of a) tacn or b) cyclen and the ammonium proton are omitted for clarity.

For 9H .Cl, the 1H and 13C NMR spectra (Figure S5a, Supporting Information) showed only one ammonium N ─ H proton signal (6.61 ppm) and four carbonyl carbon 13C signals indicating that only the asymmetric isomer (δ‐9H .Cl) is present in solution. A combination of 1H─13C and 1H─15N HSQC experiments (Figure S5b, c, Supporting Information) was used to unambiguously identify the N ─ H proton resonance for δ‐9H .Cl, with the 15N chemical shift for the ammonium nitrogen atom being 41.8 ppm. X‐ray crystallographic analysis of 9H .Br (see Structural Studies section) shows that the δ isomer is also observed in the solid state.

The ammonium salts of the cage‐amines, nona(ethylene)hexamine 10H .Cl and dodeka(ethylene)octamine 5H .Cl, were prepared by reduction of the amideamine cage molecules 8H .Cl and 9H .Cl with BH3 in THF. After the reaction for the preparation of 8H .Cl, excess borane was decomposed with methanol and the hexamine was subsequently purified using HPLC (Scheme 2).

Synthesis of 10H .Cl via reduction of 8H .Cl with BH3‐THF at RT.

In contrast to the smooth reduction of 8H .Cl, reduction of 9H .Cl with BH3‐THF afforded a mono‐substituted amine‐borane adduct of the protonated octamine cage 5H‐BH3 .Cl (Scheme 3), and while this compound could not be isolated in its pure form, its identity was unambiguously confirmed by the X‐ray crystallographic analysis (see structural studies section, vide infra). The formation of an amine‐borane adduct is unsurprising, as an excess of BH3 was used in the synthesis (9H .Cl: BH3 = 1:18) and these species are well known.[ 15 ] Cleavage of the borane group from 5H‐BH3 .Cl was achieved by treating the crude mixture containing 5H‐BH3 .Cl with 1.2 equivalents of HCl in methanol and the octamine was isolated in its di‐protonated form 5H2 .Cl2. The purified form of 5H2 .Cl2 was then mono‐deprotonated by treatment with DBU in acetonitrile yielding the mono‐protonated octamine 5H .Cl (Scheme 3).

Synthesis of the mono‐protonated octamine 5H .Cl via reduction of 9H .Cl with BH3‐THF.

The positive‐ion ESI‐MS spectra for 10H .Cl and 5H .Cl (Figure S6, Supporting Information) display one major ion in each case, located at m/z 377.3 and 449.4, respectively, with the isotope distribution patterns consistent with the expected formulations of [C18H37N6]+ for 10H⋅Cl and [C24H49N8]+ for 5H .Cl. The high‐resolution ESI‐MS spectra for 10H .Cl and 5H .Cl are shown in Figures S7, S8 (Supporting Information).

Consistent with a molecule exhibiting time‐averaged D 3h symmetry, the 1H and 13C NMR spectra (Figure 3, d 6 − DMSO, 298 K) for 10H .Cl shows three and two resonances, respectively. The ammonium N ─ H proton resonates as a singlet at 8.74 ppm, while the methylene units of the tacn fragments and ethylene “straps” (linking the tacn units) of the cage (24 and 12 protons, respectively) are in each case magnetically equivalent and resonate as two sharp singlet signals in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra (2.60 ppm and 2.75 ppm, 48.9 ppm, and 52.1 ppm, respectively). The high level of symmetry indicated by NMR is consistent with the N ─ H proton rapidly exchanging between all six of the endocyclic amine lone pairs. Similar results were previously reported for a series of mono‐protonated cage‐adamanzanes that displayed relatively simple 1H and 13C NMR spectra and downfield shifted signals for the encapsulated ammonium N ─ H protons.[ 7a‐c,7e ] To confirm that the chemical exchange process is fast on the NMR timescale at RT, a variable temperature (VT) 1H NMR study was undertaken on 10H .Cl (300 K−210 K, d 4‐MeOD) (Figure S9, Supporting Information). This study shows that the two singlet signals associated with the methylene groups broaden with decreasing temperature, with coalescence occurring at ∼240 K. With further cooling, a series of broad signals in the upfield region became apparent at 210 K, indicating a range of chemical environments for the methylene protons and establishment of the slow exchange on the NMR timescale. A slight upfield shift was observed for the ammonium N─H signal with decreasing temperature.

1H and 13C NMR spectra for 10H .Cl in d 6–DMSO.

The 1H NMR spectrum for a freshly prepared sample of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 (298 K, d 6 − DMSO, Figure S10, Supporting Information) shows broad resonances in the region 1.5–4.5 ppm for the ethane linker group protons in addition to a series of downfield shifted signals corresponding to the ammonium protons. The complexity of this spectrum results from intermediate exchange between conformational isomeric forms of [5H2 ]2+, arising due to the staggered configurations of the ethylene groups in addition to isomers associated with the relative positions of the two ammonium N ─ H protons (protomers). Interestingly, the 1H NMR spectrum for 5H2 ⋅Cl2 changes upon storage and after a period of ∼6 months, the spectrum shows only a single signal at 5.72 ppm for the two ammonium N ─ H protons, in addition to the methylene group proton signals (Figure S11). This result indicates that the freshly prepared sample of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 is a kinetic product, while on storage, the thermodynamic product is slowly formed. To further investigate this observation, a solution of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 in d 6 − DMSO was heated at 383 K for a period of 24 hours. The 1H NMR spectrum recorded from this solution (Figure S12) is similar to that observed for the aged sample with a single N─H observed at 5.72 ppm, supporting the notion that the freshly prepared sample of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 is a kinetic product. It was also evident that heating at 383 K also resulted in decomposition of 5H2 ⋅Cl2, with the d 6 − DMSO solution turning from colorless to orange, and unidentified decomposition products were evident in the 1H NMR spectrum.

There are nine possible protomers for 5H2 ⋅Cl2 (see Figure S13, Supporting Information); the protons can either be oriented into the cage cavity (endo or in) or to the outside of the cage (exo or out). Although the exact nature of the protomers present for 5H2 ⋅Cl2 has not been unambiguously characterized, it is reasonable to conclude that the freshly prepared sample of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 is composed of a mixture of isomers, while the aged sample of this compound is the single 1,4‐(in/in) protomer. This conclusion is supported by theoretical results (See Theoretical Studies section) which show that the 1,4‐(in/in) protomer is the lowest energy configuration (thermodynamic product) for this molecule. Similarly, the X‐ray crystal structure for 5H2 ⋅Br2 shows only the 1,4‐(in/in) protomer (see Structural Studies section). The presence of multiple isomeric forms for protonated cryptands and cage amines has been well documented,[ 5 , 6 ] with both the in/out and in/in protomeric forms of cryptand 2 having been previously reported.[ 6a ]

The room temperature 1H NMR spectrum of the monoprotonated octamine 5H⋅Cl (Figure 4) is considerably simplified in comparison to 5H2 ⋅Cl2. All 24 methylene units of the cage molecule give rise to a single broad 13C signal at 49.8 ppm, while the methylene group protons resonate as two broad singlet signals (1.82 ppm and 3.12 ppm), consistent with a time‐averaged O symmetric molecule and slow exchange between axial and equatorial proton environments associated with the staggered configurations of the ethylene linker groups (Figure S14, Supporting Information). These experimental chemical shifts measured for 5H⋅Cl beautifully coincide with those predicted by Bühl for the unprotonated O symmetric form of 5 (predicted 1H NMR = 1.8 and 3.1 ppm; 13C = 54.5 ppm).[ 9 ] To confirm that the chemical exchange process is slow on the NMR timescale at RT, a VT 1H NMR study (300 K−388 K, d 6 − DMSO) was conducted on 5H .Cl (Figure S15, Supporting Information). This study shows that the two methylene singlet signals broaden with increasing temperature, with coalescence occurring at approximately 343 K. With further heating, one broad singlet signal (2.51 ppm) becomes apparent at 383 K that corresponds to the 48 methylene protons, indicating a fast exchange regime has been achieved.

1H and 13C NMR spectra for 5H .Cl in d 6 − DMSO at 300 K.

The time‐averaged O symmetry indicated for 5H .Cl by the 1H and 13C NMR spectra, together with the single N − H resonance at 6.74 ppm, is consistent with the ammonium proton being encapsulated and rapid exchange between the eight nitrogen atoms. In contrast, if only a single nitrogen atom was protonated with no movement of the ammonium proton, the symmetry of the system would be lowered to C3 .[ 9 ] The free‐energy barrier for the relocation of the ammonium proton for [5H]+ was previously estimated using computational studies to be ΔG ‡ = 61.5 kJ mol−1 (and in the present study to be 59.4 kJ mol−1 vide infra) and it was previously noted that based on the magnitude of this barrier, [5H]+ may display apparent O symmetry on the NMR time scale at RT.[ 9 ] The NMR experimental data obtained here are consistent with this prediction.

Structural Studies

Single crystals suitable for X‐ray crystallography were obtained for compounds 6, 7, 8H .Cl, 9H .Br, 5H‐BH3 .Cl, 5H2 .Br2, and 5H .Cl. To obtain crystals of [9H] + and [5H 2]2+, Bu4NBr was added to the crystallization solutions of these compounds and crystals suitable for XRD analysis were obtained with bromide counter ions. The structures of the α‐chloroacetamide synthetic intermediates 6 and 7 (Figure S16, Supporting Information) are as expected with each of the macrocyclic ring nitrogen atoms (either tacn or cyclen, respectively) substituted with α‐chloroacetyl groups. Cryptand 8H .Cl (Figure 5a) crystallizes with a chloride counter ion, confirming that the cage is protonated. The ammonium proton (H5) is bound to nitrogen N5 and is located within the cavity of the cryptand. One of the three amide carbonyl oxygen atoms (O1) is oriented in the opposite direction relative to the other two carbonyl groups giving the asymmetric isomer β‐8H (see Figure 2 for conformer structure). The tetraamidetetraamine cryptand 9H .Br (Figure 5b) crystallizes with a bromide counter ion, with nitrogen N6 being protonated within the cage cavity—the amide carbonyl oxygen atom O4 of 9H .Br is oriented in the opposite direction to the other three carbonyl groups, giving an asymmetric isomer (9δ, see Figure 2 for conformer structure).

The crystal data for the octamines 5H‐BH3 ⋅Br, 5H2 ⋅Br2, and 5H⋅Cl reveal some positional disorder relating to the cage ethylene groups; the following discussion describes the major cage conformations for each crystal (for additional information, see Supporting Information). The amine bound borane group of the octamine cryptand 5H‐BH3 ⋅Br is bonded to N1 and is oriented outside of the cage cavity (Figure 6a). The N1─B1 bond distance is 1.619(3) Å.

The X‐ray crystal structure for 5H2 ⋅Br2 reveals the presence of two distinct conformations of the di‐protonated octamine in the asymmetric unit. Both conformers are present in equal amounts and in each case, the 12 ethylene fragments are staggered, and the endohedral N ─ H protons are located on diametrically opposed nitrogen atoms. One conformer (Figure 6b) can be likened to the dodeka(ethylene)octamine described by Bühl,[ 9 ] and displays (close to) perfect O point group symmetry (if the N ─ H protons are not considered); when they are considered, the point group is D 3 and this conformer is chiral. The second conformer of [5H2 ]+ (Figure 6c) has an inversion center in the middle of the cage cavity and is achiral (point group C i). The crystal structure of the monoprotonated octamine, 5H⋅Cl (Figure 6d), shows the N ─ H proton, located on N7, projects into the cavity. The cage is similar to the Bühl dodeka(ethylene)octamine (chiral), point group C 3.

Deprotonation Studies

In all cases, the cryptands prepared here were isolated with an ammonium N ─ H proton encapsulated within the molecular cavity (in the case of 5H2 ⋅Cl2 there are two encapsulated N ─ H protons). It is well documented that protonation of amine groups on the inner face of cage molecules can render the ammonium proton highly inert to deprotonation and deuterium exchange,[ 6 , 7 , 11 , 17 ] and as such, the inertness of the encapsulated proton for these compounds was of significant interest. 1H NMR spectra collected for 10H⋅Cl in 3 M NaOD in D2O over 18 days (Figure S17, Supporting Information) show the encapsulated proton is inert to deprotonation and does not undergo hydrogen‐deuterium exchange under these conditions. In contrast to 10H⋅Cl, when the octamine 5H2 ⋅Cl2 was dissolved in a mixture of 3 M NaOD in D2O, one of the ammonium protons was lost to give the monoprotonated compound 5H⋅Cl, which was not further deprotonated with substantial decomposition of the amine cage occurring over 10 days (Figure S18, Supporting Information). This result is similar to that reported previously for the deprotonation of the diprotonated form of 2, which could be deprotonated once in 5 M KOH at elevated temperatures; however, this reaction was accompanied by ligand decomposition.[ 6a ]

As acid dissociation constants are solvent dependent,[ 18 ] similar 1H NMR studies were carried out on 10H⋅Cl and 5H2 ⋅Cl2 using the organic base DBU in CD3CN and d 6 − DMSO. Treatment of the hexamine 10H⋅Cl with DBU (1.2 equivalents) in either CD3CN or d 6 − DMSO resulted in no change in the 1H NMR spectra, which remained unchanged even after 10 equivalents of DBU were added (Figures S19, S20, Supporting Information). In the case of 5H2 ⋅Cl2, the addition of a small excess of DBU (2.5 equivalents) in either CD3CN or d 6 − DMSO resulted in the rapid removal of one ammonium N ─ H proton to give the mono‐protonated octamine 5H⋅Cl. Further addition of DBU (up to 10 equivalents) did not result in the removal of the second ammonium N ─ H proton, and the spectra remained unchanged over 10 days (Figures S21, S22, Supporting Information). Treatment of 10H⋅Cl or 5H⋅Cl with the highly basic aryl amine proton sponge (1, Figure 1) (1.2 and 2.2 equivalents in d 6 − DMSO, respectively) did not result in deprotonation of these molecules (Figures S23, S24, Supporting Information). These studies demonstrate the inertness of the encapsulated proton of the mono‐protonated forms of hexamine and octamine (10H⋅Cl and 5H⋅Cl) and show that these cage molecules behave as “proton cryptates”.[ 6 , 19 ]

The strong base NaH has been successfully used previously to deprotonate a bicyclic tetraamine ligand to facilitate the synthesis of a Co(III)‐adamanzane complex,[ 20 ] and on this basis attempts were made to deprotonate the cage molecules 10H⋅Cl and 5H⋅Cl with NaH in THF. However, 1H NMR analysis showed no evidence of deprotonation. It was hypothesized that the lack of reactivity may be due to the poor solubility of NaH in THF; to overcome this issue CH3Li was employed as a more soluble and nucleophilic base. Treatment of either 10H⋅Cl or 5H2 ⋅Cl2 with CH3Li in THF resulted in rapid deprotonation of these molecules and after careful workup under anhydrous conditions in a nitrogen glovebox, mass spectrometric analysis of the products (11 and 12, respectively, Scheme 4) was consistent with the generation of the Li+ complexes of the ring‐opened cage ligands. (Figure S25, Supporting Information). 1H NMR analysis of 11 and 12 (CD3CN) produced relatively complicated spectra, with multiple overlapping signals observed in the region of 1–3.5 ppm in addition to a sharp doublet of doublets centerd between 6 and 6.5 ppm and a pair of doublets centerd between 3.8 and 4.1 ppm, respectively, with these sharp signals consistent with a vinyl group (Figure S26, Supporting Information).

(Panel A) Reaction of 10H⋅Cl with CH3Li in THF, yielding the vinyl substituted compound 11 and subsequently the tricyclic compound 13 and (Panel B) the analogous compounds 12 and 14 formed from 5H2 ⋅Cl2.

In an attempt to further purify compounds 11 and 12, the products were removed from the nitrogen box and subjected to chromatography on alumina (DCM and methanol) and a single compound was eluted and collected in each case. 1H NMR analysis of the chromatographed products (Figure S27, Supporting Information) shows that the compounds had reacted further and the sharp signals for the vinyl substituent were no longer present. Mass spectrometric analysis of the products (13 and 14) (Figure S28, Supporting Information) showed peaks at 317.3001 for 13 and 429.4030 for 14, corresponding to the molecular formulas [C16H34LiN6]+ (calc. 317.3000) and [C22H46LiN8]+ (calc. 429.4000), respectively. These formulas are consistent with the loss of a C2H2 (ethylene) fragment from the lithiated cage molecules.

The NMR and mass spectrometric results suggest that CH3Li does not selectively remove the encapsulated ammonium protons from 10H⋅Cl or 5H⋅Cl, but rather the elimination‐type process shown for 10H⋅Cl in Scheme 4 better accounts for the experimental data. Here, CH3Li deprotonates the methylene group located at the position β to the protonated ammonium nitrogen, resulting in ring opening and generation of the lithiated tricyclic hexamine bearing a vinyl substituent 11. Additionally, it appears that 11 is unstable and decomposes further during chromatographic purification via a second elimination‐type process, yielding compound 13 and presumably the generation of acetylene as a gaseous byproduct. The structures of the analogous compounds formed from 5H2 ⋅Cl2 are shown in Scheme 4 (Panel B).

Theoretical Studies

To gain additional insights into the structures and basicities of the cage amine molecules prepared here, quantum mechanics calculations were performed using a composite method leading to energies approximating the CCSD(T)/CBS level (Supporting Information). Geometries were determined at the B3LYP/6–31G(d) level and proton dissociation (pK a) constants were estimated from calculated gas‐phase basicities and solvation energies determined using the SMD method.[ 9 ]

Geometry calculations for the hexamine 10, show the lowest energy structure has D3 symmetry. Two possible structures were evaluated for the protonated form of [10H]+, with the first having a proton centralized in the molecule, while for the second just a single nitrogen is protonated. The distance between the six nitrogen atoms and the cage center of 10 is relatively large (2.224 Å), and consistent with this the structure of [10H]+ with the proton centralized in the molecule (D3 symmetry) lies 202.2 kJ mol−1 higher in energy than the global minimum. The global minimum for [10H]+ has a proton bound to a single nitrogen oriented to the inside of the cage (leading to an asymmetric molecule), with the N‐H distance being 1.053 Å. Two distinct transition structures were identified for the movement of the ammonium proton from one nitrogen to an adjacent nitrogen in [10H]+ and these transition states lie 37.7 and 50.8 kJ mol−1 higher in energy than the global minimum, respectively. The magnitude of these transition state energies is consistent with a relatively low barrier to movement of the encapsulated ammonium proton and is in agreement with the time averaged D3h symmetry observed by NMR spectroscopy.

In agreement with previous theoretical results[ 9 ] the lowest energy structure of 5 was found to have O symmetry and protonation at a single nitrogen leads to [5H]+ with C3 symmetry. The lowest energy protomer of [5H2 ]2+ has protons opposite one another (i.e., separated by three ethylene linkers) and oriented into the cage (Structure in Figure S9 Supporting Information, denoted 1,4‐(in/in)) and is consistent with the X‐ray crystal structure for this compound. The protomers separated by one or two linkers lie 73.2 and 26.2 kJ mol−1 higher in energy, respectively (Figure S9, denoted 1,2‐(in/in) and 1,3‐(in/in), respectively, Supporting Information). It is also theoretically possible to have in/out and out/out protomers for [5H2 ]2+, however, these all lie at considerably higher energies (1,4‐(in/out) = 81.3, 1,3‐(in/out) = 107.3, 1,2‐(in/out) = 128.8, 1,4‐(out/out) = 166.2, 1,3‐(out/out) = 189.1, and 1,2‐(out/out) = 210.9 kJ mol−1) than the 1,4‐(in/in) isomer.

For the monoprotonated cage [5H]+, the structure with the proton centralized in the molecule lies 336.7 kJ mol−1 higher in energy than the lowest energy conformation. The global minimum for [5H]+ has a proton bound to a single nitrogen oriented to the inside of the cage, with the N‐H distance being 1.044 Å. The transition structure for proton exchange from one nitrogen to an adjacent nitrogen lies 59.4 kJ mol−1 higher in energy than the global minimum; this value is similar to that reported previously for 5 (61.5 kJ mol−1).[ 9 ]

For comparison, tetramine 3 has T d symmetry, while the protonated form [3H]+ has C3 symmetry with the proton bound to a single nitrogen atom. The transition structure for proton exchange is low and lies 12.0 kJ mol−1 higher in energy than the global minimum. This low barrier is consistent with solution‐state NMR studies on 3H⋅Br that indicated T d symmetry due to signal averaging on the NMR time scale.[ 7e ]

In contrast to the protonated structures of [5H]+ and [10H]+, the lithiated structures [5Li] + and [10Li] + have the lithium ion located at the center of the cage. The geometry obtained for [5Li] + is consistent with a previous theoretical study investigating this compound.[ 21 ]

Proton Affinities, Basicities, and pK a Values

Theoretical predictions of proton affinities (gas‐phase), basicities and pK a values were estimated for 10, 5, [5H]+, and 3 together with a range of nitrogen bases to allow benchmarking of the results for these amine cages (Table 1). For the compounds evaluated, the theoretical results reproduce experimental observations accurately, e.g., for the amines proton affinities are within 2 kJ mol−1, while for basicities, the differences are less than 5 kJ mol−1 (Table 1). Including imines, the differences are typically less than 5 kJ mol−1 for both proton affinities and basicities; theoretical predictions of pK a in water are within 3 pK a units of experiment.

The theoretical gas‐phase proton affinities and basicities for 10 (1108.3 and 1083.7 kJ mol−1, respectively), 5 (1077.3 and 1050.2 kJ mol−1, respectively), and 3 (1118.7 and 1083.7 kJ mol−1, respectively), are all significantly larger than the other amines and imines studied here; most importantly, they are all larger than those for Barton's base, which is considered one of the most basic nitrogen bases.[ 26 ] By contrast, the predicted proton affinity and basicity of [5H]+ (Table 1) are both less than all of the other bases studied here. This prediction accounts well for the experimental observation that [5H2 ]2+ was rapidly deprotonated once using DBU to give [5H]+, however, once formed, the monoprotonated cage (with significantly higher predicted proton affinity and basicity than DBU) was not deprotonated a second time to give 5.

Whereas these theoretical studies show that proton affinity and basicity increase with the size of the cryptand, with the largest values being obtained for the octamine, the pK a is predicted to decrease with size; 3 is predicted to have the largest pK a, 24.4, while the octamine 5 is predicted to have the lowest value of 20.4.[ 3 , 5 , 7 , 27 ]

Conclusion

The protonated cage‐amines nona(ethylene)hexamine 10H⋅Cl and dodeka(ethylene)octamine 5H⋅Cl have been successfully prepared for the first time. For both 10H .Cl and 5H .Cl the encapsulated N ─ H proton is highly inert and neither deprotonation nor deuterium exchange occurred in the presence of NaOD, DBU, or NaH. The strong base CH3Li did not result in removal of the encapsulated ammonium proton but rather C─H deprotonation of a methylene group in a Hoffman‐type elimination process and ring opening of the cage molecules. Theoretical studies predict very high proton affinities and basicities of 1118.7 and 1086.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, for 5 and 1108.3 and 1083.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, for 10 placing them among the strongest amine bases ever prepared.[ 3 , 5 ] However, as has been previously demonstrated for related compounds, e.g., the protonated forms of 3 and 4 (Figure 1), [ 7 , 27 ] the very high proton affinities of 10 and 5 are unlikely to account for the inertness of these molecules alone. The inability to deprotonate these compounds can also be ascribed to the complete steric inaccessibility of the encapsulated proton to external reactants. In this way, these compounds can accurately be described as “proton traps” (a term coined to describe the inertness of protonated adamanzanes)[ 1c ] and their lack of reactivity can be significantly ascribed as a kinetic phenomenon resulting from the complete encapsulation of the ammonium proton.[ 10 , 28 , 29 ]
