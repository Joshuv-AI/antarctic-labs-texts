Na+ induced improved fluorescence of carbon nanoparticles from human hair: an exclusive sensing platform for Ag+
Authors: Priyanka Sharma, Mamta Sahu, Mainak Ganguly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluorescent carbon particles, originating from biological waste, are a recent trend in research. We synthesized novel emissive carbon nanoparticles (15 nm) from human hair via a modified hydrothermal method. The emissive behaviour was further intensified with the interaction of Na+ with the heteroatom-doped carbon nanoparticles. Thus, turn-on fluorescence was made possible for Na+ (linear in the range from 10− 2 M to 10− 5 M and limit of quantification (LOQ) was 0.0018 M). The Na+ modified the electrostatic behaviour and exhibited natural crystallization confinement effect, resulting in fluorescence enhancement. Ag+ quenched the fluorescence selectively with the formation of Ag2O in the reaction mixture, and an Ag+ sensor was designed (linear detection range to be 5 × 10− 7 M to 10− 3, while LOQ was 0.0003 M). Non-radiative energy transfer from fluorophore to Ag2O was pivotal for Ag+ sensing. A non-expensive, green, and circular economic approach was reported here for environmental monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44901-x.

Introduction

Hair, an alpha keratinous biomaterial, has several uses including cosmetics, drug coating, wound healing, antibacterial activity, blood clotting, trauma, tissue and neuron regeneration, and effective treatment for deadly liver damage1,2. Tannery by-products are valued at 5% of recovered dry hair. However, hair burning remains a widespread practice in many tanneries, leading to high levels of Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD), and Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) in the discharge and posing a risk to the environment3–5. Human hair was initially employed as a support for gold and silver nanoparticles, which were then used as a nanocatalyst in cyclo addition and aza-Michael processes. Quantum dots were created by a one-step pyrolysis technique that used hair as a precursor6.

Keratin is a structural and protective protein present in mammalian tissues (hair, wool, nails, fur, skin, hooves, and horns) and avian tissues (beaks and feathers), where it plays an important function in defending the body against microbial damage and infections7,8. Keratin is a cysteine-rich fibrous protein with two structural types: α-keratins (α-helix) and β-keratins (β-pleated sheets). Soft tissues like wool and hair mostly contain α-keratins, while avian claws and beaks have β-keratins9. Keratin is frequently employed in the manufacturing of materials for a variety of applications, including scaffolding matrices and bandages in tissue engineering and controlled medication release systems10. This is mostly owing to its outstanding biocompatibility, biodegradability, mechanical durability, biological activity (for example, wound healing and epithelial cell growth regulation), and natural abundance11,12.

Even at low concentrations, heavy metal ions have become a major source of environmental contamination. These ions have the potential to do severe harm to aquatic ecosystems, the safety of drinking water and food, and people’s overall health. Ag+, a common heavy metal pollutant, has the potential to produce argyria, which can manifest as headaches, skin irritation, stomach distress, organ edema, and even death13. When individuals are exposed to Ag, they may excrete silver in their blood and urine. The free Ag⁺ ion exhibits great bioavailability and is primarily accountable for the acute toxicity of silver species, including silver nanoparticles, to aquatic organisms; in contrast, bound or complexed silver species are far less hazardous14,15. Ag⁺ exhibits a remarkable affinity for biological molecules, particularly thiols, proteins, and nucleic acids; thus, even minimal amounts can impair cellular metabolism, enzymatic activity, membrane integrity, and ion regulation in organisms. Several analytical techniques (fluorometrically16, colorimetrically17, electrochemically18 have been developed to quantitatively detect Ag+19.These approaches involve expensive instruments and extensive sample preparation processes, their practical uses are severely limited. Fluorescence detection is a relatively recent technology that has the advantages of high sensitivity, ease of use, and good linearity20. As a result, many fluorescence-sensing technologies have been created for the sensitive and selective detection of Ag+21,22.

In our work, we synthesized fluorescent carbon nanoparticles, which were further brightened by sodium ions and quenched exclusively with Ag+ in association with the formation of Ag2O nanoparticles.

Experimental section

Material and instruments

Without further purification, all the chemicals employed in the studies were of analytical grade. Triple-distilled water was employed during the entire project. All glassware was cleaned using freshly made aqua regia, followed by soapy water and lots of distilled water. Before usage, the glassware was thoroughly dried. All of the metal salts, including sodium chloride and silver nitrate, were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. NaOH was purchased from Fisher Scientific and Qualigens, respectively. We collected Human hair from local barber shops in Jaipur (Rajasthan, India) with permission.

Using a digital spectrophotometer called the Shimadzu UV-2600, all UV/Vis absorption spectra were captured. A Horiba FluoroMax-4 spectrometer was used to analyse the fluorescence at room temperature. The JEOL Make JSM-7610FPlus FESEM, a high resolution (1 KV 1.0 nm, 15 Kv 0.8 nm) with wide range of probe scanning electron microscope, was used to analyse particle morphology. Prior to performing the FESEM experiment, samples were vacuum dried for 24 h. For microscopic examination, the water suspension was dried on carbon tape. With the help of Systronics Digital pH meter 335, pH solutions were created. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis using X-ray Diffractometer (model: SMARTLAB). Anton Paar Litesizer 500 (DLS), was used to analyze particle size. The Tecnai G2 20 S-TWIN [FEI] is a 200 Kv (HRTEM) was used for TEM micrographs.

Synthesis of HRCNPs

A NaOH solution was prepared by dissolving 3 g of NaOH in 50 mL of water (pH 14.0). In that 50 mL of solution, 0.015 g of hair was added. The mixture was allowed to stir (2000 rpm) for 1 h, keeping 3 cm below a 200 W glowing bulb. After 1 h, the hair was dissolved to produce a brown solution. We put the 5 mL of the brown solution in a 10 mL screw-capped test tube and allowed it to age for 3 h, 3 cm below a 200 W glowing bulb [modified hydrothermal (MHT)], and a decent fluorescent HRCNPs hydrosol was obtained for further experiments (Fig. 1).

Synthesis of NaHRCNPs and AgNaHRCNPs

HRCNPs hydrosol of 3 mL was mixed with 1 mL sodium chloride solution and aged for 8 h to obtain a highly fluorescent NaHRCNPs hydrosol, where the sodium chloride concentration was 0.01 M.

HRCNPs hydrosol of 3 mL was mixed with 1 mL of sodium chloride and 1 mL of silver nitrate solution thoroughly. The mixture was aged for 8 h to obtain a non-fluorescent AgNaHRCNPs hydrosol, where sodium chloride and silver nitrate concentrations were 0.01 M (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Evolution of HRCNPs via MHT

A widely available waste material, human hair (HR), was found to be a precursor of fluorescent carbon nanoparticles via a modified hydrothermal pathway (MHT). HR is insoluble in water. We dissolved it in NaOH solution with heating. The dissolved HR had weak fluorescence, while HRCNPs (produced after 3 h of MHT as mentioned in the experimental section) possessed fair fluorescence (λex 310 nm, λem 410 nm). Two absorption peaks with λmax 220 nm and 274 nm were observed in the UV-Visible spectrum (Fig. 2). Ge et al.23 demonstrated the fabrication and characterisation of keratin hydrogel derived from waste rabbit hair, utilising L-cysteine as a reducing agent through a heating-and-cooling technique at varying pH levels and heating temperatures.

Usually, CDs are ≤10 nm in size with emissive properties. However, Tripathi et al.24. reported water-soluble fluorescent CDs derived from a biosource for cellular imaging, in which carbon nanodots were 20–30 nm in size. Generally, CDs > 10 nm are called carbon nanoparticles25. That is why we termed carbon particles (synthesised from HR) as HRCNPs (mentioned above).

During the synthesis process, various functional groups such as hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), and amino (-NH2) groups are introduced onto the surface of the CDs, creating localized electronic states that facilitate electron transitions, leading to fluorescence emission. Additionally, the size and structure of the carbon core influence the electronic bandgap, with smaller sizes leading to quantum confinement effects that enhance fluorescence26,27. The unique composition of human hair contributes to the formation of these functional groups during the pyrolysis process, resulting in CDs with tuneable fluorescence properties28,29.

Na+ induced brightening of HRCNPs

The fluorescence of HRCNPs was brightened selectively with Na+ (NaHRCNPs). 8.3 times increase in the fluorescence (λex 310 nm). We also investigated the emissive behaviour by employing Fe3+, Zn2+, Al3+, Cd2+, Cu2+, Cr3+, Ni2+, K+, Mg2+, Pb2+, Ba2+ in lieu of Na+ and exclusive enhancement was observed for Na+ (Fig. 4). We varied the Na+ concentration and found that maximum fluorescence intensity was obtained when [Na+] was 10− 2 M. A plot of I/I0 vs. [Na+] was linear in the range from 10− 2 M to 10− 5 M, and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was found to be 0.0018 M (Fig. 5). The hydrodynamic diameter of HRCNPs was found to be 1504.1 nm (Fig. 4). Disintegration of the particles indicated the reduction of aggregation, leading to significant fluorescence enhancement due to a higher surface-to-volume ratio.

We dissolved HR in aqueous NaOH solution as HR was not soluble in distilled water. The HR solution in NaOH was converted to CNPs. The weak fluorescence of such CNPs was brightened with NaCl. So, along with Na+, Cl− had a significant role in fluorescence enhancement. Our observation of Na+ induced fluorescence enhancement was similar to the report of Wang et al.30. Wang et al.30 described Na+ induced fluorescence enhancement with gold nanoclusters. We followed a similar mechanism for fluorescence enhancement as reported by Wang et al.30. Sodium enhances fluorescence indirectly through ionic-strength‐driven confinement rather than direct electronic interaction with the fluorophore. At high NaCl concentrations, charged fluorophores become immobilized within a rigid ionic matrix or highly ordered environment, which restricts ligand motion and suppresses non-radiative decay pathways such as vibrational and rotational relaxation. Electrostatic screening by Na⁺ also promotes closer packing and structural rigidification without inducing detrimental aggregation. This crystallization-induced confinement stabilizes the excited states and favors radiative recombination, leading to significant fluorescence enhancement, as observed for glutathione-capped metal nanoclusters embedded in NaCl crystals.

Turn-off fluorescence of NaHRCHPs with Ag+

The strong fluorescence of NaHRCHPs was efficiently quenched with Ag+ (AgNaHRCNPs). Such quenching was quite selective towards Ag+. We used Fe3+, Al3+, Cd2+, Cu2+, Cr3+, Ni2+, K+, Mg2+, Pb2+, Ba2+ instead of Ag+. Such efficient quenching prompted us to design an Ag+ sensing platform. We titrated NaHRCNPs with Ag+ and estimated the linear detection range was found to be 10− 3 M to 5 × 10− 7 M, while LOQ was found to be 0.0003 M (Fig. 6). Table 1 indicates various reports for Ag+ sensing with carbon dots/particles. For the first time, we reported here the sensing of Na+ and Ag+ in one pot using carbon particles, synthesised from human waste (human hair). Na+ induced emission enhancement, Ag+ induced fluorescence quenching, dual sensing, and employment of human hair are completely novel in our present article.

Mechanism of Ag+ sensing

Carbon particles are used for several sensing purposes. Sensing of Ag+ via such a circular economic approach was quite new. The method was eco-friendly and cost-effective, also. To understand the mechanistic ground of Ag+ based on fluorescence quenching, we performed XPS analysis of AgNaHRCNPs. The binding energies of Ag 374.15 eV and 368.12 eV corresponded to Ag 3d3/2 and Ag 3d5/2, respectively, when silver was in the Ag2O state39,40. The binding energy of sodium was found to be 1071.8 eV, corroborated with the Na+ 1 s (Fig. 7)41.

XRD patterns also implied the presence of oxidised silver oxide. 2θ values of 46.7°, 38.8°, 30.11° represented (221), (200), and (111), respectively, for Ag2O in AgNaHRCNPs42. FESEM image identified rod-shaped as well as spherical nanoparticles. We plotted the histogram and found that the average particle size was 30 nm (Fig. 8).

The hydrodynamic diameter of AgNaHRCNPs was found to be 844.14 nm (Fig. 9). As the hydrodynamic diameter was reduced, the fluorescence was decreased due to the higher absorption cross-section and lower scattering cross-section. Lukomska et al.43 illustrated the disintegration of the particles to induce fluorescent quenching of colloids. Ag+, being a heavy metal, could exhibit fluorescence quenching due to the heavy metal effect and the fluorophore (NaHRCNPs) to Ag+charge transfer44–46. Such charge-transfer reduced Ag+ to Ag0 and Ag0 was further oxidized in the reaction matrix to silver oxide nanoparticles. Ag₂O nanoparticles accepted electrons from the excited state of carbon nanoparticles, leading to non-radiative relaxation and a decrease in fluorescence intensity. The binding energies of Ag2O 3d3/2 and 3d5/2were reported to be 373.6 eV and 367.6 eV respectively47. On the other hand, the binding energies of Ag0 3d3/2 and 3d5/2was reported to be 374.9 eV and 369.0 eV respectively48. So in AgNaHRCNPs, silver was in intermediate charge density. The drift of the electron from NaHRCNPs towards Ag2O was pivotal for fluorescence quenching with in situ produced silver oxide nanoparticles. Nimisha et al.49 reported the electron transfer from fluorophore to silver as a responsible factor for fluorescence quenching.

Effect of the temperature of HRCNPs and AgNaCNPs

We gradually increased the temperature from 25 °C to 60 °C inside the fluorescence chamber and recorded fluorescence of both HRCNPs and AgNaCNPs, at every 5 °C temperature increment. For both cases, fluorescence was decreased equally with temperature. Such observations indicated that fluorescence quenching was due to increased Brownian motion50 and fluorescence quenching had a similar nature. We plotted IT/IRT vs. temperature, as mentioned in Fig. 10. IRT = fluorescence intensity at room temperature (25 °C), and IT= fluorescence intensity at temperature T.

Dynamic quenching increases with temperature, while static quenching remains constant or decreases with temperature51. For both HRCNPs and AgNaHRCNPs cases, the trend of decrease in fluorescence with temperature was practically similar. This was due to the enhanced Brownian motion only. So, quenching with silver was not affected by enhanced temperature. It is an example of static quenching.

Circular economic approach and novelty

For the first time we disclose the human waste for the sensing of analytes. It is a sustainable and cost-effective as well as novel tactic. Using human faeces as a carbon source for producing carbon dots for heavy metal sensing promotes sustainability and the circular economy by converting a plentiful and problematic waste stream into a high-value sensing material. This waste-to-resource strategy reduces pollution, lowers treatment costs, and eliminates reliance on petroleum-based or chemically expensive precursors. Converting garbage into nanosensors capable of detecting harmful metals in water not only recovers useful carbon but also enables improved contamination monitoring, thereby protecting ecosystems and human health. Overall, this strategy closes material loops, adds value to waste biomass, and encourages cleaner and more effective resource management.

Though few reports are available regarding the formation of nanoparticles with human hair, we disclosed here for the first time the brightening of emissive carbon nanoparticles (obtained from human hair with a unique method) with Na+ and subsequent dimming of emission with Ag+. Thus, Ag+ sensing was made possible with high selectivity and sensitivity in one pot (Table 2). The work is novel with respect to synthetic tactics as well as environmental applications.

Human hair, which consists of ~ 99% keratin and ~ 1% minor constituents, serves as an inexpensive, renewable, and heteroatom-rich carbon precursor for the synthesis of carbon quantum dots (CQDs)55. As per the reported literature, keratin contains 50% C, 21% N, 17.5% H, 6.5% O, and 5% S56,57. Keratin is a fibrous protein composed of carbon-rich peptide backbones and amino acid residues containing nitrogen, sulfur (from cysteine disulfide bonds), and oxygen, making it an intrinsically doped molecular framework. During this process, heteroatoms such as N, S, and O are retained and incorporated into the carbon lattice or surface functional groups, resulting in heteroatom-doped carbon nanoparticles rather than purely graphitic carbon. These dopants introduce surface defects, localized electronic states, and abundant functional groups (–NH₂, –COOH, –SOx), which are responsible for the fluorescence behavior, enhanced solubility, and metal-ion interaction capability of the CQDs. Therefore, carbon particles derived from hair extract are more accurately described as heteroatom-doped carbon nanoparticles, with their composition and properties directly inherited from the molecular structure of keratin itself.

The nanoparticles obtained from solely hair are usually termed as carbon particles, as carbon is a major contribution. In the one-step thermal treatment reported by Guo et al.58, heating hair at around 200 °C for prolonged duration induces dehydration, denaturation, and partial carbonization of keratin, leading to the formation of nanoscale carbon dots. Singh et al.55 also termed the particles obtained from hair as carbon dots, although hair contains other hetero atoms at a lower concentration.

Conclusions

Human hair predominantly consists of keratin proteins, which constitute the structural basis of the hair fibre. Furthermore, hair comprises lipids, melanin pigments that determine colour, and trace metals including zinc, iron, and copper, which contribute to its overall makeup. Human hair was found to be a precursor for fluorescent sulphur and nitrogen-doped carbon nanoparticles. Na+ further enhanced the fluorescence, making Na+ sensing possible. Such Na+ passivated carbon nanosphere acted as a fluorometric sensing platform for Ag+ via turn-off fluorescence. Modification of the electrostatic interaction was attributed to the fluorescence enhancement for Na+, while energy transfer was attributed to the quenching for Ag+. We collected human hairs from different stores and found that the result of Na+ and Ag+ sensing was insignificantly altered. A waste was used for sustainable water management. Thus, cost-effectiveness and circular economy were the main essence of our work.
