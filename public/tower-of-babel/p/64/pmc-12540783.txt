Multimodal prediction of metastatic relapse using federated deep learning in soft-tissue sarcoma with a complex genomic profile
Authors: Charles Maussion, Jean-Michel Coindre, Jean-Yves Blay, Kathryn Schutte, Axel Camara, François Le Loarer, Raul Perret, Myriam Jean-Denis, Alexandra Meurgey, Françoise Ducimetiere, Antoine Giraud, Jean-Baptiste Courrèges, Pierre Meeus, Audrey Michot, Etienne Bendjebbar, Jean Ogier du Terrail, Antoine Italiano, Marie Karanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoft Tissue sarcomas (STS) are a group of heterogeneous and complex diseases where being able to predict the appearance of metastases is key to inform clinical decisions, especially the prescription of adjuvant chemotherapy. We developed SarcNet: a multimodal deep-learning algorithm based on histological whole-slides and clinical variables to predict metastatic relapse in patients affected with limbs and trunk wall STS. Two independent series were investigated simultaneously in a privacy-enhanced multicentric setup using Federated Learning: Centre Léon Bérard (n = 221) and Institut Bergonié (n = 390). We then collected two additional validation cohorts from Centre Léon Bérard (n = 93) and Institut Bergonié (n = 124) to validate the performance of the trained algorithm. Evaluated in cross-validation setting on the first two cohorts, SarcNet achieved a performance of 0.797 AUC (Area Under the Receiver-operating characteristic Curve, 0.762–0.833) to predict 5-year MFS comparable to the Sarculator, current state-of-the-art nomogram (0.778 (0.743–0.814)), and outperforming the FNCLCC grading, widely used in practice (0.706 (0.670–0.743), P-value < 0.001). On validation cohorts, SarcNet performance is still on-par to the Sarculator and FNCLCC rating. Interpretability investigations of SarcNet highlighted histological patterns driving the prediction of metastatic relapse such as atypia, tumor cellularity and anisokaryosis. This model may assist clinical decisions by identifying high-risk patients that could benefit from neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20495-8.Subject terms: Sarcoma, Prognostic markers

Introduction

Soft-tissue sarcomas are rare tumors with an overall incidence of 6 to 9 cases for 100 000 person-years1. Sarcomas include a variety of different tumors with over 50 histological subtypes, and more molecular subtypes. Their natural history and prognosis is very variable and is not fully explained by the histological subtype, the molecular subtype, the grading nor by the more complete current American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control (AJCC/UICC) staging system2. Predicting metastatic relapse for patients affected by limbs or trunk wall tumors is challenging, and yet it is important for treatment decision making, in particular neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment3. In this study, we investigated limb and trunk wall sarcoma patients with a complex genetic profile4 and with no neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant chemotherapy to predict the occurrence of distant metastases 5 years after diagnosis (5-year MFS, event considered being patient dying or having metastasis occuring before 5 years).

Grading systems, based on histological criteria only, have been proved to be important prognostic factors. The French Federation of Cancer Centers (FNCLCC; Fédération nationale des centres de lutte contre le cancer) / WHO classification is currently the most used grading system for soft tissue sarcomas worldwide.

While multiple nomograms have been developed to predict the prognosis of Soft-tissue sarcomas patients, the most performant method (Sarculator) developed by Callegaro et al.5 consists of a nomogram based on three variables : the tumor size, the tumor grade (as defined by the FNCLCC6 and the histological subtype (with the addition of age when considering prediction of Overall Survival). This method, validated on three different cohorts, achieved impressive results in predicting both the Overall Survival and the appearance of Metastases at 5 and 10 years and represents a major improvement to inform clinical decision for sarcoma clinicians. However, its use in clinical practice remains subject to manual grading and histotyping by pathologists.

Recently, Artificial Intelligence (AI) demonstrated great promise in predicting prognosis for several indications7. In particular, we saw the emergence of models predicting outcomes directly from digitized whole slide images using deep learning methods for mesothelioma8, colorectal cancer9 and hepatocellular carcinoma10 patients. Those models even outperformed previous methods relying on time-consuming expert annotations or gradings. Additionally, multiple studies demonstrated the benefits of using multimodal analysis to complement AI image analysis with expert knowledge and clinical data11,12. An example of such synergy was recently demonstrated for soft-tissue sarcoma (STS) by Foersch et al. by predicting Disease Specific Survival in specific STS subtypes13.

However, it is well-known that deep learning is data-hungry and will only reach satisfactory performances with a sufficiently large number of patients14. As Soft-tissue sarcoma remains a rather rare disease (around 4000 cases per year in France15, the development of such models has until now been hindered because no single center carried enough patients. To overcome this issue, French sarcoma experts created the Sarcoma BCB (www.sacomabcb.org)16, a data warehouse of clinical, pathological (histotype and subtype, grade, …) and molecular data, allowing the study of cohorts from all hospitals in France. However, in order to create a model using whole slide images (WSI), a direct access to the center’s data is necessary as a centralized database of digitized images does not exist at the moment.

To be able to use images from multiple centers, we leveraged in this study an emerging technology, Federated Learning (FL)17, allowing to train AI algorithms in a distributed fashion with enhanced patient privacy. During FL training, the data remains behind the firewalls of each center, as opposed to the usual aggregation of data in a single database. The only information that is shared between centers during training, are the parameters of the algorithm whether raw or as differences of model states (updates). This method thus allows to overcome the issue of small single-center datasets while limiting patients’ data exposure. We emphasize that, in contrast to the majority of the Federated learning literature18–22, access to the full dataset in a pooled setting is impossible due to the security constraints of each institution and because of the regulatory framework. Therefore one of the contributions of this article is performing Federated Learning on a real-world network of partners instead of simulating it in a controlled environment. Here FL is used because it allows the unlocking of additional data that would otherwise be inaccessible following in the footsteps of MELLODDY23 and HealthChain24.

Results

Cohort description

This study assembles a total of 828 patients with limb or trunk wall Soft Tissue Sarcoma diagnosed at Institut Bergonié (IB, 390 patients in training and 124 in validation) and Centre Léon Berard (CLB, 221 patients in training and 93 in validation) as shown Fig. 1. All patients went through surgery with no administration of neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy. For each patient, high-resolution hematoxylin-and-eosin-stained histopathological WSI of tumor resection tissue or biopsies were digitized. Summary statistics for the clinical, biological and histology data are presented in Table 1. The CLB validation cohort contained proportionally more core needle biopsies than the training cohort. This cohort was more recent, and showed a change in practice in the center and the centers sending cases with more neoadjuvant treatment, responsible for morphological changes in resection specimens.

Federated Learning Network. (a) Simplified workflow of the federated learning infrastructure. With all data remaining behind firewalls, the algorithms are first trained locally on each institution (step 1) then transferred through the secure federated infrastructure (step 2) to the aggregation node. Once aggregated (step 3), model weights are sent back to each institution (step 4) to be retrained on each local dataset (step 1). Once this process has been repeated a sufficient amount of iterations and the aggregated model converges, we extract the final federated model (step 5). During the entire training process, the only information going out of hospitals firewalls are model weights, no patient data, labels or slides are exchanged. (b) Cohort extraction process. Our model is designed on a specific cohort that would particularly benefit from the prediction of metastatic relapse, namely complex genomic soft-tissues sarcoma from limb and trunk wall that did not receive perioperative chemotherapy. (c) Simplified SarcNet architecture. SarcNet is a multimodal model that leverages information from raw whole-slide images and clinical variables. Compared to the sarculator method, we are adding the information of the tumor depth and superficiality to the clinical variables. (d) Localisation of datasets and network nodes in France, all network tasks are launched from the Paris node. SarcNet model : a refined stratification tool

SarcNet was fitted on the concatenation of the variables used for the Sarculator (tumor size, tumor grade, histological subtype), additional clinical variables (age, tumor depth) and the whole slide image AI analysis. Through the use of Federated learning, we are able to train a unique model on both cohorts simultaneously. This strategy, described in Methods, enables multicentric training while minimizing patients’ exposure by transferring weights between hospitals during the training process. During the whole study, patients’ data of each hospital were never pooled together and remained on the hospitals’ servers.

In cross-validation setting, the SarcNet model reaches a mean AUC to predict the 5-year MFS of 0.797 (0.762–0.833 95% confidence interval), on-par with the current gold-standard Sarculator (AUC of 0.778 (0.743–0.814), p-value of 0.08) and outperforming significantly the FNCLCC grading (AUC of 0.706 (0.670–0.743), p-value < 0.001) as presented Fig. 2.

(b) AUC scores comparison between SarcNet model, Sarculator and FNCLCC grading on the two validation cohorts. (c) Kaplan-Meier analysis of metastatic relapse of the two groups segregated using SarcNet scores in cross-validation on the training cohort. The group of High-risk defined by a SarcNet score above the median has a median relapse time of 25 months and a first quintile of 7 months compared to a first quintile relapse time of 83 months for the low risk group.

In validation, the SarcNet model performance decreased but the model is still significantly predictive of 5-year MFS with an AUC of 0.662 on the CLB validation and 0.698 on IB validation. As in the cross-validation setting, SarcNet is on-par with the Sarculator (AUC of 0.718 on CLB, p-value > 0.1 and 0.714 on IB, p-value > 0.1). However, due to the size of cohorts, it does not significantly outperform the FNCLCC grading anymore (AUC of 0.641 on CLB, p-value > 0.1 and 0.640 on IB, p-value = 0.067).

Additionally, when creating two groups of high and low risks based on the median SarcNet prediction and considering the continuous outcome of appearance of metastases (MFS), SarcNet is able to significantly (log rank test P-value < 0.0001, HR = 4.31, all hazard ratios are calculated using the Cox Proportional-Hazards Model method) isolate low-risk patients (median MFS not determined; 1st quintile of 83 months) from high-risk patients (median MFS of 25 months, 1st quintile of 7 months).

One of the specificities of the SarcNet model is its ability to stratify patients within a single pathologist grading group. Indeed, as the final output of the model is an individual continuous risk score, a finer stratification of patients can be explored within known subgroups. As presented in the Extended Fig. 1, SarcNet model stratifies grade 3 patients into a group of high-risk patients (median MFS of 21 months) and a group of low-risk patients (median MFS undetermined). This result (along with the similar stratifications within grade 2 patients) demonstrates SarcNet’s ability to extract additional information compared to the current FNCLCC grading.

Robustness and ablation study

When compared in a cross-validation setting, the model trained in a federated learning fashion also significantly outperforms models trained on single-center cohorts (p-value of < 0.0001 for IB and < 0.01 for CLB). Indeed, SarcNet reaches an AUC of 0.797 on the complete cohort compared to 0.755 for CLB-SarcNet and 0.747 for IB-SarcNet. In particular, as shown in Fig. 3, SarcNet is able to have a better performance than the single-center models even in their training center thanks to the federated training and the additional patients it unlocks. This shows the value of having more diverse and heterogeneous patients to train such algorithms.

Thanks to its built-in attention mechanism, our methodology is robust to one of the classic limitations of whole slide image models, namely the size of the tissue specimen to analyze. Thanks to its ability to focus on specific parts of the images and its multimodality, our method is not affected by the specimen size. While on the training entire dataset (n = 611), most samples represent resection specimens (n = 503), the model’s performance was confirmed on core needle biopsies (n = 80). In cross-validation, on the complete cohort the model reaches a 0.797 (0.762–0.833) mean AUC compared to a 0.797 (0.642–0.953) mean AUC for the core needle biopsy cohort.

Lastly, we developed an AI model based on the histopathology image alone (AI-histo) to evaluate the prognostic value of this analysis. Considering the explorative nature of this work, we only performed it on the cross-validation cohort; validation cohorts being reserved for the final validation of the fixed SarcNet model. While its performance is obviously inferior to the SarcNet model with an AUC of 0.678, AI-histo is similarly able to significantly (log rank test P-value < 0.0001, HR = 2.10) isolate high-risk patients (median MFS not determined; 1st quintile of 34 months) from low-risk patients (median MFS of 72 months, 1st quintile of 8 months). In addition, although our model does not perform significantly better than the expert-based histological grading (0.678 vs. 0.706, p-value:0.97), it significantly stratifies patients classified as grade 3 in the FNCLCC grading (log rank test P-value = 0.036, HR = 1.40). Within those grade 3 patients, a stratification following AI-histo model median prediction creates a group of high risk patients (median MFS 23 months) and a group of low-risk patients (undetermined median MFS), showing that this model provides again a higher level of granularity than the grading system. Additional stratification on specific grades are presented in the Extended Fig. 1. To better understand the complementarity of components involved in the SarcNet, a comprehensive ablation study is provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

Histological patterns interpretability

Finally, we leveraged the interpretable nature of our model as well as pathologists’ expertise to understand the underlying histological patterns identified as high-risk or low-risk predictors. The methodology used is described in Methods and results are presented .

The interpretability analysis allowed to highlight a few criteria that were significantly associated with the tiles scores (Fig. 4). Among the 180 reviewed tiles, 86 of 90 high score tiles (96%) and 37 of the 90 low scores tiles (41%) corresponded to tumor areas. In tumor tiles, atypia (defined as anisokaryosis and high nucleocytoplasmic ratio) appeared to be associated to tile scores as mild nuclear atypia was observed on 27% of the low-score tiles and 0% of the high-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001) and severe nuclear atypia was observed in 83% of the high-score tiles and 32% of the low-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001). Another significant criteria was the cellularity of the tumoral tissue. Low cellularity was noted in 30% of low-score tiles and 0% of high-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001) and high cellularity was noted in 80% of high-score tiles and 40% of low-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001). In addition, pathologists were asked to annotate the cell types in tumor tiles. The imbalance of tile scores was significant for two cell types among the 5 annotated. Spindle cells were noted in 78% of low-score tiles and 28% of high-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001). Pleomorphic cells were noted in 36% of high-score tiles and only 3% of low-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001). In addition, the presence or absence of stroma in tumor tissue was annotated by pathologists and stroma qualified as either myxoid or fibrosis. The absence of stroma was noted in 64% of the high-score tiles and 32% of the low-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value = 0.0016) whereas the presence of myxoid stroma was noted in 19% of low-score tiles and in none of high-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value = 0.0001). Finally, pathologists annotated the presence of hyperchromatic nuclei (nuclei that look darker than normal) in tumor cells. Hyperchromasia was noted in 84% of the high-score tiles and 40% of the low-score tiles (Fisher’s exact P-value < 0.0001).

Interpretability examples of tiles classified as predictive of high or low-risk of metastatic relapse. Three expert pathologists blinded to risk scores analyzed the 180 most predictive tiles. The features predictive of high risk of relapse included severe nuclear atypia, nuclear hyperchromasia and pleomorphic tumor cells. The features predictive of low risk of relapse included low tumor cellularity, presence of spindle tumor cells and myxoid stroma. Scale bars, 112 μm.

To summarize, the interpretability analysis of our model showed that tiles that contribute to a high risk of metastatic relapse are mostly tumoral (96%) and display severe nuclear atypia, high cellularity, nuclear hyperchromasia, the absence of stroma and the presence of pleomorphic cells. On the other hand, tiles that contribute to a low risk of metastatic relapse are mostly non-tumoral (58%) and the tumoral tiles present mild nuclear atypia, low cellularity, the presence of spindle cells and the stroma is more abundant and usually myxoid.

Discussion

In this study we demonstrate that a Deep Learning model, named SarcNet, can accurately predict metastatic relapse on STS by leveraging multimodality. This model obtains performances similar to previously validated methods like the Sarculator while considering the same main inputs (image analysis and clinical variables). The main improvement of this new model is the addition of the raw-image AI analysis to the pathologist-driven histology analysis like grading and subtyping. Thanks to this new type of analysis, we are leveraging features present in images that were not included in previous methods. Although the current performance of the algorithm does not significantly surpass that of Sarculator, it establishes a foundation for integrating additional variables in future, more advanced tools to enhance predictive accuracy.

Indeed, thanks to the interpretability analysis, we have hints that the model is driven by pathological features that are not included within the FNCLCC/WHO grading like anisokaryosis, nucleocytoplasmic ratio or tumor cellularity. Seeing those features being used by the algorithm questions us on their complementarity and additional predictivity to the current histological grading and subtyping. Likewise, as some variables used in the FNCLCC/WHO grading like presence of necrosis or mitosis were not significantly associated with the prediction of the SarcNet model, we think there is still area for improvement for the model by combining the current agnostic approach with some expert-driven annotation specifically targeted towards necrosis or mitosis. One of the possible explanations for the lack of necrosis among predictive features could be that pathologists selected only one slide representative of the patient tumor for this Deep-Learning analysis while necrosis assessment is done on all slides during the FNCLCC/WHO grading. Finally, another advantage of Sarcnet is its reproducibility compared to the histological grade which is imperfect25. Only one slide per case was selected from all the slides produced for that case. Pathologists selected the slide most representative of the tumor in terms of cellularity, cytological appearance, histological appearance, atypia, number of mitoses and necrosis. Performing AI analysis on a single slide was clearly a limitation of this study. The results show that this selection led to an underestimation of necrosis. Necrosis should be assessed macroscopically and microscopically on the whole specimen. Pathologists preferentially selected slides without necrosis. When the study was designed, the difficulty of retrospectively scanning a large number of slides was taken into account. Soon, all pathology laboratories will be scanning cases prospectively, and the constraints will be different, even if not all virtual slides can be stored. We are considering testing SarcNet on all the slides from a single case as the natural next step.

This study is also one of the first FL real-world experiments where the use of Federated Learning allows to constitute a database otherwise inaccessible. Other similar studies published previously were run on smaller cohorts: 35 patients in Hagi et al.26, and 291 patients in Foersch et al.13. This work can serve as a first step towards the constitution of a broader Sarcoma federated network connecting centers thanks to a Federated Learning infrastructure. Indeed, the FL approach is by design very scalable as adding a new center does not require additional data transfer. Having a growing number of nodes in this Sarcoma network is crucial, as we demonstrated in this study that unlocking a single additional dataset using FL allows not only to improve robustness on new center thanks to the diversity of data points encountered but also performance on the initial training center thanks to a bigger training set. Once we leveraged the federated network for training and cross-validation, we further assessed the performance of our model on two external validation cohorts previously unseen. The model is still able to significantly predict 5-year MFS on those cohorts although less accurately, like the two reference methods Sarculator and FNCLCC grading. We observe that the three methods see their performance decrease when transferred to the validation cohorts, which cannot be explained by any over-fitting issue for the Sarculator and the grading. The difference of collection date and thus potentially of clinical practice could explain why the algorithms and grading have difficulties to transfer performance to the validation datasets. Before any further use of this algorithm, we encourage additional external validation to assess its performance and robustness on a wide range of patients and data collection methods.

Finally, once properly validated, this type of model is designed to be easily deployed anywhere in the world once someone has access to a scanner for pathology images. Its promise is thus to not only improve performance but also consistency and accessibility for metastatic relapse prediction in STS. This model could replace current gold-standard methods to improve stratification of patients for treatment and clinical trials patient selection. Furthermore this study could allow the drafting of practical guidelines by providing, not only a continuous risk score allowing patient to patient rankings, but also a standard decision cutoff that practitioners can directly confront to their personal experience-based decision. Additionally, if we focus here on the specific task of metastatic relapse in STS, this method is not restricted to Metastatic relapse and STS but could be applied for other targets like overall survival or adverse events in other types of indications. This general methodology is applicable to any “weakly-supervised task” in any indication.

To conclude, this study demonstrates the ability of multimodal machine learning to predict metastatic relapse in STS. Further validation will be necessary for a clinical routine use but constant improvement of the model is made possible by the natural scalability of the Federated Learning infrastructure. We envisioned this study as a proof-of-concept of a global methodology for building multi-centric AI models in the future. Indeed, the SarcNet model was improved by the training on multiple centers thanks to a bigger and more heterogeneous training dataset. This is evidence that medical centers need to join-hands and cooperate using this secure Federated Learning framework as it will create more efficient and robust prediction models.

Methods

Validation methods and statistical analysis

To fully leverage all patients accessible in this study, we performed our analysis in a 5-time repeated 5-fold cross-validation setting (5 × 5 CV)27. Once all folds were used as a test set, a new random 5-fold split is performed to provide more robust estimates of the models’ performances. In total, we split the dataset 5 times to obtain a total of 25 different trained models, so 25 performance scores for each architecture. It is worth insisting on the point that in this setting, just like with immediate external validation, each model is totally blind to its own test set, mitigating over-fitting issues. Additionally, this cross-validation setting allows to evaluate and compare methods more accurately than a single external validation test that is subject to potentially important confidence intervals. In particular, in the entire study, we retain the same 5 × 5 splits for all models to be able to compare methods between them. This means that instead of a single comparison in the case of an external validation, we are able to compare strategies on 25 different settings.

In order to compute comparison p-values between models in a cross-validation setting, while avoiding common statistical pitfalls28, we use, for every comparison, the first cross-validation split and merge all test sets predictions and ground truth (by construction non-overlapping) into one table and bootstrap the rows of the resulting matrix to empirically compute comparison p-values. Therefore we only assume intra-split independence but not inter-split.

HE slides and digitalization

Histological slides were performed on 4 μm sections cut from formalin-fixed-paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue and stained with hematoxylin and eosin by the Tissue TEK autostainer (Sakura). The staining programme was as follows: xylene (10 min), pure alcohol (5 min), 95% alcohol (2 min), water (3 min), demineralised water (1 min), haemalun (3 min 30 secondes), water (3 min), 95% alcohol (30 s), eosin (2 min), pure alcohol (1 min), xylene (2 min).

HE slides were scanned with a Aperio GT 450DX (Leica) scanner for the CLB cohort and Nanozoomer S360 (Hamamatsu Photonics) for the IB cohort.

Histology images preprocessing and feature extraction

The application of deep-learning algorithms to histological data is a challenging problem, particularly due to the high dimensionality of the data (up to 100,000 × 100,000 pixels for a single whole-slide image) and the small size of available datasets. We therefore cannot directly try to predict labels from images like in classical image recognition problems, making it necessary to first preprocess the images to embed them in a space of smaller dimension.

Tissue segmentation

We first applied a Unet29 with a VGG11 encoder30 pre-trained with ImageNet31, a convolutional neural network to segment the tissue sections from the whole slide images. The Unet was further trained with an annotated dataset collected from publicly available sources containing 570 whole slides images of H&E, HES and Immunohistochemistry staining8. By applying this algorithm, we removed all regions without tissue or artifacts such as pen marks on the slides.

Tiling and feature extraction

We divided the whole-slide images into tiles of 112 × 112 μm (224 × 224 pixels) at a zoom level of 0.5 microns per pixel. We then use a 50-layer ResNet32 pre-trained with ImageNet in order to extract imaging features from the tiles. Each tile is therefore represented by 2048 features and each slide by a matrix of features of size n_tiles x 2048.

Modeling

AI-histo

The AI architecture used for the imaging models is the Weldon architecture33. This model is designed in a two-step manner. A first linear layer predicts a probability score for each tile, then the 35 maximum and minimum tile scores are averaged together to produce a single score per patient. This architecture is interpretable “by design” as it allows to localize the tiles associated with a poor prognosis (high scores) and a good prognosis (low scores) in the slide for further analysis by a pathologist.

AI-Clinical

The clinical model consists of a multi-layer fully connected neural network with the sigmoid activation function, that takes as input 13 clinical variables and outputs a clinical score. The MLP contains two hidden layers with the size of 16 and 32 neurons. The 13 clinical variables represent tumour size, age, grade (3 variables), depth (2) and subtypes (6).

SarcNet

The multimodal model consists of an imaging model, a clinical model, and an aggregation model. The imaging model is a modified version of the Weldon model previously described, as it outputs 13 scores per tile. The top 35 and bottom 35 scores of each of the 13 scores per tile are selected independently and averaged together to output 13 imaging scores per patient. The clinical model is the same as above. For each patient the imaging scores and the clinical scores are concatenated to produce a set of 26 features that are inputted to the final aggregation model which consists of a linear layer that outputs a single risk score per patient.

SarcNet, and all other models, are trained in an end-to-end manner with backpropagation using variants of stochastic gradient descent with mini-batches34 inside a Federated Network. The Federated training methods are further described below.

Federated learning

In order to train our models on both centers simultaneously, while minimizing patients exposure, we test different Federated Learning strategies:

Federated Averaging17 computes gradients locally and average them every E local gradient step; the weights of the average are proportional to the data quantity in each silo.

Federated Averaging17 computes gradients locally and average them every E local gradient step; the weights of the average are proportional to the data quantity in each silo.

Scaffold35 adds control variates to Federated Averaging using a stateful server allowing the optimization to reach the true global minimizer despite the clients drift induced by the repeated local updates. We use the update-rule number II as defined in the original article and a server-size learning rate of 1.

Scaffold35 adds control variates to Federated Averaging using a stateful server allowing the optimization to reach the true global minimizer despite the clients drift induced by the repeated local updates. We use the update-rule number II as defined in the original article and a server-size learning rate of 1.

For both strategies we use local ADAM optimizer36 with the same parameters as for local experiments (same batch-sizes, learning rates, regularizations, model parameters). Note that by adding this local optimizer on the client’s side, the implemented strategies deviate from their original formulations.

All strategies are enacted using the open-source framework substra37, a security-audited traceable remote-execution software. This software is directly deployed within hospitals firewalls to ensure that all actions on a patient’s data are secured and traced and thus directly auditable by either of the hospitals. The only information exchanged during the training process are model’s gradients leading to increased privacy for the patients compared to a setting where all patients data would be pooled in a single center.

We emphasize that, although FL is a privacy enhancing technology, all exchanged quantities derived from data such as gradients in our case could theoretically be targeted by dedicated privacy attacks using auxiliary knowledge about ML training such as losses, architectures, learning rates and model weights38,39. However, in practice, to the best of our knowledge, no attack has been shown to work in this specific setting of multiple instance learning on pretrained features. We relegate a full privacy investigation of gradient leakage to further work as outside of the scope of this paper.

Interpretability

In order to interpret the findings of the SarcNet model and highlight features linked with high (resp low) risk of metastatic relapse, we selected most relevant tiles using the localized risk predictions embedded in the Weldon architecture. To do so, we extracted in both center the 3 tiles with the highest (resp lowest) scores for each of the 15 patients with the highest (resp lowest) predictions. This process ensures the extraction of the most representative features of high (resp low) risk according to the model while keeping a diversity in the tiles annotated by preventing from having all tiles coming from a single patient.

Once those 180 tiles were extracted (2 centers x 2 predictivity x 15 patients x 3 tiles), three expert pathologists annotated the tiles blinded to the model predictions. This annotation followed a list of criteria defined in advance and presented in the Supplementary Table 2. Consensus was found using the majority vote of the three readers. Then, we analyzed the association between each annotated criteria and the predictivity of the tiles. For each criteria, we counted the number of high-score tiles and the number of low-score tiles in each group. Then, we used Fisher’s exact test to evaluate whether the observed imbalance in the two groups is statistically significant. We considered 5% probability as a significance level.
