Arousal shapes motivation: an integrative framework for understanding motivational deficits
Authors: Lilly Thurn, Adam J. Culbreth, Dennis Hernaus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Motivational deficits are a core feature of psychiatric disorders like major depressive disorder and schizophrenia, contributing significantly to reduced levels of daily functioning and quality of life. While most mechanistic frameworks of motivational impairments emphasize difficulties in accurately representing the value of goals – typically linked to dysfunction in dopamine-mediated reward systems – emerging evidence suggests that these models may be incomplete. In particular, dysfunctional modulation of arousal states may play a critical and underappreciated role by impairing the mobilization of cognitive and physiological resources required for goal-directed behavior in individuals with motivational deficits. Here, we aim to bridge the gap between arousal and valuation processes by offering an initial proposal for an integrative framework capable of explaining how motivational deficits may arise from their maladaptive interactions. We do this by first outlining how arousal systems support motivated behavior at a neural and cognitive level in healthy individuals, followed by highlighting converging evidence of arousal dysregulation in individuals with mood and psychotic disorders. Based on this work, we extend existing frameworks of motivated behavior by integrating arousal dynamics in order to outline how disruptions in the dynamic modulation of arousal can directly interfere with the formation and updating of value representations. We also offer recommendations for experimental and computational approaches that are needed to further clarify the role of arousal dynamics in healthy and clinical populations. A causal exploration of these mechanisms could lead to a more fine-grained understanding of disrupted processes of motivated behavior across transdiagnostic dimensions of psychopathology.

Introduction

Motivational deficits are a hallmark feature of several psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) and schizophrenia (SZ), and are key determinants of functional outcome, quality of life, and life satisfaction [1–3]. In the majority of individuals with SZ and MDD, motivational deficits are already present in the prodromal or early phase of illness [4], and remain prominent when the illness becomes chronic [5, 6], thus underlining the importance of adequate and early treatment [7]. Unfortunately, current treatments are often unable to produce clinically meaningful improvements [8], and reduced motivation commonly remains a residual symptom with a long-lasting, disabling impact [9].

Most mechanistic accounts of motivational deficits assume a key role for impaired “value representation”, that is, difficulties in learning about, representing, and integrating the costs and benefits of pursuing goals (see Box 1) [10–12]. Moreover, complementary accounts have focused on deficits in activational processes that manifest as altered behavioral expressions of vigor, effort expenditure, and motor control in the service of goal pursuit [13]. Although these frameworks are supported by a strong empirical evidence base and continue to inform therapeutic development, advances in the treatment of motivational deficits remain limited [14]. This gap highlights the need for more detailed process-level accounts that can guide the development of targeted and more effective interventions. In this manuscript, we, therefore, consider how we can enrich our current mechanistic understanding of motivational deficits. Informed by a review of self-report, psychophysiological, and neural data in people with MDD and SZ, we discuss how we can approach a fuller understanding of the pathophysiology of motivational deficits by enriching value representation frameworks with state-of-the-art knowledge on the role of arousal systems in motivated behavior.

Although arousal has been defined in various ways across research traditions [15], here we define it as a physiological state that determines an organism’s momentary cognitive and physical responsiveness to internal and external stimuli, which is critical for mobilizing resources for goal-directed behavior [16]. Conceptually, arousal can be viewed as an emergent property of multiple interacting neural systems, rather than as a single, directly measurable process [17, 18]. This perspective emphasizes that arousal is supported by complex interactions among several neuromodulatory systems, including noradrenaline (NA), dopamine (DA), acetylcholine, and serotonin, that jointly shape the capacity for cognitive and behavioral responsiveness [19, 20].

Building on this conceptualisation, a number of theoretical and computational frameworks have begun to formalize mechanistic explanations of how arousal influences goal-directed behavior [20–22]. For example, extensive work on the locus coeruleus-noradrenaline (LC-NA) system has demonstrated how arousal-related changes in neural responsiveness modulate goal-directed actions through changes in information processing [23] and choice strategies (e.g., exploration versus exploitation [20]). Other frameworks emphasize the role of arousal as a mobilizer of resources for meeting environmental demands [21]. Together, these accounts describe how arousal regulates the conditions under which goal-directed processes can be efficiently engaged.

Importantly, however, arousal does not operate in isolation from motivational and valuation systems but, rather, dynamically interacts with them. Large-scale cortical networks and multiple neurochemical systems, including serotonin, dopamine, and acetylcholine, jointly regulate valuation and decision-making in continuous, bidirectional, interaction with arousal-mediating systems [24]. Several frameworks highlight how arousal-related modulation (e.g., of “neural gain”; see below) and resource availability can shape the functioning of dopaminergic systems involved in effort valuation [13, 25], cost-benefit arbitration, and behavioral vigor [13, 26]. In these perspectives, dopamine does not constitute an arousal system per se. Instead, it can be seen as a downstream system whose influence on motivated behavior is (at least partially) constrained by arousal-mediating systems. Integrative models, such as the Reinforcement Meta-Learning model, formalize this view by proposing that cortical circuits compute state-dependent action values based on environmental information, with neuromodulatory signals (including dopaminergic and noradrenergic) regulating the relative weight of reward and effort signals [27].

Here, we build on this body of prior work to advance an integrative conceptual framework which proposes that arousal dynamically shapes goal-directed behavior by setting a “reference frame” for (downstream) motivational computations mediated by other systems, such as dopamine. We do this by distilling converging insights from basic, computational, and neurobiological studies into an explicit formulation of how arousal interacts with valuation systems to influence motivated behavior. Using this formulation as a guiding principle, we next consider its relevance for understanding motivational deficits in psychiatric disorders. As we will discuss in detail below, converging evidence indicates that individuals with MDD and SZ exhibit dysfunction in arousal systems, particularly in the dynamic modulation of arousal required to flexibly initiate, sustain, and adapt goal pursuit [28–30]. Although prior accounts have suggested that arousal modulates motivational intensity [31] or behavioral engagement and withdrawal [32], these insights have not yet been integrated into a mechanistic framework that connects arousal dynamics to specific computational components of motivated behavior, and dysfunction thereof. Our goal is to make an initial proposal towards addressing this gap. To this end, we organize findings from fundamental and clinical research within a well-established framework of motivated behavior (described in Box 1 and illustrated in Box Figure). Using this structure, we aim to bridge the levels of analysis and articulate how insights obtained from basic research on arousal may inform models of motivational dysfunction. Specifically, we start by (1) summarizing the neural and cognitive mechanisms by which arousal contributes to motivated behavior in animals and healthy humans, followed by (2) reviewing (preliminary) evidence for dysfunctional arousal in people with MDD and SZ, and, (3), how this contributes to impairments in subcomponents of motivated behavior (e.g., effort-cost decision-making (ECDM) and reinforcement learning (RL)).

Combined, this literature suggests that dysregulation of arousal systems could make individuals less able to appropriately learn about, represent, and integrate the costs and benefits of actions, resulting in reduced motivation to initiate and sustain goal-directed behavior. We use these emerging insights to propose how (dysfunction in) arousal systems may be mechanistically linked to abnormal goal pursuit in multiple psychiatric disorders, including MDD and SZ, while also highlighting critical areas for future research that could further refine and test this integrative framework.

Motivated behavior is a complex process that involves multiple interrelated sub-processes. The interplay between these processes is illustrated in Box Figure.

Representations of effort (i.e., cost) and reward (i.e., benefit) associated with actions are shaped by expectations based on prior experiences (i.e., learning). Decision-making entails the computational process of valuation, i.e., balancing these two quantities against each other to compute a “net” value that indicates which actions are “worth” pursuing. This process may be affected by the current choice strategy (also known as decision-making policies) of the organism; here, we use “exploration” versus “exploitation” as examples, although more strategies exist. These choice strategies involve placing different weights on the two aforementioned quantities; exploration reflects a state associated with acquiring information and discovering new options, even at the cost of immediate reward, whereas exploitation describes the preference to rely on well-known, high reward options. Once an organism decides to pursue the anticipated reward, energy must be mobilized to meet the effort demands. Then, during the action, cognitive and physical resources must be allocated toward goal-pursuit to obtain the anticipated outcome. After action execution, the actual outcome is compared against the expected outcome, and mismatches (i.e., prediction errors) guide learning by updating future value estimates.

How to study motivated behavior?

The Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework represents a multifaceted theoretical framework that aims to advance the understanding of cognitive and biological mechanisms that underlie key symptoms of psychiatric disorders, and to facilitate research beyond traditional diagnostic categories [151,165]. Within this framework, motivational processes are primarily captured by components of the Positive Valence system; a system comprising constructs thought to be imperative for appetitive motivation, such as reward responsiveness, reward and effort valuation, and reinforcement learning [165].

Two commonly-used research paradigms in the study of (ab)normal motivated behavior are effort-cost decision-making (ECDM) and reinforcement learning (RL). ECDM assesses mental cost-reward computations and subsequent decisions by having individuals decide on the amount of effort they are willing to expend for a given reward [140]. Arousal dynamics promote behavioral adaptation via large-scale brain state reconfigurations

Arousal is inherently dynamic, fluctuating along a continuum from sleep to wakefulness to alertness, in extreme cases escalating to hypervigilance and anxiety [20, 33]. At the neural level, dynamic changes in arousal state are facilitated by various deep-seated brainstem nuclei that project to the cortex via widely arborized projections [17]. Among these neuromodulatory systems, the locus coeruleus (LC) – the brain’s main source of noradrenaline (NA) – has been extensively studied in relation to its regulatory function in arousal dynamics [16, 20]. Although the LC-NA system certainly operates in concert with other arousal-modulating systems (e.g., acetylcholine, serotonin, and autonomic inputs), here we use it as a central reference point in light of its well-characterized role in regulating global brain states. Below, we first summarize the neuropharmacological principles by which the LC-NA system drives dynamic changes in arousal state that support behavioral adaptation through large-scale brain state reconfigurations.

Early work in non-human primates has demonstrated that the LC-NA system dynamically modulates arousal states via two distinct operational modes, each acting on a different timescale [20, 34]. While tonic LC activity is thought to be involved in slow regulation of a baseline-like arousal state, externally-evoked phasic bursts of LC activity reflect rapid, steep, and time-locked increases in arousal that facilitate more immediate adaptive responses to the environment. Phasic activity is thought to be essential for distinguishing task-relevant signals from background noise, making it particularly relevant to goal-directed behavior [35, 36]. The (combined) effects of these operational modes shape cognitive functions (e.g., attention, working memory, goal-directed action) via their influence on microcircuitry; a process that has been characterized particularly well in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) [37, 38]. Specifically, the association between LC-NA activity and cognitive functioning can be described as an inverted-U curve, where optimal cognitive performance is observed at intermediate levels of tonic LC activity that permit strong, stimulus-evoked phasic bursts [20]. NA release, via its combined actions at α2, α1, and β receptors, facilitates shifts along this curve by altering the sensitivity of neurons to incoming signals, a process referred to as “neural gain”, that involves tuning of the excitability of both excitatory and inhibitory interneurons [20, 39]. This, at the microcircuitry level, directly affects the degree to which task-relevant signals are amplified relative to background noise, thus controlling the signal-to-noise ratio [20, 39, 40]. Here, low and high arousal states co-occur with diametrically opposite (low versus high) signal-to-noise ratios, which are thought to facilitate distinct types of behavior associated with these arousal states, such as disengagement, exploration, and flexibility in working memory (WM) representations versus exploitation, reduced distractibility, and maintenance of WM representations.

In human neuroimaging studies, dynamic changes in arousal are also reflected in the reconfiguration of whole-brain network topology, with arousal mediating transitions between states of network segregation and integration. Specifically, whereas lower levels of physiological and neural indices of arousal are associated with reduced inter-regional communication and greater network segregation, higher arousal appears to facilitate inter-regional communication [41, 42]. Although network integration is generally associated with better cognitive performance [41, 43, 44], excessively high arousal states can inhibit inter-regional communication, leading to a deterioration in task performance [41, 45]. This arousal-dependent reconfiguration of large-scale brain network topology has been observed both in the context of association studies using indirect measures of naturally-occurring changes in arousal (i.e., at a second-to-minute timescale) [44], as well as causal manipulation studies using pharmacology that acts directly on the LC-NA system [41, 46, 47]. These changes in inter-regional communication at a network level are suggested to be, at least in part, mediated via changes in neural gain at the microcircuitry level [39, 43]. Collectively, these findings highlight how LC-NA-mediated changes in arousal reconfigure brain states, thereby adaptively shaping cognitive functions.

Evidence that arousal dynamics shape motivated behavior

Importantly, non-human and human studies suggest that the above-reviewed principles are of critical importance for shaping distinct subcomponents of motivated behavior as introduced and visualized in Box 1. For the sake of specificity, we summarize these results per subcomponent where possible, although we recognize that not all studies map neatly onto a single subcomponent.

Insights from non-human animal studies

Studies in non-human animals implicate LC-NA activity in facilitating distinct forms of goal-directed behavior via its effect on decision-making (see Box 1). Specifically, whereas research in monkeys has suggested that phasic, task-related, LC firing promotes reward-oriented decisions and exploitation via selective filtering of task-relevant information [48], inducing tonic LC firing (here: in the rat brain) has been shown to result in disengagement and explorative behavior [49]. Moreover, in rats, experimentally induced tonic LC activity has been associated with increased decision noise, thereby promoting explorative, as opposed to exploitative, behavior [49]. Invasive recordings of neuronal activity in the monkey brain have also shown that phasic LC activity co-occurs with the decision to pursue a prospective reward [50, 51], potentially providing a signal to engage in exploitative behavior, especially when the action that results in a reward is effortful or when it is anticipated to be less rewarding [50, 52, 53].

A second line of evidence in non-human animals implicates LC-NA activity in mobilizing energy to overcome challenges and meet effort demands during goal pursuit (Fig. 1). In monkeys performing a decision-making task, phasic LC firing has been shown to closely track the subjectively anticipated effort required to obtain a reward [50]. Moreover, LC firing rate scales with physical and cognitive effort exerted during reward pursuit, and attenuating NA function using pharmacology results in less physical effort exerted for rewards [54, 55], implying a potentially causal role for the LC-NA system in resource (i.e., energy) mobilization [53, 56]. Recent optogenetic recordings in rats confirm this role by showing that greater noradrenergic LC activity co-occurs with greater movement acceleration, thereby demonstrating the contribution of LC activity to the mobilization of physical resources to meet situational demands [57]. Importantly, evidence from physiological studies further supports this view, as noradrenergic output from the LC exerts not only central effects (i.e., on neural processing), but also modulates cardiovascular tone by reducing inhibitory parasympathetic activity, thereby increasing heart rate and promoting physiological readiness [58]. Insights from human studies

Human studies offer more indirect support for a role of arousal, potentially LC-mediated, in motivated behavior. Unlike in experimental animals, LC-NA activity cannot be measured directly in humans and is therefore often inferred from non-invasive indices such as momentary fluctuations in pupil size [59]. Importantly, however, pupil size is an indirect proxy of LC-NA activity and is influenced by multiple neuromodulatory and autonomic systems [60, 61]. As such, the pupil size studies reviewed below should be interpreted with caution.

Neurally, pupil size is controlled by two complementary pathways. Pupil constriction is mainly driven by light-induced activation of neurons in the Edinger-Westphal nucleus (EWN), which innervate the iris sphincter muscle. Pupil dilation, on the other hand, is modulated by sympathetic innervation of the iris dilator muscle [59]. During wakefulness, projections from the LC and hypothalamus stimulate the dilator muscle and regulate the degree of pupillary dilation [59]. Consistent with this, both baseline (tonic) pupil size and dynamic (phasic) reactivity to environmental stimuli have been associated with distinct LC firing modes [62, 63], thus motivating the assumption that pupil-linked arousal may be a surrogate marker of LC-NA activity [64, 65]. However, the extent to which pupil-linked arousal exclusively tracks LC-NA activity is a topic of active investigation, with accumulating evidence indicating contributions from additional systems [60].

In humans, mounting evidence suggests that phasic changes in pupil size (i.e., during task events or in response to stimuli) closely track value representation during decision-making and learning, thereby extending the well-established role of pupil-linked arousal in other cognitive domains (e.g., cognitive load [66], and allocation of attentional resources [67]). Specifically, in tasks that require participants to weigh costs (e.g., cognitive or physical effort) against benefits (i.e., rewards) to decide which goals are “worth” pursuing, dynamic changes in pupil size have been shown to track costs, benefits [68, 69], and “net” (subjective) decision value (i.e., benefits – costs) [70]. Beyond associations with choice value during decision-making, pupil-linked arousal has also been implicated in the learning of value. After receiving feedback on decision outcomes, mismatches between expected and actual decision outcomes (i.e., prediction errors) are a critical quantity that drives learning in reinforcement learning frameworks (Box 1) [71, 72]. The degree of pupil size change during outcome evaluation has been positively associated with the encoding of different types of prediction errors (i.e., cost, reward, and risk) [70, 73–75]. This may reflect a role for arousal in consolidating decisions, cognitive or physical, especially in situations of high uncertainty outcome-related information [76], which is consistent with the proposed role of arousal in mobilizing attentional resources toward expectancy-violating information (i.e., unsigned prediction errors) in the service of optimizing goal-pursuit in the future [40, 77].

Neuroimaging studies provide converging evidence that pupil dilation is not only correlated with LC activation [78, 79]; it also precedes connectivity increases between cortical areas that mediate representations of stimulus-response contingencies [46, 80]. During cognitive engagement, phasic pupillary responses are associated with activity in fronto-parietal regions, specifically the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the insula [78, 79, 81]. These regions have previously been suggested to be part of a large-scale “valuation” network associated with representing and integrating costs and benefits [20, 82–84]. Furthermore, pharmacological augmentation of NA function has been shown to increase network-level integration and functional connectivity between the LC and frontal (cortical) areas, as well as connectivity between key hubs of the valuation network, during states of cognitive engagement [41, 47, 85]. One interpretation of these results is that LC-mediated dynamic changes in arousal states directly affect neural cost and reward computations during decision-making and learning by modulating connectivity among these brain areas.

A final line of neuromodulatory evidence in humans further supports a potentially causal role for arousal dynamics in motivated behavior. A recent meta-analysis of transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS) concluded that short electrical pulses applied to the vagus nerve could augment naturally occurring LC activity by mimicking phasic vagal activity patterns [86]. As such, pulsed tVNS has the potential to induce short-term increases in pupil size [86], which co-occur with changes in motivated behavior. Specifically, work by Neuser et al. [87] showed that active versus sham tVNS resulted in more vigorous (effortful) responding for reward, potentially reflecting an increase in wanting prospective rewards through enhanced arousal. Although this research line is still in its early stages, future studies both in individuals with MDD and healthy controls [88] may potentially further clarify the role of LC-mediated arousal in effort expenditure.

Arousal as a calibration mechanism for goal-directed behavior

In sum, converging evidence across species and methodologies indicates that dynamic fluctuations in arousal states critically shape goal-directed behavior through their influence on various motivational computations illustrated in Box Figure.

Based on the results discussed above, we surmise that these effects appear to operate through both cognitive and physical pathways. On the one hand, different arousal states (e.g., low versus high) co-occur with different cognitive strategies, likely mediated via shifts in attentional allocation, which may influence the ”weight” that organisms assign to costs compared to benefits (during valuation and decision-making) or prediction error signals (during learning). On the other hand, arousal states also track with changes in the behavioral intensity during action preparation and effort exertion, reflecting modulation of the physical energy mobilized for goal pursuit. Together, these processes suggest that arousal modulates both the organism’s cognitive willingness and physical capacity to pursue goals by shaping the readiness to direct attention to task-relevant information and mobilize energy towards actions.

A simple yet intuitive analogy that integrates these two observations is that arousal dynamics set a general policy for goal-directed behavior, such that different arousal states give rise to distinct, or even opposite, modes of goal pursuit. Specifically, high-arousal states may bias behavior toward maximization of reward despite higher effort costs, whereas low arousal states may primarily bias behavior toward minimizing effort even at the prospect of higher rewards. From this perspective, arousal systems operate at a meta-level that shapes learning, decision-making, and invigoration policies that are, themselves, influenced by multiple interacting neuromodulatory systems [26, 89–92]. Put differently, NA-mediated arousal may control the amount of “push” that organisms can exert, thereby acting as a calibration mechanism for value systems that direct behavior (“pull”) towards goal attainment. Arousal systems may, thus, have a fundamental role in shaping the readiness to engage in goal-directed behavior in a manner that is complementary to, and in direct interaction with, the more well-established motivational computations attributed to dopamine, also see [56, 93], highlighting the need to formally incorporate arousal into our existing frameworks of motivated behavior (Fig. 1).

This conceptualization of arousal as a critical driver of motivated behavior is also directly relevant to advancing our understanding of motivational deficits in psychiatric disorders, as it may elucidate mechanisms that are broadly consistent with, and potentially responsible for, the impairments in goal-directed behavior that people with MDD and SZ exhibit. We, therefore, next review evidence suggesting the presence of dysfunctional arousal dynamics in people with MDD and SZ, followed by a discussion of (limited) work linking these impairments to distinct subcomponents of motivated behavior.

Converging evidence for dysfunctional arousal dynamics in clinical samples

Substantial evidence indicates that individuals with MDD and SZ who experience motivational deficits show impairments in (LC-mediated) arousal dynamics. Here, we provide a concise summary of the most important lines of evidence:

Self-report

Abundant research suggests altered functionality of arousal systems in psychotic and mood disorders. In qualitative studies, adolescents and adults with lived experience of MDD describe difficulties generating the energy required to engage in daily tasks and activities [94–96]. Specifically, individuals with MDD and SZ can perceive goal-directed activities as being extremely effortful or unattainable, creating a barrier to engaging in meaningful activities [94, 95]. Perceptions of effort can be magnified by related symptoms, including fatigue, which is commonly experienced by individuals with MDD and SZ. Fatigue can worsen mood and depressive symptoms, further diminishing energy levels and creating a cycle that worsens functioning [96]. Low energy also results in indecisiveness (a core symptom of MDD in the DSM-5), even for everyday low-stakes decisions, such as deciding what to wear [95]. Similar themes are encountered in the SZ literature, where individuals describe motivational difficulties, independent of low mood and anhedonia, that arise from fatigue, anergia, and an inability to feel energized to pursue goals [97].

Clinical reports

These qualitative reports are complemented by clinical symptom interviews and questionnaires that highlight the high prevalence of lack of energy and fatigue in individuals with MDD and SZ. Fatigue, an extreme lack of energy and tiredness that interferes with engagement in everyday activities, is considered a core symptom of depression [98], which, in some samples, can be observed in over 90% of patients [99]. Fatigue moreover differentiates depressed from non-depressed individuals [100], further underlining its key relevance to depression. Similarly, people with SZ spectrum disorders are characterized by higher scores on measures of physical and cognitive fatigue when compared to healthy controls [101], which can worsen motivational impairment [7]. Notably, while fatigue cannot be used interchangeably for arousal, it might be a downstream behavioral consequence of a dysregulated arousal system, as it is a key driver of invigoration, effort expenditure, and persistence. Consistent with this, ecological assessments show significantly reduced physical activity and engagement in goal-directed activities among individuals with SZ and MDD in daily life, also translating to reduced physical and cognitive effort expenditure in laboratory assessments [102].

(Psycho)physiology

Readouts of different physiological arousal markers, including electrodermal activity, heart rate variability, and pupil dilation, converge on the idea that individuals with MDD and SZ exhibit higher levels of baseline arousal in combination with a marked reduction in the dynamic modulation of arousal. Basal heart rate measures are commonly elevated in individuals with MDD [103, 104], while heart rate variability (HRV) seems to be decreased [103], with fewer heart rate changes in response to external stimuli [104]. Similar results have been observed in SZ: at baseline, people with SZ show higher heart rates compared to controls [105], but HRV is typically lower than in healthy controls [106, 107], suggesting parasympathetic hypo-activity and sympathetic dominance [105, 108]. Valkonen et al. [109] reported no changes in HRV during a cognitive task in psychotic individuals and argued that this reflects an inability of arousal-mediating systems to respond to external demands. Similarly, in SZ, pupillometry studies reported increased baseline pupil dilation [110], coupled with reduced phasic pupil size responsivity to light stimuli [110, 111], and during cognitive engagement [28, 112]. In MDD, more stable pupil dilation in combination with reduced responsivity to external events and stimuli has also been reported [113, 114], the latter in the context of reward anticipation [30, 115] and expectancy violation [116, 117]. Furthermore, studies measuring electrodermal activity have shown blunted skin conductance responses in depressed patients compared to healthy controls [104, 118, 119]. Another proxy of abnormal arousal (modulation) is EEG hyperstability, which has been observed in depressed individuals and is thought to represent higher tonic activity of arousal systems that are seemingly independent of external influences [120–122]. Importantly, all these measures only represent indirect and/or partial readouts of arousal, likely influenced by additional factors. However, because arousal as a latent construct cannot be measured directly, these measures, alone or in combination, provide the best estimate of hidden dynamic arousal processes. Combined, the emerging pattern of results suggests that both hypo- and hyperarousal states may render individuals unresponsive to relevant external cues, potentially through blunted phasic reactivity of the LC-NA system, thereby affecting their engagement in goal-directed behavior.

Neural assessments

In post-mortem studies, examination of brain tissue of deceased individuals with MDD revealed reduced LC levels of NA and its transporter protein [123, 124]. In SZ, increased LC cell volumes have been reported [125], although the evidence on morphological LC changes remains inconclusive [126].

While imaging deep brain structures such as the LC is challenging, functional imaging studies suggest altered LC activity in SZ. Individuals with SZ, compared to controls, show reduced LC activation during cognitive conflict resolution [127] and greater cognitive load-dependent LC activity increases that, in turn, may drive greater increases in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) activity [128]. The authors suggest that abnormally high LC activation could diminish task performance as it may desynchronize neural processes that subserve focused attention [128].

Similar findings emerged from a functional connectivity study in MDD where, over a two-year period, LC connections to same-hemisphere cortical regions involved in sensory processing, attentional processes, and executive functions were decreased, while connections across hemispheres were increased, potentially as a compensatory mechanism [129]. Together with the finding of increased metabolic connectivity between LC and PFC [130], these results suggest disruptions in the normal connectivity pattern of arousal-modulating brainstem nuclei in MDD and SZ.

Pharmacological manipulations of catecholamine levels further suggest an involvement of the LC-NA system in MDD and SZ symptomatology. Depletion of NA and dopamine in individuals with remitted depression led to transient re-occurrence of depressive symptoms with significant increases in loss of interest and loss of energy [131] while pharmacological agents that increase available NA have been reported to exert some beneficial effects on anhedonia [132]. Nevertheless, this evidence remains limited, and the relative contribution of noradrenergic effects (i.e., as the focus of the present account) over the effects of dopaminergic or other arousal-modulating compounds must still be resolved.

Pharmacological manipulations of NA levels have also been explored for the treatment of negative symptoms based on NA’s tentative role in motivational processes. However, despite theoretical support, clinical trials in patients with MDD and SZ have yielded inconsistent results [133, 134], warranting further exploration of the potential utility of NA-manipulating agents in clinical settings.

Taken together, converging lines of evidence suggest that MDD and SZ are associated with altered baseline arousal and a lack of dynamic responsivity to salient external stimuli. These impairments in phasic responsiveness to relevant cues specifically may be a key contributor to motivational deficits manifesting as fatigue, a lack of “readiness”, and blunted physiological reactivity in individuals with MDD and SZ. We next review how the above-described dysfunction in arousal dynamics contributes to impairments in distinct (sub)components of motivated behavior in this population.

Dysregulated arousal contributes to reduced motivated behavior in clinical samples

A large body of evidence suggests that individuals with SZ and MDD exhibit robust deficits in many subcomponents of motivated behavior. While it is beyond the scope of the current paper to provide an in-depth review of this evidence, previous case-control studies (i.e., people with psychotic or mood disorders versus control participants without a history of psychiatric illness) have demonstrated deficits on cognitive tasks that assess reward anticipation [135–137], decision-making [138–143], and learning [10, 144–149]. Most consistently, these studies converge on the idea that motivational deficits are associated with a fundamental problem in accurately representing the value of actions [10, 11, 150, 151]; a problem that may arise via alterations in reinforcement learning (e.g., signalling and use of prediction errors to update choice preferences [144, 145, 147, 152]) and cost-benefit integration (e.g., due to increased effort aversion and/or reduced reward sensitivity [140, 141, 143]). Surprisingly, however, only a minority of studies have collected data to measure arousal dynamics during these tasks. Here, we summarize this emerging literature by each distinct (sub)component of motivated behavior (see Box 1).

Prior to engaging in goal-directed behavior, individuals must first evaluate the costs and benefits of an action, and cognitively compute a “net” action value, to decide which rewards are “worth” one’s effort [153]. While there is, to our knowledge, no study to date that has investigated arousal dynamics in MDD or SZ during this stage of the decision-making process, previous studies have reported reduced pupil dilation during attention allocation in people with MDD and SZ [112, 154–156]. This is noteworthy because abnormal attentional allocation toward relevant cues can impair the ability to appropriately evaluate effort and reward value to guide decision-making [29].

Secondly, during reward anticipation, individuals with higher depressive symptom loads exhibit reduced pupil dilation [30, 115]. FMRI studies suggest that this is accompanied by reduced activity in the salience network (i.e., insula) [30, 115] and reduced suppression of default mode network activity (i.e., elevated activity in the posterior cingulate cortex) [115], suggesting that a failure to upregulate arousal can impact neural representations of reward anticipation. One possibility is that these reduced contributions of arousal to decision-making and reward anticipation ultimately result in the mobilization of fewer resources to obtain (anticipated) rewards. Indeed, SZ, compared to control participants, show marked reductions in pupil dilation before and during effort allocation, which correlates with negative symptom load and defeatist performance beliefs [28, 29, 157]. These studies suggest that dysregulated arousal dynamics, reflected in altered physiological and neural responses, may contribute to reduced effort expenditure during goal pursuit.

Finally, attention toward surprising and novel information is essential for learning and accurate updating of value estimates for future decisions. This process may also be affected by dysregulated arousal in individuals with motivational impairments. Specifically, depressive symptom load has been shown to negatively correlate with pupil responses to reward prediction errors [117]. Further, a recent proof-of-concept study demonstrated that, among healthy individuals, elevated depressive symptoms related to blunted pupillary responses to belief-disconfirming, positive information, and reduced updating of existing beliefs based on the novel information [116].

Combined, these studies suggest that dysfunctional arousal dynamics may negatively contribute to various subcomponents of motivated behavior, yet the currently available evidence is largely associative and cross-sectional. Further work is needed to replicate and extend these findings. Moreover, direct manipulations of arousal-mediating systems, as well as longitudinal designs in clinical populations, are needed to clarify causal pathways. In the section below, we therefore propose future designs that could be integral to better understand the contribution of arousal dynamics to motivational dysfunction.

Future directions & research priorities

The evidence reviewed above suggests that individuals with MDD and SZ can exhibit robust signs of abnormal arousal dynamics, specifically, phasic reactivity. Moreover, a small number of studies that combined arousal measures with cognitive tasks and/or fMRI tentatively suggest that this absence of phasic reactivity is associated with reduced goal-directed behavior and associated neural activation (e.g., in key hubs of the brain-wide “valuation” network). While preliminary, these results suggest the possibility that an inability to (phasically) upregulate arousal in the service of goal pursuit (e.g., during decision-making, learning, and/or effort mobilization) may be a critical driver of motivational deficits in MDD and SZ. While most frameworks emphasize dysregulation in the reward circuitry to drive abnormal learning and decision-making [139, 158, 159], it has been pointed out that this perspective may be incomplete [153]. The emerging results reviewed here offer valuable new leads on the pathophysiology of negative symptoms, as they may point to a mechanism – reduced arousal dynamics, potentially LC- and noradrenaline-mediated [20, 36] – that is compatible with, and complementary to, the more dominant and well-established role of dopamine-mediated “reward-system” dysfunction.

While the research we reviewed above supports the notion that motivational deficits in MDD and SZ may, in part, stem from dysregulated arousal systems, it is important to note that causal evidence for this mechanistic influence of arousal disturbances on motivational processes, at a subcomponent level (Box 1), is missing in humans. Existing studies have assessed indirect and neurochemically ambiguous measures of arousal (e.g., pupil size) or focused exclusively on certain subcomponents of motivated behavior. As such, many questions of mechanistic specificity remain to be resolved, including whether arousal impacts single, multiple, or all subcomponents of motivated behavior simultaneously, and, if present, whether such effects represent direct effects of arousal on these subcomponents or are mediated via intermediary processes like attentional allocation. In particular, while arousal-related effects on certain aspects of value computation (e.g., effort cost [52]) are well-characterized, its influence on other sub-components of valuation and decision-making is currently under-explored. In light of these considerations, we propose several avenues for future research to more comprehensively, and perhaps causally, elucidate the role of arousal dysfunction in motivational impairments. An expanding body of work would also allow for rigorous analyses of bias and effect sizes that go beyond the narrative synthesis of findings we present here. Our intention is to offer a conceptual scaffold within which future empirical work could start to systematically disentangle mechanistic pathways that contribute to dysfunctional motivated behavior. Importantly, within this framework, dysregulations across the full arousal spectrum may need to be taken into account, as hypo- and hyperarousal can differentially constrain the capacity to meet environmental demands, leading to distinct alterations in goal-directed behavior.

Against this backdrop, a first recommended priority of future work is the more systematic integration of arousal physiology into experimental paradigms that probe distinct (computational) subcomponents of motivated behavior. Despite its complex neurochemical origins, pupillometry, in particular, offers an easily accessible, non-invasive tool to indirectly, but continuously, measure arousal dynamics in a laboratory setting. To address the specificity-related limitations of pupillometry, eye-tracking may be combined with other arousal measurements (e.g., skin conductance) to establish convergent validity among these proximal measures. Few studies to date have integrated pupillometry, or other continuous measures of arousal, into cognitive tasks that explicitly dissociate multiple stages of motivated behavior in MDD and SZ. In contrast to existing research, which has largely focused on these subcomponents in isolation, this more integrative approach could shed light on which processes are most impaired and could help disentangle complex temporal associations between arousal dysfunction and motivated behavior. Importantly, such designs can also help disentangle arousal-driven deficits in cognitive processes versus physical resource mobilization, to the degree that such deficits may exist independently of each other. We, therefore, propose that current pupillometry studies should be extended to cover all stages of motivated behavior - from attention allocation and cue processing to effort allocation and outcome processing - to map arousal dynamics onto specific deficits in motivated behavior in MDD and SZ (see Fig. 1).

Second, we suggest that neuroimaging of functional brain networks involved in arousal regulation and decision-making (e.g., those encompassing LC, dopaminergic midbrain nuclei, striatal subdivisions, insula, ACC, and vmPFC) should be more formally integrated into fMRI studies of motivational deficits in MDD and SZ. Imaging the LC with conventional fMRI techniques can be challenging due to its location and size, but these challenges can at least in part be overcome by informed scanner calibrations and ROI selection, and thorough preprocessing of noisy data [160]. In addition, promising new MRI sequences suitable for imaging midbrain regions have emerged in recent years, which enable more reliable assessments of LC structure and function [161]. Such studies may wish to additionally incorporate information about the local expression of key neuromodulatory systems (e.g., NA or dopamine) using positron emission tomography data to understand the potential neurochemical drivers of fMRI-based activation [162, 163]. Ultimately, cognitive tasks, functional imaging of brainstem neuromodulatory regions, and pupillometry could be combined to create a comprehensive understanding of the neurobiological basis of motivational impairments in psychiatric populations.

Third, arousal manipulation studies are necessary to prove a causal relationship between arousal dysfunction and motivational impairments. Selective pharmacological manipulations of arousal-mediating systems will be essential to understanding their role in distinct subcomponents of motivated behavior and/or regulation of large-scale networks (e.g., through enhanced network integration) that subserve this behavior. Here, it will also be critical to disentangle, for example, noradrenergic contributions from influences of other systems, including dopamine, acetylcholine, serotonin, or adenosine, which may independently or interactively modulate motivated behavior. Contrasting different pharmacological manipulations or using multimodal neuroimaging approaches with information about receptor expression could be important next steps to establish the specific contributions of various neurochemical systems to arousal and motivated behavior. In addition, emerging non-pharmacological neuromodulation techniques, such as transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS), bear the potential to modulate arousal and influence decision-making processes [87, 88]. Future studies should investigate the neuromodulatory and potential therapeutic effects of such non-invasive techniques in clinical populations.

Fourth, researchers should be encouraged to move beyond the confines of the laboratory. To develop a fine-grained understanding, future work should incorporate ecologically valid approaches such as combining wearable devises that continuously capture physiological proxies of arousal with ecological momentary assessments (EMA) of motivation in real-world contexts. Although self-reported measures are susceptible to bias, they provide a critical window into subjective and contextual influences on arousal disturbances in individuals with motivational impairments, which can be an important step toward the development of novel interventions. At the same time, these designs introduce additional sources of heterogeneity and confounding, such as medication use, substance intake, sleep patterns, and illness severity, that must be carefully considered and accounted for.

Finally, if arousal dysfunction proves to be a central driver of motivational impairments, a logical next step will be to determine whether this mechanism is disorder-specific versus transdiagnostic. The current review focused on mood and psychotic disorders, given the preliminary evidence and likelihood of arousal dysfunction in these conditions. However, prominent mechanistic frameworks in this field (e.g., RDoC [164]) emphasize the cross-cutting nature of mechanisms across diagnostic boundaries. Thus, it will be important for future work to expand to neurological and other psychiatric conditions characterized by motivational deficits (e.g., Parkinson’s Disease, Bipolar Disorder).

Together, these research paths will further our mechanistic understanding of motivational deficits in psychopathological conditions like MDD and SZ. Conceptualizing the role of dysregulated arousal systems in the emergence and maintenance of disturbed motivation might open new avenues for the development of targeted interventions that restore normal functioning and improve the quality of life in affected individuals.
