Severe Metformin‐Associated Lactic Acidosis—A Case Report
Authors: Elizabet Artinyan, Georgi Nikolov, Evelina Valcheva, Prajwol Baniya, Danika Sritharan, Adeel Khan, Marina Vaysilova, Nikolay Dimov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetformin, a widely prescribed and generally safe drug for diabetes mellitus, can rarely lead to metformin‐associated lactic acidosis—a severe, potentially fatal complication. Early recognition, emergency hemodialysis, and supportive management are crucial for favorable outcomes, highlighting the importance of prompt diagnosis and intervention to reduce mortality in this critical condition.Keywords: AKI, hemodialysis, lactic acidosis, metformin

Introduction

Metformin—a complex drug with multiple sites of action and several molecular mechanisms—remains the first‐line oral agent for type 2 diabetes mellitus. Although metformin is well‐known for being safe and effective, it can also have major adverse effects, such as metformin‐associated lactic acidosis (MALA). MALA is defined by a blood pH < 7.35 coupled with lactate > 5.0 mmol/L [1]. The total mortality rate is around 36.2%, and the frequency of MALA is predicted to be between 2 and 47 cases per 100,000 metformin‐treated patients [2, 3]. We present a rare case of a patient with life‐threatening MALA, whose expeditious diagnosis and treatment lead to full recovery.

Case History/Examination

An 82‐year‐old male patient was admitted to the Emergency department (ED) in an extremely critical condition. According to his relatives, he complained of fatigue, nausea, and decreased oral food and fluid intake for several days. On the days that followed, he continued taking his regular medications, and while the symptoms persisted, he became hypotensive and subsequently became difficult to communicate with. On physical examination he presented with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 5, pale skin, dry tongue and mucosal membranes. His blood pressure was 70/32 mmHg and an oxygen saturation of 88% with Kussmaul breathing. The remaining physical findings were unremarkable. The patient has the following comorbidities: arterial hypertension, chronic heart failure class II‐III NYHA, permanent atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes mellitus, CKD G3a (baseline creatinine of 126–127 μmol/L and eGFR of 49 mL/min/1.73 m2) and surgical resection for colon carcinoma (with colostomy placement), complicated by hepatic metastases. His systemic outpatient therapy consisted of valsartan 160 mg bid, amlodipine 10 mg bid, metoprolol 50 mg bid, torasemide 10 mg q.d., rivaroxaban 20 mg q.d., and metformin 850 mg tid.

Initial laboratory results in the emergency department (Table 1) revealed anemia with hemoglobin levels measured at 82 g/L, serum creatinine of 890 μmol/L, urea of 59.4 mmol/L, severe hyperkalaemia at 9.2 mmol/L, and CRP of 43 mg/L. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated profound metabolic acidosis with a pH of 6.64, critically low bicarbonate (2 mmol/L), hypoxaemia, and markedly elevated lactate (10.7 mmol/L). ECG showed irregular heart rhythm with widened QRS complexes and peaked T‐waves (Figure 1). Abdominal ultrasonography was unremarkable; however, a chest X‐ray identified congestive changes, with possible superimposed inflammatory infiltrates at the left lung base.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The preliminary diagnosis was prerenal acute kidney injury (AKI) stage 3 per KDIGO criteria with severe lactic acidosis and hypovolemic shock. Active fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, oxygen supplementation, antibiotic therapy, and administration of PRBCs were initiated and continued during the following days. Within the next few hours an emergency intermittent hemodialysis procedure was started, with the following parameters: 3 h session length, an FX80 membrane (surface area of 1.8m2), a blood flow rate of 250 mL/min, a bicarbonate‐based dialysate composition was used, containing bicarbonate 32 mmol/L, sodium 138 mmol/L (corrected to 140 mmol/L), potassium 2 mmol/L, calcium 1.75 mmol/L, glucose 5 mmol/L, along with the use of cooler dialysate. The following day the patient was transferred to the nephrology clinic for further management where 4 h dialysis sessions continued with the same parameters. Considering the use of metformin and lactic acidosis, we performed a toxicological analysis (~24–26 h after the patients admission in the ED). Despite, two hemodialysis session were performed a result of 16 mg/L was obtained, well above the toxic threshold of 5–10 mg/L, confirming MALA.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Following the subsequent several days of therapy, the patient's condition improved with stabilization of hemodynamics, cessation of vasopressors, and improved laboratory results (Table 1). After three hemodialysis sessions, the pH level reached 7.3, allowing hemodialysis to be discontinued. The patient was discharged in a stable condition with comprehensive follow‐up care.

Discussion

Metformin is the most commonly prescribed antihyperglycemic medication worldwide and remains the foundation of type 2 diabetes treatment according to current guidelines. Beside diabetes, metformin is also used to address insulin resistance in conditions like weight management, prediabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, and gestational diabetes. Despite its relative safety and efficacy, 8.4 per 100,000 people develop toxicity [4] Metformin could be associated with certain adverse reactions, such as encephalopathy (2.5%), hepatitis/liver failure (2.1%), hypoglycaemia (1.6%), psychosis, B12 deficiency, or pancreatitis (0.4%) [5] Metformin‐associated lactic acidosis is the most serious and potentially fatal adverse effect, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 3–10 cases/100,000 patients [6] Earlier data showed mortality rates as high as 50% in the 1960s–2000, but more recent studies indicate a decline to approximately 25%–33% due to improved recognition and treatment [7].

In general, lactic acidosis is characterized by a pH below 7.35, blood lactate levels greater than 2.0 mmol/L, and a PaCO2 under 42 mmHg. There are two main types of lactic acidosis: Type A and Type B (including B2 lactic acidosis induced by metformin). Three well‐recognized types of lactic acidosis related to metformin have been described, based on the interplay between metformin exposure, its accumulation, and contributory illnesses (Table 2) [8]. Based on our findings, the diagnosis of our patient is consistent with MALA. Although metformin levels were measured after two dialysis sessions, they remained elevated well above the toxic range. Metformin causes MALA by disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism as well as increasing lactate production and decreasing its clearance, leading to higher cellular lactate levels. Intracellular redox potential then shifts from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism. A decrease in pyruvate carboxylase activity, the rate‐limiting enzyme in the formation of glucose from lactate, can also decrease hepatic metabolism of lactate, especially in the context of AKI or other conditions like sepsis, hypovolemia, or cardiac insufficiency [1, 9].

The main risk factors of metformin toxicity are advanced age, metformin > 2000 mg/day, tissue hypoxia (sepsis, shock, hypovolemia, heart failure), dysregulated lactate metabolism (alcohol abuse, liver failure), and reduced metformin clearance (AKI and CKD) [10] Patients with impaired renal function are known to have a higher likelihood of developing MALA due to reduced clearance of metformin, as well as impaired lactate metabolism [11] With a decrease in eGFR, the risk of lactic acidosis gradually increases. Older patients often have impaired renal function or other comorbidities that predispose them to MALA, making age an indirect but significant risk factor [12] In our patient, due to the presence of liver metastases, there is a potential decrease in liver function and, as a result, a higher risk of lactate accumulation. Additionally, we speculate that a rare metabolic phenomenon—the so‐called Warburg effect (the ability of tumor cells to rely on anaerobic respiration for ATP production despite sufficient oxygenation, which alters tumor metabolism and microenvironment, sometimes leading to lactic acidosis)—theoretically could play a contributing role. These factors, combined with continued metformin use during his illness, culminated in this life‐threatening MALA episode [13].

Several prognostic indicators for MALA have been identified. Factors associated with high mortality rate include referral to the ICU for shock, heightened level of disease severity on admission (SAPSII, 75 ± 23; APACHE III score, 142.3 ± 26.0), prothrombin activity < 50%, male sex [14, 15]. In contrast, early dialysis (typically within 6 h of presentation) and a lower APACHE II score are associated with decreased 30‐day mortality [16]. It is still debatable whether severe acidosis and a high peak lactate level lead to a higher mortality rate. In general, AKI with any type of metabolic acidosis in critical care settings has a very high mortality (37.5%) [17]. Lactic acidosis has a higher mortality rate compared to high anion gap metabolic acidosis with normal lactate or anion gap metabolic acidosis [17]. According to a study by Abdulkader et al., the median intensive care unit and hospital stay were higher in the lactic acidosis group compared to the high anion gap metabolic acidosis group and the group with normal anion gap [17]. Siangtrong et al.'s study found that arterial pH of less than 7.0 is strongly linked to increased mortality [18]. Lactate levels above 20 mmol/L have been significantly associated with mortality [19]. However, a systematic review of 170 MALA cases found that neither arterial pH nor lactate levels were good predictors of mortality [3]. The higher serum level of metformin does not appear to be prognostically predictive. Even patients with levels of 678 μg/mL have reportedly survived [20]. In our patient most of the poor prognostic factors were exhibited, including admission to the ICU due to shock, a high SAPS II score (average 75 ± 23) with our patient scoring 85 points, which corresponds to a 95% chance of in‐hospital mortality, prothrombin activity below 50%, and an arterial pH of 6.64.

Once MALA is suspected or diagnosed, immediate steps include cessation of metformin. Treatment for mild cases of MALA typically includes supportive care and bicarbonate infusion when the pH is less than 7.2. In severe cases, extracorporeal treatment (ECTR) plays an important role to remove metformin and correct acidosis [21]. Metformin is a small molecule (129 Da) and unbound to protein, which makes it suitable for dialysis. However, its relatively large volume of distribution (1–5 L/kg) makes its dialyzability uncertain [22]. The currently recommended indications of ECTR include lactate levels above 20 mmol/L, pH less than 7.0, presence of shock, reduced consciousness, or failure of standard supportive measures [23]. Intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) has been recommended in patients with metformin toxicity in stable condition, being more widely available and cost‐effective [9]. Alternatively, if IHD is not available, or if the patient is haemodynamically unstable, then continuous kidney replacement therapy may be used [6]. However, faster lactate clearance is associated with improved survival in critically ill patients [24]. Typically, high‐efficiency hemodialysis provides metformin clearance that surpasses 120 mL/min and a metformin half‐life of approximately 4 h [22]. In comparison, metformin clearance with continuous renal replacement therapy is usually about one‐fourth of that obtained with HD, and the half‐life is at least three times as long [22]. Additionally, lactate clearance may also be improved by using higher effluent rates and high‐flow/high‐efficiency dialyzers [5]. In our patient's case, given the presence of extremely severe lactic acidosis characterized by a critically low pH of 6.64, we chose to perform IHD (with high‐efficiency dialyzers) while on vasopressors, with the aim of achieving faster lactate clearance. A total of three hemodialysis sessions were needed for our patient to achieve a pH > 7.3. Hemodialysis was then discontinued. Continuous monitoring after ceasing ECTR is essential to detect any rebound of lactic acidosis, as metformin may redistribute from tissues back into the bloodstream [23]. When IHD is used, one study showed that a cumulative duration of 15 h was associated with normalization of metformin levels within the therapeutic range [25]. The current recommendations suggest discontinuing ECTR when serum lactate levels fall below 3 mmol/L and the pH rises above 7.35 [23].

Conclusion

MALA represents a significant challenge for clinicians both diagnostically and therapeutically. Prompt diagnosis, along with appropriate treatment strategies, is essential for a positive outcome in this rare, life‐threatening condition.
