Evaluation of pulp stone between a sub-group of Iraqi population using CBCT radiographs: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Tara Rasool Hussein AL-Barazanchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulp stones (PS) or denticles are a type of calcification foci that are found in the coronal pulp or, less frequently, radicular pulp cavity. The appearance of PS might hinder endodontic treatments in some cases.ObjectiveTo examine the distribution of PS in a subgroup of the Iraqi population and to detect the factors that induce this condition among patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted retrospectively at the FOTON Maxillofacial Imaging Centre, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2020 to December 2025, using participants’ data from the imaging centre’s database. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images of 6147 teeth of 250 participants (125 males and 125 females) aged 15 to 56 years and older were evaluated. PS were considered when separate radiopaque masses were found in the pulp cavity; the association of PS with patients’ age, gender, and dental factors was then estimated.ResultsPS were identified in 33.6% of participants and 2.9% of teeth. After adjustment for age and gender, female was identified as a significant independent predictor of PS occurrence at the tooth level only (p = 0.004). In contrast, age group was not significantly associated with PS prevalence at either the patient or tooth level. More PS was found in molars and the maxilla, specifically in restored and carious teeth.ConclusionsNearly one-third of the Iraqi subgroup had PS, frequently in the molars, maxilla, and non-intact teeth. Female sex was identified as an independent predictor of PS occurrence. These findings suggest that dental factors and gender are more important factors for developing PS than chronological aging.

Introduction

Dental pulp is a connective tissue in the inner part of a tooth that contains nerves, blood vessels, pulp cells and odontoblasts, which provides nutrition, sensation and dentine to the tooth. It is clear even after tooth formation; however, odontoblasts have capacity to form secondary or tertiary dentine, such as in the case of aging or trauma respectively [1]. Sometimes odontoblasts produce unusual dentine matrix inside pulp tissue around which calcification may arise [2]. This condition within the dental pulp known as pulp stones (PS) that may appear within pulp chamber or radicular pulp. PS are different in form, structure and chemical composition [3] that may occur in a number of teeth of the same individual [4]. Though PS is an idiopathic condition, but it has been related to a number of factors, including oral and systematic factors [5]. Examples of the factors linked to oral cavity are periodontal disease, persistent irritation of the teeth, and dental trauma [6]. While, systematic factors shown to be diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and systemic stone [7, 8].

The incidence of PS diverse greatly between populations that might be associated with the hereditary predisposition, age [9], and gender [10]. Most of the studies investigated PS via the use of traditional two-dimensional (2D) radiograph or cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). However, CBCT is the preferred imaging method because its superior properties reduce the restrictions of traditional 2D radiograph [11]. CBCT has less distortion, high resolution, and good quality of hard tissue imaging. Within CBCT, images of pulp tissue can be viewed through three plains namely sagittal, coronal and axial, which in turn contributes to more accurate radiograph for recognising PS [12]. PS is classified according to their structure into true, false and diffuse calcification. True PS is made up of localized mass of calcified tissue that resemble dentin due to their tubular nature, while false one has no tubules, and diffuse type seems to be calcific deposit [13]. When PS appear in any part of the pulp tissue, there is no need of any intervention, because it does not impact on the health of the tooth [14]. However, PS may obstacle root canal treatment when required, as it mostly block the pathway of the pulp for proper cleaning and restoring the root canal. Consequently, PS may lead to failure of root canal treatment and then loss of the tooth [15]. Thus, it is very important to find the association between the occurrence of PS and patients’ factors, such as age, gender, location of the tooth, availability of decay, or dental restorations. Unfortunately, up-to-date, in Iraq, there is no published data on this aspect to aware peoples with this condition. Therefore, this study, used CBCT to perceive the popularity of PS in a sub-group of Iraqi population, and to reveal the association of PS with some local oral factors, and to inform clinical practice about the possible complications that may arise during endodontic therapy due to the presence of PS.

Methodology

Participants

The sample size was considered through G*Power (version 3.1.9.4), using an effective size of w = 0.30, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and the maximum d.f of 5. Then, the calculated results were around 250 participants (125 males and 125 females). Thus, this cross-sectional study was done retrospectively, from January 2020 to December 2025, using the participants’ data at the database of the FOTON Maxillofacial Imaging Center, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, including 250 participants and 6147 teeth.

Inclusion criteria

Participants aged ≥ 15 years old with Iraqi nationality, that diagnosed to had PS and undertook dental treatment for various reasons, such as dental implants, missed canals, and oral surgery.

Exclusion criteria

Images that were distorted or unclear, teeth with crowns or posts, and those that had endodontic therapy, deep carious or root resorption, and restorations that covered the pulp chamber.

Study protocol

The data collection process included patient’s gender and age, together with identifying and recording the PS (presence or absence), and each of teeth’s location (right or left), group (anterior, premolars, and molars), condition (caries or restoration or periodontal lesion) and position (maxillary or mandibular). For this purpose, sequences of CBCT images of patients’ teeth were investigated in axial, sagittal, and coronal sights of both root canals and pulp chambers, in a coronal to apical direction, to identify existence of PS using the Carestream Dental CS9600, Atlanta, GA, USA, at 10 × 10 cm field view, 120 kVp exposure, 6.3 mA for 20 s, with 1147 MGY.cm2 dose and resolution of 150 microns. DICOM data were accessed, and analyzed using the CS3D 8.0.32 Imaging Viewer software (Carestream Dental, Atlanta, GA, USA), on a Lenovo Legion 5 laptop (Quarry Bay, Hong Kong). To evade oversights, the planes of CBCT was observed layer by layer, with the sagittal plane from buccal to lingual, the coronal plane from distal to mesial, and the axial plane from coronal to apical. The diagnosis of a PS was established when a radiopaque mass was revealed in the pulpal space (both the crown and root) (Figs. 1 and 2). Also, when there was a high-density filling shadow that exceeded the enamel layer in the image or a transmission shadow, the tooth was either filled or affected by caries that destroyed the dentin, in this case, the tooth state was documented as non-intact (Fig. 3). Finally, the obtained results were examined to determine the association between PS and each of patients’ age, gender, and dental factors (tooth condition, group, location, and position).

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Science (IBM, Chicago, USA, version 27) was used for the analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages. A univariate analysis (Chi-square test) was used to correlate the appearance of PS with each of gender, age, tooth’s’ condition, location, position, and group. To address potential demographic confounding and isolate true risk indicators, a multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed using the patient and tooth level pool (n = 250 and 6,147 respectively) to calculate Adjusted Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) for baseline demographic predictors. The presence (1) or absence (0) of a PS served as the binary dependent variable, while the stratified age groups and gender were entered simultaneously as independent covariates. The youngest cohort (15–35 years) and the male demographic served as the reference categories. The statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Among 250 studied patients, 84 (33.6%) were found to have one or more PS in the examined CBCT, which include 37 (29.6%) males and 47 females (37.6%), with no significant association (p = 0.181) (Table 1). Whereas, PS were significantly higher in females (n = 108, 3.6%) when number of teeth were examined for PS (p = 0.004) (Table 2). When adjusting for age simultaneously, gender emerged as a powerful, significant independent predictor of PS presence at tooth level only (p = 0.004). Females demonstrated a significantly higher probability of developing pulpal calcifications compared to the male reference cohort, exhibiting an adjusted OR of 0.641 (95% CI = 0.474–0.868). Conversely, chronological age group did not display an independent predictive effect on PS prevalence. With the youngest cohort (15–35 years) serving as the baseline reference, the risk did not increase significantly in either the 36–55 cohort or the > 55 cohort on both patient and tooth levels (Tables 3 and 4). This demonstrates that when controlling for gender, advanced aging does not act as an independent driver of pulpal calcification within this study population.

There was a significant association between PS in the teeth (n = 180) and the teeth that were not intact due to restoration, caries, periodontal lesion or combination of two of them (p = 0.000). Discussion

Research implies that the developmental pattern of PS could be similar to that of atrophic calcification. Certain constituents of the dental pulp, such as damaged blood vessels, collagen fibres, and necrotic cell remnants might undergo primary calcification [16]. This may be related to age or local pathological alterations in the pulp tissue. Consequently, on the surface of the unevenly calcified core, the material is progressively accumulated in concentric or radial rings, in which the fibre and collagen bundles in the pulp serve as structural scaffolds. The most outstanding feature of the PS is their lamellar structure, which is reinforced by concentrically covered calcifications in several oval and spherical PS in histological studies [17]. Studies have noted that CBCT has a greater validity and reliability for the identification of tooth conditions [18, 19]; thus, it has superior accuracy and specificity, surmounts superimposing limitations, and is a preferable technique for identifying PS than other radiography [20].

According to preceding studies, the frequency of PS in different areas is distinct and influenced by numerous factors to differing extents, but there are no reports in Iraq, especially in this locality. Thus, this study executed a clinical examination of CBCT images to determine the distribution of PS and to detect its association with several variables. Accordingly, this study, found that 33.6% of the patients and 2.9% of the teeth had PS. Some earlier CBCT-based studies have not attained the same conclusion analogous to these outcomes. In this regard, lower values were reported by other studies, such as Jahanimoghadam et al. [21] who reported PS of 13.4%; Alaajam et al. [22] found 14.7%, and Mirah et al. [15] observed 26% in individuals and 9.2% in teeth. Different outcomes in the population might be due to region, patients’ race, and inclusion criteria, such as including extra molars in the study. Whereas, a higher rate of PS than this study was observed by Kaabi et al. [23], who reported that 98.3% of patients and 52.1% of teeth had PS, with greater number of teeth with caries and fillings were included. Hsieh et al. [24] observed PS in 83.3% of patients, Tassoker et al. [25] demonstrated 52% of the individuals and 7.7% of the teeth, and Patil et al. [26] reported 50.93%. However, Sezgin et al. [27] and da Silva et al. [20] noted PS of 3.3% in teeth and 31.9% among participants, respectively, which is comparable to this study. Also, this finding falls within the broad global prevalence range (2 to > 45%) reported in the endodontic literature [21, 25]. Several authors have carried out histological analysis of PS using isolated tooth segments and shown that the true frequency rate was significantly greater within histological examination [28]. Though, this technique can only be performed in vitro and has some areas of concern. Another study reported that Artificial Intelligence (AI) significantly improves the specificity and sensitivity of dental pathology diagnosis compared to human observers [29]. In subsequent studies, more reliable data must be gathered from AI recordings.

Moreover, univariate analysis shown that the frequency of PS within teeth in females was significantly higher than in males, which is similar to the outcomes of another study [22]. This might be due to the hormonal fluctuations in women, while another study [4] indicated that bruxism is commonly observed in women as a long-standing chronic irritation, that has the potential to cause PS. In contrast to our outcomes, some studies [23, 26] have shown a higher occurrence of PS in males or no significant association between PS and gender [15, 24, 25]. Furthermore, no significant variation was detected between age groups in relations to the PS formation, which is similar to other studies [24, 30]. Nevertheless, several researchers have reported that PS are more predominant in the elderly [25, 31]. These disparities might be related to the number of individuals included in each age group.

By applying a multivariate binary logistic regression model, this study evaluated the concurrent impact of age and biological sex, addressing conflicting historical claims regarding the primary drivers of PS development. The regression analysis confirmed that biological sex is a powerful, independent predictor of pulpal calcification, with females exhibiting a significantly higher risk than males (p < 0.05). This strong female predilection aligns with recent 3D imaging studies [22, 26]. Conversely, multivariate analysis demonstrated that chronological age was not an independent predictor of PS presence (p > 0.05). Although older age cohorts experience cumulative exposure to oral and systemic stressors over time, the data indicates that advancing age alone did not cause PS formation. This finding is consistent with another studies demonstrating that PS rates typically stabilize or remain uniform across major adult cohorts [23, 24]. While physiologic aging induces specific pulpal changes, such as microvascular thickening, reduced endothelial cell volume, and general pulp chamber shrinkage [32]; as well as the formation of true nodular PS requires discrete and localized pathoses. Retaining structurally sound three-cohort age framework (15–35, 36–55, and 56 ≥ years) prevented mathematical instability in the model that would otherwise stem from sparse data distributions within smaller sample subgroups (e.g., n = 12 patients in the oldest cohort), thereby ensuring statistical validity [25].

In regards to dental factors, this study is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [12], signifying that maxilla contains a higher number of teeth with PS compared to the mandible. The unique vector distribution of occlusal forces across the wider anatomy of the maxillary arch can introduce persistent, low-grade mechanical stress. This chronic micro-trauma may disrupt the local pulpal vascular bed, initiating calcification pathways as a reactive tissue response [22, 23]. PS are frequent in the molars due to factors that facilitate calcification precipitation, such as complicated blood supply, high masticatory load, and larger size [16, 20]. Conversely, a different conclusion was reported for premolars and anterior teeth compared with former observations [20, 22].

Additionally, earlier studies have shown that the PS are more common in restored and carious teeth [15, 23, 26]. Alike conclusions were recorded in this study, in which restorations and caries could result in dental pulp inflammation, which triggers the pulp for a long term, causing PS formation [20]. Moreover, tooth preparation during the restoration procedure might cause damage to the blood vessels, and leakage around the filling materials can induce an inflammatory reaction in the pulp-dentin complex [27] that might contribute to the formation of PS. Berès et al. [33] documented greater magnitude of Zinc and Cupper on the PS surfaces compared to dentin, which can be contributed to the inflammatory situation of the dental pulp. Whereas, Demant et al. [34] recorded that in the late-stage of carious teeth an ectopic cartilage-like connective tissue will appear in the pulp cavity, which was hard to separate from PS on imaging. Also, some studies suggest a hierarchical spectrum of pulpal insult, typically proceeding from caries > restorations > periodontal lesions > intact/healthy teeth [1, 28, 35, 36]. This could have affected the outcomes in this study. Cumulatively, because restoration and caries are lengthy period methods, more longitudinal researches are essential to discover their link with PS.

Also, this study revealed no significant association in the distribution of PS between the right and left sides of the DA that could be clarified by the comparable functional stresses and progression patterns on both sides. A symmetrical distribution of PS is expected, because its formation is mainly effected by systemic factors. These findings are in agreement with a former record that shown PS occurred identically on both sides of DA [24]. From a clinical viewpoint, this indicates that PS must be expected similarly on both sides, and supporting the requirement for complete radiographic assessment.

In the current study, 4 patients had > 5 teeth with PS, and the highest incidence of PS was found in a female patient (16 teeth), while 11 affected teeth were identified in the image of a male. The existence of multiple PS could be associated to the genetic features of the family, in which some genes, such as FAM20A [37] and SMAD6 [38] could similarly be involved. In this instance, more genetic examinations might aid in clarifying this aspect.

The existence of PS can contribute to some difficulties to the endodontic treatments; thus, insight to its prevalence and distribution pattern will assist in proper treatment plan and successful outcomes. By utilizing CBCT imaging, this study overcomes the inherent diagnostic limitations of traditional two-dimensional periapical and panoramic radiographs, which frequently mask small calcifications due to anatomical superimposition and cortical bone density [20, 25]. While our overarching demographic model highlights who is generally predisposed to PS (females), localized dental analysis maps exactly where these obstructions tend to cluster (maxillary teeth with dental caries). The CBCT scans utilized in this study were obtained from a cross-sectional diagnostic archive of patients seeking general, comprehensive dental evaluations. These scans were not exclusively limited to patients requiring active endodontic therapy or retreatment, which minimizes selection bias and provides a more genuine representation of true community prevalence. Identifying these specific risk profiles allows clinicians to effectively utilize 3D diagnostic tools during treatment planning, ensuring complex root canal morphology can be negotiated safely to reduce chairside errors and optimize endodontic outcomes [24].

In clinical situation, ultrasound, imaging technologies, combined with microscopy, are often used to take away PS [39]. Moreover, the volume, number, and distribution of PS diverge between individuals, and comparing the imaging information of PS in forensic science might aid in the recognition of the demised. Consequently, dissimilar shapes of PS have the likely to assist as valued personal traits for forensic recognition [34].

The limitations of this study includes its single-center retrospective planed study and informations were gathered from a geographically limited area, in which the findings do not characterise the whole population of Iraq. All these outcomes highlight the necessity for additional observation that include longitudinal studies or multicentre surveys, wherein greater sample sizes will be required to attain more extensive and precise data. Through this, clinicians can improve their understanding of PS pathogenesis and provide information to more efficient therapeutic and preventive approaches in clinical situation.

Conclusions

Females in this locality had more PS in general and significantly in their examined teeth even after controlling of age factor. Dental factors that significantly associated with the occurrence of PS were teeth’s condition, location, group, and position. These results suggest that most of the factors were associated to the appearance of PS except for the age of the patients. However, clinical consideration, understanding the distribution of PS is crucial for the proper design of root canal treatment.
