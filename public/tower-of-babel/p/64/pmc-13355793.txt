Associations between smoking-induced deprivation, cigarette purchase price, and coupon use among California adults ages 18–64, 2022–2023
Authors: David A. Stupplebeen, Tam D. Vuong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCalifornia has reduced overall smoking prevalence but people with low income still experience disparities in tobacco use. We examined the associations between recent smoking-induced deprivation (SID; spending on cigarettes instead of other needs), daily smoking, cigarette prices, and coupon use.MethodsWeighted data from two waves of the Online California Adult Tobacco Survey Supplement collected between November 2022 and November 2023 were combined then limited to current smoking and other variables (n = 2288; weighted n = 1,718,238) for bivariate analysis and multivariable stepwise logistic regression with an interaction term to produce estimates of recent SID.ResultsNearly one-third of California adults (33.04%; CI: 29.36, 36.94%) reported recent SID. We found a significant interaction (p < 0.01) for coupon use at last purchase and non-daily smoking (aOR: 6.85; CI: 4.00, 11.73). Cigarette pack price was not significantly associated with recent SID. Other factors associated with SID were inability to meet basic expenses, higher educational attainment, female gender, and younger age.ConclusionsPolicymakers should consider increasing cessation outreach to populations more likely to report recent SID paired with coupon and minimum pack price policies. Interventionists should also consider new programs to address those who do not smoke daily or have high educational attainment.

Introduction

While the California adult smoking prevalence was 6.1% in 2024, approximately 1.2 million California adults <185% of the federal poverty line still used tobacco products (California Tobacco Prevention Program, 2024). Cessation programs and public policies like cigarette tax increases (Farrelly et al., 2012), coupon bans (American Nonsmokers' Rights Foundation, 2024), and minimum pack prices (White et al., 2026) have contributed to lower prevalence. However, increased cigarette costs may be indirectly related to smoking-induced deprivation (SID), or when a person spends money on cigarettes rather than household necessities, such as food (Siahpush et al., 2012). Internationally, SID prevalence ranged 10.8% in the United States to 22.8% in Australia in 2022 (Cho et al., 2025). SID can predict financial stress (Siahpush et al., 2009), is associated with socioeconomic disparities (Parnia & Siddiqi, 2020), and increased spending on cigarettes (Siahpush et al., 2013). The tobacco industry distributes coupons to people who are more likely to face financial hardship (Osman et al., 2019; Zarei et al., 2024); coupon use is associated with higher odds of smoking behaviors (Choi et al., 2018) and reduced odds of cessation (Choi et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023). While minimum price and coupon bans can reduce cigarette sales (White et al., 2026), other recent tax increases did not yield detectable reductions in cigarette consumption (Gunadi et al., 2021). While SID is a consideration for quitting smoking, it is not associated with quit attempts or success (Siahpush et al., 2009).

As coupons are distributed to those who are more likely to be lower income or facing financial hardship, and because of California's increased cigarette costs, we sought to understand how these and other socioeconomic factors were associated with SID among California adults who smoke. Given California's strong policies but persistent smoking inequities among people with low incomes, examining these factors could provide insight into reasons for SID. To our knowledge, no studies have examined SID in California; this analysis provides insight into how industry tactics intersect with tobacco policies.

Methods

Study design and population

This study uses data from two cross-sectional waves (November 2022–January 2023 and October–November 2023) from the Online California Adult Tobacco Survey Supplement from the California Department of Public Health. Eligible respondents were California residents between 18 and 64 years old. Survey respondents with a high likelihood of qualifying were randomly selected by the sample vendor, Qualtrics. Quotas were used to ensure adequate sample size for the following populations: Black/African American, current smoking, and current vaping. The opt-in design preludes calculating a response rate. Post-stratification weights adjusted for sampling bias and to reflect the state's population by age, gender, education, ethnicity, race, smoking status, and vaping status. Waves were pooled (unweighted n = 6319; weighted n = 24,768,420) and an analytic dataset was created limited to those who reported current smoking and responded to the smoking-related, financial, and demographic questions (unweighted n = 2288; weighted n = 1,718,238).

Measures

SID was assessed with the question “When was the last time that someone in your household went without something they needed, or bills weren't paid, because there wasn't enough money after tobacco products were purchased?” (New Zealand Ministry of Health, 2018). Responses were dichotomized to within the last six months (“recent”) and more than six months ago or never (“never/not recent”), as in other studies (Siahpush et al., 2012). Tobacco variables were price paid for the last pack of cigarettes after tax (range: <$5 - ≥ $10), daily smoking (yes/no), and any coupon use at last cigarette purchase (yes/no). Price paid was not adjusted for pack size or sales promotions. Financial variables were financial situation (live comfortably, meet needs with a little left over, meet basic expenses, don't meet basic expenses), hours worked (≥35 h, 15–34 h, <15 h, don't currently work for pay), health insurance status (yes/no or don't know), housing type (detached house, attached house, condominium or apartment, other [RV, boat, mobile home]), and housing tenure (owns, rents, other arrangement). Demographic variables were race (non-Hispanic White, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black/African American, non-Hispanic Other [e.g., American Indian/Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, etc.]), education level (≤high school graduate; two-year college degree, some college, or trade school; four-year college degree or higher), gender identity (male; female; transgender, questioning, or other), age groups (18–25, 26–35, 36–45, 46–55, 56–64), and survey wave (2022, 2023).

Statistical analysis

Bivariate analysis of SID and χ2 tests were conducted. Multivariable stepwise logistic regression was performed, first with tobacco-related variables, then financial variables, then demographic variables followed by combinations thereof. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and an interaction between daily smoking and coupon use was tested, and predicted probabilities estimated. Missing data were excluded from analysis. Analyses were weighted using Stata® 19.5 (College Station, Texas). This study was not human subjects research (University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa Institutional Review Board #2026–00466).

Results

Table 1 presents survey weighted results. Nearly one-third of people who smoke reported recent SID (33.04%; CI: 29.36, 36.94%). Those who spent <$5 or ≥ $10 on cigarettes were more likely to report never or non-recent SID compared to those paying between $5.00–9.99 (p < 0.01). More people who did not smoke daily (41.87%; CI: 36.75, 47.17%) experienced SID compared to people who smoke daily (19.64%; CI: 15.00, 25.28%). More respondents who used a coupon during their last cigarette purchase (53.21%; CI: 46.07, 60.21%) experienced SID compared to those who did not use a coupon (22.06%; CI: 18.02, 26.70%). In the fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression model (Table 1), pack cost was not significantly associated to recent SID, but was significantly related to recent SID for those who did not smoke daily (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.01; CI: 1.24, 3.26). Coupon use was also related to recent SID (aOR: 3.55; CI: 2.48, 5.07). Not meeting basic expenses (aOR: 2.15; CI: 1.04, 4.45), living in other types of housing (aOR: 2.73; CI: 1.44, 5.18), having a four-year degree or higher (aOR: 2.28; CI: 1.38, 3.78), female gender (aOR: 1.54; CI: 1.13, 2.09), and all younger age groups relative to the oldest group were significantly related to recent SID, but odds were higher for younger respondents (e.g., 18–25 year olds aOR: 12.05; CI: 5.36, 27.08).

In the interaction model, both coupon and non-daily smoking were significantly associated to recent SID (aOR: 6.85, CI: 4.00, 11.73), and the interaction was significant based on a Wald test (F[32285] = 30.20; p < 0.01). Adjusted odds for recent SID were higher in the interaction model for not meeting basic expenses (aOR: 2.44; CI: 1.23, 4.87) and for four-year degree or higher (aOR: 2.32; CI: 1.39, 3.89). Lower, but still significant, odds for other types of housing (aOR: 2.58; CI: 1.38, 4.85), and all age groups (e.g., 18–25-year-olds aOR: 11.36; CI: 5.14, 25.10 vs. 46–55-year-olds aOR: 5.42; CI: 2.33, 12.64), while female gender odds remained the same. Predicted probabilities of SID were highest for coupon users who did not smoke daily (57.56%; CI: 50.25, 64.88%), followed by those who smoke daily (31.19%; CI: 19.26, 43.11%), followed those who did not use coupons (non-daily smoking: 26.11%, CI: 20.68, 31.54%; daily smoking: 21.91%, CI: 15.52, 28.31%).

Discussion

We examined associations between cigarette pack price, coupon use, daily smoking, and recent SID. Almost one-third of adults who smoke cigarettes in California experienced SID, higher than the United States SID prevalence (Cho et al., 2025). In this study, pack price was not significantly associated with recent SID, but non-daily cigarette smoking and coupon use were positively associated with recent SID in both the non-interaction and interaction models. Those unable to meet basic expenses had higher odds of recent SID. Taken together, this may be related to others' findings that the tobacco industry targets those who are less well off with coupons to sustain smoking (Zarei et al., 2023, Zarei et al., 2024) and that people with lower incomes who smoke spend a higher percentage of their income on cigarettes (Farrelly et al., 2012). Though this study found non-daily smoking was associated to SID, we cannot establish causality between them; however, others have found reduced cigarette consumption is used to control spending (Dearfield et al., 2024; Zarei et al., 2024) which may help to explain our findings. SID at younger age plus the high proportion of non-daily smoking among respondents may also be indicative of social smoking, which has been increasing according to past reviews (Schane et al., 2009).

California's economic profile may contribute to higher odds of SID. Relative to the rest of the country, California has a high cost of living in addition to high levels of poverty (Laird et al., 2018). In coastal areas, housing costs puts many at risk for poverty in California: while poverty is higher among those of lower education levels, 7.6% of college graduates ages 25–64 in California were living in poverty in 2023 (Bohn et al., 2025). Regarding gender, we found female respondents had higher odds of SID. We also found higher odds of SID among those with a four-year degree. Others have found men with increased levels of school debt were found to more frequently smoke compared to women while the same study found women had higher levels of student loan debt (Qian and Fan, 2023). Another study found higher levels of worry related to debt was related to increased smoking (Nigatu et al., 2024). High cost of living, student loan debt, and smoking for stress relief are underexplored.

This study reveals potential policy implications. First, the industry's use of coupons may provide short-term relief to those who experience SID while prolonging nicotine addiction; localities should adopt minimum price and coupon policies to address this issue. Cessation practitioners should consider integrating behavioral economics tools to further generate quit planning and nudge those experiencing SID to quit smoking. One study used such an approach to address delay discounting, where people delay longer-term future rewards for smaller rewards in the present, and found it may increase intention to quit smoking among low-income people (Rogers et al., 2022). As California localities push for higher cigarette prices and coupon bans, integrating economic planning lessons into cessation programs could help empower people with lower incomes to successfully plan to quit smoking.

Localities considering pairing minimum price policies with coupon bans should increase cessation services marketing tailored to people with low incomes who may experience SID since SID is related to quit intent (Siahpush et al., 2009). These marketing efforts should include ethnically and gender diverse people; counselors should be trained in cultural competence for these populations. Interventions to reach people who smoke infrequently are still needed (Schane et al., 2009), also accounting for highly educated people who may be participating in social smoking. Combined with the above recommendations, this would require a more holistic view of tobacco cessation. Lastly, studies are needed to explore the relationship between quit plans, tobacco abstinence, and SID, plus studies that examine financial stress and smoking.

Limitations to this study included the cross-sectional design preventing establishing causality, potential recall bias due to the self-report nature of questions, and potential selection bias due to the opt-in survey methodology. The lack of geographic indicators to examine the influence of minimum price and coupon policies directly, and to account for stark differences between high and low cost of living counties in the state (e.g., San Francisco vs. Modoc County). We lacked measures on nicotine dependence and number of cigarettes smoked per day, individual indicators of income, quit planning, previous quit attempts, and 7-month quit status. Lastly, the question used to measure SID combined multiple concepts; respondents may have interpreted the meaning of this measure in different ways. Because of limitations with this survey question, caution is warranted in interpreting the meaning of deprivation for the respondents. Future studies should consider using multiple measures of deprivation such as food insecurity and inability to pay utility bills, and rent or mortgage when examining tobacco use and intent to quit.

Conclusions

The above limitations notwithstanding, this study contributes to our understanding of how cigarette pricing, coupon use, and non-daily smoking are associated with SID among adults who smoke in California. The tobacco industry continues to make coupons available in California which may keep people who experience SID or who smoke less than daily using cigarettes. This study helps to point in new directions to address persistent tobacco use disparities among people with low incomes.
