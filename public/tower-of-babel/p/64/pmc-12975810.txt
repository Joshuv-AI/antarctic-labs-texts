Porto-sinusoidal Vascular Disease is Associated with Gastrointestinal Disorders in Common Variable Immunodefiency
Authors: Emilie Corvilain, Laurence Gérard, Jehane Fadlallah, Lionel Galicier, Laure Delaval, Jessy Alchidiac, Aurélie Plessier, David Boutboul, Claire Fieschi, Eric Oksenhendler, Pierre Emmanuel Rautou, Marion Malphettes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe aimed to describe porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD) in a large cohort of predominantly antibody deficiency (PAD) patients and sought to identify contributing factors for PSVD development by comparing CVID patients with and without PSVD.MethodPatients were retrospectively identified from a national database.ResultsSixty-eight of 386 PAD patients, including 63 of 267 CVID patients were diagnosed with PSVD. Median time from PAD to PSVD diagnosis was 9.2 years (interquartile range, 4.6–14.5). CVID patients with late onset combined immunodeficiency and non-infectious complications were overrepresented among PSVD patients (37.5% and 87.3%, respectively). Nodular regenerative hyperplasia (NRH) was the most common finding on liver biopsy (79%), alongside with intra-sinusoidal CD8+ T-cell infiltration (65.7%). In an adjusted multivariate model, gastro-intestinal (GI) complications (adjusted odds ratio, 4.94; 95% CI, 1.91–12.79; p = 0.001) and low naive CD4+ T-cell count (p = 0.001) were strongly associated with the occurrence of PSVD in CVID patients, together with low serum IgA level (p = 0.018) and lymphoproliferation (p = 0.027). GI complications were observed in 90.5% of PSVD patients, preceding PSVD diagnosis in 84% of them by a median duration of 10.7 years. The 20-year probability to develop PSVD after CVID diagnosis was estimated at 38.7% (95% CI, 30.1–48.7) and 11.7% (95% CI, 5.0–26.0) in patients with or without GI complications, respectively (p < 0.001). The median overall survival from onset of PSVD was 8.4 years.ConclusionOur data suggest that impaired intestinal barrier in a context of T-cell defect may play a key role in the development of PSVD in CVID patients. Whether complete control of GI complications would prevent the occurrence of PSVD remains to be evaluated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-026-01985-4.

Introduction

Predominantly antibody deficiencies (PAD) encompass a broad spectrum of conditions, ranging from selective antibody deficiency to severe reduction of all serum immunoglobulin levels and absence of circulating B-cells. PAD patients share common features such as increased susceptibility to bacterial infections, mainly of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts [1–3]. In addition, they are prone to various autoimmune, inflammatory or lymphoproliferative disease-related disorders, sometime leading to severe organ damage [4–9]. Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is the most frequent symptomatic entity in PAD [10]. Efforts to stratify CVID patients according to B- and T-cell phenotype [11, 12], clinical features [13, 14] or genetic screening [15] have led to a better understanding of the disease as well as improvement in patient management. Although overall survival (OS) of CVID patients has improved over time, it remains significantly shorter than in the general population, particularly in patients with noninfectious complications [16, 17]. Liver damage is of special concern in CVID [18–23], as it is associated with significant mortality [24, 25]. The most frequent feature of liver disease in CVID is porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD), an entity encompassing a heterogeneous group of rare liver diseases characterized by abnormalities of the small intrahepatic vessels in the absence of cirrhosis [26, 27]. PSVD has been associated with various conditions, including thrombophilia, hematologic malignancies, human immunodeficiency virus infection, genetic, and immunological disorders. The main complications of PSVD are portal venous thrombosis and gastrointestinal bleeding related to portal hypertension. Development of hepatic encephalopathy and refractory ascites are uncommon, and hepatocellular carcinoma exceptional [28]. A better understanding of PSVD pathogenesis in the setting of PAD is crucial, to delineate at risk patients as well as to find appropriate therapeutic approaches.

The aim of this study was to describe the natural history of PSVD in a large cohort of PAD patients followed in a tertiary referral center. In addition, we sought to identify contributing factors for PSVD development by comparing CVID patients with and without PSVD.

Patients and Methods

Data Collected

Patients with PSVD were identified from the DEFI database [1]. Briefly, this ongoing database was created in 2004 to collect clinical, laboratory and pathological data from prospectively enrolled adult patients with PAD within a national network. The database is centralized in the Department of Clinical Immunology, Hôpital Saint-Louis in Paris. A blood sample collected at inclusion was used to determine serum IgG, IgA et IgM levels and to characterize B-cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells, and NK-cells phenotype in a centralized reference laboratory. Flow cytometry was performed on whole-blood, using markers to determine naive (CD45RA+ CCR7+) CD4 T-cells, switched memory (CD19+ CD27+ IgD−) B-cells and CD21low (CD19hiCD21lowCD38−) B-cells. All patients from the PSVD group were screened for HBV and HCV infections by mean of polymerase chain reaction test at the time of liver disease diagnosis. HEV was retrospectively screened in the whole cohort [29]. Direct detection of blood CMV DNA was not performed. The present work focuses on a single referral center, the department of Clinical Immunology in Saint Louis hospital. As the DEFI study was not designed to collect extensive data on liver condition, medical charts of PSVD patients were reviewed by two physicians in order to expand data with liver ultrasound, CT scans and ultrasound-based transient elastography reports, presence and grade of esophageal varices, hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG), fecal calprotectin and lactulose breath test results. We updated the database through a double medical chart review conducted from October 2022 to June 2023. The time of last evaluation corresponds to the latest patient information available in June 2023 when the database was frozen.

Definitions

The diagnostic criteria used for CVID at inclusion in DEFI were consistent with the European Society for Immunodeficiencies/Pan-American Group for Immunodeficiency criteria 1999 [30, 31], . All patients had a serum IgG level below 5 gr/L. Late onset combined immunodeficiency (LOCID) was defined by previous opportunistic infection or naive CD4+ CD45RA+ CCR7+ T-cells below 20 × 106/L. Switched memory B-cell defect (SmB-) was defined by CD19+ CD27+ IgD− / CD19+ B-cells ≤ 2% of or total CD19+ B-cells < 1%. CVID patients may be subclassified according to the clinical phenotype (infection only/non-infectious complications) [13], the presence of a severe T-cell defect, referred to as LOCID [11, 32] and the presence of a deleterious mutation or deletion in a disease-causing gene in genetically-defined CVID patients [33]. Patients with hypogammaglobulinemia not fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for CVID such as IgG-subclass(es), selective IgA, selective IgM, and/or specific antibody deficiencies, were referred to as unclassified predominantly antibody deficiency (unPAD) [34]. Good syndrome (GS) was defined as an antibody deficiency associated with thymoma. Even if GS is not anymore considered as a PAD and rather as a phenocopy of primary combined immunodeficiency [35], we considered that these patients, included in the DEFI cohort, represented an interesting group for the purpose of the study. The diagnosis of PSVD was based on VALDIG criteria, as stated in Baveno VII consensus [26]: it required the exclusion of cirrhosis together with at least one of the following three criteria : (i) one feature specific for portal hypertension; (ii) one histologic lesion specific for PSVD; (iii) one feature not specific for portal hypertension together with at least one histologic lesion compatible although not specific for PSVD. Post-transplantation or per/post-immunosuppressive treatment biopsies were excluded from the analysis. Specific signs of portal hypertension included gastric, esophageal, or ectopic varices, history of portal hypertensive bleeding, and portosystemic collaterals at imaging. Non-specific signs of portal hypertension were adapted in the present study, retaining ascites, but excluding splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia, given the high frequency of infiltrative splenomegaly and auto-immune thrombocytopenia in PAD. Specific histological PSVD lesions were obliterative portal venopathy and nodular regenerative hyperplasia (NRH) while portal tract abnormalities, architectural disturbance, non-zonal sinusoidal dilatation or mild perisinusoidal fibrosis were considered as non-specific lesions. The date of PSVD diagnosis was the date of the liver biopsy basing PSVD diagnosis or the date of portal hypertension diagnosis. Of note, elevated hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥ 5mmHg was neither sufficient nor required for the definition of portal hypertension.

Gastrointestinal disorders were defined by the occurrence of infectious or non-infectious complications involving the gastro-intestinal tract, including atrophic gastritis, duodenal villous atrophy, inflammatory bowel disease, lymphoid hyperplasia as well as infectious diarrhea. Common practice in the department is to monitor CVID patients every 6 months and to seek for portal hypertension by means of computed tomography or ultrasound scan and/or gastroscopy in case of liver function test abnormality, thrombocytopenia, or splenomegaly. Liver biopsy was performed in all patients with portal hypertension or persistent abnormal liver function tests. Patients with PSVD were jointly followed by physicians from the department of Hepatology, Hôpital Beaujon. At least one gastroscopy and one liver biopsy was performed in all patients with a diagnosis of PSVD.

Ethical Statements

The DEFI study has been approved by the local ethics committee (Comité Consultatif de Protection des Personnes dans la Recherche Biomédicale d’Ile-de-France) and all patients gave written informed consent for publishing their data. For ethical considerations, patients were also informed that data regarding liver condition that were additionally collected in medical records might be used for research study in accordance with the privacy rule. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Analyses

Patient’s characteristics were tabulated, and descriptive statistics were reported as numbers with percentage (%) or medians with interquartile range (IQR). Proportions of patients between groups were compared using the Pearson’s chi-squared test or the Fischer’s exact test when appropriate. The Student t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for nonnormally distributed variables. Demographic and clinical factors associated with the occurrence of PSVD were evaluated in a generalized linear model including univariate regression and mulyivariate regression with stepwise forward selection. Interactions between variables included in the final model were tested for significance. Survivals were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier product-limit method and compared using log-rank test. Median follow-up was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier technique where the censoring indicator is reversed. All tests were two-tailed and a p value < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed with Stata statistical software (version 16.1, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Characteristics of 68 PSVD Patients from the PAD Cohort

In June 2023, 908 PAD patients had been enrolled in the national cohort. From these patients, 386 were followed in a single center (Saint-Louis Hospital): 267 CVID (including 72 LOCID), nine X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA), one X-lymphoproliferative (XLP) disorder, 94 unPAD and 15 Good syndrome (GS) patients. Among these 386 PAD patients, 68 patients (17.6%) had a confirmed diagnosis of PSVD (Table 1), of whom 63 were CVID patients, including 27 considered with a diagnosis of LOCID and 12 patients with genetically defined CVID. Altered disease-causing genes were CTLA4 (n = 4), LRBA (n = 2), DKC1, ZBTB24, IKZF1, RTEL1, STAT1 and STAT3 (one each). The five PSVD patients without CVID were diagnosed with XLP due to a mutation in SH2D1A (n = 1), IgG subclass deficiency (n = 1), IgM-IgG subclass deficiency associated with a familial autosomal dominant syndrome related to a mutation in IRF4 (n = 1) and GS (n = 2).

Clinical and pathological characteristics of the 68 patients with PSVD are detailed in Table 1. Median age at PSVD diagnosis was 50 years (Supplementary Figure S1). Median time between PAD and PSVD diagnosis was 9.2 years (IQR, 4.6–14.5). Prior exposition to toxics was reported in eight patients: alcohol (n = 4), azathioprine (n = 4). Antiphospholipid autoantibodies were detectable in one out of 43 tested. No patient had chronic hepatitis B virus infection and one had spontaneously cleared hepatitis C virus. In 58 patients, liver function test abnormality preceded PSVD diagnosis by a median time of 45 months (IQR, 11–80), while liver function test was normal at PSVD diagnosis in 10 patients. Most patients had a fluctuating evolution of liver function test abnormalities throughout follow-up. Progressive elevation was uncommon. At last evaluation, median gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) level was 120 UI/L (IQR, 30–384; upper limit of normal [ULN], 60), median alkaline phosphatase (AP) level was 230 UI/L (IQR, 101–778; ULN, 129), median aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level was 48 UI/L (IQR, 22–100; ULN, 40), median alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level was 35 UI/L (IQR, 12–122; ULN, 41), median bilirubin level was 9.75 µmol/L (IQR, 4–20,5; ULN, 21). Median albumin level was 36 g/L (IQR, 29–39), median hemoglobin level was 12 g/dL (IQR, 9.8–13.1), median platelet count was 95 G/L (IQR, 61–156). Ultrasound-based transient elastography was measured in 47 patients and median elasticity was 11.8 kPa (IQR, 5.6–49). Hepatic venous pressure gradient was measured at the time of liver biopsy in 39 patients and the median value was 8 mmHg (IQR, 2–18). The most frequent findings on liver biopsies were nodular regenerative hyperplasia (79%), intra-sinusoidal CD8 + T-cell infiltration (65.5%) and architecture disturbance (50.7%) while obliterative portal venopathy was detected in 10% of biopsy of the patients. Four patients had advanced fibrosis on biopsy specimen, two at PSVD diagnosis, the remaining two developed advanced fibrosis during the follow-up.

Sixty-three out of 68 patients with PSVD reported GI disease throughout follow-up. At last evaluation, iron deficiency has been reported in 32 patients (47%), and vitamin B9 or vitamin B12 deficiency in 13 and 6 patients, respectively. Sixteen of 24 tested patients (66.7%) had increased levels of fecal calprotectin (> 50 µg/g), a tool used to raise suspicion of inflammatory bowel disease in patients with gastro-intestinal symptoms [36]. Nineteen of 43 tested patients (44.2%) had a positive lactulose breath test, suggestive of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. Norovirus was positive in the stools of 7 out of 55 tested patients, Helicobacter pylori was detected in 13 out of 66 gastric biopsies.

The most frequent complication associated with PSVD was ascites, occurring in 41% of the patients. Other complications, such as esophageal varices bleeding, hepato-pulmonary syndrome, hepatic encephalopathy or portal thrombosis were less common, noted in approximatively 10% of patients each (Table 1). Altogether, 54% of the patients experienced at least one complication related to PSVD. Three patients underwent liver transplantation, two patients for liver failure and one patient for hepato-pulmonary syndrome. Two of them experienced recurrence of PSVD on liver transplant within one year and died. The third patient remained free from PSVD for ten years and eventually died 13 years after transplantation from metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma. Median OS since the diagnosis of PSVD was 8.4 years (IQR, 5.3–17) (Supplementary Figure S2). Thirty patients (44%) had died. Twenty-six deaths (86.7%) were related to liver complications. Three patients died from malignancy (gastric, colonic cancer and lymphoma) and one from end-stage pulmonary disease.

Comparison of Clinical and Biological Phenotypes of CVID Patients with and without PSVD

We then focused on the group of 267 patients with CVID. PSVD was diagnosed in 63 (23.6%) of them. Median time from CVID diagnosis to last evaluation was 14.8 years (IQR, 11.9–22.2) in PSVD patients vs. 12.9 years (IQR, 7.4–19.5) in patients without PSVD (p = 0.007). LOCID was diagnosed in 42.9% of patients with PSVD vs. 22% of patients without PSVD (p = 0.001). Non-infectious complications were recorded in 87.3% of the patients with PSVD compared to 42% of the patients without PSVD (p < 0.001). Main clinical and biological characteristics of patients with or without PSVD are more specifically described in Table 2. As shown, PSVD patients had significantly lower serum IgG, IgA, and IgM levels, more frequent switched memory B-cell deficiency, as well as significantly lower CD4+ and naive CD4+ T-cell counts at CVID diagnosis. In a subset of 60 patients tested, the percentage of CD21low CD19+ B-cells was significantly higher in PSVD patients (Table 2). Absolute IgA deficiency (serum IgA level < 0.08 g/L) was observed in 54.1% PSVD vs. 34.8% non-PSVD patients (p = 0.011). During the period of observation, the prevalence of GI complications of any type was significantly higher in patients who developed PSVD than in patients who did not (90.5% vs. 47.6%, p < 0.001). Of note, GI complications preceded PSVD diagnosis in 84% of these patients by a median duration of 10.7 years (Fig. 1). More specifically, infectious complications of the GI tract were significantly more frequent in PSVD patients (77.8% vs. 20.6%, p < 0.001) as were GI immune dysregulations (58.7 vs. 21.1%, p < 0.001). In addition, patients with PSVD experienced auto-immune manifestations, lymphoproliferation, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, pneumonia, and septicemia more frequently than patients without PSVD (Table 2).

Given the numerous differences observed in Table 2 between patients with and without PSVD, we implemented a generalized linear model to determine associations between patients’ characteristics and the development of PSVD. Variables selected for the model, how they were considered, and results of univariable modeling are detailed in the Supplementary Appendix and Supplementary Table S1. Results of the multivariable model with stepwise forward selection are shown in Table 3. In this multivariate model, GI complications and low naive CD4+ T-cell count (p = 0.001 for both) were strongly associated with PSVD development (Table 3). The two other independent covariables were low serum IgA level (p = 0.018) and lymphoproliferation (P = 0.027).

Discussion

In this large series on PSVD occurring in a cohort of PAD patients followed in a single tertiary center, we evidenced that PSVD is predominantly a CVID complication, affecting 63 of the 267 CVID patients (23.6%), while only 3 out of the 94 patients (3.2%) with unPAD and 2 of the 15 patients with GS. Using the more restricted ESID2019 definition and because of missing data on the extended lymphocyte phenotype for some patients, the total CVID population was 186 patients among whom 49 (26%) developed PSVD.

CVID patients with LOCID and noninfectious complications were overrepresented among PSVD patients, (37.5% and 87.3%, respectively). In contrast, none of the nine XLA patients developed PSVD. The distribution of PSVD among these different subgroups suggests that in addition to the humoral immune defect, some T-cell defect may contribute to PSVD.

In line with previous reports, we found that NRH is the main histological feature in CVID-related liver disease [37]. A specific pattern of focal or diffuse peri-cellular fibrosis associated with NRH was recently described in CVID patients, leading to coin the term NRH-like changes, to underline the typical fibrotic changes associated to regeneration nodules in the setting of immune deficiency [38]. While two of the patients from our PAD cohort presented with advanced fibrosis, mild perisinusoidal and portal fibrosis were respectively reported in 26.5 and 20.6% of PSVD patients. Mild liver inflammation with moderate lymphocytic infiltrate in the portal and lobular regions and intrasinusoidal lymphocytosis has been reported as more frequently associated with NRH in the context of CVID [25, 38]. In our study, intrasinusoidal CD8+ T-cell infiltration was present in 65.7% of the patients. This high frequency may even be underestimated given the fact that it was not a permanent feature in patients who underwent two or more liver biopsies.

Focusing on CVID patients, we were able to demonstrate in an adjusted multivariate model that gastro-intestinal (GI) complications and low naive CD4+ T-cell count were strongly associated with the occurrence of PSVD in CVID patients. While non-infectious complications have already been reported associated with liver disease in several studies on CVID [18–25, 39], the strong association of GI disorders with PSVD paired with the association of serum IgA deficiency with PSVD prompted us to discuss the relationship between GI disorders and PSVD. It should be underlined that patients with GI diseases without immunodeficiency background, such as celiac disease [40] or inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), especially when treated with azathioprine [41] are prone to develop PSVD. In addition, idiopathic non-cirrhotic portal hypertension, a condition included in the spectrum of PSVD, is reported at high incidence in lower social classes in India, where it has been attributed to the high frequency of tropical enteropathies encountered in this population [42]. It is also interesting to note that PSVD can be generated in an animal model by repeatedly injecting Escherichia coli into portal circulation [43]. In our study, both infectious and non-infectious GI disorders were overrepresented in the PSVD group. A feature shared by these conditions is an increased intestinal permeability likely to facilitate entrance into the portal venous system of microbial antigens deriving from the gut.

The pathogenesis of PSVD is unclear. One study on 44 PSVD patients, including few CVID patients, demonstrated a preferential distribution of CD8+ T-cells next to atrophic liver cell plates, in close contact with apoptotic endothelial cells, suggesting a link between intrasinusoidal CD8+ T-cells and porto-sinusoidal endothelial cell damage [44]. Moreover, it has been recently shown that NRH like changes coexist along with altered sinusoidal endothelium and intrasinusoidal CD3+ T lymphocyte infiltration, which further supports the hypothesis of a T-cell mediated sinusoidal endothelium injury [45]. It can be speculated that translocated microbial antigens may promote immune mediated injury of porto-sinusoidal endothelial cells, those cells acting as antigen presenting cells to CD8+ T-cells at high antigen concentration [46]. In another hand, intestinal barrier defect can also be a contributor to the systemic immune activation through chronic translocation of bacterial products which may induce some levels of T-cell impairment, associated with non-infectious complications [7].

In our study, infections of the gastro-intestinal tract were significantly more frequent in patients with PSVD than without (77.8 vs. 20.6%, p < 0.001). Campylobacter spp prevailed as infectious pathogen (29 patients), while enteroviruses were detected in 12 patients with chronic diarrhea. A condition named “enteric virus infection associated with hepatitis (EVAH)”, with liver pathologic features reminiscent of NRH was recently described after stem cell transplantation in severe combined immune deficiency patients [47]. EVAH is defined by chronic detection in liver and/or stools of enteric viruses such as Aichi virus and Sapovirus. Another recent study showed that CVID related liver disease was associated with an expansion of circulating CD57+ CD8+ T-cells while CD57+ CD8+ T-cell count correlated with a history of CMV infection [48]. As we had not collected data on Aichi virus, Sapovirus or CMV, we cannot assess the specific role of these pathogens in liver disease in patients from the present study. Thrombophilia being a condition reported to be linked to PSVD, vitamin B12 deficiency associated with GI disorders and subsequent hyperhomocysteinemia could play a role in portal venous obliteration [49].

PSVD is a late complication in CVID, unlike immune dysregulation complications, such as autoimmune cytopenias, interstitial lung disease or bronchiectasis (DEFI cohort, data not shown). We confirm that liver disease is associated with a high mortality with a median OS since the diagnosis of PSVD with portal hypertension of 8.4 years (IQR, 5.3–17). It should be underlined that in these patients, the cause of death was mainly related to liver disease (86.7%).

This study is the largest monocentric study on PSVD in CVID patients to our knowledge. A key strength of this study is that all patients are managed at a single center, by physicians with a consistent clinical approach, with particular emphasis on the screening for hepatic involvement. This uniformity in clinical practice minimizes risk of false negative PSVD diagnosis, which makes the comparison between patients with and without PSVD more reliable. In the other hand, one limitation of this study is that it involves patients from a referral center, which may introduce a selection bias, by recruiting patients with more severe PAD. Our current practice is to perform liver biopsies in patients with signs of portal hypertension, which may lead to an underestimation of PSVD frequency among PAD patients by a selection of patients with a more advanced and severe liver disease. This possible bias may contribute to an overestimation of liver-related adverse clinical outcomes and mortality in this study. However, the poor prognosis was confirmed once portal hypertension is established.

In summary, our study supports the hypothesis that consequences of an impaired intestinal barrier in a context of T-cell defect may play a key role in the development of PSVD in patients with PAD. The pathogenesis of PSVD is probably complex, involving disease-specific factors like immune dysregulation as well as microbial factors coming from the gut. Considering the high mortality rate and the limited therapeutic options available, prevention should be a major objective. Whether a control of GI disorders would prevent the occurrence of PSVD remains to be evaluated. As liver injury may be partly related to an exacerbated immune response, immunosuppressive treatments can also be discussed in this setting.
