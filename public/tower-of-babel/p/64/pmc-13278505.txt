Behavioral change and oral health literacy among elementary students after a school-based oral health education program
Authors: Selviawaty Sarifuddin Panna, Ayub Irmadani Anwar, Irfan Sugianto, Nurlindah Hamrun, Marhamah Firman Singgih, Ichlas Nanang Afandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Oral health knowledge and behavior are essential in preventing dental caries among school-aged children, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to preventive education is limited. This study aimed to evaluate changes in oral health literacy, behavior, and clinical oral health status among Indonesian elementary students following a school-based oral health education intervention.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study employed a cluster-randomized longitudinal intervention design with pre- and post-test assessments. A total of 582 students aged 8-12 years from public elementary schools in Makassar, Indonesia, were recruited using cluster random sampling and allocated into three groups: Multimedia-based education, conventional education, and a control group. Oral health literacy was assessed using a validated knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) questionnaire with confirmed internal reliability (Cronbach’s α >0.80). Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Oral Hygiene Index-Simplified (OHI-S) and the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) index based on the World Health Organization criteria. Data were analyzed statistically to assess associations between literacy and clinical outcomes.RESULTS:The multimedia-based education group showed the greatest improvement in KAP scores and the most pronounced reductions in OHI-S and DMFT scores compared with the conventional and control groups (P < 0.001). Higher oral health literacy was significantly associated with better oral hygiene and lower caries experience. Qualitative findings indicated increased awareness, improved habits, and peer support after the intervention.CONCLUSION:Multimedia-based school oral health education effectively enhances literacy, behaviors, and clinical oral health outcomes. Integrating interactive multimedia into the school curriculum may support sustainable preventive oral health strategies for children.

Introduction

The oral health of children has a substantial impact on their overall health, well-being, and quality of life, influencing physical comfort, psychosocial development, and academic performance. Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic conditions among school-aged children worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to preventive services and oral health education is limited. Evidence from Indonesia indicates a high prevalence of dental caries and inadequate oral hygiene practices among elementary school pupils, with a persistent gap between oral health knowledge and daily practices.[123]

Oral health literacy refers to the ability to obtain, understand, and apply oral health information to make appropriate health-related decisions. In children, oral health literacy encompasses not only factual knowledge but also motivation, self-regulation, and decision-making skills that influence long-term behavior. Higher oral health literacy levels have been associated with improved tooth-brushing practices, more positive attitudes toward preventive care, and reduced caries experience among school-aged children.[456]

Schools represent a strategic setting for oral health promotion because they shape daily routines and health-related behaviors during critical developmental stages. School-based oral health education programs have been shown to improve oral hygiene behaviors and reduce caries risk, particularly when educational activities are engaging and reinforced over time. However, many programs continue to rely on didactic instructional methods that increase knowledge without producing sustained behavioral change.[78]

Contemporary educational approaches emphasize learner-centered strategies and the use of multimedia tools, including videos, animations, and interactive visualizations, to enhance engagement and knowledge retention. Multimedia-based learning aligns with children’s cognitive development by leveraging visual and auditory stimuli, thereby facilitating understanding and recall. Interactive and repetitive educational interventions combined with peer interaction have been shown to strengthen habit formation and self-care behaviors among children.[910]

Despite the preventive role of oral health education, evidence from developing countries remains limited, particularly regarding studies that simultaneously evaluate behavioral and clinical outcomes. Many interventions report improvements in knowledge but fail to assess objective oral health indicators such as the Oral Hygiene Index-Simplified (OHI-S) and the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) index. This gap underscores the need for comprehensive evaluations linking educational exposure with measurable behavioral and clinical outcomes among school-aged children.[1112]

Guided by Social Cognitive Theory, this study evaluates the impact of multimedia-based oral health education on elementary school students’ knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) and clinical outcomes. The novelty of this research lies in the integration of a multimedia-enhanced, school-based intervention with both behavioral and clinical measures (KAP, OHI-S, and DMFT) using a cluster-randomized longitudinal mixed-methods design in Indonesian elementary school children, a population with limited prior evidence.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cluster-randomized longitudinal intervention design with pre- and post-test assessments to assess changes in oral health literacy and behavior among elementary school students following a school-based oral health education program. The study was conducted in several public elementary schools in Makassar, South Sulawesi, Indonesia. These schools were selected to represent diverse sociodemographic characteristics while maintaining similar learning environments and class sizes. The program was implemented over a defined intervention period to allow adequate exposure to oral health education activities, and data collection was performed before and after the program.

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in Social Cognitive Theory and health literacy frameworks, which posit that behavior change occurs through the interaction of cognitive acquisition, motivation, observational learning, and environmental reinforcement. Multimedia-based oral health education enhances visual engagement and comprehension, leading to improved oral health KAP. These behavioral improvements subsequently translate into measurable clinical outcomes, including improved oral hygiene status (OHI-S) and reduced caries progression (DMFT). Peer interaction and repeated exposure within the school environment further reinforce sustained behavior change [Figure 1].

Study participants and sampling

The study population consisted of 582 students from grades four to six, aged between 8 and 12 years. A total of three groups were established: A multimedia-based education group, a conventional education group, and a control group. Sample selection was carried out using a cluster random sampling technique to ensure representativeness and minimize bias. Inclusion criteria included enrollment as an active student in the participating schools, parental consent for participation, and the ability to follow program activities. Students with systemic illnesses or cognitive impairments that could affect participation were excluded from the study.

The oral health education program was developed to enhance students’ KAP regarding oral hygiene. The multimedia-based group received education through interactive videos, animations, and visual presentations emphasizing tooth brushing techniques, dietary control, and prevention of dental caries. The conventional group received standard oral health lectures using posters and printed materials. Both intervention groups participated in classroom-based sessions integrated into regular learning schedules. The control group did not receive any specific oral health education during the study period. The intervention consisted of two 45-60-minute classroom-based sessions delivered over a four-week period. Cluster randomization was applied at the school level. Non-parametric statistical analyses were used due to non-normal data distribution.

Data collection tools and techniques

Quantitative data were collected using a validated structured questionnaire adapted from the Ministry of Health’s oral health promotion tools, which measured three main behavioral domains: KAP related to oral hygiene. The questionnaire was pretested to ensure clarity and internal consistency, and reliability was confirmed through Cronbach’s alpha analysis (>0.80). Clinical assessments were conducted to evaluate oral hygiene and caries status using the OHI-S and DMFT index. Examinations were performed by calibrated dental professionals under standardized conditions using disposable dental mirrors and probes, following the World Health Organization diagnostic criteria. Clinical examiners were not blinded to group allocation, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. To complement quantitative findings, focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with a purposive sample of students from each intervention group to explore perceptions and experiences related to the program. Each FGD consisted of 6-8 students and was facilitated by a trained moderator using a semi-structured guide that covered topics on oral hygiene awareness, motivation, and behavior change. Discussions were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically to identify recurring patterns and insights that supported the quantitative outcomes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Hasanuddin University, Makassar, Indonesia.

Statistical analysis method

Data were entered and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, including mean, standard deviation, and frequency distribution. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Because most data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and post-intervention changes within groups, while the Friedman and Kruskal–Wallis tests were employed to assess differences across the three groups. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Correlation analysis between behavioral and clinical variables was conducted using Spearman’s rho test to explore the association between oral health literacy (KAP) and oral health status (OHI-S and DMFT).

Following the implementation of the school-based oral health education program, a marked improvement was observed in all behavioral and clinical indicators among students in both intervention groups. The greatest improvements were found in the multimedia-based education group. The mean knowledge score increased substantially from pre- to post-intervention (Δ = 0.30 ± 0.18), followed by attitude (Δ = 0.50 ± 0.23) and practice (Δ = 0.39 ± 0.25), all statistically significant (P < 0.001). The conventional education group also showed improvement in all behavioral domains but to a lesser degree. In contrast, the control group exhibited minimal changes, and none were statistically significant.

In terms of clinical parameters, both intervention groups demonstrated a significant decrease in OHI-S scores, indicating improved oral hygiene. The multimedia-based education group achieved the largest mean reduction (Δ = –0.73 ± 1.15, P < 0.001), followed by the conventional education group (Δ = –0.13 ± 0.49, P < 0.001). Similarly, DMFT scores declined in the intervention groups, suggesting a reduction in caries progression and improved preventive behavior, whereas the control group’s DMFT values remained largely unchanged. The ΔDMFT represents the change in mean caries experience during the intervention period and does not indicate absolute caries reversal.

Comparative analysis across the three groups confirmed significant differences in post-intervention outcomes for all variables (P < 0.001). The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed that the multimedia-based education group achieved the highest ranks for improvements in KAP scores as well as for reductions in OHI-S and DMFT indices [Table 3]. Post-hoc Mann–Whitney tests further indicated that the differences between the multimedia-based group and both other groups were statistically significant, while the gap between the conventional and control groups was smaller.

Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between oral health literacy and clinical indicators. Knowledge and attitude scores were inversely correlated with both OHI-S (r = –0.310 and r = –0.381, respectively; P < 0.001) and DMFT (r = –0.061 and r = –0.170, respectively; P < 0.001), indicating that students with higher oral health literacy exhibited better oral hygiene and fewer carious teeth [Table 4]. These findings support the hypothesis that cognitive and behavioral improvement translates into tangible oral health benefits. It should be emphasized that the observed changes in DMFT represent differences in caries experience over the intervention period rather than the true reversal of existing carious lesions.

Qualitative findings from the FGDs complemented the quantitative results and provided deeper insights into the behavioral change process. Three major themes emerged from the analysis. The first theme, awareness and understanding of oral hygiene importance, reflected students’ recognition of why oral care is necessary and how neglect leads to dental problems [Table 5]. Students expressed that multimedia visuals, such as animated brushing demonstrations, helped them “see the dirt” and “understand what happens if teeth are not cleaned.” The second theme, habit formation through repetition and visualization, described how interactive lessons and video-based instruction helped students remember correct brushing techniques and encouraged routine practice at home. The third theme, peer motivation and reinforcement, revealed that social interaction among classmates played a strong role in sustaining new behaviors, as students reminded and motivated each other to brush regularly and avoid sugary snacks.

Overall, both quantitative and qualitative findings demonstrated that the school-based oral health education program significantly enhanced oral health literacy and led to measurable behavioral improvements among elementary students. The multimedia-based approach proved more effective than conventional education methods, not only in increasing oral health knowledge and awareness but also in translating these improvements into sustained behavioral change and measurable clinical benefits.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a school-based oral health education program significantly improved oral health literacy and behaviors among Indonesian elementary school students. Increases in KAP scores, along with reductions in oral hygiene and caries indices, indicate that students not only acquired oral health information but also translated it into daily practice. These findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that structured educational interventions can effectively improve children’s oral health behaviors.[911]

The observed improvement in knowledge scores reflects enhanced cognitive engagement facilitated by interactive and multimedia-based learning. Visual and contextual educational materials make oral health messages more concrete and memorable, enabling children to better understand the consequences of poor oral hygiene. Similar findings have been reported in recent studies demonstrating that multimedia-supported education improves comprehension and retention of health information among school-aged children.[410]

Improvements in attitudes and practices suggest deeper internalization of oral health behaviors beyond cognitive understanding. Behavioral change in children requires emotional engagement, motivation, and self-regulation, which are reinforced through participatory and peer-supported learning environments. In accordance with social learning principles, children are more likely to adopt health-promoting behaviors when they observe and receive reinforcement from peers and teachers.[713]

Reductions in oral hygiene and caries indices further support the clinical relevance of the behavioral improvements observed in this study. These findings emphasize that oral health education combined with behavioral reinforcement can lead to measurable preventive benefits, whereas information alone is often insufficient to improve clinical outcomes. The inverse association between oral health literacy and clinical indicators aligns with previous studies demonstrating that higher literacy levels are associated with better oral hygiene and reduced caries risk.[614]

Qualitative findings from FGDs provide additional insight into the mechanisms underlying behavioral change. Students perceived multimedia materials as engaging and motivating, which enhanced their confidence and willingness to practice proper oral hygiene. Themes, such as awareness, repetition, and peer reinforcement, highlight the importance of social and environmental factors in sustaining new health behaviors among children.[1516]

Schools play a critical role in promoting oral health by providing a structured environment for repeated exposure to health messages. Integrating oral health education into routine school curricula facilitates habit formation and ensures equitable access to preventive information across socioeconomic groups. These findings support broader health promotion strategies that emphasize early life interventions to improve health literacy and prevent chronic disease.[817]

Overall, this study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting multimedia-based and behaviorally informed oral health education. By addressing cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions of learning, such interventions are more likely to produce sustained behavioral change. Future school-based oral health programs should incorporate multimedia strategies to enhance engagement and long-term effectiveness in improving children’s oral health outcomes.[18]

This study supports the considerable literature on health literacy and preventive practices. Children’s oral health literacy is influenced by exposure, reinforcement, and context (Evaristo-Chiyong TA et al., 2025).[19] Active learning programs, especially those that engage many senses, provide more lasting behavioral results. This study suggests that multimedia-based training should be part of future school-based health interventions since it engages children’s curiosity, inventiveness, and visual learning.

Conclusion

The school-based oral health education program enhanced primary kids’ oral health literacy and behavior. Multimedia and interactive activities improved comprehension, motivation, and hygiene. These findings emphasize the importance of participatory and contextually relevant teaching to foster lifelong oral health in children. This approach also supports national school health promotion policies and aligns with preventive oral health strategies in primary education. Moreover, these findings support integration of multimedia-based oral health education into national school health programs and align with preventive oral health policies targeting early life behavioral modification. This study is novel because it integrates multimedia-based oral health education with both behavioral (KAP) and clinical outcomes (OHI-S and DMFT) using a cluster-randomized longitudinal mixed-methods design in Indonesian elementary school children, a population that remains underrepresented in current oral health literacy research.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The intervention period was relatively short, which may limit understanding of long-term behavioral sustainability. Self-reported questionnaires may introduce social desirability bias. Variation in school environment and teacher engagement may have influenced response rates and learning outcomes. Additionally, although OHI-S and DMFT were assessed, caries progression typically requires a longer period to observe significant changes. Future longitudinal studies with extended follow-up and the use of objective digital monitoring tools are recommended. Clinical examiners were not blinded to group allocation, which may have introduced observer bias and represents a major methodological limitation of this study.

Recommendations

School-based oral health programs should incorporate multimedia learning and interactive demonstrations to improve behavioral outcomes. Collaboration between schools, parents, and community dental services is recommended to maintain oral health routines at home. Future research should assess the cost-effectiveness and scalability of multimedia interventions and utilize digital gamification to enhance self-regulated brushing habits among students. Future studies should evaluate long-term sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and digital gamification-based oral health education strategies using multicenter randomized controlled trials.
