Health-related quality of life after acute myocardial infarction and one-year clinical outcomes: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Beatriz Mantilla Pérez, Alexandra Hurtado-Ortiz, Juliet López, Jose Guerrero, Miguel Enrique Giraldo Romero, Maricel Licht-Ardila, Edgar Fabián Manrique-Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of death worldwide and in Colombia. Beyond survival, AMI can markedly impair health-related quality of life (HRQoL), yet the prognostic value of HRQoL measured during the index hospitalization remains insufficiently explored, particularly in middle-income countries.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study of 1093 adults with type 1 myocardial infarction admitted to a tertiary referral center in Colombia between 2023 and 2025. HRQoL was assessed within 24 h of admission using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level (EQ-5D-3L) instrument and the EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS). Multivariable Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate adjusted risk ratios (RRs) for one-year mortality and rehospitalization.ResultsAt 12 months, all-cause mortality was 4.3% and rehospitalization was 2.6%. Impairment in the mobility domain was independently associated with higher risks of mortality (RR = 1.80; p = 0.045) and rehospitalization (RR = 2.22; p = 0.031). Percutaneous coronary intervention (RR = 0.28; p < 0.001) and male sex (RR = 0.54; p = 0.034) were associated with lower mortality risk.ConclusionBaseline HRQoL assessed during hospitalization for AMI was associated with one-year mortality and rehospitalization. Mobility limitations identified patients at higher risk and may complement conventional clinical risk stratification.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a major manifestation of ischemic heart disease, the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 13% of global deaths in 2021 [1]. In the United States, between 2005 and 2014, the annual incidence of AMI was estimated at approximately 605,000 new cases and 200,000 recurrent events [2]. In Colombia, national statistics from DANE reported 44,703 deaths due to AMI in 2022, making it the first cause of mortality in the country [3]. Although advances in reperfusion strategies and secondary prevention have improved survival, AMI continues to impose a substantial burden that extends beyond mortality alone.

According to the World Health Organization, quality of life is defined as an individual's perception of their position in life within the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns [4]. In the setting of AMI, myocardial injury may lead to ventricular dysfunction, reduced exercise tolerance, functional impairment, and psychological distress, all of which negatively affect health-related quality of life (HRQoL). These limitations may persist beyond the acute event and contribute to recurrent healthcare utilization and long-term morbidity [5].

The EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) instrument is a widely used tool for measuring HRQoL and provides a standardized utility index that allows quantification of health states and comparison across populations [6]. In patients with cardiovascular disease, lower EQ-5D scores have been associated with greater functional and psychosocial limitations. For example, a study conducted in Greece reported significant associations between age and limitations across emotional, physical, social, and pain domains using the EQ-5D-5L instrument [7]. Prior research suggests that impaired HRQoL after cardiovascular events may be linked to increased rehospitalization and mortality risk. However, most studies have focused on HRQoL assessed months after discharge, while the prognostic value of HRQoL measured during the index hospitalization remains insufficiently explored.

Early assessment of HRQoL during the index hospitalization, using the EQ-5D-3L and EQ-VAS within the first 24 h of admission, may provide information on the patient-perceived functional, symptomatic, and emotional burden of AMI. The EQ-5D-3L captures limitations in mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression, whereas the EQ-VAS reflects the patient's overall perception of health. These patient-reported dimensions may complement traditional clinical risk scores, which primarily incorporate demographic, hemodynamic, electrocardiographic, laboratory, and clinical severity variables but do not directly assess the patient's perceived health status [8,9]. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the association between baseline in-hospital HRQoL and one-year all-cause mortality and rehospitalization among patients with AMI.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective cohort study at the Cardiovascular Foundation of Colombia (FCV), a tertiary referral center and Joint Commission International–accredited Center of Excellence for AMI care in Northeast Colombia. The study period spanned from January 2023 to February 2025. The institution delivers comprehensive cardiovascular care through a multidisciplinary team including interventional cardiologists, specialized cardiovascular nurses, psychologists, social workers, nutritionists, and physiotherapists.

Study population

Consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) admitted with a confirmed diagnosis of type 1 MI, either ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), were screened for eligibility. AMI diagnosis was established according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction [10], integrating clinical presentation, electrocardiographic findings, and significant elevation of cardiac biomarkers.

Eligible participants were adults who survived the index hospitalization and completed the baseline HRQoL assessment within the first 24 h of hospital admission. We excluded patients who died during the index hospitalization, duplicate records, incomplete records, and those lost to follow-up (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study flowchart of patient selection and follow-up.

Health-related quality of life assessment

Baseline HRQoL was assessed during the index hospitalization using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level (EQ-5D-3L) instrument. This standardized tool evaluates five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Health states were converted into a single utility index score using the Ecuadorian population-based value set, given the absence of a nationally validated Colombian tariff [11]. The Ecuadorian value set was selected due to geographic and sociocultural proximity.

In addition, patients’ global self-perceived health status was measured using the EQ Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS), ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state). Baseline EQ-5D-3L index and EQ-VAS scores were considered the primary exposure variables [6].

Data collection and variables

Clinical, laboratory, procedural, and sociodemographic data were prospectively recorded by a single trained cardiovascular research nurse using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure web-based electronic data capture platform [12,13]. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, marital status (without partner vs married or cohabiting), living arrangement (living alone vs not living alone), area of residence (rural vs urban), and education level (no education, primary, secondary and university). Medical history variables included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, active smoking within the year prior to admission, alcohol consumption, and self-reported obesity. Anthropometric status was additionally captured through body mass index (BMI) categories (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obesity class I, obesity class II, and obesity class III).

Clinical presentation and severity were characterized by AMI subtype (STEMI vs NSTEMI) and Killip class (I–IV). Risk stratification was further summarized using validated prognostic scores, including TIMI, GRACE, and CRUSADE. Laboratory biomarkers collected during the index hospitalization included troponin and creatinine. Cardiac structure and function were assessed using echocardiography, with left ventricular ejection fraction as the primary parameter. In-hospital management variables included percutaneous coronary intervention, myocardial revascularization, and receipt of cardiac rehabilitation during hospitalization. Pharmacological therapy at discharge, specifically the prescription of Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs) and statins, was also documented. Length of hospital stay was recorded as an intrahospital marker of clinical course.

Outcomes

A structured longitudinal follow-up protocol was implemented through standardized telephone interviews conducted by a trained cardiovascular nurse at 72 h, 1 month, 2 months, 6 months, and 12 months after hospital discharge. These contacts were designed to ascertain survival status, interval clinical events, and hospital utilization.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 12 months after discharge from the index hospitalization. The secondary outcome was all-cause rehospitalization within 12 months, defined a priori as any unplanned hospital admission lasting >48 h, for any cause, occurring at any healthcare institution (not restricted to FCV/HIC). For patients experiencing multiple admissions, rehospitalization was analyzed as a binary event (≥1 rehospitalization vs none) during follow-up. Mortality and rehospitalization were systematically collected via structured telephone surveys and corroborated, when available, with institutional medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were initially evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical inspection. Given the non-normal distribution of HRQoL measures, continuous data were summarized using medians and Percentiles (P25-P75), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Univariate associations between categorical variables and EQ-5D index or EQ-VAS were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Associations between continuous variables and HRQoL outcomes were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Additionally, model fit for the Tobit regression was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which allow evaluation of model adequacy by balancing goodness of fit and model complexity. Multivariable regression analyses were performed to identify variables associated with the EQ-5D index score and the EQ-VAS. Model assumptions and overall fit were examined to ensure the validity of the estimates.

Variables demonstrating clinical relevance or a univariate association threshold of p < 0.20 were considered for multivariable analysis. The EQ-5D domain responses were dichotomized as 0 = no problems and 1 = any problems, given that the original instrument includes three response levels (no problems, some problems, and extreme problems). Adjusted risk ratios (RRs) were estimated using Poisson regression models with robust variance. This approach provides consistent and directly interpretable estimates of RR for binary outcomes. Model adequacy was assessed using the Wald test and goodness-of-fit analyses. Rate ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p values were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Cardiovascular Foundation of Colombia (Comité de Ética en Investigación, CEI-2024-07461-37). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with national regulations governing biomedical research in Colombia, including Resolution 8430 of 1993 issued by the Ministry of Health. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The handling, storage, and processing of personal data were carried out in strict accordance with Colombian data protection regulations, including Law 1581 of 2012 and its regulatory decrees, ensuring confidentiality, secure data management, and protection of participants’ privacy.

Results

A total of 1093 patients with AMI were included in the analysis. The cohort was predominantly male, with 774 men (70.8%) and 319 women (29.2%), at 12-month follow-up, 47 patients died, yielding a cumulative mortality incidence of 4.3%, while 28 individuals (2.6%) experienced at least one rehospitalization during the follow-up period. Overall perceived health status was high, with a median EQ-5D-3L index of 1.0 and a median EQ-VAS score of 80. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors were not significantly associated with baseline quality of life. Patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention reported higher EQ-VAS (p<0.001). Moreover, receipt of cardiac rehabilitation education during hospitalization was associated with higher index values (p<0.001) and better EQ-VAS (p=0.043). No significant differences were observed according to type of myocardial infarction (Table 1).

Baseline HRQoL also showed modest associations with markers of clinical severity. Higher troponin levels correlated with lower EQ-5D index scores, whereas GRACE, TIMI, CRUSADE, length of hospital stay, and LVEF showed selected associations with EQ-5D index and/or EQ-VAS (Supplementary Table S1).

In multivariable analyses of baseline HRQoL, higher troponin levels and class II obesity were associated with lower EQ-5D index values, whereas statin prescription was associated with higher values. ACE inhibitor prescription showed a small inverse association (Supplementary Table S2).

Patients who died during follow-up exhibited significantly lower EQ-5D Index scores compared to survivors (p = 0.047). While the VAS scores also showed a downward trend in the mortality group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.071). Regarding rehospitalization, a significant association was found with the EQ-5D Index (p = 0.041), where readmitted patients reported lower utility scores. Discussion

In this prospective cohort of patients with AMI treated at a tertiary referral center in a middle-income country, we found that HRQoL, assessed during the index hospitalization using the EQ-5D-3L instrument, was associated with one-year mortality and rehospitalization. In particular, impairment in the mobility dimension was independently associated with both outcomes. These findings suggest that early patient-reported health status may capture clinically relevant vulnerability not fully reflected by conventional biomedical variables alone.

Previous studies have increasingly demonstrated the prognostic relevance of patient-reported outcome measures after acute coronary syndromes. Lower self-reported health status has been associated with adverse cardiovascular events and mid-to long-term mortality, even after adjustment for conventional clinical characteristics [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Arnold et al. reported that patient-reported health status after AMI was independently associated with long-term mortality [18], while other studies have examined the prognostic contribution of EQ-5D measurements in cardiovascular populations [15]. Our findings are consistent with this evidence and suggest that HRQoL assessed during the acute hospitalization may already be associated with subsequent clinical outcomes.

Several prognostic scores are used to estimate outcomes after acute coronary syndromes. GRACE and TIMI incorporate demographic, clinical, hemodynamic, electrocardiographic, and laboratory variables, whereas the Intermountain Risk Score has been evaluated for short- and long-term mortality in patients with STEMI [19]. The ACEF score combines age, serum creatinine, and left ventricular ejection fraction and has demonstrated prognostic value for in-hospital mortality among patients with STEMI-related cardiogenic shock [20]. PRECISE-DAPT, although originally developed to estimate bleeding risk during dual antiplatelet therapy, has also been evaluated as a predictor of in-hospital mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI [21]. However, these tools do not directly assess patient-perceived functional limitations, symptom burden, or emotional well-being. HRQoL may therefore provide complementary information, although our study did not formally compare EQ-5D with these scores or evaluate improvements in discrimination, calibration, risk reclassification, or clinical utility; consequently, incremental prognostic value cannot be inferred.

The mobility domain emerged as the most consistently associated dimension with mortality and rehospitalization. From a pathophysiological perspective, mobility limitations may reflect a convergence of multiple risk determinants, including ventricular dysfunction, systemic inflammation, frailty, sarcopenia, and cumulative comorbidity burden [22]. Damluji et al. demonstrated that frailty is independently associated with mortality and complications after AMI [22,23]. Similarly, impaired physical function and reduced functional capacity have been linked to major cardiovascular events. In this context, the mobility dimension of the EQ-5D may serve as a pragmatic surrogate marker of functional reserve in settings where formal frailty assessments are not routinely implemented [24].

Higher troponin levels were associated with lower EQ-5D index scores, suggesting that greater myocardial injury is linked to worse perceived health status at hospital admission, consistent with previous reports showing that infarct severity and in-hospital clinical status are major determinants of impaired HRQoL after AMI 17]. In addition, obesity and other cardiometabolic comorbidities have been consistently associated with lower quality-of-life scores, likely due to reduced functional capacity and increased symptom burden in patients with cardiovascular disease [25].

Conversely, statin prescription was associated with higher utility values, which may reflect better adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy and closer follow-up, factors that have been linked to improved functional recovery and patient-reported outcomes after myocardial infarction [26]. The inverse association observed with ACE inhibitor use may be explained by confounding by indication, since these agents are more frequently prescribed in patients with ventricular dysfunction or higher cardiovascular risk, who tend to report poorer baseline health status [27]. Overall, these findings support that HRQoL after myocardial infarction is strongly influenced by infarct severity, cardiometabolic burden, and the intensity of secondary prevention therapy.

Regarding rehospitalization, our findings align with prior evidence identifying multimorbidity, particularly diabetes as determinants of recurrent hospital use after AMI [28]. Lower HRQoL may reflect not only greater disease burden but also reduced self-care capacity, poorer treatment adherence, or heightened social vulnerability. Emerging evidence indicates that PROMs independently predict readmission risk beyond traditional biomedical markers [29,30]. Therefore, integrating HRQoL assessment into routine clinical evaluation may enhance the identification of patients at higher risk of rehospitalization and inform targeted transitional care strategies.

We also observed an association between PCI and lower one-year mortality, consistent with contemporary evidence supporting timely revascularization as a cornerstone strategy to improve survival in AMI [31,32]. Additionally, NSTEMI was associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization in the adjusted model, which may reflect differences in clinical presentation, infarct size, or patient selection; however, this interpretation warrants caution.

A distinctive contribution of our study is the inclusion of prospective data from Colombia, as most investigations examining the prognostic relevance of HRQoL after AMI have been conducted in Western Europe or North America, within healthcare systems and socioeconomic contexts that differ substantially from those of Latin America [15,17,33]. Despite the high burden of cardiovascular disease and persistent disparities in access to care across the region, evidence from middle-income countries remains limited [34,35]. In these settings, HRQoL assessment may be particularly informative because it may reflect not only clinical and functional vulnerability but also contextual barriers affecting recovery and continuity of care.

These findings may also inform future post-AMI care strategies. Patients reporting mobility limitations could potentially benefit from closer follow-up, early cardiac rehabilitation, and digitally supported interventions aimed at promoting physical activity, medication adherence, symptom monitoring, and continuity of care. Telemedicine, mobile applications, wearable devices, and remote cardiac rehabilitation may be especially useful where access to specialized services and frequent in-person follow-up is limited [[36], [37], [38]]. However, these approaches should be regarded as potential implementation strategies rather than direct implications of our findings, because telemedicine and digital health interventions were not evaluated in this study.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center study, which may limit external generalizability. The use of the Ecuadorian EQ-5D value set, in the absence of a validated Colombian tariff, may introduce variability in utility estimation. Moreover, HRQoL measured during the acute phase may be influenced by the emotional and clinical impact of the index event. The relatively low number of outcome events may have limited statistical power for more detailed subgroup analyses. In addition, dichotomizing EQ-5D-3L domains as no problems versus any problems may have resulted in some loss of information from the original three-level response structure. HRQoL was assessed only at baseline; therefore, we could not evaluate longitudinal changes in HRQoL or determine whether recovery trajectories were associated with subsequent outcomes. Accordingly, the EQ-5D-3L findings should be interpreted as reflecting the five health dimensions included in the instrument rather than a comprehensive assessment of all factors affecting quality of life. Nonetheless, early HRQoL assessment represents a conceptual strength, as it captures patient-perceived functional and psychosocial status at a moment of heightened clinical vulnerability.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort of patients hospitalized with AMI in a tertiary center in Colombia, baseline HRQoL measured during the index admission was associated with one-year all-cause mortality and rehospitalization. Among EQ-5D dimensions, reporting mobility problems showed an independent association with both outcomes, suggesting that early functional limitations may help identify patients at higher risk during follow-up. These findings support the potential usefulness of incorporating a brief HRQoL assessment during hospitalization as a complement to established clinical risk stratification, particularly in middle-income settings. Further multicenter studies with longitudinal HRQoL measurements and locally derived EQ-5D value sets are warranted to confirm generalizability and clarify how patient-reported outcomes can be integrated into post-AMI care pathways.
