N6-methyladenosine modification of FZR1 mRNA positively regulates antiviral innate immunity by targeting the MAVS–TRAF3/6 axis
Authors: Kaiwen Dou, Yiyi Hu, Mingyang Li, Jiale Xu, Weihao Zhang, Hui Wang, Qi Cheng, Zihao Jiang, Liangjun Chen, Shi Liu, Ke Chen, Yuanyuan Liu, Hairong Xiong, Fan Luo, Wei Hou, Shuliang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFizzy-related protein 1 (FZR1) is essential for mitotic exit and G1/S transition, yet its role in antiviral immunity remains poorly characterized. This study reveals that m6A modification of FZR1 mRNA enhances its translation during RNA viral infection, thereby strengthening antiviral innate immunity. FZR1 promotes mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) aggregation by competing with the glycolytic enzyme 6-Phosphofructo-2-Kinase/Fructose-2, 6-Biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) and facilitates K63-linked autoubiquitination of TNF receptor-associated factor 3/6 (TRAF3/6), independently of APC/C. We also found that the chemotherapeutic agent Paclitaxel (PTX) downregulated antiviral immunity by degrading FZR1. Overall, our findings uncover a critical posttranscriptional regulatory mechanism that strengthens interferon responses and provide mechanistic insight into how PTX impairs antiviral immunity by degrading FZR1.

Innate immunity constitutes the host’s primary defense against microbial pathogens (1). Upon infection, host cells detect viral invaders through germline-encoded pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), and cytosolic DNA sensors such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and interferon gamma inducible protein 16 (IFI16) (2). RIG-I, a prototypical RLR, senses cytoplasmic viral RNA and initiates immune signaling through mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) (3). MAVS subsequently activates TBK1/IKKε and IKKα/β kinases, which phosphorylate transcription factors interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), respectively, driving the production of type I interferon (IFN-I) and proinflammatory cytokines (4). IFN-I induces expression of many interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including ISG15 and ISG56 (also known as IFIT1), establishing a robust antiviral state (5, 6).

MAVS serves as a critical signaling hub linking viral RNA recognition to immune activation (7, 8). Upon detection of viral RNA, RIG-I undergoes conformational changes and oligomerization, exposing its caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) to engage the N-terminal CARD of MAVS (9). This interaction triggers MAVS prion-like aggregation, a hallmark of its activation and essential for antiviral function (10). Aggregated MAVS scaffolds tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factors (TRAF-2, -3, -5, and -6) through specific TRAF-binding motifs, activating downstream kinases (11). MAVS aggregation is tightly regulated, such as aldehyde dehydrogenase 1B1 (ALDH1B1) (12), sorting nexin 8 (SNX8) (13), ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18) (14) and tripartite motif-containing protein 31 (TRIM31) (15) promote MAVS prion-like aggregation, whereas Rac1 GTPase (16), Fas-associated factor 1 (FAF1) (17), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 5B (COX5B) (18), and protein arginine methyltransferase 9 (PRMT9) (19) inhibit this process. These findings collectively underscore MAVS prion-like aggregation as a critically regulated event in antiviral responses. However, the molecular mechanisms governing MAVS aggregation remain incompletely characterized.

Ubiquitination is a pivotal posttranslational modification that regulates IFN-I signaling (20, 21). During RNA virus infection, RIG-I, MAVS, and TRAF3/6 are ubiquitinated by E3 ligases, yielding distinct functional outcomes (22–24). K48-linked polyubiquitination targets substrates for proteasomal degradation, establishing negative feedback, while K63-linked chains activate downstream signaling and enhance IFN-α/β transcription (25). Multiple E3 ligases modulate RLR pathway components. The RING-UIM family member RNF166 interacts with TRAF3 and TRAF6 through its RING domain, enhances the ubiquitylation of TRAF3 and TRAF6, and positively regulates RNA virus-triggered IFN-β production (26). NEDD4L directly interacts with TRAF3 and catalyzes its K29-linked ubiquitylation at the Cys56 and Cys124 sites (27). HECT-type E3 (AIP4) can be hijacked by virus to degrade MAVS, TRAF3, and TRAF6 (28). In addition, galectin 3 binding protein (LGALS3BP), a non-E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been shown to enhance TRAF3 and TRAF6 autoubiquitination (29).

As one of the most prevalent RNA modifications, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) plays critical roles in various biological processes (30). Studies have shown that m6A is involved in regulating the life cycle of numerous viruses. For instance, it promotes the replication of simian virus 40 (SV40) and HIV, while suppressing that of hepatitis B virus (HBV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) (31). Beyond directly modulating viral RNA to influence replication, m6A has also been implicated in regulating immune responses during infection. Viral infection can alter m6A methylation levels on specific cellular transcripts, thereby affecting their translation or alternative splicing (32). Mechanistically, m6A and its associated cellular machinery contribute to this regulation through the posttranscriptional control of mRNAs encoding key molecules in the innate immune response to viruses. Specifically, m6A sites have been identified within the 3′ UTRs of TRAF3, TRAF6, MAVS, and IFN-I mRNAs (33, 34).

Fizzy-related protein 1 (FZR1, also known as Cdc20 homologue 1, Cdh1), a substrate adaptor for the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) (35), governs cell cycle progression and genomic integrity by targeting Cyclin B, Securin, Geminin, and Cdc20 for ubiquitin-mediated degradation (36). Emerging evidence indicates FZR1 functions extend beyond cell cycle regulation, encompassing DNA repair, neuronal development, and metabolic regulation including nucleotide synthesis and glycolysis. Notably, FZR1 operates independently of APC/C in certain contexts, as demonstrated by its regulation of osteoblast function and tumorigenesis through Smurf1 and WWP2 modulation (37, 38). A recent study has shown methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)-mediated m6A modification of FZR1 mRNA enhances its translation, promoting gemcitabine insensitivity in pancreatic cancer (39). Many viral proteins have been shown to interact with FZR1, derailing cell cycle progression in order to facilitate viral replication (40). However, the potential involvement of FZR1 in antiviral immunity and the RIG-I-MAVS pathway remains unexplored.

Here, we show that m6A modification of FZR1 mRNA and expression of FZR1 protein are significantly upregulated in the host cells infected with RNA viruses, including vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), and Sendai virus (SeV) or stimulated with poly (I:C). FZR1 promotes antiviral immunity by targeting the MAVS–TRAF3/6 signaling axis. Mechanistically, FZR1 reduced the binding of MAVS to PFKFB3, which led to enhanced MAVS aggregation. FZR1 promotes the recruitment of TRAF3/6 by MAVS and the activation of TRAF3/6 by K63 ubiquitination. Consequently, FZR1 facilitated the RLR-triggered activation of IRF3 and NF-κB as well as the expression of IFN-I and proinflammatory cytokines. Consistently, knockdown of FZR1 leads to higher viral burden by impairing IFN-I-dependent antiviral innate immunity in vivo. Furthermore, Paclitaxel (PTX), an anticancer drug, downregulated antiviral immunity by degrading FZR1 and promoted the IAV replication in vitro and in vivo.

Results

Viral Infection Upregulates FZR1 Protein via m6A Modification of Its mRNA.

To explore the antiviral potential of FZR1, we first analyzed FZR1 expression during RNA virus infection. HEK293T cells were infected with VSV, SeV, or stimulated with poly(I:C) for 0, 6, 12, or 24 h. We found that the level of FZR1 protein but not mRNA was significantly elevated compared with the untreated cells (Fig. 1 A and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). To demonstrate the reliability of the experimental infection system, we measured the expression of ISG15 as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 1B, both mRNA and protein levels of ISG15 were induced by VSV infection, confirming the interferon response to VSV infection in our system. Furthermore, the increase in protein but not mRNA level of FZR1 was also observed in A549 (Fig. 1D) and HeLa cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) after VSV infection, suggesting it is not a cell type–specific phenotype.

Viral infection triggered the production of IFN-I, which activates the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and subsequently induces the expression of ISGs (5). To test whether FZR1 could be induced through the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, we knocked out the expression of interferon alpha/beta receptor subunit 1 (IFNAR1) in A549 cells. The phosphorylation of STAT1 was significantly impaired in IFNAR1-silenced cells upon IFN-β treatment (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, when IFNAR1 knockout A549 cells were infected with VSV, FZR1 expression showed a similar pattern compared to wild-type (WT) cells (Fig. 1F vs. Fig. 1D). This result suggests that the increase of FZR1 protein in these cells responding to viral infection is not regulated by a mRNA transcriptional process, indicating that alternative regulatory mechanisms may be posttranscriptional modification or degradation. We thus hypothesized that virus infection affects FZR1 protein synthesis or degradation. To test this hypothesis, HEK293T cells with or without VSV infection were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) or the proteasome inhibitor MG132. The Western blot results showed that VSV infection markedly increased the protein synthesis of FZR1 but did not affect the half-life of the FZR1 protein (Fig. 1 G and H), suggesting that VSV infection increased the protein level of FZR1 by enhancing its translation.

m6A is a prevalent modification in cellular mRNA (41). It has been reported that the m6A modification exists in the 3′-UTR of FZR1 mRNA (Fig. 1I) and that targeting its modification can regulate the protein level of FZR1 (39). We hypothesized that VSV infection increased FZR1 mRNA m6A modification. Of note, m6A immunoprecipitation (m6A-IP) followed by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) revealed that the ratio of m6A-modified/total FZR1 mRNA in HEK293T cells after VSV infection or stimulated with poly(I:C) was significantly increased (Fig. 1J and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). RIP assays demonstrated that VSV infection promotes the binding of the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 to FZR1 mRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). A previous study has indicated that VSV infection induces phosphorylation of METTL3 at serine 67 (S67), which is mediated by the kinase TBK1 (32), an upstream component of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Consistent with this report (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E–G), TBK1 knockout in HEK293T cells abolished the VSV-induced increase in m6A modification levels of FZR1 mRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). We next performed knockout of METTL3 and examined its effects on FZR1 expression. Knockout of METTL3 significantly reduced the protein level of FZR1 but did not change its mRNA level (Fig. 1 K and L). To assess the requirement of VSV infection for regulation of FZR1 translation, we conducted dual luciferase reporter assays. As expected, compared with mock cells, VSV-infected cells increased the translation efficiency of the luciferase reporter constructs bearing the FZR1 3′-UTR (Fig. 1M), while the reporter gene carrying FZR1 5′-UTR or CDS was not changed (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 I and J). A previous study mapped an m6A modification site at adenosine 774 (A774) within the FZR1 3′-UTR (39). Site-directed mutagenesis of this residue to thymidine (A774T) in a reporter construct abrogated the VSV-induced enhancement of reporter gene translation efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S1K). Taken together, these results suggest that virus infection increases FZR1 protein expression by upregulating m6A modification of FZR1 mRNA.

FZR1 Inhibits RNA Virus Infection.

The above finding that FZR1 was a potential virus-induced host factor led us to investigate its influence on cellular antiviral activity. As the results showed, ectopic expression of FZR1 significantly reduced the RNA levels of VSV, IAV (H1N1), and EV71, and the VP1 protein level of EV71 was also decreased (Fig. 2 A, C, and G). Fluorescence staining also showed that FZR1 overexpression decreased the abundance of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged VSV (Fig. 2B). In a VSV plaque assay, we observed a lower viral titer in FZR1-transfected A549 cells relative to the control cells (Fig. 2J). In contrast, knockdown of FZR1 promoted the replication of VSV, H1N1, and EV71, and the VP1 protein levels of EV71 were increased (Fig. 2 D, F, and H). FZR1 knockdown increased the abundance of GFP-tagged VSV (Fig. 2E). Expression of FZR1 plasmid with Flag- tag and the knockdown efficiency of endogenous FZR1 in HEK293T cells were measured by western blotting analysis, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). To exclude the possible off-target effects of FZR1-specific shRNA treatment, we demonstrated that reintroduction of a nontargeted version of FZR1 in FZR1 knockdown A549 cells led to a significant reduction in the expression of RNA levels of H1N1 (Fig. 2F). Consistently, VSV titers were higher in FZR1-knockdown A549 cells than that in control cells (Fig. 2K). Interestingly, we tested the effect of FZR1 on VSV replication in the Vero cell line, which lacks functional IFN-I genes. The result indicated that VSV titer was not affected by FZR1 overexpression in Vero cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Further, overexpression of FZR1 had no effect on the replication of either H1N1 or VSV in IFNAR1-knockout A549 cells (Fig. 2 I and L), suggesting that FZR1’s antiviral activity is strongly dependent on IFN-I response. Taken together, these results suggest that FZR1 can inhibit RNA virus replication through the interferon signaling pathway.

FZR1 Promotes RNA Virus-Triggered Innate Immune Signaling.

To analyze the functional role of FZR1 protein in virus-triggered innate immune signaling pathway, an overexpression experiment was conducted to detect transcription factor (IRF3 and NF-κB) activation (42). HEK293T or HeLa cells were cotransfected with FZR1-expressing plasmids and IFN-β promoter prior to VSV infection. Overexpression of FZR1 significantly promoted VSV-triggered activation of IFN-β promoter in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3 A and B). Consistent with these results, we found that overexpression of FZR1 potentiated VSV-triggered activation of the IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE) and NF-κB luciferase reporters (Fig. 3 C and D).

To further explore the effect of FZR1 on antiviral immunity, we applied the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology to deplete FZR1 expression (sg-FZR1), effectively knocking out FZR1 in HeLa, A549, and HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). To determine if FZR1 plays a biological role in virus-triggered expression of IFN-I and proinflammatory genes, qRT-PCR was performed to analyze the mRNA levels of IFNB1, IL6, TNFα, CXCL10, and ISG56. We found that their mRNA levels were down-regulated in FZR1 knockout HeLa and HEK293T cells relative to those in control HeLa (Fig. 3E) and HEK293T cells (Fig. 3F) in response to VSV infection or poly(I:C) transfection (Fig. 3G). To rule out off-target effects, we reconstituted the expression of FZR1 in FZR1 knockout HEK293T and HeLa cells with an FZR1-expressing plasmid that was resistant to Cas9 cleavage. We found that the enhancement effect of FZR1 ablation on the expression of IFN-I and proinflammatory genes was reversed with the reconstitution of FZR1 (Fig. 3 E and F). Consistent with these findings, overexpression of FZR1 substantially promoted the phosphorylation of IRF3 and P65 induced by VSV infection, which is a prerequisite for IFN-I induction (Fig. 3H). In addition, FZR1 knockout cells exhibited decreased phosphorylation of IRF3 and P65 compared with control cells after VSV infection (Fig. 3I). These results indicate that FZR1 enhances RNA virus-triggered innate immune response.

FZR1 Targets MAVS, TRAF3, and TRAF6.

To identify the targets of FZR1, we analyzed how FZR1 affected IFN-β promoter activity regulated via different adaptors. Upon FZR1 overexpression, RIG-I-N (43) (a constitutively active variant) and MAVS-mediated activation of IFN-β promoter was markedly increased and TBK1-mediated IFN-β promoter activity was slightly increased. However, IRF3/5D- (activated form of IRF3), cGAS, or STING-mediated activation of the IFN-β promoter was not significantly different (Fig. 4A). These data suggest that FZR1 promotes the IFN-I signaling pathway upstream of TBK1.

To determine the key factors in the IFN-I signaling pathway that could be targeted by FZR1, we cotransfected HEK293T cells with constructs expressing Flag-FZR1 with HA-tagged innate immune signaling proteins. As revealed by a coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay, FZR1 interacted with MAVS, TRAF3, and TRAF6 but not RIG-I, MDA5, NEMO, TBK1, TAK1, IκBa, IRF3, and P65 (Fig. 4B). The interactions of FZR1 with MAVS, TRAF3, and TRAF6 (TRAF3/6) were confirmed by reciprocal Co-IP experiments, in which RIG-I served as a negative control (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–D). Furthermore, endogenous Co-IP assays showed that the interaction between FZR1 and MAVS, TRAF3/6 was increased upon VSV infection and varied in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 4 C and D). In line with these results, confocal microscopy showed that FZR1 was colocalized with MAVS, TRAF3/6 in HeLa cells under VSV infection (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H and I). Interestingly, we noticed that the subcellular localization of FZR1 could change during viral infection. Nuclear and cytoplasmic separation experiments demonstrated that FZR1 is mainly located in the nucleus in the resting state (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Following 3 h of VSV infection, cytoplasmic FZR1 protein levels increase significantly, while nuclear levels decrease (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–D). In time-course experiments, FZR1 protein levels began to increase at 6 h post–VSV infection, which is consistent with the temporal pattern of VSV-induced m6A modification on FZR1 mRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). Collectively, these results suggest that VSV infection triggers a biphasic regulation of FZR1, which include initial nucleocytoplasmic translocation, and followed by a subsequent increase in protein abundance.

Next, we further examined the domain that binds FZR1 to MAVS and TRAF3/6. As revealed by Co-IP assays with various FZR1, MAVS, and TRAF3/6 truncation mutants (29, 44), FZR1 formed precipitates with amino acids 361 to 540 rather than amino acids 1 to 180 or 181 to 360 of MAVS (Fig. 4F), with amino acids 1 to 266 and 347 to 568 of TRAF3 (Fig. 4G) and with all domains of TRAF6 (Fig. 4H). Additionally, the WD40 domain (amino acids 182 to 496) of FZR1 might be necessary for its interaction with MAVS (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E), while the N-terminal domain (amino acids 1 to 181) of FZR1 mainly mediated the binding of FZR1 to TRAF3/6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Because FZR1 interacts with MAVS and TRAF3/6, we reasoned that FZR1 might enhance the interaction between MAVS and TRAF3/6 (11). Results from endogenous Co-IP experiments indicated that the interaction between MAVS and TRAF3/6 was substantially enhanced by FZR1 overexpression (SI Appendix, Fig. S4J). We further performed the above experiments using FZR1 knockout cells, and the results indicated that FZR1 deficiency decreased VSV-induced MAVS and TRAF3/6 association (SI Appendix, Fig. S4K). To further corroborate this conclusion from a structural standpoint, we modeled the potential interaction patterns among FZR1, MAVS, and TRAF3/6 using AlphaFold3. The prediction indicates that the WD40 domain of FZR1 is positioned to interact with MAVS, while its N-terminal domain is positioned to interact with TRAF3/6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 L and M). This structural model is consistent with the aforementioned functional data. Collectively, these data indicate that FZR1 interacts with MAVS and TRAF3/6.

FZR1 Downregulates Glycolysis and Promotes MAVS Aggregation by Targeting PFKFB3.

We then sought to examine how the interaction between MAVS and FZR1 is modulated during viral infection. Since FZR1 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase (36), we speculated that it might affect MAVS ubiquitination. It has been shown, upon viral infection, MAVS undergoes polyubiquitination and subsequently aggregates to recruit and activate downstream signaling effectors (8, 10). However, our data showed that overexpression of FZR1 did not have an effect on MAVS ubiquitination (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). In addition, gradient overexpression of FZR1 does not affect the protein levels of MAVS and TRAF3/6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Interestingly, the semidenaturing detergent agarose-gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE) results revealed that FZR overexpression increased VSV-induced MAVS aggregation in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). Consistently, knockout of FZR1 reduced MAVS aggregation in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5B). While the aggregation and activation of MAVS in cells have been reported to depend on its ubiquitination (15), this suggests that FZR1 promotes MAVS aggregation through a mechanism that is independent of its ubiquitination.

FZR1 has been shown to target PFKFB3 to affect glycolysis and reduces lactate production while lactate can inhibit MAVS aggregation (45, 46). Thus, we hypothesized that FZR1 might increase MAVS aggregation by reducing lactate production. To test this hypothesis, we employed an unbiased, systemic metabolomics approach to examine the global metabolic changes during FZR1 overexpression. Intriguingly, most metabolic intermediates downstream of glucose, such as Fructose-1, 6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP), pyruvate, and lactate, exhibited decreased levels after FZR1 overexpression in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5C). Consistently, overexpression of FZR1 downregulated the lactate level in HeLa cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). In addition, VSV-infected cells showed lower lactate levels than control cells (Fig. 5D). Based on these observed changes in metabolite levels, we inferred that FZR1 could interfere with the glycolysis, potentially affecting the regulation of key enzymes. The analysis revealed that a gradual downregulation of PFKFB3 expression, but not hexokinase2 (HK2) or lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), during the initial 12 h after VSV infection (Fig. 5E). Consistent with a previous report (47), our results showed that FZR1 mediated PFKFB3 degradation via the proteasome pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F).

To test the effect of PFKFB3 on MAVS aggregation, we first constructed HEK293T cells with stable knockdown of PFKFB3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). After VSV infection, MAVS aggregation was increased in PFKFB3-knowdown cells (Fig. 5F). Consistently, PFKFB3 knockdown not only decreased glycolysis determined by lactate levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H) but also markedly enhanced TBK1-IRF3 signaling and IFN-β mRNA level (Fig. 5 G and H). Moreover, restoration of PFKFB3 expression compromised the heightened effect of PFKFB3 knockdown on IFN-β induction (Fig. 5G). Next, we explored whether PFKFB3 knockdown directly affected MAVS aggregation or caused by the decrease of downstream lactate. Of note, addition of lactate into PFKFB3 knockdown cells still increased VSV-induced MAVS aggregation (Fig. 5I). Furthermore, lactate add-back also had no effect on FZR1 overexpression promoting MAVS aggregation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6I). To further rule out the potential confounding effect of lactate on PFKFB3-mediated suppression of MAVS aggregation, control and PFKFB3 knockdown HEK293T cells were treated with sodium oxalate (Oxa), a lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor. This treatment effectively and comparably reduced intracellular lactate levels in both cell groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S6J). Following the Oxa treatment, VSV-induced MAVS aggregation remained significantly higher in the PFKFB3-knockdown group than that in the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S6K).

It has been reported that HK2 inhibits the RIG-I-MAVS signaling axis by directly binding to MAVS (46). We hypothesized that PFKFB3, a kinase downstream of HK2, has similar functions. To test this hypothesis, Co-IP in HEK293T was performed with an anti-MAVS antibody. The results showed that MAVS interacts with PFKFB3 but not LDHA (Fig. 5J). Remarkably, the interaction between MAVS and PFKFB3 declined during activation of RLR signaling triggered by VSV infection (Fig. 5K). To verify whether this process was caused by the competitive binding of FZR1 to MAVS, Vector or Flag-FZR1 plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells, and then, Co-IP was performed with an anti-PFKFB3 antibody. The results showed that overexpression of FZR1 impaired PFKFB3 binding to MAVS (Fig. 5L). To identify the key region of MAVS for the interaction with PFKFB3, Myc-PFKFB3, and HA-MAVS or truncation mutants were cotransfected into HEK293T cells, and Co-IP was performed with an anti-HA antibody. The results showed that the C-terminal (amino acid 361 to 540) of MAVS mainly mediated the interaction with PFKFB3 (Fig. 5 M and N). To directly assess whether PFKFB3 inhibits MAVS aggregation, an in vitro reconstitution assay was performed. Crude mitochondrial fractions (P5) isolated from HEK293T cells were incubated with purified GST–RIG-I-N and unanchored K63-linked ubiquitin chains in a cell-free system. As previously reported, the addition of these components induced MAVS aggregation, as detected by SDD-AGE (10). However, the inclusion of recombinant PFKFB3 in this system markedly attenuated MAVS aggregate formation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6L). To further confirm the above results, we overexpressed FZR1 in PFKFB3 knockdown cells and found that FZR1 lost its role in promoting MAVS aggregation (Fig. 5O). Together, our results suggest that FZR1 promotes MAVS aggregation by competing with PFKFB3 for MAVS binding and contributes to glycolysis inhibition through PFKFB3 degradation.

FZR1 Facilitates TRAF3 and TRAF6 Autoubiquitination Independently of the APC/C Complex.

We next investigated whether FZR1 affected TRAF3/6 ubiquitination. Co-IP experiments with anti-TRAF3, anti-TRAF6, and anti-HA antibodies showed that TRAF3/6 ubiquitination increased with FZR1 overexpression (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). Various Myc-tagged ubiquitin mutants (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63), where only one of these seven lysine residues could be reserved and the other lysine residues were mutated, were used to identify lysine residues within ubiquitin necessary for FZR1-mediated polymeric ubiquitin chain generation. As shown in Fig. 6 C and D, the K63 mutation of ubiquitin was associated with TRAF3/6 in an efficient manner relative to WT ubiquitin, indicating that FZR1 markedly promoted K63-linked TRAF3/6 polyubiquitination. However, after replacing the K63 of ubiquitin with arginine (K63R), FZR1 did not promote TRAF3/6 polyubiquitination (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F).

FZR1 has been reported to serve as a coactivator that assembles into an E3 ubiquitin ligase with the APC/C core, whereas CDC27 is one of the intrinsic subunits of that APC/C core (36). In keeping with previous reports (37), CDC27 interacts with FZR1 but not FZR1 C-Box deletion mutant (FZR1 ΔC-Box) (Fig. 6 E and G). The latter failed to degrade conventional APC substrates such as Cyclin B or PFKFB3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D), because of its E3 ligase activity deficient due to its impaired ability to interact with the APC core complex. However, TRAF3/6 could associate with either FZR1 or FZR1 ΔC-Box (Fig. 6 F and G). We hypothesized that FZR1 can augment TRAF3/6 ubiquitination through direct association with them, and independent of its own intrinsic E3 ligase function. Consistent with this hypothesis, HEK293T cells were transfected with the FZR1 C-Box deletion mutant (ΔC-Box), E3 ubiquitin ligase activity was deprived, and it still promoted TRAF3/6 polyubiquitination (Fig. 6 H and J). It was reported FZR1 could inhibit or promote the autoactivation of multiple NEDD4 E3 ligase family proteins in an APC/C-independent manner and TRAF3/6 have been shown to undergo K63-linked autoubiquitination (38, 48). We next sought to determine whether FZR1 could also promote TRAF3/6 autoubiquitination. It has been reported that TRAF3-C68A and TRAF6-C70A mutation in the RING domain abolishes the ligase activity of TRAF3/6 and TRAF6-K124 is the predominant ubiquitin acceptor site for TRAF6-mediated autoubiquitination (48, 49). We next investigated the role of those two residues in FZR1-regulated TRAF3/6 ubiquitination. Results showed that FZR1 failed to induce polyubiquitination of TRAF3-C68A, TRAF6-C70A, and TRAF6-K124R mutants (Fig. 6 I and K). In addition, overexpression of both FZR1 and FZR1(ΔC-Box) increased VSV-induced transcription of IFNB1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7G).

To further exclude the possibility that FZR1-promoted ubiquitination of TRAF3/6 was a consequence of MAVS activation, we generated MAVS knockout HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7H). Consistent with our earlier findings, overexpression of FZR1 enhanced the ubiquitination of TRAF3/6 both in MAVS-knockout and control cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 I and J). Moreover, FZR1 itself is not a substrate of the E3 ubiquitin ligases TRAF3 or TRAF6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 K and L). In in vitro ubiquitination assays, purified GST-FZR1 expressed in a prokaryotic system promoted autoubiquitination of WT His-TRAF3/6, but did not stimulate ubiquitination of the catalytic mutants His-TRAF3(C68A) and His-TRAF6(C70A) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–C). These results indicate that FZR1 facilitates TRAF3 and TRAF6 autoubiquitination and the E3-ligase activity of FZR1 is not required for TRAF3/6 ubiquitination.

FZR1 Is Required for Protecting Mice against VSV Infection.

APC/C–FZR1 specifically promotes protein degradation during late mitosis and G1. Embryos lacking FZR1 die at E9.5–E10.5 due to defects in trophoblast cell endoreduplication and placental malfunction (50). To investigate its role in antiviral innate immunity in adult mice, we employed an siRNA-mediated knockdown approach. Mice were administered control siRNA (si-Ctrl) or siRNA targeting FZR1 (si-FZR1) via tail-vein injection on two consecutive days. At 72 h after the first injection, mice were infected intravenously with VSV for 24 h (Fig. 7A). FZR1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in the liver and lung of si-FZR1–treated mice (Fig. 7B), and FZR1 protein was likewise decreased in lung tissue (Fig. 7C). Following VSV challenge, both Ifnb1 and Tnfα mRNA levels in liver and lung were lower in the si-FZR1 group than in the si-Ctrl group (Fig. 7 D and E). Expression of the representative interferon-stimulated gene Isg56 was also markedly attenuated in FZR1-deficient liver and lung after infection (Fig. 7 D and E). Consistent with the mRNA data, IFN-β and TNF-α protein levels were significantly reduced upon FZR1 knockdown (Fig. 7 F and G). Moreover, VSV RNA expression was higher in si-FZR1 mice than in controls at 24 h post-infection (Fig. 7H). Histopathological analysis revealed more severe lung injury in si-FZR1 mice, characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar wall thickening, and alveolar space reduction (Fig. 7I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that FZR1 down-regulation impairs IFN-I–dependent antiviral innate immunity and that FZR1 is essential for protection against VSV infection in mice.

PTX Impairs Antiviral Immunity In Vitro and In Vivo by Degrading FZR1.

FZR1 protein expression is regulated by the proteasome pathway. Numerous microtubule-targeting agents, such as PTX, a well-known anticancer drug, can regulate FZR1 expression (51). Studies reported that the combination of oncolytic virotherapy and PTX promotes viral replication in various tumors by downregulating IFN-β production (52–54), although the exact mechanism remains unclear. The above results prompted us to consider whether PTX impaired IFN-β production by downregulating FZR1. To verify our hypothesis, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments. Consistent with previous reports (51), PTX decreased FZR1 levels in HEK293T and HeLa cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). The control knockout HEK293T cells treated with PTX markedly decreased the production of IFN-β and ISG56, and TBK1-IRF3 signaling, while this effect disappeared in FZR1 knockout HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C–E). Moreover, the effect of PTX on TRAF3/6 ubiquitination and MAVS aggregation was also abolished in FZR1 knockout HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F–H). In addition, we found that PTX promoted the replication of H1N1 at the mRNA and protein levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 I and J).

Finally, we also investigated the antiviral immune effects of PTX in vivo; C57BL/6J mice were treated with PTX (10 mg/kg) seven times and infected with H1N1 virus (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Mice treated with PTX via intraperitoneal injection for 7 d did not lose weight (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B), indicating PTX had no obvious toxicity to mice and FZR1 protein expression in multiple organs (lung, spleen, and liver) of mice was reduced upon PTX treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). Mice receiving the PTX treatment exhibited lower resistance than the control group upon H1N1 infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). Furthermore, when challenged with H1N1, mice treated with PTX showed much lower IFN-β and proinflammatory cytokines induction in the lung tissue or serum compared to that in control mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F). Consistent with elevated IFN-β level, the viral NP RNA abundance and viral load in the lung tissues of PTX-treated mice were significantly higher than that in the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 G and H). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining results of lung tissues from mice at 4 d postinfection also confirmed that mice treated with PTX had more severe pathological changes than the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S10I). These results clearly showed that PTX treatment suppressed H1N1-induced IFN-β production and inflammatory responses, thereby promoting the replication of H1N1 in vitro and in vivo by targeting FZR1 degradation.

Discussion

Maintenance of immune homeostasis requires precise regulation of antiviral signaling (55). Within the RLR pathway, viral infection triggers sequential activation of key signaling proteins. MAVS, a central adaptor in this pathway, undergoes prion-like aggregation. These MAVS aggregates serve as molecular scaffolds that recruit TRAF-2, -3, -5, -6 through specific TRAF-binding motifs, thereby promoting transcription of IFN-I and proinflammatory cytokines (56). Herein, we identify FZR1 as a host factor that potentiates antiviral innate immunity by targeting the MAVS–TRAF3/6 signaling axis.

The APC/C is an evolutionarily conserved multisubunit E3 ubiquitin ligase that primarily regulates the metaphase-to-anaphase transition during mitosis (57). Over the past decade, multiple viral proteins have been identified that target the APC/C substrate adaptors FZR1 (Cdh1) and CDC20 (40). Human T cell leukemia virus type 1(HTLV-1), the etiological agent of adult T cell leukemia, induces unscheduled CDC20 activation via its Tax oncoprotein, driving premature mitosis, genomic instability, and senescence. Another tumor-associated virus, HPV, employs its E2 protein to interact with CDC20 and FZR1. This interaction triggers G2/M cell-cycle arrest and metaphase-restricted apoptosis while the virus drives oncogenic transformation. Moreover, HCMV specifically targets FZR1 to force cell cycle reentry in quiescent cells, culminating in premature senescence. Given this conserved viral subversion of APC/C components, we hypothesized that FZR1 modulates antiviral responses. Our data demonstrate that FZR1 enhances RNA virus-induced activation of IRF3 and NF-κB signaling cascades, potentiating interferon and proinflammatory cytokine production to restrict viral replication. While CDC20 shares structural homology with FZR1, its immunological functions remain uncharacterized, which can be a focus for future investigation.

m6A represents the most prevalent internal posttranscriptional modification in mammalian RNA (30). This modification is catalyzed by the m6A methyltransferase complex (“writer”), whose core components include METTL3 and METTL14. m6A methylation dynamically regulates multiple RNA processing events (58), including RNA splicing, subcellular localization, stability, and translation efficiency. In pancreatic cancer cells, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of FZR1 contributes to gemcitabine insensitivity due to its promotion of FZR1 translation (39). Our experiments confirmed that VSV infection can increase the m6A modification of FZR1 and increase its translation. Notably, study has shown that VSV infection promoted TBK1 interacted with METTL3 directly, which promoted the phosphorylation and activation of METTL3 (32). This may lead to the increase in FZR1 m6A modification. In addition, the increase of FZR1 protein levels induced by VSV occurred 6 h after virus infection, while FZR1 translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm at the early stage of virus infection (3 h). We speculate that this might be a way for the host to quickly respond to viral infection.

FZR1 is a WD40 repeat-containing protein which interacts with MAVS through its WD40 domain. Previous studies have shown that the glycolysis-promoting enzyme PFKFB3 can be degraded by the E3 ubiquitin ligase APC/C-FZR1 (45), which reduced lactate production while lactate could inhibit MAVS aggregation (46). Our study found that PFKFB3 can directly bind to and inhibit MAVS aggregation while FZR1 promotes MAVS aggregation by competing with PFKFB3 for MAVS binding. Truncation mutant experiments revealed that FZR1 and PFKFB3 both bind to the C-terminal domain (amino acids 360 to 540) of MAVS. We speculated that the binding of FZR1 might occupy the binding site of PFKFB3 on MAVS. In contrast, another WD repeat-containing protein, WDR77, can also inhibit MAVS aggregation in antiviral signaling (59).

FZR1 mainly plays an important role in mitosis, and its function in different species is very conservative. Generally, FZR1 acts as an E3 ubiquitin ligase to ubiquitinate and degrade substrates (60). However, recent studies have demonstrated the ability of FZR1 to regulate cancer-associated functions independent of the APC/C (37, 38). Ubiquitination is essential for TRAF3/6 activation. Our results show FZR1 promotes TRAF3/6 autoubiquitination independent of the APC/C, which likely depends on FZR1 directly binding to the functional domain of TRAF3/6.

PTX is a well-known anticancer agent (61) and several reports have shown that the combination of oncolytic virotherapy and PTX promotes viral replication in different tumors by downregulating IFN-β production (52, 54). Our studies confirmed that PTX downregulated antiviral immunity by degrading FZR1 and promoted the H1N1 replication in vitro and in vivo. Our data provide a basis for future clinical testing of the combination therapy. These findings have potential implications for the future treatment of virus-infected patients with tumors.

Based on our findings, we propose a model (Fig. 7J) that viral infection induces an increase in the m6A modification level of FZR1 mRNA, which in turn promotes an increase in its protein level. FZR1 can directly target the degradation of the proteasome pathway of the rate-limiting enzyme PFKFB3 in the glycolysis pathway to downregulate lactate production. In addition, FZR1 competes with PFKFB3 to bind to MAVS, which promotes MAVS aggregation. FZR1 also acts as a scaffold to promote the interaction between MAVS and TRAF3/6. Finally, FZR1 promotes K63 ubiquitination activation of TRAF3/6 in a manner independent of APC/C, promoting innate immunity and gradually inhibiting viral infection.

In summary, our work reveals the molecular mechanism by which FZR1 promotes antiviral innate immunity by targeting the MAVS–TRAF3/6 axis, providing a mechanistic basis for future antiviral drug design. It also demonstrated the mechanism by which PTX downregulates IFN-I production, providing key evidence for the combination therapy using oncolytic virus and PTX.

Our study still has some limitations. First, given that FZR1 plays a critical role in both physiologic and pathologic states, mice deficient in FZR1 are embryonic lethal (50). Using a conditional FZR1 knockout may yield more convincing evidence. In addition, although we demonstrated that FZR1 attenuates PFKFB3 binding to MAVS, thereby promoting MAVS aggregation, our results did not provide structural evidence for how FZR1 and PFKFB3 regulate the conformational change of MAVS by the C-terminal (amino acid 360 to 540) of MAVS. Last, it appears that FZR1 can regulate the autoubiquitination of TRAF3 and TRAF6; however, the process and underlying mechanisms remain elusive. These important questions could be addressed in the future studies.

Materials and Methods

All animal experiments adhered to the guidelines established by the Wuhan University Animal Care and Use Committee. Detailed information regarding reagents, antibodies, cells, constructs, PCR primers, and gRNA target sequences is provided in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Virus Infection.

Cells were infected with various viruses at indicated multiplicities of infection (MOI). For in vivo studies, mice were infected with H1N1 or VSV following specific pretreatment protocols. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Plasmids and Transfection.

Target gene coding sequences were cloned into epitope-tagged expression vectors. Truncation mutants were generated from corresponding full-length plasmids. Plasmid transfections were performed using a commercial reagent. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

CRISPR-Cas9 Knockout.

Lentiviruses expressing gene-specific gRNAs were packaged in HEK293T cells. Target cells were infected and selected with puromycin to generate knockout clonal lines. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Plaque Assay.

Viral titers for H1N1 and VSV were quantified by standard plaque assay on MDCK or Vero cell monolayers. After adsorption and overlay, plaques were visualized by staining. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Confocal Microscopy.

Transfected HeLa cells were infected, fixed, and immunostained according to standard protocols. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Real-Time qPCR Analysis.

Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Gene expression was quantified by qPCR using SYBR Green and normalized to GAPDH. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay.

Cells were cotransfected with promoter-driven firefly luciferase reporters and control plasmids. Luciferase activity was measured after lysis using a commercial assay system. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Extraction of Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Proteins.

Cellular fractionation was performed using a commercial kit to separate nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and Immunoblot (IB) Assays.

For IP, cell lysates were incubated with specific antibodies and Protein A/G beads. For IB, proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and detected with HRP-conjugated antibodies. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

m6A-IP-qPCR.

m6A-modified RNAs were immunoprecipitated using a specific antibody conjugated to magnetic beads. Enriched RNA was eluted, purified, and analyzed by qRT-PCR. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Semidenaturing Detergent Agarose Gel Electrophoresis (SDD-AGE).

Mitochondrial fractions were isolated and lysed. Protein complexes were analyzed by SDD-AGE and by immunoblotting. Detailed information can be found in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Statistical Analysis.

Comparisons between two groups with normally distributed variables were analyzed using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test, while multiple comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Significance was defined as P < 0.05; ns indicates no significant difference. For animal survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was adopted to generate graphs, and the survival curves were analyzed with the log-rank test.
