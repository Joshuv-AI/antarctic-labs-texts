Predicting and identifying determinants of stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia: a machine learning approach
Authors: Shibiru Jabessa Dugasa, Merga Abdissa Aga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood stunting remains a major public health concern, reflecting chronic undernutrition and long-term socioeconomic disadvantage. Among school-aged children, stunting is associated with impaired physical growth, reduced cognitive development, and poorer educational outcomes. Traditional statistical approaches, such as logistic regression, have been widely used to examine factors associated with stunting; however, their ability to capture complex and nonlinear relationships is limited. Machine learning (ML) methods provide a flexible alternative for modeling such relationships.ObjectiveThis study aimed to model and classify stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia using school- and household-level data, and to compare the performance of machine learning algorithms with multivariable logistic regression.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using secondary data from Round 5 (2016–2017) of the Young Lives study in Ethiopia. Stunting was defined as a binary outcome based on World Health Organization height-for-age Z-score criteria. Several machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Gradient Boosting Machine, were implemented. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Variable importance measures were used to identify predictors contributing to model performance. To avoid potential circularity, anthropometric variables closely related to the outcome (e.g., child weight) were excluded from the final models.ResultsThe Random Forest model demonstrated modestly improved performance compared with logistic regression and other machine learning methods, and its performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and F1-score. Key predictors included school type, household wealth index, literacy-related indicators, and region of residence. Notable regional variation in stunting classification was observed, suggesting the influence of broader socioeconomic and environmental conditions.ConclusionMachine learning models, particularly Random Forest, showed slightly better performance than conventional logistic regression in classifying stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia. The identified predictors highlight the multifactorial and context-dependent nature of stunting. These findings support the use of ML approaches as complementary analytical tools for understanding patterns of child undernutrition, although their application for prediction should be interpreted within the limitations of cross-sectional data.Keywords: Stunting, School-aged children, Machine learning, Ethiopia, Classification

Introduction

Childhood stunting, defined as a height-for-age Z-score (HAZ) below − 2 standard deviations of the World Health Organization (WHO) child growth standards, remains one of the most persistent forms of child undernutrition worldwide [1]. Stunting reflects chronic nutritional deprivation and recurrent infections during critical periods of growth and development, resulting in long-term consequences for physical growth, cognitive development, educational attainment, and adult productivity [2–5]. Consequently, childhood stunting represents not only a nutritional disorder but also a major constraint to human capital formation and sustainable development.

Globally, an estimated 148.1 million children under five years of age were stunted in 2022, accounting for approximately 22.3% of children in this age group [6]. Although global stunting prevalence has declined over the past two decades, progress has been uneven, with a growing concentration of the burden in low- and middle-income countries. Evidence indicates that the adverse effects of early-life stunting often persist into later childhood and adolescence, manifesting as impaired school performance, reduced physical capacity, and diminished economic productivity in adulthood [3, 4, 7].

Africa bears a disproportionate share of the global stunting burden, with more than 61 million stunted children residing on the continent, the majority of whom live in Sub-Saharan Africa [6, 8]. Unlike other regions where substantial reductions have been achieved, progress in Sub-Saharan Africa has been comparatively slow due to persistent structural challenges, including food insecurity, poverty, limited access to healthcare, inadequate sanitation, and socioeconomic inequalities [9–11]. Rapid population growth has further offset modest gains in prevalence reduction, resulting in a persistently high absolute number of affected children.

Ethiopia remains among the countries most affected by childhood stunting in Sub-Saharan Africa. National and subnational studies consistently document high levels of growth faltering and pronounced regional disparities in child nutritional outcomes [12–18]. While much of the existing evidence focuses on children under five years of age, emerging research suggests that stunting often persists into school age, where it continues to affect educational participation, cognitive development, and long-term life opportunities. Understanding stunting among school-aged children is therefore critical for capturing its broader developmental implications.

Most epidemiological studies on childhood stunting in Ethiopia have relied on cross-sectional data analyzed using conventional regression techniques, particularly logistic regression models, to examine factors associated with stunting [13, 16–19]. Although these approaches have yielded important insights into biological, maternal, household, and environmental correlates, they are limited by assumptions of linearity, restricted ability to capture complex interactions, and modest classification performance. Given that stunting arises from interdependent biological, social, and environmental processes operating over the life course, more flexible analytical frameworks are needed to better capture these relationships.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have gained increasing prominence in public health research due to their ability to model nonlinear relationships, accommodate high-dimensional data, and enhance classification performance [20–22]. Several studies have applied ML techniques such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Gradient Boosting Machine models to classify childhood stunting in low-income settings, reporting improved performance compared with traditional statistical models [23–25]. In Ethiopia, recent studies employing ensemble ML algorithms have demonstrated strong performance and highlighted key socioeconomic factors associated with stunting [26].

Despite these advances, important gaps remain. Existing ML-based studies have largely emphasized predictive performance, with comparatively limited attention to model interpretability, identification of associated factors, and systematic comparison with traditional regression models treated as baseline classifiers [27]. Moreover, child-level educational and developmental indicators such as literacy skills, school participation, and school type—have received limited attention in prior ML-based stunting research, particularly among school-aged children, despite strong evidence linking nutritional status with cognitive development, educational achievement, and long-term human capital outcomes [5, 28–30]. And a relationship between height-for-age and cognitive development in school-aged children was reported by Author using multilevel analysis [31].

To address these gaps, the present study adopts an integrated analytical framework that combines logistic regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Gradient Boosting Machine models to classify childhood stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia using data from the Young Lives longitudinal study. Logistic regression is treated as a baseline model and systematically compared with nonlinear ML approaches using multiple performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). In addition to model performance, the study emphasizes interpretability by using odds ratios and variable importance measures to identify factors associated with stunting across child-level, household-level, educational, and environmental domains.

By explicitly incorporating educational indicators alongside nutritional and socioeconomic factors, this study provides additional insights into the nutrition education relationship and demonstrates how interpretable machine learning approaches can complement conventional epidemiological analyses. The findings aim to support evidence-based decision-making and contribute to more context-sensitive strategies for addressing childhood stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a supervised machine learning and statistical modeling framework to identify key determinants of stunting among school-aged children and to compare the predictive performance of classical and modern classification algorithms. The analysis was based on secondary data from the Young Lives longitudinal study in Ethiopia, specifically using data from Round 5 (2016–2017) [32].

Young Lives is a large-scale, longitudinal observational study that collects detailed information on children’s health, nutrition, education, and household living conditions across multiple regions of Ethiopia. Although Young Lives follows children over time, the present analysis adopted a cross-sectional design, utilizing observations from the fifth survey round only. This approach allowed for the application of classification-based machine learning models to predict current stunting status while maintaining consistency with the available outcome and predictor variables [32].

The dataset employed in this study is publicly accessible and can be obtained by submitting a justification for the data request via the following link: https://ukdataservice.ac.uk/deposit-data/. Additional information concerning the sample, variable construction, and other relevant aspects is available in [32]. The analytical dataset comprised approximately 1,000 school-aged children, drawn from five major administrative areas (Addis Ababa, Amhara, Oromia, SNNP, and Tigray). A total of 18 predictor variables were included, capturing child-level characteristics, household socioeconomic conditions, environmental factors, school-related attributes, and parental characteristics [32].

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was childhood stunting, defined using the World Health Organization (WHO) growth reference standards based on height-for-age z-scores (HAZ):

Stunted: Height-for-age z-score < − 2 SD.

Stunted: Height-for-age z-score < − 2 SD.

Not stunted: Height-for-age z-score ≥ − 2 SD.

Not stunted: Height-for-age z-score ≥ − 2 SD.

This binary formulation enabled the use of classification-oriented machine learning algorithms, as well as logistic regression for interpretable estimation of associations.

Data preprocessing

Missing data handling

Prior to model development, the dataset was examined for missing values. Given the relatively low proportion of missing observations and to preserve the full analytical sample, simple and robust imputation strategies were employed. Continuous variables were imputed using their median values, while categorical variables were imputed using the most frequent category (mode). Median imputation was selected to reduce sensitivity to extreme values and to maintain distributional stability.

Data splitting

The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%). The training set was used for model fitting and hyper parameter tuning, while the testing set served as an independent hold-out sample for final performance evaluation. This split balanced learning efficiency with unbiased assessment of predictive performance.

Feature encoding and scaling

Categorical predictors were encoded as factor variables suitable for classification modeling. Continuous variables, such as child weight and household size, were retained in their original scales. Feature scaling was not applied globally, as tree-based methods (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine) are inherently scale-invariant, and logistic regression results were interpreted using odds ratios rather than standardized coefficients.

Class imbalance handling

The stunting outcome exhibited class imbalance, with a lower proportion of stunted children relative to non-stunted children. Instead of applying data-level resampling techniques, this study adopted a metric-based and algorithm-robust imbalance handling strategy. Model evaluation emphasized imbalance-sensitive and threshold-independent performance metrics, including sensitivity (recall), F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), rather than accuracy alone.

Additionally, ensemble learning algorithms (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine), which are known to be relatively robust to class imbalance due to their tree-based structure and recursive partitioning, were prioritized. Cross-validation procedures based on ROC optimization were used during model tuning to further enhance discrimination between stunted and non-stunted children.

Multicollinearity assessment

To assess potential multicollinearity among predictors, Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were calculated for variables included in the logistic regression model. All VIF values were below commonly accepted thresholds, indicating no serious multicollinearity.

Model development

Four predictive models were developed and compared:

Logistic Regression (GLM)

Logistic Regression (GLM)

A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to estimate adjusted associations between predictors and the odds of stunting. Regression coefficients were reported alongside odds ratios (ORs) and statistical significance levels.

2.Random Forest (RF)

Random Forest (RF)

An ensemble tree-based classifier was trained to capture nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions among predictors. Variable importance was assessed using both Mean Decrease in Accuracy and Mean Decrease in Gini indices.

3.Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

A kernel-based SVM classifier was implemented to model complex decision boundaries in the predictor space.

4.Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)

Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)

A boosting algorithm was applied to iteratively improve classification performance by correcting errors from previous trees.

Model validation and hyper parameter tuning

Model training and tuning were performed using repeated 10-fold cross-validation (5 repetitions) to ensure stable and unbiased performance estimates. For the Random Forest model, the optimal number of variables randomly sampled at each split (mtry) was selected by maximizing the cross-validated ROC value.

Model evaluation

The predictive performance of all models was assessed on the same validation framework using the following metrics: Accuracy, Sensitivity (Recall), Specificity, F1-score and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC).

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves was generated to visually compare model discrimination. The Random Forest model’s ROC curve was additionally examined under cross-validation to confirm robustness.

Model Interpretation

To enhance interpretability of the Random Forest model, variable importance analysis was conducted. Variables with higher Mean Decrease in Accuracy were interpreted as having stronger influence on predictive performance, while Mean Decrease in Gini reflected their contribution to node purity. Logistic regression results were interpreted using adjusted odds ratios to provide epidemiologically meaningful insights.

Software

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software, employing standard libraries for regression modeling, machine learning, cross-validation, and performance evaluation.

Ethical review statement

This study utilizes data from the fifth round of the Young Lives household, child, and school surveys, which are publicly accessible and archived through the UK Data Archive. Obtaining access required registration, a password application through the UK Data Service, and the signing of a confidentiality agreement. Further information on relevant research protocols is available at the following link: https://ukdataservice.ac.uk/events/ethical-and-legal-guidelines-in-data-sharing-2025-05-08/. Ethical clearance for the data collection was granted by the University of Oxford’s Central University Research Ethics Committee.

The prevalence of stunting was slightly higher among boys (32.9%) than girls (32.0%), suggesting minimal sex-based differences in nutritional status.

Descriptive characteristics of school-aged children by stunting status

Stunting was more prevalent among rural children (37.0%) than urban children (24.2%), indicating disparities in living conditions, access to services, and food security. Higher stunting prevalence was observed among children from households where caregivers were divorced (39.6%) or single (40.0%) compared with those in permanently married households (31.4%). Similarly, children living only with their mother (37.3%) showed higher stunting prevalence than those living with both parents (31.9%).

Maternal education showed a clear gradient with stunting. Children whose mothers had no formal education had higher prevalence (35.6%) compared with those whose mothers had primary (26.9%) or secondary and above education (25.8%).

Stunting prevalence was substantially higher among children attending private schools (63.3%) compared with public schools (25.9%). Children with poor literacy skills had markedly higher stunting prevalence. Specifically, 43.3% of children who could not read and 43.5% of those who could not write were stunted, compared with 19.6% and 17.6%, respectively, among children with literacy skills.

Environmental factors also showed important patterns. Children from households without toilet facilities had higher stunting prevalence (39.0%) compared with those with sanitation access (21.4%). Similarly, higher stunting prevalence was observed among children using public standpipes (37.4%) compared with piped water (22.0%). Use of solid fuels such as wood (32.4%) and cow dung (33.6%) was also slightly higher stunting prevalence compared with cleaner energy sources.

Stunting prevalence varied modestly by birth order, with slightly higher prevalence among children with two to three older siblings (34.8%), suggesting potential intra-household resource constraints.

Discussion

This study applied machine learning (ML) techniques alongside multivariable logistic regression to predict childhood stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia using school-based data. By focusing on predictive performance and associated factors, the study contributes to the growing literature on data-driven approaches to understanding childhood undernutrition beyond early childhood and outside nationally representative household surveys.

Among the evaluated models, the Random Forest algorithm demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving the highest accuracy, sensitivity, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that ensemble tree-based methods perform well when applied to health and educational datasets characterized by nonlinear relationships, correlated predictors, and mixed variable types [20–22, 27]. The relatively high sensitivity of the Random Forest model indicates a strong ability to correctly identify stunted children.

Although Logistic Regression showed lower predictive performance compared with ML models, its discriminatory ability remained acceptable. This highlights the continued relevance of traditional statistical models in epidemiological research, particularly due to their interpretability and transparency. Logistic regression therefore remains a useful benchmark against which more complex ML approaches can be evaluated. The observed improvement in predictive accuracy with ML models suggests that nonlinear modeling frameworks capture additional structure in the data that may be missed by conventional linear assumptions [27] and [33].

The socioeconomic, educational, and contextual variables emerged as the most important predictors of stunting. In particular, school type, household wealth index, and child literacy indicators (reading and writing ability) showed substantial importance in the Random Forest model. These variables likely reflect broader socioeconomic and developmental conditions rather than direct causal effects. For example, limited literacy may indicate underlying disadvantages such as poor school quality, constrained household resources, or reduced cognitive stimulation, which are themselves associated with nutritional outcomes [4, 5, 28, 34, 35].

School type was identified as one of the most influential predictors, with differences observed between children attending private and public schools. This finding should not be interpreted as a direct effect of school type itself, but rather as a proxy for underlying socioeconomic and environmental differences, including household wealth, parental education, and access to better nutrition and health services. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies linking educational context and socioeconomic status with child nutritional outcomes [7, 30, 35, 36].

Substantial regional variation in stunting risk was also observed, suggesting that geographic and contextual factors continue to play an important role among school-aged children. These differences may reflect disparities in food security, infrastructure, health service availability, and broader socioeconomic development. The persistence of regional inequalities highlights the importance of context-specific strategies to address undernutrition [9, 11, 12, 30, 37].

Household environmental factors, including water source and sanitation, demonstrated moderate importance in the Random Forest model. Their effects are likely mediated through indirect pathways such as exposure to infections and overall living conditions. The mixed patterns observed in this study are consistent with prior evidence indicating that environmental determinants interact with socioeconomic and behavioral factors in complex ways, particularly in cross-sectional analyses [36–38]. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as indicative of associations rather than definitive relationships.

From a practical perspective, the results suggest that ML models can effectively utilize school-based data to identify patterns associated with childhood stunting. However, their application as standalone screening tools should be approached with caution. Instead, such models may serve as complementary decision-support tools alongside standard anthropometric assessments within school health and nutrition programs. Importantly, variable importance measures derived from ML models reflect predictive contribution rather than causal importance and should not be used in isolation to guide policy decisions.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Second, the absence of detailed dietary intake, feeding practices, and morbidity data may restrict the explanatory capacity of the models. Third, some predictor’s particularly educational variables likely function as proxies for broader socioeconomic and developmental processes, raising the possibility of reverse causality. In addition, the lack of external validation may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should incorporate longitudinal data, conduct validation in diverse settings, and explore the integration of additional behavioral, dietary, and environmental variables.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the value of integrating machine learning techniques with conventional statistical approaches to predict childhood stunting among school-aged children in Ethiopia using school-based data. The Random Forest model showed improved predictive performance compared with logistic regression, indicating its ability to capture complex and nonlinear relationships within the data.

Key associated factors identified in the analysis include school type, household wealth, literacy indicators, and region of residence, highlighting the multifactorial and context-dependent nature of stunting among school-aged children. These findings underscore the importance of considering socioeconomic, educational, and environmental contexts when examining childhood undernutrition.

While machine learning models show promise in identifying patterns within complex datasets, their role should be viewed as complementary rather than substitutive to established public health approaches. Future research should focus on longitudinal validation, improved data integration, and careful consideration of interpretability to enhance both the scientific and practical relevance of predictive models in child nutrition.
