Investigating multimorbidity trajectories in people living with MASLD diagnosis: A trajectory analysis using the UK Biobank
Authors: Fang Lu, Hailin Yang, Bingyang She, Qianhui Lu, Yining Bao, Wai‐Kay Seto, William C. W. Wong, Man‐Fung Yuen, Yingli He, Xinyuan He, Fanpu Ji, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is an emerging global health concern, and its presence increases the risk of multi‐system diseases. This study aimed to investigate the multimorbidity trajectories of chronic diseases in people living with MASLD.MethodsWe identified 137 859 MASLD patients in UK Biobank and used ‘propensity score matching’ to match an equal number of non‐MASLD controls. Diseases were reclassified into 472 categories based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) chapters. Multimorbidity trajectories post‐MASLD diagnosis were mapped using validated trajectory analysis. We introduced the ‘Multimorbidity Trajectory Position Index (MTPI)’ to denote a disease's position across trajectories, highlighting its temporal pattern.ResultsParticipants had a median age of 59 (52–64) years, with 65.6% being male. Over 13 years of follow‐up, Phenome‐wide association analysis (PheWAS) identified 128 diseases with elevated risks post‐MASLD diagnosis, with obesity (HR: 8.77, 95% CI: 8.37–9.18), diabetes (HR: 4.34, 95% CI: 4.15–4.53), and sleep disorders (HR: 3.21, 95% CI: 3.01–3.42) showing the strongest associations. Trajectory analysis revealed 6637 common trajectories involving 69 diseases, grouped into metabolic, inflammatory, and cardiovascular clusters. These clusters are linked to downstream conditions, with intermediary diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and inflammatory arthritis, ultimately leading to electrolyte imbalances and sepsis. MTPI demonstrated a gradient in disease progression, with early‐stage conditions showing low values, mid‐stage conditions moderate values, and late‐stage conditions high values.ConclusionPeople living with MASLD demonstrated multimorbidity trajectories involving co‐occurrence of metabolic diseases, chronic inflammation, and cardiovascular diseases. If replicated, these pathways may serve as promising targets to improve late‐life health in individuals with MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is characterized by fat accumulation in more than 5% of hepatocytes in individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors. 1 This renaming reflects the metabolic roots of the disease and is strongly linked to metabolic dysfunction. Affecting 25% globally, it drives the rising burden of chronic liver disease and poses a major public health challenge. 2 , 3 , 4

MASLD is increasingly recognized as a multi‐system disease that affects various extrahepatic organs and regulatory pathways. 5 Individuals with MASLD face a heightened risk of developing multimorbidity, notably chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, 6 , 7 type 2 diabetes mellitus, 8 chronic kidney disease, 9 , 10 sleep apnea, and osteoporosis. 7 , 11 Mendelian Randomization analyses have confirmed causal associations between MASLD and multiple disease outcomes. 12 , 13 A cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic syndrome (CKM) syndrome pathway was also observed, which is characterized by metabolic dysfunction progressing to cardiovascular disease and subsequent renal impairment. 14 The temporal sequence of diseases forms a complex network originating from MASLD, underscoring the need to better understand its interactions with other conditions. Effective identification and management of MASLD‐related multimorbidity are essential, as they affect prognosis and require targeted therapeutic adjustments for MASLD. 15 With limited effective therapies for MASLD, analysing post‐diagnosis multimorbidity trajectories is also crucial for identifying key pathways in disease progression and improving MASLD management. 3 , 16

Disease trajectory analysis is an innovative method that is used in epidemiological research. The analysis systematically maps the temporal sequence of diseases, revealing their progression and interactions, and offering key insights into potential causal pathways among complications. 17 , 18 , 19 Disease trajectory analysis has been successfully employed to identify patterns in the development of sequential diseases. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Given the rapid rise in MASLD burden and its complications, mapping multimorbidity trajectories is crucial for understanding disease progression and improving prognosis accuracy.

By focusing on medical conditions associated with prior MASLD diagnoses, we aimed to map the multimorbidity trajectory network following MASLD using the UK Biobank (UKB) data. This exploratory, descriptive study does not infer causality but identifies a wider spectrum of potential disease progression pathways. Since clustering of sequential diseases may reflect shared etiologies or mechanisms, this study provides new insights for targeted interventions to reduce MASLD complications and improve prognosis.

METHODS

Study design and population

This study utilized UKB data, a large‐scale prospective cohort of over 500 000 adults (aged 39–69) recruited via the UK National Health Service between 2006 and 2010. The study design was documented previously. 15 , 23 Briefly, at enrollment, participants completed a 90‐min assessment covering demographic information, medical history, and physical measurements recorded via an electronic questionnaire. 24 , 25 Biological samples were collected and laboratory tests were conducted. The detailed data collection methods are available at http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk. All participants provided written informed consent and the study followed the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol, and the North West Research Ethics Committee approved its data usage (06/MRE08/65).

Of the 502 409 enrolled participants, we excluded those who withdrew from the UKB (n = 158), lacked age or Townsend deprivation index data (n = 626), had insufficient information for MASLD identification (n = 35 473), and reported excessive alcohol consumption (n = 40 651). This resulted in 425 501 eligible participants. We employed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) based on age, sex, and Townsend deprivation index to control for potential confounding factors, creating two groups, each consisting of 137 859 participants (Figure S1).

Ascertainment of MASLD

MASLD is defined based on the latest international expert consensus as hepatic steatosis with any of the following cardiometabolic criteria: (1) overweight/obesity (body mass index (BMI) ≥25 kg/m2), or increased waist circumference (WC) (>94 cm for males; >80 cm for females); (2) prediabetes (fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL or Haemoglobin A1c ≥5.7%), or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or being treated for T2DM; (3) hypertension (≥130/85 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication); (4) hypertriglyceridemia (≥1.7 mmol/L), or use of lipid‐lowering drug therapy; (5) low high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (<1.0 mmol/L for males; <1.3 mmol/L for females). 1 Although MRI‐based proton density fat fraction (PDFF) is more accurate for hepatic steatosis, its limited availability in UKB restricts large‐scale analysis. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the fatty liver index (FLI) was used, with PDFF‐based cohort analysis included in the sensitivity analysis.

Hepatic steatosis was assessed using the FLI according to the formula based on WC, gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), triglycerides (TG), and BMI. 26 , 27 The FLI formula that calculates the likelihood of fatty liver disease is FLI = e x /(1 + e x ) × 100, where x = 0.953 × log e (TG) + 0.139 × BMI + 0.718 × log e (GGT) + 0.053 × (WC) − 15.745. FLI ≥60 was used to diagnose hepatic steatosis, which yielded a sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 86%. 27 A meta‐analysis based on a multiethnic population also confirmed that FLI provides adequate diagnostic accuracy, with sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 90%. 28 This approach aligns with previous UKB studies that also used FLI for hepatic steatosis classification. 15 , 23

Diagnoses of medical conditions

In this study, medical conditions were retrieved from UK inpatient hospital data using ICD‐10 codes, focusing on Chapters 1–14. Medical conditions were identified using three‐character ICD‐10 codes consolidated based on clinical or biological similarities. Detailed information on combined disease codes is provided in Supplementary Data 1. For multiple records of the same individual, only the first record and hospital visit date were used as diagnosis dates. The study end date was set to 12 November 2021, based on the latest available diagnosis in the dataset. Follow‐up for individuals in the cohort started after the index date to the date of death, date of loss to follow‐up, or end date.

Statistical analysis

Trajectory analysis

We used a validated method to identify multimorbidity trajectories following MASLD diagnosis. 19 , 23 , 29 This method includes three steps (Supplementary Material 1; Figure S2). First, a phenome‐wide association analysis (PheWAS) using Cox regression was used to compare the disease risk in MASLD participants with matched controls, focusing on 472 medical conditions classified by ICD‐10 codes. Participants with a history of disease outcome at baseline based on self‐reported medical history and ICD‐10 coding were excluded. To ensure statistical power, only diseases with a prevalence of ≥1% in MASLD participants (n = 1378) were analysed. Diseases with a ‘hazard ratio (HR) >1’ and ‘P < the Bonferroni‐corrected threshold’ were retained for further analysis. PheWAS was chosen for its ability to assess the associations between MASLD and a broad range of clinical phenotypes to capture multiple diseases that may be linked to MASLD diagnosis. Second, disease pairs were identified based on temporal order, including those occurring in ≥0.5% of the MASLD participants (n = 689). We verified the sequence using a binomial test to ensure that more than 50% of participants with both D1 and D2 diagnoses had D2 diagnosed later than D1, retaining only those disease pairs with ‘P < the Bonferroni‐corrected threshold’. The binomial test was applied to statistically validate the temporal sequence of disease pairs, ensuring that the observed order of diagnoses reflects a significant temporal relationship, which can eliminate the possibility of reciprocal causation between D1 and D2. Third, we confirmed these associations using a nested case–control design and conditional logistic regression, constructing datasets with D2 as the outcome and D1 as exposure. Controls were matched to cases based on age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, and baseline year. Participants with a history of D1 and D2 at baseline were excluded. We retained disease pairs with a significantly increased risk of D2 after D1 (odds ratio [OR] >1; P< Bonferroni‐corrected threshold). This method was selected for its ability to match cases and controls efficiently, controlling for confounders and accounting for the correlation within matched pairs. Multimorbidity trajectories were formed by combining disease pairs with overlapping diseases (e.g., disease pairs D1 → D2 and D2 → D3 with overlapping D2 were combined in the trajectory D1 → D2 → D3, and the connecting line from D1 to D3 could be deleted).

Multimorbidity trajectory position index (MTPI) analysis of trajectories

We introduced the MTPI to identify key pathways and quantify potential risks in MASLD multimorbidity trajectories by considering the position of the disease and number of individuals in each trajectory. Diseases were classified as ‘initial’, ‘intermediary’, or ‘final’ according to their sequential position within each trajectory: the disease appearing first in a trajectory was designated as the ‘initial’ disease, the last as the ‘final’ disease, and those in between as ‘intermediary’ diseases. In essence, MTPI represents the relative position of a disease in a multimorbidity trajectory and was calculated using the formula: MTPID1=∑i=1nPiCi/∑i=1nCi, where P i represents the position of a disease across all possible trajectories and C i denotes the count of individuals associated with each specific position of the disease within these trajectories. Full details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

To investigate sex‐specific differences in MASLD multimorbidity trajectories, we performed separate analyses for female and male participants. A sensitivity analysis excluded participants with hospitalization records within the first 6 months to reduce bias from pre‐existing severe conditions. We also excluded individuals with prior diseases in the same ICD‐10 chapter as the outcome disease to validate our findings. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis of the small cohort defined by PDFF assessed whether our findings held with a more accurate diagnostic subset.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.3 and Cytoscape Desktop version 3.10.0. Statistical significance was set using a Bonferroni‐corrected p‐value of 0.05 divided by the number of analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

After PSM for age, sex, and Townsend deprivation index, 275 718 participants were included, with 137 859 diagnosed with MASLD and 137 859 otherwise. The overall cohort consisted of 65.6% males, with a median age of 59 (52–64) years, a median BMI of 27.43 (24.84–30.64) kg/m2, a median alcohol intake of 16 (5.76–28.8) g/day, and a median follow‐up duration of 13 years (Table 1).

Incidence density of 472 medical conditions during follow‐up

The incidence densities of the 472 diseases were analysed in the study population. The top three diseases according to cumulative case count and incidence density were primary hypertension (61 081 cases; 151.20/1000 person‐years), osteoarthritis (41 994; 145.40/1000 person‐years), and hyperlipidemia (36 015; 145.30/1000 person‐years). MASLD individuals showed significantly higher incidence densities than controls for metabolic and cardiovascular conditions, including obesity (130.34 vs. 115.37/1000 person‐years), primary hypertension (154.60 vs. 145.92/1000 person‐years), hyperlipidemia (147.44 vs. 141.69/1000 person‐years), and chronic ischaemic heart disease (150.72 vs. 144.52/1000 person‐years). Detailed incidence densities are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Medical conditions related to MASLD

Following restriction to diseases with a prevalence of ≥1% among MASLD individuals and performing PheWAS with Cox regression models, MASLD individuals had a higher risk of 128 out of 472 medical conditions (Table S1; Figure 1). The top three strongest associations were for obesity (HR: 8.77, 95% CI: 8.37–9.18), diabetes (HR: 4.34, 95% CI: 4.15–4.53), and sleep disorders (HR: 3.21, 95% CI: 3.01–3.42). Among the 128 associated conditions, Chapters IV (Endocrine system disease), IX (Cardiovascular disease), XI (Digestive system disease), and XIV (Genitourinary system disease) included the most significant number of diseases.

Multimorbidity patterns across ICD‐10 chapters by sex

Disease co‐occurrence analysis in MASLD individuals revealed sex‐specific patterns across the first 14 ICD‐10 chapters (Figure S3). Co‐occurring conditions were predominantly found in the metabolic, cardiovascular, and digestive chapters (IV, IX, X, and XI), with higher frequencies emphasising their key roles in disease progression. Endocrine disorders (Chapter IV) typically precede other conditions and drive the subsequent disease progression. In males, cardiovascular diseases (Chapter IX) showed notable temporal precedence. In females, mental disorders (Chapter V) typically occur earlier and affect progression in other systems, such as the genitourinary (Chapter XIV) and cardiovascular systems (Chapter IX). These patterns were statistically validated (FDR <0.05, Fisher's exact test), and detailed data are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Multimorbidity trajectories of MASLD

Trajectory analysis identified 323 disease pairs with ORs >1, creating a comprehensive trajectory map of MASLD multimorbidity (Figure 2). Initial diseases included primary hypertension (I10), asthma (J45), thyroid disease (E00) and diabetes (E10). Key intermediaries, such as obesity (E66), chronic ischemic heart disease (I25), and inflammatory arthritis (M15), contribute to the transition toward circulatory (electrolyte imbalances: E87; hypotension: I95; and sepsis: A41), digestive (functional gastrointestinal disorders: K59; haemorrhoids: K64), and genitourinary disorders (acute kidney failure, N17; and urinary system infections, N39). Notably, a pathway of interconnected diseases resembling CKM syndrome has emerged, characterised by the sequential transition from metabolic dysfunction to cardiovascular disease and renal impairment.

MTPI derived from MASLD trajectories

From the constructed multimorbidity trajectory map, 6637 common trajectories involving 69 diseases were identified and further analysed (Supplementary Data 4; Table S6). Figure 4 shows the distribution of these diseases across multimorbidity trajectories, illustrating the relationship between MTPI and average age at diagnosis. Diseases with lower MTPI values (<1.5), such as primary hypertension (I10), asthma (J45), and tobacco abuse (F17), typically appear earlier in the trajectories, with diagnosis ages of 63–66 years. Diseases with moderate MTPI values (1.5 ≤MTPI ≤2.0), such as obesity (E66), angina pectoris (I20), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (J44), gastroesophageal reflux disease (K21), and other inflammatory arthritis (M10), emerge in mid‐stage trajectories with diagnosis ages between 66 and 69 years. In contrast, diseases with higher MTPI values (MTPI <1.5), including sepsis (A41), electrolyte imbalances (E87), and hypotension (I95), tended to occur later in multimorbidity trajectories, with diagnosis ages over 69 years.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The PheWAS results indicated significant associations between MASLD and medical conditions across various subgroups. We further examined multimorbidity trajectories in male and female individuals using the same approach. Although PheWAS results for both sexes showed similar subsequent diseases following MASLD diagnosis, the association strengths varied. Both sexes exhibited comparable risks of obesity and chronic rheumatic heart disease. However, females with MASLD had a higher risk of diabetes, lipid metabolism disorders, depression, sleep disorders, and primary hypertension (Table S1). Disease trajectory analysis revealed overlapping pathways in male (Figure S4) and female (Figure S5) MASLD individuals, with primary clusters consisting mainly of metabolic, chronic inflammatory, and cardiovascular diseases.

Sensitivity analyses restricting the cohort to individuals without any inpatient records of diseases within 6 months before the index date yielded consistent results (Table S3). Additionally, we obtained largely similar PheWAS results when excluding MASLD individuals with a history of any disease within the same category as the outcome disease (Table S4). In a sensitivity analysis of 8769 MASLD individuals identified by PDFF, all 39 conditions with significantly increased post‐MASLD risks were encompassed within the 128 identified in the main FLI‐based analysis. The multimorbidity trajectories from the smaller PDFF‐defined subset were largely contained within the broader trajectory network identified in the main analysis (Table S5 and Figure S6). The PDFF‐based findings may reflect more clinically prominent pathways, while the broader FLI‐based analysis reveals a wider spectrum of potential trajectories for future exploration.

External verification by Danish disease trajectories browser

Prior research utilizing Denmark's national data (1994–2018) developed a Disease Trajectory Browser (DTB) available online (http://dtb.cpr.ku.dk/). 19 Since MASLD lacks a specific ICD‐10 code, K70 (alcoholic liver disease) and K76 (nonalcoholic liver disease) codes were used to validate the reproducibility of MASLD's multimorbidity trajectories. Trajectories for K70 and K76 were visualized using the DTB (Figures S7, S8) with the following filter settings: 114 099 patients, pair length ≥2, and relative risk >1.0. A total of 81 medical conditions and 266 pairs were identified for K70 and K76, respectively (Supplementary Data 5, 6). K70 mediates a broad spectrum of downstream diseases, including diabetes, osteoporosis, chronic kidney disease, liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, bacterial infections, intracerebral haemorrhage, respiratory failure, mineral metabolism disorders, electrolyte imbalances, and depression. K76 cells showed nearly identical downstream conditions, except for cardiac arrest. Most downstream diseases for both K70 and K76 aligned with the MASLD multimorbidity trajectories identified in our analysis, although differences in cardiovascular disease outcomes were noted.

DISCUSSION

This study utilized UKB data to map multimorbidity trajectories in MASLD individuals, revealing their dynamic progression. After restricting disease prevalence to ≥1%, MASLD individuals showed an elevated risk of 128 diseases. Trajectory analysis identified three main clusters, suggesting that alterations in the metabolic system, chronic inflammation, and cardiovascular system were key pathways linking MASLD to downstream health multimorbidities. Diabetes, obesity, and gout initiated the metabolic cluster, driving further metabolic abnormalities. Asthma and osteoarthritis, prominent in the inflammatory cluster, were linked to obesity and chronic inflammation, contributing to multi‐system multimorbidity. In the cardiovascular cluster, primary hypertension was the earliest driver, followed by end‐stage complications, such as electrolyte imbalance and acute myocardial infarction. These findings enhance our understanding of MASLD's natural history and multi‐system progression of MASLD, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to improve outcomes.

The PheWAS results confirmed MASLD's broad association with multi‐system diseases, consistent with previous studies. 15 , 23 , 30 , 31 In addition to common conditions like diabetes and obesity, new associations with less prevalent diseases (e.g., sepsis, lens disorders) were identified. Using the DTB to replicate key disease associations for MASLD, we found a significant overlap (e.g., metabolic disorders and chronic inflammation), although some heterogeneity, particularly with cardiovascular diseases, was noted. This discrepancy likely stems from our focus on MASLD individuals compared to the general population analysed by the DTB. These differences highlight distinct multimorbidity trajectories in MASLD, emphasizing its role in health decline through specific pathways. Our gender analysis revealed that while MASLD prevalence is higher in males, females face greater risks for subsequent diseases, likely due to oestrogen. 32 This highlights the importance of incorporating sex‐specific disease patterns in personalised treatment strategies.

This study's trajectory network highlights the impact of MASLD on the metabolic system. MASLD, as a core component of metabolic syndrome, is closely linked to obesity and diabetes, primarily through insulin resistance and hepatic lipid accumulation, which disrupt metabolic balance. 33 , 34 The connection between MASLD and conditions such as dyslipidemia is rooted in the liver's role in lipid metabolism. Fatty liver‐induced lipid metabolism disturbances affect other metabolic systems via complex pathways, increasing the risk of metabolic diseases and cardiovascular complications. 3 , 35 MASLD significantly raises cardiovascular disease risk, even after adjusting for metabolic factors and traditional risks. 36 Insulin resistance and lipid dysfunction impair vascular function, promoting atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction. 37 These mechanisms, along with elevated LDL and triglyceride levels, further accelerate atherosclerosis and coronary disease. 38

We also observed an increased risk of inflammatory diseases post‐MASLD diagnosis, likely due to hepatic fat accumulation and inflammation triggering systemic responses. 39 MASLD promotes chronic inflammation via lipotoxicity, activating Kupffer cells and releasing inflammatory cytokines. 40 Vitamin D deficiency has been implicated in the progression of MASLD, likely due to its role in liver inflammation and insulin sensitivity. Low vitamin D levels may exacerbate hepatic fat accumulation and inflammation, further accelerating MASLD progression. 41 , 42 Obesity‐related adipose expansion worsens inflammation and insulin resistance. 43 , 44 MASLD‐induced gut microbiota imbalance is also linked to higher risks of gastrointestinal conditions, such as gastroenteritis and colitis. Liver immune dysfunction leading to microbial imbalance may drive MASLD‐related systemic inflammation. 45 Further research is needed to validate these links and inform interventions.

Given these interconnections, clinicians managing conditions such as diabetes should consider screening for MASLD because early detection may help prevent complications. Beyond confirming these relationships, trajectory analysis further revealed temporal progression and complex multimorbidity patterns in MASLD individuals. Disease development follows a specific sequence: metabolic disorders typically arise first, followed by cardiovascular diseases, ultimately leading to multi‐system complications. These findings suggest the need for early metabolic management of MASLD to inform clinical interventions.

The MTPI was proposed to quantify the temporal position of diseases within MASLD trajectories. Its relationship with the average age at diagnosis provides valuable insights into multimorbidity dynamics. Early stage conditions, such as primary hypertension and asthma, with lower MTPI, often initiate disease cascades, while mid‐stage diseases, such as obesity and angina pectoris, amplify complexity. High‐MTPI conditions, such as sepsis and electrolyte disorders, emerge later, marking advanced systemic decline. These findings underscore MTPI's novel utility the MTPI in identifying high‐impact diseases, providing a new approach for targeted multimorbidity interventions.

The main strength of this study is using disease trajectory analysis to map multimorbidity trajectories following MASLD. The data‐driven approach overcomes limitations of traditional single‐disease analyses based on predefined hypotheses. 23 The introduction of MTPI allowed for the quantitative assessment of each disease's cumulative impact, highlighting key trajectory nodes. Sex‐specific analyses revealed distinct disease pathways, providing insights into personalised interventions. MASLD trajectories clarified disease clusters, temporal sequences, and critical disease positions, contributing new findings. Other strengths include a large cohort, prospective diagnostic data, and validation with electronic health records.

This study had several limitations. First, reliance on inpatient data, excluding primary care records, may have missed less severe conditions, and the >1% prevalence threshold likely omitted rare diseases. Although FLI allows larger sample sizes, it is an indirect measure less accurate than imaging‐based PDFF and may cause overestimate MASLD prevalence, potentially impacting trajectory accuracy. The relatively short follow‐up period may also limit the completeness of the trajectories, especially for late‐onset conditions. Additionally, variations in clinical guidelines and physician practices could lead to inconsistent ICD‐10 coding, affecting diagnostic accuracy and the study's conclusions. 46 Observational study limitations prevent causal inferences, underscoring the need for more rigorous methods to clarify the disease pathways. However, we acknowledge that some residual bias may still remain, especially in such a large‐scale cohort where accurately capturing the exact onset time of diseases can be challenging. Furthermore, the lack of ethnic diversity in the UKB cohort, which is predominantly composed of white participants, may limit the generalizability of these findings to non‐white populations. Ethnic differences in MASLD progression are well documented, and future studies should explore similar multimorbidity trajectories in more ethnically diverse cohorts to validate and expand our findings. 47 Finally, the study population, primarily from the United Kingdom with a median age of 59 years, may not reflect the younger age of MASLD onset, 4 limiting generalizability to other age groups.

In conclusion, this study offers key insights into multimorbidity trajectories following MASLD by identifying three main clusters that contribute to health decline. The proposed MTPI quantifies the average position of a disease within trajectories, capturing its temporal characteristics. Our findings highlight the importance of addressing early disease drivers in order to prevent further complications. Future clinical strategies should focus on phased, individualised interventions based on disease progression to improve outcomes.
