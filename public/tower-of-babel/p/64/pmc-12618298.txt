Visualizing Improvements in Airway Dysfunction After Inhaled Therapy in Patients With Uncontrolled Asthma: A Narrative Review
Authors: Sam Tcherner, Ali Mozaffaripour, Cory Yamashita, Grace Parraga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clinical trials investigating asthma therapies typically rely on pulmonary function tests including spirometry markers, such as forced expiratory volume in 1 s, to evaluate efficacy. While these measures provide insights into the action of bronchodilators on global lung function, their specific effects on the airways and the relationship between clinical improvements and airway dysfunction remains poorly understood. In recent years, pulmonary functional imaging methods, such as hyperpolarized 129Xe magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have enabled the analysis of airway dysfunction in patients with asthma utilizing ventilation defect percent (VDP). In this narrative review, we summarize clinical evidence about the impact of inhaled bronchodilator therapies on airway dysfunction in patients with asthma, focusing on studies that utilized 129Xe MRI to visualize and quantify MRI VDP. 129Xe MRI VDP has been shown to be a well-tolerated and sensitive technique for enabling the visualization and quantification of airway functional changes over time in patients with asthma. This has been shown not only in a controlled clinical trial environment but also in a real-world setting, in patients with both controlled and poorly controlled asthma. A recent study evaluating single-inhaler triple therapy in patients with uncontrolled moderate–severe asthma, despite inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist maintenance therapy, demonstrated that daily fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol (FF/UMEC/VI 200/62.5/25 µg) led to significantly improved 129Xe MRI VDP after only 6 weeks, which was in line with broader central/distal airway function and quality of life improvements. These results highlight the capacity of 129Xe MRI VDP to detect early responses to treatment. In addition, the mechanistic insights provided by 129Xe MRI VDP also indicated that these benefits are likely due to the combination of UMEC (a long-acting muscarinic antagonist) and an efficacious inhaled corticosteroid, addressing undertreated inflammatory bronchoconstriction, helping to restore airway caliber and function that more closely resemble the airways of a healthy individual.Videos available for this article.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03392-4.

Introduction

Up to 60% of patients with asthma remain poorly controlled despite receiving current recommended treatment with inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist (ICS/LABA) therapies [1, 2]. Patients with uncontrolled asthma have greater disease burden, risk of serious comorbidities, and worse health-related quality of life [1, 2]. As such, understanding why current treatments fail to fully meet patient needs is key to improving outcomes. Traditionally, clinical trials investigating inhaled asthma therapies have relied on pulmonary function tests and spirometry parameters, such as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), to evaluate efficacy, despite these measures being insensitive to small airways disease, not supported by current clinical guidelines, and considered highly variable and poorly reproducible [3, 4]. While these measures provide some insight into the action of bronchodilators on broader lung function, the effect on the airways and the relationship between clinical improvements and airway dysfunction has not been fully characterized and remains poorly understood.

In recent years, pulmonary imaging methods such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using inhaled hyperpolarized gas have enabled the analysis of airway occlusions [5] and airway dysfunction in patients with asthma [5]. In particular, the quantification of ventilation defects, for example, by using 3He MRI or 129Xe [known as the ventilation defect percent (VDP)], has been shown to correlate with the number and extent of mucus occlusions, poor disease control, increased airway resistance, and reduced quality of life, highlighting its potential as a measure of disease severity [5–8]. While both 3He and 129Xe MRI have been used to investigate lung dysfunction and disease severity in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [9–13], 129Xe appears to be emerging as the preferred option due to its greater availability and lower cost [3, 14]. In addition, the higher density and viscosity of 129Xe gas, which is closer to that of molecular oxygen (in air) than 3He, may help reveal ventilation defects that are not observed with 3He MRI in asthma and COPD [8, 14]. This preference is reflected by 129Xe being the first hyperpolarized MRI contrast agent approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the evaluation of lung ventilation in December 2022 [15].

In this narrative review, we summarize the current clinical evidence on the impact of bronchodilator therapies on airway dysfunction in patients with asthma, focusing on studies that utilized 129Xe MRI techniques to visualize and quantify VDP.

Literature Search

A literature search using PubMed was conducted on 20 May 2025 to identify clinical trials published in the past 15 years that used 129Xe MRI techniques in patients with asthma, using the search terms “129Xe” or “Xenon” AND “asthma”, together with the ‘clinical trial’ filter. Of the 10 publications identified, 2 reported pre- and post-inhaled bronchodilator imaging data and were therefore deemed relevant for inclusion. Of these, one used xenon ventilation CT and was excluded. The remaining publication was included in this narrative review. The bibliographies of selected asthma imaging review papers [16, 17], together with author knowledge, were also used to supplement the search, resulting in 4 additional studies being included. Thus, 5 studies in total were included in this narrative review.

As a narrative review, this article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. As such, no additional approvals or informed consent were obtained above those collected as part of the original clinical studies.

Imaging Methodology

Across the 5 studies, similar techniques were utilized to acquire 129Xe MRI and to calculate VDP. Typically, this involved a whole body 1.5 or 3.0 Tesla MRI, following inhalation of up to 1.0 L of a mixture of hyperpolarized gas, with MRI performed with the participant at inspiratory breath-hold for around 8–10 s [3, 8, 18–20]. VDP was calculated using semi-automated segmentation software that analyzed voxel intensity and generated a gas distribution cluster map, with the categories of clusters ranging from signal voids (or ventilation defects) to areas of hyper-intense signal [3, 8, 18–20]. The volume of all the ventilation defects was then normalized using the total thoracic cavity volume to calculate the VDP.

Studies Outcomes

Single-Inhaler Bronchodilator Therapy

The majority (4 out of 5) of asthma studies utilizing 129Xe MRI VDP involved single-inhaler bronchodilator therapy (usually salbutamol). An early study by Svenningsen et al. provided proof-of-concept for using 129Xe MRI VDP to evaluate inhaled bronchodilator efficacy in newly-diagnosed adults with asthma (n = 43) [8], and the findings indicated that 129Xe MRI had a higher sensitivity for VDP detection than conventional 3He MRI, particularly pre-salbutamol (8% vs. 6%; p = 0.003), with a remodeled airway identified only with 129Xe MRI [8]. This increased sensitivity was attributed to the higher density and viscosity of 129Xe gas, which is closer to that of molecular oxygen in air [8]. In this study, significant improvements in 129Xe MRI VDP were observed after salbutamol administration, consistent with improvements in FEV1 (Table 1) [8].

Later studies expanded upon these early findings and demonstrated the feasibility and utility of 129Xe MRI VDP in (1) children with well-controlled severe asthma (n = 6) [3], (2) adults with severe asthma (n = 7) [19], and (3) adults with uncontrolled severe asthma (n = 26) [18]. In the former, all the children showed a significantly improved 129Xe MRI VDP after salbutamol use (0.28–0.02%; p < 0.004), despite the changes being smaller than those observed previously in adults with asthma, with levels returning to those of healthy patients [3]. Interestingly, FEV1 did not significantly improve in these patients with asthma, suggesting that 129Xe MRI VDP may be a more sensitive outcome measure than conventional spirometry measures [3]. Similarly, in adults with uncontrolled severe asthma, both FEV1 and 129Xe VDP were significantly improved after salbutamol use in a real-world clinical setting (up to 1100 mL and 19.4% improvements, respectively; both p ≤ 0.0004) [18].

Triple-Inhaled Bronchodilator Therapy

The most recently published study involving 129Xe MRI was an open-label, single-arm, 6- to 12-week, mechanistic study in adults with moderate–severe asthma who were uncontrolled despite ICS/LABA maintenance therapy (NCT04651777) [20]. Patients stopped their ICS/LABA medication the day before the first study visit, when they were switched to single-inhaler triple therapy (FF/UMEC/VI 200/62.5/25 µg) once daily for 6 weeks (with the option to continue treatment for up to 12 weeks). To date, this is the only study utilizing 129Xe MRI to evaluate the efficacy of single-inhaler triple therapy in asthma.

The primary endpoint was improvement in airway dysfunction, as measured using 129Xe MRI VDP, at 6 weeks. Secondary/exploratory outcomes included: 129Xe MRI VDP at 12 weeks, as well as measures of lung function, such as spirometry (FEV1) and oscillometry {i.e., distal airway resistance which is measured as resistance at 5 Hz [reflecting all airways] subtracted from resistance at 19 Hz [reflecting the central or large airways (R5–19)]}, as well as assessments of asthma control [Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ)-6] and quality of life [St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ)] at 6 weeks.

In total, 31 patients with uncontrolled asthma were enrolled in the study, 28 completed the 6-week treatment period, and 17 elected to continue treatment up to the optional Week 12 visit [21]. Full baseline demographics and characteristics have been published previously [20–22]. In brief, patients had a mean (standard deviation) age of 54 (15) years, % predicted FEV1 of 72% (19), ACQ-6 score of 2.1 (1.0), and SGRQ score of 49 (16). At Week 6, pre-bronchodilator 129Xe MRI VDP was both visibly and quantitatively improved compared with baseline (mean VDP: 9% vs. 13%, respectively; p = 0.02). The 4% reduction represents a clinically meaningful improvement, based on the minimal clinically important difference for 129Xe MRI VDP, which has been established as 2% in patients with asthma using both anchor-based and distribution-based approaches [23]. Similar results were observed for secondary outcomes, with patients showing significant improvements at Week 6 versus baseline in mean FEV1 (pre-bronchodilator % predicted: 78% vs. 72%, respectively; p = 0.008), R5–19 (1.1 vs. 2.0 cm·H2O/L/s; p = 0.001) and SGRQ score (35 vs. 49; p = 0.001), as well as a numerical improvement in ACQ-6 (1.4 vs. 2.0; p = 0.2). Discussion

Results from five studies over the past 15 years have demonstrated that 129Xe MRI enables the visualization and quantification of functional airway changes over time in patients with asthma, not only in a controlled clinical trial environment but also in a real-world setting, in patients with both controlled and uncontrolled asthma. This novel approach enables the observation and quantification of the mechanistic changes at the level of the airways following the administration of bronchodilators (or other asthma therapies), potentially aiding in the personalization of treatment, the development of new and more effective therapies, and the monitoring of disease changes over time.

The results from the most recent 129Xe MRI study involving single-inhaler triple therapy in patients with uncontrolled moderate–severe asthma despite dual ICS/LABA maintenance therapy [20] provided important new insights. In these patients, current Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines first recommend a step-up in ICS dose to achieve disease control, followed by the addition of a LAMA if asthma remains uncontrolled (Steps 4 and 5) [24]. The addition of the LAMA, UMEC to the ICS/LABA dual therapy FF/VI was recently investigated in the Phase 3A, double-blind, randomized CAPTAIN trial (NCT02924688) [25]. Primary results from CAPTAIN showed that treatment with single-inhaler triple therapy FF/UMEC/VI 200/62.5/25 µg led to significant improvements in lung function (FEV1) and nominally statistically significant improvements in asthma control (ACQ-7 responder rates) versus FF/VI [25]. The results of the most recent 129Xe MRI study were consistent with those of CAPTAIN, with FF/UMEC/VI 200/62.5/25 µg leading to significant improvements in central airway function (FEV1), distal airway function (R5–19), and quality of life (SGRQ) after only 6 weeks in patients with uncontrolled moderate–severe asthma on maintenance ICS/LABA [20]. Of note, for the first time, the use of 129Xe MRI enabled the visualization of these benefits, providing mechanistic insights and indicating that these may be due to a combination of the LAMA component, UMEC, which was delivered at the same time as a potent ICS, and together addressed small airways bronchoconstriction, helping restore airway caliber and function to more closely resemble airways of a healthy individual. This novel pulmonary imaging method may also be applied to other asthma therapies, such as anti-interleukin-5 receptor α (anti-IL-5Rα) therapies (e.g., benralizumab) or anti-IL-4Rα (e.g., dupilumab); early evidence has shown significant changes after treatment in patients with severe eosinophilic asthma and ongoing studies aim to further refine 129Xe VDP as an outcome in this setting [26–28].

Limitations of this narrative review, which should be considered when interpreting the results, include the small number of patients in many of the studies, the overall lack of control groups, and differences in imaging techniques and methodologies. These limitations reduce the statistical power of the studies and leave the data open to various sources of bias, including selection, classification, and confounding biases, potentially making any causal inferences challenging. Despite this, 129Xe MRI VDP showed broadly consistent sensitivity and specificity as a marker for airway dysfunction, enabling the detection and analysis of early responses to treatment (as early as 6 weeks). In the open-label, mechanistic study of single-inhaler triple therapy with FF/UMEC/VI 200/62.5/25 µg, the consistency of clinical outcomes with those observed in the Phase 3A CAPTAIN trial lends further credence to the MRI findings, in the absence of a control arm. It is hoped that as awareness of the capabilities of 129Xe MRI increases, through the efforts of groups such as the 129Xe Clinical Trials Consortium, more standardized protocols such as those already proposed by the Consortium will find more widespread use, reducing variation and potential errors with 129Xe imaging techniques [29].

Conclusion

Although evidence in this review is limited to five clinical studies over the past 15 years, results have shown that 129Xe MRI VDP is sensitive to functional airway changes over time in patients with asthma receiving inhaled bronchodilator therapy. Visualization of these changes provides further mechanistic insights into the broader clinical benefits measured by conventional spirometry outcomes, and could aid the development and personalization of future asthma treatments.
