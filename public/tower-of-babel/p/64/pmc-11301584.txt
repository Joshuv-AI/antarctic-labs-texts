Contralateral approach to giant ruptured and unruptured ophthalmic artery aneurysms: patient series
Authors: Arsen Seferi, Arben Rroji, Ridvan Alimehmeti, Mirel Grada, Eugen Enesi, Elsa Nico, Mithat Demneri, Mentor Petrela
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDGiant ophthalmic artery (OphA) aneurysms remain surgically challenging despite the progress in endovascular treatments. This study describes the contralateral interoptic corridor in select patients based on imaging criteria suitable for clipping. The aim of this study was to show that despite the growing use of novel endovascular techniques, such as coil embolization and flow diversion, for the treatment of OphA aneurysms, microsurgical clipping may still be preferred for giant ones under certain conditions.OBSERVATIONSThe authors retrospectively reviewed the records of the microsurgical treatment of unruptured and ruptured giant OphA aneurysms at the University Hospital Center “Mother Teresa,” Tirana, from 2007 to 2016. Four patients were selected for microsurgery and the contralateral approach using ophthalmic evaluations and coronal imaging on computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and digital subtraction angiography that demonstrated aneurysms with a small neck and an orientation between 11 and 13 on the coronal clock face. Ophthalmic artery (OphA) aneurysms arise from the paraclinoid segment of the internal carotid artery (ICA), between the proximal dural ring and the posterior communicating artery, in clear relation to the OphA.1 Presenting with visual deficits from mass effect on the optic nerve, subarachnoid hemorrhage from rupture, or no symptoms from an incidental finding, OphA aneurysms account for a majority of paraclinoid aneurysms.2, 3 Given their proximity to vital neurovascular structures, such as the optic apparatus, oculomotor nerve, cavernous sinus, and branches and perforating arteries of the ICA, OphA aneurysms are particularly challenging to treat.1, 4, 5 This challenge is further augmented by a giant aneurysm size, defined as having a diameter of at least 25 mm.2

Despite the growing use of novel endovascular techniques, such as coil embolization and flow diversion, for the treatment of OphA aneurysms, microsurgical clipping may still be preferred for giant ones under certain conditions.6 While ipsilateral approaches are the gold standard for clipping OphA aneurysms, multiple studies have defended the contralateral approach because of its ability to better expose the paraclinoid anatomy since Yasargil et al. published the first case in 1977.7, 8 Recently, Zhao et al.6 proposed a patient-selection algorithm for the contralateral interoptic approach to paraclinoid aneurysms. However, other than the studies by Vajda et al.2 and Hongo et al.,8 none of the aforementioned studies included giant OphA aneurysms. Herein, we describe an additional 4-patient series of ruptured and unruptured giant OphA aneurysms that were successfully microsurgically clipped through a contralateral approach.

Study Description

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of all patients who had undergone microsurgical treatment of a ruptured or unruptured giant OphA aneurysm at the University Hospital Center “‘Mother Teresa,” Tirana, Albania, by the senior author (M.P.), between 2007 and 2016. During this 9-year review period, the senior author operated on 6 giant OphA aneurysms. Of these 6 patients, 4 (3 unruptured and 1 ruptured) underwent microsurgical clipping using the contralateral interoptic approach.

Preoperative visual field impairments were evaluated by a neuro-ophthalmologist, and the indication for microsurgery versus endovascular treatment was discussed with a neurointerventional radiologist. After these discussions, the recommendation for microsurgery was proposed to the family. The necessity for open surgery was based on the anatomy of the aneurysm visualized using computed tomography (CT), maximum intensity projection (MIP), and multiplanar reformation (MPR), which demonstrated the dome orientation and calcium presence in the neck of the aneurysm (Fig. 1). Four patients were selected for contralateral interoptic corridor clipping of giant OphA aneurysms whose domes pointed upward at 11–13 o’clock using the clock face method on coronal MIP imaging and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; Table 1). A calcium-free neck was confirmed on CT MPR (Fig. 1A). Using MRI, normal and postfixed chiasms were evident in all 4 cases, and a partial thrombus was noted in 3 cases (Fig. 1C). The origin of the OphA was identified superior to the neck of the aneurysm using three-dimensional (3D) digital subtraction angiography (DSA; Fig. 1E). DSA also identified small vessels around the origin of the aneurysm (Fig. 1D). The small ICA branches and superior hypophyseal arteries around the neck of the aneurysm were visible only intraoperatively (Fig. 2C). Open proximal control of the cervical ICA was considered unnecessary in the 3 unruptured aneurysms, given the speed at which it could be achieved if necessary.9 In the ruptured aneurysm, cervical exposure of the ICA was prepared but not used.

Through the interoptic corridor, the contralateral optic nerve was gently elevated by the aspirator to expose the neck superiorly, dissecting it from the OphA and inferiorly to confirm the small neck size (Video 1). The use of long 11-mm Yasargil clips permitted better visualization of the neck, allowing the tips to touch the contralateral clinoidal dura before clipping. After neck exclusion, this approach prevented aneurysm residuals and small artery injuries (Fig. 2C, Video 2). When the contralateral dura clip tips were not able to secure the neck during the first attempt, a second sandwich optic clip was used (Fig. 2B).

Postoperatively, DSA showed exclusion of the aneurysm without any residual and preservation of the OphA in all of the patients (Fig. 3). The patients did not receive corticoid therapy. After clipping, all patients were kept on a hypertensive regimen. There were no postoperative complications in visual outcomes in all 4 cases. In fact, the visual deficits remained the same in 3 cases and improved in 1 case. Discussion

Most OphA aneurysms are now treated endovascularly. However, ever since the first experiences of Hunterian ligation for giant OphA aneurysms, these aneurysms remain suitable for microsurgery despite the evolution of flow-diverting devices (FDDs) and coiling.10 Endovascular treatment of giant aneurysms is challenging because it is difficult to achieve wall acquisition, requires multiple devices, carries risks of embolus and occlusion, and involves a blind approach that requires special maneuvers during tractability.1 The contralateral approach is one solution in experienced hands and at high-volume cerebrovascular centers. Vajda et al. and Hongo et al.2, 8 were the first to report this approach, and Zhao et al. recently proposed the algorithm for its application in small OphA aneurysms.6 Given that retreatment for residual or recurrent aneurysms is more common after endovascular treatment, microsurgical clipping is indicated for giant thrombotic OphA aneurysms in younger patients.

Observations

Endovascular treatment of OphA aneurysms is associated with greater visual field complications and retreatment of residuals or recurrent aneurysms compared to open microsurgery.11–15 However, a recent study showed that coiling or FDD placement had safer neurological outcomes compared to microsurgical clipping.16 Due to the aneurysm neck’s proximity to the OphA and increased risk of visual complications during coiling, endovascular treatment may be limited.17 Nevertheless, a comprehensive retrospective study of 161 patients treated endovascularly for an unruptured carotid OphA aneurysm demonstrated noticeably reduced morbidity.18 This study, however, also showed that 18.9% of endovascularly treated patients “needed additional intervention on the basis of early angiographic results.”18

The multicenter International Retrospective Study of Pipeline Embolization Device (IntrePED) registry study of 906 treated aneurysms showed that large ICA aneurysms had a noticeably greater incidence of complications than small ICA aneurysms.19 While Carnevale et al.20 noted the increasing use of FDD as the primary treatment for giant unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms, no giant OphA aneurysms were reported. Moreover, Enriquez-Marulanda et al.21 concluded that further innovations of FDD technologies are expected in order to improve the safety and results of endovascular procedures.

Hendricks and Spetzler used Hunterian ligation and revascularization in a previously coiled giant OphA aneurysm.10 Microsurgery is indicated for giant OphA aneurysms in younger patients when the position of the aneurysm and anatomical features allow ease of access and atherosclerotic variants impede endovascular treatment.3

In another study, the ipsilateral microsurgical approach was used in a giant OphA aneurysm. The ipsilateral approach is not ideal for OphA aneurysms because it increases the risk of visual deficits, does not provide enough space to gain proximal control, may require a clinoidectomy or various procedures such as suction decompression, and is associated with a high risk of stroke.4–6 As long as the trajectory is correctly planned and certain anatomical conditions are met, the contralateral approach offers better anatomical exposure of the superomedial aspect of the OphA segment and its perforating branches, including the OphA, than the ipsilateral approach even with the removal of the anterior clinoid process.12

The use of the contralateral interoptic approach for giant OphA aneurysms depends on the aneurysm anatomy (e.g., dome orientation, small neck), a calcium-free neck, and thrombus presence and chiasm position on MRI. Under the aforementioned preoperative criteria, the results of this study indicate that the contralateral approach is less likely to provoke new or worsened visual deficits.

Study Limitations

The bias in patient selection in this retrospective study was determined by discussions among experienced surgeons and neurointerventional radiologists. Furthermore, the progress of balloon/stent-assisted coiling and FDD technologies has altered the alternatives for endovascular therapy, and it is unknown how these novel techniques may influence clinical results. Another limitation of this study is the small number of cases, as experiences may differ at other institutions.

Lessons

According to this study, giant OphA aneurysms may still be suitable for open surgery despite the progress of endovascular FDD. The selection bias in favor of microsurgery was determined by the expert experiences of the senior author and the neurointerventional radiologist. Giant OphA aneurysms can be treated safely through the contralateral interoptic corridor when meeting the criteria of a small noncalcified neck, a dome position of 11–13 on the coronal clockface, and a non-prefixed chiasm.
