Psychosocial factors and susceptibility to upper respiratory infections in community‐dwelling older adults of Japan
Authors: Noriko Okamoto, Hiroki Matsui, Hirohito Tsuboi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUpper respiratory infections (URIs), including the common cold, can lead to community‐acquired pneumonia and pose significant risks for older adults. In this study, we aimed to examine the influence of psychosocial factors on URIs among community‐dwelling older adults.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was administered to 500 community‐dwelling older adults via an internet research panel (386 males and 114 females). The prevalence of colds in the past year and demographic variables were assessed. Psychosocial factors were measured using the 8‐item short‐form health survey (SF‐8). Participants were categorized into two groups: those who experienced a cold within the past year (cold group) and those who did not (no‐cold group). Group comparisons were conducted using t‐tests or Chi‐square tests, followed by logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsAfter adjusting for age, BMI, and smoking and drinking habits, scores for general health, vitality, role emotional functioning, mental health, and mental component summary score (MCS) on the SF‐8 were significantly higher in the no‐cold group than in the cold group.ConclusionsPsychosocial factors, such as enhanced general health, vitality, role emotional functioning, mental health, and MCS are associated with decreased susceptibility to URIs.Keywords: older adult, psychosocial factor, QOL, social tie, upper respiratory infection

INTRODUCTION

Cold is an illness that can impact both the upper and lower respiratory tracts. Although most colds are mild and individuals recover within a week without treatment or a few treatments, the viruses that cause colds can lead to infections such as pneumonia. Certain viruses have the potential to directly cause viral pneumonia, and colds may also be complicated by bacterial pneumonia. Upper respiratory infections (URIs) are a contributing factor to community‐acquired pneumonia (CAP). 1 Pneumonia severity can range from mild to life‐threatening, posing the greatest risk to individuals over 65 years of age, as well as to infants and young children with compromised immune systems. As the susceptibility of older adults to pneumonia increases with age, 2 the prevention of URIs in this population is crucial for maintaining population health and wellness.

Although various viruses and bacteria can induce URIs, susceptibility to viral infections and the progression of symptoms vary among individuals. The clinical course of an infection is ultimately shaped by the complex interactions between the patient and the virus. An individual's overall susceptibility to viral or bacterial exposure is influenced by multiple innate factors (e.g., age, nutritional status, genetics, immune competency, and preexisting chronic diseases) and external variables (e.g., concurrent drug therapy). 3

Additionally, some studies have identified psychosocial factors as influencing upper respiratory tract illnesses. The impact of psychosocial stress and dysphoric mood on acute upper respiratory tract infections and the common cold has been frequently documented. However, the clinical relevance of psychosocial factors necessitates confirmation through naturalistic studies of infectious illnesses in the population. The relationship between psychosocial factors and URIs is proven to be independent of various health practices. 4 For instance, Cohen et al. examined the impact of psychological status and social conditions on the common cold.5, 6 Building upon their findings, mechanisms and preventive strategies for URIs have been established.7, 8

Since pneumonia remains a leading cause of mortality, particularly among older adults, 9 implementing various preventive measures in daily life against URIs is essential. Therefore, pneumonia prevention is a paramount public health priority for the older population. Health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) is linked to self‐care behaviors in managing the common cold, with individuals opting for self‐care reporting higher HRQOL compared with those seeking medical attention. 10 We aimed to elucidate the role of psychosocial factors in respiratory infectious diseases among community‐dwelling older adults.

METHODS

A cross‐sectional survey was administered to 500 community‐dwelling older adults in Shiga Prefecture, Japan. Participants diagnosed with dementia were excluded. Data were gathered through an internet research pooled panel (Rakuten Insight, Inc) from November 25 to 29, 2021. Individuals aged over 65 years and registered with the agency were eligible, and recruitment concluded once the target sample size of 500 was reached. The survey was administered once, with an estimated completion time of ~ 20 min.

The following variables were assessed: age, gender, history of diseases, daily infection prevention behaviors, and occurrence of a cold in the past year. In this survey, the occurrence of a cold in the past year was defined as having experienced a common cold. In addition to collecting demographic information, such as age, height, weight, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, participants completed several questionnaires: the 8‐item Short‐Form Health Survey (SF‐8), 11 a comprehensive HRQOL tool assessing eight health domains (general health, physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, vitality, social functioning, role emotional, and mental health). In this scores include both a physical component summary score (PCS) and a mental component summary score (MCS); the Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS), 12 which screens for social isolation in older adults; and the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Gerontology Index of Competence, 13 evaluating activities of daily living through Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) across three subscales: instrumental self‐maintenance, intellectual activity, and social role.

Statistical analysis

The participants were categorized into two groups: those who experienced a cold in the past year (cold group) and those who did not (no‐cold group). The groups were compared using t‐tests or Chi‐square tests, and logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for covariates. All statistical analyses were calculated using the Japanese version of the IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM, Tokyo, Japan).

RESULTS

The study comprised 500 participants (386 males and 114 females; mean age = 70.9 years; SD = 4.5). Among individuals aged over 65 years, 16 males and none of the females developed pneumonia (Fisher's exact test: p = 0.027).

DISCUSSION

We analyzed physical, psychosocial, and lifestyle factors related to the common cold in 500 individuals aged over 65 years using an internet research panel. Higher general health, vitality, role emotional, mental health, and MCS were associated with lower cold susceptibility among females (n = 69). In males (n = 312), additional factors, such as higher role physical, bodily pain, social functioning, and PCS also correlated with reduced cold susceptibility. Furthermore, lower intellectual activity in IADL and LSNS was linked to decreased susceptibility to colds. However, no relationships were noted between the common cold, social functioning, and interpersonal skills. This might be attributable to the unique circumstances of the COVID‐19 pandemic.

The SF‐36 measures health status, with the SF‐8 serving as its shortened form. Several SF‐8 sub‐scores, excluding “physical functioning,” were significantly lower in the cold groups than in the no‐cold groups: MCS including “vitality,” “role emotional,” and “mental health” were lower among both genders, and “social functioning” was lower only among males; PCS including “general health” were lower among both genders, and “role physical” and “bodily pain” were lower only among males. Although certain scores did not exhibit significance, which may be attributable to the low sample size, these scores were lower in the cold group.

Numerous studies indicate that mental states, including stress, depression, and vitality, affect susceptibility to the common cold. Psychological stress increases vulnerability to URIs. 14 Stress impacts the pathogenesis of physical diseases by inducing negative affective states (e.g., anxiety and depression), which directly influence biological processes or behavioral patterns, thereby affecting disease risk. 15

Social relationships have also been identified as important predictors of health and well‐being.16, 17 Susceptibility to colds decreases as the number of social roles increases. Individuals with the least social integration (one to three social roles) were 4.2 times more likely to develop a cold compared with those with the highest integration (six or more). 5 Furthermore, perceived social support is hypothesized to mitigate the pathogenic effects of stress.18, 19 These findings support the present findings related to the SF‐8. However, LSNS scores, which assess social engagement, were lower in the cold group among males, aligning with previous research hypotheses. Nonetheless, LSNS measures the quantity of social engagements without necessarily evaluating the quality of social ties. Possibly, individuals who had poor quality of the social ties exhibited a tendency toward forming more ties.

Although we aimed to identify pneumonia‐related mortality factors, particularly among older adults, 9 only 16 of the 386 males and none of the 114 females reported pneumonia cases, resulting in an incidence too low for meaningful discussion.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data relied on self‐reported questionnaires, potentially introducing recall bias. Second, participants were restricted to individuals registered with the Internet research agency, which may not comprehensively represent the general older adult population; therefore, the possibility of a selection bias cannot be ruled out. Third, we classified having a cold as an upper respiratory infection; however, responses were based on self‐assessment, with definitive diagnoses unavailable or unknown. Fourth, the status of nutrition and chronic disease was unknown; future research is required to include these variables.

CONCLUSIONS

We identified that psychosocial factors, including better general health, vitality, role emotional, mental health, and MCS, were linked to reduced susceptibility to experiencing colds. These findings suggest that the aforementioned factors may aid in preventing pneumonia, a condition with a high fatality rate among older adults.
