Overcoming gaps and barriers to effectively integrate social science in European conservation
Authors: Claudia Gruenewald, Thomas E Fish, Eick von Ruschkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe importance of social science to address the human dimensions of natural resource management is increasingly recognized in the conservation field, yet the application of associated concepts, theories, methods, and data remains underrepresented in parts of Europe. Common barriers and gaps, persistent over decades, including institutional constraints, work environment, different cultures and languages between natural and social science disciplines, lack of qualified personnel, and an accessible professional community, are often cited as underlying and driving factors. To better understand, contextualize, and inform solutions for wider use of social science, we analyzed interactions with conservation researchers and practitioners across a series of organized events from 2018 to 2023 (e.g., in‐depth interviews, facilitated discussions, expert workshops, international conferences, knowledge exchange forums). Frequently mentioned challenges to integrating social science were limited opportunities to engage across disciplines in the workplace, to interact with like‐minded colleagues, and for 2‐way communication and knowledge exchange between scientists and practitioners, especially given the diversity of subject matter expertise and disciplinary training of conservation professionals. The needs identified included capacity building to strengthen individual and institutional competencies, such as creating and maintaining multidisciplinary teams and professional networks, working across different organizations at multiple scales, and advancing institutional support for acceptance and validation of social science as a routine element of conservation practice, including representation of social scientists in the workforce. Steps to address such challenges can be taken along 2 pathways that conservation researchers and practitioners consistently mentioned: first, increasing conservation social science literacy through awareness building, targeted professional development training, and institutional support, and, second, fostering knowledge exchange, enhancing professional networks, and bolstering a robust community of practice across Europe.Keywords: capacity building, community of practice, conservation practitioners, Germany, human dimensions, qualitative, science–practice gap, workforce, Alemania, brecha ciencia‐práctica, comunidad de práctica, cualitativo, desarrollo de capacidades, dimensiones humanas, fuerza laboral, practicantes de la conservación

INTRODUCTION

The call for integrating different disciplines and their respective concepts, theories, and methods is not new. In the context of conservation, it dates back at least to the 1960s (Henning, 1970; Potter et al., 1973; Watson et al., 2016). Integrated fields benefit from engaging diverse perspectives, practices, technical expertise, and interpretations to address complex problems that are beyond the scope of single disciplines (e.g., global change, One Health, social–ecological systems, risk and resilience, evidence‐based decision‐making) (e.g., Breckon & Dodson, 2016; Ciocca Eller, 2024; Holterman et al., 2023; Lischka et al., 2018; Mahajan et al., 2023; Walsh et al., 2019; Weaver et al., 2014; Weichselgartner & Kasperson, 2010). Successful integration requires mutual respect and appreciation of the disciplinary training, conventions, and approaches other fields bring to the table and the value of their respective contributions. The practice of conservation is value laden and should therefore be framed in terms of both the inherent ecological and societal aspects necessary to achieve stated outcomes (Hackmann et al., 2014).

Social science has gained traction in natural resource management and conservation over the past 20 years. For example, human dimensions (HD) are discussed in notable articles (cf. Mascia et al., 2003), conservation social science (CSS) has been elevated to a field of study unto itself (cf. Bennett & Roth, 2015; Bennett, Roth, Klain, Chan, Christie, et al., 2017; Bennett, Roth, Klain, Chan, Clark, et al., 2017; Manfredo et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2023), and social science is part of learned society activities (e.g., establishment of the Society for Conservation Biology Social Science Working Group) (Thornhill, 2003). Although there is general agreement on the importance of integrated approaches (e.g., Campbell, 2005; Serota et al., 2023; Teel et al., 2018), the social sciences have not been meaningfully or routinely integrated in conservation practice. By integration, we mean bringing together social and natural science and their outputs, which in our experience is not limited to interdisciplinary approaches but also includes cross‐, multi‐, and transdisciplinary approaches. We focused on conservation practice but recognize that science is an inextricable part of effective evidence‐based conservation. The role of social science in conservation can apply to both social science research for and on conservation (Sandbrook et al., 2013). Our emphasis is primarily on the integration of social science in conservation by practitioners in a European context, but we also applied a transatlantic lens to such integration based on well‐established knowledge exchange with colleagues in North America.

Decades‐long efforts to integrate social science in natural resources management in North America (Decker et al., 2024; Manfredo et al., 2019) stand in strong contrast to the present situation across Europe (Blicharska et al., 2016; Straka et al., 2023), where a recognition of the value of the social sciences in conservation has been slow to develop. In Germany, for example, a remarkable disconnect exists between nature conservation problem framings and insights from psychological and other social sciences. Consequently, integrative approaches have not taken hold. Although research on environmental awareness and behavior grew with the advent of the environmental movement (de Haan & Kuckartz, 1996; Fietkau & Kessel, 1981; Hamann et al., 2016; Schahn & Giesinger, 1993), findings never diffused into conservation practice. We have observed that German conservation managers are often puzzled by the lack of success of conservation projects, yet recommended success factors based on social science research (Brendle, 1999) have not pervaded the profession. Likewise, an analysis and hands‐on guide for the European Ecological Network Natura 2000 (Sauer et al., 2005) has been largely neglected by its target audience.

Ecological research in the European Union (EU) is significantly more prevalent (79%) than social and policy research (Popescu et al., 2014). Blicharska et al. (2016) reviewed social science contributions relative to Natura 2000 and identified “a non‐negligible” amount of research on social aspects. However, they noted that publications are unevenly distributed among EU countries and lack sufficient synthesis. Besides an overall underrepresentation of practically relevant findings, some topics rarely have been investigated or findings have not been meaningfully integrated into practice (e.g., low‐quality research, studies often not based on local scales or in local contexts, only symbolic efforts, power imbalances ignored, overall processes rushed). These findings especially concern public participation and stakeholder involvement, 2 major issues surrounding Natura 2000 driven by generally negative perceptions, low acceptance, resistance or slow acceptance, and obstacle‐rich implementation (Blicharska et al., 2016). Although no empirical data exist, and despite slightly different approaches in recent years (e.g., conflicts with the return of wolves and other large carnivore species to densely populated parts of Europe), even large‐scale EU conservation projects often do not contain adequately proportionate social science components (i.e., majority of budgets allocated to ecologically grounded conservation measures).

Several studies (e.g., Bennett, Roth, Klain, Chan, Clark, et al., 2017; Claus, 2022; Fox et al., 2006; Heberlein, 1988; Marchini et al., 2023; Niemiec et al., 2021; Pooley et al. et al., 2014; Sexton et al., 2013; Welch‐Divine & Campbell, 2010) describe challenges to integrative efforts, for example, lack of a common vocabulary, unbalanced power hierarchies, social scientists reduced to facilitator or communicator, and lack of rewards for collaborative projects. Niemiec et al. (2021) developed a framework for conservation planning, which they used to examine challenges and enabling factors to advance social science integration. Chua et al. (2020, p. 42) recommend moving beyond the “critique (of conservation by social scientists) or co‐optation (of social scientific methods and insights by conservationists)” that often frames the discourse. They present a case study on how theoretical reflections and ideal‐world recommendations might work in practice. Jones (2024) argues that increasing CSS research alone does not ensure integration, suggesting rather that conservation practitioners require a level of “social science fluency” based on a good understanding of the conceptual underpinnings tied to the application of social science to investigate management issues and inform solutions. Despite a growing body of literature, and even with the best intentions, aspects of direct practical relevance to integration benefitting practitioners are often not highlighted or findings and recommendations are not readily applicable to on‐the‐ground conservation without some translational effort (Laurance et al., 2012; Walsh et al., 2019).

Such persistent science‐to‐practice gaps have, in part, been the impetus for hosting the CSS events discussed here. We addressed how‐to questions related to the implementation and practice side of CSS and share insights in the spirit of an appreciative inquiry solution‐oriented approach, especially with respect to conservation practitioners’ everyday work experiences. Many of these aspects have been largely absent in previous studies. We sought to provide a unique look at practical challenges and solution pathways as groundwork for advancing CSS across Europe.

ORGANIZED EVENTS AND INTERACTIONS

We interacted with conservation researchers and practitioners across a series of organized events from 2018 to 2023 (e.g., in‐depth interviews, facilitated discussions, expert workshops, international conferences, knowledge exchange forums). Each event included a number of activities with questions to prompt discussion (e.g., Where do you envision CSS in a project or issue you are working on? What challenges exist in applying CSS to this project or issue? What actions would address challenges in applying CSS? What is the current CSS capacity within your organization or country? How do you raise awareness about the need for CSS capacity?). Events involved participants from EU and non‐EU countries, including ongoing transatlantic knowledge exchange between EU and North American partners (Table 1). Many participants’ home organizations operate in coordination with other EU member states and EU agencies or at a global scale. Some participants have worked and lived in several European countries and represented a multinational perspective. Participants comprised a mix of professionals representing the research and academic perspective (i.e., universities, research institutes) and the applied practitioner perspective (i.e., federal, state, and local natural resource agencies, protected areas, training academies, conservation nongovernmental organizations [NGOs], environmental foundations, private consultancies, small business owners, livestock producers). Overall, there were slightly more interactions with practitioners. We did not aim to distinguish between academic and nonacademic groups; rather, we were interested in identifying and characterizing challenges and ways to advance CSS on a broader scale and in embracing multiple points of view.

Each activity produced a series of different documentation materials, including observations, retrospective storytelling (Defila & de Giulio, 2016), personal notes, interview transcripts, worksheets and flip charts (capturing participant input), photos, and project reports (Table 1). First, we reviewed materials for each event separately to extract qualitative data related to problems and challenges (with potential root causes, if given) and to ideas, needs, and recommendations for solutions. Second, we grouped information from each event into categories based on themes emerging from the data. Third, we compared the information across all events, first within and then across categories, to identify recurring ideas, commonalities, differences, and patterns. In the final step, we compiled results into 3 major challenge areas and 2 potential pathways for CSS integration.

Information extracted from the documentation materials in the first and second steps corresponded to inputs during activities rather than to individuals, single countries, or specific affiliations and backgrounds. The use of quotations and italics in the text reflects participant statements and input without attribution to preserve individual participant anonymity and privacy. All descriptions are based on the lived experiences and perspectives of participants in CSS events over the last 5 years and do not reflect—although we might agree with many of them, given our own expertise as conservation professionals—our own opinions. We based our approach of data collection, extraction, and analysis on well‐established qualitative research methods. Although such methods may not involve as many participants and are not representative, quantifiable, and generalizable in the same way as some quantitative approaches, they provide no less rigorous or valid results and offer unique insights that may remain hidden otherwise. Many of the open‐ended, interactive formats we used over the years allow participants to express what is important to them with respect to the discussion topics and afford greater time for depth, detail, context, and explanation.

MAJOR CHALLENGES

Based on the ideas, needs, and obstacles raised by participating conservation researchers and practitioners, persistent challenges identified focused on multiple gaps and different interfaces between theory and practice, work environment and conditions for implementation (including working with stakeholders and communities), and observed differences in levels of awareness (i.e., modest to nonexistent) of CSS among heterogeneous actors in conservation.

Theory and practice

A general gap between theory and practice or between science and practice was cited repeatedly. This gap was mentioned in reference to interfaces between science and practice at multiple levels. This occurs on a metalevel between science and practice (i.e., between those outside academia [often with an academic background] regarding the mismatch between scientific research outputs and practical needs or relevance to practitioners). Conservation social science research was described as too abstract; too theoretical, impractical, or infeasible; or not practice oriented enough. Regarding the latter point, practitioners thought there was a lack of understanding or even empathy from scientists of the needs and functional realities of the practitioner. This they said is characterized by detachment, a “fear of contact,” lack of openness, willingness, or interest, and a focus on one‐way or top‐down approaches. When scientific projects and initiatives with social science components were applied to conservation practice and were implemented, they were often thought of as superficial, insufficient, missing the point, or too late. This was sometimes further compounded by a similar lack of understanding, open‐mindedness, and professionalization (e.g., NGOs) in the practitioner community. Hence, an overall lack of exchange, a nonexistent community, and missing contact points or networking opportunities (“Who can I turn to?”) were also described as symptomatic.

Practitioners (i.e., those outside academia) were not a homogenous group but can be characterized as representing various institution types and levels with their own respective gaps, mismatches, and practical challenges. Many conservation practitioners work for intermediary organizations or agencies that interact with academic scientists on one side and on the ground with stakeholders and communities on the other side. Challenges to CSS integration arose concretely at the workplace level in terms of how to engage colleagues and supervisors and when working with and reaching out to stakeholders and communities. Many employees of intermediary organizations held mostly office‐based jobs and felt detached from on‐the‐ground activities and disconnected from field staff and stakeholders and communities. The distance between this group of practitioners and CSS approaches was the greatest, and local and regional conservation authorities were least familiar with CSS, which suggests a potential need for direct links between science and those working on the ground and doing practical work on a regional scale. Here, the need for targeted research (e.g., case studies) that demonstrates the benefits of CSS in projects becomes evident.

Work environment and implementation

Aspects of capacity building and workforce development were mentioned across all events. Lack of training, qualifications, experience, and a lack of confidence to engage with other disciplines were described as reasons for low levels of or no CSS integration. Acquisition and development of CSS skills and competencies often proved difficult without appropriate training opportunities, and participants noted that training materials and resources should be tailored to diverse actors, experiences, backgrounds, and contexts. Despite often feeling unqualified for tasks and projects that require personnel with different disciplinary backgrounds or specific social science qualifications, these were often cited as part of job descriptions and work realities for many participants. Social science methods and tools were often absent in conservation‐related degree programs. One conservation manager explained, “I studied ecology because I wanted to deal with animals in the wild. Today, 95% of my time, I deal with people.”

Many workplaces were described as having insufficient internal knowledge transfer and management support and as exhibiting “silo thinking” (i.e., rigid separation between research fields, areas of responsibility, departments or sectors) and a lack of interest or willingness to cross disciplinary or institutional lines by a majority of people in the workforce. This was exacerbated by a strong reluctance to change and a sticking with the status quo, especially among supervisors and organizations (e.g., “simply no need recognized”). For many, it was difficult to engage colleagues in CSS endeavors (e.g., no interest, no understanding), which left those eager to get involved alone with little to no comrades in arms and often an inability to make changes on their own. Many decisions were made and priorities were set by others (i.e., top‐down with structures and hierarchies in place that do not empower employees with the professional space, flexibility, or freedom to pursue professional development related to expanded disciplinary breadth). This occurred in already high‐pressure work environments with demanding workloads that allowed no time outside of defined daily responsibilities for exploring extras, such as integration of CSS (cf. Sanborn & Jung, 2021).

Some said workplace challenges reflected increasing demand for engagement with stakeholders and communities (e.g., personal time investments, institutional constraints, dealing with hostile behavior [i.e., agency staff viewed as service providers]). Working constructively with stakeholders and communities was frequently referred to as crucial (“those you work with out there are the key, even if they do not want to… e.g., farmers”). Yet, such relationships were often characterized by a tendency to favor the status quo (“the way it has always been done,” “do not believe it works”), by a lack or even breach of trust (i.e., with government agencies), and by differing worldviews. Meeting face‐to‐face, talking, active listening (e.g., sharing stories instead of just facts), building trust and close relationships, the role of empathy, and the need for continuity and accountability were listed as key factors. Many felt that CSS approaches could really support collaborations and assist with reaching and including relevant stakeholder groups (e.g., in terms of their values, attitudes, behaviors, perspectives, and worldviews).

Along with the aforementioned challenges come other commonplace obstacles not unfamiliar to most workplaces and employees that constrain collaboration potential for those interested in expanding horizons, such as office politics, lobbying and strategizing, codependencies, playing one person against another, internal conflicts, and power differentials. Such workplace complexities require endurance, a thick skin, and personal commitment, often leading to frustrations for those who want to engage in CSS. Additional hindrances mentioned were a lack of evaluation, consistent error culture (i.e., institutional handling of mistakes and wrong decisions), and adaptive strategies. Age and generational differences were also referenced as overarching elements (e.g., old‐school thinking, agencies not ready for new topics), as were funding and other resource constraints.

Awareness and familiarity

Reported awareness and familiarity with CSS ranged from no to little awareness, to not enough information and knowledge, to difficulties in “pinning it down,” resulting from perceived fluid boundaries and lack of a clear definition with respect to CSS as a newer field of study. The breadth of CSS was also viewed as problematic (e.g., identifying overlap between other disciplines and one's own work is not easy), although it was perceived by some as both a negative and as enriching. Advantages in CSS approaches are not always clear, and skepticism existed toward the use, benefits, and effectiveness of CSS (e.g., only “empty phrases” used in papers or for grant proposals). Although this finding might not appear novel, lack of awareness and familiarity with CSS are constants that should not be neglected because they are prevalent in Germany and exist in other European countries with nascent and isolated CSS communities, where much effort remains to embed social science in conservation (“one has to explain it”).

Some similarities with previously identified barriers and challenges (e.g., Campbell, 2005; Fox et al., 2006; Welch‐Divine & Campbell, 2010) were revealed, such as different disciplinary cultures (Kagan, 2009) between the natural and social sciences; a latent unwillingness on both sides, often coupled with a lack of academic incentives and rewards; and a tendency for silo thinking and teaching in academia. These aspects were not as pronounced in our findings. Essential attributes of practical implementation seem to go beyond a pure natural–social science dichotomy (Chua et al., 2020), especially when the workplace and the workforce are concerned. The fact that not all of our findings are new is novel in itself—indicating the persistence of certain challenges for integrating CSS. This does not suggest they do not need to be remedied. Many of the problems we delineated here, in particular concerning multilayered gaps between theory and practice and the work environment as a crucial space for implementation, have been little addressed or omitted in previous studies. Certain challenges differed geographically, suggesting that some lessons learned and best practices could have merit if introduced to areas experiencing challenges that have been overcome elsewhere.

THE WAY FORWARD

Participants identified the need for capacity building to increase CSS literacy vis‐à‐vis the practical relevance of social science to achieve conservation outcomes. Fostering interdisciplinary teams with social scientists as core team members (and leaders) in codevelopment and coproduction phases of projects from start to finish was universally regarded as important. Although some of these have been recommended previously, a thorough survey of potential solutions for advancing CSS in Europe has not been undertaken. With few exceptions (e.g., Sexton et al., 2013), practical relevance of recommendations still often falls short, and suggestions remain rather broad and theoretical. Awareness of the problems without action will only lead to partially effective integration. Our recommendations for a way forward presented are based on ideas, identified needs, and potential solutions raised by event participants drawing from the diverse perspectives of their professional lived experiences. Certainly, some suggested actions seem simple or obvious, yet if this were true, CSS integration would be much further along in Europe. Until some of the necessary building blocks are in place, advancing integration of social science in conservation practice will remain limited. We offer the following suggestions and considerations to begin to lay the groundwork.

Awareness, training, and support

A need for “building awareness, sensitizing, explaining and demonstrating” was expressed throughout the CSS events. It was suggested that trainings and other forms of teaching and learning about CSS be integrated as early as possible (e.g., as part of academic degrees) and that they be introduced at different phases of educational and professional career development. Also, distinctions between the executive and implementation levels were regarded as necessary. Beyond a general demand for more training opportunities, some developed to address specific CSS topics (e.g., participatory approaches), on‐site trainings were highly sought after (e.g., dealing with difficult situations, conflicts on the ground; problem‐solving skills). In this context, conflict management tools and (institutionalized) strategies for conflict prevention and resolution with access to conflict transformation or mediation expertise were also emphasized. An application‐oriented focus, with a balanced mix of theoretical background (e.g., fundamentals of social science) and practical exercises, was highlighted as important for all trainings. Hence, not only organizational and structural capacities (e.g., knowledge management, monitoring and evaluation, time, funding, personnel) are considered vital, but also individual skills and capacities are vital because CSS integration is not just the responsibility of organizations and agencies. All professionals, including in their professional networks, need to strive for change.

Many of the aspects mentioned above also apply to increasing overall awareness of CSS. Some of the ideas are well known (e.g., getting people in touch with the topics, getting them interested, provide knowledge and information) and closely related to different levels of awareness that need addressing (e.g., Blooms Taxonomy; Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). Participants stressed the importance of the “diffusion of innovation theory” (Rogers, 2003) and acknowledged that raising awareness is not that simple and is only the foundation for further processes of decision‐making, implementation, and adoption influenced by various factors (Walsh et al., 2019). For example, some participants recommended being bolder by stressing a flagship or poster‐child of CSS to motivate people to adopt new approaches. However, when it comes to marketing and awareness raising, emphasis was placed on the need to clearly articulate the benefits and practicality of social science integration.

Increasing awareness and ultimately advancing adoption and diffusion of CSS in the conservation community will require support from multiple levels. This includes a better connection of theory and practice (i.e., demonstrating added values and advantages for practical work in a concrete and clear way) with positive examples, success stories, and case studies, as well as references to worst practices and walls of fame and shame to document impacts of poor outcomes. Additionally, development of CSS‐relevant guidelines, standard operating procedures, checklists, and toolboxes, coupled with accessible online demonstrations or trainings, could help elevate CSS and related processes in the conservation community. Ways to communicate practical relevance were discussed somewhat controversially. Some participants favored more proactive transfer of science to practice, whereas others expressed a strong need for bottom‐up, practice‐driven efforts. This involves engaging both sides from the start in, for example, in mutual learning, codevelopment, coproduction, and overlap identification (“it is possible, one just has to find it and do it”), which segues into pathway 2 as an essential element to advance CSS implementation.

Knowledge exchange, professional networks, and fostering community of practice

Knowledge exchange at different levels and interfaces in different contexts and settings was most commonly identified as a key pathway—a crucial cornerstone for bringing the field forward, bridging science‐to‐practice gaps, and improving social science integration in conservation practice. This starts quite simply with creating opportunities and making space and room for direct exchange, for getting together, discussing, brainstorming, talking, learning, and collaborating. It also includes providing safe spaces (for being vulnerable), perhaps informal, small groups, which also help in meeting and getting to know one another on a more personal level. Such initial efforts would foster professional networks by providing access to experts (local, national, international), knowledge of social structures, and local and regional opportunities for engagement with (key) stakeholders and by establishing communication cultures and channels for sharing ideas.

Participants shared numerous ideas for bringing people together (e.g., “start somewhere and let it spread,” “it is a process, step‐by‐step”) that operate at different levels (e.g., scientist peer‐to‐peer, between science and practice, peer‐to‐peer at the workplace, engaging with stakeholders and communities). For example, to facilitate participation in discussions, workshops, or forums, encourage those already involved to share and become multipliers by bringing representatives from science and management together physically. Other ideas included organizing round tables, working groups, work shadowing, and staff rotations and developing participatory processes or adaptive programs and partnerships. Such interactions could provide a supportive network that allows participants to explore new content, skills, and methods and could cultivate an error culture that encourages learning from failures and sharing successes and positive examples.

ACTION AREAS

Building on the pathways identified through engagement with our CSS event participants as a way forward and our own experiences, we concluded there are 4 areas for potential action going forward.

Conservation practitioners everywhere need to be conversant in more subject matter areas than those in which they were originally trained in (e.g., at university). Although the educational landscape for conservation practitioners has been a topic of study in North America for more than 50 years (Hendee & Schoenfeld, 1973), CSS content remains largely absent from conservation‐oriented academic curricula (Dayer & Mengak, 2020; Teel et al., 2022). In addition, few lifelong learning or professional development opportunities in CSS exist at scale sufficient for those already on the job. Both of the shortcomings described above are true to an even greater extent in Europe. Coordination between employers (e.g., agencies, NGOs) and academic programs to better align curricula and degree requirements with minimum position qualifications (e.g., credit hours in specific topics) could go a long way toward meeting conservation workforce needs. Identifying a consistent practical set of core CSS competencies (IUCN, 2015; Teel et al., 2022) for delivery via academic institutions, nonacademic training academies, individual agencies, or boundary organizations would help advance individual and institutional capacity.

Representation of social scientists in the conservation workforce remains low compared with natural scientists, especially in public sector natural resource agencies (Decker & Enck, 1996; Hanna et al., 2009; Jakes et al., 1998; Primo & Brooks, 2022). Morales et al. (2021) report the continuing challenge of the “N of 1” scenario in North America—employing one social scientist position for an entire agency. In Europe, such deliberations are largely absent. For example, interviews with protected area managers in Germany revealed a general lack of workforce capacity; fewer than 20% of sites employed staff with social science or economics backgrounds (Fee et al., 2009). Social science literacy is essential among individuals in leadership positions at public sector agencies and allied organizations to influence decisions concerning workforce balance (e.g., complement of trained social scientists), professional development (e.g., in‐service training), and recruitment and retention through development of career ladders.

Language and culture were specifically identified as potential barriers for CSS integration in Europe, with the EU alone working across 24 official languages (Glikman & Frank, 2011). For example, predominance of German language across Austria, Germany, Liechtenstein, and parts of Belgium, Luxemburg, and Switzerland contributes to an island effect, in which one can train and practice chiefly in German‐speaking circles. In German, as with many other languages, there are no equivalent terms for conservation social science or human dimensions (Johansson et al., 2016). Differences in language and culture present a substantial challenge for conservation practice in general (Amano & Berdejo‐Espinola, 2025; Amano et al., 2016; Chowdhury et al., 2022; Negret et al., 2022), suggesting the need for additional research on the effects of these difference on the adoption and diffusion of CSS in Europe.

A diverse community of professionals involved in HD and CSS has evolved over a number of decades in North America. By comparison, no equivalent community currently exists in Europe. A robust online platform could be used to connect interested parties to provide expertise and consultation on or support for various actions that are often beyond daily workloads and organizational capacities (e.g., knowledge exchange, social science methods, data and tools, training opportunities, stakeholder engagement). The concept of a broadly accessible interface (e.g., clearing house, coordination body) also emerged over the past several years from dialog during our CSS events. Initial discussions have begun regarding development of a community of practice in a collaboration between Germany and other European and transatlantic partners to learn from, leverage, and build on existing examples (e.g., HDgov [https://doi.sciencebase.gov/hd/#/], Conservation Social Science Partnership [https://consosci.org/en‐us/], International Union for Conservation of Nature Species Survival Commission's Human‐Wildlife Conflict and Coexistence Specialist Group [https://www.hwctf.org/], Marine Social Sciences Network [https://www.marsocsci.net/], and IASNR European Chapter and Pathways Europe conferences).

We hope our findings will inspire continued efforts by those working in the CSS space and encourage others to engage at their respective workplaces, through professional networks or other means, to bridge gaps and cultivate opportunities to advance CSS in Europe.
