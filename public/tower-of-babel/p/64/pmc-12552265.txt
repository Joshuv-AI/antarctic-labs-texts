“B” symptoms and CBV conditioning negatively impact survival in mantle cell lymphoma patients undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation
Authors: Karolina Chromik, Agata Wieczorkiewicz-Kabut, Patrycja Zielińska, Anna Koclęga, Sabina Kadłubek, Jakub Kamiński, Grzegorz Helbig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction: Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma with limited curative options. Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) is widely used as consolidation in younger, fit patients. This study aimed to evaluate long-term outcomes after ASCT and identify clinical factors influencing survival. Methods: This retrospective study included 119 patients with MCL who underwent ASCT between 2007 and 2024 in first complete or partial remission. Patient characteristics, outcomes, and transplant-related complications were assessed. Results: At 3 and 5 years, overall survival (OS) rates were 78.3% and 75.2%; progression-free survival (PFS) rates were 76.6% and 73.4%. Median OS and PFS were not reached. The presence of B symptoms at diagnosis and use of CBV (cyclophosphamide, carmustine, and etoposide) conditioning were independently associated with inferior OS and PFS. In contrast, BeEAM (bendamustine, etoposide, cytarabine, melphalan) conditioning was associated with improved outcomes. Treatment-related mortality at day 100 was 3.4%. Post-transplant complications occurred in 64% of patients, most commonly infections and mucositis. Rituximab maintenance was used in only 17% of cases. The median time to relapse was 13 months, and relapse remained the leading cause of treatment failure. Conclusions: ASCT remains a safe and effective consolidation approach in MCL, offering favorable long-term survival. However, the presence of B symptoms and use of CBV conditioning are associated with worse outcomes. These findings underscore the need for risk-adapted treatment strategies and the broader implementation of post-transplant maintenance to enhance disease control.Keywords: Mantle cell lymphoma, Autologous stem cell transplantation, B symptoms, CBV conditioning, BeEAM, Survival, Prognostic factors

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an aggressive and currently incurable subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL). In Western countries, the annual incidence is approximately 0.8 per 100,000 population [1]. The characteristic t(11;14)(q13;q32) translocation leads to overexpression of cyclin D1 (CCND1) and is commonly associated with clonal proliferation of small- to medium-sized atypical B cells [2]. The prognosis remains poor, with a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 5 years [3]. For younger and fit patients (< 65–70 years), first-line therapy includes induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (ASCT) [4]. Some retrospective studies demonstrated a progression-free survival (PFS) benefit with ASCT [5, 6]. The recent TRIANGLE trial has shown that conventional high-dose therapy had similar efficacy to ibrutinib-containing regimen, prompting further evaluation of the role of ASCT in this setting [7].

This report presents the outcomes of 119 MCL patients who received ASCT for remission consolidation.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and methodology

A retrospective analysis was conducted on a cohort of 119 patients. Immunochemical analysis confirmed cyclin D1 overexpression in all patients, in addition to the presence of CD5+, CD10+, CD19+, CD20+, and CD79b + antigens and the absence of CD3-, CD23-, and CD10-. All patients were classified using the Mantle Cell Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (MIPI) [3] and according to the Ann Arbor (AA) stage. The diagnostic workup included a physical examination, a complete blood count with differential, and biochemical tests. Imaging studies, including computed tomography (CT) scans of the neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis, and/or positron emission tomography (PET) scans, were performed at diagnosis and for response assessments. Bone marrow biopsy was performed in all patients at the time of diagnosis and repeated prior to ASCT only in those with initial bone marrow involvement, which accounted for 56% of the entire cohort. In all of these cases, complete remission (CR) in the bone marrow was confirmed before the transplant procedure based on both cytological evaluation and immunophenotypic assessment using flow cytometry. Patients meeting the following criteria were eligible for ASCT: (1) first CR or partial remission (PR), (2) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–2, (3) adequate liver, kidney, and heart function. All patients signed an informed consent form.

Response criteria

CR was defined as resolution of all disease-related symptoms and measurable lesions for at least 4 weeks; PR was defined as >50% reduction in the sum of the two largest diameter products of all measurable lesions for at least 4 weeks. Progressive disease (PD) was defined as any increase >25% in the summed diameter of any measurable lesion or the appearance of a new lesion [8].

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed using the statistical package StatSoft. Inc.(2014) Statistica (data analysis software system) version 12.0. www.statsoft.com and an Excel spreadsheet. Quantitative variables were characterized by the arithmetic mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values (range), and 95% confidence interval (CI).

The Shapiro-Wilk analysis was used to check whether the quantitative variable came from a population with a normal distribution. However, the Levene test (Brown-Forsythe) was used to test the hypothesis of equal variances. A Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess the significance of the differences between the two study groups. The F test (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis was used to determine the significance of differences between more than two groups. In all calculations, the significance level was set at p = 0.05. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate OS and PFS rates. The date of transplantation was used for all calculations. The log-rank test was used to compare variables. Transplant-related mortality (TRM) was defined as death within 100 days of the high-dose regimen that was not related to disease, relapse, or progression.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 2007 and 2024, 119 patients (76 men and 43 women) with a median age of 59 years (range, 48–70) underwent ASCT at our center. MCL treatment was conducted in accordance with the standards of practice at the time. The histological diagnosis of MCL was made by a local pathologist based on the material obtained from lymph nodes (63%), bone marrow (16%), tonsils (7%), intestine (7%), and other sites. Treatment regimens

Induction treatment consisted of R-CHOP (rituximab-cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, prednisone) in 64 patients, R-CHOP/R-Ara-C (cytarabine) in 34, and R-CVAD (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, dexamethasone) in 8, CHOP in 5, and other regimens in 8 patients. CR was achieved in 86 patients (72%), and PR in the remaining patients.

Ara-C was most frequently used for stem cell mobilization (n = 76; 64%), followed by IVE regimen (ifosfamide, etoposide, epirubicin) (n = 17; 14%), R-DHAP (rituximab, cisplatin, dexamethasone, and high-dose cytarabine) (n = 8; 7%), and R-ESHAP (rituximab, etoposide, methylprednisolone, high-dose cytarabine, and cisplatin) (n = 4; 3%). In the remaining 14 cases (12%), G-CSF was used alone or in combination with plerixafor. Stem cells were successfully mobilized in all patients.

The following preparative regimens were used: total body irradiation (TBI, total dose 12 Gy) with cyclophosphamide (TBI + Ctx; n = 36; 30%), carmustine, etoposide, cytarabine and melphalan (BEAM; n = 32; 27%), bendamustine EAM, (BeEAM; n = 33; 28%), cyclophosphamide, carmustine and etoposide (CBV; n = 12; 10%) and other (n = 6; 5%). All patients received stem cells from peripheral blood.

Cell dose and engraftment

All patients were transplanted. The median number of transplanted CD34 + cells/kg b.w. was 4.97 × 106(range 1.83–35 × 106). The median time to absolute neutrophil count (ANC) > 0.5 × 109/L was 10 days (range: 8–19), and the median time to platelet count (PLT) > 20 × 109/L was 12 days (range: 7–21). The median hospital stay after transplantation was 27 days (range 14–39 days).

Outcome measures and prognostic factors

The TRM on day 100 post ASCT was 3.4%. The estimated OS rates at 3 and 5 years were 78.3% (95% CI, 69.6–84.8) and 75.2% (95% CI, 66.1–82.2; Fig. 1), respectively, while the corresponding PFS rates were 76.6% (95% CI, 67.8–83.3) and 73.4% (95% CI, 64.2–80.7; Fig. 2). Median OS and PFS were not reached. Within 3 and 5 years, 25 and 28 patients died, representing 21% and 23.5% of the population, respectively. Events related to disease progression or death were reported in 27 patients (22.7% of the population) within 3 years and in 30 patients (25.2% of the population) within 5 years. There was no significant difference in OS and PFS with respect to age, Ann Arbor staging, MIPI score, beta2 microglobulin level (B2M), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity, and disease status at transplantation.

Median follow-up from diagnosis and ASCT was 70.8 months (range 6.7–191) and 50.8 months (range 0.13–182), respectively. The following complications of ASCT occurred in 76 patients (64%): gastrointestinal symptoms (n = 19; 16%), grade III and IV mucositis (n = 18; 16%), bacterial pneumonia (n = 11; 9%), septic shock (n = 10; 8%), urinary tract infections (n = 7; 6%) and bacteremia (n = 11; 9%): Streptococcus pneumoniae (3), Staphylococcus epidermidis (2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (2), Enterococcus cloacae (1), Escherichia coli (1), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (1), Enterococcus faecalis (1). Up to 30 days after transplantation, 4 patients died. Rituximab was administered as a maintenance therapy after ASCT in 20 patients (17%). The median time to relapse was 13 months (range 7–62). Of the 51 patients who died, 4 died from post-transplant complications due to septic shock, 2 died as a result of severe infections during anti-CD20 maintenance therapy, and the remaining patients relapsed.

None of the patients included in this cohort received CAR-T cell therapy, as this treatment was not widely available during the study period. Among patients who experienced relapse and were eligible for second-line salvage therapy (n = 37), various regimens were administered, including rituximab plus bendamustine (RB) in 14 patients (38%), rituximab combined with cisplatin, cytarabine, and dexamethasone (RDHAP) in 11 patients (29%), rituximab with fludarabine and cyclophosphamide (RFC) in 7 patients (19%), and BTK inhibitors in 5 patients (14%). Data on disease progression and mortality were systematically collected; however, detailed information regarding responses to salvage therapy and long-term outcomes remains unavailable, as remission assessment following salvage treatment and subsequent follow-up were conducted independently at the patients’ primary hematology centers.

One patient received allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) from a related donor after the first ASCT and is still alive.

Discussion

Our study reinforces the role of ASCT as an effective consolidation strategy in MCL, demonstrating survival outcomes that are comparable to, or modestly superior to, those reported in contemporary multicenter cohorts. Importantly, our findings highlight that the presence of B symptoms at diagnosis and the use of CBV-based conditioning regimens are independently associated with inferior survival, underscoring the need for personalized risk-adapted approaches in this setting.

In our cohort, the 5-year OS and PFS rates were 75.2% and 73.4%, respectively. These outcomes align with those reported by Metzner et al. [9], who observed a 5-year OS of 70% following high-dose chemotherapy and ASCT, and are more favorable than those observed by Mathys et al. [10], particularly in patients with high-risk disease features. These differences may reflect variations in patient selection, disease biology, and supportive care practices.

Population-level data from Maranzano et al. [11], derived from the California Cancer Registry, demonstrated a survival benefit with ASCT in real-world settings but reported somewhat lower 5-year OS rates compared to our findings. This disparity may be attributable to differences in demographic and clinical characteristics, conditioning regimens, transplant timing, or the use of maintenance therapies. Interestingly, commonly utilized prognostic markers— including age, MIPI score, beta-2 microglobulin (B2M), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and disease status at transplantation —did not predict outcomes in our ASCT-treated cohort.

Notably, in our analysis, the assessment of treatment response prior to ASCT - CR versus PR - did not demonstrate significant prognostic value for overall survival or progression-free survival. This observation may be attributed to several factors. First, the heterogeneity of response evaluation methods employed across different centers and time periods, particularly regarding imaging modalities and remission criteria, likely introduced variability that limited interpretability. Second, the relatively small number of patients in the PR group may have reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful differences. Additionally, it is plausible that ASCT itself contributed to further clinical improvement in patients who entered the procedure in PR, thereby diminishing outcome disparities between the CR and PR groups. These findings underscore the need for future prospective studies involving larger patient cohorts and standardized response assessment protocols to more accurately evaluate the prognostic impact of pre-transplant remission status.

The lack of significant prognostic value observed in our study for the MIPI score contrasts with the findings reported by Hoster et al. [12, 13], who validated MIPI as a strong and reliable prognostic tool in treatment-naïve patients across large, prospective clinical trials. This difference is likely due to the inherently selective nature of patients who proceed to ASCT — typically younger individuals with preserved performance status and well-controlled disease — which may limit the ability of baseline prognostic models to discriminate in this context. These findings suggest that prognostic tools developed at diagnosis may have limited usefulness in post-transplant populations, highlighting the need for their adaptation or revalidation specifically within this clinical setting.

Conversely, B symptoms at diagnosis emerged as a strong adverse prognostic factor for both OS and PFS. These symptoms- fever, night sweats, and weight loss- are indicative of systemic inflammation and have been associated with higher tumor burden, elevated proliferation indices, and more advanced disease. Biologically, the inflammatory milieu associated with B symptoms may facilitate tumor progression through cytokine-driven modulation of the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing lymphoma cell survival and dissemination. Our findings reinforce prior reports and support the use of B symptoms as a clinically accessible surrogate for aggressive disease biology in MCL [14, 15].

The choice of conditioning regimen significantly influenced survival outcomes. In our analysis, CBV-based regimens were associated with increased mortality, whereas BeEAM-based regimens demonstrated comparable efficacy with a more favorable toxicity profile. These findings are consistent with meta-analytic data by Wu et al. and toxicity analyses by Carranza et al., which support BeEAM as a less toxic yet effective alternative to traditional CBV or BEAM regimens. BeEAM may be particularly advantageous for older patients or those with comorbidities, where treatment-related toxicity is a major concern [16, 17].

The selection of conditioning regimen was based on prevailing clinical guidelines and institutional practices at the time of treatment. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the extended inclusion period, these approaches varied over time and were evaluated retrospectively. Due to variability in treatment protocols and the retrospective design, data on cumulative cytarabine dosing were not consistently documented and, therefore, could not be incorporated into the outcome analysis. This represents a potential limitation and will be addressed in future prospective evaluations.

Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 64% of patients, predominantly low-grade (CTCAE grade 1–2) and manageable with standard supportive care measures, reflecting an acceptable toxicity profile. However, six infection-related deaths were reported- four from early post-transplant septic shock and two during rituximab maintenance therapy, highlighting the critical importance of rigorous infectious prophylaxis and vigilant monitoring in this high-risk population. The 100-day treatment-related mortality (TRM) rate in our cohort was 3.4%, closely mirroring the 2.5% reported in the California Cancer Registry among 652 ASCT recipients [11]. This low TRM underscores the feasibility and safety of ASCT in appropriately selected patients and reflects advances in supportive care and patient selection.

Despite favorable 5-year survival rates, disease relapse remains the leading cause of treatment failure, with a median time to relapse of just 13 months. Notably, only 17% of patients in our cohort received rituximab maintenance therapy, despite available data confirming its role in prolonging remission duration. This observation aligns with findings by Gerson et al., who demonstrated improved survival outcomes in younger patients with mantle cell lymphoma treated in the rituximab era, including the use of maintenance strategies [5]. The low proportion of patients receiving rituximab maintenance reflects the extended duration of the study period, during which this approach had not yet become standard practice or was not widely accessible. The underutilization of maintenance therapy may have contributed to the observed relapse rates and highlights an area for quality improvement. Emerging data support the incorporation of targeted agents, such as Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, in the maintenance setting, particularly for patients with high-risk features or early relapse [18–20].

These findings should be interpreted within the context of a rapidly evolving treatment landscape for MCL. The recent results of the TRIANGLE trial have challenged the role of upfront ASCT, particularly in younger, fit patients receiving intensive chemoimmunotherapy followed by maintenance with BTK inhibitors. Furthermore, the growing use of novel targeted therapies—including BTK inhibitors, BCL2 inhibitors, and CAR-T cell therapy—is reshaping treatment algorithms, especially in the relapsed/refractory setting.

While our study supports the continued effectiveness of ASCT in selected patients, future treatment strategies are likely to become increasingly individualized, incorporating molecular risk profiling and novel agents to optimize long-term outcomes.

In our cohort, none of the analyzed patients received CAR-T cell therapy, as this option was not available during most of the study period. However, five patients were treated with BTK inhibitors in the salvage setting, i.e., in second or subsequent lines of therapy.

Due to the extended retrospective timeframe of the study (2007–2024), biological data such as TP53 mutation status, Ki-67 proliferation index, and morphological subtype (blastoid/pleomorphic) were available only for a subset of patients. These parameters were not routinely assessed in earlier years, and pathology reports were issued by different laboratories, limiting consistency. Consequently, these variables could not be included in the statistical analysis, which represents a limitation of our study.

In conclusion, our study affirms the effectiveness and safety of ASCT as a consolidation approach in MCL. However, it also identifies critical prognostic variables — including B symptoms and conditioning regimen choice — that significantly influence outcomes. These findings underscore the need for individualized treatment strategies that incorporate clinical, biological, and molecular risk factors. Future research should prioritize refining prognostic models for post-ASCT populations, integrating molecular profiling (e.g., TP53, KMT2D mutations), and optimizing maintenance strategies with novel agents to reduce relapse risk and enhance long-term disease control.
