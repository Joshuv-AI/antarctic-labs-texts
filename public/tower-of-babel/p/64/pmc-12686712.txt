Resurgence of epidemics in Zinder: effect of the decrease in vaccination coverage and the impacts of climate change
Authors: Doutchi Mahamadou, Adamou Bara Abdoul-Aziz, Lamine Mahaman Moustapha, Ibrahim Alkassoum, Souleymane Adoum Fils, Bagnou Hamsatou, Goni Bachir, Yacouba Abdourahmane, Manzo Farouk, Adamou Lagare, Adehossi Eric, Djibo Issifou, Nasser Hassane, Idé Habibatou, Serge Paul Eholié
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The Zinder region has been facing a resurgence of epidemic phenomena in recent years.•These epidemics are linked to a decline in vaccination coverage and climate change.•The decline in vaccination coverage is due to insecurity, population migration, and the impact of COVID-19.•Climate change has led to the outbreak of cholera through flooding.Keywords: Epidemics, Climatic variation, Armed conflicts, Vaccination, Republic of Niger

Introduction

Vaccination represents one of the most effective pillars in the fight against preventable diseases. Since the introduction of the Expanded Immunization Program (EIP) by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1974, remarkable progress has been recorded in reducing the incidence of several infectious diseases, especially measles and diphtheria. These diseases, once endemic in many regions, can now be controlled or even eradicated thanks to effective and available vaccines [1,2]. WHO recommends a vaccination coverage of at least 95% for measles to ensure collective immunity and 90% for diphtheria [3].

However, despite the sustained efforts of states and partners, epidemics continue to occur in several countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, highlighting the persistent challenges in equitable access, the quality of vaccination services, community awareness, and the reliability of reported coverage data. These epidemics often occur in contexts of structural vulnerability, political instability, massive population displacements, distrust in the health system, or even low epidemiologic surveillance capacity [4].

Niger, a landlocked Sahelian country, is one of the priority countries for the Global Vaccination Initiative. It has integrated the EIP into its national health policy since the 1980s, with the support of partners such as United Nations Children’s Fund, GAVI, WHO, and the Global Alliance for Vaccines. Administrative data from 2023 report a vaccination coverage of 100% for the first dose of the measles vaccine (Var 1) and 101% for the third dose of the pentavalent vaccine (Penta3) nationwide. These vaccination covers are theoretically sufficient to prevent community transmission of the disease [3,5].

However, since 2018, Niger has been facing a worrying resurgence of measles outbreaks, as well as the re-emergence of cases of diphtheria. In 2023, the country notified 2337 cases, with significant outbreaks in the regions of Zinder, Maradi, Tahoua, and Agadez. Several deaths have been recorded, particularly, among children under the age of 5 years [6]. At the same time, cases of diphtheria have been confirmed, especially in the border areas with Nigeria and sites hosting displaced populations. These cases of diphtheria have been confirmed in all regions of the country with the exception of Dosso [5,7]. These epidemics are mainly concentrated in the Zinder region. Apart from these EIP target diseases, the Zinder region has been confronted with epidemics of meningitis, cholera, and COVID-19 [[8], [9], [10]].

By identifying the factors associated with these outbreaks, the results will help guide strategies to strengthen the EIP, improve surveillance, adapt interventions to local specificities, and support decision-makers in the implementation of more effective and sustainable vaccination policies.

Methodology

Type and approach used

This paper is a documentary review of the epidemics of meningitis, measles, cholera, COVID-19, and diphtheria, which occurred in the Zinder region from 2015 to 2023, as well as their determinants. We have exploited the data of the gray and white literature available covering this period. These were initially used: the linear list of notifiable diseases of the regional directorate of health and public hygiene of Zinder, documents on the decline in vaccination coverage, and the impact of insecurity, migration, and climate change on the health system. Then, several online data sources were used, such as PubMed, WHO, Ministry of Health and Public Hygiene, Regional Directorate of Health and Public Hygiene, and the Regional Council of Zinder.

A two-stage work was carried out

The first step: identification of epidemics recorded during the study period from the linear list of notifiable diseases of the region. Research of the factors that can explain the appearance of an epidemic and determine the associated factors.

The second step: interpretation of these documents through a critical reading confronted with the data of the literature. A systematic literature review methodology was adopted.

Eligibility criteria

Articles, activity reports, and documents on the decline in vaccination coverage and the impact of insecurity, migration, and climate change on the health system have been included.

Search strategy

The search was based on keywords related to the causes of the appearance of epidemics. These keywords are the following: “vaccination coverage” [MeSH], “insecurity and access to care” [MeSH], “consequence of migration on health” [MeSH], “climate change and health” [MeSH]. Google sites; Google scholar; PubMed; WHO.int; and documents and activity reports of the Ministry of Public Health of Niger, the Regional Directorate of Public Health, and the Regional Council of Zinder were consulted.

A total of 10,327 articles were identified through the search. After eliminating duplicates, 9327 articles were examined by title and abstract. Of these, 9227 articles were eliminated because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. After a careful examination of the full-text articles, 91 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria described previously. The systematic review (Table 1) finally focused on nine articles. Figure 1 shows the selection process of the nine identified articles.

Results

The analysis was performed using a linear list of notifiable diseases (unpublished document), nine articles, one activity report, two documents, and four web pages.

Meningitis

During the study period, meningitis epidemics were notified respectively in 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024, with a total of 5019 notified cases. During these epidemics, 317 deaths were recorded (a lethality rate of 6.31%). The 5-14 years age group was the most affected (Supplementary Table 1). The frequency peak is observed in 2023 (1658 of 5019 cases).

Measles

Measles is the most common disease, with five reported epidemics (2015, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022) at the time of the study. During these epidemics, 13,887 cases were declared, including 42 deaths (a lethality rate of 0.30%). The age group of 1-4 years was the most represented (Supplementary Table 1). The frequency peak was observed in 2022 (5553 of 13,887 cases).

Cholera

From 2015 to 2020, the Zinder region was spared from cholera. An epidemic appeared in 2021, with 609 cases including 21 deaths (a lethality of 3.44%). In 2022, 31 cases were registered, with one death. Then in 2024, 244 cases were recorded, with two deaths. The 15 years and over age group predominated during these two epidemics (Supplementary Table 1).

COVID-19

A total of 364 cases of COVID-19 has been confirmed, with 17 deaths, representing a lethality rate of 4.67%. Most of the cases were registered in 2020 and in 2021, with 166 and 162 cases, respectively. The age group of 15 years and older was mostly affected (Supplementary Table 1).

Diphtheria

In 2022, diphtheria reemerges in the Zinder region with a high incidence. A total of 678 cases were recorded in the same year, with 36 deaths. In 2023, 897 cases were declared, with 47 deaths. In 2024, 1735 cases were registered, with 90 deaths. The 5-14 years age range was predominant (Supplementary Table 1).

Decrease in vaccination coverage

In Niger, 93% of health facilities offer routine vaccination services. The number of zero-dose children is 92,386 from 2019 to 2022 throughout the country (Supplementary Figure 1). The Zinder region is strongly affected by this problem of vaccination, on the one hand, by its health coverage of 44%, which is the lowest in the country, and, on the other hand, by the phenomenon of migration especially in the district of Gouré, which welcomes refugees from the Diffa region and Nigeria after the armed Boko Haram conflict and the District of Kantché [11].

Insecurity

Insecurity strongly affects the health system in the Zinder region, mainly, in the department of Gouré, which receives thousands of refugees from the armed conflict of Boko Haram [12]. This problem of insecurity negatively impacts health care by limiting access to care in frontline health centers in war-torn areas. Indeed, the multiplication of terrorist attacks has led to a reduction in access to health services [13,14]. Many health centers are closed, infrastructures are destroyed, and some workers have fled abandoning the health structures. Insecurity impacts the supply of health centers with inputs of all kinds, which is how shortages of vaccine antigens and essential medicines are observed in conflict zones [15]. The displacement of these populations fleeing insecurity leads to a high demand for care in the reception areas, causing an imbalance between supply and demand [16]. Children living in refugee camps have limited access to immunization services [17]. This has further reduced the vaccination coverage in this area, thus leading to the appearance of epidemics such as diphtheria. The state and its partners have carried out an immunization campaign in response to the diphtheria epidemic in the district of Gouré [15].

Migration

The Zinder region is strongly affected by structural and cyclical poverty, with a poverty index of 52% [18,19]. Kantché is the department of the region that has the greatest population density (168 inhabitants per km2). The main economic activities of the area, particularly, agriculture and livestock, are negatively impacted by demographic pressure and climatic hazards with the consequences of a shortage of foodstuffs. These problems have further increased the migration phenomenon, which is mainly carried out by women in 80% of cases and generally accompanied by children aged between 5 and 10 years. The interest of taking children with them during migration is to appeal to alms lovers. Because of this, women borrow children from poorer families as part of this migration process. These women who are accompanied by these children migrate to Nigeria, Algeria, Libya, and other West African capitals (Abidjan, Accra, etc.). The children spend months or even years without access to vaccination services. In addition, Kantché receives migrants from the Districts of Tanout, Gouré, and Mirriah. These migrants also engage in begging around the district area. This migration phenomenon concerns all the southern districts of the region (Mirriah, Magaria, Dungass) for the same reasons [20].

The impact of COVID-19

The COVID-19 pandemic has impacted countries with limited resources. In Niger, the crisis has highlighted major technical, structural, and organizational deficiencies. Similar to the other regions of the country, Zinder welcomed the pandemic in a total unpreparedness [9]. The impact of the pandemic on the health system is huge. Difficulties in the supply of vaccine inputs due to the closure of borders and the refusal of the population to attend health facilities for fear of the disease or its vaccine are all factors in the decline of vaccination coverage [21].

Climate change

Niger, similar to other Sahelian countries, is experiencing the adverse effects of climate change such as droughts and floods. These floods result from the heavy rainfall that has occurred in recent years, with flash floods and local bad weather, especially along the riverbanks. In 2022, the heavy rainfall recorded led to floods with disastrous consequences; 365,438 people were affected throughout the country. The Zinder region is one of the regions most affected by these floods [22,23]. The floods have resulted in unplanned urbanization and limited access to drinking water and basic sanitation services, constituting risk factors for cholera epidemics [24,25]. Climate change has also led to the appearance of the first cases of dengue fever in the region [26].

Discussion

It was observed in the Zinder region between 2015 and 2024 that there were four meningitis epidemics, five measles epidemics, three diphtheria epidemics, three cholera epidemics, and three COVID-19 epidemics. Several factors explain the occurrence of these epidemics.

The decline in vaccination coverage

Vaccination is an important means for the prevention of many infectious diseases [27]. Despite the high vaccination coverage reported in Niger and Zinder, there is still a significant number of children who have not received any vaccine antigen [11,28]. This presence of zero-dose children or those who have not received any vaccine antigen is multifactorial. The migration phenomenon in Zinder is complex, with disastrous consequences, particularly, on the health system.

This migration, whether internal or external, is mainly carried out by women accompanied by children, as reported by other authors [20]. These women are engaged in begging in the border countries (Nigeria, Algeria, etc.) and other West African capitals (Abidjan, Acra, Dakar, etc.) and deprive the children who accompany them of vaccination. They are returning to the region after months or years of exile, bringing with them children who have grown older, and, therefore, out of the target of the expanded vaccination program. The decrease in vaccination coverage leads to an increase in vaccine-preventable diseases and the appearance of epidemics among migrants and the local population [29].

Insecurity and its corollaries impact the offer of care and access to the vaccination service. The Zinder region partly borders the Diffa region, where the armed conflict of the Boko Haram sect is raging. The Zinder region, which already has the weakest health coverage in the country, has welcomed refugees from the Diffa region after the armed conflict [11,30]. The massive displacement of populations fleeing conflict zones leads to overstrain of the health system in the reception area [31]. This could explain the high prevalence of diphtheria in this area [32].

The decrease in vaccination coverage after the phenomena described previously is mainly observed in the health district of Gouré and Kantché [32,33]. This decrease in vaccination coverage would explain the measles and diphtheria epidemics observed [1,2]. Another element explaining the observed decrease in vaccination coverage is the COVID-19 pandemic; in fact, the COVID-19 pandemic had strongly impacted the health care system by the population’s fear of attending health centers in general and by the limitation of the delivery of vaccines in our health structures [34,35]. COVID-19 had caused an interruption of the surveillance system for epidemic diseases, particularly, measles and meningitis, leading to the appearance of epidemics [9,36].

Other phenomena can be highlighted in this resurgence of epidemics in the Zinder region, particularly, climate change [37]. Thermal variations, droughts, and floods would lead to the occurrence and maintenance of epidemics, particularly, meningitis and cholera [38]. The Zinder region is facing climate change in all its forms with consequences for human health, particularly, the resurgence of cholera and meningitis epidemics [39]. Lapeyssonnie has already mentioned the role of drought in the occurrence of meningitis epidemics in the meningitis belt area [40]. N'krumah in Ivory Coast and Sultan made the same observation by highlighting the correlation between climate change and the occurrence of cerebrospinal meningitis epidemics [41,42]. Located in the heart of this belt, the Zinder region and Niger in general are facing an upsurge in meningitis epidemics [43]. In addition, studies have determined the impact of climate change on the occurrence of cholera. The increase in temperature and flooding favor the spread of the disease [44,45].

Study limitations

This study has some limitations that should be emphasized. First of all, it is partly based on surveillance data from notifiable diseases, which may be subject to underreporting or reporting errors, especially in areas with low health coverage or affected by insecurity. In addition, the analysis of the determinants is based on a narrative review of the literature and institutional documents, which implies a risk of selection bias and does not allow causal relationships to be established. The absence of multivariate statistical analyses also limits the ability to quantify the relative weight of each identified factor. Finally, some contextual data, particularly, on vaccination coverage at the integrated health center level or the precise quantitative impact of climatic phenomena, were not available, which restricts the depth of interpretation. Despite these limitations, this study provides a useful summary to guide research and public health priorities in the Zinder region.

Conclusion

The impact of epidemics on the health of the population and the socio-economic development of the region implies a greater mastery of the root causes mentioned in this study. A more in-depth analytical study should make it possible to identify the determinants of these epidemics and propose appropriate solutions.

Improving vaccination coverage, especially in areas hosting displaced people, sanitation and environmental control, and an early warning system for climate events could be a solution to these epidemics.
