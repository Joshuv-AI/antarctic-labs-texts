An Indirect Treatment Comparison of Avalglucosidase Alfa versus Cipaglucosidase Alfa Plus Miglustat in Patients with Late-Onset Pompe Disease
Authors: Mark E Roberts, Irina Proskorovsky, Patricia Guyot, Pragya Shukla, Nathan Thibault, Alaa Hamed, Ruth Pulikottil-Jacob, Lasair O’Callaghan, Laurence Pollissard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNo head-to-head studies comparing the efficacy of avalglucosidase alfa (AVA) with cipaglucosidase alfa + miglustat (Cipa+mig) have been conducted in patients with late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD). Two indirect treatment comparisons (ITCs) were conducted to estimate the effects of AVA versus Cipa+mig.MethodsITCs were conducted using simulated treatment comparisons (STCs), adjusting for differences in prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers. An analysis of patients who were naïve to enzyme replacement therapy (ERT-naive) used anchored STC with individual patient data (IPD) from the Phase 3 COMET (NCT02782741) study of AVA versus alglucosidase alfa (ALG) and aggregate data from patients who were ERT-naïve in the Phase 3 PROPEL (NCT03729362) study of Cipa+mig versus ALG + placebo. For patients who were ERT-experienced, an analysis used unanchored STC with IPD for AVA from the COMET open-label extension, and from NEO-1 (NCT01898364)/NEO-EXT (NCT02032524) studies, and aggregate Cipa+mig data from PROPEL and ATB200-02 (NCT02675465).ResultsIn patients who were ERT-naïve, the difference (95% confidence interval, CI) in mean change from baseline (CFB) at Weeks 49–52 in forced vital capacity percent predicted (FVCpp) showed a numerical improvement for AVA versus Cipa+mig with values of 5.49% (– 0.87, 11.86) with mixed model repeated measures analysis (MMRM/MMRM) and 4.69% (− 3.22, 12.61) with MMRM/analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). For the 6-min walk test (6MWT), differences were 57.08 m (11.04, 103.12) with MMRM/MMRM and 41.88 m (− 5.46, 89.22) with MMRM/ANCOVA, the former being statistically significant (p < 0.02) and the latter numerically in favour of AVA. In patients who were ERT-experienced, at Weeks 48–52 differences for AVA versus Cipa+mig with MMRM/MMRM (CIs/p values unavailable) were 1.40% for FVCpp and 18.85 m for 6MWT; with MMRM/Mean CFB, differences of 1.16% (− 1.88, 4.19) for FVCpp and 7.67 m (− 21.67, 37.02) for 6MWT, indicating a numerical improvement in favour of AVA.ConclusionsITCs suggest more favourable respiratory and mobility outcomes with AVA versus Cipa+mig in patients with LOPD, regardless of prior ERT-experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03301-9.Keywords: Avalglucosidase alfa, Cipaglucosidase alfa, Late-onset Pompe disease, Indirect treatment comparison, Efficacy, Enzyme replacement therapy

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has been the mainstay of treatment for Pompe disease for more than a decade and, while new ERTs have recently become available, to date there have been no head-to-head studies comparing their efficacy and safety.This indirect treatment comparison (ITC) was conducted using data from clinical trials to estimate the comparative treatment effects of avalglucosidase alfa (AVA) and cipaglucosidase alfa in combination with miglustat (Cipa+mig) on key respiratory and motor function outcomes in patients with late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) in order to inform clinical decision-making.What was learned from this study?The results of this ITC, conducted using robust methodology (anchored and unanchored simulated treatment comparisons), suggest that AVA has a numerically more favourable effect on respiratory function (forced vital capacity, maximal inspiratory pressure, and maximal expiratory pressure) and mobility [6-minute walk test (6MWT)] than Cipa+mig, in both patients who were ERT-naïve or ERT-experienced.This was demonstrated in both statistical analyses conducted, with the difference in 6MWT [mixed model repeated measures (MMRM) AVA vs. MMRM Cipa+mig) reaching statistical significance in favour of AVA in patients who were ERT-naïve.

Introduction

Pompe disease, also known as glycogen storage disease type II, is a rare, progressive, debilitating neuromuscular disorder [1, 2]. It is caused by a deficiency of acid α-glucosidase (GAA), a lysosomal enzyme responsible for glycogen degradation within cells [3]. The resultant enzymatic deficiency causes glycogen accumulation within muscle cells, predominantly in skeletal and cardiac muscle [4]. Intracellular glycogen build-up precipitates irreversible muscle damage, leading to the debilitating features of Pompe disease, including limb-girdle muscle weakness, motor function impairment, and respiratory insufficiency [5, 6].

Pompe disease can be broadly categorised into two phenotypes: infantile-onset Pompe disease and late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) [7]. LOPD may present at any time during childhood through to adulthood. Patients typically present with progressive muscle weakness, leading to difficulties in performing activities of daily living, reduced mobility, and, in severe cases, necessitating wheelchair use. LOPD is associated with early mortality, often due to respiratory failure [8–10].

Since 2006, enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has been the mainstay of treatment for Pompe disease following approval of recombinant human acid α-glucosidase (alglucosidase alfa [ALG]) [11, 12]. Although ALG initially improves motor and respiratory function in the majority of patients with LOPD [13], some patients may experience a plateau or decline in health after 3–5 years of ALG therapy [14]. Avalglucosidase alfa (AVA), a next-generation ERT approved in the United States and Europe in 2021 and 2022, respectively, was designed to increase GAA enzyme uptake in cells, improving on the efficacy of ALG [15–18]. The efficacy and safety of AVA were compared with that of ALG in the randomised, double-blind, Phase 3 COMET trial (NCT02782741) in patients aged ≥ 3 years with treatment-naïve enzymatically confirmed GAA deficiency from any tissue source and/or two confirmed pathogenic GAA variants [15]. Treatment with AVA exhibited a more favourable safety profile than AVA over the 49-week double-blind treatment period [15]. AVA also demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements relative to ALG in forced vital capacity percent predicted (FVCpp [between group difference: 2.43% (95% CI –0.13, 4.99)] and 6-min walk test (6MWT) [between group difference: 30.01m (95% CI 1.33, 58.69)] [19], where the between-group clinically meaningful threshold (CMT) for improvement in FVC was defined as 2.1% predicted (range: 1.1–3.1) [19]; and a range of 0.35–7.47% predicted for 6MWT (equivalent to 2.18–46.61 m and 1.97–42.13 m, respectively, a man or a woman age 50 years, height 1.75 m and weight 80 kg) [20]. Another ERT, cipaglucosidase alfa, which is administered in conjunction with miglustat (Cipa+mig), was approved in Europe and the United States in 2023 for the treatment of adults with LOPD [21–23], based on data from the randomised, double-blind, Phase 3 PROPEL trial (NCT03729362), which assessed the safety and efficacy of Cipa+mig compared with ALG plus placebo in patients with LOPD (aged ≥ 18 years) who were either ERT-naïve or had received ALG for ≥ 2 years [24]. The combination of Cipa+mig failed to achieve statistical superiority over ALG for the primary endpoint of change from baseline in 6MWT at Week 52. However, Cipa+mig seemed to show clinically meaningful improvements in motor and respiratory function outcomes after 52 weeks of treatment in the sub-population of patients who were ERT-experienced [24].

Evidence on the comparative efficacy of treatments is key in assisting clinicians in selecting an optimal treatment regimen [25]. However, no direct comparative study has been conducted to evaluate the efficacy of AVA versus Cipa+mig. The indirect treatment comparison (ITC) approach can be used to obtain estimates of the comparative efficacy and safety of treatment regimens using data from different trials [26]. Population-adjusted ITCs such as simulated treatment comparisons (STCs) use individual patient data (IPD) from index trials and published trial data for comparator agents to adjust for differences between trials in the distribution of variables that may influence outcomes, thus providing a valid estimate of comparative treatment effects in the adjusted population. ITC is a widely accepted approach in health technology assessment reimbursement submissions and is often relied upon when comparative clinical trials of available treatment options are not feasible [25, 27, 28].

The objective of this study was to conduct ITCs of AVA versus Cipa+mig using data from clinical trials in patients with LOPD who were naïve to ERT and those with previous ERT treatment to provide information on the comparative efficacy of these two therapies in these different populations. The main outcomes of interest were FVCpp and 6MWT, which were key endpoints in both trials [15, 24] and are used in clinical practice to assess the impact of Pompe disease on the respiratory and muscular systems.

Methods

Study Overview

ITCs were conducted separately for patients who were (1) ERT-naïve and (2) ERT-experienced with LOPD using data from comparative and single-arm clinical trials of AVA and Cipa+mig to obtain population-adjusted estimates of comparative efficacy. The use of a common comparator (ALG) in the studies of patients who were treatment-naïve enabled use of an anchored ITC approach to preserve randomisation and limit the effect of any study-level differences that might affect the outcomes. For the ERT-experienced population, the information available for AVA comprised a single-arm dataset from the COMET open-label extension. Given the absence of a common comparator with Cipa+mig studies to serve as an anchor in the ERT-experienced data network, an unanchored ITC approach was employed for this population. Anchored and unanchored simulated treatment comparisons (STCs) were conducted [29], with adjustment for differences between studies in prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers.

Evidence Base and Data Extraction

Publications on clinical studies investigating the efficacy of AVA and Cipa+mig in the treatment of LOPD used in this analysis were identified from a systematic literature review (SLR) conducted in 2021. Additional details are included in the Supplementary Material. Key characteristics of the studies used in the ITCs are summarised in Table 1.

Ethical Approval

The study protocols for the studies included in the ITC were reviewed and approved by appropriate ethics committees and/or institutional review boards and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was obtained from participants.

Outcomes

Outcomes were assessed at 49 weeks (COMET) or 52 weeks (PROPEL) with the assumption that, at most, a 3-week difference in the timepoints would not affect the results. The outcomes included absolute change from baseline in FVCpp, maximal inspiratory pressure percent predicted (MIPpp), maximal expiratory pressure percent predicted (MEPpp), and 6MWT. In the COMET and PROPEL trials, these outcomes were assessed as either primary or secondary endpoints (Table 1). In NEO-1/NEO-EXT, FVCpp, 6MWT, MIPpp, and MEPpp were assessed as exploratory efficacy endpoints. In ATB200-02, FVCpp and 6MWT were assessed as secondary endpoints but MIPpp and MEPpp were not reported. Comparative effectiveness estimates were derived at the timepoints, as shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Identification of Prognostic Variables and Effect Modifiers

STCs are population-adjusted ITCs that adjust for the differences in prognostic factors and effect modifiers through regression analyses [29]. Effect modifiers and prognostic variables were identified based on prior published studies, and clinical feedback [34–37]. Mean baseline age, sex, baseline values of each outcome, and ERT duration of treatment (for the ERT-experienced population only) were selected as effect modifiers for adjustment in the STC analyses. In addition, region (America vs. other) was included as a prognostic variable only. All explanatory variables were centred to the average baseline values from the PROPEL (or ATB200-02) studies to enable predictions in a similar population.

Statistical Analyses

As the STC method accounts for differences in prognostic factors and effect modifiers between the included studies, and some inclusion/exclusion criteria, the compatibility of the studies must also be assessed on study design, including eligibility criteria, on definition of the outcomes of interest, and on availability of similarly defined baseline data in known characteristics that influence the outcomes to include in the population-adjustment (Table 1). Analyses were conducted separately for ERT-naïve and ERT-experienced populations. The assumptions required for each of the planned ITC comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Since the results from COMET were analysed by MMRM and various results from PROPEL were analysed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), mean CFB and/or MMRM, three separate comparisons (hereafter referred to as MMRM/ANCOVA, MMRM/Mean CFB and MMRM/MMRM) between AVA and Cipa+mig are reported for the relevant ERT population, as detailed below. Although it would be preferable to compare MRMM analyses for both datasets, this was not possible for all outcomes and timepoints: CFB in FVCpp and 6MWT at week 52 were the only outcomes reported using MMRM. The data for PROPEL were taken from peer-reviewed publications (ANCOVA/Mean CFB) [24, 30, 31] and the Pombiliti Summary of Product Characteristics (MMRM) [23]. The MMRM data for avalglucosidase alfa were derived using IPD, as described further below.

The statistical models used for estimation of treatment effects and details of handling of missing data in COMET and PROPEL studies are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Statistical significance for all outcomes was set at the level of 0.05. Estimated differences in CFB in each of the outcome measures in the ERT-naïve and ERT-experienced populations were considered to be statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include 0.

ERT-Naïve Population

MMRM/ANCOVA Analysis

For the ERT-naïve population, anchored STC analyses were conducted. Anchored STC was selected as the optimal approach since, unlike subgroup network meta-analyses (NMAs), they can adjust for several effect modifiers simultaneously. To adjust for comparator characteristics, a linear mixed model for repeated measurements (MMRM) was fitted to the IPD from the primary analysis period of the COMET study (Fig. 1). The model included: sex, baseline age, baseline values for outcomes of interest (FVCpp, 6MWT, MIP and MEP), treatment group, visit, region, and interaction terms between treatment groups, and each of the variables (visit, age, sex, and baseline endpoint value). At week 52, for the ERT-naive population, all PROPEL study results were derived using ANCOVA analysis, adjusted for baseline covariates [24], while for weeks 12, 26 and 38, the FVCpp and 6MWT values were obtained from differences in simple mean and change from baseline. Variables included in the model were centred on the average baseline values from the PROPEL study to estimate the treatment effect for AVA versus ALG in a PROPEL-like population. To anchor the analysis, a Bucher’s ITC was subsequently conducted to derive an estimate of AVA versus Cipa+mig using the population-adjusted estimate for AVA versus ALG with the reported estimate for Cipa+mig versus ALG. This anchored STC was conducted in accordance with the NICE Decision Support Unit (DSU) guidelines [28]. For reference, a standard Bucher ITC was also conducted without population adjustment, with the assumption that the distribution of effect modifiers was similar across the included studies.

MMRM/MMRM Analysis

This analysis was performed similarly to the MMRM/ANCOVA analysis described above, except that the MMRM results were also used for the PROPEL trial. For the PROPEL trial, efficacy data using the MMRM analysis data were only available for two outcomes (FVCpp and 6MWT), and only at week 52 [22]. Hence, comparisons for the MMRM/MMRM analysis could only be conducted for FVCpp and 6MWT at weeks 49–52.

ERT-Experienced Population

MMRM/Mean CFB Analysis

For the ERT-experienced population, due to the lack of a common comparator, unanchored STCs were conducted as a first step using results analysed by linear MMRM for the COMET-OLE/NEO1/NEO EXT trials. For the PROPEL trial, at all-time points, absolute outcomes were reported as change from baseline and standard error for Cipa+mig. Due to the small sample sizes in ATB200-02, statistical models were not fitted to estimate least-squares means; thus, observed mean changes from baseline were reported instead.

This analysis used IPD from the COMET-OLE (patients switched from ALG to AVA at 49 weeks) and the NEO1/NEO-EXT study, and aggregate data from the PROPEL-experienced population and ATB200-02 (Fig. 1), with adjustment for differences in population characteristics through regression adjustment [29]. The unanchored STC included the following variables: sex, baseline age, ERT duration, baseline values for outcomes of interest (FVCpp, MIPpp, MEPpp and 6MWT), visit and region. Explanatory variables were centred on the average baseline values from the PROPEL and ATB200-02 studies. Comparisons were made for the COMET-OLE plus NEO1/NEO-EXT [in a PROPEL-experienced-like population (based on the Cipa+mig group characteristics)] versus PROPEL-experienced, and for COMET-OLE plus NEO1/NEO-EXT [in an ATB200-02-like population (based on the Cipa+mig group characteristics)] versus ABT200-02. The results of the STCs in the ERT-experienced population were pooled using random effect meta-analysis to evaluate the overall comparative treatment effects for FVCpp and 6MWT only (as the ATB200-02 study did not report data for MIPpp and MEPpp). Meta-analysis was conducted using STATA 15.

MMRM/MMRM Analysis

This analysis was performed similarly to the MMRM/Mean CFB analysis described above, except that the MMRM results were also used for the PROPEL trial and results were confined to FVCpp and 6MWT and Weeks 48–52 due to data availability from PROPEL only at Week 52 and only for these outcomes. As the MMRM results were not available for the ATB200-02 study, the comparative efficacy was measured between COMET-OLE/NEO1/NEO-EXT and the PROPEL trial only. As standard error values based on the MMRM analysis were not available for the PROPEL study, point estimates were derived but not confidence intervals.

Results

Patient Characteristics

ERT-Naïve Population

Data from 100 patients (AVA, n = 51; ALG, n = 49) from the COMET study and 28 patients (Cipa+mig, n = 20; ALG, n = 8) from the PROPEL-naïve study population were included in the ERT-naïve patient population. Baseline characteristics are summarised in Table 2. Overall, the proportion of male patients was higher in the COMET study versus the PROPEL study (52.0% vs. 45.5%), whereas the proportion of patients from the Asia Pacific region and European Union was lower (57.0% vs. 66.7%, respectively). Data for sex and region for the PROPEL ERT-naïve population were unavailable and were assumed to be similar to those of the overall PROPEL study population (ERT-naïve and experienced). The mean baseline values for respiratory and mobility outcomes in the overall PROPEL-naïve cohort were higher than those for the overall COMET-naïve cohort (Table 2).

ERT-Experienced Population

Data from 59 patients treated in clinical studies of AVA (COMET-OLE, n = 44; NEO1/NEO-EXT, n = 15) and from 82 patients treated with Cipa+mig (PROPEL-experienced, n = 65; ATB200-02, n = 17) were included in the ERT-experienced patient population.

Table 3 summarises the patient characteristics for the ERT-experienced population. Limited baseline data were reported for the ATB200-02 study. The proportion of male patients (52.5% vs. 42.4%) and patients from the North/South American region (50.8% vs. 30.6%) was higher in the overall AVA cohort than in the Cipa+mig cohort of patients who were ERT-experienced in PROPEL. The duration of prior ERT treatment also varied across these studies. In the PROPEL-experienced population, the majority of patients included in this analysis had received treatment for > 5 years, whereas patients from the COMET-OLE had only received ERT since switching from ALG at Week 49. In the NEO1/NEO-EXT studies, 40.0% of the patients had a prior ERT duration of less than 3 years and, in ATB200-02, the duration of prior ERT treatment was approximately 6 years (Table 3).

Comparative Efficacy

ERT-Naïve Population

Results for patients who were ERT-naïve were derived from 98 patients from COMET (AVA, n = 51; ALG, n = 47; post-baseline measurements were unavailable for the remaining two patients), and 27 patients from the PROPEL-naïve population (Cipa+mig, n = 20; ALG, n = 7). One patient who was ERT-naïve in the alglucosidase alfa group was deemed by the principal investigator as likely to have deliberately underperformed at baseline; this patient was excluded from all efficacy analyses in the PROPEL publication. Sample size varied across outcomes and time points.

Respiratory Outcomes

The unadjusted (Bucher’s ITC) and population-adjusted anchored STC estimates for the difference in absolute change from baseline in FVCpp generally favoured AVA versus Cipa+mig (Table 4). Adjusting for variations in effect modifiers increased the differences in favour of AVA; however, the differences were non-significant. From the MMRM/MMRM analysis, the estimated treatment difference at 49–52 weeks in FVCpp for AVA versus Cipa+mig was 5.49% (95% CI–0.87, 11.86; p < 0.09). This compared with an estimated treatment difference for AVA versus Cipa+mig of 4.69% (95% CI –3.22, 12.61; p = 0.25) using the MMRM/ANCOVA comparison (Fig. 2A).

Mobility Outcome

The estimated change from baseline in 6MWT for AVA versus Cipa+mig favoured AVA at all time points in both the unadjusted and adjusted analyses (Table 4). In the MMRM/MMRM analysis, the estimated treatment difference for 6MWT at 49–52 weeks for AVA versus Cipa+mig was 57.08 m (CI 11.04, 103.12; p < 0.02). Discussion

Our analysis presents the comparative treatment effects of AVA versus Cipa+mig using various ITC methods. The results of this ITC demonstrated that respiratory and mobility outcomes numerically favoured AVA compared to Cipa+mig, with AVA demonstrating a statistically significant enhancement in MEPpp compared with Cipa+mig in the ERT-experienced group (MMRM/Mean CFB analysis) and in 6MWT results in patients who were ERT-naïve (MMRM/MMRM analysis).

The greater improvements estimated for AVA across clinical outcomes are clinically relevant for several reasons. FVC is commonly used in clinical practice to measure respiratory function in LOPD, and diminished FVC is linked to increased respiratory complications and mortality [39]. Two recent analyses estimated the clinically meaningful between-group differences in FVCpp in patients who were ERT-naïve with LOPD to be 2.1% (range 1.1–3.1) and 2.47–4.83%, respectively [19, 20]. Notably, the between-group differences of 4.69% (MMRM/ANCOVA) and 5.49% (MMRM/MMRM) in favour of AVA in the ERT-naïve population in our study are either between or above these ranges. Moreover, longitudinal changes in FVC are positively associated with other measures across multiple domains in LOPD, including endurance (6MWT), muscle strength and patient-reported outcomes [39]. The greater improvements in respiratory muscle strength (MIPpp and MEPpp) estimated for AVA in this analysis are also clinically relevant, as improvement in respiratory muscle strength alongside FVC improvement could delay respiratory failure and minimise dependency on mechanical ventilation support [15]. The wide confidence intervals for MIPpp and MEPpp are mainly driven by the large standard errors reported for PROPEL for these outcomes. The 6MWT is a recognised endpoint for assessing activities of daily living and is an indicator of physical improvement [40]. A recent study estimated the clinically meaningful between-group difference in 6MWT to be 0.35–7.47% (equivalent to 1.97–46.61 m) for a sample treatment-naïve population [20]. Therefore, the estimated 6MWT improvement for AVA versus Cipa+mig in this population is both indicative of clinical progress and may be deemed clinically meaningful and relevant to patients’ quality of life [30].

The comparative treatment effects of AVA over Cipa+mig were directionally lower in the ERT-experienced versus ERT-naïve populations. Differences in sample size per population may have been a contributing factor for this difference.

Our analysis included single-arm trials (for the ERT-experienced population) and studies with varying distributions of potential effect modifiers. These variations can introduce bias in findings derived from standard ITC methods such as Bucher’s ITC and network meta-analysis [41, 42]. To mitigate this potential for bias, we used population-adjusted indirect comparison methods, specifically STC [27, 29, 43]. Population-adjusted indirect comparisons enable reliable comparisons, even when the involved studies present variations in effect modifiers or lack a common reference arm [26, 43]. Use of STC for this analysis further strengthens the validity of the findings as it exhibits lower bias when adjustments for continuous effect modifiers are needed, particularly for studies with smaller sample sizes [42]. The prognostic factors and effect modifiers included in our analysis were identified based on established research and clinical feedback [34–37]. In addition, patients in our analysis were categorised based on prior ERT experience, which is a known treatment effect modifier [34]. This latter categorisation informed use of an anchored STC approach for studies that included a common ERT-naïve comparator arm, while an unanchored STC approach was employed for the individuals who were ERT-experienced due to the absence of a common comparator.

The results of the PROPEL trial, as reported in peer-reviewed publications, were primarily based on mean change from baseline (CFB) and/or ANCOVA analyses. These publications provided data for multiple time points and outcome measures and thus were used for most of the STC analyses. Additionally, the SmPC for Cipa+mig presented PROPEL trial results using the MMRM method. To ensure methodological consistency, we repeated the STC analyses using MMRM-analysed data for both comparators. Although the latter dataset did not include all the data required to repeat every MMRM/ANCOVA or MMRM/Mean CFB analysis, the analyses that were feasible were important for providing the most consistent comparison between the two trials.

For all respiratory and mobility outcomes analysed, numerical improvements in favour of AVA were observed regardless of the statistical methods used for the Cipa+mig data. Notably, in the MMRM/MMRM comparison, the difference in 6MWT reached statistical significance in the ERT-naïve population.

Another recently published ITC from Shohet et al. [44] compared AVA to Cipa+mig using data from the same clinical trials as our analysis but applied a different technique to account for varying distributions of potential effect modifiers [multi-level network meta-regression ([ML-NMR)] [42]. The resultant data demonstrated superiority of AVA over Cipa+mig when they were limited to the double-blind portions of the phase III trials (COMET and PROPEL). In contrast, the results demonstrated superiority of Cipa+mig when data from the single arm open label extensions were included in the analyses. The latter analysis incorporated single-arm trials by pairing them to arms of other studies in the network with no adjustment for differences in patient characteristics, effectively treating naively paired outcomes as if they were derived from randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Potential explanations for the discrepancy between the two analyses in this other publication, and between our analyses and the ML-NMR including single-arm data include the introduction of bias in the ML-NMR from a variety of sources. First, due to the heterogeneity in baseline values between the single-arm and randomised trials, introducing single-arm data into their analysis could lead to bias owing to an imbalance of ERT duration, and/or overfitting and misspecification [45]. Additionally, no adjustment was made to account for including ALG patients from COMET and PROPEL multiple times. These limitations may explain the presence of contradictions between the ITC outputs and direct results from the phase III studies. Specifically, while Cipa+mig did not achieve statistical superiority over ALG for improving 6MWT in PROPEL, it did in the ML-NMR [24, 46] and, while the COMET study showed a nominally significant benefit of AVA versus ALG based on 6MWT, the direction of results from the ML-NMR suggests otherwise. Since consistency between the direct and indirect evidence is a fundamental assumption of ITC analysis, these contradictory results raise questions about the validity of the ML-NMR findings. Recent statements from NICE and an Orphan Maintenance Assessment Report [12] released by the European Medicines Agency cited similar fundamental concerns with the Shohet et al. ITC, concluding that the methods used could not compensate for the direct results of RCTs, including the PROPEL trial of Cipa+mig, which did not meet its primary endpoint [47].

The strengths of our analyses include their conduct in accordance with the NICE DSU guidelines for population-adjusted indirect comparisons [28] and the use of rigorous methodology. The STC is a robust technique for population adjustment when requisite assumptions are met [26]. Further, it is the recommended method to adjust for baseline differences in treatment effect modifiers and prognostic factors. In addition, separate comparisons for patients who were ERT-naïve and who were ERT-experienced were performed, including all available data and using methodologically valid approaches.

Limitations

Our analyses have certain limitations. As with many ITCs, IPD was not available for all included studies. Our ITC used IPD from all AVA studies and aggregate data from Cipa+mig studies (notably, Shohet et al. used IPD from one study, PROPEL, and aggregate data from all the others). Other limitations of our analysis include the small sample size of the included studies and the heterogeneity of baseline characteristics and treatment effects, which may lead to some uncertainty in the estimates. These two factors, which are commonly present in rare disease research studies, make it difficult to demonstrate statistical significance in ITCs. The methods used rely on the internal validity of the trials and adjustment of all potential effect modifiers and sufficient overlap amongst the populations. While efforts were made to adjust populations on all treatment effect modifiers and prognostic factors, residual differences in unmeasured and/or unreported variables may remain [15, 24, 30, 32, 33]. Few demographic patient characteristics (age, sex, race and region) were reported for PROPEL and none were reported according to prior ERT exposure; thus, only overall values were considered in this analysis. Information on other patient characteristics, such as disease duration, ventilation use at baseline, and baseline weight and height, were not reported for the PROPEL and ATB200-02 studies and thus could not be adjusted for in our analyses. The ERT-experienced analysis has certain further limitations: the analysis was unanchored (in the absence of a common comparator) requiring additional assumptions that increase the susceptibility to bias; differences in ERT duration were adjusted on the mean but not on the distribution categories (< 3 years, 3–5 years, > 5 years), so some differences remain on this variable; and pooling of the results did not account for the correlation between the ITC estimates of AVA versus Cipa+mig (PROPEL) and AVA versus Cipa+mig (ATB200-02), which may lead to an underestimation of the uncertainty around treatment effects. Due to the differences in patient population across studies, the unadjusted comparisons are most susceptible to bias, and the results should be interpreted cautiously.

While the results of ITCs cannot replace those from head-to-head comparisons, they can provide insight into potential differences between treatments. The use of comparable study designs in randomised trials and the availability of real-world data could improve the robustness of future ITCs to ensure consistency and validity for use in health-technology assessment and clinical practice [27, 28]. Future ITCs including analyses of safety outcomes would be valuable in this regard.

Conclusion

The findings of this ITC indicate that AVA may be considered a more favourable option than Cipa+mig in terms of all analysed respiratory (FVCpp, MIPpp, MEPpp) and mobility (6MWT) outcomes at approximately 1 year for patients with LOPD, regardless of prior ERT experience. The results of these analyses, conducted in accordance with NICE DSU guidelines for population-adjusted indirect comparisons, were consistent across both Bucher’s ITC and anchored STC methods. However, the small sample size for the included trials likely impeded statistical significance in the results. This ITC may aid policymakers and clinicians in understanding potential differences between LOPD treatment options. Future real-world studies comparing AVA and Cipa+mig are warranted to confirm our findings from this ITC.
