Relationship between dietary inflammation index and progression of vitiligo
Authors: Yang Yang, Jingying Tian, Lili Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitiligo is a melanocyte-destructive depigmenting disease linked to immune inflammation, with diet potentially mediating inflammatory processes via immune dysregulation. However, the specific role of diet in vitiligo pathogenesis remains understudied.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and vitiligo onset or progression, for developing novel prevention strategies.MethodsA case-control study included 80 progressive vitiligo patients (2024 Jan-Mar, Shuguang Hospital) and 80 sex/age-matched controls. Dietary intake was assessed via semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (SQFFQ), with DII scores calculated. Capsule summary

Diet-induced inflammation may be associated with immune dysregulation, which potentially contributes to melanocyte damage. This study provides new insights for exploring the mechanisms of vitiligo occurrence and development and dietary intervention strategies.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a prevalent acquired pigmentary disorder with depigmentation marked by depigmented patches on the skin or mucous membranes. Its clinical treatment is difficult, and skin lesions in exposed or special areas often cause serious psychological burdens to patients. The specific cause of vitiligo is still unclear, but it is generally believed that in individuals with a genetic predisposition, immune, neuropsychiatric, endocrine, and metabolic dysfunctions are triggered by multiple internal and external factors, leading to melanocyte destruction or dysfunction and the appearance of skin depigmented patches [1].

Most evidence supports that the direct cause of melanocyte destruction in vitiligo is an immune-inflammatory response [2, 3]. As an important environmental factor, diet may participate in the disease process by regulating the body’s inflammatory state [4]. Inappropriate diets may damage melanocytes by inducing immune disorders and causing inflammatory processes. Specifically, dietary patterns can modulate systemic inflammation through the gut-skin axis, which may further affect cutaneous immune responses and melanocyte survival [5, 6]. However, the specific role of diet-induced inflammation in vitiligo progression has not been deeply studied A deeper investigation into the link between diet and vitiligo may enhance our understanding of how dietary factors influence the inflammatory status of the immune system, thereby offering a theoretical foundation for the prevention and management of this condition.

The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) is a quantitative instrument formulated from literature, which gauges the combined impacts of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory components in the diet to reflect the overall inflammatory potential of dietary patterns [7]. The DII uses food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) to obtain intake data of macronutrients and micronutrients and then calculates the inflammatory score of an individual’s overall dietary pattern. The more pro-inflammatory components in the dietary pattern, the higher the DII score. DII can not only qualitatively distinguish the anti-inflammatory/pro-inflammatory tendency of the diet but also quantify the overall inflammatory potential of an individual’s dietary structure. Previous studies have shown that DII is of great value in the risk assessment of chronic diseases such as reflux esophagitis [8].

In summary, diet-induced inflammation is one of the important pathogenic factors of vitiligo, but there have been no studies on the dietary inflammation index in the vitiligo population. This study aims to investigate the dietary inflammation index in vitiligo patients, collect relevant dietary intake data through questionnaires, calculate the dietary inflammation index, and explore the relationship between the dietary inflammation index and progression of vitiligo, with a view to further exploring the pathogenesis of vitiligo and providing a basis for formulating treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

Eighty patients with progressive vitiligo (case group) who visited Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine from January to March 2024 and 80 healthy physical examinees (control group) during the same period were selected and matched 1:1 by age (± 5 years) and sex. Inclusion criteria: Meet the diagnostic criteria for vitiligo [9], in the progressive stage (meeting VIDA score ≥ 3 points, clinical features, isomorphic reaction, Wood’s lamp or auxiliary examination in any one item); no other skin diseases; voluntary participation in the survey. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study before enrollment.

The sample size of this study was determined by a priori power calculation. Assuming an odds ratio (OR) of 1.2 for the association between the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and vitiligo (referencing the effect size reported in similar studies on diet and autoimmune diseases [7]), a two-sided α level of 0.05, and a β of 0.2 (corresponding to 80% statistical power), calculations performed with PASS 15.0 software indicated a minimum required sample size of 72 participants per group. Taking into account the questionnaire response rate and data integrity, we ultimately enrolled 80 participants in each group to ensure adequate statistical power.

Diagnosis of progressive vitiligo

The diagnosis of vitiligo combines multi-dimensional examinations [9]: ①VIDA score: scoring based on skin lesion changes in the past 3 months, ≥ 3 points for progressive stage. ②Clinical features: progressive stage may show blurred margins, inflammatory erythema, trichrome sign, and pityriasis-like depigmented patches. ③Isomorphic reaction: the appearance of depigmented patches at the skin injury site within the past 3 months supports the diagnosis of the progressive stage. ④Wood’s lamp examination: progressive stage skin lesions show bright white color, unclear borders, and an area larger than visual estimation.

Assessment of dietary inflammation index

In this study, a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (SQFFQ) was used to collect the dietary status of the samples over the past three months to obtain the average intake of macronutrients and micronutrients, collect the dietary habit data of vitiligo patients and healthy people, and calculate their respective DII values to assess their dietary inflammation levels. Dietary data were collected through face-to-face interviews by asking patients to record and assess the frequency and quantity of various foods consumed over the past three months. The surveys were conducted through one-on-one interviews with the informed consent of the respondents, and the questionnaires were returned on the spot. The collected questionnaire data were sorted, coded, and entered, and SPSS software was used for data analysis, including descriptive statistics, chi-square test, regression analysis, etc., to reveal the correlation between DII and vitiligo.

However, given the lack of a validated China-specific reference standard for DII calculation, we used the global average intake framework developed by Shivappa et al. 5 as the reference (Table 1). While this system has been adopted in numerous Chinese population-based studies [8] and ensures comparability with existing literature, it may not fully reflect the distinct features of Chinese dietary patterns, particularly the traditional high-carbohydrate, low-fat dietary profile.

Correlation analysis between DII and vitiligo

The scoring system developed by Shivappa N et al. [7] was used to calculate DII, which can be divided into 5 steps: ①Obtain the average intake of macronutrients and micronutrients through dietary surveys; ②Calculate the exposure Z-score of individual dietary components: Z=(individual daily average intake of dietary components - global average intake of dietary components)/standard deviation of global average intake of dietary components. Representative global population daily average intake data are shown in Table 1; ③Centralize the Z-scores: to minimize the influence of “right skewness”, convert the obtained Z-scores into percentile values n (ranging from − 1 to + 1, centered at 0); ④Calculate the DII of individual dietary components (hereinafter referred to as “single DII”): single DII = n×b (the corresponding inflammation score for each food component, see Table 1); ⑤The overall DII score is derived by summing the component-specific DII scores: DII = b1×n1 + b2×n2⋯+b45×n45. Consistent with prior research, and accounting for Chinese dietary practices, the current study ultimately incorporated 27 dietary components in the DII calculation. These include individual foods, macronutrients, micronutrients, and bioactive substances: energy, protein, total fat, cholesterol, carbohydrates, dietary fiber, vitamins A, C, D, E, B6, B12, thiamine, riboflavin, caffeine, niacin, folic acid, iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, isoflavones, alcohol, garlic, ginger, onion, and green or black tea. The mean DII scores for the case group and the control group were computed separately. After all data were collected, the data were checked for accuracy and completeness. After data collation, SPSS software was used for data processing, with a test level of α = 0.05.

Discussion

Association between lifestyle and vitiligo

As a prevalent chronic skin disorder, the onset and progression of vitiligo are closely linked to lifestyle factors. In the present study, univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in staying up late and dietary regularity between vitiligo patients and healthy controls.

Correlation between dietary inflammation index and progression of vitiligo

Irregular eating may lead to disordered expression of circadian rhythm genes (such as BMAL1 and CLOCK), affecting metabolic homeostasis and inflammation regulation [10]. For example, late dinners or overeating can induce visceral fat accumulation, releasing adiponectin and leptin, and exacerbating chronic inflammation [11]. Therefore, the impact of dietary patterns on systemic chronic inflammation is persistent and cumulative. A pro-inflammatory diet refers to a general term for diets that drive inflammatory reactions in the body after a large intake of certain foods. Pro-inflammatory diets activate the NF-κB pathway and TLR4/MyD88 pathway through multiple mechanisms such as gut microbiota dysregulation, intestinal barrier disruption, and direct effects of saturated fatty acids, inducing the production of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, and triggering chronic low-grade inflammation [12]. Previous research has shown that serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-17 are significantly higher in patients with progressive vitiligo compared to healthy subjects [13]. Among them, TNF-α can regulate immune responses, induce keratinocyte apoptosis, trigger autoimmune reactions leading to melanocyte loss, and promote CD8 + T cell infiltration at the lesion margins to exacerbate melanocyte destruction. IL-6 has been demonstrated to promote the activation of local cutaneous immune cells and the establishment of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. In addition, pro-inflammatory factors also interact with immune cells, driving naive CD4 + T cells to differentiate into Th1/Th17 subtypes, inhibiting regulatory T cell function, breaking immune tolerance, and enhancing the recognition and killing of melanocytes by CD8 + T cells through upregulating antigen-presenting molecules. Consequently, pro-inflammatory factors play a substantial role in the pathogenesis of vitiligo, emphasizing the imperative for further investigation into the association between the dietary inflammatory index and progression of vitiligo [8].

The results of this study showed a significant positive correlation between the dietary inflammation index and progression of vitiligo. Each 1-unit increase in the DII score was associated with a 23% increase in vitiligo risk. Moreover, the dietary inflammation index of the vitiligo case group was distributed in the pro-inflammatory diet range represented by positive values, and its value was higher than that of the healthy control group, suggesting that the overall dietary structure of vitiligo patients tends to have a pro-inflammatory potential, which may be one of the potential factors associated with disease progression. Therefore, adjusting the dietary structure and reducing the intake of pro-inflammatory foods may help reduce the risk of vitiligo occurrence and progression and stabilize the disease condition.

In this study, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and vitiligo was 0.161 (P < 0.05), indicating a relatively weak correlation. This finding is consistent with the nature of diet as an environmental factor: the progression of autoimmune diseases is generally driven by the combined effects of genetic susceptibility and multiple environmental factors, and the independent effect of a single dietary factor may be limited [4]. Despite the weak correlation, logistic regression analysis revealed that each 1-unit increase in DII score was associated with a 23% higher risk of vitiligo progression (21% after adjustment for confounding factors), with a statistically significant association. This suggests that even moderate dietary adjustments in clinical practice, such as reducing the intake of pro-inflammatory foods, may exert a beneficial effect on mitigating vitiligo progression.

Classification of pro-inflammatory diets and intervention suggestions

Pro-inflammatory diets can be mainly divided into six categories, including diets rich in red meat, refined grains, imbalanced ratios of omega-6 unsaturated fatty acids (n-6) to n-3, or rich in trans fatty acids, fried and barbecued diets, high-sugar and high-fat diets [10]. In the present study, the proportion of patients in the case group who consumed high-sugar, high-fat pastries and fried foods was significantly higher than that in the control group. Both categories of foods—high-sugar, high-fat pastries and fried foods—are classified as pro-inflammatory diets. Studies have shown that high-sugar diets may stimulate the release of interleukin placenta trophoblast cells IL-1β and IL-18, thereby increasing the levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 [11]. High-fat diets may lead to increased levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Fried and barbecued diets may increase the levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1 [14, 15], thereby increasing systemic inflammation levels. Furthermore, the long-term consumption of diets with high concentrations of red meat—including pork, beef, and mutton—has been associated with elevated levels of biomarkers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), leptin, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). This increase in biomarkers can contribute to an elevated dietary inflammatory index [13]. Including refined grains such as rice, noodles, steamed buns, rice noodles, and pizza also carry pro-inflammatory risks.

It is recommended that patients avoid long-term consumption of pro-inflammatory foods (e.g., high-sugar/high-fat pastries, fried/barbecued foods, pickled items, red meat, alcoholic beverages), which can increase dietary inflammation index and potentially aggravate vitiligo. Instead, they should increase intake of anti-inflammatory foods rich in high-quality protein, dietary fiber, healthy fats, and antioxidants, such as unsweetened nuts, soy products, omega-3-rich fish, whole grains, vegetables, and fruits. Lifestyle interventions, particularly dietary optimization, may offer new scientific evidence for vitiligo prevention and management.

Exploration of the pathogenesis of dietary inflammation and vitiligo

Pro-inflammatory diets can damage intestinal barrier function, induce microbiota dysregulation (such as an increase in Firmicutes and a decrease in Bacteroidetes), and trigger systemic inflammation through the “gut-skin axis” [5]. In the future, the diversity of gut microbiota in patients can be detected to verify the correlation between DII and microbiota dysregulation. Long-term pro-inflammatory diets and bad living habits may lead to higher systemic inflammation levels in vitiligo patients. Diet-induced inflammation may affect vitiligo progression primarily through the ‘gut-skin axis’, a bidirectional communication pathway between the intestinal microbiota and cutaneous immune system [6]. Pro-inflammatory diets (e.g., high in saturated fat, sugar, and red meat) can disrupt intestinal barrier integrity, leading to increased intestinal permeability (‘leaky gut’). This allows translocation of bacterial endotoxins (e.g., lipopolysaccharide, LPS) and undigested food antigens into the systemic circulation, triggering low-grade systemic inflammation.

Meanwhile, pro-inflammatory diets alter the gut microbiota composition—such as increasing the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes and reducing the abundance of anti-inflammatory bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) [16]. These microbiota changes further promote the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) by intestinal immune cells, which are transported to the skin through the bloodstream. In the skin microenvironment, these systemic pro-inflammatory factors can activate cutaneous immune cells (e.g., keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, and CD8⁺ T cells), upregulate the production of IFN-γ and IL-15, and disrupt the immune tolerance balance. Ultimately, this cascade leads to enhanced melanocyte destruction and disease progression.

Multiple studies have shown that the levels of pro-inflammatory factors interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), interleukin-7 (IL-7), and interleukin-25 (IL-25) in the serum of vitiligo patients are higher than those in normal people, while the anti-inflammatory factor interleukin-10 (IL-10) is lower than that in normal people [13, 17]. In addition, studies have found that in an inflammatory environment or under the induction of inflammatory factors IL-1 and IL-6, adaptive Treg (iTreg) cells with immune homeostasis functions in vitiligo patients will undergo phenotypic changes, inhibiting the activity of forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) and Treg cells through Deleted in breast cancer gene 1 protein (DBC1), leading to specific Foxp3 loss and transformation into pro-inflammatory cells Th17, resulting in reduced peripheral immune tolerance and increased inflammatory cells, thereby triggering melanocyte destruction. Due to the unstable phenotype of iTreg, the imbalance between Treg cells and Th17 cells in an inflammatory environment may be a key link in inducing vitiligo and promoting disease development [18].

Regulatory mechanisms of pro-inflammatory diets on vitiligo core cytokines (IFN-γ/IL-15)

Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-15 (IL-15) are core pathogenic cytokines driving the onset and progression of vitiligo. IFN-γ is predominantly secreted by CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and T helper 1 (Th1) cells, while IL-15 is mainly produced by keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, and macrophages [2, 19]. Pro-inflammatory diets may regulate the production of these two cytokines through the following pathways:

Regulatory effects on IFN-γ production: Pro-inflammatory diets high in saturated fat and refined sugar can promote the activation of circulating CD8+ T cells and Th1 cells via the gut-skin axis mediated by gut microbiota dysbiosis [5]. This process upregulates the expression of the transcription factor T-bet, which in turn enhances IFN-γ secretion. Meanwhile, pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) induced by pro-inflammatory diets can form a positive feedback loop to further stimulate IFN-γ release from CD8⁺ T cells [3]. In turn, IFN-γ can directly induce melanocyte damage and recruit more immune cells to infiltrate the lesional skin sites.

Significance of the cross-disease associations of the dietary inflammatory index (DII)

Accumulating evidence from multiple peer-reviewed studies has confirmed that elevated Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) scores are not only associated with vitiligo, but also linked to an increased risk of other autoimmune and autoinflammatory diseases:

Psoriasis: DII is closely correlated with the pathogenesis of psoriasis. One retrospective study analyzed the clinical data of 200 patients with plaque psoriasis to explore the predictive value of DII for disease recurrence. The results showed that DII was an independent risk factor for recurrence in plaque psoriasis [21]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further indicated that DII had high sensitivity and specificity for recurrence prediction, with favorable predictive performance. These findings support that DII can serve as a valuable biomarker for predicting plaque psoriasis recurrence, providing a theoretical basis for reducing recurrence risk through dietary intervention.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): Studies have revealed a positive correlation between DII and the risk of RA, where higher inflammatory potential of the diet corresponds to a greater risk of RA onset [22]. DII may exert additive effects with other risk factors to further elevate the risk of RA development. Therefore, reducing the intake of pro-inflammatory foods may represent an effective strategy for RA prevention. Notably, certain pro-inflammatory nutrients (including protein, total energy, and saturated fatty acids) are essential for maintaining the normal physiological functions of the body and cannot be completely eliminated from the diet. In addition, population-specific differences have been observed: at the same DII level, the Spanish population has a relatively higher risk of RA onset.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted in this study. First, the relatively small sample size may introduce statistical bias; large-scale, multi-center studies with expanded sample sizes are warranted in the future to improve the reliability of the findings. Second, given the lack of validated dietary intake standards for Chinese or Shanghai residents, DII scores were calculated based on global reference values. Dietary habit differences across ethnic groups and regions may lead to result deviations, and may either underestimate or overestimate the inflammatory potential of dietary patterns in the Chinese population. Future studies should establish a validated China-specific DII reference standard to enhance the accuracy and generalizability of the results. Third, this study only conducted a preliminary exploration of the correlation between DII and vitiligo, without grouping by disease stage (pre-onset, progressive phase, and stable phase). Further studies stratified by these time periods are needed to clarify the association of DII with vitiligo onset and disease progression, to provide more robust evidence for formulating clinical treatment strategies. In addition, dietary information over the prior 3 months was collected via a retrospective semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias and compromise the accuracy of DII scoring. Future research could adopt prospective food diaries and objective inflammatory biomarker measurements to mitigate this bias. Finally, this study did not assess gut microbiota composition, intestinal permeability, or related inflammatory metabolites. Future studies incorporating these biomarkers are required to further validate the gut-skin axis mechanism linking DII to vitiligo progression.

Conclusion

Diet-induced inflammation may be associated with vitiligo progression through potential mechanisms, and the impact of bad diet patterns on diseases is persistent and cumulative. Pro-inflammatory diets are potentially associated with an increased risk of vitiligo progression. Adjusting the dietary structure may help prevent and control the disease. This study provides a new basis for dietary interventions in vitiligo.
