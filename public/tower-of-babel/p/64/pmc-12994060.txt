Real-Time Assessment of Dentinal Fluid Flow Reduction by Desensitising Agents Using Subnanolitre Flow Measuring System
Authors: Soyeon Kim, Satoshi Yamaguchi, In-Bog Lee, Young-Seok Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the dentinal tubule-occluding efficacy of various desensitising toothpastes and a sodium fluoride varnish using a subnanolitre scaled fluid flow measuring device (NFMD).MethodsBovine dentin specimens were treated with four desensitising toothpastes (Colgate, Median, Bamboo Salt, and Sensodyne), a 2.1% sodium fluoride professionally-deliverable varnish (Clinpro), and distilled water (DW). Real-time dentinal fluid flow (DFF) was measured under simulated pulpal pressure (32 cm H₂O) for 1000 seconds using the NFMD system. Each toothpaste was applied directly to assess its chemical occlusion capability, with measurements recorded before, during, and after material removal. Surface morphology and the extent of tubule occlusion were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) in both cross-sectional and surface views. Data were analysed by one-way analysis of variance.ResultsA significant overall treatment effect was observed (P < .001). Bamboo Salt, Sensodyne, and Colgate showed greater mean reductions than Clinpro, whereas Median did not outperform the DW group. SEM findings supported these trends, revealing substantial tubule occlusion for Bamboo Salt and Sensodyne and minimal occlusion for Median and DW.ConclusionDesensitising toothpastes reduced dentinal permeability to levels comparable to a sodium fluoride varnish. Real-time fluid flow measurement using NFMD enabled functional validation of dentinal tubule occlusion beyond surface morphology.Clinical RelevanceCommonly available desensitising toothpastes can provide meaningful reductions in dentinal permeability. Understanding how different formulations interact with exposed dentin may help guide product selection based on a patient’s specific needs. Real-time assessments of dentinal fluid movement may also assist in evaluating emerging desensitising technologies.Key words: Dentinal fluid flow, dentin hypersensitivity, desensitising toothpaste, subnanolitre-scaled fluid flow measuring device

Introduction

Dentin hypersensitivity is a common and persistent clinical condition, affecting nearly one-third of the global population.1 It arises from the loss of enamel and cementum, exposing the underlying dentin and allowing external stimuli to directly interact with the dentinal tubules.2 According to the hydrodynamic theory, pain results from the inward and outward movement of fluid within these open tubules, stimulating the A-delta nerve fibres located at the pulpal side of the dentin.3, 4, 5, 6 This mechanism explains the sharp, short-lived pain characteristic of the condition. Hypersensitive teeth typically exhibit a higher density and larger diameter of dentinal tubules, leading to greater hydraulic conductance and amplified nerve excitation.7,8 The primary etiological factors include chemical erosion from acidic food intake or gastric reflux, mechanical abrasion from brushing or abrasive toothpaste, and gingival recession resulting from trauma, ageing, or periodontal disease.9,10 Erosive wear in particular promotes the removal of the smear layer, leading to dentin demineralisation and ultimately causing increased dentinal permeability due to widened tubules.11,12 Additionally, dental procedures such as bleaching and restorative treatments can transiently increase sensitivity by altering surface integrity.13 Consequently, the management of dentin hypersensitivity focuses on reducing dentinal permeability through tubule occlusion and modulating neural excitability.

Over the years, extensive research has focused on developing strategies to treat and prevent dentin hypersensitivity. These approaches can be broadly categorised into at-home treatments and professional interventions. Among them, desensitising toothpastes are the most accessible and widely used first-line treatment.14 The mechanisms include occluding dentinal tubules to physically block stimulus transmission and reducing nerve excitability by depolarising pulpal nerve endings.15 Desensitising agents such as calcium carbonate, stannous fluoride, arginine, and bioactive glass act primarily by sealing the tubules.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 On the other hand, potassium salts reduce the excitability of nerve fibres by elevating extracellular potassium concentration at pulpal nerve endings, thus mitigating pain transmission associated with dentin hypersensitivity.21,22

Despite the extensive use of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and conventional hydraulic conductance methods, most previous studies have relied on static or end-point measurements, providing limited insight into the dynamic behaviour of dentinal fluid flow during and after application of desensitising agents. SEM has been widely used to evaluate dentin surface morphology following treatment, whereas hydraulic conductance and permeability tests provide quantitative assessments of dentinal fluid flow (DFF). However, these approaches do not capture real-time changes in fluid movement and the immediate chemical tubule-occluding effects of different desensitising formulations, independent of mechanical brushing, remain insufficiently understood.19,23, 24, 25, 26, 27

Building on these established approaches, the subnanolitre-scaled fluid flow measuring device (NFMD) offers an additional means to assess dentinal permeability with exceptional precision. Capable of detecting fluid displacements as small as 0.196 nanolitres under simulated pulpal pressure, the NFMD allows real-time monitoring of both the rate and direction of fluid movement.28,29 This allows for highly precise quantification of dentinal permeability and immediate evaluation of desensitising effects following material application. Moreover, the inclusion of a professionally applied sodium fluoride varnish provides a meaningful benchmark, allowing the performance of over-the-counter desensitising toothpastes to be interpreted relative to a commonly used clinical intervention.

The objective of this study was to evaluate and compare the dentinal tubule-occluding efficacy of various desensitising toothpastes and a sodium fluoride varnish using an NFMD. The null hypothesis was that there would be no significant differences in dentinal fluid flow reduction among the treatment groups.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Bovine mandibular lateral incisors were used in this study as substitutes for human teeth due to their comparable dentinal structure and number of tubules (n = 10/group).30, 31, 32, 33 Ethical approval was not required according to the guidelines of the ethics committee of Seoul National University. Each tooth was sectioned using a diamond saw to expose the coronal dentin and remove the root portion (Figure 1). The pulp tissue was carefully removed using a hand file (H-File 31 mm, Diadent), and a high-speed drill was employed to enlarge the canal for tube insertion. The exposed root surface was acid-etched for 15 seconds, followed by primer and adhesive application and then light cured for 10 seconds (Adper Scotchbond Multi-Purpose Adhesive System, 3M ESPE). A 2.5 cm metal tube (outer diameter: 0.9 mm) was inserted through the root canal and secured using a flowable composite resin (GSB Flow, DV Mall), which was light-cured for 20 seconds, following a previously described protocol.28,29 The prepared tooth was then positioned within a custom rubber mould (Flexitime Light Flow), and epoxy resin (EZR-101, Leadup) was poured into the mould to completely embed the tooth, leaving only the coronal dentin surface exposed. After complete polymerisation for 48 hours, the specimen was removed from the mould and stored in distilled water. The exposed dentin surface was sequentially polished using 600- and 1200-grit silicon carbide sandpapers (SiC sand paper, R&B) for an even, flat surface. Finally, the polished dentin was etched with 32% phosphoric acid (UNI-ETCH, Bisco) for 15 seconds to remove the smear layer and increase permeability, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in distilled water to ensure a clean, debris-free surface.

Subnanolitre fluid flow measuring device

The NFMD is a highly sensitive system developed to monitor DFF in real time at the subnanolitre level (nanoflow, IB Systems).34 It is composed of three integrated components: (1) a glass capillary paired with a photosensor which detects microscopic movements of the air/water interface; (2) a servomotor mechanism, comprising a lead screw and ball nut, that follows the meniscus motion precisely to ensure continuous tracking; (3) a rotary encoder connected to a computerized data acquisition program that converts the optical and mechanical inputs into quantifiable flow measurements (Figure 2). The device can register fluid displacements as small as approximately 0.2 nanolitres in real-time, allowing highly precise evaluation of dentinal permeability. The specimen was connected to the device using a silicone tube, which connected the device’s glass capillary at one end and the metal tube of the tooth specimen at the other. During the experiment, a hydrostatic pressure of 32 cm H₂O was applied to replicate normal pulp conditions.27 The pressure was applied 24 hours before measurements to allow stabilisation.

Schematic showing the key components and operation of the subnanolitre scaled dentinal fluid flow measurement system (NFMD).

Varnish/toothpaste application

Measurements were taken for up to 1000 seconds per specimen using the NFMD. After an initial stabilisation and baseline measurement of 200 seconds, a pea-sized amount of each treatment or desensitising toothpaste was applied directly to the exposed dentin surface, making sure the whole area was covered (Table 1). The selected toothpastes represent commonly used commercial formulations with distinct desensitising mechanisms, including calcium carbonate–based mineral deposition (Median, Bamboo Salt), stannous fluoride–mediated precipitation (Sensodyne), and arginine-based tubule occlusion (Colgate). A 2.1% sodium fluoride varnish was included as a clinical reference, representing a commonly used professional fluoride therapy. The flow was continuously recorded for 400 seconds following application. The material was removed using dry wipes (Kimtech, Kimberly-Clark) and the specimen was rinsed under running water for 10 seconds to eliminate any remaining toothpaste, then blotted dry. Measurements continued for an additional 400 seconds to evaluate consistency in dentinal fluid flow reduction after material removal (post-application). SEM imaging

Following the DFF measurements, specimens from each group were selected for FE-SEM (Apreo S LoVac, ThermoFisher Science) analysis to observe the extent of dentinal tubule occlusion. Before sputter-coating the specimens with platinum, the specimens were rinsed with running distilled water for 10 seconds to remove residual varnish/toothpaste and left to dry in open air for 24 hours. Images were acquired at 10,000× magnification in both surface and cross-sectional views. Cross-sections were obtained by sectioning the specimens using a low-speed diamond saw under continuous irrigation. The cut surfaces were gently dried with dry wipes (Kimtech) and sputter-coated with platinum before analysis.

Statistical evaluation

The percentage reduction in dentinal fluid flow was used as the primary outcome variable for statistical analysis. After confirming a normal distribution and homogeneity of variances using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s test, respectively, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed along with Tukey’s HSD (honestly significant difference) post hoc analysis. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

The baseline dentinal fluid flow of the specimens ranged from 1.49 to 36.83 nL/s. Among all formulations, including the sodium fluoride varnish, the Bamboo Salt toothpaste showed the greatest reduction in flow, followed closely by the Sensodyne group (Table 2). The blocking potential of these two toothpastes was higher than that of the DW and Median groups. After the removal of the applied toothpastes, the DW and Median groups showed a slight increase in flow (Figure 3). In contrast, the Bamboo Salt and Sensodyne groups maintained nearly constant flow levels that reached a plateau (Figure 3). To confirm whether these observed differences were statistically significant, a one-way ANOVA was conducted.

The one-way ANOVA analysis revealed a statistically significant main effect of treatment, F(5,54) = 5.34, P < .001, indicating that the mean flow differed significantly among the treatments.

The DW group exhibited significantly lower flow than all groups except the Median group; Clinpro (P = .04), Colgate (P = .0047), Bamboo salt (P < .0023), and Sensodyne (P = .0039). No other pairwise differences were statistically significant.

These statistical trends were consistent with SEM observations of dentinal tubule occlusion (Figure 4). Specimens treated with DW exhibited widely open dentinal tubules with no evidence of surface deposits. The Median group showed predominantly open tubules with only sparse and discontinuous deposits, resembling the DW group. In contrast, the Colgate group demonstrated partial tubule occlusion characterised by uniform surface coverage and occluding material at tubule orifices, although some tubules remained visible. More extensive occlusion was observed in the Bamboo Salt and Sensodyne groups, which exhibited dense, continuous surface layers with minimal exposed tubule openings. Clinpro-treated specimens displayed intermediate characteristics, with scattered precipitates and partial tubule coverage.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that desensitising agents differ significantly in their ability to reduce dentinal fluid flow, with Bamboo Salt, Sensodyne, and Colgate showing greater reductions than the clinical sodium fluoride reference, whereas Median did not differ significantly from the DW. These findings indicate that not all desensitising formulations provide equivalent tubule-occluding efficacy. The statistically significant treatment effect led to rejection of the null hypothesis, which proposed no differences in dentinal fluid flow reduction among groups.

Among the formulations tested, Bamboo Salt and Sensodyne exhibited the greatest and most sustained reductions in fluid flow. SEM observations confirmed these findings, revealing dense and uniform tubule occlusion in both groups. Sensodyne contains stannous fluoride, which is known to form insoluble tin-rich compounds that chemically bind to the dentin surface and occlude tubules effectively.35 Bamboo Salt toothpaste, which contains calcium- and phosphate-based components, likely promotes mineral precipitation within tubules, resulting in stable physical blockage.36 A previous study also observed that stannous fluoride and tricalcium phosphate formulations achieved more durable and acid-resistant occlusion compared with sodium fluoride pastes.27 Similarly, a comparative in vitro study reported that calcium- and phosphate-rich agents maintained lower permeability and greater surface coverage over repeated cycles of acid exposure, supporting the present study’s observation of sustained blockage in the Bamboo Salt group.21 In contrast, the Median toothpaste did not significantly outperform the DW group, indicating limited immediate chemical occlusion under the experimental conditions. Although Median showed a reduction in flow, the absence of statistical significance suggests that its effect was weak and insufficient to produce meaningful tubule sealing in the short term.

The DW group consistently exhibited the lowest reduction in flow, confirming its role as an unoccluded baseline. Although a droplet of water on the dentin surface slightly reduced DFF, the subsequent increase after removal indicates limited and reversible tubule occlusion. This temporary effect is expected because a water droplet holds its shape primarily due to surface tension rather than any substantive interaction with the tubules.37

The sodium fluoride varnish (Clinpro) demonstrated a moderate reduction in DFF, which is consistent with its mechanism that relies primarily on fluoride ion exchange and remineralisation rather than rapid tubule occlusion.38,39 Sodium fluoride reacts with calcium ions on the dentin surface to form calcium fluoride deposits that serve as temporary barriers against external stimuli.40 However, studies have found that deposits from sodium fluoride tend to be less resistant to mechanical removal and acid exposure compared to the more insoluble compounds formed by stannous fluoride.41,42 Although erosion tests or brushing have not been performed in this study, previous evidence supports that stannous fluoride–based toothpastes maintain superior durability because tin ions chemically interact with the dentin substrate, forming a tightly bound complex by forming complexes with calcium as well as phosphate, whereas sodium fluoride mainly reacts with calcium only.43 Therefore, the stronger and more persistent reductions in fluid flow observed for Bamboo Salt and Sensodyne suggest that multifactorial mechanisms, such as mineral-rich surface precipitation in Bamboo Salt and insoluble tin compound formation in Sensodyne, contribute to their enhanced and durable tubule occlusion.

Colgate toothpaste showed greater fluid-flow reduction than Clinpro, despite both formulations containing sodium fluoride. The arginine in Colgate facilitates calcium-rich plug formation within dentinal tubules through electrostatic interactions with the dentin surface.44 Evidence also suggests that arginine can enhance the performance of fluoride-based formulations, with arginine–fluoride combinations demonstrating greater reductions in hypersensitivity and improved tubule occlusion compared with fluoride alone.45 These findings support the possibility that the arginine component contributes to the higher fluid-flow reduction observed in the Colgate group relative to Clinpro.

The sustained reduction in dentinal fluid flow after product removal (at 600 seconds) shows that these agents achieved true tubule occlusion through mineral precipitation rather than forming a superficial layer that is easily washed away. SEM analyses are consistent with these quantitative results. The DW and Median specimens showed open tubules with minimal surface deposits, demonstrating their limited flow reduction. Colgate-treated specimens showed partial but uniform tubule occlusion, with occluding material observed along tubule orifices. In contrast, Bamboo salt and Sensodyne produced overall mineral coverage across the dentin surface, indicating effective occlusion. This observation agrees with previous studies that reported similar SEM morphologies following the application of stannous- and calcium-based desensitizing agents.21,27 Clinpro-treated surfaces exhibited partial coverage with scattered calcium fluoride nodules, supporting its moderate efficacy as measured by NFMD.

The wide range in baseline dentinal fluid flow is primarily attributed to anatomical and methodological variability in the dentin specimens. Although a constant pulp pressure of 32 cm H2O was maintained throughout the experiment, differences in dentinal tubule diameter, number, or surface condition (eg, smear layer) can alter permeability.46,47 To minimise the influence of these intrinsic variations on the outcomes, the reduction in fluid flow was used as a comparative parameter instead of the absolute flow. Furthermore, the desensitising toothpastes were applied directly onto the dentin surface without brushing to eliminate the confounding effects of mechanical abrasion and focus solely on their chemical tubule-occluding ability.48,49 This approach allowed the efficacy of each formulation to be evaluated under standardised conditions, ensuring that any observed reduction in dentinal fluid flow reflected the chemical performance of the active ingredients rather than differences in brushing pressure or technique.

The rheological properties, such as viscosity, spreadability, and particle suspension, may influence how uniformly the active ingredients in the toothpastes/varnish interact with the dentin surface. A toothpaste that is too fluid may fail to remain in contact with exposed dentin long enough for effective tubule occlusion, whereas an overly viscous formulation may hinder adequate ion diffusion or particle penetration into the tubules.50 In the context of this study, where toothpastes were applied without brushing, consistency was particularly important because mechanical forces that normally assist in distributing and compacting the paste were absent. Thus, the overall performance of each formulation may have been influenced not only by its active ingredients but also by its physical consistency. This is especially notable given that both Median and Bamboo Salt toothpastes share a calcium carbonate base, yet demonstrate different outcomes. Moreover, although Colgate, Bamboo Salt, and Sensodyne differ in composition, their performance did not vary significantly, suggesting that rheological properties such as flow and adherence may play a more decisive role than the formulation itself. Direct measurement of these properties should be considered in future investigations to better elucidate their role in dentin desensitisation.

Overall, the findings of this study illustrate how different desensitising agents influence dentinal permeability in real time, with all toothpaste formulations achieving reductions comparable to the sodium fluoride varnish under the conditions tested. Although distinct patterns of fluid-flow reduction were observed, these results should be interpreted within the experimental context. The evaluation focused on immediate effects following application and was conducted using an in vitro bovine dentin model under controlled conditions, without the influence of saliva, biofilm, or mechanical brushing. In addition, distilled water was used as the control medium rather than deionised water. This may influence ionic interactions at the dentin surface, although such effects are expected to be minimal under the short-term measurement conditions employed. The NFMD offers high temporal resolution and sensitivity for detecting subtle changes in dentinal fluid flow; however, it is limited to in vitro application and requires precise specimen preparation. While in vitro findings cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical outcomes, the observed differences in tubule occlusion provide mechanistic insight relevant to hypersensitivity management. Future studies should therefore assess the durability of tubule occlusion over time, including resistance to acidic and abrasive challenges, the influence of oral environmental factors, and validation under clinically relevant conditions using human dentin.

Conclusion

Desensitising toothpastes effectively reduced dentinal permeability to levels comparable to professionally-delivered sodium fluoride varnish, demonstrating formulation-dependent differences among desensitising agents. Through real-time fluid flow measurement, the NFMD provided functional validation of dentinal tubule occlusion beyond surface morphology.
