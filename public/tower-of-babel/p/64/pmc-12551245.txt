Knowledge, attitude, and practice on mushroom poisoning among residents of Jilin province
Authors: Xiuxian Zang, Wanling Xu, Dahai Xu, Lei Chen, Haixia Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMushroom poisoning is a significant public health concern, with diverse species causing various degrees of toxicity. This study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) towards mushroom poisoning among the population in Jilin Province.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among the general population in Jilin province between August 2023 and September 2023. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect demographic information and KAP.ResultsAmong 503 valid collected questionnaires, 331 (65.8%) were filled out by women and 188 (37.4%) by individuals aged 31–40. The median (25% quartile and 75% quartile) knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 11 (6, 15) (possible range: 0–22), 37 (32, 42) (possible range: 10–50), and 24 (22, 26) (possible range: 10–30), respectively. Correlation analysis showed significant positive correlations between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.110, P = 0.014), as well as attitude and practice (r = 0.512, P < 0.001). Conversely, although a negative correlation was observed between knowledge and practice (r = -0.035), this relationship was not statistically significant (P = 0.439). Multivariate logistic regression showed that never eating wild mushrooms (OR = 4.413, 95% CI: 2.858, 6.816, P < 0.001) and not experiencing food poisoning (OR = 3.113, 95% CI: 1.208, 8.025, P = 0.019) were independently associated with the practice.ConclusionsThe study found that the general population in Jilin Province demonstrated moderate knowledge levels, with only 10.93% of participants achieving adequate scores (≥ 80% of the total score). Attitudes were relatively positive, with 29.42% of participants scoring ≥ 80%, but practice remained suboptimal, as only 26.04% of participants achieved proactive practice scores (≥ 80%). Significant positive correlations were observed between knowledge and attitude, and between attitude and practice. Background

Mushrooms, prized for their nutrition and taste, have gained widespread popularity due to their nutritional and medicinal properties [1, 2]. There are over 22,000 known mushroom species; however, only a few are edible [3]. China is home to more than 500 species of wild mushrooms, some 400 of which are considered safe for consumption after proper processing, but over 30 species remain highly toxic and resistant to detoxification even through high-temperature methods [4, 5]. Due to the deceptive similarity in appearance between toxic and edible mushrooms, accidental ingestion may occur, resulting in a significant number of mushroom poisoning cases annually. Mushroom poisoning causes an annual average of approximately 70 deaths, constituting the leading public health threat among food poisoning dangers in China [6].

Yunnan Province stands out as the region most severely affected by mushroom poisoning. From 2015 to 2020, Yunnan reported a total of 3,428 incidents of mushroom poisoning, involving 13,931 individuals and 180 fatalities. The mortality rate reached 1.3%, with the number of deaths accounting for 60.2% of all food poisoning-related deaths in the province during the same period [7, 8]. In addition, according to Zhang et al., although the poisoning incidents caused by accidental ingestion of poisonous mushrooms accounted for only 13.1% of the total food poisoning reports from 2004 to 2013, the fatality rate was as high as 42.86% [9]. Mushroom poisonings constitute a global health concern, with verified or suspected toxic fungi consumption often necessitating emergency department admissions and presenting educational challenges for healthcare professionals [10]. The complexity arises from the vast array of species with toxic properties, the difficulties in species identification by both patients and hospital staff, and the concurrent ingestion of multiple mushroom species in meals, making clinical management and prevention of mushroom poisoning a critical area for improvement [11].

The knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) survey is a valuable diagnostic research tool in the context of health literacy, offering insights into the understanding, beliefs, and actions of a particular group on a specific subject. This approach is grounded in the belief that knowledge positively influences attitudes, shaping behaviors [12–14]. Wild mushrooms are frequently foraged in regions such as Jilin Province, endowed with abundant forest resources, particularly during the warm and rainy summer, when these fungi flourish [15]. Consequently, the risk of mushroom poisoning incidents increases. Given the wide variety of wild mushroom species, a significant deficiency in accurate knowledge and public awareness is highly expected. This knowledge gap can give rise to misconceptions, such as the incorrect belief in the high nutritional value of wild mushrooms while neglecting their potential toxicity. Notably, no prior studies have explored this specific area. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the KAP towards mushroom poisoning among the population in Jilin Province.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among the general population in Jilin Province between August 2023 and September 2023. The inclusion criteria encompassed the resident population of Jilin Province. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with mental illness, terminal illness, illiteracy, minors, those whose legal guardians declined participation, and those with cognitive impairment. Respondents who answered negatively to the participant question, those with response times < 80 s or > 1800 s (single-choice questions were calculated to take approximately 2 s to comprehend and respond, while multiple-choice questions required a minimum of 3 s to read, select, and confirm each option), those who failed to complete the entire questionnaire, or those who provided identical responses for all items except basic demographic information were also excluded.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Bethune Hospital of Jilin University (23K158-001), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Procedures

The questionnaire was developed based on previously published literature [11, 16–18]. After these modifications, minor adjustments were made, resulting in a favorable Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.819 and a KMO value of 0.884. The final questionnaire utilized in this study was in Chinese and comprised four distinct dimensions (see Questionnaire). A cover letter was included. The cover letter assured participants of confidentiality, emphasized the non-intrusive nature of the survey, and provided an option for withdrawal, reinforcing ethical considerations in participant involvement. The first dimension included 11 questions related to the participants’ demographic information. The second dimension focused on knowledge and consisted of 11 questions, with a scoring system that assigned 1 point for a correct answer and 0 points for unclear or incorrect responses; questions assessing the depth of knowledge comprehension received scores in the range of 0 to 2 points based on the level of understanding. The third dimension explored attitudes and featured 10 questions, all evaluated on a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 points, reflecting the degree of agreement. Finally, the fourth dimension, centered on practice, encompassed 12 questions, with scores varying from 1 to 3 points, depending on the extent of action taken; noteworthily, questions 1 and 12 were open-ended and did not entail score assignment. Participants achieving scores > 80% of the total were categorized as possessing adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice [19].

Data collection

This study used a stratified random sampling method. The stratification was based on the nine prefecture-level administrative areas in Jilin Province, including Changchun, Jilin, Siping, Liaoyuan, Tonghua, Songyuan, Baishan City, Baicheng, and Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture. A total of 18 regions were selected across nine prefecture-level administrative areas in Jilin Province. These included four regions in Changchun (Chaoyang District, Nong’an, Shuangyang, and Jiutai), four in Jilin City (Changyi District, Yongji County, Panshi City, and Huadian City), one in Siping (Tiexi District), two in Tonghua (Meihekou and Tonghua County), one in Liaoyuan (Dongfeng), one in Songyuan (Changling), two in Baishan (Hunjiang District and Linjiang), two in Baicheng (Taonan and Da’an), and one in Yanbian (Yanji). Based on historical epidemiological data pertaining to mushroom poisoning in Jilin Province, weighted sampling was employed in high-risk areas such as Jilin City, Tonghua City, and Baishan City, where mushroom poisoning incidents are more prevalent. In these areas, we increased the sample proportion to capture more individuals with relevant experiences.

Random sampling process: Within each stratum, municipalities or residential areas were first numbered and then selected using computer-generated random numbers. Within the selected communities, individuals were chosen randomly. The sample size for each administrative region was proportional to the distribution of mushroom poisoning incidents, ensuring that the sample reflects the actual epidemiological characteristics of the region.

Questionnaire distribution

Questionnaire Star (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd), a professional online survey software platform, was used to design and create a survey link. An online survey was constructed using the WeChat-based Questionnaire Star applet, and a QR code was generated to collect data via WeChat. Participants logged in and completed the survey by scanning the QR code sent via WeChat. In order to ensure the quality and completeness of the survey results, each IP address could only be used once, and all items needed to be filled out.

Feasibility analysis and sensitivity analysis

A feasibility analysis was conducted using a median split based on the total scores, categorizing samples into high (≥ 71 points) and low (< 71 points) groups. The difficulty indices were calculated by averaging the percentages of correct responses for each item between the high and low-scoring groups. All item difficulty indices fell within the acceptable range [0.2, 0.9], with a minimum value of 0.22 and a maximum value of 0.77, indicating appropriate difficulty levels for all items.

To avoid the potential impact of wild vegetable consumption on the overall KAP correlations, item 3 in the practice dimension was removed. Pearson’s correlation analysis was then conducted to evaluate the robustness of the KAP correlations.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used for statistical analysis. First, the normal distribution test was performed (Table S1, Fig. 1), and continuous variables were described using the median (IQR). To compare the differences in dimension scores between respondents with different demographic characteristics, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was utilized for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis analysis of variance was used for continuous variables with three or more groups. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%). Spearman correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlations between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores. In multivariate analysis, the median score served as the cut-off value. Univariate variables with P < 0.1 were included in multivariate regression. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and KAP score

Among the initially collected 548 questionnaires, 45 questionnaires were excluded due to the following: 34 disagreed with the use of the collected data for scientific research, 9 had response time < 80 s or > 1,800 s, and 2 had all the same answers to all the questions on the questionnaires. Finally, 503 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis, reaching a validity rate of 91.79%. Among them, 331(65.8%) were completed by women and 306 (60.8%) by those with an education in a junior college or bachelor’s degree. A total of 297 (59.0%) participants reported they had ever eaten wild mushrooms, with 473 (94.0%) not experiencing food poisoning. Also, 431(85.7%) lived in urban areas, and 231 (45.9%) stated they had access to wild mushrooms near their living area (Table 1).

Baseline sheet

The median (IQR) knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 11 (6, 15), 37 (32, 42) and 24 (22, 26), respectively. To further evaluate the proportion of participants who demonstrated a high level of mastery, we set a benchmark of ≥ 80% of the total score for each dimension. Based on this criterion, the proportions of participants scoring ≥ 80% in knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions were 10.93% (55 participants), 29.42% (148 participants), and 26.04% (131 participants), respectively. For the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions, the percentage of people scoring above the median was 54.9%, 53.9%, and 51.7%, respectively (Table 2). Knowledge scores differed significantly by age (P < 0.001), education (P = 0.010), occupation (P < 0.001), and income (P = 0.031). For example, participants aged 41–50 scored higher in knowledge (median 13, IQR 8–16), while those over 60 had lower scores (median 8.5, IQR 3–12). Attitudes varied by age (P = 0.040), gender (P = 0.001), and education (P = 0.001), with females showing more positive attitudes (median 38, IQR 34–42, P = 0.001). Those who had experienced food poisoning had lower attitude and practice scores (P < 0.001). Eating wild mushrooms was linked to higher knowledge (P = 0.005), but participants who had not eaten wild mushrooms had more positive attitudes (P < 0.001) and practice (P < 0.001). Urban residents showed better scores in knowledge and attitudes (P = 0.047, P < 0.001), and access to wild mushrooms near the residence positively impacted all KAP domains (P < 0.001, P = 0.005, P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 1).

Analysis of the distribution of knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions

Regarding awareness of wild mushrooms, most participants knew that dizziness, nausea (76.3%, K10.1), abdominal pain, bloating, and diarrhea (75.3%, K10.2) were most likely to be the symptom of mushroom poisoning. Over half (53.3%) knew that high-temperature cooking could not remove the toxins from wild mushrooms (K8). Similarly, 52.7% knew that wild mushrooms should not be mixed together (K5) or cooked together (K6). However, 73.6% were uncertain about adequately disposing of poisonous mushrooms (K9). In case of mushroom poisoning, 77.9% of the participants would “Dial 120 and induce vomiting” as the first action to take (K11) (Table S2.1).

A notable proportion of respondents (31.0%) believed that wild mushrooms offered a more delicious flavor (A1), while a similar percentage (26.8%) considered they had higher nutritional value (A2). A significant number (29.6%) remaining neutral on this aspect. On the safety front, 28.5% agreed that once an experienced person has identified mushrooms as non-toxic, they were safe to consume (A3), while a larger group (47.1%) disagreed with this statement. Regarding the perception of toxicity, 51.9% strongly disagreed with the idea that even if a mushroom was poisonous, its toxicity was not significant (A4), and 45.5% strongly disagreed with the notion that, as long as the quantity of consumed wild mushrooms was small, there was generally no harm (A5). Moreover, 53.7% strongly disagreed with the belief that thoroughly washing and cooking wild mushrooms could prevent poisoning (A6). When considering purchasing decisions, 34.8% disagreed that wild mushrooms bought in the market could be safely consumed (A7). A substantial majority (59.6%) strongly disagreed with the notion that mushroom poisoning was a minor issue and not a big deal (A8). Concerns about poisoning after consuming wild mushrooms were expressed by 56.1% of the respondents (A9), and 48% considered eating wild mushrooms a risky behavior (A10) (Table S2.2). Although only 30 participants in our sample had experienced food poisoning, these findings suggest that the cautious attitudes observed are not primarily driven by personal experience but are more likely influenced by external sources such as public health campaigns, media coverage, and prevailing social consensus.

Regarding dietary habits, 65.4% of respondents reported occasional consumption of mushroom-based foods (P1), while 52.9% mentioned occasional consumption of wild mushrooms (P2). Regarding foraging for wild vegetables (P3), 56.7% reported never engaging in this practice. Similarly, 56.7% revealed that they never actively sought or purchased wild mushrooms (P4). In addition, 48.5% who expressed reluctance to experiment with various mushroom-based dishes (P5), while 50.9% disclosed that they occasionally consumed other wild plants (P6). Regarding proactive learning about the risks of wild mushroom poisoning (P7), 53.9% indicated occasional engagement in such education. Of the total sample, 85.7% (n = 431) were urban residents and 14.3% (n = 72) were rural residents. Given the significant differences in knowledge and attitudes between these groups, this overall proportion may largely reflect the behavior of the urban majority in our sample. Furthermore, 58.1% reported occasionally trying new dishes (P8). Food safety was a priority for many, as 64.4% frequently paid attention to it in their daily lives (P9). Additionally, 57.3% reported that they often supervised family members and friends to prevent indiscriminate food consumption (P10). Lastly, 49.1% stated that no one in their circle recommended that they try wild foods or plants (P11). Furthermore, 45.5% mentioned having relatives and friends who had not experienced mushroom or wild game poisoning (P12) (Table S2.3). Mobile phone access to the internet was the most common source of knowledge, with 40.2% of respondents relying on it (Table S2.4).

Analysis of factors associated with KAP scores

Correlation analysis showed significant positive correlations between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.110, P = 0.014), as well as attitude and practice (r = 0.512, P < 0.001). Conversely, knowledge and practice were negatively correlated (r = −0.035, P = 0.439) (Table 3).

Univariate factors with a P-value of < 0.1 were included in the multivariate logistic regression, which showed that women (OR = 0.641, 95% CI:0.433, 0.949, P = 0.026), those who lived in rural areas (OR = 0.527, 95% CI: 0.309, 0.900, P = 0.019), and those who lived in areas where no wild mushrooms could be collected (OR = 0.536, 95% CI: 0.354, 0.810, P = 0.003) were independently associated with knowledge (Table 4). Meanwhile, women (OR = 1.938, 95% CI: 1.313, 2.860, P = 0.001) who never consumed wild mushrooms (OR = 2.103, 95% CI: 1.442, 3.068, P < 0.001), have not experienced food poisoning (OR = 2.456, 95% CI: 1.057, 5.708, P = 0.037), and lived in rural areas (OR = 0.425, 95% CI: 0.246, 0.732, P = 0.002) were independently associated with attitude (Table 5). Feasibility and sensitivity analysis of the questionnaire

The feasibility analysis involved categorizing samples into high and low-scoring groups based on a total score median of 71 points. Difficulty indices for each item were computed, all falling within the acceptable range of [0.2, 0.9]. Results indicated satisfactory difficulty levels (0.22 to 0.77). The normality test showed that the difficulty index distribution of all items followed the normal distribution of the mean value of 0.50 and the variance of 0.02, indicating that the difficulty of the scale was moderate, and the difficulty index distribution of each item showed P = 0.841, demonstrating good applicability (Table S3).

To mitigate the influence of dietary habits on practical scores related to the consumption of wild vegetables, we excluded the third question in the practical section. After removing this question, the median and interquartile range for practical scores was 21 (19, 23). Correlation analysis results indicated that removing the question did not significantly alter the correlation coefficients among the three dimensions, as shown in Table S4. The results suggested that the deletion of the specific question had minimal impact on the correlation structure among dimensions, confirming the robustness of our study findings.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal significant gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practice related to mushroom poisoning among the general population in Jilin Province. Knowledge levels were particularly insufficient, with only a small proportion of participants reaching adequate scores. This gap reflects a critical need for public education, particularly for groups such as rural residents and women, who demonstrated lower knowledge levels. Attitudes and practice were similarly suboptimal, emphasizing the importance of targeted interventions. The positive correlation between knowledge and attitudes suggests that improving understanding could lead to more informed attitudes. However, the disconnect between knowledge and practice highlights challenges in translating awareness into behavior. Tailored educational strategies that address specific misconceptions and emphasize actionable practice are essential for improving public safety. Educational programs emphasizing safer practice and immediate medical attention for potential poisoning cases can help mitigate risks.

Promoting knowledge about the safe consumption of mushrooms is essential to reducing mushroom poisoning incidents. In China, the primary health risks associated with toxic mushrooms are acute liver failure, rhabdomyolysis, acute renal failure, and hemolysis, which pose significant threats to the population [20]. In 2021, there were 327 reported cases of mushroom poisoning across 25 provincial regions, involving 923 patients and 20 deaths, reaching a mortality rate of 2.17% [16]. Mushroom poisoning remains a common reason for contacting poison centers, particularly among adults. Our results showed that 45.9% of the participants had easy access to wild mushrooms in their living areas, underscoring the importance of educating the public about mushroom poisoning. Identifying mushroom species remains challenging for the general population, highlighting the need for professional oversight. Despite being a relatively uncommon reason for contacting poison centers, the inspection of wild mushrooms should always be conducted by certified mushroom experts or mycologists to prevent poisoning [21]. Our results also showed that 73.6% of participants had gaps in their knowledge of correctly handling wild mushrooms. The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to address the significant gaps in KAP regarding mushroom poisoning. The general population’s lack of knowledge, negative attitudes, and inactive practice present public health risks, underscoring the need for enhanced health education on this issue [22].

KAP scores related to mushroom poisoning varied significantly across demographic and experiential factors, including age, gender, education, occupation, monthly income, prior food poisoning experience, and whether individuals have consumed wild mushrooms significantly influenced KAP scores. Specifically, those who had experienced food poisoning exhibited lower attitude and practice scores. This may be partly explained by a reduced perception of risk after recovery, leading some individuals to underestimate the potential severity of mushroom poisoning and to adopt less cautious behaviors. In contrast, participants who reported eating wild mushrooms tended to have higher knowledge scores, possibly because they actively sought and acquired more information about mushroom species, identification, and preparation. Conversely, those who had not eaten wild mushrooms demonstrated more positive attitudes and safer practices, likely because they were inherently more cautious and perceived wild mushrooms as risky, thus avoiding behaviors that could lead to poisoning. Women had lower knowledge scores but more positive attitudes, while those living in rural areas demonstrated decreased knowledge but more favorable attitudes.

The distribution of responses to proactive learning behaviors (P7) should also be interpreted in light of residence differences. The finding that 53.9% reported occasional engagement in such education may largely reflect urban residents’ behaviors, as they constituted the majority of the sample and demonstrated significantly higher knowledge (P = 0.047) and more positive attitudes (P < 0.001) compared with rural residents. Our multivariate analysis further identified rural residence as an independent factor associated with lower knowledge and attitude scores. These results suggest that approaches and frequencies of proactive learning may differ by residence, with urban residents relying more on formal sources such as the Internet, while rural residents may engage in more experiential or community-based learning.

Urban residents’ higher scores in knowledge and attitudes may be partly attributable to greater access to educational resources and health information, indicating that rural residents require more targeted educational interventions to improve their KAP levels. In addition, access to wild mushrooms near the residence positively impacted all KAP domains, possibly because easy access to wild mushrooms prompts individuals to actively seek more information about them. Such individuals represent an important target group for KAP education, as they may be more motivated to adopt safer behaviors after receiving relevant training. These findings emphasize the need for tailored interventions targeting different demographic groups to enhance clinical practice and public awareness, and underscore the importance of addressing knowledge gaps and promoting safer practices, especially among those with limited experience with wild mushrooms and those residing in rural areas [23, 24]. Specifically, interventions should be tailored to address the knowledge gaps identified in different demographic groups and focus on fostering safer practice, especially among those with limited experience with wild mushrooms and those residing in rural areas [25, 26].

Participants displayed limited knowledge about identifying poisonous mushrooms, types of toxic mushrooms in Jilin Province, and safe handling of wild mushrooms. In addition, the study results showed that medical professionals and high-income people had a clearer understanding of wild mushrooms but no corresponding advantages in attitude and practice, which may be because this knowledge did not lead to the right attitude and positive practice. Misconceptions surrounding mushroom toxicity and disposal pose significant public health risks. Our study underscores the urgent need for comprehensive educational initiatives aimed at the general public, focusing on mushroom identification and safe consumption practice [27, 28]. Additionally, healthcare providers should be equipped with the knowledge required to recognize the diverse symptoms associated with mushroom poisoning [29, 30], enabling prompt and accurate diagnosis and treatment. Collaboration between healthcare institutions, local authorities, and non-governmental organizations is critical to addressing knowledge gaps and improving community safety [31, 32].

The findings of the present study revealed a concerning set of attitudes and beliefs that could potentially impact clinical practice in the context of mushroom poisoning. Many individuals held misconceptions about wild mushrooms, believing in their safety when consumed in small quantities or thoroughly washed and cooked. Furthermore, a notable portion of the participants believed that even if a mushroom was poisonous, its toxicity was insignificant, and mushroom poisoning posed a minor issue. Such attitudes can potentially lead to risky behaviors and a lack of caution when handling and consuming wild mushrooms. To improve clinical practice in addressing mushroom poisoning, it is imperative to address these attitudes through educational initiatives emphasizing the potential dangers associated with wild mushrooms and promoting safer practice [11, 30]. Healthcare professionals should lead efforts in raising awareness about the risks and advocating for cautious, evidence-based practice, while public health campaigns should target these misconceptions to reduce the incidence of mushroom poisoning cases [29, 33].

The practice dimension illustrated participants’ behaviors and knowledge sources regarding mushroom consumption. Evidently, many individuals consumed mushroom-based foods and wild mushrooms, with some even actively foraging for wild vegetables. This behavior, combined with their willingness to try different mushroom-based foods and new dishes, highlights the need to ensure they are well-informed about mushroom toxicity. Most participants were open to education on wild mushroom risks due to their concern for food safety. Many have also taken proactive measures to prevent indiscriminate consumption by family and friends, reflecting a sense of responsibility. Individuals who had not experienced food poisoning scored higher in attitudes and practice, underscoring the importance of positive attitudes and good practice in public health. These findings further suggest that promoting knowledge alone is insufficient; emphasis on positive attitudes and practice is critical. Initiatives should harness this willingness to learn and focus on shifting public attitudes. In order to improve clinical practice and reduce the incidence of mushroom poisoning cases, initiatives should focus on harnessing this willingness to learn and participate in educational campaigns [29]. Healthcare professionals can assume a pivotal role by disseminating information on the dangers associated with wild mushrooms while leveraging various knowledge sources such as the Internet and expert consultations [11, 34, 35]. Collaboration with these engaged individuals within communities experienced in advising others regarding food choices can also serve as a valuable means of spreading awareness [36].

The correlation analysis results revealed positive relationships between knowledge, attitude, and practice in the context of mushroom poisoning. Notably, a positive correlation was found between knowledge and attitude, suggesting that individuals with a better understanding of mushroom toxicity tend to hold more cautious and informed attitudes. Similarly, a strong positive correlation between attitude and practice indicated that individuals with favorable attitudes toward safe mushroom consumption tended to exhibit corresponding safe practice. However, the negative correlation between knowledge and practice, albeit not statistically significant, indicated a disconnect between knowledge and the translation of that knowledge into actual practice. Bridging this gap between knowledge and practice is crucial to improving clinical practice and public safety. Initiatives should focus on increasing knowledge and encouraging individuals to translate their understanding and positive attitudes into safer practice [37, 38].

The present study has some limitations, including a cross-sectional design, which prevents the establishment of causal relationships. Self-administered questionnaires may introduce response bias, and there is a potential for recall bias as the data relies on self-reported information. The study was conducted in a specific geographic area (Jilin Province), limiting its generalizability to other regions. Additionally, the study did not consider the influence of socio-economic factors, such as economic and educational levels, which may correlate with behaviors and the incidence of mushroom poisoning. Future studies should address these regional and socio-economic factors to provide more comprehensive insights into KAP related to mushroom poisoning.

Conclusions

The study revealed significant gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practice regarding mushroom poisoning among the general population in Jilin Province. Participants demonstrated limited understanding of mushroom toxicity, particularly in rural areas and among women, who had lower knowledge scores but more favorable attitudes. The disconnect between knowledge and safe practice further highlights the need for targeted interventions. To improve public health outcomes and clinical practice, educational campaigns should focus on enhancing knowledge about mushroom toxicity, promoting safer practice, and encouraging immediate medical attention in case of poisoning. Tailored strategies are especially important for rural residents and individuals with limited experience in consuming wild mushrooms, aiming to reduce the risk of mushroom poisoning in Jilin Province.
