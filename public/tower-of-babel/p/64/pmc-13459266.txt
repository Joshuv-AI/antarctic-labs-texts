Attitudes toward bereavement support and associated factors in the Japanese population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Akari Uno, Keishi Soga, Tomohiro Uchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecently, public health approaches are increasingly recognized as crucial to bereavement support, and many initiatives based on grief literacy and compassionate community movement are being developed. In this context, this study aimed to examine adults’ attitudes toward bereavement support and their related factors considering regional and individual characteristics to provide a foundation for examining community-based bereavement support.MethodsA web-based survey was conducted from October to November 2024 among adults living in Japan, with 3,378 participants included in the analysis. The survey measured attitudes toward bereavement support, psychological connections with community, and individual help-seeking styles. We employed descriptive statistics to examine attitudes toward bereavement support and multiple regression analyses to examine the association between attitudes toward bereavement support and regional and individual characteristics, categorized by residential area scale (large, medium-sized, and small cities).ResultsThe results of this survey indicate that while awareness of and experience with bereavement support in Japan are limited, a certain degree of interest and perceived applicability of such support is present. Several factors associated with attitudes toward bereavement support were found to be common across residential areas, particularly the extent of thinking about death. Psychological connections with community and help-seeking styles differed depending on the scale of the residential area.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that attitudes toward bereavement support are influenced by both individual psychological characteristics and community context, with patterns of association differing according to residential area. Region-sensitive approaches to bereavement support and foundational evidence for its development in Japan are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04988-3.

Background

Although the need for bereavement support is well-recognized, a substantial gap persists between the support systems currently available and the actual needs of bereaved individuals [1]. Addressing this gap has increasingly been framed as a public health challenge rather than solely as an issue of individual clinical intervention [2–4]. Population-based cohort studies have demonstrated that the death of a spouse is associated with an increased risk of subsequent mortality [5], and that differing trajectories of grief are linked to long-term physical and psychological health outcomes [6]. These findings suggest that grief is not merely a problem confined to certain individuals but a phenomenon that can impact beyond individuals and families, underscoring the importance of addressing it from a public health perspective. The report titled, “The Value of Death” published by the Lancet Commission, recommends moving away from an overly medicalized approach to death and reintegrating social and community elements into expert care [7]. To achieve this, engaging communities, strengthening collaboration with healthcare systems, promoting public education and awareness, and enhancing empowerment have become key international challenges [3, 8].

The background to these movements is the concept of compassionate communities proposed by sociologist Alan Kellehear [9, 10]. This concept applies the principles of public health outlined in the Ottawa Charter to palliative care, positioning serious illness, death, caregiving, and grief as issues that concern everyone, and seeks to build mutual support networks within communities. Based on this concept, new theoretical models have been proposed for bereavement support. For example, Abel et al. presented the “New Essentials Model,” which incorporates a community development perspective [2]. Meanwhile, Aoun et al. proposed an intervention framework tailored to the need for support from a public health perspective [11–13]. In this global context, the concept of “grief literacy” has emerged as a framework for understanding how communities can build capacity to respond to loss [14]. Under the frameworks of compassionate communities and grief literacy, a variety of initiatives addressing death and loss have been widely implemented through the participation of local community members [15–18].

Previous empirical research on bereavement support has primarily focused on bereaved family members, examining their needs [13, 19], effectiveness of support [12, 20], barriers to receiving support [11, 21], and process of social network formation [22]. For example, Aoun et al. analyzed support needs according to grief risk levels, revealing that while support from family and friends was central to low-risk groups, specialized support was sought by medium- and high-risk groups [13]. However, few studies have focused on the attitudes of the general public beyond those of bereaved individuals, and foundational data on public-level attitudes are lacking. Accordingly, the present study focuses on “attitudes toward bereavement support” in the general population.

In this study, “attitude toward bereavement support” is operationally defined as an individual’s overall evaluation of receiving bereavement support. In social psychology, an attitude is defined as “a psychological tendency that is expressed by evaluating a particular entity with some degree of favor or disfavor” [23] and is understood as an evaluative disposition toward a specific object. In addition, the tripartite model, which conceptualizes attitudes in terms of cognitive, affective, and behavioral components, is recognized as a classical framework in attitude research [24]. The cognitive component refers to beliefs, knowledge, and evaluations about an object of an attitude, whereas the affective component refers to emotional responses toward that object. By contrast, the behavioral component includes actual behaviors, behavioral tendencies, and behavioral intentions toward the object. This study conceptualizes attitudes in terms of awareness (cognitive), interest (affective), and applicability (behavioral). Although these components may overlap conceptually, we distinguish them according to their primary psychological functions. Awareness is considered a cognitive state since it is attained through mental reflection. Differently, interest is treated as an affective component, as it can function as an emotion that motivates approach behavior toward a target [25], whereas perceived applicability is regarded as reflecting behavioral intention to use support when needed.

In Japan, the terms “grief care” and “bereavement care” are being increasingly recognized in recent years [26]. However, bereavement support policies have developed piecemeal across specific contexts, such as cancer care and suicide prevention [27, 28], and have not yet been integrated into a comprehensive national bereavement support strategy. Furthermore, in Japan, death has traditionally been associated with the concept of “kegare” (impurity), rooted in Shinto beliefs. Consequently, death has often been treated as a taboo in everyday life, and a tendency to avoid discussing death exists [29]. This cultural context may contribute to individuals’ psychological resistance to seeking support, as well as to low awareness of the existence of bereavement support itself. In previous studies targeting general university students in Japan, only about 3–5% of respondents reported recognizing the term “grief care” [30, 31]. In additon, a nationwide survey of 1,000 Japanese adults found that only 9.9% reported knowing theterm to some extent, whereas 76.7% reported that they were unfamiliar with both the term and its meaning [32]. These findings suggest that public awareness of bereavement support remains limited in Japan. These factors highlight the need to examine public attitudes toward bereavement support across a broad adult population, regardless of bereavement experience.

Whether bereavement support is sought or received depends on individual, social, and geographical levels of acceptance [33], including various intertwined factors. At the individual level, perceptions and beliefs about bereavement support can discourage people from using support services [34]. Simultaneously, such perceptions are shaped within broader social and regional contexts. Community structure [35], social relationships [36], and attitudes toward death [37] vary substantially between urban and rural areas. These contextual factors suggest that attitudes toward bereavement support are not formed solely at the individual level but are embedded in social environments. In particular, geographical distance and social bonds are considered important conditions for the development of grief literacy [14], indicating the need to consider psychological connections with community when examining public attitudes toward bereavement support.

Access to and utilization of bereavement support are influenced by both social factors and individual psychological characteristics. In the context of grief, individuals often hesitate to seek support from others [38], and help-seeking tendencies play a key role in determining access to support [39]. Help-seeking behavior refers to individuals’ tendencies to seek assistance when facing difficulties and is considered a relatively stable characteristic [40].

Based on the above, this study aims to examine attitudes toward bereavement support and their associated factors among the general adult population in Japan, with particular attention to regional characteristics of residence, psychological connections with community, and help-seeking styles. Previous research on bereavement support has primarily been based on a multi-level public health model and has examined a wide range of support needs, from specialized to everyday support, in relation to the severity of grief and associated risk factors [12, 13, 41]. By contrast, this study focuses on psychosocial factors, including individuals’ connections with their communities and help-seeking tendencies, as well as the population size of residential areas.

In Japan, a comprehensive national system for bereavement support has yet to be fully established. Consequently, support is fragmented and decentralized, with local governments and community-based organizations playing central roles. Therefore, population size can be understood not merely as an administrative classification but as a contextual factor reflecting differences in community structure, resource distribution, and social networks. By examining these factors, this study provides foundational evidence for future discussions on community-based bereavement support in Japan.

Methods

Participants and procedures

From October to November 2024, an online survey was conducted among adults residing in Japan who were registered as panel members with a web-based survey company (Cross Marketing Inc.). The panel consists of individuals who have been recruited through various online channels to participate in surveys.

For this survey, 4,297 men and women aged 18 years or older (legal adults) currently living in Japan were recruited. All participants read a comprehensive explanation of the research procedures and provided informed consent. Although participants were asked about their experiences with bereavement as part of their demographic information, this was not used as an inclusion criterion.

Participants received reward points as compensation upon completion of the survey. We refer to the city classifications established by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications [42]. Thus, we divided the scale of the residential areas into three categories, allocated respondents such that the response rates for each category would be similar, and collected the responses.

We employed a Directed Questions Scale (DQS) to ensure the quality of the survey responses [43]. This method is widely used for screening respondents for excessive carelessness. Among the 4,297 responses, data with incorrect DQS responses and the nine cases where gender was not specified were excluded (in order to investigate gender-specific effects), and 3,378 respondents (2,087 males and 1,291 females, with an average age of 55.78 ± 12.76 years) were included in the analysis.

Measurements

The questionnaire consisted of basic attributes, items related to bereavement support, psychological connections with community, and items on help-seeking styles. Several terms are used to refer to bereavement support in Japan [44], but grief care is the most widely used. Therefore, it was used consistently throughout this questionnaire.

Demographic variables

The surveyed attributes included age, gender, and residential area (1) large cities: designated cities and special wards; (2) medium-sized cities: cities with a population of 100,000 or more, excluding those in Category 1; and (3) small cities: municipalities with a population of less than 100,000), marital status, parental status (whether they have children), the highest level of education, and bereavement experience. Regarding bereavement experiences, participants were not asked to specify their relationship with the deceased; instead, they were instructed to conceptualize bereavement as “the experience of losing someone important to you.”

Items related to bereavement support

Items related to bereavement support included (1) awareness (whether one is aware of the existence of bereavement support), (2) interest (whether one is interested in bereavement support), (3) applicability (whether one would like to use bereavement support in the future; each using a three-point scale, with scores of 2 = yes, 1 = unsure [have heard of it but do not know the details], and 0 = no), (4) experience of receiving bereavement support, (5) and experience of a close person receiving bereavement support (each using a two-point scale). Additionally, the extent to which participants think about death in their daily lives was measured as an (6) extent of thinking about death using a five-point scale (from “Often” to “Not at all”). The item was phrased as follows: “Do you ever think about your own death or the death of those close to you?” Higher scores indicate a tendency to think about death more frequently. Each of the six items above consists of a single question.

In this study, the total score for awareness, interest, and applicability was used as an index of “attitude toward bereavement support.” These three items were constructed based on the tripartite model of attitude theory, and their sum was treated as a composite indicator reflecting the overall evaluative orientation toward bereavement support. In this study, a high score on this index is interpreted as indicating a tendency toward a more active orientation toward bereavement support. Although this index was constructed as a composite indicator, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as a reference to assess internal consistency (α = 0.61). Direct and indirect experiences of bereavement support may influence attitudes; therefore, items (4) and (5) were included as related factors [45]. In addition, (6) was measured as a psychological factor reflecting individuals’ orientation toward and interest in death. Previous research has shown that lower resistance to death is associated with more positive attitudes toward end-of-life care [46]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that engaging in open discussions about death, such as through participation in Death Cafés, may contribute to the development of grief literacy [47]. Based on these findings, this item was included as an indicator reflecting individuals’ orientation toward death.

At the beginning of the questionnaire, the following explanation was provided to ensure a common understanding of bereavement support:

Grief care refers to support for people who are grieving the death of a loved one. It is sometimes called ‘Bereavement care’ or ‘Care for the bereaved.’ Grief care includes various approaches, such as professional treatment and counseling by medical professionals and self-help group and bereavement support groups (where bereaved family members talk with each other), and so on.

Furthermore, to aid respondents’ understanding, although not included in the analysis, the following options regarding experiences with grief care were presented:

(1) Treatment at a medical institution, (2) Counseling, (3) Self-help/ support group (bereavement support groups, sharing, etc.), (4) Other professional support (nursing, caregiving, funeral services, etc.), (5) Support from religious institutions or clergy, (6) Support from people close to you, (7) Other.

Psychological connections with community

We used the Community Commitment Scale to measure psychological connections with community. This scale was developed in Japan [48]. This scale measures psychological attachment to the community and the degree of socialization, comprising two factors: “socialization” and “belonging.” Participants were asked to respond to eight items on a four-point Likert scale.

Help-seeking styles

To measure the style of help-seeking, the Help-Seeking Style Scale was used. This scale was developed in Japan [40]. This scale indicates qualitative differences in the tendency to seek help from others when faced with difficulties and consists of three factors: “excessive,” “avoidance,” and “self-directed.” Each factor is measured using four items on a seven-point scale. Higher scores indicated a stronger tendency.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shokei Gakuin University (Approval No.: 2024-019). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to understand the attitudes toward bereavement support among the general adult population in Japan. Furthermore, to identify the characteristics of the factors associated with attitudes toward bereavement support by residential area, a multiple regression analysis was conducted with attitudes toward bereavement support as the dependent variable stratified by residential area. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Windows software version 28.0. All tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at α = .05.

Regarding prior experience of bereavement, 73.0% of respondents reported having experienced bereavement, while 27.0% reported not having experienced bereavement. Across all residential areas, being female, having experiences of bereavement and bereavement support (both personal and involving close others), and having a higher extent of thinking about death were positively associated with attitudes toward bereavement support. In addition, a self-directed help-seeking style was consistently positively associated with attitudes toward bereavement support across all regions.

In contrast, some factors showed associations specific to residential context. In large cities, a sense of belonging and an excessive help-seeking style were positively associated with attitudes toward bereavement support, whereas in medium-sized cities, socializing was positively associated with attitudes toward bereavement support. Young age was positively associated with attitudes toward bereavement support in large and small cities, while the highest level of education showed a positive association in large and medium-sized cities.

Discussion

This study examined attitudes toward bereavement support among the general adult population in Japan and explored factors associated with residential area. The results indicated that, although awareness of bereavement support remains limited, there exists a certain level of interest in and perceived applicability of bereavement support. Regarding the factors related to the scale of the residential area, while many factors were common to all three areas, individuals’ psychological connections to their communities and help-seeking styles clearly differed depending on the scale of the area. These results suggest that both community and individual characteristics must be considered when developing approaches to community-based bereavement support.

First, the awareness rate of bereavement support in this study was only 16.3%, with only 5.9% of respondents understanding the content of the support, and the experience rate was extremely low at 1.7%. Compared with the awareness levels of approximately 3–10% reported in previous Japanese studies [30–32], the level of awareness observed in this study was relatively higher when superficial understanding was included.

Previous studies conducted in other countries have reported differences in utilization rates according to bereavement risk level: 52% among individuals at high risk for mental illness following bereavement, compared with 18% among those at low risk [49]. In addition, reports indicate that more than half of low-risk bereaved individuals seek emotional support from family and friends [12, 13, 20]. In Japan, such support may be provided informally in close relationships, even without a formal attitude toward it, which could explain the low awareness and experience rates observed in this study. In other words, the recognition that support from close others is part of bereavement support is not yet a widely shared in Japan. This lack of recognition may contribute to stigma and lower willingness to engage with formal bereavement support. Consequently, when faced with situations requiring specialized support, there is a risk of being unable to access appropriate services or spending a significant amount of time waiting to access them. To address these challenges and enhance the continuity and accessibility of support across a continuum ranging from specialized care and organized support provided by self-help and support groups to informal support, the role of local communities is considered extremely important [2]. Improving “grief literacy”, the widespread dissemination of knowledge and skills related to support is an effective strategy to achieve this [18].

Factors related to attitudes toward bereavement were identified as common and different, depending on the scale of the residential area. First, the factors common to all regions in this study were being female, experience of bereavement, experience of receiving bereavement support, experience of a close person receiving bereavement support, and the extent of thinking about death. Furthermore, Being younger was significantly associated with attitudes toward bereavement support in large and small cities, whereas having a higher level of education was significantly associated with such attitudes in large and medium-sized cities. These findings are partially consistent with prior research showing that being female and higher educational attainment are associated with greater use of bereavement support services [49, 50]. Therefore, this study’s results suggest some overlap between the characteristics of individuals who have received bereavement support and those who hold more favorable attitudes toward such support.

Additionally, in this study, the extent of thinking about death was most strongly associated with attitudes toward bereavement support across all regions. This indicates that daily reflection on and attitudes toward death play a decisive role in increasing sensitivity and interest in bereavement support. The depth of reflection on death has excellent potential to improve individuals’ literacy regarding death and grief [47], and attempts have been made to enhance individuals’ attitudes toward death through initiatives such as Death Cafés [15, 47, 51, 52]. The findings suggest that fostering more reflective attitudes toward death may be associated with greater engagement with bereavement support. Therefore, approaches that encourage reflection on death may be linked to more positive attitudes toward bereavement support in the general population.

Different patterns of association were observed across residential areas between attitudes toward bereavement support and both psychological connections with community and help-seeking styles. First, with respect to psychological connections with community, “belonging” was significantly associated with the outcome in large cities, while “socialization” was significantly associated with the outcome in medium-sized cities. However, no such significant association was observed in small cities. Large cities have a notably higher population density than rural areas, which preserves anonymity but also tends to weaken close relationships [36]. Individuals with a strong sense of community belonging tend to actively engage in interpersonal relationships and community activities even in such environments [53], which in turn facilitates access to information and resources within the community. Consequently, such individuals are more likely to have positive attitudes toward support after bereavement.

In medium-sized cities, “socialization,” or daily social exchanges, was associated with attitudes toward support after bereavement. Having close ties with neighbors within a community is a predictor of mutual aid attitudes [54]. Therefore, the accumulation of experiences of close interpersonal connections within the community may be linked to interest and expectations regarding support after bereavement.

Rural areas, which are often incorporated into small cities, tend to have a higher level of willingness to help neighbors, a sense of a close-knit community, and greater trust among neighbors than urban areas [55]. Hence, mutual support among neighbors may be internalized as a natural part of daily life, which could explain why no significant association was observed in this study. In fact, in some rural areas of Japan, neighbors assisting the bereaved family in funeral arrangements is customary; this practice is viewed as a natural part of life.

Regarding aid request styles, the “self-directed” style was significant in all regions, and the “excessive” style was significantly associated with attitudes toward bereavement support in large cities. When individuals tend toward a self-directed style, they tend to avoid dependence on others and actively choose aid when they judge it to be beneficial for themselves [40]. The unusual experience of losing a loved one imposes severe stress. It serves as a significant risk factor for mental health issues such as depression [56, 57], and it was suggested that many individuals are highly willing to seek support when necessary.

The “excessive” type, which tends to easily request assistance even when the problem is not serious and could be dealt with on one’s own [40], was only found in large cities. Some reports suggest that access to bereavement support is equitable between urban and rural areas [58]. Nonetheless, there are regional variations in the specialization, level of support, and awareness of bereavement support [58, 59]. In large cities, individual beliefs and environmental conditions (such as the relative ease of accessing support from a wide range of options) are thought to be related to the attitude toward bereavement support.

The findings hold some practical implications. In large urban areas, where a sense of community belonging was associated with attitudes toward bereavement support, interventions that strengthen psychological connections to the community, such as creating opportunities to foster a sense of belonging (e.g., local events and participatory community activities) may be effective. In medium-sized cities, where everyday social interactions are associated with attitudes, approaches that promote social engagement through community-based activities (e.g., gatherings aimed at facilitating interactions among residents) may enhance interest in and acceptance of bereavement support. In smaller communities, where mutual support may be embedded in daily life, it may be important to maintain and strengthen existing informal support networks while also fostering a foundation for the acceptance of formal support. In addition, as a self-directed help-seeking style was associated with attitudes across all regions, it may be important to develop environments that enable individuals to access support when needed based on their own judgment. Together, these findings suggest that the interaction between regional characteristics and individual psychosocial factors is associated with attitudes toward receiving bereavement support, and provide foundational insights that complement existing risk-based approaches to bereavement support.

This study had several limitations. First, regional characteristics were defined based on three categories of population size: large cities, medium-sized cities, and small cities. However, differences in the cultural and social backgrounds of each region, attitudes toward death, and access to support resources were not fully taken into account. For example, even within the same small-city area, attitudes toward bereavement support may differ depending on religious rituals and the presence or absence of a culture of mutual support in the community. More detailed regional classification and a qualitative understanding of regional characteristics are required in future research.

Second, although the respondents were asked about their interest in and applicability for bereavement support, using the vignette method, as in the study by Logan et al. [60], may have allowed them to imagine bereavement in greater detail, leading to different responses.

Third, the measurement methods employed in this survey questionnaire have several limitations. For example, the “extent of thinking about death” is based on subjective self-reports, and options such as “never think about it” may depend on the respondent’s interpretation, meaning that equal intervals between response categories are not necessarily guaranteed. Furthermore, the attitude indicator toward bereavement support used in this study is a composite measure designed to comprehensively capture theoretically distinct aspects: cognition, interest, and applicability. In addition, although the term "grief care" used in this study refers to bereavement support in a broad sense, encompassing a range of support from informal support to specialized care, its scope was not clearly defined in the questionnaire. As a result, respondents may have interpreted this term more narrowly, as referring primarily to specialized and organized forms of bereavement support. Therefore,the results of this study may reflect attitudes toward such specialized and organized support rather than attitudes toward bereavement support in its broader sense. Moreover, the questionnaire items in this study were designed for exploratory research and have not undergone sufficient item validation through prior pilot testing or similar procedures. Therefore, caution is required when interpreting the results obtained in this study. In addition, this study examined attitudes toward bereavement support primarily from the perspective of those who receive support. However, within the context of compassionate communities, individuals are not only recipients, but also providers of support. Therefore, future research should adopt a more comprehensive approach that incorporates both perspectives when examining attitudes toward bereavement support.

Fourth, the sampling method used may have limited the representativeness of the findings. Because the participants were recruited from a web survey company’s registered panel, the perspectives of older adults, individuals without Internet access, and socially isolated individuals may not have been adequately represented. Furthermore, the gender distribution of the sample in this study is unbalanced, which may reflect the characteristics of the survey panel and limit the generalizability of the results. Future research should consider combining recruitment methods such as postal surveys and collaborations with local governments to obtain a more diverse and representative sample.

Furthermore, this study aims to provide a basic understanding of attitudes toward bereavement support and does not directly evaluate support for specific public health policies or the appropriateness of resource allocation. Moving forward, it will be necessary to clarify the applicability within a public health framework by conducting cumulative research examining how the attitudes of the general adult population relate to specific interventions and measures within communities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that while awareness of bereavement support was limited, interest and perceived applicability were relatively present. Factors related to attitudes toward bereavement support exhibit distinct relationships depending on the scale of the residential area. Understanding these characteristics provides foundational insights for developing community-based bereavement support strategies appropriate for Japan’s social context.
