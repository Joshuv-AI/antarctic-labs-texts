Multimodal large language models in ultrasound diagnosis of breast masses: a multicenter comparative analysis based on GPT-4o, radiologists, and convolutional neural network (CNN)
Authors: Jia-Qian Yao, Rui Zhang, Ze-Bang Yang, Bo Zhang, Shuang-Quan Jiang, Lin Jiang, Xiao-Er Zhang, Xiao-Yan Xie, Tong-Yi Huang, Ming Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn advanced version of large language model (LLM), ChatGPT 4o (GPT-4o), has shown capacity in image-text pair interpretation, yet the performance in medical image analysis remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic capacity of GPT-4o in breast ultrasound (US) datasets.MethodsUS exams including breast images and original reports were respectively included from January 2021 to December 2023 in three hospitals throughout China. The diagnostic performance in distinguishing benign or malignant breast masses of GPT-4o was assessed through two approaches: image-strategy and image-combined-text-strategy. Fleiss kappa was calculated to determine intra-LLM consistency. Thereafter, diagnostic accuracy was evaluated and compared with the convolutional neural network (CNN) model and 95 human experts with various levels of expertise from 60 institutions in China. Responses from GPT-4o were rated by diagnostic confidence and radiologist’s evaluation.ResultsThe observations of 80 breast masses (37 malignant, 43 benign) from 80 patients [median age, 42.5 years; interquartile range (IQR), 37.0–53.0 years] were enrolled. GPT-4o with image-strategy exhibited a fair consistency [0.25, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.07–0.43], whereas the agreement of image-combined-text-strategy was excellent (0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.91). Diagnostic accuracy improved when deploying the image-combined-text-strategy compared to only image [58% (46 of 80) vs. 70% (56 of 80), P=0.031], which resembled board-certified moderately experienced radiologists (70% [56 of 80] vs. 71% [57 of 80], P=0.450) and customized CNN [70% (56 of 80) vs. 74% (59 of 80), P=0.701]. Heterogeneous Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS)-related signs and ground truth labels affected GPT-4o significantly. Besides, answers from image-combined-text-strategy were associated with higher proportions of correctness and completeness, and lower errors.ConclusionsThe effectiveness of GPT-4o in interpreting real-world US images was limited, yet improved in image-combined-text-strategy. Deploying LLM warrants scrutiny from radiologists.

Introduction

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have showcased impressive general knowledge and reasoning abilities in medical downstream tasks (1-3). The latest advancement of LLMs, ChatGPT 4o (“o” for “omni”, abbreviated as GPT-4o), based on the OpenAI GPT-4 architecture, can integrate both text and image input (4). Leveraging self-supervised learning, the potential deployment of LLMs in the medical care sector has evolved to interpret images, such as label anatomies (5), identify abnormalities (6), and assist in clinical decision-making (7). When applied appropriately, the adaptability and efficiency of ChatGPT could represent a significant leap forward, fostering innovation in diagnostic practices and ensuring a higher standard of care (8).

Although LLMs demonstrate remarkable performance in radiological knowledge and associative reasoning in image analysis, inadequacies include poor multi-step reasoning and limited accuracy in complex clinical scenarios (9,10). Additionally, designed primarily for text-based tasks, LLMs are less effective in analyzing visual information compared to convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which have demonstrated broad success in multi-modality analysis (11). Furthermore, hybrid ensemble learning frameworks could offer performance improvements over single-model approaches (12). Original studies have largely focused on natural language handling capabilities (13-16) and reported relatively limited performance in image analysis (17,18). However, most of them have concentrated on image-based research, neglecting the interaction between LLMs and physicians. To be more specific, an image-combined-text-strategy incorporates image analysis from LLMs and physicians’ interpretation, which creates a synergistic collaborative system to deliver more accurate and tailored medical assistance.

As the application of LLM chatbots continues to expand, timely and comprehensive assessment of their accuracy, strengths, and weaknesses remains essential. Comparative studies between LLMs, human experts with various levels of expertise, as well as CNN models are not currently available in the medical image analysis domain. In this study, we conducted a multi-case study focused on the diagnosis of breast masses based on ultrasound (US) images. Breast cancer imposes a substantial global disease burden, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases in 2020 worldwide (19). Radiologists’ interpretations are subject to inter-reader variability due to heterogeneous manifestations in US images, with false positive rates of 4–39.8% (20). To thoroughly evaluate the reliability and diagnostic accuracy of publicly available LLMs such as GPT-4o in US-based diagnosis of breast masses, we compared GPT-4o’s performance to that of board-certified radiologists with varying levels of expertise and a traditional CNN model. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-92/rc).

Methods

This retrospective multicenter study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. Ethics [2023]842) on 4 December 2023. All participating institutions were informed of the study design and agreed to participate. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study design

ChatGPT 4o (OpenAI, as of 13 May 2024), an advanced version of the ChatGPT chatbots, was used as generative LLMs in this study (4). Overall, two strategies of LLMs were employed: (I) image-strategy: in which the LLM described the characteristics of breast mass outlined in the image firstly, then assigned American College of Radiology Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (ACR BI-RADS) category and generated a diagnosis independently; (II) image-combined-text-strategy: in which the LLM was provided with both image and BI-RADS descriptors from original reports and then created a diagnosis. Considering the variability in responses created by generative LLMs, each prompt was repeated for three rounds. Reliability was defined as the intra-rater consistency of LLM in three outputs. To mitigate memory bias of the LLM chatbot, we restarted the conversation after a round. No extra pretraining was performed specific to radiologic data. Subsequently, a senior radiologist with at least 12 years of experience assessed the response quality for each response. To minimize assessment bias, all responses were anonymized and copied into a vacant document. The senior doctor was blinded to the origin of the response (LLM vs. human) and patient outcomes. Completeness was assessed from 1 (very incomplete) to 5 (very complete), with 3 representing a borderline (neutral) encapsulation. Correctness was rated by how well the content reflected the clinical implications for the patient, rated from 1 (very incorrect) to 5 (very correct). The medically adverse effects from errors were classified as ‘no harm’, ‘commission error’ (incorrectly diagnosing a normal sonogram as containing a malignancy), or ‘omission error’ (incorrectly diagnosing a sonogram with malignancy as normal). Finally, diagnostic performance in identifying benign and malignant breast masses was compared between two strategies of GPT-4o, radiologists with various levels of expertise, and a CNN model (Figure 1).

Examination collection

Exams including breast images and original reports were consecutively evaluated from January 2021 to December 2023 in three tertiary referral hospitals in southeast China (hospital 1: the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University), in north China (hospital 2: China-Japan Friendship Hospital), and in northeast China (hospital 3: the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University), as depicted in Figure 2. The ground truth for malignancies was core needle biopsy or surgery. Benign cases were either confirmed through pathology or required for stability over 3 years. Patients aged 18 years or older who underwent routine or diagnostic breast US examinations were included. Patients were drawn if the reports or images were unqualified. A random selection was performed to achieve a final of 80 eligible observations, which were independent of training data used by GPT-4o. Five BI-RADS-related signs (shape, orientation, margin, posterior features, calcifications in a mass) and overall categories were retrieved and documented from original reports by two radiologists who each had 3 years of experience. Each image referred to an independent breast mass outlined with a rectangle and was deidentified to protect the privacy of patients. No clinical data were provided to the LLM.

Reader enrolment

Our study aimed to include a large scale of readers to enhance a broad representation across locality and levels of expertise. Thus, we recruited 95 board-certified radiologists who commonly assessed patients with breast masses in clinical practice from 60 institutions throughout China. A total of 64 (67.37%) radiologists came from academic hospitals, 29 (27.37%) from community clinics, and 2 (5.26%) from private practice. Among them, 27 (28.42%) were inexperienced radiologists (1–3 years of experience), 23 (24.21%) were moderately experienced radiologists (3–10 years of experience), and 45 (47.37%) were very experienced radiologists (over 10 years of experience) (Table S1). Eligible participants were invited to participate in the reader study through a website-based questionnaire and guided to the study landing webpage via email. On the platform on website, each radiologist was presented with the aforementioned 80 images in random order, without accessibility to the patient’s clinical data or other imaging information. We requested that the radiologists complete the reader study independently at a pace consistent with their usual clinical routine. They were also informed that the study was anonymous, and they could leave the study at any time and return to the point where they left off. The platform automatically recorded the diagnostic choices, benign or malignant.

Artificial intelligence algorithm

To produce the artificial intelligence (AI) decisions of breast masses in US images, the CNN model was developed. We employed classic Resnet18 (21) pre-trained on ImageNet (22) through a 5-fold cross-validation approach, in which the optimal threshold on the validation set was logged. During the training process, the images of the masses were resized with histogram equalization. Common data augmentation techniques are also incorporated. The training code for the CNN model framework was deposited at: https://github.com/CuberMessenger/BNAI. Details of the parameters and structure are elaborated in Appendices 1-5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the software SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For the outputs of three rounds, intra-rater consistency was calculated by Fleiss kappa coefficients with 95% confidence interval (CI). The Fleiss kappa coefficients were categorized using the following criteria: 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, good agreement; and 0.81–1.00, excellent agreement. The final diagnostic result was determined through a majority voting mechanism of three rounds (13). The performance of LLM was evaluated using the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Besides, accuracy was compared with LLM and CNN through the McNemar test due to paired design for continuous variables, and compared with 95 radiologists through one-sample t-test for comparison of mean accuracies. Subgroup analyses of diagnostic accuracy based on ground truth labels and BI-RADS signs for breast masses were conducted through Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparison and Kruskal-Wallis H test for multiple group comparison. Chi-squared analysis was used to reveal the relationship between diagnostic performance and diagnostic confidence, and also between diagnostic performance and the radiologist’s assessment. P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction method to account for multiple hypothesis tests. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The dataset comprised 80 patients [median age, 42.5 years; interquartile range (IQR), 37.0–53.0 years] (Table 1). Of the 80 original reports, 36% were categorized as BI-RADS 3 (29 of 80), 41% as BI-RADS 4 (33 of 80), and 23% as BI-RADS 5 (18 of 80). The ground truth for 37 (46%) malignant cases was established through pathology, whereas 19 benign cases were confirmed by pathology (24%) and 24 were confirmed by follow up (30%). Overall performance

In the overall comparison demonstrated in Table 2, GPT-4o with image-strategy exhibited inferior diagnostic accuracy than that with image-combined-text-strategy [58% (46 of 80) vs. 70% (56 of 80), P=0.031], radiologists [inexperienced radiologists: 58% (46 of 80) vs. 68% (54 of 80), P<0.001; moderately experienced radiologists: 58% (46 of 80) vs. 71% (57 of 80), P<0.001; very experienced radiologists: 58% (46 of 80) vs. 71% (57 of 80), P<0.001], and CNN [58% (46 of 80) vs. 74% (59 of 80), P<0.001]. Notably, GPT-4o using the image-combined-text-strategy demonstrated comparable diagnostic accuracy involving inexperienced radiologists [70% (56 of 80) vs. 68% (54 of 80), P=0.137], moderately experienced radiologists [70% (56 of 80) vs. 71% (57 of 80), P=0.450], and CNN [70% (56 of 80) vs. 74% (59 of 80), P=0.701]. An evident difference was observed in the comparison with very experienced radiologists [70% (56 of 80) vs. 71% (57 of 80), P=0.048]. The Fleiss kappa coefficients of image-strategy and image-combined-text-strategy for interpreting breast US images were 0.25 (95% CI: 0.07–0.43) and 0.81 (95% CI: 0.67–0.91), respectively. Performance analysis based on ground truth labels

Table 3 summarizes the average accuracy of GPT-4o, radiologists, and CNN for each ground truth label subgroup. GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy for pathology-confirmed malignancies using both the image-strategy [95% (95% CI: 87–100%)] and the image-combined-text-strategy [97% (95% CI: 92–100%)]. Inexperienced and very experienced radiologists also performed well in this subgroup, with accuracies of 74% (95% CI: 66–81%) and 77% (95% CI: 71–84%), respectively. However, GPT-4o struggled with follow-up-confirmed benign cases using the image strategy [25% (95% CI: 6–44%)] and pathology-confirmed benign cases using the combined strategy [26% (95% CI: 5–48%)]. The CNN model achieved the highest accuracy for follow-up confirmed benign cases, with values of 88% (95% CI: 73–100%). Accuracy for pathology-confirmed benign cases was on par with follow-up-confirmed benign cases for moderately experienced radiologists [71% (95% CI: 62–80%) for both]. Subgroup analysis revealed evident differences in performance of GPT-4o (P<0.001 for GPT-4o with both strategies), yet no significant differences in accuracy for radiologists or the CNN (P=0.058 for inexperienced radiologists; P=0.652 for moderately experienced radiologists; P=0.524 for very experienced radiologists and P=0.092 for CNN).

Performance analysis based on BI-RADS-related signs

The average accuracy of GPT-4o, radiologists, and the CNN for each BI-RADS-related sign are compared in Table 4. GPT-4o showed significant differences in diagnostic accuracy for all types of breast masses, except for posterior features (P=0.742 and 0.828 for image and image-combined-text-strategy, respectively). Using the image-strategy, GPT-4o exhibited higher accuracy for irregular masses [64% (95% CI: 52–77%)] compared to regular masses [38% (95% CI: 15–61%), P=0.037]. Conversely, inexperienced radiologists demonstrated higher accuracy for regular masses [72% (95% CI: 67–90%)] than irregular masses [64% (95% CI: 57–72%), P=0.028]. GPT-4o’s image-strategy yielded higher accuracy for masses that were not parallel [90% (95% CI: 67–100%) vs. 67% (95% CI: 56–78%), P=0.027], not circumscribed [71% (95% CI: 56–85%) vs. 44% (95% CI: 27–60%), P=0.015], and with calcifications [86% (95% CI: 71–100%) vs. 47% (95% CI: 33–60%), P=0.001]. The image-combined-text-strategy also showed higher accuracy for masses with calcifications [95% (95% CI: 86–100%) vs. 60% (95% CI: 47–73%), P=0.002]. No significant differences were observed in the diagnostic performance of moderately or very experienced radiologists or the CNN, as reported in Table 4.

Interpretability of LLM

As illustrated in Figure 4, LLM with image-combined-text-strategy had higher diagnostic confidence in correct cases (correlation coefficient =0.249, P<0.001), whereas confidence between correct and incorrect cases was not significant in image-strategy (correlation coefficient =0.024, P=0.697). Regarding the senior radiologist’s assessment, cases with correct diagnoses of LLM were associated with higher assessment scores (image-strategy: correlation coefficient =0.388, P<0.001; image-combined-text-strategy: correlation coefficient =0.195, P=0.002).

Discussion

Whether the diagnostic capacity of LLMs can achieve clinically relevant performance, especially in breast US datasets, remains to be evaluated. Comprehensive accuracy assessments and ongoing optimization are central to pushing forward clinical applications (23). This multicenter retrospective study evaluated the diagnostic performance of a LLM, GPT-4o, in analyzing the US images of breast masses. The results showed that the GPT-4o displayed a fair consistency using an image-strategy [0.25 (95% CI: 0.07–0.43)], whereas image-combined-text-strategy yielded excellent agreement [0.81 (95% CI: 0.67–0.91)]. Diagnostic accuracy significantly improved from 58% (46 of 80) to 70% (56 of 80) when descriptive text was provided alongside the images. Leveraging both image and text data, the accuracy of GPT-4o approached that of board-certified radiologists with moderate experience [70% (56 of 80) vs. 71% (57 of 80), P=0.450] and a customized CNN [70% (56 of 80) vs. 74% (59 of 80), P=0.701].

However, GPT-4o’s performance was significantly impacted by heterogeneous signs and distinct ground truth labels, even when using the image-combined-text-strategy (reported in Table 3 and Table 4). GPT-4o’s diagnostic confidence positively correlated with accuracy when text was included (correlation coefficient =0.249, P<0.001). Senior radiologists rated correctly diagnosed cases by GPT-4o more highly (image-strategy: correlation coefficient =0.388, P<0.001; image-combined-text-strategy: correlation coefficient =0.195, P=0.002), indicating a link between analytic process and results. It should be noted that the performance of CNN and radiologists in this study did not match previously published benchmarks (24-26), likely because the included cases were more challenging than typical clinical cases. Additionally, the CNN model in our study adopted a traditional architecture without extensive fine-tuning of the parameters. Future work could further optimize the model to enhance performance.

The study found that although LLMs hold promise for natural language processing, their effectiveness for medical image interpretation fell short of clinical needs, which was consistent with prior research (6,7,21,25-29). To solve this problem, the inclusion of image preprocessing technology may improve the performance of LLM for medical images (30). Insufficient consistency and accuracy compared to human experts limited their real-world applicability at present. Incorporating standardized BI-RADS descriptors from radiology reports improved GPT-4o’s performance by translating key visual features into intelligible text, enabling more comprehensive diagnosis through contextual inference. This underscores the potential of multimodal strategies combining image and text data to enhance LLM capabilities, instead of a single-model approach (12). Wu et al. reported that intra-LLM agreement achieved 0.65 to 0.85 of GPT-4.0 for pure texts (13). Other image-based analyses revealed consistencies ranging from 0.13 to 0.70 of GPT-4V for independent images (6,17). Therefore, the remarkably elevated agreement could be ascribed to deploying textural characteristics.

Subgroup analysis revealed accuracy gaps in GPT-4o’s handling of certain case characteristics, especially benign masses, that were not seen with the CNN or human radiologists. Benign cases, often diagnostically challenging, posed difficulties for GPT-4o. Experienced radiologists were less impacted by heterogeneous presentations than GPT-4o or junior colleagues, likely due to their ability to accurately identify mass features and synthesize varied imaging findings through systematic training. Improving GPT-4o’s recognition of key benign signs such as regular shape, parallel orientation, circumscribed margins, and absence of calcifications is crucial. Continual updates to the model’s training data are needed to maintain accuracy and relevance.

Compared to “black box” CNNs (31), LLMs offered greater inherent interpretability through natural language explanations of their predictions, enabling clarification of ambiguous findings. This was key for building trust and adoption of AI assistance tools. Aligned with prior research (32), cases where GPT-4o correctly identified signs and provided logical reasoning tended to result in accurate diagnoses as rated by senior doctors. Although a performance gap remains, the added value of explainability and adaptability supports GPT-4o’s potential role as a complementary diagnostic aid. However, persuasive but inaccurate narratives, known as “hallucinations” (33), remain a risk with LLMs. The interactive nature of the image-and-text-strategy helped to capture radiologist expertise while providing an analytic process for their consideration, reflecting a synergistic collaboration. Given the high stakes of medical decision-making, these findings underscore the importance of human supervision when deploying LLM-based tools (34). Besides, the model’s occasional hallucinations and closed-source nature raise concerns about transparency, interpretability, and accountability. Responsible deployment should involve real-time monitoring, clear human–AI task boundaries, and ongoing performance evaluation. Importantly, LLMs should augment but not replace judgment from clinicians.

In this study, we conducted a comparative diagnostic study based on the US images of breast masses, with images from external centers as validations. The overall dataset reflected ethnic and geographic diversity. To evaluate the clinical capacities of GPT-4o, comparisons were made with multiple diagnostic entities, including board-certified radiologists with varying experience levels and traditional CNN models. Additionally, we outlined the masses to avoid the attention of GPT-4o for irrelevant regions.

This study had some limitations. First, the number of cases was small. To solve this problem, sufficient radiologists with varying levels of expertise were enrolled to assess average performance. Second, clinical information and other imaging examinations were not included. The clinical diagnosis or decision-making process is a comprehensive judgment that cannot rely solely on image analysis. Third, although we maintained environmental consistency, the variability in the image strategy was significant. Fourth, a direct comparison with a text strategy was not included. Future research will aim to separately quantify the contributions of image and text inputs in multimodal LLMs. Fifth, emerging reasoning-oriented LLMs such as OpenAI o3 may offer improved performance in complex diagnostic tasks. Future studies should consider benchmarking GPT-4o’s performance against newer models. Besides, stricter environmental controls and large-scale multimodal data training are warranted in future studies.

Conclusions

GPT-4o demonstrated limited US image interpretation capabilities for real-world breast masses. Chatbots should serve as supportive tools in specific scenarios rather than replacing human experts. Purpose-built CNNs may provide superior accuracy for well-defined clinical tasks at present. Future research should explore specialized prompts, advanced image recognition techniques, and expanded medical imaging datasets to improve LLM performance. Enhancing accuracy, consistency, and domain-specific capabilities are key priorities for unlocking the full potential of LLMs in medical imaging.
