Trends in pancreatic cancer mortality among US adults, 1999–2023
Authors: Tianxing Wu, Zhongliu Bian, Nan Qin, Jingtao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is characterized by an extremely poor prognosis and a high mortality rate, posing a significant burden on society.MethodsWe analyzed PC mortality data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database from 1999 to 2023. Annual death counts and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated and stratified by sex, age, race/ethnicity, state, census region, and 2013 urbanization level. Temporal trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent changes (APCs) and average annual percent changes (AAPCs) with 95% confidence intervals. Analyses by urbanization level were limited to 1999–2020 due to data availability.ResultsIn 2023, there were 49,441 deaths from PC in the United States, representing a 70.03% increase compared to 1999. During the same period, the AAMR for PC rose from 16.41 (95%CI: 16.22–16.60) to 17.33 (95%CI: 17.17–17.48) per 100,000 population, showing a statistically significant upward trend. This increase was observed in both males and females, with a more pronounced increase among males, with an AAPC of 0.24 (95%CI: 0.19–0.30). Age subgroup analysis revealed that individuals aged 55 and older had higher mortality rates, which continued to rise, while mortality rates declined among those under 55. Although the AAMR for non-Hispanic(NH) Black individuals declined from 1999 to 2023, with an AAPC of -0.19 (95% CI: -0.26 to -0.12), it remained the highest among all racial and ethnic groups, with an AAMR of 20.68 (95% CI: 20.14–21.22). Geographically, the Midwest exhibited a higher AAMR (18.72, 95% CI: 18.36–19.07) and a more pronounced growth trend (AAPC = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.46–0.59). In addition, non-metropolitan areas have higher AAPC (0.62, 95CI: 0.53—0.72). And in 2020, AAMR value is higher than that of metropolitan area.ConclusionPC remains a significant and rising burden in the United States, with notable disparities by sex, age, race/ethnicity, and region. These findings highlight the importance of targeted prevention strategies, such as smoking cessation and metabolic risk control, and improving access to specialized care in non-metropolitan areas. Strengthening early detection efforts and optimizing resource allocation may help reduce these disparities and improve outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04354-4.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, CDC WONDER, Mortality trends, Age-adjusted mortality rate, Average annual percent change, Regional

Introduction

PC is one of the most lethal malignancies of the digestive system, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 13%, making it a major contributor to cancer-related mortality despite its relatively low incidence [1]. Its deep anatomical location and lack of specific early symptoms contribute to late-stage diagnoses in the majority of cases [2–4]. As a result, PC remains a public health concern with limited opportunities for early intervention and curative treatment. As a result, PC remains a public health concern with limited opportunities for early intervention and curative treatment.

While progress has been made in diagnostic techniques and treatment modalities, population-level outcomes have improved only marginally. Currently, no effective screening strategies exist for the general population [5], and many established risk factors—such as smoking, obesity, and diabetes—remain prevalent or increasing [6, 7].

Importantly, recent data indicate that PC mortality continues to rise, paralleling upward trends seen in several other major gastrointestinal cancers. Understanding long-term trends across different populations and geographic regions is essential for guiding effective prevention and control strategies [8].

This study aims to evaluate long-term trends in PC mortality in the United States from 1999 to 2023 using nationally representative data from the CDC WONDER database. To provide a comprehensive understanding of these trends, we conducted stratified analyses by sex, age group, race/ethnicity, geographic region, and urbanization level. In addition to examining annual death counts and AAMRs, we calculated AAPCs using Joinpoint regression. Compared with previous studies, our analysis incorporates the most recent available data and offers a more detailed subgroup assessment, providing updated evidence on temporal patterns and disparities to guide targeted cancer prevention and control efforts.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is an ecological analysis based on data from the CDC WONDER database, focusing on the burden of PC mortality in the United States. The distributional differences and temporal trends of PC deaths and AAMR were examined across various stratifications, including sex, age, race, state, Census Region, and the 2013 urbanization level, among individuals aged 25 years and older. As this research is based on a publicly available database and does not involve any personally identifiable patient information, ethical approval was not required. AAMRs were calculated by standardizing PC‐related deaths to the year 2000 US Population as previously described.

Data source

The data used in this study were obtained from the Underlying Cause of Death database available on the CDC WONDER platform [9, 10]. This mortality data is compiled by the National Center for Health Statistics, a division of the CDC, based on death certificates of United States residents. These records are collected by state vital statistics offices and aggregated into the National Vital Statistics System. Researchers can access nationwide information on the number of deaths, crude death rates, AAMRs, as well as their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and standard errors. These data can be stratified by place of residence, age group, race, Hispanic origin, sex, year, cause of death, intent and mechanism of injury, drug/alcohol-induced causes, and the 2013 urbanization category. This database has been widely used in various epidemiological and public health studies [11, 12].

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently carried out by two researchers, with subsequent cross-checking by one of them to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data. PC cases were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code C25, including subcodes C25.0, C25.1, C25.2, C25.3, C25.4, C25.7, C25.8, and C25.9 [13]. To assess disparities in mortality among population subgroups, this study extracted essential demographic and geographic variables. These included sex, age grouped in 10-year intervals from 25 to 85 years and older, race categorized as Hispanic, NH White, NH Black, and NH Other, as well as state, Census Region, and the 2013 urbanization level.

Based on previous research, individuals identified as NH American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or multiracial were categorized as “NH other” [14]. Urbanization was categorized according to the National Center for Health Statistics classification, where Large Central Metro, Large Fringe Metro, Medium Metro, and Small Metro were grouped as “urban” areas, and Micropolitan (nonmetro) and NonCore (nonmetro) areas were grouped as “non-urban” [15, 16]. As the CDC WONDER database does not provide AAMR for 10-year age groups, crude death rates were used for analyses involving these age categories [11]. Additionally, due to the unavailability of AAMR data for urbanization subgroups from 2021 to 2023, the analysis for urbanization-based AAMR was limited to data up to the year 2020 [17].

Statistical analysis

This study assessed trends in PC deaths in the United States from 1999 to 2023 using percentage change, calculated as: (deaths in 2023 – deaths in 1999)/deaths in 1999 [18]. The same method was applied across all subgroups.

Joinpoint regression analysis was conducted to evaluate changes over time in the AAMR of PC in the United States [19]. Joinpoint analysis has been widely applied in epidemiology, cancer trend research, public health surveillance, and studies of survival rate changes, and has gained broad recognition among researchers [20–22]. The software is developed by a team from the United States National Cancer Institute and is freely available on its official website (surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/) [23, 24]. This study applied Joinpoint regression to AAMR data from 1999 to 2023, stratified by sex, age, race, state, census region, and the 2013 urbanization level. For analyses based on 10-year age groups, crude mortality rates were used, and the AAPC was also calculated based on the crude mortality rate. For urbanization level based on the 2013 classification, the analysis was limited to the period from 1999 to 2020. In terms of methodological choices and parameter settings, default options were used in most cases. For Model Selection Method, the Permutation Test was selected. For APC/AAPC/Tau Confidence Intervals, the Parametric Method was used.

Joinpoint regression analyses in this study were conducted using the Joinpoint (5.1.0.0) software, while all other data cleaning and statistical analyses were performed using R (4.4.3) software.

Results

Overall and sex-specific mortality trends

Between 1999 and 2023, a total of 956,079 deaths due to PC were recorded in the United States, including 484,680 males (50.7%) and 471,399 females (49.3%). The annual number of deaths rose from 29,077 in 1999 to 49,441 in 2023, representing a 70.0% increase. Male deaths increased by 80.5%, compared with 60.1% in females (Supplementary Table 1).

The overall AAMR increased from 16.41 (95% CI: 16.22–16.60) to 17.33 (95% CI: 17.17–17.48) per 100,000, corresponding to a statistically significant AAPC of 0.24 (95% CI: 0.19–0.30). Males had consistently higher AAMRs than females throughout the study period, rising from 19.00 to 19.74 per 100,000 (AAPC = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.19–0.30), whereas females increased from 14.38 to 15.26 per 100,000 (AAPC = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.15–0.28) (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

Trends by age group

Over the 25-year period from 1999 to 2023, the majority of PC-related deaths occurred among older adults, particularly those aged 65 years and above. The highest number of deaths was recorded in the 65–74 age group (275,296 deaths), followed by the 75–84 (276,365 deaths) and 55–64 (180,149 deaths) age groups. In contrast, the 25–34 age group had the fewest deaths, with only 1,412 reported cases (Supplementary Table 1).

AAMRs increased significantly in older adults, particularly those aged 75–84 years (from 73.07 to 81.55 per 100,000; AAPC = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.29–0.46) and ≥ 85 years (AAPC = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.47). The 65–74 group showed a modest overall rise but a clear upward inflection after 2013 (APC = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.32–0.78). In contrast, younger groups showed declining trends: the 35–44 group had the steepest decrease (AAPC = –0.81, 95% CI: –1.09 to –0.54), and the 45–54 group showed a gradual but sustained APC decline from 2005 onward (Table 1 and Fig. 2).

Trends by race

The NH White population accounted for the highest number of deaths, with a total of 749,111 cases, representing 78.35% of all deaths. This was followed by NH Black individuals (113,414 cases, 11.86%), Hispanic individuals (60,221 cases, 6.30%), and NH individuals of other races (31,601 cases, 3.31%). During this period, the number of PC-related deaths increased across all racial and ethnic groups. (Supplementary Table 1).

AAMR trends varied notably by race. NH Whites showed a significant increase from 16.23 to 18.00 per 100,000 (AAPC = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.37–0.49), while Hispanics also had a modest rise (AAPC = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.07–0.33). In contrast, NH Blacks experienced a decline from 22.10 to 20.68 per 100,000 (AAPC = –0.19, 95% CI: –0.26 to –0.12) but still had the highest mortality rates throughout the study period. NH others showed no significant overall change, with a slight rise before 2011 and a decline thereafter (Table 1, Fig. 3).

Trends by census region

PC mortality varied across the four U.S. Census regions. The South reported the largest cumulative number of deaths (349,713; 36.6%), followed by the Midwest (220,036; 23.0%), West (195,891; 20.5%), and Northeast (190,439; 19.9%) (Supplementary Table 1).

In 2023, the Midwest had the highest AAMR at 18.72 per 100,000 (95% CI: 18.36–19.07), with the steepest upward trend (AAPC = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.46–0.59). The South (AAPC = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.21–0.33) and West (AAPC = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.03–0.22) also showed significant increases, whereas the Northeast remained largely stable over time (AAPC = 0.08, 95% CI: –0.01 to 0.17) (Table 2 and Fig. 4).

Trends by urbanization level

From 1999 to 2023, a total of 668,547 deaths occurred in metropolitan regions, accounting for the vast majority of the burden, while nonmetropolitan areas reported 141,878 deaths. In metropolitan regions, the number of PC-related deaths increased by 73.8%, rising from 23,699 in 1999 to 41,178 in 2023. In contrast, nonmetropolitan regions experienced a smaller relative increase of 53.6%, with deaths rising from 5,378 to 8,263 during the same period (Supplementary Table 1).

By 2020, the AAMR had risen more substantially in nonmetropolitan regions, reaching 17.80 per 100,000 (95% CI: 17.40–18.20), whereas metropolitan areas experienced a more modest increase to 16.94 per 100,000 (95% CI: 16.77–17.11). This translated into an AAPC nearly four times higher in nonmetropolitan areas (0.62, 95% CI: 0.53–0.72) than in metropolitan areas (0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.21) (Table 2 and Fig. 5).

Trends by state

From 1999 to 2023, the AAMR of PC in most states in the United States increased. California reported the most cumulative deaths, while Alaska reported the least.

In 2023, the highest AAMRs were observed in Rhode Island, Delaware, and Mississippi, while the lowest were reported in Alaska and Colorado. The largest relative increases occurred in several western states such as Utah and Arizona, while the smallest changes were noted in the District of Columbia and parts of the Northeast. Discussion

This study examined the trends in the mortality burden of PC in the United States from 1999 to 2023. The results indicate that during this period, the number of deaths showed a significant upward trend, while AAMR exhibited a slight increase. Additionally, subgroup analyses by sex, age, race, and region demonstrated notable disparities.

This study found that both the number of deaths and the AAMR from PC are higher in men than in women, with a more pronounced upward trend in males. This gender disparity may result from a combination of modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors. Among them, smoking is widely recognized as one of the main risk factors for PC, with research indicating that smokers have approximately twice the risk compared to non-smokers, and about one-quarter of PC cases can be attributed to smoking [25–27]. Given the significantly higher smoking rate among men, the rise in smoking-related incidence likely contributes substantially to the higher number of deaths and AAMR observed in males [28]. Abdominal obesity is another key risk factor; studies have shown that even with a normal overall body weight, an increase in waist circumference can significantly elevate the risk of developing PC [25]. Since men are more prone to accumulating fat in the abdominal region, the prevalence of abdominal obesity is generally higher among males, potentially increasing their risk of both incidence and mortality [29, 30]. In addition, occupational exposure is a notable factor, as certain high-risk industries such as dry cleaning and metalworking involve carcinogenic chemicals that may be linked to the development of PC [25, 31]. Men are more likely to be employed in such occupations, which further exacerbates the gender gap. In summary, gender differences in modifiable risk factors such as smoking, abdominal obesity, and occupational exposure may collectively explain the higher PC mortality and AAMR in men, along with the more rapid upward trend. Therefore, public health strategies should pay close attention to this gender disparity and implement targeted interventions focusing on high-risk male populations to mitigate the growing burden of PC.

In this study, the analysis revealed that the AAMR was significantly higher among individuals aged 55 and above, exhibiting a statistically significant and continuous upward trend. In contrast, the AAMR among those under 55 was markedly lower and showed a declining trend. This pattern is closely linked to the nature of PC as a highly age-related malignancy. PC is typically considered a "disease of aging," with its development strongly associated with various aging-related biological mechanisms, including genomic instability, telomere shortening, epigenetic dysregulation, and age-related metabolic changes [32–34]. These aging processes act synergistically to promote the initiation and progression of PC.

This study reveals significant disparities in the burden of PC mortality among different racial and ethnic groups. NH Whites accounted for the highest number of PC deaths, primarily due to their large population base [35]. In terms of absolute growth in death counts, NH Other and Hispanic populations showed the most substantial increases, at 269.51% and 254.71% respectively, far exceeding the increases observed in NH Whites (55.47%) and NH Blacks (76.95%). However, given the relatively small increases in AAMR for these two groups, it is likely that the marked rise in death counts is largely driven by population growth [35]. Throughout the study period, NH Blacks consistently exhibited the highest AAMR, which, despite a slight decline (from 22.10 to 20.68 per 100,000), remained significantly higher than that of other groups. Research has found that Black individuals with stage I PC are less likely to undergo surgery [36]. They are also associated with poorer postoperative outcomes, including higher rates of sepsis and postoperative bleeding [37]. Concurrently, NH Black patients exhibit higher proportions in the lowest quartile for income or education, and have higher rates of being uninsured or underinsured [38], indicating a persistently disadvantaged position in terms of PC incidence and mortality risk. Notably, while the NH White did not show a dramatic increase in death numbers, it had the largest increase in AAMR among the four racial groups. Furthermore, NH Whites, who have historically occupied a dominant position in the United States’ social structure, with greater access to political, economic, and educational resources, also have a higher average age and a more advanced degree of population aging. Therefore, age is likely one of the key factors influencing the elevated risk of PC mortality in this group.

From a regional perspective, the burden of PC mortality is significantly higher in non-metropolitan areas compared to metropolitan areas, with a more pronounced upward trend. Previous studies from countries such as Denmark, Australia, and Canada have reported higher PC mortality rates in rural areas [39–41]. Compared to many other common cancers, the diagnosis and management of PC require a higher level of expertise that is often unavailable in smaller or rural hospitals [4]. Patients living in rural regions frequently face long travel distances to reach specialized cancer centers, and these barriers may ultimately prevent some individuals from receiving appropriate care at all [42]. Additionally, residents in rural areas often have lower socioeconomic status, reduced health insurance coverage [43], and higher smoking rates, obesity rates and lack of exercise rates [43].These challenges can lead to late-stage diagnoses of PC, negatively affecting prognosis and increasing mortality rates.

In remote or rural regions, residents often face substantial barriers to receiving high-quality healthcare, such as a lack of specialized oncologists and advanced diagnostic equipment, long travel distances to medical facilities, and extended wait times. These challenges can lead to late-stage diagnoses of PC, negatively affecting prognosis and increasing mortality rates. Additionally, residents in rural areas often have lower socioeconomic status, reduced health insurance coverage.

Among the four major census regions, the Midwest exhibits significantly higher PC AAMR rates than other regions, with a more pronounced increase. Inequalities in access to healthcare resources play a crucial role, encompassing screening coverage, insurance coverage, and population behaviors such as diet, smoking, and obesity. Research indicates persistently high smoking rates in the U.S. Midwest alongside a significant rise in e-cigarette use [45]. Obesity rates vary significantly across the United States, with the Midwest (33.9%) having the highest prevalence [46].

Recent advances in immunogenomics and inflammation- and nutrition-based prognostic markers in other malignancies may offer valuable insights into PC research. Immune-related regulators such as IL27RA [47] and TMEM71 [48] have been identified through transcriptomic and single-cell analyses as predictors of response to immune checkpoint blockade. In addition, inflammation- and nutrition-based indices, including the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score [49], combined cancer inflammation prognostic index and platelet-albumin ratio index [50], and multi-biomarker panels [51–53], have shown strong prognostic value in colorectal and breast cancers. Incorporating such biomarkers into future PC studies may improve risk stratification, enable earlier identification of high-risk populations, and guide more individualized prevention and treatment strategies.

Improving access to specialized care in non-metropolitan areas—through referral networks, regional care coordination, or telemedicine—may help reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment. Public health initiatives should emphasize primary prevention strategies, including smoking cessation programs, diabetes management, obesity control, and expansion of health insurance coverage, with a particular focus on high-burden regions and NH Black populations [54]. At present, no effective population-wide screening strategy exists, and the persistently poor prognosis of PC underscores the urgent need to strengthen prevention strategies, implement earlier diagnostic interventions, and develop more effective treatment options [8].

This study utilized the CDC WONDER database to provide a comprehensive analysis of the mortality burden of PC in the United States, highlighting its geographic disparities and temporal trends. However, this study has several limitations. First, mortality data from the CDC WONDER database are derived from death certificates and may be affected by misclassification or coding errors, potentially biasing estimates. Second, as an ecological analysis, our study cannot infer causality; population-level associations may not reflect individual-level risks, and links to specific risk factors should be interpreted with caution. Third, incidence, survival, and stage-at-diagnosis or treatment-pattern data were not included, preventing us from determining whether observed mortality trends are driven by changes in incidence, survival, earlier detection, or treatment advances. Fourth, subgroup comparisons may be influenced by migration and population structure changes over time, particularly the growth of the Hispanic population. Fifth, while percentage changes were calculated, especially in subgroup analyses, small absolute numbers may exaggerate relative differences and should be interpreted cautiously. Sixth, crude rates were used for age-specific groups and AAMRs for overall and other subgroup trends, which may limit comparability and could partially confound temporal trends due to differences in population growth rates across age groups. Seventh, AAMR data for urbanization subgroups were available only through 2020, so more recent changes in urban–rural disparities could not be fully captured. Finally, although Joinpoint regression identified trend changes, we did not perform formal statistical tests to compare AAPCs between subgroups. Despite these limitations, our study provides one of the most up-to-date and comprehensive assessments of PC mortality trends in the United States and offers important evidence to guide prevention and control efforts.
