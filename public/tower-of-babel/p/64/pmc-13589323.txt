Foregut mediastinal cysts: a narrative review

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveForegut mediastinal cysts are rare congenital anomalies arising from aberrant embryonic foregut development, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 42,000 to 1 in 100,000. These benign lesions, including bronchogenic, esophageal duplication, and neurenteric cysts, account for 12–20% of mediastinal masses. They may remain asymptomatic or cause respiratory, gastrointestinal, or neurological symptoms due to compression or complications like infection. This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of their embryogenesis, clinical presentation, diagnostic strategies, treatment options, and prognosis to guide clinical decision-making.MethodsA review of the current literature was conducted with a focus on the embryologic origins, histopathologic classification, imaging characteristics, and treatment outcomes of foregut cysts. This narrative review addressed studies describing bronchogenic, esophageal duplication, and neurenteric cysts, with emphasis on diagnostic modalities and management strategies encompassing surgical resection and observation.Key Content and FindingsBronchogenic cysts, the most common subtype (50–60%), typically occur near the carina, while esophageal duplication and neurenteric cysts are often posterior. Introduction

Foregut mediastinal cysts are rare congenital anomalies resulting from abnormal development of the embryonic foregut. They are estimated to occur in 1 in 42,000 to 1 in 100,000 individuals; however, the true incidence remains uncertain owing to limited investigation and the frequently asymptomatic presentation of these lesions, which constrains the reliability of reported figures (1-3). These benign cysts, primarily located in the middle or posterior mediastinum, include bronchogenic cysts, esophageal duplication cysts, and neurenteric cysts. They account for approximately 12–20% of mediastinal masses, with bronchogenic cysts comprising the majority (50–60%), followed by esophageal duplication cysts (5–15%) and neurenteric cysts (2–5%) (1-3). Sex distribution remains unclear, with some studies noting a slight predilection for one sex and others reporting no difference (1,2). Despite their benign nature, foregut mediastinal cysts are clinically relevant because they may cause respiratory symptoms, mimic malignancy and prompt unnecessary diagnostic or therapeutic procedures, or result in complications such as infection or compression of critical mediastinal structures (2,4). This review examines the embryogenesis and pathogenesis of foregut mediastinal cysts, characterizes their clinical presentation and imaging features, and evaluates current diagnostic strategies, treatment options, and prognosis. It also presents an update on current medical understanding and management of these rare entities, serving as a useful reference for clinicians. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://med.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/med-2025-1-59/rc).

Embryogenesis and pathogenesis

The foregut, an endodermal derivative, forms during the third to fourth week of embryogenesis, giving rise to the respiratory and digestive systems from the pharynx to the duodenum at the ampulla of Vater (2,5). During this period, the foregut undergoes complex morphogenetic processes, including elongation, budding, and vacuolization with the ventral segment forming the trachea and bronchi and the dorsal segment forming the esophagus, as well as the proximal gastrointestinal tract (Figure 1) (2,4). Disruptions in these processes result in foregut cysts, each with distinct embryologic origins that are classified by histologic features (Figure 2).

Bronchogenic cysts

These arise from abnormal budding of the ventral foregut, specifically the tracheobronchial tree, during the fifth to sixth week of gestation (5,6). Failure of bud reabsorption or detachment leads to isolated cystic structures. The cysts often form near the carina or mainstem bronchi due to the proximity of the developing bronchial tree; however, their location may vary with approximately 15% found in other locations within the thorax (Figure 3) (1,7).

Esophageal duplication cysts

These result from incomplete vacuolization of the solid esophageal tube, which normally canalizes by the eighth week of gestation (1,6,8). Persistent epithelial remnants form cysts within or adjacent to the esophageal wall, often in the posterior mediastinum and typically located to the right of the esophagus (Figure 4) (8).

Neurenteric cysts

These occur due to incomplete separation of the foregut from the notochord, leading to an anomalous connection between endodermal and ectodermal structures, causing disruption of vertebral development (1,9). This results in cysts in the posterior mediastinum, frequently associated with vertebral anomalies such as hemivertebrae or spinal dysraphism (9). Due to the cephalocaudal movement of the two embryonic layers, these defects are usually found superior to the associated cyst (Figure 5) (1).

Pathogenesis involves disrupted cellular signaling, potentially involving Sonic hedgehog (SHH), Wnt and other not fully elucidated pathways, which regulate foregut patterning (10). Genetic predispositions or environmental factors remain poorly understood, but congenital syndromes such as vertebral anomalies or intraspinal extension of the cyst may coexist with foregut cysts, most prominently neurenteric cysts (1,8,11).

Classification, histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Foregut cysts are classified based on their embryologic and histologic features (Table 2). Histologic examination identifies the type of epithelium lining the cyst wall, which in turn determines cyst type. These lesions are typically unilocular and often contain proteinaceous fluid (serous, mucinous, or mixed), with variable degrees of calcification and inflammation. The cyst wall frequently shows a denuded epithelial lining or nonspecific histologic features, rendering lineage indeterminate. Given the significant overlap in epithelial types across these entities, classification based solely on epithelial lining can be challenging (6,12); accordingly, the key histopathological features that differentiate the three types of mediastinal foregut cysts rely primarily on wall composition, particularly the presence or absence of cartilage, smooth muscle layers, and glands (2). Classification may be further compromised in up to 20% of cases by superimposed hemorrhage or infection (1).

Neurenteric cysts

IHC may be used as an ancillary tool in the evaluation of mediastinal foregut cysts, primarily for supporting endodermal origin when histological features overlap and for excluding mimics such as mesothelial cysts, thyroglossal duct cysts, and Müllerian cysts. Expression of cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6), calretinin, and Wilms tumor 1 protein/gene (WT-1) should raise consideration for a mesothelial cyst (26), whereas thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) and paired box gene 8 (PAX8) may support a thyroglossal duct cyst (27). Positivity for PAX8, WT-1, estrogen receptor (ER), and progesterone receptor (PR) should favor a Müllerian cyst (26,28-31). In routine practice, however, an extensive IHC panel is often unnecessary when the morphological diagnosis is straightforward. The presence of mixed epithelial types alone does not warrant additional workup, as epithelial heterogeneity is common across all foregut cyst subtypes and classification should rest primarily on wall composition. Immunohistochemical results should always be interpreted in conjunction with morphological, clinical, and radiological findings.

Indeterminate foregut cysts

A subset of foregut cysts cannot be definitively classified, as they are detached from adjacent structures and lack the distinguishing wall elements, namely cartilage or dual smooth muscle layers, that are required for accurate subtype assignment. In such cases, categorization based on histology alone is not possible, and the designation of indeterminate foregut cyst is appropriate (12).

Differential diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis of foregut mediastinal cysts requires careful differentiation from other mediastinal cystic lesions. While thymic cysts are not of foregut origin, congenital thymic cysts (arising from remnants of the thymopharyngeal duct originated from the third pharyngeal pouch) are important in the differential diagnosis, particularly for anterior mediastinal masses. They may be unilocular (congenital) or multilocular (acquired/inflammatory) and typically contain thymic tissue with Hassall’s corpuscles on histopathology, distinguishing them from foregut cysts that lack thymic elements and feature cartilage (bronchogenic) or muscularis propria (esophageal duplication) (32).

Other benign cystic lesions include pericardial cysts (most commonly at the right cardiophrenic angle) and lymphatic malformations (lymphangiomas). Malignant or potentially malignant lesions that may appear cystic include mature teratomas with cystic components, cystic thymomas, and necrotic lymphadenopathy or tumors. These lesions often demonstrate solid enhancing components, irregular walls, or local invasion on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (33,34). A modern anatomic compartmental classification of the mediastinum (prevascular, visceral, and paravertebral compartments) further assists in narrowing the differential diagnosis based on lesion location (34).

Anatomic distribution and imaging characteristics

Bronchogenic cysts are typically located in the middle mediastinum near the carina or mainstem bronchi, while esophageal duplication and neurenteric cysts are more commonly found in the posterior mediastinum (23). Imaging is critical for diagnosis (Table 3).

Identifies well-defined masses but is nonspecific (35).

Shows smooth, fluid-filled lesions (0–20 Hounsfield units) with no enhancement that are typically round or oval in appearance (2,7,23). MRI

Demonstrates T2-hyperintense, wall-enhancing cysts with variable T1 signal (36). This imaging modality is ideal for assessing spinal involvement in neurenteric cysts and for further evaluating cysts that have elevated attenuation on CT imaging due to hemorrhage; or increased protein, as well as those containing calcium oxalate in the wall, which may obscure delineation on CT imaging (24,36).

PET/CT

Reduces the probability of malignancy due to low fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake, although uptake may be falsely increase in the setting of infection (37).

Multimodal imaging is essential for precise characterization and management planning of foregut mediastinal cysts. Contrast-enhanced CT offers excellent anatomic localization and attenuation assessment. MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast for evaluating proteinaceous or hemorrhagic content and for assessing spinal involvement in neurenteric cysts. Positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) helps exclude malignancy, as uncomplicated foregut cysts typically show low FDG uptake. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) can provide additional characterization and guidance for aspiration in selected cases, although with caution due to infection risk. Integration of these modalities improves diagnostic accuracy and guides the decision between observation and surgical intervention (33,38,39).

Clinical presentation

Foregut cysts are usually asymptomatic, discovered incidentally on imaging for unrelated conditions (7,39,40). Symptomatic presentations depend on cyst size, location, and complications.

Bronchogenic cysts

May present with cough, dyspnea, chest pain, wheezing, hemoptysis, cyanosis or recurrent pulmonary infections due to airway compression (2,24,41). Large cysts may lead to tracheomalacia or bronchial obstruction. Symptoms are more common in pediatric patients than adults (79% vs. 29%) likely due to the relatively larger cyst-to-body size ratio and the more frequent occurrence of cysts in anatomically critical locations prone to causing compressive symptoms (42).

Esophageal duplication cysts

Present with dysphagia, chest pain, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, weight loss or regurgitation, particularly if adjacent to the esophagus (2,43). Ectopic gastric mucosa may cause bleeding or perforation in up to 33% of cases (2,6,44).

Neurenteric cysts

May result in neurological symptoms (e.g., radiculopathy), headache, back pain or respiratory compromise due to spinal or mediastinal compression and involvement (45-47).

Complications include infection, rupture, hemoptysis, or compression of the trachea, esophagus, or great vessels (14). Rare malignant transformation, particularly adenocarcinoma in bronchogenic cysts, has been reported, necessitating vigilance (48).

Diagnostic workup

Physicians should approach foregut cysts systematically. Chest CT or MRI is essential to characterize lesion size, location, and relationship to airways or vasculature (7,23). EUS and EBUS provide high-resolution imaging and can guide fine-needle aspiration, though this carries risks of infection or inadequate sampling and is not recommended as a first-line diagnostic tool (49,50). Bronchoscopy may reveal airway compression or communication with the bronchial tree, while esophagoscopy can evaluate esophageal involvement (51). Definitive diagnosis requires histologic evaluation post-resection, as imaging alone cannot confirm cyst type (52).

Observation

Small, asymptomatic cysts in low-risk patients may be monitored with serial imaging (e.g., annual CT or MRI), although optimal interval and duration are not well established (52,53). However, observation is controversial due to potential complications, and the propensity of mediastinal foregut cysts to increase in size over time with up to 66% of patient eventually developing symptoms (1,2,54). When chosen, observation is more likely to succeed in adult patients due to the lower rate of growth and symptom progression.

Surgical resection

Indicated for symptomatic cysts, diagnostic uncertainty, or complications (e.g., infection, compression, suspected malignancy) (55). Due to the risk of growth, increasing involvement of mediastinal structures, and frequent diagnostic ambiguity, early resection is often the preferred treatment strategy whenever feasible (2,52). Complete resection minimizes recurrence, though involvement of mediastinal structures may preclude complete excision. In such cases, partial resection with de-epithelialization by cauterization or fulguration can reduce recurrence risk (2,52,56).

Minimally invasive techniques: video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) are preferred for reduced morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery (2,57,58). VATS is effective for cysts <5 cm without complex adhesions (59). Additionally, some central lesions may be amenable to mediastinoscopy (2).

Aspiration/drainage

Rarely used due to high recurrence and infection risks even with periprocedural antibiotics (61); may be considered for temporizing symptomatic relief in inoperable patients. Some authors have proposed the use of sclerosing agents to destroy the epithelial lining to potentially reduce recurrence (1,2,52).

Surgical risks include bleeding, infection, pneumothorax, and injury to adjacent structures (e.g., trachea, esophagus, or phrenic nerve) though the rate is relatively low (55,58). Multidisciplinary teams should be used to optimize treatment strategy, preoperative planning, including pulmonary function testing and airway assessment.

Prognosis and outcomes

Complete resection offers excellent prognosis, with recurrence rates reported as very low (4). Incomplete resection increases recurrence risk, particularly for bronchogenic cysts with bronchial communication, some of which have been reported to recur as many as 25 years following surgery (2,62). Long-term follow-up with imaging is recommended with most sources advising surveillance for 1–2 years post-resection to monitor for recurrence or complications (63). Neurenteric cysts require multidisciplinary management with participation of neurosurgery due to spinal involvement, which can significantly impact prognosis (64). Infected cysts or those with malignant transformation have worse outcomes, emphasizing the importance of early intervention (2,65).

Special considerations

Pediatric population

Foregut cysts are often associated with congenital anomalies necessitating early resection to prevent complications (66,67). Pediatric patients may present with more severe or frequent symptoms than adults, including respiratory distress and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms due to a higher likelihood of compressive lesions and greater anatomic disruption (66).

Pregnancy

MRI is preferred to avoid exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation, and surgery is deferred unless symptoms are severe. Multidisciplinary coordination with obstetrics is essential.

Infected cysts

Require antibiotics and often urgent resection to prevent abscess formation or sepsis (68). At least one case has been completed in which management was achieved through EBUS drainage followed by 6 weeks of antibiotics (69). Oral pathogens are commonly identified. Given the poor vascularization and lack of natural drainage, these cysts should be managed similarly to other intrathoracic abscesses with prolonged antibiotics and definitive source control (70).

Future directions and research gaps

Advances in imaging, such as three-dimensional (3D) MRI or molecular imaging, may help improve diagnostic precision and reduce the need for invasive procedures. Prospective registries are needed to standardize management protocols, particularly for asymptomatic cysts and pediatric cases. Research into minimally invasive techniques, such as robotic surgery, may help reduce morbidity associated with treatment.

Conclusions

Foregut mediastinal cysts are rare but clinically significant lesions. Their complex embryogenesis and frequent diagnostic overlap with solid mediastinal masses underscore the need for accurate characterization using advanced imaging modalities. Surgical resection, preferably performed using minimally invasive techniques, remains the cornerstone of treatment for suitable cases and is associated with an excellent prognosis when complete excision is achieved. However, the existing literature is limited by small sample sizes, with much of the evidence based on case series, leading to variability in management and surveillance practices across centers and constraining the strength of current recommendations. Continued investigation into molecular pathogenesis and the development of standardized management protocols may help address these limitations and further improve patient care.
