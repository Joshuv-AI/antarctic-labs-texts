The PLAGL1-KLF4-IRX5 axis promotes the osteogenesis of periosteal progenitors during mandible regeneration
Authors: Enhui Yao, Yili Liu, Jingyi Xu, Zeqian Xu, Yilei Huang, Mingliang Zhou, Sihan Lin, Xinquan Jiang, Jiahui Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transcription factor (TF) networks are pivotal regulators of stem/progenitor cell fate. However, the regulatory mechanisms mediated by key TFs in adult periosteal stem/progenitor cells (PSPCs) remain poorly understood, impeding targeted therapies development for craniofacial bone regeneration. By integrating an analysis of regeneration-related, tissue-specific TF networks with insights from embryonic development, we demonstrate that the imprinted TF PLAGL1 is critical for the osteoblast differentiation of PSPCs and that the loss of Plagl1 compromises mandibular bone regeneration. Mechanistically, PLAGL1 transcriptionally activates TF Irx5 synergistically with TF KLF4, thereby inducing the expression of downstream osteogenic genes. Using the CRISPR-dCas9-Tet1-CD/sgRNA system, we develop a differentially methylated region-targeted therapeutic strategy to reactivate the maternal allele of Plagl1, leveraging its imprinted function to promote mandibular bone regeneration. This study delineates the PLAGL1-KLF4-IRX5 regulatory axis controlling osteoblast differentiation of PSPCs and further proposes an application strategy integrating TF modulation with epigenetic regulation for craniofacial bone regeneration.

Introduction

Craniofacial bone defects are susceptible to various conditions, such as trauma, infection, tumors, congenital diseases, and progressive deformable diseases, which can significantly affect the function and appearance of the oral and maxillofacial systems, leading to a diminished quality of life for affected individuals1. The treatment of craniofacial bone defects has always been a challenging task in the clinic because of the need for higher and faster effectiveness1,2. Skeletal stem/progenitor cells (SSPCs), which are characterized by self-renewal and multipotency, are essential for bone regeneration. In recent years, distinct SSPC populations in different cellular and matrix environments, such as the bone marrow, growth plate, periosteum, and calvarial sutures, have been identified and indicated to play different roles in development, homeostasis, and regeneration. Unlike long bones, craniofacial bone regeneration is accomplished mainly by intramembranous ossification resulting from the osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs in the periosteum and sutures3–5. Several markers have been verified to label SSPCs in craniofacial bones, including cathepsin K (Ctsk), paired related homeobox 1 (Prrx1), GLI-Kruppel family member 1 (Gli1), axis inhibition protein 2 (Axin2) and MX dynamin-like GTPase 1(Mx1)6–9. In particular, a previous study has shown that mandibular periosteal cells exhibit considerable plasticity and can adopt the skeletogenic lineage appropriate to their locale through distinctive signaling pathways compared with those derived from the long-bone periosteum10. Due to the special anatomical structures and physiological features of craniofacial bones, understanding the critical regulatory mechanisms of craniofacial periosteal stem/progenitor cells (PSPCs) is urgent for providing insights into therapeutic strategies for the treatment of craniofacial bone defects and the development of more effective regenerative therapies that harness the full potential of endogenous stem cells.

Transcription factors (TFs) constitute a class of sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins that orchestrate spatiotemporal gene expression patterns through combinatorial interactions with cis-regulatory elements in target gene loci11. Emerging evidence suggests that TF networks establish self-reinforcing regulatory circuits that determine and maintain the identity of stem/progenitor cells. For example, the pluripotency and self-renewal capabilities of embryonic stem cells can be maintained by a core TF network of OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG12. Lineage-specific TFs become pivotal once stem cells embark on the path to defined differentiation. Recent studies have explored the application of TFs, including RUNX2, SOX9, PPARγ, MYOD, GATA4, and GATA6 in the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteocytes, chondrocytes, adipocytes, smooth muscle cells, skeletal muscle cells, and cardiomyocytes13. Furthermore, through the introduction of lineage-specific TF combinations, somatic cells can be directly converted into pluripotent stem cells or specific cell types without transitioning through intermediate developmental stages via reprogramming techniques14–16. Subsequently, researchers have increasingly focused on promoting osteogenic differentiation through the precise manipulation of TF expression, showing that transcriptional reprogramming strategies targeting these regulators hold significant promise for bone regeneration therapy17–19.

During craniofacial bone development, lineage-specific TFs act in a combinatorial fashion to specify osteoblastic differentiation20. However, a relative gap in our understanding of the regulatory network of TFs in PSPCs during the regenerative repair of adult craniofacial bone defects exists. A previous study has shown that SSCs can acquire a neural crest fate during distraction osteogenesis and initiate a gene-regulatory program and retrotransposons that are normally active in the primitive cranial neural crest (CNC) during mandible bone regeneration21. In the present study, we compared marker TFs in mesenchymal lineage cells via a single-cell transcriptomic analysis of the mandibular defect area during regeneration and compared the upregulated TFs in postmigratory CNC-derived mandibles with premigratory CNC progenitors to study the specific TF regulatory network in adult PSPCs during mandible regeneration22. Five TFs whose gene expression was upregulated during the osteogenic fate decision of CNC progenitors during development and showed a mesenchymal lineage-specific expression pattern during adult mandible regeneration were enriched, including Prrx1, basonuclin 2 (Bnc2), pleiomorphic adenoma gene-like 1 (Plagl1), nuclear factor I B (Nfib) and X (Nfix). Among these regulators, the expression of Plagl1 has also been shown to be increased during early SSC fate determination in the initial phases of bone repair and regeneration, as observed in both fracture and maxillary sinus floor lifting (intramembranous-like ossification only) models23. Therefore, we speculate that the intriguing and largely unexplored role of PLAGL1 in the osteogenic lineage differentiation of craniofacial PSPCs represents a promising avenue for future research.

PLAGL1 is a zinc finger TF belonging to the “imprinted gene network” (IGN)24. The mammalian genome contains ~150 imprinted genes (IGs)25, each monoallelically expressed in a parent-of-origin-specific manner due to the selective methylation of the maternal or paternal allele. Members of the IGN family play a central role in embryo development26,27 and some remain highly expressed in adult stem cells, whereas the overall expression levels decline postnatally28,29. Interestingly, several IGs that encode TFs, including PLAGL1, seem to be key players in the network of processes associated with tissue development and gene regulation30. Our previous study confirmed that PLAGL1 works with ARID1A to promote the odontoblast differentiation of tooth root progenitors during tooth root development31, suggesting its potential key role during CNC-derived craniofacial tissue development. However, knowledge of the function of the IG TF PLAGL1 in PSPCs during craniofacial bone regeneration is lacking.

In this study, we clarified the value of predicted TF networks in our scRNA-seq bioanalysis and the function of the IG TF PLAGL1 during mandible regeneration by constructing Ctsk+ lineage-specific Plagl1-knockout mice and a mandibular defect model. We found that PLAGL1 was essential for the osteoblast differentiation and that the loss of Plagl1 compromised the osteogenic lineage differentiation, resulting in impaired mandibular bone regeneration. In terms of the mechanism, we found that PLAGL1 modulated transcriptomic profiles and chromatin accessibility in craniofacial bone-related osteogenesis directly and indirectly. In particular, we identified that PLAGL1 cooperates with Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) to activate iroquois homeobox transcription factor 5 (Irx5), promoting the expression of downstream osteogenic genes. Furthermore, the catalytically inactive Cas9 protein (dCas9) fused to the catalytic domain of the epigenetic effector ten-eleven translocation (Tet1-CD) with a target sgRNA system has been reported to activate gene expression in vitro and in vivo by the demethylation of the promoters of target genes32–35, providing powerful tools to manipulate the differentially methylated region (DMR) of Plagl1. Additionally, using the CRISPR-dCas9-Tet1-CD/sgRNA system, we specifically demethylated the DMR of Plagl1 in the maternal allele, thereby accelerating mandibular regeneration. These findings provide valuable insights into the complex regulation of craniofacial bone regeneration, suggesting strategies that combine TF modulation and epigenetic editing for future therapeutic applications in regenerative medicine.

TFs play a pivotal role in determining the fate of stem/progenitor cells by binding to specific DNA sequences and regulating gene transcription, thereby maintaining the delicate balance between pluripotency and lineage specification11. We compared the marker TFs in the mesenchymal group (Supplementary Data 1), and the upregulated TFs in the postmigratory CNC-derived mandible with premigratory CNC progenitors (Supplementary Data 2) to study the important TFs in PSPCs during the early stage of bone regeneration. Five TFs, Prrx1, Bnc2, Plagl1, Nfib, and Nfix, whose gene expression was upregulated during the osteogenic fate decision of CNCs during development (Fig. 1e) and whose expression pattern was mesenchymal lineage-specific during adult mandible regeneration (Fig. 1f), were enriched. Among these regulators, the expression of Plagl1 was also increased during early SSC fate determination in the initial phases of bone repair and regeneration, as observed in both fracture and maxillary sinus floor lifting (intramembranous-like ossification only) models23. In addition to the well-characterized roles of PRRX136,37, BNC138, NFIB39, and NFIX39,40 in craniofacial osteogenesis, the functional significance of PLAGL1 in the osteogenic lineage differentiation of craniofacial PSPCs remains largely unexplored, indicating a promising avenue for future mechanistic investigations.

Previous studies have identified Ctsk+ cells that mediate intramembrane bone formation during regeneration5,9. We found that the expression of Ctsk was enriched mainly in mesenchymal lineage cells in the defect area (Fig. 1g). Using Ctsk-iCre;tdT reporter mice, we demonstrated that a subset of Ctsk+ cells gives rise to SP7+ osteoblasts at the defect site at POD 21, and their abundance was significantly increased compared to uninjured controls (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 2). By pulse-labeling new bone with calcein prior to harvest, we showed that part of derivatives of Ctsk+ cells differentiates into osteocytes embedded within the regenerated bone, thereby directly contributing to the new mineralized matrix (Fig. 1i).

To further determine whether PLAGL1 was associated with mandibular bone regeneration, we evaluated its expression in vivo and found that PLAGL1 is expressed in Ctsk+ lineage cells during mandible regeneration (Fig. 1j). We then performed a more detailed subclustering of osteolineage cells and assessed Plagl1 expression along their differentiation trajectory. The results revealed a continuous developmental trajectory originating from PSPCs, progressing through pre-osteoblast and osteoblast stages, and ultimately reaching the osteocyte stage (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). Along this trajectory, expression of the PSPC marker Ctsk progressively declined, whereas early osteogenic commitment genes Runx2 and Sp7 began to rise during the transition from PSPC to Pre-Ob. Consistent with late-stage maturation, functional osteocyte markers including Dmp1 and Spp1 peaked at the terminal phase of the trajectory (Fig. 1k). Notably, Plagl1 expression was upregulated during early osteoblast differentiation, coinciding with increased Runx2 and Sp7 expression, and downregulated upon osteoblast lineage commitment, as indicated by elevated Dmp1 and Spp1 levels (Fig. 1k). These findings suggest that PLAGL1 may serve as a key TF involved in osteogenic lineage differentiation during mandible regeneration.

The loss of Plagl1 results in impaired mandibular bone regeneration

We generated a Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mouse model to investigate the functional role of PLAGL1 in Ctsk+ cells during mandible regeneration (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The immunofluorescence colocalization analysis revealed PLAGL1 expression within tdT+ cells at bone defect sites in control mice (Ctsk-iCre;tdT), with nearly complete ablation observed in the mutants (Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl;tdT) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Western blot analysis further confirmed the knockout efficiency of Cre-mediated Plagl1 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 3c). We subsequently constructed a round defect with a diameter of 0.8 mm in the mandibles of control and Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice at 2 months of age (Fig. 2a). Micro-CT analysis revealed that the bone volume/total tissue volume (BV/TV), bone surface/tissue volume ratio (BS/TV), and bone mineral density (BMD) decreased significantly with an increasing porosity percentage (Po) in Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice compared with control mice at POD 21 (Fig. 2b, c). Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2d) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 2e) showed that the defects in control mice were well repaired, with massive new bone formation. In contrast, the defects in Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice were much larger, with primarily fibrous tissue and less new bone formation. Given the IG character of PLAGL1, we compared the phenotypes after the maternal or paternal allele of Plagl1 was knocked out. We found that Plagl1 heterozygotes had phenotypes resembling homozygotes if the knockout allele was provided paternally but had no obvious phenotype if the knockout allele was provided maternally (Supplementary Fig. 4).

PLAGL1 promotes the osteoblast differentiation of Ctsk+ cells

We detected osteoblastic and osteoclastic activities to determine whether the impaired mandibular bone regeneration was due to attenuated osteogenesis by osteoblasts or excessive bone resorption by osteoclasts, respectively. Using immunofluorescence staining and quantification, we detected fewer RUNX2+ and SP7+ osteoblasts derived from Ctsk+ lineage cells after the loss of Plagl1 (Fig. 3a–d), suggesting that PLAGL1 is required for the osteoblast differentiation of Ctsk+ cells. Previous studies of tooth root development have shown that PLAGL1 is involved in cell cycle arrest, which is important for differentiation31. Although Ctsk also labels osteoclasts, in our present scRNA-seq data, Plagl1 was specifically expressed in mandibular mesenchymal cells during regeneration after surgery, which did not express any other cell cluster, including Clusters 2 and 11, representing monocytic cells and osteoclast precursors (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 5a). In addition, no expression of PLAGL1 was detected in myeloid lineage cells in LysM-CreER;tdT reporter mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Therefore, excluding the direct regulatory function of PLAGL1 in osteoclasts is safe, although an indirect effect on the function of osteoclasts after the loss of Plagl1 in osteoprogenitors could occur. Using tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, we detected no significant difference in the ratio of bone resorption surfaces between the two groups, suggesting that the loss of Plagl1 in Ctsk+ cells did not apparently affect the number of osteoclasts (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). These results suggest that impaired mandibular bone regeneration is due to compromised osteogenic lineage differentiation.

We further validated the above findings by collecting and evaluating the osteoblastic differentiation capacity of mandibular PSPCs from control and Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice (Fig. 4a). We found that the expression of osteoblast differentiation-associated genes, such as Runx2, Col1α1, Sp7, Alpl, Dmp1 and osteocalcin, was markedly lower in Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice than in control mice after osteogenic induction (Fig. 4b, c). In addition, using immunofluorescence staining, we found that the numbers of RUNX2+, SP7+ and DMP1+ osteoblasts and mature osteoblasts decreased after the loss of Plagl1 (Fig. 4d–f). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and quantification further revealed that the osteoblastic differentiation was inhibited after Plagl1 knockout in Ctsk+ lineage cells (Fig. 4g, h). PLAGL1 drives osteogenesis via transcriptional and chromatin remodeling

To further decipher the molecular mechanisms by which PLAGL1 regulates the osteoblastic differentiation of PSPCs, we performed bulk a mRNA sequencing analysis to compare the gene transcription profiles of periosteal mesenchymal cells from control and Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice after 3 days of osteogenic induction. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles between the control group and the Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl group (Fig. 5a). We performed a differential expression analysis to pinpoint specific gene alterations, and identified 208 upregulated and 560 downregulated genes (fold change >1.5, p < 0.05), indicating that PLAGL1 acted as a major transcriptional activator during osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 5b, c and Supplementary Data 3). We performed a Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to further delineate the biological significance of PLAGL1-regulated transcriptional changes. Strikingly, the top enriched biological processes associated with the downregulated genes in Plagl1-knockout PSPCs were closely associated with skeletal development, including ossification, extracellular matrix organization, and skeletal system morphogenesis. In contrast, the upregulated genes were not enriched in osteogenesis-related pathways; instead, they were associated with muscle cell differentiation and cell junction assembly (Fig. 5d). Given that lineage-determining TFs typically prime chromatin accessibility to activate target genes and considering the widespread alterations in gene expression observed after loss of Plagl1, particularly the downregulation of genes involved in osteogenesis-related pathways, we hypothesize that PLAGL1 regulates the osteogenic commitment not only via direct transcriptional activation but also through direct or indirect remodeling of the epigenetic landscape. We next performed genome-wide chromatin accessibility profiling using ATAC-seq in control and Plagl1-knockout periosteal mesenchymal cells after 3 days of osteogenic induction to investigate this possibility. PCA revealed distinct chromatin accessibility profiles after the loss of Plagl1 (Fig. 5g). The differential analysis revealed 15,221 differentially accessible regions (DARs, |log2FoldChange| ≥ 0.2, p < 0.05), with 6593 DARs displaying decreased accessibility (annotated to 4481 genes) and 8628 exhibiting increased accessibility (annotated to 5300 genes) (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Data 4 and 5). We further investigated the correlation between transcriptional changes and chromatin accessibility by performing an integrated analysis of mRNA-seq and ATAC-seq data from Plagl1-knockout PSPCs. Surprisingly, we found that genes associated with regions with decreased accessibility were significantly downregulated (p = 1 × 10−10, Fig. 5i), whereas those linked to regions with increased accessibility were not correlated (p = 0.40, Fig. 5i). The integrated analysis of DEGs and DARs revealed that more than half of the PLAGL1-regulated DEGs co-occurred with accessibility alterations (Supplementary Data 6). Specifically, 52/208 upregulated DEGs with apparent increased chromatin accessibility, 70/208 upregulated DEGs with apparent decreased chromatin accessibility, 162/560 downregulated DEGs with apparent increased accessibility, and 201/560 downregulated DEGs with apparent decreased chromatin accessibility were identified (Fig. 5j). These findings indicate that PLAGL1 works mainly as a transcriptional activator by increasing chromatin accessibility at target osteoblast-associated genes.

In summary, these results demonstrate that PLAGL1 plays a pivotal role in regulating the transcriptional and chromatin landscape of osteogenic genes. The deletion of Plagl1 leads to widespread changes in both gene expression and chromatin accessibility, resulting in the dysregulation of key pathways necessary for osteogenic differentiation.

We conducted Klf4 siRNA assays to validate the role of KLF4 during mandible regeneration. After osteogenic induction, Irx5, Alpl, Sp7, and Opn expression were suppressed after Klf4 knockdown (Fig. 8e). ALP staining further showed that osteoblastic differentiation was inhibited after Klf4 knockdown (Fig. 8f). Consistent with our findings, KLF4 expressed in the Sp7+ lineage was reported orchestrating osteoblast differentiation, with Klf4 haploinsufficiency in the Sp7+ lineage leading to mandibular hypoplasia phenotypes53. The putative KLF4-activated genes, which were identified among the downregulated DEGs in the Klf4 haploinsufficiency Sp7+ lineage, presented significantly decreased expression in Plagl1-knockout group, suggesting a potential functional relationship between KLF4 and PLAGL1 during osteoblast differentiation, especially with the activation of Irx5 (Fig. 8g and Supplementary Data 10).

Coimmunoprecipitation was performed to validate the direct physical interaction between KLF4 and PLAGL1 in regulating Irx5 expression (Fig. 8h). Luciferase assays revealed that KLF4 overexpression significantly increased Irx5 promoter activity (Fig. 8i), demonstrating the transcriptional regulatory role of KLF4. Furthermore, we conducted CUT&Tag-seq of KLF4 and identified a common binding profile of PLAGL1 and KLF4 on the promoter region of Irx5, while Plagl1 loss decreased KLF4 binding at the Irx5 promoter (Fig. 8j). Collectively, these results indicate the cooperative regulation of Irx5 expression by KLF4 and PLAGL1.

Notably, cross-regulatory analysis revealed no reciprocal transcriptional control between PLAGL1 and KLF4—Klf4 expression remained unaltered following Plagl1 knockout (Fig. 8k), while Plagl1 levels showed no changes upon Klf4 knockdown (Fig. 8l). The mutually independent expression patterns between PLAGL1 and KLF4 suggest parallel rather than hierarchical regulatory positioning in osteogenic networks.

PLAGL1-targeted therapy enhances mandible regeneration

Studies have demonstrated that DNA methylation, rather than genetic mutation, is a key mechanism that suppresses PLAGL1 expression in breast cancer patients and that a partial restoration of PLAGL1 expression can be achieved in certain breast cancer cell lines through methylation inhibitor treatment54. Additional evidence indicates that the hypomethylation of Plagl1 DMR is associated with transient neonatal diabetes, and maternal factors such as alcohol consumption and vitamin B2 intake can modulate DMR methylation55,56. These findings collectively highlight the PLAGL1 DMR as a dynamically regulatable epigenetic locus. Therefore, we speculated that a demethylating agent could induce the expression of Plagl1 in PSPCs and promote osteoblast differentiation.

We treated PSPCs with the methylation-interfering agent decitabine (5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine) and compared Plagl1 expression in control and treated cells. An increase in the expression of Plagl1 was detected in the 0.1–1 μM concentration groups after 3 days of treatment (Fig. 9a). After 3 days of osteogenic induction, decitabine at 1 µM activated osteoblast differentiation, with increased expression levels of Runx2, Sp7, Alpl, Col1α1 and Dmp1 (Supplementary Fig. 8). This promotion was partially dependent on PLAGL1, as it was compromised in Plagl1-deficient cells (Fig. 9b). While decitabine cannot specifically demethylate the Plagl1 DMR, we leveraged the CRISPR dCas9-based targeted DNA demethylation system and combined the CRISPR dCas9-Tet1-CD and sgRNA to target the region upstream of the Plagl1 DMR (GenBank: AF314094.1), to activate the imprinted function in the maternal allele. The transfection of CRISPR dCas9-Tet-CD and sgRNA1 or sgRNA2 via the adenoviral vector (AdV) led to an approximately 1.5-fold increase in Plagl1 gene transcription (Fig. 9c). After osteogenic induction, this gene target editing system effectively activated osteoblast differentiation with an over 1.5–4-fold increase in Runx2, Sp7, Alpl, and Col1α1 gene transcription (Fig. 9d). We conducted targeted bisulfite sequencing of the DMR in AdV-dCas9-Tet1-CD- and AdV-DMR-sgRNA1-transfected mandibular periosteal mesenchymal cells after 3 days of osteogenic induction to validate the demethylation effect, with AdV-EGFP-transfected cells used as the control group. The methylation levels at the Plagl1 DMR were quantified and compared for each group. The results revealed that the dCas9-Tet1-CD/sgRNA1-cotransfected groups presented significantly lower methylation levels than did the dCas9-Tet1-CD-only group or the EGFP-transfected group, in which the two groups presented comparable DNA methylation levels (Fig. 9e). Taken together, these findings confirmed that this dCas9-based demethylation system could indeed upregulate Plagl1 mRNA transcription and promote osteoblastic differentiation through sequence-specific demethylation of the Plagl1 DMR.

We used this epigenetic editing system loaded in an injectable methacrylated silk fibroin (Sil-MA) hydrogel system for mandibular bone regeneration in vivo to further validate its potential therapeutic applications in bone regeneration57. Micro-CT scans and quantification suggested that the CRISPR/dCas9-based targeted DNA demethylation system could significantly promote rapid mandible regeneration at the early stage of POD 14 compared with the dCas9-Tet1-CD-only group or the EGFP-transfected group (Fig. 9f, g). H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that the new bone formation and collagen deposition in the mandible defects in the targeted DNA demethylation group, whereas mainly fibrous tissue was observed in the defects in the control groups (Fig. 9h, i). These findings suggest that this system could enhance osteoblast differentiation and bone regeneration and is a promising strategy that combines TF modulation and epigenetic regulation for future therapeutic applications in regenerative medicine.

Through multi-omics data integration analyses, we further confirmed that the imprinted TF PLAGL1 cooperates with KLF4 to activate the craniofacial bone-related TF Irx5, promoting the expression of downstream osteogenic genes, and that its loss impairs mandibular bone regeneration (Fig. 10b). Discussion

TF networks determine the pluripotency and lineage specification of stem cells and hold the powerful potential for reprogramming in regenerative medicine. The osteoblast-specific TFs, RUNX2, and SP7, in combination with OCT4 and RXOL transduction, were reported to reprogram fibroblasts into functional osteoblasts without passing through an intermediate pluripotent cell type58. Subsequent studies have successfully employed diverse TF combinations to reprogram fibroblasts into osteoblasts59,60, establishing TF-mediated reprogramming as a feasible cell-based therapy for bone regeneration. Notably, mandibular PSPCs exhibit TF regulatory mechanisms distinct from those of their long bone counterparts, particularly in terms of TF HOX expression patterns, which may underpin their enhanced plasticity and regenerative capacity10. This divergence underscores the imperative to elucidate craniofacial TF networks for craniofacial bone regeneration, although mechanistic insights remain limited. Our study delineates a transcriptional regulatory network in the osteoblast differentiation of Ctsk+ cells during mandibular regeneration, with Plagl1-knockout experiments functionally validating its predictive utility. The PLAGL1-KLF4-IRX5 axis identified here represents a promising transcriptional toolkit for targeted craniofacial bone reprogramming therapies.

Transcriptomic profiling in our study revealed that PLAGL1 functions as a master transcriptional activator, with its ablation resulting in a threefold greater number of downregulated versus upregulated genes. The GO analysis of DEGs from Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice compared with controls revealed the significant enrichment of extracellular matrix structural organization pathways. Consistent with these findings, PLAGL1-regulated genes in Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells are predominantly associated with extracellular matrix-activated signaling cascades, including cytoskeleton binding, and cell adhesion and movement24. This striking conservation of extracellular matrix-related transcriptional control across distinct cellular contexts—PSPCs and neuroblasts—indicates the fundamental role of PLAGL1 in orchestrating the matrix remodeling processes essential for tissue morphogenesis and repair. We performed CUT&Tag sequencing in periosteal mesenchymal cells to delineate the direct transcriptional targets of PLAGL1, revealing that PLAGL1 binding peaks were predominantly localized to promoter regions. This promoter-centric binding pattern aligns with previous ChIP-seq findings from PLAGL1-transfected Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells, which revealed the preferential occupancy at G4C4-like motifs near TSS regions24. The conserved genomic targeting of PLAGL1 across evolutionarily divergent cell types suggests a fundamental mechanism for its transcriptional regulatory function.

In addition to its canonical role as a TF, our findings reveal the broader regulatory influence of PLAGL1 on chromatin architecture. An integrative analysis of ATAC-seq and mRNA-seq data from Plagl1-deficient cells revealed that DEGs were more strongly associated with regions of decreased chromatin accessibility than with regions with increased accessibility, suggesting that PLAGL1 functions primarily as a transcriptional activator by potentiating chromatin openness at target loci. Analysis of the 201 downregulated DEGs with reduced chromatin accessibility after Plagl1 knockout showed that only 47 genes had significant reductions in accessibility specifically at their promoter regions, while others exhibited reduced accessibility at non-promoter regions. This distribution indicates that although PLAGL1 binding is enriched at promoters, its loss broadly compromises chromatin accessibility, suggesting it may also function at distal regulatory elements, either directly or indirectly—an important avenue for future research. Mechanistically, we speculate that PLAGL1 collaborates with chromatin remodellers to modulate accessibility: our previous work established its cooperation with the chromatin remodeller ARID1A during tooth root development31, whereas biochemical studies revealed its scaffolding role in enhancing PCAF/p300 complex assembly, thereby increasing histone acetyltransferase activity towards histone H450. Given that histone H4 tail acetylation (H4Kac) is a well-established modulator of chromatin architecture and accessibility61, we propose that PLAGL1 may increase the rate of H4Kac or cooperate with chromatin remodellers, enabling increased chromatin accessibility and efficient transcription. This dual capacity to engage sequence-specific DNA binding and orchestrate epigenetic modifications positions PLAGL1 as a multifunctional regulator of transcriptional competence, although detailed mechanistic validation is warranted.

Through integrative motif and bioinformatic analyses, we identified that PLAGL1 regulates Irx5 via cooperative binding with KLF4. This collaborative regulatory mechanism parallels findings in neuronal differentiation, where PLAGL1 coordinates developmental pathways by coupling sequence-specific DNA binding with coactivator recruitment50. Previous studies established that KLF4 alone cannot initiate osteoblast differentiation but requires cobinding with osteogenic TFs to regulate skeletal development53. These findings align with our observation that PLAGL1 and KLF4 exhibit overlapping binding profiles at the Irx5 promoter during osteogenic differentiation. Notably, Plagl1 deficiency did not alter Klf4 expression levels, mirroring reports that PLAGL1 overexpression similarly preserves cofactor expression (e.g., p53/p63/p73)50. This conserved pattern suggests that PLAGL1-KLF4 cooperate through chromatin-level interactions rather than through transcriptional cross-regulation. We fully acknowledge that additional regulatory partners likely exist and warrant further mechanistic investigation to fully delineate the complexity of the transcriptional network.

A methodological limitation arises from our reliance on in vitro-expanded periosteal mesenchymal cells subjected to osteogenic induction to model in vivo differentiation—a pragmatic approach to obtain sufficient material for multi-omics analyses. Future lineage sorting methods with scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic studies will validate these mechanisms within intact craniofacial regeneration contexts.

In 2023, the FDA approved the first CRISPR-based gene-editing therapy for sickle cell disease, marking a milestone in CRISPR-mediated therapeutics. While TF-mediated reprogramming holds therapeutic potential for bone regeneration, current CRISPR-based genome-editing systems that rely on DNA double-strand breaks are prone to irreversible genomic alterations, limiting their clinical utility. Epigenome editing employs artificial TFs, such as the CRISPR/dCas9 system, to modulate gene expression through heritable yet reversible epigenetic modifications33. This paradigm shift, which preserves genomic integrity while enabling precise transcriptional control, has profound implications for both fundamental research and therapeutic development35. Our study proposes leveraging the CRISPR/dCas9-Tet1-CD system to reprogram the epigenetic state of the Plagl1 DMR without requiring exogenous lineage-determining factors. This approach effectively circumvents the risks associated with undesired permanent mutations in target genomes and irreversible interference.

While this strategy has significant potential for achieving precision-controlled osteogenesis in mandibular bone regeneration, with promising translational applicability in craniofacial reconstruction therapies, we acknowledge critical limitations requiring resolution for clinical translation, particularly with respect to delivery system optimization, targeting specificity, and therapeutic efficacy enhancement. Notably, sequences encoding dCas9-Tet1-CD and sgRNA exceed the packaging threshold of adeno-associated virus, necessitating the use of the AdV in our studies, despite its inherent immunogenicity risks. Collective advances in epigenetic mapping, editor engineering, and delivery optimization position sgRNA-guided dCas9 demethylation as a transformative platform bridging chromatin biology studies to targeted bone regeneration therapies.

In summary, this study revealed that PLAGL1 regulated Irx5 by binding to its promoter in cooperation with another key TF, KLF4, during the osteoblastic differentiation, providing insights into the potential molecular mechanisms involved in the osteogenic commitment. These findings advance our understanding of epigenetic‒transcriptional crosstalk in skeletal regeneration and establish the PLAGL1 as a therapeutic target for precision craniofacial reconstruction, highlighting the synergistic potential of combined TF modulation and epigenetic editing for future therapeutic applications in regenerative medicine.

Methods

Mice

H11-Ctsk-iCre mice (strain no. T006950), Plagl1flox/flox mice (strain no. T038311), and LysM-CreER (strain no. T052789) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China), and Prrx1-Cre mice (strain no. 005584) and tdT (tdTomato, strain no. 007909) mouse lines from the Jackson Laboratory (Maine, USA) were used and crossbred in this study. All the mice were used for the analysis, regardless of sex. All the mice were housed under pathogen-free conditions at a constant ambient temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (55 ± 10%), with an alternating 12-h light/dark cycle. All the mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide overdose followed by cervical dislocation. All the animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Ninth People’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (SH9H-2022-A926-1).

Mandibular defect model

Under anesthesia, an incision of the angle of the mandible was made, and the muscles were bluntly dissected to expose the mandibles of the mice. A drill hole defect 0.8 mm in diameter was made approximately 1 mm from the lower border of the mandible and distal to the incisor. The bone debris was flushed out with a sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution. Prior to layered wound closure, the Sil-MA hydrogel encapsulating either AdV or LV vectors was injected into the defect site.

The Sil-MA powder was prepared by the following procedure. Briefly, silkworm (Bombyx mori) cocoons were boiled in an Na₂CO₃ solution to remove sericin. The degummed silk fibers were thoroughly dried and subsequently dissolved in a 9.3 M LiBr solution at 60 °C. After complete dissolution, GMA was added, and the grafting reaction was performed at 60 °C with continuous stirring for 3 h. The resulting reaction mixture was transferred into dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cut-off of 10 kDa and dialyzed against distilled water for 3 days. After dialysis, the mixture was centrifuged at 9000 × g for 20 min to remove precipitates, frozen, and lyophilized to yield the Sil-MA powder, which was stored at −80 °C for future use57. The freeze-dried Sil-MA powder was dissolved in PBS containing 0.25% (w/v) of the photoinitiator LAP at a final concentration of 30% (w/v). The Sil-MA solution was then thoroughly mixed with the particular viral vector at a volume ratio of 1:2 and subsequently crosslinked under UV light in situ. The mice were sacrificed and the mandible was harvested from each mouse at POD 14 or 21 for micro-CT imaging and histologic and molecular examinations.

Micro-CT analysis

The micro-CT analysis of the fixed mandible was performed using a SkyScan 1176 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) at 50 kVp, 450 µA, and a resolution of 9 μm. The obtained images were reconstructed with NRecon software (v1.7.1.0, Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). The defect region was analyzed for BV/TV, BS/TV, BMD, and Po using the program CTAn (v 1.16, Bruker, Kontich, Belgium).

Histological analysis

Mouse mandibles were dissected and fixed, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA in PBS for 1–3 weeks depending on the age of the samples. The decalcified samples were dehydrated with serial ethanol and xylene solutions and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded samples were then cut into sections with a thickness of 4 µm using a microtome (Leica). H&E staining (Servicebio, G1005), Masson’s trichrome staining (Leagene, DC0033), and TRAP staining (Sigma, 387 A) were conducted according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol.

For the dynamic bone histomorphometric analysis, double fluorescence labeling was performed62. Briefly, calcein (Sigma-Aldrich, C0875, 25 mg/kg) and Alizarin Red S (Sigma-Aldrich, A5533, 30 mg/kg) were administered to the mice by intraperitoneal injection at 10 d and 17 d after surgery for double labeling, respectively. Calcein was administered to the mice by intraperitoneal injection at 3 d before collection for single labelling, which revealed the newly formed bone. Then, the uncalcified bone tissues were dehydrated and embedded before being sectioned at 8 µm. A Nikon Eclipse Ti-U microscope (Nikon, Japan) with NIS-Elements software (v4.5000.1117.0), Zeiss Axio Scope A1 microscope (ZEISS, German) with DP2-TWAIN software (v3.0.0.6212) and Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) were used to acquire images.

Immunofluorescence staining

The decalcified samples were dehydrated in serial sucrose solutions and embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura). The OCT-embedded samples were cryosectioned at 8 µm using a cryostat (Leica CM1850), followed by staining. For immunofluorescence staining, cryosections were soaked in a blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in a blocking solution at 4 °C overnight. After being washed three times with PBS, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. The antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining were as follows: SP7 antibody (Abcam, ab22552, 1:100); DMP1 antibody (Affinity, DF8825, 1:100); KI67 antibody (Cell Signaling, 9129, 1:100); PLAGL1 antibody (Novus, NBP3-41420, 1:50); RUNX2 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-41185, 1:100); goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11008, 1:200); donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21207, 1:200); goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21244, 1:200). A Nikon Eclipse Ti-U microscope (Nikon, Japan) with NIS-Elements software (v4.5000.1117.0), Zeiss Axio Scope A1 Microscope with DP2-TWAIN software, and Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope were used to acquire images.

Cell culture and siRNA transfection

Mandibular angle blocks were dissected from 2-month-old mice. Most of the muscle and connective tissues were removed before the bones were flushed with ice-cold PBS to remove blood and bone marrow. Under a dissecting microscope, the periosteum was peeled from the bone surface63. The resulting periosteum was digested with type 1 collagenase (Worthington, LS004194, 2 mg/mL) and dispase II (Roche, 04942078001, 2 mg/mL) in HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14175) for 20 min at 37 °C. The digestion mixture was then filtered through a 70 μm filter, and the resulting cells were cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS (Cyagen Biosciences, OriCell FBSAD-01011-500) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. For osteoblast differentiation, the cells were cultured in a complete medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, A4544), 5 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, G9422), and 10−7 M dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, D4902). The medium was changed every 2 days until the indicated time points were reached.

For the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor treatment experiments, the cells were treated with either vehicle or various concentrations (0.1–1 μM) of decitabine (5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine, AmBeed, A156645) 24 h after plating, followed by osteogenic induction for the indicated times.

For siRNA transfection, the mouse target sequences were as follows: Klf4 siRNA1 sense 5′-GCGUGAGGAACUCUCUCACAU-3′, antisense 5′-AUGUGAGAGAGUUCCUCACGC-3′; Klf4 siRNA2 sense 5′-ACCUAUACCAAGAGUUCUCAU-3′, antisense 5′-AUGAGAACUCUUGGUAUAGGU-3′; and control siRNA: sense 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, antisense 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′. The final concentration of each siRNA was 25 nM. Forty-eight hours after transfection, periosteal mesenchymal cells were subjected to osteogenic induction for the indicated times.

Lentivirus transfection

The Plagl1-overexpressing lentivirus (LV-Plagl1-OE-HA), Irx5-overexpressing lentivirus (LV-Irx5-OE), klf4-overexpressing lentivirus (LV-Klf4-OE-FLAG), and control lentivirus (LV-ZsGreen) were purchased from Hanbio Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai). For lentivirus transfection, 2 × 105 periosteal mesenchymal cells were seeded in 12-well plates in α-MEM, cultured overnight, and infected with the lentivirus expressing either the target gene or the control lentivirus at a multiplicity of 10 for LV-Plagl1-OE-HA and LV-Klf4-OE-FLAG and a multiplicity of 1 for LV-Irx5-OE. Two days after transfection, the cells were induced to undergo osteogenesis for the indicated times.

For in vivo application, LV-Irx5-OE (3 × 106 TU per site) or its control lentivirus (LV-ZsGreen, 3 × 106 TU per site) encapsulated in the Sil-MA hydrogel were injected into the mandible defect and subsequently crosslinked under UV light. The mice were sacrificed and the mandible was harvested from each mouse at POD 21 for micro-CT imaging and histologic and molecular examinations.

Adenovirus transfection

Recombinant adenoviruses expressing the dCas9-Tet1-CD cDNA (dCas9-SV40 NLS-Tet1-CD)33, Plagl1 DMR CpG sgRNA1 (AGTCTGGGCTTTACCAGCGA) and Plagl1 DMR CpG sgRNA2 (CCAGGCCCTCCCCGTGAACT) targeting the upstream of Plagl1 DMR (GenBank: AF314094.1) were prepared using the pAdEasy vector system. Adenoviruses harboring green fluorescent protein (AdV-EGFP) were used as controls. The propagated recombinant adenoviruses in the HEK293 cells were purified, and the titer of the virus was measured by performing plaque assays.

For adenovirus transfection, 2 × 105 periosteal mesenchymal cells were seeded in 12-well plates in α-MEM, cultured overnight, and infected with an adenovirus expressing either dCas9-Tet1-CD and the DMR sgRNA1 or sgRNA2 or control adenovirus at a multiplicity of 100. Two days after transfection, the cells were induced to undergo osteogenesis for the indicated times. For in vivo application, a volume of 6 μl of complex with AdV-dCas9-Tet1-CD (2 × 108 pfu per site) and AdV-DMR-sgRNA1 (2 × 108 pfu per site) or an equal volume of control adenovirus mixed with the Sil-MA hydrogel at a volume ratio of 1:1:1 was injected in situ at mandible bone defects and subsequently crosslinked under UV light. The mice were sacrificed and the mandible was harvested from each mouse at POD 14 for micro-CT imaging and histologic and molecular examinations.

ALP activity assay

ALP staining and quantification were performed to examine the osteogenic ability of the periosteal osteogenic progenitors. Briefly, periosteal osteogenic progenitors were induced to undergo osteogenic differentiation, and ALP staining was performed. At the corresponding time points, the cells were washed twice with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by two additional washes with PBS. Then, the cells were incubated with ALP staining solution (Beyotime, C3206) for 30 min. After two washes with PBS, the stained cells were visualized and photographed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti-U Microscope (Nikon) with NIS-Elements software (v4.5000.1117.0). For quantification, the analysis was conducted using an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (Beyotime, P0321S).

qPCR analysis

For the qPCR analysis, RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Sigma, T9424) and reverse-transcribed with the Prime Script RT master kit (TakaRa Bio Inc., RR036A). The relative amounts of each mRNA transcript were subsequently analyzed using the Roche LightCycler 480 system (v1.5.1.74) with qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, 11201). The β-actin was used as an internal control. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Western blot and coimmunoprecipitation

For western blot analyze, total protein was obtained and homogenized in RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling, 9806s) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32959). Western blot was performed using a standard protocol, and signals were detected using the UVITEC Alliance system (v16.0.3.0). The antibodies used in western blot were as follows: PLAGL1 antibody (Novus, NBP3-41420, 1:500), DMP1 antibody (Affinity, DF8825, 1:1000), RUNX2 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-41185, 1:1000), osteocalcin antibody (ABclonal, A6205, 1:1000), β-actin antibody (Bioworld, BS67001, 1:2000), and rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated antibody (R&D, HAF008, 1:1000).

For coimmunoprecipitation, after 3 days of osteoblast induction, LV-Plagl1-HA- or LV-Klf4-Flag-transfected periosteal mesenchymal cells were harvested, lysed, and immunoprecipitated using the anti-HA Agarose beads (Proteintech, no. atak) or anti-FLAG Agarose beads (Proteintech, no. ffak). The immune complexes were washed and subjected to immunoblotting with HA (Cell Signaling, 3724, 1:1000), KLF4 (Proteintech,11880-1-AP, 1:1000), FLAG (Cell Signaling, 14793, 1:1000), and PLAGL1 (Novus, NBP3-41420, 1:500) antibodies.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

The Irx5 promoter region (2.2 kb, Chr8: 93,082,377-93,084,576) was synthesized, cloned, and inserted into the pGL3-basic luciferase reporter as the pGL3-Irx5 promoter. For transfection, 293 T cells (1 × 105) were seeded in 24-well plates in α-MEM, cultured overnight, and cotransfected with the above reporter clones (200 ng) and Renilla luciferase plasmids (pRL-TK) (100 ng) together with Plagl1-overexpressing plasmids (200 ng), Klf4-overexpressing plasmids (200 ng) or control plasmids (200 ng) using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000008) and Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31985062) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were lysed with a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Beyotime, RG027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Renilla luciferase was used as an internal standard control, and the normalized reporter activities of all the samples were compared. Each group had six replicate samples. Independent experiments were repeated in triplicate.

scRNA-seq analysis

The dissected mandibles were pooled for digestion with 4 ml of HBSS, 40 µl of collagenase II (Sigma C6885), 8 µl of DNase (Applichem, A3778) and 8 µl of Liberase TL (Roche 5401020001) at 37 °C for a total of 15 min, with trituration for 15 s every 2 min using a 1 ml pipet. Single cells were then resuspended in red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, R1010), incubated for 2 min, and then pelleted by centrifugation at 125 × g for 5 min. Single cells were loaded on a 10X Genomics GemCode single-cell instrument. Libraries were generated and sequenced from the cDNAs with Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3 exactly according to the manufacturer’s instructions and subjected to Illumina NovaSeq. Quality control, alignment, quantification, and aggregation of sample count matrices were performed using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

The raw sequencing output data were processed using CellRanger 7.1.0 to obtain a gene-cell data matrix. Strict quality control (QC) was applied to filter low-quality cells. Cells only with more than 300 genes, fewer than 20% mitochondrial gene content, and more than 1000 total UMI counts were retained. To minimize technical artifacts, potential doublets were predicted and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4). Post-QC data were normalized and subjected to Principal Component Analysis. To correct for batch effects across samples, we applied Harmony (v1.2.0) for data integration. The top 2000 genes with the highest variability among cells were identified using Seurat’s FindVariableGenes function. These highly variable genes were used for principal component analysis with the Seurat package (version 5.1.0, R 4.4.1 library). All clustering was unsupervised, without driver genes. The cell groupings were determined by the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) method using the Louvain algorithm. Mesenchymal lineage cells (Clusters 2, 6, and 11) were extracted and re-clustered following a consistent computational workflow. Cells directly associated with osteogenesis were isolated from these mesenchymal sub-clusters and subjected to further dimensionality reduction and clustering. All marker genes were determined for each cluster using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function with the MAST (v1.30.0) test. Pseudotime trajectory analysis of the osteogenic sub-clusters during mandibular regeneration was performed using Monocle3 (v1.3.7).

mRNA-seq analysis

For the mRNA-seq analysis, libraries were the prepared using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit and then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw reads were filtered using Cutadapt (v1.15) and aligned with the GRCm39 genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5. The read count values for each gene were compared using HTSeq (v0.9.1). The differential expression of genes was subsequently analyzed using the R package DESeq2 (v1.44.0). For visualization, the read counts were normalized using the trimmed mean of M values method and analyzed with the R packages Pheatmap (v1.0.12) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1). The GO enrichment analysis and GSEA were performed using the R package ClusterProfiler (v4.12.0) to functionally annotate the relevant genes and identify enriched biological processes and signaling pathways.

mRNA-seq data from CNC populations were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (accession number GSE89434), and mRNA-seq data from Klf4 conditional knockout osteoblasts were downloaded from the China National Center for Bioinformation (accession number CRA003341). The data were downloaded in raw form (fastq) and processed using the same pipeline as described above.

ATAC-seq analysis

After 3 days of osteogenic induction, mandibular periosteal mesenchymal cells from control and Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice were used for the ATAC-seq analysis. A total of 1 × 10⁵ cells from each group were used to prepare the ATAC-seq libraries with the Hyperactive ATAC-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, TD711) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transposed DNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X-plus platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads were filtered using trim_galore (v0.6.10). The clean reads were subsequently aligned to the GRCm39 mouse genome using Bowtie 2 (v2.5.4). Primary aligned reads were processed through duplicate marking using GATK4 (v4.5.5.0), followed by duplicate removal, unmapped and multimapped reads exclusion, and quality filtering (MAPQ > 20) with samtools (v1.21). Tn5 transposase-mediated sequence offset correction was subsequently performed using the alignmentSieve function in deepTools (v3.5.5). Then the processed reads were called for peaks using MACS3 (v3.0.2), with the threshold set at q < 0.05 and the default parameters. For differential accessibility analysis, we used DiffBind (v3.16.0) to identify consensus peaks (detected in ≥2 biological samples with normalized signal intensity >1 reads per kilobase per million mapped reads [RPKM]) coupled with edgeR (v4.2.0) to identify differential accessibility regions (|log2Foldchange| > 0.2, p < 0.05) between samples from control and Ctsk-iCre;Plagl1fl/fl mice. All differential accessibility regions were then annotated using HOMER (v4.11.1) with default parameters. For visualization, deepTools (v3.5.5) was implemented to generate bigWig files, heatmap and profile plots using 10-bp genomic bins. For Gene Ontology enrichment analysis, R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.0) was utilized to functionally annotate the relevant genes and identify enriched biological processes and signaling pathways. For motif enrichment analysis, we used AME (v5.0.2) with the same background model and negative control. AME tool of the MEME suite provides motifs that are enriched compared to the shuffled background. We used motif file “JASPAR2024_CORE_vertebrates_non-redundant_pfms_meme.txt”, downloaded from https://jaspar.elixir.no/download/data/2024/CORE/JASPAR2024_CORE_vertebrates_non-redundant_pfms_meme.txt, to test for enrichment.

CUT&Tag-seq analysis

After 3 days of osteogenic induction, mandibular periosteal mesenchymal cells from different treatment groups were subjected to CUT&Tag sequencing with the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme Biotech, TD904) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 × 10⁵ cells per biological sample were centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min, resuspended in PBS buffer, and immobilized on ConA magnetic beads. Cells were permeabilized with 0.05% digitonin, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies: H3K4me3 (Cell Signaling, 5326, 1:100), HA (Cell Signaling, 3724, 1:100), FLAG (Cell Signaling, 14793, 1:100). Normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling, 2729, concentration-matched to target antibodies served as a negative control. After washing, samples were incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. The pA-Tn5 transposase complex was then assembled on antibody-bound chromatin through 1 h incubation, followed by tagmentation in optimized buffer at 37 °C for 1 h to fragment DNA. Purified DNA fragments were amplified by PCR using dual-indexed primers (i5/i7) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X-plus platform with 150 bp paired-end reads.

The raw reads were filtered using trim_galore (v0.6.10). The clean reads were subsequently aligned to the GRCm39 mouse genome using Bowtie 2 (v2.5.4). Primary aligned reads were processed through duplicate marking using GATK4 (v4.5.5.0), followed by duplicate removal, unmapped and multimapped reads exclusion, and quality filtering (MAPQ > 20) with samtools (v1.21). Tn5 transposase-mediated sequence offset correction was subsequently performed using the alignmentSieve function in deepTools (v3.5.5). Then the processed reads were called for peaks using MACS3 (v3.0.2). All narrowpeaks were then annotated using HOMER (v4.11.1) with default parameters. For visualization, deepTools (v3.5.5) was implemented to generate bigWig files, heatmap and profile plots using 10-bp genomic bins. For Gene Ontology enrichment analysis, we used the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.0) to functionally annotate the relevant genes and identify enriched biological processes and signaling pathways.

Targeted bisulfite sequencing assay

DNA extraction and bisulfite conversion were performed in AdV-dCas9-Tet1-CD- and AdV-Plagl1-DMR sgRNA1-transfected mandibular periosteal mesenchymal cells after 3 days of osteogenic induction, with AdV-dCas9-Tet1-CD- or AdV-EGFP-transfected cells used as the control group. Gene-specific DNA methylation was analyzed by next-generation sequencing-based bisulfite sequencing. The primers were designed using MethPrimer software; the forwards sequence was GTTAAYGYGGTATTTAGGAGATTTTGG, and the reverse sequence was ACAAATCRCAAATACCRTAAACTTTACC. The genomic DNA (1 μg) was bisulfite-converted using the ZYMO EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). One-twentieth of the elution products was amplified by PCR (35 cycles) using the KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ ReadyMix PCR Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). The products were pooled equally, 5’-phosphorylated, 3’-dA-tailed and ligated to a barcoded adapter using T4 DNA ligase. Barcoded libraries from all samples were sequenced on the Illumina platform.

Quality control of raw sequencing data involves a basic statistical evaluation of raw read yield and quality metrics using FastQC v0.11.7, followed by the removal of adapter sequences and low-quality bases/reads using Trimmomatic-0.36 to generate clean reads64. The cleaned reads were subsequently aligned to target sequences for comparative analysis using the bisulfite sequencing alignment software BSMAP (v2.7.3)65. Site-specific methylation levels were quantified for each sample. We counted the ratio of methylated C sites in the sequence environment (CG).

Statistical and reproducibility

All the statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism v10.1.2 software, and the results are presented as the means ± standard deviations. The unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc test were used for comparisons among three or more groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated in triplicate or more, unless stated otherwise.
