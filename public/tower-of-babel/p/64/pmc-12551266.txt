Medical student coaching in the U.S.: a national survey of clinical skills educators
Authors: Delphine Taylor, Katherine Anderson, Shubha Dathatri, Jean E Klig, Henry Park, Megan Fix
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoaching is increasingly recognized as a valuable tool to amplify learning in undergraduate medical education (UME), yet national data on its implementation, particularly in the context of clinical skills education, remain limited. Previous survey studies on coaching in medical education have focused on select institutions with established coaching programs, leaving a gap in understanding the broader landscape of coaching in UME. This study aims to address three critical gaps: current descriptions of coaching programs across U.S. medical schools, the nature of support provided to faculty coaches in these programs, and understanding how coaching is integrated into clinical skills development.MethodsA national survey was distributed to members of the Directors of Clinical Skills (DOCS) organization, targeting clinical skills educators at U.S. allopathic medical schools. The 29-item survey included multiple-choice, ranked, and open-ended questions. Responses were analyzed using both qualitative and quantitative approaches, combining descriptive statistics with content and thematic analysis to explore coaching program structure, goals, clinical skills impact, evaluation practices, and faculty development.ResultsOf the 44 respondents, 38 completed the majority of the survey. Nearly half (47%) reported having an active coaching program, with an additional 14% planning to implement a program. Coaching commonly begins in the pre-clerkship phase (93%) and emphasizes longitudinal relationships (87%). Key priorities included professional identity formation and clinical skills development, with history-taking, presentation skills, and clinical reasoning identified as skills most amenable to coaching. Respondents highlighted coaching’s role in fostering constructive learning environments, promoting skill development, and increasing student confidence. However, variations persist in program structure, oversight, and evaluation practices between institutions. Faculty development is widely implemented, with 94% of programs offering ongoing training and 79% compensating their coaches. Communication skills were ranked as the most critical competency for coach development. Evaluation practices were varied, with most programs relying on student satisfaction surveys (69%) and formative feedback (73%).ConclusionThis study provides a current national snapshot of coaching in UME through the lens of clinical skills educators. It underscores the growing recognition of coaching’s value in clinical skills development and professional identity formation. Introduction/background

Coaching is increasingly recognized as a key tool for competency-based medical education that supports the development of self-regulated, adaptive learners [1, 2]. Coaching in medical education is a distinct educational strategy that supports learner development through goal-setting, feedback, and reflection. Unlike advising, which often focuses on academic planning, or mentoring, which may emphasize career guidance and psychosocial support, coaching is characterized by a structured, learner-centered relationship aimed at fostering self-regulated learning and professional growth [3, 4].

The literature on coaching in undergraduate medical education (UME) has grown in recent years, highlighting its potential to enhance professional identity formation, clinical skills acquisition, and learner well-being. Lovell [5] conducted a comprehensive review of coaching in medical education, identifying its role in promoting reflective practice, individualized feedback, and mastery learning. Parsons et al. [6] emphasized that coaching can simultaneously support clinical competency and professional identity formation, suggesting that coaching is uniquely positioned to address the holistic needs of medical students. Furthermore, Cutrer [7] et al. proposed the Master Adaptive Learner framework, which aligns well with coaching principles by encouraging learners to continuously adapt and improve through feedback and reflection.

While coaching programs continue to evolve in many medical schools, a gap persists in describing these programs and identifying common features and areas of practice. A 2019 national survey on coaching by Wolff et al. [8] examined coaching programs across 32 U.S. medical schools affiliated with the AMA’s Accelerating Change in Medical Education Consortium Their findings revealed significant variability in coaching structures, goals, and implementation strategies, but underscored a shared emphasis on longitudinal relationships and formative feedback. Additionally, the study was limited to institutions already engaged in coaching initiatives, leaving unanswered questions about the broader landscape of coaching in UME, particularly in the context of clinical skills education. Also, coaching clinical skills development toward clinical competency has been described in the literature and is intertwined with professional identity formation [6]. There has not been a published survey re coaching programs in UME since the Wolff study and this study is an attempt to gather updated information about the landscape of coaching across the US from the lens of clinical skills educators.

Our aim was to explore how coaching is being integrated into foundational medical training, what goals and competencies are prioritized, specifically which clinical skills areas are most amenable to coaching, and how faculty coaches are supported and evaluated in UME. The study focuses on clinical skills educators as a cohort that is often involved in longitudinal student support [6]. The conceptual framework guiding this study draws from the Master Adaptive Learner model and emphasizes the importance of feedback-rich environments, learner autonomy, and continuous professional development. Coaching is positioned as a transformative approach to medical education that supports the development of adaptive, reflective, and competent physicians. Pubmed search did not reveal any national surveys related to clinical skills coaching and while an important area, details of coaching specific to remediation was beyond the scope of our survey.

Materials and methods

We designed a survey for distribution to clinical skills educators engaged in the Directors of Clinical Skills (DOCS) national organization. (See Supplementary File for complete survey). Survey questions from the 2019 Wolff study [8] were adapted and expanded by key members of the DOCS Coaching special interest group (DT, KA), and then tested by members of the research team who were either involved in the earlier survey (MF) or had expertise in survey methodology (SD, HP). The survey was reviewed and approved by the DOCS Research Committee. The research study (protocol IRB-AAAV3712) was determined exempt by the Columbia University Administrative Review Committee of the Human Research Protection Office at Columbia University in New York. The study was determined exempt from IRB approval and therefore informed consent from participants was waived. This work was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, including informing participants that the survey was for research purposes, that participation was voluntary, and that data collected anonymously and results stored on a password-protected site.

The study survey was anonymous, contained 29 questions (multiple choice, ranked choices and short answer), and was estimated to take between 7 and 10 min to complete. The survey was designed for members of the DOCS organization, a consortium of educators who lead clinical skills courses for medical students. After receiving permission from DOCS leadership to survey their membership, the survey was distributed by email to all 300 members and shared via QR code at the 2024 annual meeting in November 2024. This cohort included representatives from 87 US allopathic medical schools. Institutions were asked to select a designee to fill out the survey on behalf of the institution, a common practice in the DOCS organization. Two reminder emails were sent, and the survey was closed after one month. All responses were anonymized, aggregated and summarized by members of the research team from CERE, Columbia University’s Center for Education, Research and Evaluation (SD, HP). Survey data were analyzed using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Background and demographic data were summarized to capture aspects of program dynamics that can shape the coaching process. Quantitative ratings and rankings provided by respondents in the survey were summarized. Narrative data were reviewed using a content analysis approach [9, 10] to examine themes represented in responses around the domains of coaching program structure and goals, impact of coaching on clinical skills, prioritized outcomes of coaching, coaching program evaluation, and resources to support coach development. Analysis of the survey data, including ratings/rankings and narrative data, was supported through use of the Columbia enterprise version of ChatGPT. This tool was used mainly for secondary and confirmatory analysis, notably to organize the demographic/background and ranking and to review themes identified by the research group.

Coaching meetings

93% of coaching programs assign a coach at matriculation and 82% percent report the coaching relationships are longitudinal throughout medical school. Approximately half of the schools have group coaching meetings as part of their programs. All programs have ‘as needed coaching’ and 87% of programs report that students work longitudinally with the same coach using predetermined intervals for one-on-one coaching meetings. For these one-on-one coaching meetings, the dynamics are variable with a majority of respondents indicating that 3–4 meetings are expected prior to the core clerkship year. Goals & priorities of coaching programs

Respondents were asked to rank order the goals/priorities of their coaching program from a list that included academic performance, clinical skills development, wellbeing, professional identity formation, specialty selection, community building, and remediation. Goals associated with professional identity formation and clinical skills development tended to be highest ranking in importance, with top or second ranking for 11 and 10 programs, respectively. Other goals such as community building and remediation were more evenly distributed in terms of rank.

Open-ended responses on the structure and goals of coaching revealed three themes:

Flexibility: Coaching structures should be flexible, with meetings driven by student needs and goals.

Individualized Support: Programs aim to offer personalized guidance, addressing both academic and personal development.

Individualized Support: Programs aim to offer personalized guidance, addressing both academic and personal development.

Holistic Development: Goals include fostering professional growth, improving communication and clinical skills, and promoting well-being.

Holistic Development: Goals include fostering professional growth, improving communication and clinical skills, and promoting well-being.

Respondents were also asked to prioritize which skills and behaviors are focused on when working with students. Interpersonal communication and clinical skills were often ranked as top priorities, while professionalism and decision-making skills were typically ranked lower. Goal-setting and reflection were variably distributed on rank lists.

Coaching & clinical skills development

Respondents were asked to rank order common doctoring skills including physical exam, history-taking, clinical reasoning, presentation skills, and note-writing. History-taking was identified as the skill most likely to benefit from coaching (five programs ranked this as first or second). Presentation and clinical reasoning (both ranked as either first or second by seven programs) were also ranked highly. Perceptions about the value of coaching for documentation and physical examination skills were more variable. Evaluation practices in coaching programs

Feedback & assessment of students

Respondents indicated that formative feedback/assessment (73%) was the predominant way that coaches assess the students with whom they work, and 12% reported that their coaches contributed to summative evaluations of their students.

Evaluation of coaches

Coach evaluation relied mainly on student satisfaction ratings (69%, n = 11) provided through student surveys and focus groups. In addition, the majority of programs asked students to evaluate their coaches using institution-designed competencies (56%, n = 9), while fewer programs offered direct observation of coaching activities (40%, n = 6) and these were predominantly administered in group settings. Self-evaluation of coaching based on institutional-designed competencies was only noted by one program.

Evaluation of coaching programs

Student surveys and focus groups were used by 92% of respondent institutions to evaluate their coaching program. Respondents commented that they would prefer robust metrics on the following aspects of their coaching programs:

Student Engagement: A strong focus on understanding how students interact with and benefit from coaching.

Discussion

As the implementation of coaching programs increasingly expands across medical schools, our understanding of the size, scope, and impact of these programs is essential to considerations of future directions for coaching in UME. While this survey captures a small portion of allopathic medical schools in the US, the lens of clinical skills educators offers a key perspective and adds an updated snapshot of how coaching is being incorporated in US medical schools. This study builds upon previous work from an AMA conference-based study that was aimed at medical schools interested in growing or developing a coaching program at their institution [8]. Our findings reveal an expanding adoption of coaching models, albeit with significant variability in program structure, implementation, and evaluation.

Prevalence & oversight of coaching programs

Our data indicate that nearly half of responding medical schools currently offer a coaching program, with a number of schools actively planning to implement one. This suggests a rising recognition of the potential benefits of coaching in UME, aligning with existing literature emphasizing its positive impact on student development [5, 11]. However, nearly forty-percent of the responding institutions do not offer coaching programs, which underscores the importance of identifying structures and processes that may be in place at those schools to support student reflective practice and adaptive learning. Variability in administrative oversight suggests that there are likely differences in institutional priorities, resources and structure which determine where a coaching program is housed. Future studies may identify types of organizational models for coaching programs and clarify how these models can be tailored to different institutional contexts and goals.

Coaching meetings

A clear trend emerged for the timing of coaching interventions, with the majority of programs initiating coaching in the pre-clerkship phase and often at matriculation. This early intervention aligns with the developmental nature of coaching, allowing students to benefit from guidance and support throughout their years of medical school [12]. The predominance of longitudinal coaching relationships and predetermined meeting intervals further emphasizes the commitment to sustained student support and suggests that student participation in coaching at the surveyed institutions is a requirement. Incorporation of group coaching in approximately half of the programs surveyed highlights alternate coaching approaches that offer peer learning and community-building opportunities. The reported number of coaching sessions during the core clerkship year lends insight to opportunities for sustained longitudinal engagement with coaching. As the clerkship phase of the curriculum is a time when students receive and can benefit from more narrative feedback on their performance, it is a prime opportunity to identify areas of strength and avenues for growth through self-reflection that is enhanced with the guidance of a coach. Future studies can explore the unique nature of coaching during the clerkship years as it is a time of rapid growth in clinical learning and professional identity formation.

Coaching program goals & priorities

Our findings on the priorities of coaching programs reinforce the existing literature emphasizing the role of coaching in professional identity formation (PIF) [6, 8, 13]. The high ranking of PIF and clinical skills development as key priorities in our data underscores the alignment of coaching with core goals of medical education. The data also highlights clinical skills as a priority, which may reflect the fact that our responders represent clinical skills educators and also may bias study results. Surprisingly, despite this being a survey of clinical skills educators, the emphasis of coaching was not on remediation, aligning with previous literature [6] which describes the intertwinement of professional identity formation and clinical competency. Also, this may be a result of a broad interpretation of what remediation entails and further exploration of this priority, and other lesser priorities, is needed to fully understand the diverse aims of coaching programs.

Coaching & clinical skills

This survey specifically explored respondents’ perceptions of how coaching supports clinical skills development, and which skills can most benefit from a coaching approach. Our findings generally align with previous studies on the effectiveness of coaching in developing clinical competencies [8, 13]. Notably, the variability in perceived benefit for documentation and physical exam skills in our data suggests a need for further investigation into if and how coaching can be optimized for these areas. The reported efficacy of coaching in clinical skills development is an identified strength yet may result from selection bias as respondents were affiliated with the DOCS community. Beyond clinical skills development, our qualitative data indicated that a coaching approach fosters a constructive learning environment and promotes increased confidence for clinical skills among students. This may in part be due to the ongoing formative feedback cycle that is common to clinical skills training [7] and the nature of the longitudinal relationship between coaches and their trainees.

Evaluation

Evaluation of coaching programs remains a significant challenge for all. While our data indicate that student satisfaction surveys and focus groups are widely used, their subjective nature may limit their ability to provide comprehensive insights into program effectiveness. The use of institution-designed competencies and direct observation of coaching activities suggests a move towards more objective evaluation methods to evaluate coaches, although these approaches are not yet universally adopted [14]. A key focus for future study would be to validate and disseminate instruments that evaluate coaches [15] and the low utilization of coach self-evaluation represents an area for future development. Additionally, as respondents wanted better tools to measure program outcomes, investigations that include multiple institutions are needed to describe impact of coaching on student clinical skills, professional development, and well-being.

Support & faculty development

Coaches often have a strong commitment to students and education and given the niche skill set required in coaching, additional faculty development helps enhance support for coaches [16]. Strong communication skills have been recognized as important to the coaching relationship [17, 18], and our study further validated this as “communication skills” ranked highest in importance for faculty development. Both initial and ongoing faculty development, as well as compensation, have increased relative to prior surveys [8], which may reflect the need for coach training along the medical school continuum and also demonstrates a growing institutional commitment to coaching.

Conclusion

This national survey of clinical skills educators provides new information about the landscape of coaching in UME. While coaching programs in US medical schools continue to support student professional identity formation and provide longitudinal academic support, there remains variability in program structure, support, and evaluation. It is clear that coaching programs can be cross-sectional, inclusive of aims for both student affairs and curricular spaces. Key features revealed through our data include increased investment in faculty support and training, and expansion of coaching into the clerkship phase. These likely reflect a shift towards greater incorporation of coaching as an institutional priority. Future research can expand our understanding of the coaching landscape with a comprehensive survey of U.S. medical schools to further identify best practices, develop and share standardized evaluation frameworks, and demonstrate the long-term impact of coaching on student success and professional development. By addressing these gaps, we can further optimize the role of coaching in shaping the future of medical education.
