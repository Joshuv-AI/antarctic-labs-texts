Hydrodynamic control of fluvial oncoid formation in a modern high-energy tropical cascade system
Authors: Antun Husinec, Audrey L Bowman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncoids are microbially-coated carbonate grains that form in diverse aquatic environments, yet their modern fluvial examples remain comparatively understudied. This study documents modern fluvial oncoids from Dunn’s River Falls, Jamaica, a dynamic high-energy tropical cascading waterfalls and stepped pools with active tufa deposition over Miocene and Pleistocene limestones. Oncoids from seven plunge pools were analyzed for size, shape, and internal structure using granulometry and petrography, and selected cortices were microdrilled for X-ray diffraction, stable-isotope analysis, and radiocarbon dating. Overwhelmingly gravel-size oncoids are spherical to ellipsoidal, with their internal fabric strongly influenced by nucleus size and geometry. Oncoids display concentric lamination of alternating light and dark sets, with individual laminae often poorly developed, discontinuous, and variably thick. Oncoids are commonly truncated, with local rehealing, indicating repeated abrasion and frequent overturning under turbulent flow. Mineralogy is dominated by low-Mg calcite. Stable isotope values are narrowly clustered (mean δ13C = − 9.97‰; mean δ1⁸O = − 5.09‰), consistent with precipitation from well-mixed freshwater, short residence times, and strong soil-zone CO2 contribution. Conventional radiocarbon ages (3095–3225 BP) yield older apparent ages in outer lamina sets, suggesting incorporation of 14C-depleted dissolved inorganic carbon and a freshwater reservoir effect. The findings demonstrate that spherical morphology in fluvial carbonates does not imply low-energy conditions, and that their radiocarbon ages cannot be used as straightforward chronological archives.Keywords: Fluvial tufa, Oncoids, Tropical cascade systems, Stable isotopes, Freshwater reservoir effect, Radiocarbon datingSubject terms: Biogeochemistry, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Oncoids were first described by Arnold Heim from the Swiss Jurassic deposits1. They are coated, granule-to-pebble-sized carbonate grains with a cortex of irregular, more or less concentric, or partially overlapping laminae of microbial origin2,3 (Fig. 1). The cortex commonly consists of alternating light and dark laminae, which have been attributed to (a) variations in microbial groups and their abundance, (b) variations in the arrangement or growth pattern of microbial filaments, (c) changes in mineralogy, and (d) imbalance between mineral precipitation and organic matter decomposition4. It may also be interpreted as reflecting shifts in the relative importance of slower, biologically mediated versus physicochemical carbonate precipitation, with dark laminae enriched in microbial organic matter and light laminae dominated by cleaner micritic carbonate formed during more rapid precipitation phases often linked to increased CO₂ degassing, turbulence, or discharge5,6. The temporal significance of microbialite lamination in freshwater carbonates is difficult to discern, as cyclic processes occur over different and/or conflicting time scales (sub-seasonal, seasonal, multi-annual, solar) and are further modified by non-periodic processes (e.g.,7–10). Traditionally, various names for different oncoid types have been proposed, with spongiostromate and porostromate descriptive terms gaining more traction than others1,11. Spongiostromate oncoids have a dense micritic or spongy fabric, with no visible microbial filaments. Porostromate oncoids are layered or non-layered oncoids consisting of preserved tubular microstructures within a micrite matrix11. All oncoids form by the precipitation of calcium carbonate through CO₂ removal, which in fluvial settings occurs through cyanobacterial photosynthetic activity and the outgassing from turbulent cascading waters6,11. Carbonate accumulation may also occur by trapping of fine sediments onto the oncoid surface by cyanobacteria11. Some of the most abundant cyanobacteria recorded from modern fluvial oncoids include Schizothrix calcicola, Calothrix gypsophila, Rivularia haematites, Phormidium incrustatum, Pleurocapsa minor, Aphanothece saxicola, and Hyellasp12. Cyanobacterium Phormidium incrusatum dominates in fast-flowing conditions and forms extensive laminae of tight calcite tubes, whereas Phormidium aerugineo-caeruleum commonly produces uneven lamination in slow-moving water13,14.

Simplified sketch showing internal fabric of an oncoid with dark and light lamina sets around a nucleus.

Oncoids flourished in various marine and freshwater settings throughout the Phanerozoic (e.g.,2–4,9,11,15,16) but their pre-Quaternary fluvial record is very limited6. The majority of porostromate oncoids formed in marine settings from the Cambrian to the Jurassic and reflect slower growth rates. During this time, spongiostromate oncoids occurred in lacustrine and transitional continental-marine settings. Spongiostromate oncoids became dominant in marine environments in the Jurassic and continued their high occurrence in lacustrine environments. They formed over short periods of time and in unstable ecological conditions2. Modern oncoids occur in various settings, including freshwater lakes, streams, marshes, brackish rivers, hydrothermal systems, and inter- to sub-tidal marine environments6,10,11,17,18.

Fluvial oncoids are the dominant microbially laminated structures, along with calcite-coated stems, in fluvial systems without stepped longitudinal profiles and with rapid lateral changes of facies10. Typically, they are associated with tufa in vegetated karstic settings, whereas climatic conditions range from temperate to arid to tropical, with the underlying carbonate rocks providing Ca2+ and HCO3- necessary for the precipitation of CaCO33,6. Although fluvial oncoids commonly display alternating dark and light growth laminae similar to their marine counterparts (Fig. 1), the environmental and temporal significance of these laminae is more difficult to interpret in fluvial systems12,19–22. They are typically cm-sized and varied in shape, commonly exhibiting ellipsoidal, spherical, sub-cylindrical, and discoidal morphologies. Laminations can be discontinuous, irregular, and concentric, predominantly with radial fibrous microstructures11. It is worth noting that terminology for coated carbonate grains in continental, as well as in shallow-marine environments, has varied considerably in the literature, with terms such as oncoid, oncolith, pisoid, pisolith, and ooid sometimes used inconsistently (e.g.,23).

Fluvial oncoids form at the intersection of biological carbonate precipitation and physical reworking, yet the relative importance of these processes remains unresolved in high-energy tufa rivers, where transport, abrasion, and rapid CO₂ degassing may overwhelm primary biological growth signals. This study presents the first documentation of modern freshwater fluvial oncoids from the Americas and investigates their development in a high-energy tropical cascade system at Dunn’s River, Jamaica. We test the hypothesis that fluvial oncoid morphology, internal fabrics, and geochemical signatures are controlled primarily by hydrodynamic instability and rapid precipitation rather than prolonged biological stabilization. Specifically, we address three questions: what controls the architecture of oncoid lamination under conditions of frequent transport and overturning; what stable carbon- and oxygen-isotope compositions of fluvial oncoids record in open, turbulent systems; and whether radiocarbon ages of oncoid cortices meaningfully reflect growth histories in carbonate-rich freshwater environments. Resolving these issues is critical for assessing the paleoenvironmental significance of fluvial oncoids and for improving interpretations of oncoid-bearing strata in both modern and ancient depositional systems.

Geologic and geographic setting

Geological background

Jamaica is situated south of the Cayman Trough on the uplifted eastern margin of the Nicaraguan Rise, surrounded by an extensive system of shallow banks and shoals24 (Fig. 2). The island’s geological evolution is characterized by a complex interaction between plate convergence, strike-slip deformation, and carbonate platform development. Jamaica’s tectonic evolution can be summarized in four broad stages25: (1) construction of a Cretaceous to early Eocene island arc; (2) Paleocene–early Eocene extensional rifting; (3) a tectonically quiescent interval from the middle Eocene to mid-Miocene, during which the Yellow Limestone and White Limestone groups accumulated; and (4) mid-Miocene to Recent left-lateral transpression that drove uplift and progressive emergence of Jamaica above sea level. This final phase of deformation and uplift provides the tectonic framework for the tufa deposits examined in this study.

Map of the Caribbean showing the location of Jamaica (J) on the Nicaraguan Rise. Jamaica lies at the northern edge of the Caribbean plate and the easternmost point of the Nicaragua Rise. Modified after26.

Structurally, Jamaica is composed of three principal tectonic blocks: Hanover, Clarendon, and Blue Mountains, which are separated by Tertiary sedimentary belts24,27. The western Hanover Block is dominated by volcaniclastic rocks with minor limestones, the central Clarendon Block contains volcanic flows, pyroclastic deposits, and plutons, and the eastern Blue Mountains Block includes metamorphic and ophiolitic slices within a volcanogenic succession. Blueschist assemblages in the Blue Mountains record subduction under low-temperature, high-pressure conditions27,28. These basement terranes are mantled by extensive Tertiary limestones that today form classic cockpit karst landscapes, with widespread cave development in dolomitized and recrystallized shallow-water carbonates29.

In the study area, four north-flowing rivers—Little River, Roaring River, Dunn’s River, and Cave River—drain and incise into the karstified Eocene to lower Miocene chert-bearing deep-water limestones of the Montpelier Formation and the middle Miocene to lower Pleistocene mixed clastic–carbonate deposits of the Coastal Group29–33 (Fig. 3). Upper Pleistocene (?) to modern tufa deposits are confined to these catchments, with the best-exposed succession developed along the lower reaches of Dunn’s River.

Physical geography of Jamaica

Jamaica, situated in the west-central Caribbean Sea, ranks as the fourth-largest island nation in the Caribbean, encompassing an area of 10,990 km2. The island extends 234 km in length and 80 km in width, characterized by mountainous regions in the east, central valleys and plateaus, and coastal plains. A complex network of surface and subterranean rivers traverses the karstified limestone bedrock. The island’s watersheds flow towards the coast, which is distinguished by bays, small cays, and islands34.

The climate of Jamaica is tropical, with warm months from August to September, where temperatures average approximately 27 °C (80.6 °F). The cooler months, from January to February, see temperatures around 24 °C (75.2 °F)35. Rainfall exhibits a bimodal pattern, peaking in May and October, influenced by the North Atlantic high-pressure system, sea surface temperatures, easterly waves, and northeasterly trade winds. The island receives consistent sunlight for about 7 to 9 h daily, resulting in an evaporation rate of 7.23 mm per day at Manley International Airport in Kingston and 5.5 mm per day at Sangster International Airport in Montego Bay34.

Methods

Study site

Dunn’s River Falls is a ~ 55 m high and ~ 180 m long series of cascading waterfalls in the lower reaches of the Dunn’s River (Fig. 4). It is a popular tourist hotspot located less than 4 km west-northwest of Ocho Rios on the northern coast of Jamaica (Fig. 3). The individual cascades range in height from less than a meter to several meters (Fig. 4A–D). Lush tropical vegetation surrounds the riverbanks and forms a dense canopy above the river and the surrounding area (Fig. 4A). The tufa outcrops exhibit wavy to crinkly mm-scale microbial lamination, and some of the trees and fallen tree trunks are completely encased by tufa (Fig. 4E). Potholes and pools below the cascades indicate heavy mechanical abrasion and consistent water flow and are filled with gravel-size oncoids (Fig. 4F–G). Ubiquitous microbial mat growth is visible as greenish, slimy, and slippery films on the outcropping horizontal surfaces, but is absent in heavily trafficked paths walked by the tourists.

The source of the Dunn’s River is at ~ 360 m above sea level (Fig. 3A), but its underground drainage in this heavily karstified terrain is not known. The water rises from the Montpelier Formation near Malvern Park by Davis Town, St. Anne Parish, and empties into the Caribbean Sea at a white sand beach.

Field sampling

The fieldwork at Dunn’s River Falls was conducted in March 2023 during the climatic dry season and tourist high season. A total of 2.8 kg of sediment was randomly hand-collected from the bottom of seven different pools located below the cascading falls (Fig. 4). A Mares Puck 2 dive computer was used to measure the water temperature, which was in the range of 26–27 °C. Salinity was less than 1 g/cm3, as determined by a TRZ salinity refractometer (Hamh Optics & Tools). The hand samples were stored in sample bags and transported to the Carbonate Sedimentology Laboratory, Department of Geology at St. Lawrence University for laboratory preparation and analyses.

Granulometry

Upon a month-long drying at room temperature, the samples were weighed, and grain-size analysis was performed by allowing the material to pass through a set of sieves of progressively smaller mesh size (12,500 µm, 2800 µm, 2000 µm, 1400 µm, 1000 µm, 710 µm, 250 µm, 180 µm, and 125 µm). The samples were sieved for five minutes each using a W.S. Tyler RO TAP Sieve Shaker. After all samples were sieved, each grain-size class was photographed and weighed (Fig. 5). Grain-size distribution and statistics were analyzed using the Gradistat statistics package (Version 9.136,) using a single sample data input. Summary of Gradistat analysis is available in37.

Petrographic analysis

Smaller, sand-size oncoids (250–2000 µm) were analyzed in grain-mount thin sections using a Leica DM 750P standard petrographic microscope and microphotographed using a Leica MC190 HD camera. Grain-size fractions below 180 µm were excluded due to the lack of the sample material needed for thin sections.

Forty oncoids coarser than 12.5 mm were cut with the Felker Bay State Dresser 10’’ saw along their long axis and polished on one side using 300-micron and then 600-micron bit grains. These oncoids were analyzed using an Olympus SZX10 stereo microscope mounted with an Olympus DP27 camera.

X-ray diffraction

Three grams of each grain-size fraction were powdered for 5 min in an agate grinding jar using a SPEX 8000 mixer mill. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out at Union College using a Rigaku SmartLab SE and a copper tube. Approximately 0.3 mg of powdered samples were scanned from 23° to 32° 2Θ, sampling width of 0.03°; halite was added to the sample as a standard (~ 20% of the total sample mass). Mineral abundances and Mg contents were determined from raw XRD data using Rietveld refinements in GSAS38 with the EXPGUI interface39, using halite as an internal standard. Relative abundances of aragonite, high-Mg calcite, and low-Mg calcite were estimated from diagnostic position of the calcite (104) peak after40,41.

Stable isotope analysis

The unpolished halves of eight selected oncoids coarser than 12.5 mm were used to obtain powder for carbon and oxygen stable isotope analysis and radiocarbon dating. Nine additional oncoid halves were used for stable isotope analysis only. A Dremel 400 XPR with a carbide bur was used to microdrill the innermost laminae of these oncoids; on two of them, the outermost laminae were also microdrilled (Fig. 6). Precision drilling was done under a magnifying glass, and the powder was collected in small vials. The sampled area was limited by the diameter of the bur (1.8 mm), so some of the powdered samples may contain multiple laminae and represent a mix of ages and isotope values.

Stable isotope ratios of 19 samples were analyzed using a Thermo GasBench II coupled to a Thermo Delta Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer operating in continuous-flow mode at Union College Stable Isotope Mass Spectrometer Laboratory. Reference materials IAEA-603 and NBS-18 were used for isotopic calibration and to assign values to the appropriate δ-scale via linear regression. LSVEC was analyzed as a quality-control check standard. Assigned values for δ13C and δ1⁸O were as follows: IAEA-603, + 2.46‰ and − 2.37‰; NBS-18, − 5.014‰ and − 23.2‰; and LSVEC, − 46.6‰ and − 26.7‰, respectively. A linearity correction was applied following42. Analytical reproducibility (1σ), based on replicate analyses of four IAEA-603 standards within one analytical session, was ± 0.03‰ for δ13C and ± 0.05‰ for δ1⁸O (VPDB). Measured values of carbonate reference standards differed from accepted values by less than 0.1‰ for both δ13C and δ1⁸O.

Radiocarbon dating

Approximately 1 mg of sample powders were radiocarbon dated at the Keck Carbon Cycle AMS Facility at UC Irvine. Sample powders were obtained from both the innermost (n = 8) and outermost lamina sets (n = 2) (Fig. 6). The samples were leached with 50% diluted HCL before hydrolysis with 85% phosphoric acid. The AMS Spectrometer was used to produce the δ13C values that were measured on prepared graphite. These values may differ from the δ13C of the original material, but those are not shown. Radiocarbon concentrations are given as fractions of the modern standard D14C and the conventional radiocarbon age following the conventions of43. Isotopic fractionation is accounted for in the results according to the practices of43. The sample preparation backgrounds were subtracted based on measurements of 14C-free calcite.

Image analysis

The photographs of polished oncoid halves were uploaded to Image J (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html). Oncoid cortices are characterized by alternating darker and lighter tan layers representing different lamina sets. Since these are sometimes hard to differentiate, given the small differences in color shades, ImageJ was used to aid with lamina set identification. Digital image analysis was preferred because many laminae are discontinuous, truncated, or laterally variable, making direct optical measurements difficult and less reproducible. ImageJ-based thresholding and repeated measurements allowed more consistent identification of lamina-set boundaries and reduced operator bias relative to direct measurements in crossed-polarized light alone. For simplicity, in this analysis, only light and dark colors were used to distinguish the sets. The photographs were set to 8-bit and then given a black-and-white filter based on an automatic threshold calculated using the “Shanbhag” filter (Fig. 7). The black and white filter was used to systematically differentiate the dark and light lamina sets. These represent rings where the light or dark color values predominate. In reality, the black and white filtered rings contain finer laminae within, so the calculated width does not represent individual laminae but sets of laminae of the same hue. The width of individual laminae couldn’t be assessed due to the difficulty of generating accurate counts. This inaccuracy arises due to the lack of distinct dark and light shades and the inconsistency of the connecting ring formation.

Example of an oncoid (sample 33) analyzed using the black and white filter (ImageJ). Red dots indicate counted sets of dark laminae, and blue dots represent counted sets of light laminae. The green lines indicate the measured thickness of lamina sets.

For each of the 40 analyzed oncoids37, the number of lamina sets, as displayed by the Shanbhag filter, was counted from the nucleus outward, with the first lamina set directly coating the nucleus (Fig. 7). The width of the thickest light and dark lamina set in each oncoid was measured, and its location was noted by counting lamina sets from the nucleus outward. The shade (light or dark) of the first lamina set was also noted, along with the number of nuclei.

Results

Oncoid size

Grain-size analysis reveals a strongly gravel-dominated deposit, composed of 96% gravel, 4% sand, and no mud. The grain-size histogram (Fig. 8) shows an extremely coarse distribution with two prominent modes in the gravel range: 14.25 mm for over 60% of class weight, and a secondary gravel mode of 3.4 mm that accounts for an additional ~ 30%. All size classes within the sand fraction contribute only minor amounts (< 5%), and no measurable particles occur in the mud range. The lower end of the sand fraction (0.1–1 mm) is represented by very small class weights, producing a strongly fine-skewed pattern.

Statistical parameters derived from both the method of moments and the44 method consistently reflect this coarse, gravel-rich character (Table 1). The arithmetic and geometric mean grain sizes are 9057.5 µm and 6740.0 µm, respectively, with corresponding logarithmic means of –2.753 φ (moments) and –3.05744, placing the bulk of the material within the medium gravel size range. Sorting values are high (5560.5 µm, 2.353, 1.234 φ by moments; 1.976, 0.983 φ by44, indicating moderate sorting, consistent with the visually broad spread of gravel classes in the histogram. Skewness values (–0.155 to –0.872 φ, 0.790 to 0.872 µm) reflect a distribution with a tail extending into finer size classes. Kurtosis values span 1.067–3.888 for the moments method and 0.530 µm for the44 method, all indicating a very platykurtic distribution with a broad dispersion relative to a normal curve.

Overall, the grain-size spectrum (Fig. 5) is dominated by coarse gravel with minor sand and no mud, characterized by moderate sorting, fine skewness, and platykurtic form, as represented in both the statistical parameters and the grain-size frequency plot (Fig. 8, Table 1).

Oncoid shape and microfabric

The fresh oncoid outer surface is light tan, mottled with a light blueish-green hue due to the presence of cyanobacteria (Fig. 4F–H). The oncoids display either a spherical, elliptical, or irregular (triangular) shape (Figs. 5, 9–10), which is largely determined by the shape and number of their nuclei. The studied oncoids contain one or two nuclei (Fig. 10A, P) that are predominantly composed of older carbonate rock fragments, whereas chert and other non-carbonate nuclei are subordinate (Figs. 9H, 10G). Of the 40 cut-and-polished oncoids, those more irregularly shaped (e.g., Figs. 9I, 10G) have a lower number of lamina sets compared to those that are spherical (e.g., Figs. 9A, 10E). Some of the largest oncoids contain large nuclei and only a few lamina sets.

Thin section analysis shows that some nuclei of oncoids finer than 2.8 mm are fragments of gastropods (Fig. 9B), corals (Fig. 9I, L), and tufa (Fig. 9D). Some of the oncoids are broken (Fig. 9H, J), whereas others exhibit rehealing with the development of younger lamina sets around broken older oncoids (Fig. 9C) (cf.,45). Sand-sized oncoids (250–2000 µm) have predominantly carbonate nuclei and exhibit a varying number of lamina sets.

The oncoid cortex is characterized by alternating sets of light (pale brown to cream) and dark (medium to dark brown) lamina sets (compositional laminae type 3 sensu11 with shades that vary within each oncoid and between oncoid samples. These reflect lighter microsparitic and darker micritic laminations. The laminae within the light and dark lamina sets generally follow the outline of the nucleus and commonly form concentric rings around it, corresponding to geometric type C of oncoids sensu46. Individual laminae are commonly underdeveloped, discontinuous, and vary in width. The number of lamina sets per oncoid is slightly right-skewed, with two outliers that have 25–30 lamina sets (Fig. 11A). The average and mode numbers lie between 10–15 lamina sets per oncoid (Fig. 11A). For all but four oncoids, the first lamina set lining the nucleus is a dark shade with a more orangish-brown color (Fig. 10L) compared to the remaining dark lamina sets. The average width of the thickest light lamina set is 0.542 mm, while the thickest dark lamina set averages 0.327 mm. The widest dark lamina sets appear earlier in the lamina set count, whereas the widest light lamina sets appear in the middle of the oncoid lamina set count. Histograms based on the thickest light and dark lamina set width are both right-skewed, with a long tail extending toward thicker laminae (Fig. 11B, C). The light lamina set width’s mode is 0.4–0.5 mm, whereas the dark lamina set is 0.2–0.3 mm.

Mineralogy

The oncoids consist of low-Mg calcite (LMC) as indicated by their primary XRD peak value at 29.4° 2θCu (Fig. 12A). Secondary and tertiary peaks occur at 26.64° 2θCu and 27.33° 2θCu, indicating minute quantities of quartz and sanidine. The XRD values indicate an average of 89.15% LMC, 9.39% quartz and feldspar, and 1.46% aragonite (Table 2) for the samples analyzed. One outlier, sample JDR-I, contains a higher percentage of quartz and sanidine of 27.49% (Fig. 12B, Table 2).

Coarser oncoid laminae (medium sand to granule-sized, > 250 µm; samples JDR-B to JDR-G) are overwhelmingly made up of LMC (> 90%), with quartz and sanidine ranging from 4.25 to 6.92%, and aragonite 1.1–1.54% (Table 2, Fig. 12A). Discussion

Controls on oncoid growth in modern freshwater fluvial systems

Modern freshwater fluvial oncoids form under diverse hydrodynamic, chemical, and microbial conditions (Table 5). Their morphology and internal structures reflect the interplay between carbonate precipitation, microbial activity, and grain mobility. Oncoids from Dunn’s River, Jamaica, serve as a high-energy tropical analogue, contrasting with lower-energy European and Asian systems (e.g.,12,20,47–49). They refine our understanding of the environmental parameters influencing fluvial oncoid accumulation and preservation, highlighting both constructive microbial processes and destructive mechanical abrasion. Note that modern fluvial oncoids have also been documented from a high-altitude brackish river in Argentina18. However, given the unique environmental conditions of the Salado (‘Salty’) River (~ 15 g/l total dissolved solids), the discussion below focuses exclusively on modern freshwater oncoids.

Hydrodynamic controls on growth and preservation

Fluvial oncoids typically form in low-gradient settings with low hydraulic energy, allowing microbial films to establish on grain surfaces while permitting periodic rotation49. Examples include the Dulce River20, French cyanobacterial streams49,52, and the River Alz, where flow velocities of 0.5–1 m s⁻1 support large, ellipsoidal oncoids12 (Table 5). In these systems, oncoid motion is intermittent, promoting regular lamination and microbial accretion.

In contrast, Dunn’s River oncoids form in a turbulent cascade environment, where water accelerates across waterfalls and cascades, promoting perpetual overturning and abrasion in shallow plunge pools. The common high sphericity of Jamaican oncoids reflects sustained mobility, a pattern rarely seen outside high-gradient tropical settings. Truncated or irregular lamination suggests episodic transport or temporary stabilization6,9. Tourist activity over the last several decades has further enhanced physical disturbance, increased grain movement, and shortened lamination cycles. Thus, hydrodynamic energy can dominate over biological accretion, resulting in rounded yet structurally irregular oncoids.

Criteria for distinguishing in-situ from transported oncoids clarify the hydrodynamic implications of Jamaican grains. Three features of oncoids forming without overturning can be identified53: (1) stable resting position on a smooth surface, (2) concentric laminae with polar thickening on the upper surface, and (3) a shallow depression on the basal side. Similar patterns occur in the River Alz, where laminae are thicker on illuminated surfaces due to reduced cyanobacterial productivity on shaded undersides12. These asymmetries are classic indicators of subpolar in-situ accretion, applied to ancient oncoids53,54. However, fully concentric oncoids may also grow in situ when substrates are coarse and water depths shallow enough for diffuse light to reach lower surfaces, supporting uniform cyanobacterial calcification50,53. In-situ growth typically results in enhanced carbonate production on upper surfaces and reduced lamination on basal surfaces, whereas frequent overturning yields symmetrical or irregularly truncated laminae, as seen in Dunn’s River oncoids. The absence of polar thickening and the common occurrence of truncated laminae in Jamaican specimens support the interpretation that transport and abrasion, rather than stable attachment, controlled their accretional history.

Morphology, nucleus characteristics, and transport dynamics

The nuclei of fluvial oncoids vary widely, from quartz and detrital lithics (France;49) to gastropod shells, plant fragments, and pebbles (Germany;12) and anthropogenic debris (Spain;20) (Table 5). Nucleus size and density influence grain rotation and carbonate cortex geometry.

Dunn’s River oncoids have nuclei primarily of LMC, with chert and other non-carbonate lithologies also present. Such nuclei are dense and durable, withstanding prolonged abrasion and supporting repeated microbial recolonization. Smaller Jamaican oncoids are more spherical, reflecting efficient rotation in shallow pools, while coarser ones with irregular nuclei display asymmetrical surfaces. This size–mobility relationship echoes patterns in the smallest Vietnamese oncoids, which are regularly coated and symmetrically laminated47).

Internal fabrics and lamination architecture

Lamination styles in modern fluvial oncoids are sensitive to the balance between microbial precipitation, detrital trapping, and mechanical disturbance (Table 5). Spanish oncoids show rhythmic alternations of dark organic-rich laminae and thicker, light-toned laminae with quartz grains and diatoms, consistent with periodic productivity and sediment influx20. French streams exhibit dense to spongy concentric laminae from cyanobacterial mat development52, while River Alz oncoids display alternating parallel and radial fabrics, sparse organic remains, and cavities from insect larvae12. Polish and Vietnamese oncoids feature pelitic microlaminae and thick, undulating concentric bands47, recording fluctuating flow and sediment supply.

Dunn’s River oncoids share the broad concentric organization typical of fluvial oncoids but differ in their commonly poorly developed, discontinuous, and variably thick laminae. Many laminae terminate abruptly or are truncated, indicating repeated abrasion before complete microbial consolidation. This lamination pattern is compatible with high turbulence and rapid precipitation6,55. Fine-scale laminae often occur as nested sets within thicker envelopes, implying episodic pauses in motion during which microbial communities re-established temporary stability. The predominance of thick light-toned sets, relative to dark sets, resembles Spanish examples but contrasts with well-organized fabrics in temperate British barrage pools48,56. These textural attributes record a depositional milieu where constructive microbial precipitation was repeatedly interrupted by high-magnitude mechanical reworking.

Climatic influences on morphology and fabrics

Climate exerts secondary but significant control on oncoid accretion through its influence on water temperature, carbonate saturation, and microbial productivity. Warm semi-arid conditions in Spain favor rapid carbonate precipitation and relatively thin laminae, whereas cool-temperate British settings produce slower, more regular accretion and enhanced organic material preservation56. Tropical monsoonal Vietnam supports large, thickly laminated oncoids in shallow streams47, indicating high metabolic activity and carbonate supersaturation.

The Jamaican system represents a humid tropical endmember, where high temperatures, persistent carbonate supersaturation, and robust microbial activity favor rapid accretion, even as turbulence limits lamination continuity. Dunn’s River material shows that climate alone does not dictate lamination regularity; high-energy hydrodynamics can override climatic controls, generating spherical yet internally irregular grains despite favorable conditions for microbial growth. The decoupling between climate and oncoid fabric in open, groundwater-fed fluvial systems indicates that hydrology, bedrock configuration, and flow dynamics exert more significant control on facies architecture than climate alone6.

Implications for fossil oncoid interpretation

Comparing Dunn’s River oncoids with other modern fluvial examples shows that the most consistent predictors of oncoid morphology and fabric are: (1) hydrodynamic energy regime, (2) frequency of grain transport, (3) size and composition of the nucleus, and (4) microbial community density and continuity.

Low-energy European systems produce well-organized concentric laminae and varied morphologies, while moderate-energy settings like the River Alz yield larger, robust grains with partially regular fabrics (Table 5). High-energy cascades, exemplified by Dunn’s River, generate oncoids that are well-rounded and structurally irregular, with laminae repeatedly disrupted by abrasion. These observations have direct implications for fossil interpretation, where spherical or ellipsoidal shapes should not imply quiescent water, and irregular lamination may reflect mechanical truncation rather than diagenesis. Jamaican examples show that high-energy tropical cascades can produce oncoids indistinguishable in gross morphology from low-energy types, but with internal fabrics preserving episodic transport rather than continuous in situ growth.

Global comparison (Table 5) indicates that reliable paleoenvironmental interpretation of fossil oncoids requires integrating morphology, lamination architecture, nucleus attributes, and associated facies. Modern analogues like Dunn’s River provide essential constraints on the range of hydrodynamic and microbial conditions capable of generating diverse oncoid morphologies preserved in the geological record.

Isotopic homogeneity and implications for oncoid growth dynamics

Stable carbon- and oxygen-isotope values of modern tufa deposits are important indicators of the depositional environment. Whereas δ18O values of tufa are controlled by water δ18O value and temperature, as well as by evaporation in exclusively arid and semi-arid climates, the δ13C values primarily reflect the relative contribution of plant- and/or soil-derived, isotopically light carbon and aquifer limestone-derived isotopically heavier carbon5,6,48. The isotopic characteristics of Jamaican oncoids provide insights into hydrology, carbon sourcing, and precipitation mechanisms, supporting sedimentological evidence of pervasive overturning rather than prolonged in-situ stability.

Hydrology and kinetic effects on oxygen isotopes

Jamaican oncoids have heavierδ18O values than those from other oncoid-bearing fluvial systems6, except for Eocene–Oligocene examples from Mallorca that overlap closely with the Jamaican field (Fig. 13). This suggests Jamaican oncoids formed from meteoric-derived freshwater, not strongly evaporative conditions, reflecting the hydrodynamic openness and continuous flow of Dunn’s River. δ18O values of carbonate are largely inherited from meteoric recharge and groundwater composition, with minor downstream modification as degassing tapers off5,6,57.

Disequilibrium processes, primarily vigorous CO₂ degassing and rapid calcification (as evidenced by thickly calcified tree trunks in and around the Dunn’s River), are the likely culprits for the less negative δ18O values of Jamaican tufa. These kinetic conditions are typical of waterfalls, cascades, and high-energy pool margins, where carbonate precipitation is rapid and where oncoids are repeatedly overturned5. Under such circumstances, individual laminae accrete under broadly similar physicochemical conditions, resulting in isotopically homogeneous coatings that lack systematic inner–outer or top–bottom differentiation. Similar kinetic effects have been documented in fast-growing modern tufas and travertines, where precipitation rates outpace isotopic equilibration between dissolved carbonate species and ambient water62,63.

The absence of covariance between δ18O and δ13C observed in Jamaican oncoids is typical for hydrologically open systems with low residence time and minimal evaporation effects, where δ18O generally reflects the stream water δ18O5,6,57,64. Such conditions are consistent with turbulent pool environments where oncoids are frequently rotated, preventing the development of stable upper and lower growth surfaces. In contrast, stable in-situ oncoid growth in low-energy or semi-closed settings would produce isotopic gradients through progressive CO₂ degassing downstream or evaporative enrichment in partially stagnant waters5,65. Previous studies also showed that fluvial carbonates with longer residence times exhibit δ13C–δ18O covariance and broader isotopic ranges, particularly in barrage-dammed reaches and ponded environments6.

Homogenous δ18O values also reflect limited seasonal δ18O differences, with lower δ18O values typically associated with warm intervals, and higher δ1⁸O values in cooler intervals55,66), and limited changes in catchment altitude (cf.,58). In tropical island settings like Jamaica, reduced altitudinal gradients and uniform rainfall sources suppress δ18O variability in stream waters, which is subsequently recorded in precipitating carbonates5.

Sampling was conducted during March and therefore represents only a single hydrologic interval within Dunn’s River’s bimodal rainfall regime. Seasonal variations in discharge, turbulence, sediment mobility, carbonate saturation, and microbial colonization may influence carbonate precipitation dynamics and lamina development during wetter periods. However, because the analyzed oncoids integrate multiple generations of carbonate accretion and transport, their fabrics and isotopic compositions likely reflect time-averaged conditions accumulated over repeated hydrologic cycles rather than a single seasonal state. Future seasonal monitoring would help better constrain short-term environmental variability and its influence on oncoid growth.

Dominance of biogenic CO2

Jamaican oncoids exhibit lighter carbon-isotope values compared to most oncoid-bearing fluvial tufas, including Miocene and Paleogene examples from the Ebro Basin (Fig. 13). This isotopic depletion indicates a dominant contribution of isotopically light CO₂ derived from C₃ plants and soil, reflecting high biological productivity and rapid carbon cycling5,6), driving δ13C values toward more negative compositions irrespective of downstream degassing trends. The latter is normally associated with increasing δ13C values downstream65, as preferential loss of 12CO₂ enriches remaining DIC in 13C. In Jamaican oncoids, however, continuous biological input homogenizes the isotopic signal during repeated oncoid rotation and accretion.

The lack of isotopic differentiation between inner and outer laminae reflects not only stable environmental conditions but also growth under dynamic physical conditions. Frequent overturning exposes all oncoid surfaces to similar water chemistry and degassing regimes, suppressing isotopic asymmetry. This aligns with sedimentological observations of truncated laminae and absence of polar thickening, indicating rotation, not stable attachment, governed oncoid accretion. This is consistent with findings from other fluvial oncoids and stromatolites, where alternating laminae show no significant isotopic differences because carbon incorporation integrates signals from multiple processes6,67.

Freshwater reservoir effect and anomalous radiocarbon ages of oncoids

Radiocarbon dating of the Dunn’s River oncoids indicates very little age difference between innermost and outermost laminae, with the younger, outer lamina sets paradoxically yielding the oldest apparent ages. This pattern contradicts the expected outward accretionary growth of oncoids, analogous to ooid cortices, where systematic age offsets between innermost and outermost laminae may be used to infer their precipitation rates (e.g.,68,69). Instead, the inverted age structure observed here suggests decoupling between the timing of carbonate precipitation and the DIC age in the oncoid cortex. This indicates reservoir effects, in which carbon in carbonate minerals is not isotopically in equilibrium with atmospheric CO₂, resulting in deceptively old radiocarbon ages70. Although the paired inner–outer lamina dataset is limited (n = 2), the reproducible age offsets of 55–80 years exceed analytical uncertainty and are consistent with a modest reservoir effect associated with incorporation of older dissolved inorganic carbon. In freshwater systems, this phenomenon is referred to as the freshwater reservoir effect (FRE), often associated with dissolved ancient carbonates, i.e., the hard-water effect71,72. Under these conditions, carbonate dissolution introduces 14C-depleted carbon into the DIC pool, violating the assumption that carbon is derived from an atmospheric reservoir in isotopic equilibrium.

Understanding the anomalous ages of Dunn’s River oncoids requires considering dominant carbon sources during growth, including atmospheric CO₂ and carbon from the tufa bedrock upstream. Given that present-day atmospheric carbon should yield modern ages for the outermost oncoid laminae, the consistently old apparent ages indicate that carbonate precipitation was instead dominated by DIC sourced from older tufa deposits. This suggests the radiocarbon system is overprinted by a hard-water FRE sensu70, where dissolved ancient carbonates substantially dilute the 14C activity of the aquatic carbon reservoir. In freshwater environments, FRE magnitude depends on the carbonate dissolution and atmospheric CO₂ exchange balance. Under closed-system conditions, calcite dissolution by carbonic acid can dilute 14C by up to 50%, producing reservoir ages approaching one radiocarbon half-life (~ 5,370 years), whereas open-system conditions allow isotopic exchange with soil CO₂, eliminating reservoir offset70. Natural river systems typically occupy intermediate states with FREs ranging from negligible to several thousand years70,73. Dunn’s River system appears toward the latter end, with tufa dissolution dominating DIC pool isotopic composition.

Radiocarbon ages of Dunn’s River oncoids imply tufa deposits supplying dissolved carbonate are at least ~ 3,225 years old (constrained by the oldest conventional age in oncoid cortices; Table 4), and photosynthesizing cyanobacteria mediating carbonate precipitation continue incorporating 14C-depleted carbon derived from progressively older tufa as erosion exposes deeper/older strata. The lack of age differentiation between successive lamina sets reflects a persistently inherited carbon signature masking true depositional timing. This aligns with observations from other freshwater systems, where FREs vary within a river or lake and may change over time as hydrological and geochemical conditions evolve70,73. Future monitoring of Dunn’s River hydrochemistry (including alkalinity, saturation indices, stable isotope values, and radiocarbon age) would provide valuable tests of the precipitation mechanisms and FRE inferred here.

Reliable dating of tufa and tufa-derived carbonates is critical, given their use as archives of Holocene and Pleistocene paleoclimatic and paleoenvironmental change. These results underscore the need for caution when interpreting radiocarbon ages of freshwater carbonates, including oncoids, as the proportion of older or ‘dead’ carbon incorporation may be substantial where carbonate bedrock or older tufa is actively weathered (cf.,74). Unlike the marine reservoir effect, often approximated using a global mean correction, no universal offset exists for freshwater systems due to their pronounced spatial and temporal variability70,73. Consequently, independent age control or paired terrestrial–aquatic dating approaches are essential for constraining FREs in fluvial tufa environments.

Conclusions

Sand- to granule-sized oncoids from Dunn’s River, Jamaica, provide the first well-documented example of modern freshwater fluvial oncoids from the Americas, and demonstrate that oncoids can form and persist in high-energy tropical cascade systems. The Dunn’s River system expands the range of known fluvial environments for oncoid growth and shows that spherical morphologies are not restricted to low-energy or hydrodynamically stable settings. Instead, oncoid development in this system reflects the combined influence of grain mobility, rapid carbonate precipitation, and repeated microbial recolonization under turbulent flow conditions.

Hydrodynamics exerts first-order control on fluvial oncoid morphology and internal fabric. Continuous grain overturning driven by waterfalls and cascades promotes the development of spherical to subspherical grains. Irregular, discontinuous laminae and the general absence of polar thickening indicate growth dominated by rotation and transport rather than prolonged stability. Therefore, sphericity alone cannot be used as a proxy for low-energy conditions and must be evaluated in the context of internal fabrics and depositional setting.

Dense, abrasion-resistant low-Mg calcite nuclei permit repeated cycles of coating, erosion, and recolonization, facilitating sustained growth under energetic conditions. Smaller nuclei tend to develop more uniform cortices, whereas larger nuclei produce asymmetrical and locally truncated lamination, emphasizing the role of nucleus geometry in controlling cortex architecture.

Internal fabrics record episodic disruptions of microbial precipitation. Discontinuous laminae and nested lamination sets reflect brief periods of stabilization during which microbial mats develop, followed by renewed transport and abrasion. These fabrics provide a valuable modern analogue for interpreting irregularly laminated oncoids in ancient fluvial and marginal-marine successions, where similar textures may otherwise be attributed to diagenetic overprinting or low-energy growth.

Stable carbon- and oxygen-isotope compositions support growth under hydrologically open and chemically uniform conditions. The narrow range and homogeneity of δ1⁸O and δ13C values indicate rapid precipitation from well-mixed waters with short residence times and minimal evaporative modification. The lack of δ13C–δ1⁸O covariance is consistent with precipitation in turbulent settings rather than ponded or closed systems. Carbon-isotope depletion reflects a dominant contribution of biogenic CO₂ derived from C₃ vegetation and soils within the Dunn’s River catchment.

Radiocarbon ages reveal the influence of a freshwater reservoir effect, decoupling apparent ages from true depositional timing. Older apparent ages in outer laminae indicate incorporation of 14C-depleted carbon derived from older tufa, underscoring the limitations of conventional radiocarbon dating in carbonate-rich fluvial environments and the need for caution when interpreting absolute ages of oncoid growth.

Overall, the results highlight the importance of integrating morphology, internal fabrics, nucleus characteristics, geochemistry, and facies context when interpreting fossil oncoids. High-energy tropical cascades such as Dunn’s River can generate oncoids that closely resemble those from lower-energy systems in external form, yet differ fundamentally in growth mechanisms, internal fabrics, and isotopic behavior. Modern analogues from such environments provide critical insight into the processes governing oncoid formation and improve our ability to interpret fluvial oncoid-bearing deposits in the geological record.
