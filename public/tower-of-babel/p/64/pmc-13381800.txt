Dynamic breast MRI with Flexible Temporal Resolution Aided by Deep Learning
Authors: Sungheon Gene Kim, Jonghyun Bae, Linda Moy, Laura Heacock, Li Feng, Eddy Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMRI is the most effective method for screening high-risk breast cancer patients. While current exams rely on the qualitative evaluation of morphological features before and after contrast administration and less on contrast kinetic information, recent developments in fast acquisition methods aim to combine both. However, balancing spatial resolution, temporal resolution and scan time poses a considerable challenge in dynamic MRI. Here, we introduce a radial MRI reconstruction framework for Dynamic Contrast Enhanced (DCE) imaging, termed Enhanced Locally low-rank Imaging for Tissue contrast Enhancement (ELITE), to address these limitations. ELITE combines locally low-rank subspace modeling to capture spatially localized tissue dynamics with deep learning. We evaluate its effectiveness using the publicly available fastMRI breast initiative, demonstrating substantial improvements in CNR and noise reduction while enabling flexible temporal resolution down to 1 second. ELITE also shows benefits in neck and brain imaging, making it a viable alternative for other DCE-MRI applications.Subject terms: Cancer imaging, Biomedical engineering, Machine learning, Breast cancer, CNS cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer worldwide and is the second most common cause of cancer death for women in the United States1 with a lifetime risk of 13%2. In recent years, Dynamic Contrast Enhanced (DCE) MRI has been applied widely in high-risk screening population, resulting in a sensitivity of 90–93%, compared with 48–63% for mammography and ultrasound combined3. DCE-MRI evaluates the vascular environment following the intravenous injection of a contrast agent, with signal enhancement characterized using quantitative kinetic information4,5. While the latest trend in acquisition protocols involves incorporating both detailed morphological information from high spatial resolution images and kinetic information6, most DCE-MRI exams solely rely on the qualitative comparison of morphological information before and after contrast. One reason for this is the poor temporal resolution available with current standard protocols, typically 60–120 s per volume, which severely obscures the fast-changing kinetics and the underlying physiological features of malignancy. The development of new acceleration strategies, on the order of a few seconds per frame, can potentially enable not only the high spatial resolution needed to detect small pathology7 but also accurate kinetic measurement8.

Balancing between temporal and spatial resolution poses a challenge in dynamic MRI, where spatial resolution is often compromised for higher frame rates9. To address this challenge, a fast acquisition technique that combines compressed sensing10,11 and parallel imaging with golden-angle radial sampling, called Golden-angle RAdial Sparse Parallel (GRASP) MRI, has been developed to achieve high spatial and flexible temporal resolution12,13. By exploiting the golden-angle radial sampling in GRASP, high temporal resolution subframes can be achieved, each based on a limited number of spokes, substantially less than the Nyquist sampling requirement (i.e., sparse k-space sampling). While golden-angle ordering helps maintain incoherent sampling of k-space across the subframes, using only a few spokes per subframe can lead to undersampling effects, resulting in streak artifacts. GRASP is a reconstruction method that addresses these challenges by combining parallel imaging and compressed sensing methods. The use of parallel imaging14 for accelerating MRI is widely used in clinical imaging exams today and is capable of generating images from undersampled measurements. However, its efficiency in breast DCE-MRI can be limited by the coil array design. Due to DCE-MRI’s inherent sparse representation, compressed sensing10,11 has also become an attractive method by imposing a suitable sparsifying transform along the temporal domain15,16. Another way to exploit the temporal correlations in dynamic MRI is to leverage its low-rank property17. Similar to compressed sensing, low-rank methods provide sparse representations of images over time by assuming they can be represented by a linear combination of a small number of principal components (PC), thereby allowing the recovery of dynamic images from sparse samples18. Low-rank approaches have been used in multiple applications, including quantitative parameter mapping19,20, MR fingerprinting21,22 and DCE-MRI23–25. With the assumption that all image voxels lie in the same temporal subspace and thus are globally correlated, the use of globally low-rank (GLR) modeling for basis function estimation has proven to be useful in representing global contrast change17. To further improve its effectiveness to capture more localized spatially signal dynamics, locally low-rank (LLR) modeling26,27 confines this correlation to local voxel neighborhoods by forming a spatially localized Casorati matrix. Nevertheless, this block-based reconstruction may require parameter tuning to balance between the size of the block and its sensitivity to nearby tissue structures. Recently, the utilization of deep learning (DL) has been shown to be effective in further accelerating MRI measurements by facilitating the recovery of highly undersampled data, making it especially useful in fast dynamic MRI28,29. However, the use of DL to accurately resolve highly undersampled MRI experiments under fast-changing contrast dynamics has not yet been fully addressed.

In this study, we propose an image reconstruction framework for radial k-space data, referred to as Enhanced Locally low-rank Imaging for Tissue contrast Enhancement (ELITE), which offers a joint solution to the aforementioned spatial and temporal DCE-MRI limitations. Our framework leverages a locally low-rank (LLR) subspace model to represent spatially localized dynamics based on tissue-segmented information. By enforcing an explicit LLR subspace constraint for each breast tissue and learning its temporal basis from low-resolution undersampled radial DCE data, a subspace representation is generated and used for the reconstruction of high-resolution images. Our framework also enables the generation of dynamic image series with flexible temporal resolution, up to 1 second/frame, while exhibiting images with reduced undersampling penalty. For this purpose, a dedicated Residual Network (ResNet) is proposed to effectively remove common streak artifacts at a high acceleration rate, while maintaining high temporal fidelity. Notably, our neural network is trained and tested on a unique publicly available dataset of raw k-space data recently generated and released by our group30,31. The proposed framework is evaluated for clinical use and image quality in comparison with other conventional reconstruction methods, and for its temporal information through a pharmacokinetic analysis.

Results

Enhanced locally low-rank (LLR) reconstruction for DCE-MRI

Here, we propose a GRASP-based LLR reconstruction framework (Fig. 1). This framework takes advantage of the radial sampling trajectory, where the densely sampled k-space center (Fig. 1a, light orange radial spokes) allows a reliable reconstruction of low spatial resolution dynamic image-series with standard GRASP reconstruction (Fig. 1b). Based on these low-resolution images, LLR basis functions are estimated using principal component analysis (PCA) for n number of blocks (‘LLR’) or tissue-segments (‘ELITE’) (Fig. 1c), each represented by K most dominant principal components (PC) (Fig. 1d). Next, the high-resolution image-series is compressed to a low-dimensional subspace (Fig. 1e), and as a final step, the high-resolution time-series is reconstructed by multiplying the set of coefficients by the basis functions (Fig. 1f). A detailed comparison between the GLR and LLR approaches for the use of DCE-MRI can be found in the Methods section (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of the image reconstruction framework.

a, b GRASP low-resolution images are reconstructed by solving the optimization problem in Eq. 1. c Based on these images, temporal basis estimation is performed using a principal component analysis (PCA) for each block (‘GRASP-LLR’) or tissue-segments (‘ELITE’). d By utilizing the most dominant basis components, (e) a high-resolution image series is compressed to a low-dimensional subspace. f A high-resolution time series is reconstructed by multiplying the set of coefficients by the basis functions and by solving the optimization problem in Eq. 2.

Improved lesion delineation and noise reduction

For comparison, all radial reconstruction methods were utilized for the reconstruction of breast images with a temporal resolution of 4.2 sec (Fig. 2). The proposed reconstruction framework (Fig. 1), based on either blocks (‘GRASP-LLR’) or tissue-segments (‘ELITE’), showed improved delineation of fine tissue structure (Fig. 2a, b) compared to conventional radial Non-Uniform Fast Fourier Transform (NUFFT), GRASP, and GRASP-GLR methods. While the contrast between the tumor lesion and surrounding tissue appears noisy and blurry for the latter methods, a sharp delineation of the lesion is achieved with both GRASP-LLR and ELITE methods. Moreover, while conventional Cartesian VIBE images were acquired with a temporal resolution of 120 s, GRASP-LLR and ELITE images achieved comparable image quality with a temporal resolution of 4.2 s. Using the GRASP-based LLR approach, we can also gain a substantial reduction in background noise by representing the background signal with a single principal component (K = 1). While NUFFT, GRASP and GRASP-GLR methods experience strong streak artifacts (Fig. 2c, d), the ELITE framework demonstrated a reduction in background noise, revealing sharper delineation of breast vessels (Fig. 2d, indicated by blue dotted frames). Supplementary Video 1 and 2 exhibit the streak artifacts time-series observed in each of the reconstruction methods. The advantage of the ELITE framework is also evident in other breast cases, revealing clear breast morphological features (supplementary Fig. 2, indicated by yellow arrows). These features are largely obscured by streak artifacts when reconstructed by NUFFT, GRASP, GRASP-GLR and GRASP-LLR methods (supplementary Fig. 2, indicated by red arrows). A quantitative analysis of contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) (Fig. 2f) and background noise estimation (Fig. 2g) was conducted by taking into consideration the fibroglandular tissue (Fig. 2e, green mask), the lesion (Fig. 2e, red mask) and its background signal (Fig. 2e, dotted blue squares). When averaged over multiple breast cancer subjects (n = 25), all GRASP-based methods showed a fold increase in CNR and a fold decrease in background noise levels compared to NUFFT with 8.6 ± 4.2/0.2404 ± 0.059, 79.4 ± 27.6/0.0262 ± 0.0129, 187.6 ± 155.7/0.0116 ± 0.0057 and 751.5 ± 309.7/0.0024 ± 0.0013, for GRASP, GRASP-GLR, GRASP-LLR and ELITE, respectively. In addition, a significant increase in CNR and a significant decrease in noise level were observed across the methods, with ELITE achieving the highest fold change in both categories. Individual CNR and noise level fold changes per subject can be seen in supplementary Fig. 3. Notably, the higher CNR and lower noise levels do not come at the cost of longer reconstruction times but rather the opposite. When comparing the reconstruction times, GRASP-LLR and ELITE demonstrated faster convergence with fewer iterations (Supplementary Fig. 4), achieving a 64% reduction compared to conventional GRASP and a 33% reduction compared to GRASP-GLR.

Breast DCE-MRI with high temporal fidelity

To investigate how reliably DCE-MRI reconstruction methods can represent temporal information, we assessed their temporal fidelity information through the signal enhancement-based tissue over time and a pharmacokinetic model (PKM) analysis with a temporal resolution of 4.2 s. Signal enhancement sampled from fibroglandular tissue (Fig. 3a) did not show major differences between GRASP, GRASP-GLR, GRASP-LLR and ELITE, when compared to that of NUFFT (Pearson correlation coefficient/Euclidean norm = 0.59/0.32, 0.51/0.34, 0.5/0.35, 0.5/0.35, respectively). Signal enhancement based on ELITE exhibited a slightly higher correlation and lower norm values for benign (0.98/0.91, Fig. 3b) and malignant lesion tissue (0.98/1.08, Fig. 3c), among the rest of the GRASP methods. The mean and standard deviation of PKM parameters were estimated for healthy fibroglandular tissues and compared across the different reconstruction methods (Fig. 3d). Except for blood flow (Fp) that was found to be significantly higher (P < 0.0001) for NUFFT, all other pharmacokinetic parameters exhibited consistent values with no significant difference across all five reconstruction methods. Pharmacokinetic parameter maps of ve, vp, Fp and Ktrans for fibroglandular tissue, benign and malignant lesion (Supplementary Figs. 5–7, respectively), corresponding to those shown in Fig. 3a–c, demonstrated comparable parametric maps, except for NUFFT that was distorted by streak artifacts.

Signal enhancement ratio curves of (a) fibroglandular tissue, (b) benign, and (c) malignant lesion tissue, are plotted for different GRASP-based reconstruction methods. Each curve is scored for its correlation and Euclidean norm, in comparison to NUFFT. Curves are plotted based on the average signal given by the region of interest (ROI) marked in red. d Pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters (ve, vp, Fp, PS and Ktrans) of a two-compartment exchange model were measured for healthy fibroglandular tissue cases (n = 19), showing no significant difference across all five reconstruction methods, except for blood flow (Fp) that was found to be significantly higher (p < 0.0001) for NUFFT. Fast DCE-MRI aided by deep learning

Based on the image quality and temporal fidelity achieved with the proposed ELITE framework, we next utilized it for reconstructing fast dynamic images with 1-sec temporal resolution (2 spokes/frame). As expected, the reconstruction of these highly undersampled images resulted in strong streak artifacts that distort all parts of the breast tissue. To address this challenge, a Residual Neural Network (ResNet) was trained with simulated data, generated by a Digital Reference Object (DRO) simulation toolkit for breast DCE-MRI30. To enhance machine learning training augmentation, each tissue (i.e., malignant or benign, fibroglandular, pectoral muscle, skin and heart), across all available cases, was estimated for its rise time, creating a dictionary of typical rise times per tissue (Fig. 4a). Low resolution simulated DCE dynamic data included fully sampled images as reference and undersampled images reconstructed with two spokes (Fig. 4b). Once trained, ResNet was tested on real undersampled images (‘input’), generating images with reduced noise (‘output’) (Fig. 4c). A representative time-series data of the ResNet input and output can be found in Supplementary Video 3. Furthermore, to validate the temporal fidelity of the data generated by ResNet, we compared the temporal information of the output image-series with the input image-series, plotted for each tissue separately (Fig. 4d). Dynamic curves showed high correlation and norm of 0.99/0.4, 0.99/1.08, 0.98/0.61, 0.92/0.38 for fibroglandular, malignant, muscle and skin tissues, respectively. The combination of ELITE with ResNet also proved to show better results when comparing its performance against other convolutional neural network approaches, such as UNet. When replacing ResNet with UNet, output DCE images showed inferior results when compared to ResNet, demonstrating more spatial artifacts and less faithful temporal dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 8). Finally, the output image series generated by ResNet were plugged into our reconstruction framework (Fig. 1a) for basis estimation, generating high spatial and 1-sec temporal resolution image series (Fig. 4e) with 144-time frames.

ELITE reconstruction aided by Residual Neural Network (ResNet).

a A ResNet was trained with simulated data generated by a Digital Reference Object (DRO) toolkit. To augment training data, each tissue was estimated for its rise time, creating a dictionary of typical rise times per tissue segment. b DRO-simulated data included undersampled and fully sampled low-resolution DCE images. c The trained ResNet was tested on real undersampled DCE images, generating output images with reduced noise. d ResNet ‘input dynamics’ and ‘output dynamics’ image-series were compared for their temporal information, demonstrating high correlation for different breast tissues. e Output image-series generated by ResNet, were plugged into the ELITE reconstruction framework for basis estimation, generating high spatial and temporal resolution ELITE images series. Source data of d are provided as a Source Data file.

Clinical assessment through qualitative and quantitative DCE analysis

For the purpose of assessing breast tissue under typical clinical conditions, we examined the use of the ELITE framework for the assessment of pre-, post-, and subtracted DCE (sub-DCE) images reconstructed with a temporal resolution of 4.2 s (Fig. 5a–d), and its use for quantitative pharmacokinetic analysis with a 1-sec temporal resolution (Fig. 5e, f). The pre-, post-, and sub-DCE images reconstructed with ELITE demonstrated a clear improvement in image quality and lesion delineation compared to the GRASP method. They also exhibited high correspondence with conventional Cartesian VIBE images. The advantage of the ELITE framework remained consistent, also when compared to the other GRASP-based methods, across various examples (Supplementary Figs. 9–13). To validate these findings, we conducted a clinical evaluation performed by two board-certified breast radiologists who were blinded to the reconstruction methods. The radiologists assessed each case for diagnostic BI-RADS scores, lesion conspicuity/delineation, image quality, and artifact level (see Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Positive prediction using BI-RADS 4 and above resulted in a sensitivity of 0.920 (95% CI: 0.740, 0.990) for ELITE, and 0.880 (95% CI: 0.688, 0.975) for VIBE and GRASP, based on both readers. Specificity was 0.655 (95% CI: 0.457, 0.821) for ELITE and VIBE, and 0.724 (95% CI: 0.528, 0.873) and 0.759 (95% CI: 0.565, 0.897) for GRASP for both readers. Sensitivity and specificity were also calculated for BI-RADS 3 and above (see Supplementary Tables 2 and 3) as positive prediction. Lesion conspicuity was rated on a 3-point scale (see Method section). For ELITE, all lesions were either rated as “Visible” or “Only visible if location is given”, with 50 out of 54 lesions rated as “Visible” by Reader 1, and 49 out of 54 by Reader 2. VIBE showed slightly more variability, with 2 lesions rated as “Not visible”, 3 as “Only visible if location is given”, and 49 as “Visible” for both readers. GRASP exhibited the greatest variation, with 8 lesions rated as “Not visible”, 9 as “Only visible if location is given”, and 37 as “Visible” for Reader 1, and 9 as “Not visible”, 9 as “Only visible if location is given” and 36 as “Visible” for Reader 2. Image quality and artifact level were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (see “Method” section). For image quality, VIBE received the highest mean scores of 4.80 ± 0.45 for Reader 1, and 4.78 ± 0.46 for Reader 2. ELITE yielded also high scores of 4.07 ± 0.82 and 4.06 ± 0.83, respectively. GRASP yielded the lowest scores of 2.22 ± 0.50 for Reader 1, and 2.24 ± 0.61 for Reader 2. Artifact level scores followed a similar trend, where VIBE received scores of 4.67 ± 0.51 for Reader 1, and 4.63 ± 0.56 for Reader 2. ELITE was rated at 3.93 ± 0.80 and 3.96 ± 0.85, while GRASP received scores of 2.28 ± 0.53 and 2.13 ± 0.75 for Readers 1 and 2, respectively.

When sampling the aorta lumen based on ELITE images with different temporal resolutions (Fig. 5e), the dynamic curve with a time resolution of 1 sec (2 spokes) exhibited a fast-rising curve with the highest peak compared to 4.2 s (8 spokes) and 5.25 s (13 spokes). Next, we examined the use of ELITE images for quantitative PKM assessment of healthy fibroglandular tissue, benign and malignant lesions. A PKM analysis (Fig. 5f), based on 1-sec temporal resolution, showed a good separation between patient groups for most pharmacokinetic parametric parameters. While the mean values of extracellular-extravascular space volume fraction (ve) showed no significant difference between healthy, benign and malignant cases, the blood volume fraction (vp), blood flow (Fp), permeability surface area product (PS) and exchange rate (Ktrans) indicated statistically significant differences between patient groups.

Applications in neck and brain imaging

Our reconstruction framework can also be utilized for other DCE-MRI applications. For head and neck imaging (Fig. 6a), a K-means clustering-based segmentation was used for ELITE reconstruction, generating time-series images with accurate temporal enhancement of blood veins and carotid artery, consistent with NUFFT (Fig. 6a, orange arrows). However, for the same time point, GRASP-GLR showed false enhancement, as indicated by the red arrows. Another important anatomy that can benefit from the ELITE framework is the brain, where white matter (WM), gray matter (GM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) information can be utilized for its tissue-based reconstruction (Fig. 6b, upper panel), resulting in T1-weighted DCE-MRI images with lower noise artifacts compared to conventional NUFFT and GRASP reconstruction (Fig. 6b, lower panel, white arrows).

Applications in DCE-MRI for head and neck, and brain.

Other anatomical information can be utilized for ELITE image reconstruction: (a) ELITE DCE head and neck data yield time-series images with accurate temporal enhancement of arterial and venous blood, consistent with NUFFT (orange arrows), while GRASP-GLR exhibits a false enhancement in posterior vessels (red arrows). b Brain imaging can also be utilized for ELITE segmented-based reconstructed composed of white matter (WM), gray matter (GM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) maps, resulting in enhanced T1-weighted anatomical images with reduced streak artifacts (white arrows).

Discussion

In this work, we propose a radial DCE-MRI reconstruction framework, tailored for clinical breast DCE-MRI exams, that leverages advanced acceleration concepts, combing low-rank subspace modeling and machine learning, while providing sharp images in a single scan. Specifically, the proposed framework (‘ELITE’) does not require any specific acquisition parameters and is shown to not only improve breast DCE image quality in terms of CNR and noise reduction, but also provide reliable temporal information, even at 1-sec resolution. This work has the potential to improve current breast DCE-MRI protocols and may also benefit head and neck and brain DCE-MRI.

Standard DCE-MRI imaging methods are limited by the need to balance between spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and scan time. While many methods have proposed to accelerate MRI temporal and spatial resolution, only a few are optimized to capture the dynamics of breast DCE-MRI. Wang et al. have employed a multitasking DCE-MRI technique based on Cartesian sampling, achieving a temporal resolution of 1.4 seconds with good image quality (0.9 × 0.9 × 1.1 mm3), tested on a small breast cancer cohort32. Pineda et al. proposed to increase temporal resolution (2.0 s) during early-phase breast DCE-MRI by using reduced FOV imaging and subsequently resolving the Cartesian aliasing artifacts. As demonstrated in other patient exams33,34, radial GRASP imaging can also serve reliably and is able to deliver comparable diagnostic performance to conventional DCE-MRI. Moreover, the possibility of radial imaging to flexibly undersample its data while providing diagnostic images, is an advantage over the counterpart Cartesian protocols. Initially, GRASP DCE-MRI studies relied primarily on total variation (TV) regularization along the temporal dimension, which prioritized spatial fidelity for multiphase evaluation, typically resulting in modest temporal resolutions13. More recently, advanced acceleration techniques, such as low-rank subspace modeling23, have enabled higher temporal resolutions, often below 10 seconds, depending on the clinical application. For example, it has been shown that when utilizing radial sampling for abdominal imaging, a highly accelerated DCE-MRI can be employed, achieving sub-second temporal resolution35. The ability to reconstruct DCE-MRI time-series from limited data is due to the inherent undersampling nature and the high degree of sparsity of these experiments. Building upon previous work17,20,24, a reliable low-dimensional representation of temporal DCE-MRI information can be achieved with low-rank subspace modeling.

One way of estimating a temporal basis through low-rank subspace modeling is by a model-based approach19,21. By utilizing a physical signal model that generates an ensemble of possible signal enhancement patterns, a temporal basis can be generated using principal component analysis (PCA). However, a single physical model of a breast DCE-MRI may not be sufficient to accurately represent the dynamics of all breast tissues. Alternatively, a data-driven approach that estimates its temporal basis from real training data36, acquired separately or from low-resolution data, can be more reliable and efficient. In this work, the temporal basis is estimated using low-resolution data, without the need for additional data acquisition. In this sense, the geometry of radial sampling provides an optimal solution, as undersampling rates are much lower around the central k-space than the periphery. Thus, low spatial resolution data can provide sufficient temporal information for estimating a temporal basis, which can then be utilized to represent the high-resolution image series in a low-dimensional subspace37. To ensure that no breast anatomy is lost within the low-resolution images, the images in this work were restricted to half the original spatial resolution.

Moreover, given that DCE correlations tend to be higher among neighboring voxels, it is logical to confine the estimation of the temporal information to local voxel neighborhoods through locally low-rank (LLR) modeling. Indeed, as opposed to the globally low-rank (GLR) approach that estimates its temporal basis function based on the whole image, we show that when using GRASP with locally low-rank (LLR), either with block (‘GRASP-LLR’) or tissue segment-based reconstruction (‘ELITE’) (Supplementary Fig. 1), sharper DCE time-series images can be generated with negligible streak artifacts (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2), which results in higher CNR and lower background noise (Fig. 2f, g). Nevertheless, in some cases, GRASP-LLR still exhibited some residual streaking artifacts due to blocks located on the boundary between tissue and background signal (Supplementary Video 2). As such, block-based reconstruction requires some parameter tuning to balance between small size block, characterized by high sensitivity to nearby structures, and large size block, characterized by less specific correlated structures38. Notably, in our study, common block flickering19 across the time domain was barely noticeable, due to an overlapping sliding-window strategy used39. Overall, employing the tissue segment-based reconstruction (‘ELITE’) approach, where each tissue type is represented by a set of principal components that capture the unique temporal dynamics for that tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1, far right), resulted in improved image quality. This approach was further extended to confine background noise using a single principal component, resulting in a significant reduction in background noise and streak artifacts. One reason for the sharper images by GRASP-LLR and ELITE over conventional GRASP-based images is the ill-conditioning of the GRASP reconstruction problem18. For non-Cartesian imaging, such ill-conditioning often shows up as blurring artifacts, when the reconstruction has not yet converged. Notably, the ELITE reconstruction framework approach, shown in Eq. 2, exhibited faster and more efficient convergence (Supplementary Fig. 4) in comparison to GRASP and GRASP-GLR.

To test our reconstruction framework in providing reliable temporal information, we compared all GRASP-based reconstruction methods and their corresponding pharmacokinetic parameters (Fig. 3), as there is no established gold standard for measuring these parameters. Except for NUFFT that was heavily distorted by streak artifacts, all other reconstructions methods showed comparable parametric maps (Supplementary Fig. 5-7). Studies have shown that a temporal resolution of at least 16 seconds is necessary to depict tracer dynamics of the tumor, and towards 1-second to capture the dynamics of the arterial input function (AIF)8, both of which are key factors in the pharmacokinetic modeling. However, reaching higher temporal acceleration requires higher undersampling factors, which are associated with Nyquist violation and thus result in stronger streak artifacts. To address this challenge, we designed a Residual Neural Network (ResNet) with two main goals in mind: (i) to effectively overcome the spatial undersampling penalty (i.e., streak artifacts), while (ii) maintaining high temporal fidelity. Deep neural networks have demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing MRI artifacts and noise in non-Cartesian image reconstruction40. However, their ability to mitigate undersampling artifacts in the presence of contrast injection while maintaining reliable temporal information has been limited. Moreover, the lack of large open-source databases for DCE-MRI has posed a significant hurdle for training and testing new machine learning models. In this study, we used a Digital Reference Object (DRO) toolkit30 and a publicly available dataset31, recently released by our group, to generate breast DCE data, undersampled and fully-sampled, which were then utilized for the training of our neural network. Using ResNet, ELITE reconstruction was able to yield reliable 1-sec DCE time-series with high temporal fidelity, free from streak artifacts (Supplementary Video 3). For optimal diagnostic assessment of breast DCE-MRI, both qualitative examination of pre-, post- and sub-DCE images, as well as quantitative kinetic analysis, should be used6. Since high temporal resolution is not required for qualitative morphology examination, ELITE images were reconstructed with 4.2 s temporal resolution, resulting in clearer and sharper anatomy and good correspondence to conventional Cartesian VIBE images (Fig. 5a–d). To test the diagnostic impact of DCE images, we conducted a clinical evaluation and image quality assessment by two readers (see Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, and Supplementary Fig. 14). When setting BI-RADS 4 and above as a positive prediction cut-off, sensitivity was highest for ELITE (0.920), while VIBE and GRASP showed slightly lower values (0.880). However, GRASP achieved the highest specificity (0.724, 0.759), while ELITE and VIBE achieved the same score (0.655), indicating better avoidance of false positives by GRASP. The relatively low specificity with both ELITE and VIBE may be partly explained by the fact that readers did not have access to prior imaging studies or complementary non-MRI modalities (such as mammography or ultrasound), which would typically help in making more accurate diagnostic assessments. In terms of lesion conspicuity, ELITE showed the best lesion visibility, with nearly all lesions rated as “Visible” by both readers. VIBE had slightly more variability, while GRASP had many lesions rated as “Not visible” or “Only visible if location is given”. Finally, VIBE and ELITE received the highest scores for both image quality and artifact suppression, while GRASP scored lowest on both metrics. Overall, ELITE offered the most balanced diagnostic and overall image quality performance. To quantify vascular properties through kinetic analysis, high temporal resolution is desirable. Therefore, ELITE images were reconstructed with 1-sec temporal resolution, exhibiting improved enhancement curves when sampled from the aorta (Fig. 5e), compared to lower temporal resolutions. The kinetic parameters estimated for fibroglandular tissue, benign and malignant lesions, were within the range of other reported studies32,41, indicating a good separation between the three tissue types.

Several potential future directions can be considered for utilizing the ELITE framework in other DCE-MRI applications. For head and neck imaging (Fig. 6a), ELITE images showed an accurate temporal enhancement of blood in the veins and carotid artery, consistent with NUFFT (Fig. 6a, orange arrows). Furthermore, by performing temporal basis estimation for white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), we achieved improved brain images with minimal artifact patterns (Fig. 6b, white arrows). Another interesting potential application of this framework is for the improvement of breast fat saturation (Supplementary Fig. 15). In this example, fat (masked in blue) was represented by a single principal component, effectively removing residual fat signals and improving overall breast image quality.

When comparing ELITE with a broader range of dynamic MRI reconstruction approaches, several important technical and qualitative insights emerge (Supplementary Table 4). Most existing DCE-MRI methods are designed for abdominal, liver, or pulmonary imaging rather than breast applications. Among DL-based techniques, supervised learning is the most used training paradigm, with networks trained either in the image domain or k-space domain. In contrast to Cartesian trajectories, golden-angle radial sampling, used by ELITE and several other methods, offers greater flexibility in temporal resolution, which is particularly advantageous for highly undersampled DCE-MRI. Although ELITE is still in its early development, its integration into the clinical workflow is feasible. Leveraging existing platforms already in use at our clinical center, like Yarra42, which supports radial sampling, would allow a smooth transition into clinical practice and help broaden accessibility of advanced image reconstruction methods such as ELITE. Overall, this study is limited by the relatively small patient cohort, its reliance on a single acquisition protocol, and validation within a single center. As a result, the generalizability and clinical translatability of the proposed approach are not yet fully established. To address this limitation, future work will focus on expanding the study to a larger clinical population, including multi-center validation, to evaluate ELITE’s clinical applicability.

In conclusion, among other advance developments in MR breast cancer imaging, such as diffusion43, hyperpolarization44 and spectroscopy45, DCE-MRI remains the most effective clinical method today. In this study, we present a valuable step towards establishing an image reconstruction framework for radial imaging as a viable alternative for breast DCE-MRI exams. Overall, our approach offers enhanced image quality and high temporal fidelity, with potential impact in a range of DCE-MRI applications.

Methods

Patient population

This study was conducted with fifty-four breast cancer patients who underwent clinical diagnostic breast MRI exams. Patients were eligible to participate in this study if they are over 18 years of age, while those undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy were excluded. It was performed with the approval from New York University (NYU) Langone Health Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from each subject. Sex-based analyses were not performed because the cohort included female participants only; therefore, the findings may not generalize to males with breast cancer. Participants did not receive compensation for participation. To ensure that all reconstruction methods were evaluated with a broad range of lesion types, our study included a balanced representation of both malignant and benign lesions. It included patients with malignant lesions (n = 25; 30–75 years old; 50.1 ± 12.2 years) and patients with benign lesions (n = 29; 25–68 years old; 40.1 ± 11 years). Malignant lesions included invasive ductal carcinomas (IDC) (n = 18), invasive lobular carcinomas (ILC) (n = 3), a mix of IDC and ILC (n = 2), and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) (n = 2). The breast lesion tissue (malignant and benign) was manually segmented by a trained breast imaging radiologist (LH with eight years of experience), while the breast healthy fibroglandular tissue was manually selected from contralateral benign breast cases, avoiding both lesions and fat tissues. The average size of the malignant and benign lesions (along the major axis in millimeters) is 22.5 ± 19.5 and 9.4 ± 3.6, respectively (see Supplementary Table 1), and for the healthy fibroglandular tissue it is 20.6 ± 6.2. The slices used for segmentation were selected from the center of each lesion.

Data acquisition

Patients were scanned on a 3 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM TimTrio, Siemens Healthcare) with a 16-channel breast coil. Diagnostic exam included DCE-MRI with a free-breathing gradient-echo radial volume-interpolated breath-hold exam (VIBE) sequence and reconstructed with Golden-angle RAdial Sparse Parallel (GRASP)13. Scanning parameters included 288 radial views, 192 slices with partial-Fourier, TE/TR = 1.8/4.87 ms, flip-angle = 10 degrees, field of view = 320 × 320 × 212 mm3 in axial orientation, bandwidth = 520 Hz/pixel and spatial resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.1 mm3. The GRASP DCE-MRI total scan time was 2.5 min. A single SPAIR (Spectrally selective Adiabatic Inversion Recovery) fat saturation pulse (116.6 ms) was applied once every golden angle view. A single dose of gadobutrol (Gadavist, Bayer Healthcare Pharmaceuticals) at 0.1 mmol/kg body weight was injected at 2 mL/s intravenously at 30 s into the scan. In addition to a radial VIBE scan, a Cartesian gradient-echo VIBE scan with fat suppression was used. Cartesian VIBE was acquired one phase before radial VIBE, and three phases post-contrast. Each T1-weighted VIBE scan took 120 s with TR/TE = 1.52/4.01 ms, flip-angle = 12 degrees, and spatial resolution of 1.2 × 0.7 × 1.1 mm3.

Globally versus locally low-rank reconstruction for DCE MRI

The globally low-rank (GLR) model enforces that dynamic image-series with a low-rank condition can be represented by a few dominant basis functions and their corresponding coefficients (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The GLR approach estimates its basis function from the whole image, which is less effective in representing spatially localized signal dynamics commonly observed in breast DCE images. With the locally low-rank (LLR) approach, the image can be divided either into blocks (‘LLR’, Supplementary Fig. 1b) or tissue-segments (‘ELITE’, Supplementary Fig. 1c). This way, each region can be decomposed into a product of temporal basis functions. Following the ELITE reconstruction approach, each tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1c, far right) can be represented by its own set of singular values, which can then be expressed by K most dominant basis components.

The number of iterations and λ value was empirically tested to balance between image quality and noise reduction (Supplementary Fig. 17). The ELITE framework and all the rest of the reconstruction methods were tested on a Linux workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5975WX (32 cores), 512 GB RAM, and 2 × 48 GB GPUs.

Network architecture and simulation

A 2D Residual Neural Network (ResNet) with ten convolutional layers was implemented in Pytorch and trained using the Adam optimizer. ResNet was trained in a supervised fashion using an L2-norm loss function between undersampled images reconstructed with two spokes and fully sampled reference images. The training data was generated by a breast DRO simulation toolkit30 that creates realistic morphology and contrast kinetics of breast tissues. Training datasets for the simulation were derived from a bigger repository database that we recently released31. Training included eighteen cases (nine malignant and nine benign) and two cases for validation (one malignant and one benign). To enhance machine learning training through data augmentation, each tissue (i.e., malignant or benign, fibroglandular, pectoral muscle, skin and heart) across all available cases was estimated for its rise time (Fig. 4a), creating a dictionary of typical rise times per tissue. This information was then incorporated into the DRO simulation toolkit (Fig. 4b), generating 50 randomly augmented datasets per existing case. In total, this resulted in 900 different datasets for training and an additional 100 datasets for validation. Once the ResNet model has been trained, the inference time for processing a new dataset is 1-2 s. For comparison purposes, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, we used a 2D UNet convolutional neural network with an encoder–decoder architecture. The number of feature maps doubled at each level from 32 to 256 ([32, 64, 128, 256]), with batch normalization and transposed convolutions used for upsampling.

CNR and noise estimation

Contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) estimation was performed between a malignant lesion (Fig. 2e, red region) and fibroglandular tissue (Fig. 2e, green region) segmentation regions (CNR=Meanmalignant−MeanfibroglandularSTDEVNoise). To minimize the influence of streak artifacts on noise estimation (STDEVNoise), noise was calculated from the subtraction of consecutive dynamic images (after contrast injection), such that the only difference between the reconstruction methods is due to random noise49,50. Standard deviation of noise was calculated based on six ROI’s sampled in the background (Fig. 2e, dotted blue frames). CNR and background noise estimation were performed on fifteen malignant cases and was calculated for the fold change between each GRASP-based reconstruction method and conventional NUFFT.

Dynamic signal and pharmacokinetic model analysis

Signal enhancement plots sampled for different tissues (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 16) were evaluated in comparison to the dynamic signal derived from NUFFT images as a reference to assess temporal fidelity, as demonstrated in the original GRASP study13. Reader study

We performed a reader study to compare the diagnostic performance of VIBE, GRASP, and ELITE. The reader study included two board-certified breast radiologists with 8 (L.H.) and 23 (L.M.) years of experience, who were blinded to the reconstruction methods. The readers were provided with post-contrast images, subtraction images and lesion masks for all fifty-four cases. For GRASP and ELITE, the last frame images were used as the post-contrast images while, for VIBE, the image acquired immediately after the radial acquisition was included as the post-contrast image. Subtraction images for GRASP and ELITE were from subtracting the first frame from the last frame images. For VIBE, the one acquired before the radial acquisition was used to generate a subtraction image. The assessment was based on post-contrast images, while sub-DCE and masked lesion images were used for reference by the readers. For clinical evaluation, the readers were asked to rate each case with a diagnostic BI-RADS score (1, 2, 3, 4 or 5) and for lesion conspicuity/delineation on a three-point Likert-type scale (1: Not visible; 2: Only visible if location is given; 3: Visible). The diagnostic performance was calculated for each reader by comparing AUC and BI-RADS scores with ground-truth outcomes (biopsy results for all malignant cases; biopsy and/or imaging follow-up for benign cases). For cases with more than one lesion, if one lesion was benign and another was suspicious, the case was rated with an actionable BI-RADS score. For assessment of image quality, the readers were asked to rate each case for overall image quality on a five-point Likert-type scale (1: Extremely poor; 2: Moderately poor; 3: Average; 4: Moderately good; 5: Excellent), and for artifact level on a five-point Likert-type scale (1: Severe artifacts; 2: Moderate artifacts; 3: Mild artifacts; 4: Barely Noticeable; 5: No artifacts).

Statistical analysis

Differences in CNR and background noise between GRASP-based reconstruction methods were performed using two-tailed paired Student’s t tests in Microsoft Excel, while differences in PK parameters were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with a two-sided Tukey’s multiple comparisons test in Matlab. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation coefficients between signal enhancement ratio curves were calculated using MATLAB.

The BI-RADS scores were used to compute the diagnostic performance of VIBE, GRASP, and ELITE by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) (AUC), sensitivity and specificity. The difference (i.e., p-values) between methods based on sensitivity and specificity was calculated using the McNemar test. Differences between methods in image quality, artifact level and lesion conspicuity were calculated using the Kruskal–Wallis test.
