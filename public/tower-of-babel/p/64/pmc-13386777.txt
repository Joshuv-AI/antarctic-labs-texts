The evolving clinical profile of laryngeal tuberculosis: a 10-year retrospective study from a tertiary hospital in northern China
Authors: Zhitao Fan, Wenxin Dong, Xuexia Wang, Lina Han, Sunan Shi, Zhilei Cao, Zhenhua Qiao, Xiaolan Zhang, Chaobing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to elucidate the contemporary clinical characteristics and evolving trends of laryngeal tuberculosis(LTB), thereby providing evidence for optimizing clinical diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical data from patients diagnosed with LTB at a single-center medical institution in northern China between July 2014 and June 2025.ResultsA total of 49 patients with LTB were included in this study, predominantly males aged 55–64 years. The most common presenting symptom was hoarseness (67.34%), while systemic symptoms were infrequent. Laryngoscopic examination revealed diverse lesion morphologies, with the mixed-pattern type being the most prevalent (38.78%). Furthermore, lesions frequently involved two or more laryngeal subsites (65.31%). Chest CT findings indicated that 73.47% of patients had concomitant active or inactive pulmonary tuberculous foci. Laboratory investigations showed a PPD-positive rate of 55.10% and a sputum smear-positive rate for acid-fast bacilli of 38.88%. Among the 32 patients who underwent pathological biopsy, classic histopathological features, including epithelial granulomas with caseous necrosis, were observed. Following anti-tuberculosis treatment, the laryngeal lesions showed significant improvement or complete resolution in the vast majority of patients.ConclusionDiagnosing LTB is often challenging due to its clinical resemblance to other laryngeal pathologies. Otorhinolaryngologists should consistently consider LTB in the differential diagnosis, particularly for patients with high-risk factors for tuberculosis who present with polymorphic lesions involving multiple laryngeal sites on laryngoscopy. Re-evaluating and thoroughly understanding the shifting trends in the clinical presentation of LTB are crucial for achieving early identification and accurate diagnosis of this disease.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Primary laryngeal tuberculosis, Secondary laryngeal tuberculosis, Clinical characteristics, Retrospective study

Introduction

Laryngeal tuberculosis (LTB) is a chronic granulomatous disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, characterized by the formation of granulomas in laryngeal tissue and a cell-mediated immune response [1]. It represents the most common site of extrapulmonary tuberculosis infection following cervical lymph node involvement [2].

In 2018, approximately 860,000 new tuberculosis cases were reported in China, accounting for 8.6% of the global total and ranking second worldwide [3]. Although the incidence of LTB in pulmonary TB patients is estimated at 0.08–5.1%, the true figure is hard to define. The lack of systematic otorhinolaryngologic assessment in most patients suggests that LTB is underdiagnosed and likely occurs more frequently than reported [4]. In recent years, alongside the resurgence of tuberculosis and the increase in drug-resistant cases, the clinical presentation of LTB has become increasingly complex and variable [5, 6]. This often leads to diagnostic delays, which may elevate the risk of complications and adversely impact both disease prognosis and the formulation of public health strategies [7, 8].

LTB can occur at any site within the larynx, accompanied by diverse clinical and endoscopic findings. Auxiliary diagnostic methods include skin tests and sputum analysis [9, 10]. However, histopathologic and microbiologic evaluation of biopsied laryngeal lesions is fundamental-both for confirming LTB and for excluding other chronic diseases, such as neoplasms and other infectious granulomatous disorders [11, 12]. Although literature reports show that the quadruple antituberculosis therapy is effective in treating tuberculosis with low drug resistance and few sequelae, it is still important to obtain early diagnosis and intervention of LTB [13].

This study aims to conduct a retrospective analysis of LTB cases managed at a single center in northern China over the past decade. By summarizing their clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic responses, we seek to elucidate the contemporary features and evolving trends of this disease, thereby providing a basis for optimizing clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Patients and methods

Data source and patient selection

Clinical data from 49 patients diagnosed with LTB in the Department of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery at Hebei Eye Hospital between July 2014 and June 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. The cohort comprised 39 males and 10 females (male-to-female ratio of 3.9:1). The median age was 48 years (range: 25–80 years).

Data collection

Demographic data including age, gender, systemic and laryngeal symptoms, and risk factors predisposing to LTB were obtained. All patients underwent laryngoscopy, and the endoscopic characteristics of the lesions-including location, extent of involvement, and morphological features-were documented. Some patients underwent purified protein derivative (PPD) testing and bacteriological examinations. Laryngeal and chest CT examination were performed for all cases. A subset of patients underwent further diagnostic direct laryngoscopy, during which biopsies were taken for histopathological diagnosis, the Ziehl-Neelsen stain were performed to identify Mycobacterium tuberculosis and to exclude other possible pathogens, and observation of tuberculous granulomas.

The diagnostic criteria for LTB infection required, in addition to the identification of a laryngeal lesion, at least one of the following: histopathology consistent with chronic granulomatous inflammation with or without caseous necrosis; positive acid-fast staining or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) on histopathological sections; or detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in sputum smear. Patients with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Classification of morphological features under laryngoscopy

Lesions were classified into four types based on endoscopic appearance: Inflammatory-infiltrative: Manifested as mucosal congestion and edema, with gray-white miliary nodules on the surface of the local mucosa and swelling of the submucosal tissue. Ulcerative-necrotic: Presented with irregularly bordered ulcers of varying depth on a background of congestion and edema. The ulcer surfaces were often covered with gray-white caseous necrotic material. Ulcer depth varied with disease severity, and the affected area was prone to bleeding on contact. Granulomatous-proliferative: Characterized by obvious granulation tissue hyperplasia at the site of pronounced pathology, with visible necrotic material on the surface. Mixed: Exhibited features of two or more of the above types. (Fig. 1)

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using absolute frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using histograms. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the rank-sum test. The Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was used for comparing non-normally distributed continuous variables, while the T-test was applied for normally distributed ones. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS22.0 (IBM SPSS Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results

General characteristics

This study included a total of 49 patients with LTB infection. A higher incidence was observed in males, with the most common age group being 55–64 years. The interval from symptom onset to the first medical consultation ranged from 12 days to 16 months (median: 3.9 months). Among the cohort, 18 patients had a long-term smoking history, 8 had a history of excessive alcohol consumption, 8 had diabetes mellitus, and 6 had concurrent immune system disorders (rheumatoid arthritis (n = 2), autoimmune thyroiditis (n = 1), and unspecified immune system disorder (n = 3). Clinical characteristics

Among the cases, 33 patients (67.34%) presented with hoarseness, 15 patients (30.61%) experienced odynophagia (painful swallowing), and 11 patients (22.44%) reported pharyngeal discomfort such as a foreign body sensation. Significant respiratory symptoms, including cough with sputum production, dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), and breathing difficulties, were observed in 12.24% (6 cases), 10.20% (5 cases), and 4.08% (2 cases) of patients, respectively. Only 6 patients exhibited tuberculosis-related systemic symptoms, primarily including fever, fatigue, night sweats, loss of appetite, and progressive weight loss (Fig. 2).

Endoscopic features

Based on endoscopic appearance, the inflammatory-infiltrative and granulomatous-proliferative type were identified in 13 (26.53%) and 11 (22.44%) cases, respectively. The ulcerative-necrotic type was observed in only 6 cases (12.24%). In contrast, the mixed type, exhibiting features of two or more of the aforementioned patterns, was the most common, identified in 19 cases.(Table 2).

The larynx was further subdivided into seven anatomical sites for lesion distribution analysis: epiglottis, true vocal fold, false vocal fold, arytenoid, interarytenoid region, aryepiglottic fold, and subglottis. Endoscopic evaluation revealed lesions were confined to a single laryngeal site in 17 cases (34.69%). Among these, the true vocal folds were involved in 10 cases. The epiglottis was involved in four cases while false vocal folds were involved in two case and aryepiglottic folds in one case. Involvement of two or more subsites was observed in 32 cases (65.30%). Specifically, involvement of both the glottis and subglottis was seen in 15 cases, and involvement of both the supraglottis and glottis in 21 cases. In three cases, the disease extended beyond the larynx to involve sites such as the oropharynx, tonsils, tongue base, and hypopharynx (Fig. 3).

Laryngeal and thoracic imaging findings

Among patients who underwent laryngeal CT, 85.71% (42 cases) exhibited non-specific findings such as local thickening, bulging, or inflammatory changes. All 49 patients with LTB underwent chest CT examination, with 36 (73.47%) showing positive findings. The predominant patterns observed in 22 cases included infiltrative patchy shadows, tuberculous cavities, and chronic fibrocavitary lesions. Four cases presented as hematogenously disseminated tuberculosis. Stable or inactive pulmonary tuberculosis, characterized by well-defined calcified tuberculomas or long-standing linear or nodular opacities, was identified in 5 cases. Four patients had other concurrent pulmonary lesions, while 13 showed no abnormalities on chest CT (Fig. 4).

Laboratory and pathological examination results

The PPD test was positive (including positive and strongly positive reactions) in 27 cases, yielding a positivity rate of 55.10%. Sputum smear for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) was performed in 36 cases, with 13 (38.88%) returning positive results. A total of 32 patients (65.30%) underwent laryngeal biopsy. The typical histopathological features observed were the formation of epithelioid cell granulomas accompanied by varying degrees of caseous necrosis, surrounded by lymphocytes and Langhans giant cells. In biopsies from ulcerative lesions, the predominant feature was caseous necrosis with reduced granuloma formation. Conversely, biopsies from exophytic lesions primarily revealed epithelioid granulomas with or without distinct caseous necrotic foci (Fig. 5).

Treatment and prognosis

All diagnosed patients were referred to specialized tuberculosis hospitals for standardized anti-tuberculosis treatment. Follow-up was completed for 43 patients (6 were lost to follow-up). Treatment consisted of systemic medication and, in some cases, nebulized anti-tuberculosis therapy. 20 patients underwent repeat laryngoscopy 3–6 months post-treatment. Among these, mucosal edema resolved or significantly improved in all 10 cases of the inflammatory-infiltrative type. Ulcers healed in 5 out of 6 cases of the ulcerative-necrotic type, with one case showing granulation tissue hyperplasia. Significant reduction in granulation tissue was observed in cases of the proliferative and mixed types.

Symptomatic improvement began between 3 days and 7 months after initiating anti-tuberculosis therapy. Post-treatment laryngoscopy in 43 patients showed marked improvement in laryngopharyngeal mucosa, with the morphology returning to essentially normal in 24 cases (24/43, 59.4%) or presenting as scar-like in 16 cases (16/43, 34.4%). Local scar adhesion was observed in only 3 patients (3/43, 6.98%), located at the anterior commissure and supraglottis, respectively. Discussion

With the rising incidence of tuberculosis in both some developing and developed nations, the clinical presentation of LTB may differ from earlier descriptions. This necessitates increased vigilance among otorhinolaryngologists for emerging, atypical manifestations and mandates its inclusion in the differential diagnosis of laryngeal diseases [14, 15]. This retrospective analysis of 49 patients with LTB over a 10-year period in northern China revealed a median age of 48 years and a male predominance, aligning with previously reported shifting trends [16, 17]. Analysis of predisposing risk factors and comorbidities confirmed that, alongside well-established factors such as immunodeficiency, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, and immunosuppressant use [2, 18], chronic alcohol intoxication and tobacco abuse were also among the most common predisposing factors [16]. Furthermore, the interval from symptom onset to definitive diagnosis ranged from months to years, with a median of 3.9 months. Most patients sought medical attention multiple times before diagnosis, underscoring the non-specific nature of the clinical presentation and its propensity to be overlooked.

Historically, LTB often presented with odynophagia and systemic symptoms like weight loss and night sweats. In our cohort, however, the most common clinical feature was still hoarseness, a non-specific symptom. Only a small portion of patients experienced varying degrees of pharyngalgia, while non-specific systemic symptoms such as fever and night sweats were present in merely 12.24% of cases. This pattern is consistent with recent trends of increasing atypical presentations [8, 16, 17]. The evolution in clinical manifestations may be linked to the irregular long-term use of anti-tuberculosis drugs or the emergence of drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis [18–21].

The symptoms of LTB are indistinguishable from any other condition causing hoarseness, rendering diagnosis based solely on clinical features challenging. Therefore, laryngoscopy is crucial for delineating the lesion’s location, size, and morphological characteristics [15]. In our endoscopic evaluation, the mixed-type was the most common morphological finding (38.78%), a feature rarely detailed in prior series. This polymorphic nature–where inflammatory, ulcerative, and proliferative changes coexist-is a key contributor to diagnostic difficulty, as it mimics malignancy, nonspecific granulomatous disease, or fungal infections. The anatomical distribution of LTB has shifted from the traditionally described posterior laryngeal predominance towards a more diffuse pattern. Notably, the true vocal fold was the most frequently involved single site, and nearly two–thirds of our patients presented with lesions involving two or more laryngeal subsites, particularly the glottis and supraglottis. This suggests a potential change in the route of infection or host immune response, making the endoscopic appearance less ‘classic’ and more easily confused with widespread inflammatory or neoplastic processes [22, 23]. Furthermore, in a study by Ling et al., videostroboscopy in 19 LTB patients revealed that markedly reduced mucosal waves and absent vocal fold vibrations were the most common findings, aiding in narrowing the differential diagnosis [15].

Regarding imaging, laryngeal CT typically showed local thickening or inflammatory changes, but was often non-specific for etiology, with some cases raising suspicion for space-occupying lesions or malignancy. The respiratory tract remains the most common portal of entry for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Literature reports that 34.4%–97.0% of LTB patients have concomitant pulmonary tuberculosis. In our study, 73.46% patients had positive findings on chest CT, with 8 showing typical pulmonary tuberculosis manifestations. Chest CT demonstrated high detection rates for cavities and bronchial lesions, supporting its recommendation as a routine screening tool [24].

Similarly, when LTB is suspected, tuberculosis-specific laboratory tests are essential to strengthen the differential diagnosis and facilitate earlier confirmation. Commonly used methods include the PPD test and sputum microbiological examination. the positive rates for these were 55.1% and 38.88%, respectively. Our study quantifies the diagnostic dilemma posed by the near-absence of systemic symptoms coupled with the low yield of traditional tests. This combination of atypical presentation and insensitive conventional diagnostics underscores a paradigm shift: in contemporary northern China, LTB can no longer be reliably ruled out based on the absence of pulmonary symptoms or negative tuberculin tests. The interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) shows a higher sensitivity (approximately 80% in active TB) compared to the PPD test (53%), but both have relatively low specificity and cannot distinguish active from latent infection [8, 25]. IGRA has also demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity over the PPD test in diagnosing LTB [26]; however, this test was not routinely performed in our study. Microbiological detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis remains the diagnostic gold standard and indicates active disease, but it does not localize the specific respiratory site of infection and has a low yield, with sputum smear positivity around only 20% [4]. Therefore, the key takeaway for clinicians is that laryngoscopic recognition of mixed, multi-site lesions in patients with risk factors, followed by low-threshold biopsy for histopathology and molecular testing, is not merely an option but a necessity [10]. The pathological spectrum of tuberculosis includes exudative, proliferative, and necrotic changes. Due to its ulceroproliferative morphology and non-specific appearance, it is easily mistaken for malignancy, and such diagnostic challenges have been described in the literature [27]. Histopathological examination is indispensable for definitive diagnosis, with epithelioid cell granulomas accompanied by caseous necrosis being the hallmark. Necrotizing granulomas are present in approximately 80% of tuberculosis cases, while AFB are identified by staining in 27–60% of cases [28]. In our study, all 32patients who underwent biopsy showed granulomatous inflammation with or without necrosis. The AFB stain positivity rate was 38.88%, which remains relatively low overall. Furthermore, studies report that employing molecular detection techniques, such as PCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA, on biopsy tissue offers high sensitivity and specificity, providing a basis for the early diagnosis and management of LTB [29].

Standardized, adequate, and complete anti-tuberculosis treatment can cure the majority of patients [30]. Hoarseness often improves within weeks of treatment initiation, and laryngeal lesions gradually heal over several months. Only a minority of patients experience persistent hoarseness due to fibrosis, scar adhesion, or vocal fold fixation [16, 31, 32]. However, studies suggest that voice therapy can help improve voice quality following anti-tuberculosis treatment [33]. Severe laryngeal defects or scarring may lead to permanent functional impairment, and surgical intervention may be required in cases of significant airway obstruction. The recommended anti-tuberculosis regimen lasts at least 6 months, extending to 12 months (with a 2-month intensive phase) for patients with concomitant pulmonary tuberculosis [34]. In our cohort, patients with pulmonary comorbidity showed a good therapeutic response. Treatment duration ranged from 6 to 24 months. Symptom improvement occurred between 3 days and 7 months, with 92.9% of patients experiencing improved hoarseness. Post-treatment laryngeal mucosa appeared essentially normal or showed scar-like changes, with only several cases developing local adhesions.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and given that the included patients primarily presented with laryngeal symptoms, selection bias may have been introduced. Second, reliance on medical records meant that some tuberculosis-related factors could not be comprehensively assessed, including adequate evaluation of comorbidities in patients with LTB. Additionally, issues such as a high loss-to-follow-up rate and incomplete retrospective laboratory data were present. Future multi-center, large-sample cohort studies are warranted to obtain more comprehensive conclusions.

Conclusions

Diagnosing LTB is often challenging due to its clinical resemblance to other laryngeal pathologies. Otorhinolaryngologists should consistently consider LTB in the differential diagnosis, particularly for patients with high-risk factors for tuberculosis who present with polymorphic lesions involving multiple laryngeal sites on laryngoscopy. Re-evaluating and thoroughly understanding the shifting trends in the clinical presentation of LTB are crucial for achieving early identification and accurate diagnosis of this disease.
