Combination Versus Monotherapy in Acute Anti‐LGI1 Encephalitis: A Real‐World Dose Optimization Analysis
Authors: Yao Tan, Ming Sun, Qinqin Liu, Beibei Huang, Chao Wu, Jie Lu, Donglin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnti‐leucine‐rich‐glioma‐inactivated 1 encephalitis (LGI1e) predominantly affects older adults, who frequently present with chronic comorbidities. Given their heightened susceptibility to adverse effects, optimizing immunotherapy is critical for this high‐risk population. Therefore, this study compared the efficacy and safety of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) at 500 or 1000 mg/day, and combined IVIg with IVMP to define an optimal efficacy‐safety balanced regimen for LGI1e.MethodsA total of 131 LGI1e patients were comprehensively analyzed. The efficacy was assessed through the therapeutic response rate, response latency, and the improvement in the Clinical Assessment Scale for Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE), the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), and faciobrachial dystonic seizure (FBDS), while safety was evaluated by analyzing the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs).ResultsCompared with IVIg or IVMP monotherapy, the IVIg+IVMP combination achieved higher response rates, shorter latency, and greater improvements in CASE, mRS, and FBDS. While 1000 mg IVMP reduced response latency and FBDS cessation time versus 500 mg, both doses showed comparable response rates. IVMP‐containing regimens increased AEs, especially at 1000 mg. Diabetes was the sole independent AE risk factor during acute immunotherapies. Initial regimens had no long‐term outcome association, and cognitive impairment persisted as a major disability contributor.ConclusionFor LGI1e patients, combined IVIg and corticosteroid demonstrate superior efficacy to monotherapy as first‐line treatment, with a starting IVMP dose of 500 mg (vs. 1000 mg) considered optimal. Furthermore, post‐immunotherapy cognitive impairment requires sustained management. These findings necessitate validation in larger randomized trials.

Introduction

Anti‐leucine‐rich‐glioma‐inactivated 1 encephalitis (LGI1e) is the second most common autoimmune encephalitis after anti‐N‐Methyl‐d‐Aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis (NMDARe), characterized by faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS), memory impairment, and neuropsychiatric symptoms [1, 2]. Early immunomodulatory therapy is crucial [3, 4, 5], with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and corticosteroids—primarily intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP)—constituting common first‐line therapeutic interventions [1, 5]. Although combined IVIg+IVMP may benefit autoimmune encephalitis prognosis [5, 6, 7], LGI1e shows distinct features requiring specific strategies. Firstly, LGI1e predominantly affects middle‐aged and elderly individuals, who often have preexisting chronic comorbidities—such as hypertension and diabetes—thereby raising immunotherapy safety concerns [8]. Secondly, LGI1e patients exhibit milder initial modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores and lower mortality than NMDARe [9], questioning high‐dose immunotherapy necessity. Thirdly, while combination therapy is recommended for NMDARe [6], corticosteroid monotherapy shows efficacy in LGI1e [5], prompting cost–benefit debates about IVIg. However, treatment selection often relies on clinician experience due to limited comparative studies, while personalized medicine principles (e.g., age, comorbidities, and cost) further complicate regimen choices [10, 11]. Although the administration of IVMP at a dose of 1000 mg/day for 3 consecutive days is widely regarded as the standard regimen for acute‐phase treatment in autoimmune diseases [6, 12], emerging evidence suggests that a lower dosage regimen—500 mg/day typically for 5 consecutive days—may also demonstrate therapeutic efficacy [13, 14, 15, 16]. Moreover, recent emphasis on corticosteroid dose minimization [17] highlights the need for optimized acute‐phase dosing. Given this context, the dose‐dependent effects of high‐dose IVMP in the acute phase require urgent evaluation in LGI1e—specifically comparing 500 mg versus 1000 mg initial doses—as their distinct efficacy and safety profiles remain undefined.

To identify the optimal regimen for LGI1e, this real‐world study compared efficacy and safety among: (1) IVIg, IVMP, and IVIg+IVMP; (2) 500 mg versus 1000 mg IVMP starting doses (IVMP500, IVMP1000, IVIg+IVMP500, IVIg+IVMP1000). Efficacy was assessed through: immunotherapy response (rate/latency), symptom changes (Clinical Assessment Scale for Autoimmune Encephalitis, CASE), cognitive function (Mini‐Mental State Examination, MMSE), FBDS frequency/cessation time, and neurological recovery (mRS). Safety was assessed by monitoring the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs) across all study cohorts.

Methods

Subjects

This prospective‐retrospective cohort study, conducted from June 2016 to June 2024, systematically evaluated patients with confirmed LGI1e through monthly comprehensive assessments utilizing electronic medical record reviews, telephone follow‐ups, and outpatient consultations. LGI1e diagnosis followed the Graus criteria for autoimmune encephalitis [18]. Patients were included if meeting all criteria: (1) presence of anti‐LGI1 antibodies in serum and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), confirmed by cell‐based assay with tissue‐based assay verification when necessary; (2) clinical onset within the preceding 1 month and manifestations consistent with encephalitis; (3) availability of comprehensive clinical data; (4) receipt of a defined initial treatment regimen of IVMP, IVIg, or their combination (IVIg+IVMP). Exclusion criteria included: (1) positive antibody status without evident central nervous system involvement or limited to peripheral neuropathy; (2) co‐occurrence of other anti‐neuronal antibodies or active systemic autoimmune diseases that could obscure the etiology of encephalitic symptoms; (3) confirmation of tumor; (4) incomplete clinical data records; (5) unconfirmed cessation of the assigned treatment, defined as discontinuation without medically documented justification (e.g., transfer to another facility with unavailable records, voluntary withdrawal without explanation), excluding interruptions with verified causes such as AEs or lack of efficacy. Totally 131 patients were included this study, and their clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Chinese version of the MMSE (maximum score 30), with cutoff points set at 17, 20, and 24 for patients with no formal education, primary school education, and middle or high school education, respectively, in accordance with previous studies [19, 20]. To ensure that MMSE scores reflected neuroinflammation‐related impairment rather than the reversible effects of hyponatremia, assessments were timed to avoid this confounder. At baseline, any presenting hyponatremia was corrected prior to cognitive testing. Crucially, as there was a median interval of 8 days from symptom onset to immunotherapy initiation (Table 1), all patients had achieved normonatremia well before the pre‐treatment baseline MMSE was performed. During long‐term follow‐up, MMSE was administered only after confirming normal serum sodium levels at the time of the visit.

Antibody Detection

LGI1 antibodies and other autoimmune encephalitis‐related antibodies (anti‐NMDAR, anti‐contactin‐associated protein‐like 2, anti‐alpha‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid receptor 1/2, and anti‐gamma aminobutyric acid receptor) were detected using transfected cell–based immunofluorescence assays. Based on clinical presentation, paraneoplastic antibodies (including anti‐Hu, anti‐Ri, anti‐Yo, anti‐CV2, anti‐amphiphysin, anti‐Sox1, anti‐Zic4, anti‐glutamic acid decarboxylase 65, and Purkinje cell cytoplasmic antibody type 2) along with anti‐myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies were assessed. All positive findings were confirmed by orthogonal cell‐based assays; tissue‐based immunohistochemistry was employed for supplementary verification where clinically indicated. Next‐generation sequencing of CSF pathogens was performed when needed.

Treatment Regimens

In this study, all participants or their legally authorized representatives received comprehensive counseling regarding the available immunotherapeutic options prior to providing written informed consent. These options included IVIg monotherapy (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days), IVMP monotherapy at two dosage levels (500 mg/day for 5 days or 1000 mg/day for 3 days), and combination therapy (IVIg+IVMP). Treatment regimens were not randomly assigned but were determined by treating physicians based on comprehensive clinical assessments, including disease severity at presentation, specific comorbidities (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes precluding high‐dose steroids), patient/family preferences, and economic considerations. This resulted in 31 patients receiving IVIg alone, 49 receiving IVMP alone (comprising 23 at 500 mg/day and 26 at 1000 mg/day), and 51 receiving combination therapy (22 with IVIg +500 mg IVMP and 29 with IVIg +1000 mg IVMP), as illustrated in Figure 1.

Patients who were classified as non‐responders to the initial immunotherapies at the 1‐month efficacy assessment became eligible for salvage therapy. The salvage interventions available per protocol included therapeutic plasma exchange, IVIg re‐administration, intravenous cyclophosphamide, or rituximab. It is noteworthy that in the actual execution of the study, only IVIg re‐administration and rituximab were utilized (Figure 1). The IVIg regimen for salvage was consistent with the initial therapy at 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 consecutive days [6]. The total dose of rituximab administered was 600 mg, delivered as 100 mg intravenously on day 1 followed by 500 mg on day 2 [21]. Following the initial intravenous phase, all patients who received IVMP, either as monotherapy or in combination with IVIg, commenced an oral prednisone taper with a starting dose of 1 mg/kg/day, capped at 60 mg/day for patients weighing over 60 kg. The tapering schedule employed 5 mg decrements every 2 weeks, targeting complete discontinuation by week 24 (6 months from initiation), with no intended long‐term maintenance dose; specifically, the taper was structured to reach 5 mg/day by week 22, followed by full cessation at week 24. For patients experiencing moderate AEs during the taper, the protocol allowed for accelerated discontinuation: the dose was reduced to 10 mg/day for a maximum of 4 weeks, then further reduced to 5 mg/day for 2 weeks, with subsequent complete discontinuation. All patients adhered to the 6‐month oral regimen, with prednisone systematically discontinued at the study endpoint, and AEs were assessed and documented at this final 6‐month time point.

Throughout the immunotherapy course, all patients received routine prophylactic measures including omeprazole (40 mg/day) and calcium carbonate (1 tablet/day) to mitigate corticosteroid‐related AEs. Additionally, vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, and body temperature) and key laboratory parameters (blood glucose, hepatic/renal function, electrolytes, and complete blood counts) were dynamically monitored. Where clinically indicated, supplemental assessments such as electrocardiography, gastrointestinal endoscopy, or chest computerized tomography were performed.

All patients, including those who required salvage therapies, remained in their originally assigned treatment groups during the 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month follow‐up assessments. This strategy maintained cohort integrity for longitudinal comparisons and reflected real‐world clinical outcomes following initial therapeutic intervention.

Evaluation of Immunotherapies

Following intravenous immunotherapies, patients who received IVIg monotherapy were managed solely with symptomatic treatment (e.g., antiepileptic drugs), while those who underwent IVMP or combination therapy were transitioned to a 6‐month oral prednisone taper. Clinical records were thoroughly reviewed to assess symptoms, diagnostic evaluations, and clinical outcomes.

Assessment of Treatment Efficacy

Efficacy outcomes were evaluated at predetermined time points spanning the acute and long‐term phases: baseline (prior to treatment initiation), 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and at the final follow‐up. This structured timeline facilitated a distinct assessment of the initial immunotherapeutic response and the subsequent clinical trajectory. The 1‐month post‐treatment assessment served as the primary endpoint for quantifying the acute‐phase treatment response, a timeframe validated by prior interventional studies [5, 22]. Treatment response during this critical phase was specifically characterized by two principal metrics. The treatment response rate was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a clinically meaningful improvement, specified as a reduction of at least 1 point on the mRS within the first month. The treatment response latency, representing a core acute efficacy indicator, was defined as the time interval (in days) from treatment initiation until the first observed and sustained improvement of ≥ 1 point on the mRS. These definitions were adapted from established methodologies in autoimmune encephalitis research [6]. Additionally, comprehensive clinical profiles included CASE scores and FBDS analysis when present. For FBDS, the seizure frequency was recorded daily by patients or their caregivers using standardized seizure diaries. No wearable devices were used for detection. The seizure burden in each month of follow‐up during the first 4 months post‐treatment was categorized based on the highest daily frequency observed in that month, using predefined brackets (0, < 10, 10–19, 20–29, ≥ 30 episodes/day). The representative FBDS frequency for each month was defined as the category with the highest proportion of days; when multiple categories shared the same maximum proportion, the highest frequency bracket was selected. A day with no recorded FBDS episode was defined as a seizure‐free day. The proportion of patients within each frequency category was then calculated. Moreover, the time to persistent cessation—defined as the number of days from treatment initiation to the last recorded FBDS attack with no subsequent recurrence—was documented.

Subsequent clinical course and treatment durability were monitored at 3‐month, 6‐month, 12‐month, and final follow‐up visits. Evaluations at these time points focused on the stability and/or continued improvement in functional status (mRS) and disease‐specific severity (CASE). Relapse was defined as the emergence of new or worsening clinical features after at least 2 months of stability or improvement, as previously described [23]. To ensure rigorous and consistent application of these outcome definitions throughout the data collection process, any ambiguities or discrepancies were resolved through a consensus review by the principal investigators.

Assessment of Safety

AEs associated with steroid and IVIg therapies were assessed based on previously published literature [24, 25, 26]. A comprehensive and predefined list of treatment‐emergent AEs was established, cataloged by bodily systems including infusion site reactions, neuropsychiatric symptoms, cutaneous manifestations, musculoskeletal complaints, gastrointestinal disturbances, cardiovascular effects, respiratory symptoms, urinary tract issues, hematological changes, and others, as detailed in Table S1. This framework was developed following robust methodologies outlined in prior comparable studies [6, 27]. Two independent physicians, blinded to the treatment allocation groups, performed a retrospective review of all electronic medical records to identify and grade each event. The grading adhered to a validated three‐tier classification system [6, 28]: (1) Mild AEs: Events that were transient, well‐tolerated, and required no intervention or modification to the immunotherapeutic regimen; examples include elevated blood pressure or hyperglycemia that resolved and returned to a normal range within 48 h after administration of appropriate antihypertensive or hypoglycemic agents. (2) Moderate AEs: Events requiring active medical management or adjustments to the treatment plan. For example, elevated blood pressure or hyperglycemia was classified as moderate if it persisted or exhibited significant fluctuations beyond 48 h despite standard therapeutic interventions (e.g., initiation or dose titration of antihypertensive or hypoglycemic agents), with such management complexity necessitating a protocol‐defined slowing of the oral steroid tapering schedule (e.g., from biweekly 5 mg reductions to weekly 5 mg reductions). (3) Severe AEs: Events leading to permanent discontinuation of the assigned immunotherapy, or resulting in permanent disability, life‐threatening complications, or death. This category also included events that precipitated serious complications, such as gastrointestinal bleeding, new‐onset stroke, or angina pectoris, directly attributable to the study treatment. For specific AEs, further objective criteria were applied. The severity of headaches, for example, was quantified using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), with scores of 1–4 categorized as mild, 5–6 as moderate, and 7–10 as severe, ensuring a standardized and reproducible assessment across the patient cohort [29]. To guarantee rigor and consistency, any discrepancy in the identification or grading of an AE between the two independent reviewers was escalated for resolution through a consensus discussion involving the principal investigators.

Statistical Analysis

All clinical data were systematically recorded and managed in a dedicated Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) database. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 16.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), while data visualization was conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.4 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A rigorous, multi‐step analytical approach was employed. First, the normality of distribution for all continuous variables was formally assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on this assessment and the nature of the data, group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using either the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons between two independent groups or the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across three or more groups, with Dunn's post hoc test applied where significant differences were detected. For categorical variables, the χ 2 test was used when expected frequencies exceeded 5; otherwise, Fisher's exact test was applied. For significant categorical associations identified in multi‐group comparisons, Bonferroni‐adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed to identify specific group differences. For the analysis of longitudinal measures, such as the sequential mRS and CASE scores obtained at multiple follow‐up time points, the Friedman test was employed, followed by Nemenyi post hoc analysis for detailed temporal comparisons. Logistic regression analysis followed a hierarchical strategy: an initial screening of variables was performed using likelihood ratio tests (α = 0.05), which was then followed by univariate analysis and forward stepwise multivariate analysis (with an entry threshold of p < 0.05 and a removal threshold of p > 0.10). Throughout the analysis, a p‐value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Co‐Existing Chronic Comorbidities Significantly Influence Treatment Modality Selection

As illustrated in Table 1, the median age of the 131 patients diagnosed with LGI1e was 69 years, with an age range spanning from 49 to 87 years. Additionally, the male‐to‐female ratio was recorded at approximately 3:2. Among all the recorded symptoms, cognitive impairment was the most prevalent, affecting 71.0% of the patients. Characteristic FBDS was observed in 61.1% of cases, followed by hyponatremia (45.3%), psychobehavioral abnormalities (32.8%), epileptic seizures (20.6%), sleep disorder (15.3%), and decreased consciousness (9.2%). At diagnosis, comorbidities were present in 113 patients (86.3%), with the spectrum including hypertension (36.7%), diabetes mellitus (35.1%), cerebral infarction (29.0%), coronary artery disease (13.0%), chronic pulmonary disease (encompassing chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or bronchial asthma) (9.2%), gastrointestinal ulcers (3.8%), and recent fractures (2.3%). Statistical analysis revealed significant associations between comorbidity profiles and treatment selection in LGI1e patients. When stratified by comorbidity burden (0, 1, or ≥ 2 comorbidities), treatment groups showed distinct distribution patterns (Pearson χ 2 (4) = 46.852, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.423, 95% CI [0.313, 0.533]; Figure 2a). The IVIg group was characterized by a high prevalence of patients with multiple comorbidities (≥ 2, 87.1% [27/31]). In contrast, the majority of patients in the IVMP group also presented with ≥ 2 comorbidities (55.1% [27/49]), whereas the IVIg+IVMP group consisted mainly of patients with a single comorbidity (70.6% [36/51]). Further subgroup analyses showed IVMP500 had most ≥ 2 comorbidity cases (17/23, 73.9%) versus balanced distribution in IVMP1000 (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 11.960, p = 0.003, Cramer's V = 0.435, 95% CI [0.183, 0.687]; Figure 2b). Similarly, IVIg+IVMP500 had most single‐comorbidity cases (20/22, 90.9%) compared to half in IVIg+IVMP1000 (16/29, 55.2%, Pearson χ 2 (2) = 12.638, p = 0.002, Cramer's V = 0.428, 95% CI [0.180, 0.676]; Figure 2b). Specific comorbidities were significantly associated with treatment allocation (Table 2). Patients with diabetes were more frequently assigned to IVIg or IVMP (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 32.118, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.495, 95% CI [0.346, 0.644]). Those with coronary artery disease were more commonly treated with IVMP or IVIg+IVMP (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 6.683, p = 0.035, Cramer's V = 0.226, 95% CI [0.059, 0.393]). Gastrointestinal ulcers were exclusively documented in patients receiving IVIg (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 16.769, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.358, 95% CI [0.198, 0.518]), as were fractures (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 9.904, p = 0.007, Cramer's V = 0.275, 95% CI [0.110, 0.440]). In contrast, chronic pulmonary disease was exclusively observed in the IVMP group (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 22.107, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.411, 95% CI [0.255, 0.567]). At the subgroup level, patients with diabetes or chronic pulmonary disease were predominantly administered IVMP500 (diabetes: Pearson χ 2 (1) = 17.307, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.594, 95% CI [0.369, 0.819]; chronic pulmonary disease: Pearson χ 2 (1) = 17.965, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.605, 95% CI [0.382, 0.828]). However, no significant associations were found between the three main treatment groups and either hypertension (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 1.290, p = 0.525, Cramer's V = 0.099, 95% CI [0.000, 0.269]) or cerebral infarction (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 1.963, p = 0.375, Cramer's V = 0.122, 95% CI [0.000, 0.292]).

Acute IVMP Initial Dose Is Unrelated to Response Rate but Is Linked to Response Latency

As illustrated in Figure 3a, 1 month of treatment with IVMP alone or in combination with IVIg showed no significant differences in CASE score changes across dose subgroups (IVMP500 vs. IVMP1000: Mann–Whitney U = 221.000, p = 0.822, r = −0.034, 95% CI [−0.333, 0.265]; IVIg+IVMP500 vs. IVIg+IVMP1000: Mann–Whitney U = 195.500, p = 0.362, r = −0.128, 95% CI [−0.402, 0.147]). Regarding functional outcomes, the IVMP500 and IVMP1000 monotherapy groups demonstrated comparable efficacy, with 11/23 (47.8%) and 12/26 (46.2%) patients respectively achieving mRS score improvements of at least 1 point (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 0.014, p = 0.907, Cramer's V = 0.017, 95% CI [0.000, 0.297]; Figure 4b). In the IVMP500 group, 12 patients (52.2%) required salvage therapy (IVIg retreatment: 11; rituximab: 1; Figure 1) for inadequate initial response, compared with 14 patients (53.8%) in the IVMP1000 group (IVIg retreatment: 13; rituximab: 1; Figure 1). Consistently, no significant differences were observed in overall mRS score changes between these dose subgroups (Mann–Whitney U = 215.000, p = 0.687, r = −0.062, 95% CI [−0.356, 0.243]; Figure 4c). However, the IVMP1000 regimen showed a distinct advantage in treatment response latency, with a mean latency of 15.1 days compared to 23.1 days for IVMP500 (Mann–Whitney U = 8.000, p < 0.001, r = −0.748, 95% CI [−1.000, −0.339]; Figure 4d).

Commonly reported AEs included injection site pain (predominantly observed in IVIg recipients), headache, sleep disturbances, electrolyte imbalances, and hyperglycemia. Combined Immunotherapy Yields Greater Improvements in CASE, mRS, and FBDS Scores

At the 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month assessments, all surviving patients were evaluated within initially assigned treatment groups. At the 3‐ and 6‐month follow‐ups, the IVIg group included 31 patients (no discontinuations), the IVMP group included 43 patients (of 49 assigned; 6 discontinued due to serious AEs), and the IVIg+IVMP group included 43 patients (of 51 assigned; 8 discontinued due to serious AEs). By 12 months, cohort sizes decreased: IVIg group to 28 patients (3 lost to follow‐up), IVMP group to 39 patients (4 lost to follow‐up), and IVIg+IVMP group to 38 patients (5 lost to follow‐up). Patients completing IVMP‐based therapy subsequently continued oral prednisone tapering per protocol.

The analysis of CASE scores across follow‐up time points revealed distinct temporal patterns among the treatment groups. At the 3‐month assessment, significant intergroup differences were observed (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 53.653, p < 0.001, ε 2 = 0.453, 95% CI [0.255, 0.350]). While the median CASE scores in both the IVIg and IVMP monotherapy groups had decreased to 6 (p = 1.000 between them), the IVIg+IVMP combination group demonstrated a significantly lower median score of 4 (p < 0.001 vs. IVMP). This pattern of improvement with combination therapy persisted at the 6‐month follow‐up (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 31.623, p < 0.001, ε 2 = 0.260, 95% CI [0.146, 0.278]). The median score was 4 in both IVIg and IVMP monotherapy groups (between‐group p = 1.000), compared to 3 in the IVIg+IVMP group (p < 0.001 vs. IVMP). By the 12‐month mark, the treatment response had further evolved (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 38.399, p < 0.001, ε 2 = 0.357, 95% CI [0.192, 0.344]). The IVMP monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP combination groups both achieved a median CASE score of 2, indicating no significant difference between them at this late stage (p = 1.000). In contrast, the IVIg monotherapy group exhibited a less favorable outcome, with its median score remaining at 3, which was significantly higher than that in the IVMP group (p < 0.001).

Following the analysis of CASE scores, the evaluation of mRS scores across the 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month follow‐ups revealed a distinct and similarly divergent pattern among the treatment groups. At the 3‐month mark, significant differences in mRS scores were observed (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 57.481, p < 0.001, ε 2 = 0.487, 95% CI [0.255, 0.391]). The median mRS score was 3 in both the IVIg and IVMP monotherapy groups (p = 1.000), whereas the IVIg+IVMP combination group achieved a significantly lower median score of 2 (p < 0.001 vs. IVMP). By the 6‐month follow‐up, a significant intergroup disparity persisted (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 49.060, p < 0.001, ε 2 = 0.413, 95% CI [0.230, 0.357]). The median mRS score decreased to 2 in both the IVMP and IVIg+IVMP groups (p = 1.000), in contrast to the IVIg monotherapy group, where the median score remained at 3 (p < 0.001 vs. IVMP). At the 12‐month assessment, the previously noted differences in mRS scores were no longer statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 2.934, p = 0.231, ε 2 = 0.009, 95% CI [0.000, 0.039]), with all treatment groups converging to a median score of 2.

Longitudinal analysis of FBDS frequency patterns demonstrated significant therapeutic effects across all treatment groups (Figure 6c). At baseline, high‐frequency episodes (> 30 episodes/day) were observed in 52.6% (10/19) of patients receiving IVIg, 41.4% (12/29) of those receiving IVMP, and 48.4% (15/31) of those receiving combination therapy with IVIg+IVMP. All groups showed progressive reductions in FBDS frequency over time (IVIg: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 69.840, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.919, 95% CI [0.777, 1.000]; IVMP: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 111.240, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.959, 95% CI [0.844, 1.000]; IVIg+IVMP: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 109.445, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.855, 95% CI [0.745, 0.965]). The combination therapy group showed faster symptom control, with 47% achieving FBDS freedom by month 1 versus 0% in monotherapy groups. This finding was supported by median time‐to‐seizure‐cessation data (Figure 5d): the IVIg+IVMP group had a median time of 42.0 days, significantly shorter than IVMP monotherapy (62.0 days; Mann–Whitney U = 67.000, p < 0.001, r = 0.731, 95% CI [0.584, 0.830]) and IVIg monotherapy (78.0 days; Mann–Whitney U = 18.500, p < 0.001, r = 0.780, 95% CI [0.641, 0.870]).

The analysis of FBDS revealed a rapid and significant reduction in frequency across all dose‐based subgroups following immunotherapy (IVMP500: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 52.944, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.882, 95% CI [0.722, 1.000]; IVMP1000: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 53.032, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.947, 95% CI [0.781, 1.000]; IVIg+IVMP500: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 40.209, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.773, 95% CI [0.601, 0.945]; IVIg+IVMP1000: Friedman χ 2 (4) = 67.717, p < 0.001, Kendall's W = 0.891, 95% CI [0.749, 1.000]; Figures 7f and 8e). However, significant differences were observed in the time to seizure cessation. Specifically, the median time to FBDS cessation was significantly longer in the IVMP500 subgroup at 70.0 days compared to 56.0 days in the IVMP1000 subgroup (Mann–Whitney U = 14.500, p < 0.001, r = −0.735, 95% CI [−1.000, −0.371]; Figure 8g). A similar pattern was evident within the combination therapy groups, where the median time was 51.0 days for the IVIg+IVMP500 subgroup, which was significantly longer than the 38.0 days observed in the IVIg+IVMP1000 subgroup (Mann–Whitney U = 55.000, p = 0.003, r = −0.439, 95% CI [−0.786, −0.093]; Figure 8g).

A dose‐dependent safety profile was observed during corticosteroid tapering and maintenance (Figure 8h; Table 4). The IVMP500 group showed significantly lower AE incidence (34.8%, 8/23 patients with mild AEs) compared to IVMP1000 (90.0%, 18/20 patients; 46 AEs including 10 moderate events; Pearson χ 2 (1) = 13.640, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.563, 95% CI [0.316, 0.810]; Figure 8h). This dose‐dependent pattern was consistent in combination groups: IVIg+IVMP500 had AEs in 45.5% (10/22) of patients versus 81.0% (17/21) for IVIg+IVMP1000 (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 5.795, p = 0.016, Cramer's V = 0.367, 95% CI [0.086, 0.648]; Figure 8h). AE severity and type distribution did not differ between dose subgroups (all p > 0.05, Table 4), indicating higher acute‐phase doses primarily increased AE frequency without altering severity profiles.

Comorbid Diabetes Is an Independent Risk Factor for AEs During Acute Immunotherapies

Patients with LGI1e were stratified into groups with and without AEs based on acute immunotherapy outcomes. Comparative analysis (Table 5) revealed the group with AEs had significantly higher proportions of older age at onset (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 11.426, p = 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.295, 95% CI [0.130, 0.444]), females (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 10.004, p = 0.002, Cramer's V = 0.276, 95% CI [0.110, 0.427]), cognitive impairment (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 59.160, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.672, 95% CI [0.545, 0.799]), diabetes comorbidity (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 14.455, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.332, 95% CI [0.170, 0.476]), and IVMP‐containing regimens (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 8.507, p = 0.004, Cramer's V = 0.216, 95% CI [0.087, 0.409]), but lower proportions of IVIg‐containing regimens (Pearson χ 2 (1) = 34.409, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.513, 95% CI [0.374, 0.629]). To identify risk factors associated with AEs during acute phase, candidate variables—including baseline characteristics, pre‐existing comorbidities, and therapeutic regimens—were first screened using the likelihood ratio test, with a model inclusion threshold set at p < 0.05. Variables meeting this statistical criterion were subsequently advanced to univariate logistic regression analysis for further assessment. Notably, acute clinical manifestations of LGI1e, such as cognitive impairment and hyponatremia, were excluded from this analysis, as they represent sequelae of the disease itself rather than plausible etiological factors for treatment‐emergent AEs. This identified age ≥ 75 years, female sex, diabetes, and IVMP‐containing regimens as AE risk factors (Table 6). Forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression ultimately identified comorbid diabetes as the only independent predictor significantly associated with AEs (adjusted odds ratio = 6.271, 95% CI [2.253–17.450], p < 0.001).

Initial Treatment Regimens Do Not Alter Long‐Term Outcomes

Of the 96 patients who completed final follow‐up (IVIg cohort: n = 22; IVMP cohort: n = 38; IVIg+IVMP cohort: n = 36), the median follow‐up duration was 38 months (range: 14–90 months). Four patients (4.2%) experienced disease relapse during follow‐up. The entire cohort demonstrated median scores of 2 on the mRS and 2 on the CASE. Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences among the treatment cohorts in relapse rates (Fisher–Freeman–Halton test: p = 0.886, Cramer's V = 0.056, 95% CI [−0.098, 0.019]; Figure 9a), CASE scores (Kruskal‐Wallis H (2) = 0.305, p = 0.859, ε 2 = −0.022, 95% CI [−0.098, −0.019]; Figure 9b), mRS scores (Kruskal–Wallis H (2) = 2.570, p = 0.277, ε 2 = 0.006, 95% CI [−0.073, 0.006]; Figure 9c), or proportions of patients with cognitive impairment (Pearson χ 2 (2) = 0.137, p = 0.934, Cramer's V = 0.038, 95% CI [0.000, 0.206]; Figure 9d). Notably, approximately 80% of patients across all three cohorts exhibited cognitive impairment.

Discussion

The present study represents the first comprehensive comparison of the efficacy and safety of three immunomodulatory regimens as well as varying initial doses of IVMP in treating LGI1e. Several significant findings emerged from this study: (1) The comorbidity burden significantly influences the formulation of initial treatment strategies for patients; (2) In the absence of contraindications to corticosteroids, early initiation of combined therapy with IVIg+IVMP demonstrates superior efficacy compared to monotherapy with either IVIg or IVMP for short‐term improvement. This is evidenced by an enhanced treatment response, reduced response latency, shortened duration of FBDS, and no significant increase in AEs; (3) Higher‐dose IVMP treatment was associated with a significantly shorter response latency and faster FBDS cessation, but concurrently resulted in an increased incidence of AEs; (4) Acute treatment with IVIg+IVMP was associated with greater improvements in both CASE and mRS scores across all follow‐up periods; (5) Initial immunotherapies did not affect the incidence of AEs during the maintenance phase; (6) Comorbid diabetes constitutes an independent risk factor for AEs in patients undergoing acute immunotherapies; (7) Divergent initial therapeutic approaches did not dictate long‐term outcomes for patients.

While the demographic and clinical profile of our cohort was consistent with typical LGI1e populations described in the literature [1, 8, 23, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34], this study provides, to our knowledge, the first systematic quantification of comorbidity burden and its impact on initial immunotherapy selection. We found that 86.3% of patients had at least one chronic medical condition, with hypertension and diabetes being the most prevalent. These comorbidities directly influenced therapeutic strategies: IVIg or IVMP monotherapy was preferentially used in patients with multiple comorbidities, whereas IVIg+IVMP combination therapy was reserved for those with fewer comorbidities. A higher comorbidity burden also correlated with the selection of lower IVMP doses (500 mg) in both monotherapy and combination regimens. Additionally, condition‐specific associations (e.g., a history of gastrointestinal ulcers favoring IVIg) suggest contraindication‐driven choices. These findings underscore the imperative to incorporate comorbidity assessment into personalized therapeutic planning for LGI1e, particularly in this predominantly middle‐aged and elderly population where such conditions are common.

Acute immunotherapy is pivotal for both short‐ and long‐term prognoses in LGI1e [33]. Notably, while existing literature consistently reports treatment initiation delays of several weeks from symptom onset [23, 33, 34], our cohort demonstrated a significantly reduced median interval of 8 days. This discrepancy likely stems from our inclusion criteria limiting enrollment to acutely presenting patients (≤ 1 month from onset), thereby excluding subacute or insidious‐onset cases, coupled with heightened clinician awareness facilitating prompt autoimmune encephalitis screening. Although plasma exchange remains a first‐line option, IVMP, IVIg, and their combination constitute the predominant initial therapies, evidenced by a recent systematic review of 431 LGI1e patients revealing the following treatment distribution [1]: IVIg monotherapy (15.8%), corticosteroid monotherapy (40.3%), combination therapy (41.5%), and plasma exchange (6.3%, primarily as rescue therapy). Indirect evidence suggests therapeutic efficacy differences—corticosteroids demonstrate superior acute symptomatic improvement in FBDS and neurological scores compared to IVIg [5], whereas IVIg exhibits distinct advantages in reducing seizure frequency among steroid‐refractory patients [35]. Although IVMP+IVIg combination therapy has been recommended for acute‐phase management [36], direct comparisons with monotherapies remain limited. Prior observational outcomes, including rapid cognitive/behavioral improvements within 3–5 days post‐treatment [33] and high remission rates (corticosteroids: 93.02%; IVIg: 87.50%; combination: 96.67%) [1], are constrained by methodological heterogeneity in assessment timelines and endpoints. To address these limitations, our study implemented standardized dynamic metrics: treatment response rates and latency periods (adapted from NMDARe protocols [6]) for acute efficacy evaluation, supplemented by serial mRS and CASE assessments for longitudinal monitoring across acute, maintenance, and long‐term phases. Direct comparisons confirmed the superiority of IVIg+IVMP therapy across acute and maintenance phases, significantly improving response rates, shortening response latency and FBDS duration, and accelerating sustained improvements in CASE and mRS scores. This corroborates findings from other autoimmune conditions, where synergistic mechanisms operate: IVIg modulates immunity through antibody neutralization, complement inhibition, and Fc receptor regulation, while corticosteroids primarily suppress inflammatory cascades and immune cell activation [37, 38, 39, 40]. Notably, while our data demonstrated comparable efficacy in CASE scores between IVIg and IVMP monotherapy with oral prednisolone at both the 3‐month and 6‐month assessments, the 12‐month follow‐up revealed a significant advantage for IVMP in this metric (median 2 vs. 3 for IVIg, p < 0.001). This divergence may reflect the impact of prolonged oral tapering in the IVMP group, potentially extending immunotherapy duration and contributing to improved long‐term outcomes. These findings suggest that although IVIg monotherapy achieves therapeutic effects similar to those of IVMP monotherapy in the short term (within 6 months), additional maintenance therapies—such as azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil—may be necessary to optimize long‐term prognosis, particularly in cases where oral corticosteroid use is restricted or contraindicated. Collectively, our results position IVIg+IVMP combination therapy as the preferred frontline regimen for LGI1e, optimizing recovery trajectories probably through complementary immunomodulatory pathways.

While IVIg (0.4 g/kg/day) and IVMP (1000 mg/day) constitute widely accepted standard immunotherapies for autoimmune encephalitis including LGI1e [6], real‐world constraints—particularly comorbidities like diabetes that heighten safety concerns regarding high‐dose corticosteroids—motivate exploration of lower‐dose alternatives. An early report documented favorable outcomes with approximately 500 mg/day IVMP in LGI1e, though formal comparisons with 1000 mg regimens were absent [33]. Our study addresses this gap through direct analysis of IVMP500‐containing regimens (monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP500) versus IVMP1000‐based therapies (monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP1000). Notably, while statistically significant differences emerged in response latency and FBDS duration, no clinically meaningful variations were observed in acute‐phase response rates or longitudinal functional outcomes. Specifically, mRS and CASE scores demonstrated comparable trajectories across all assessment intervals (months 1, 3, 6, and 12). These findings confirm therapeutic equivalence between 500 mg × 5‐day and 1000 mg × 3‐day IVMP protocols for both short‐ and long‐term neurological recovery in LGI1e, validating the utility of dose‐reduced regimens for patients with corticosteroid‐associated contraindications.

Safety constitutes a paramount consideration in immunotherapeutic regimen selection, particularly for LGI1e patients—typically elderly with significant comorbidity burdens. While IVIg and IVMP demonstrate generally favorable safety profiles in other autoimmune encephalitis [6, 41], our analysis reveals critical regimen‐specific distinctions: IVMP monotherapy was associated with higher rates of AEs compared to IVIg monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP combination therapy—a pattern corroborated in an NMDARe research [6]. Moreover, in the present study, the total cumulative IVMP dose was largely equivalent between groups (2500 mg vs. 3000 mg), yet dose‐dependent effects were evident: shorter high‐dose regimens (1000 mg IVMP over 3 days, encompassing IVMP1000 monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP1000 combination) showed increased AE incidence compared to prolonged regimens (500 mg IVMP over 5 days, encompassing IVMP500 monotherapy and IVIg+IVMP500 combination), consistent with corticosteroid toxicity being driven by peak exposure intensity and duration rather than total cumulative dose [42, 43]. AE subtype analysis further revealed distinct patterns: IVIg monotherapy primarily caused injection‐site pain (as reported [6]), IVMP monotherapy showed elevated sleep disturbances versus combination therapy, and IVIg+IVMP was associated with increased electrolyte imbalances. Mechanistically, corticosteroid‐induced sleep alterations may involve hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis modulation, circadian rhythm disruption, and neuroinhibitory pathway hyperarousal [33, 44], whereas IVIg's neutral or beneficial sleep effects remain undefined despite its overall symptom efficacy [1]. Electrolyte disturbances in combination therapy likely reflect compounded physiological impacts, as both IVIg and IVMP independently disrupt homeostasis [26, 45]. Tapering oral prednisone over 6 months post‐IVMP or IVIg+IVMP was linked to manageable mild‐to‐moderate AEs like weight gain and electrolyte imbalances, consistent with known corticosteroid safety profiles [46]. A multicenter NMDARe study confirmed AE incidence correlates with tapering duration, especially beyond 3 months [47]. Furthermore, several studies suggest that certain adverse effects associated with prolonged corticosteroid therapy—such as osteoporosis, hypertension, and impaired glucose tolerance—are frequently linked to the patient's physiological condition and the activity of the underlying disease rather than being solely attributable to the medication itself [48, 49]. Through effective monitoring and management, these reactions are generally manageable, allowing patients to persist in their treatment regimen aimed at controlling the progression of their target diseases [48, 49]. However, it should be noted that the discussion of further treatment strategies here is limited to repeat IVIg and rituximab, as other remedial treatment strategies such as azathioprine add‐on or plasma exchange were not evaluated in our protocol. Importantly, no long‐term maintenance dose exceeding the Cushing threshold (e.g., 10 mg/day) was administered in the present study. A dosage of 10 mg/day was used only transiently and for a duration not exceeding 4 weeks, specifically under certain conditions requiring accelerated tapering due to moderate AEs. Although this study demonstrated that a carefully titrated, gradual corticosteroid tapering regimen was sufficient to mitigate adverse reactions—as evidenced by the absence of serious AEs—it did not exclude the potential utility of corticosteroid‐sparing agents (e.g., azathioprine, repeated IVIg, mycophenolate mofetil, or rituximab). While the study protocol centered exclusively on corticosteroid tapering, clinical judgment warrants early consideration of adjunctive immunomodulatory agents—such as azathioprine—to reduce cumulative corticosteroid exposure and associated long‐term risks.

Crucially, our multivariate analysis identified diabetes—a prevalent comorbidity in LGI1e cohorts reflecting its high population baseline [50]—as an independent risk factor for AEs. This association may be biologically plausible through several integrated pathways in diabetic patients: pre‐existing diabetic neuropathy (peripheral or trigeminal) may lower the threshold for injection‐site pain and headaches during immunotherapy [51]; nephropathy‐induced electrolyte dysregulation could exacerbate treatment‐related imbalances [52]; dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and neurotransmitter systems might mediate disorders of sleep and agitation [53]; and underlying vasculopathy could contribute to gastrointestinal bleeding and hypertension [54]. Consequently, diabetes should be recognized as more than a coincidental condition; it is a significant, independent risk marker that warrants preemptive consideration during treatment planning to optimize safety in personalized LGI1e management.

Cognitive impairment manifests not only as a cardinal presenting symptom in LGI1e but also frequently persists as a major contributor to long‐term disability, with studies indicating incomplete cognitive recovery despite immunotherapeutic interventions [33, 55, 56]. Our longitudinal follow‐up corroborated this observation, demonstrating that variations in initial treatment strategies did not significantly influence cognitive outcomes, as most patients continued to exhibit persistent deficits. These persistent cognitive impairments have been neurobiologically linked to hippocampal pathology, particularly volumetric atrophy and microstructural integrity loss [55], suggesting that hippocampal damage may serve as the neuroanatomical substrate for residual cognitive dysfunction in LGI1e survivors. Additionally, while cognitive sequelae represent irreversible neurological outcomes, disease recurrence constitutes a modifiable factor influencing long‐term prognosis. Contrasting prior descriptions of LGI1e as a frequently relapsing disorder [2], our cohort exhibited a substantially lower recurrence rate (4.2%) than literature benchmarks (9.3%–27.0%) [1, 33, 34, 56, 57]. This divergence likely stems from methodological variations in follow‐up duration, patient demographics, and therapeutic protocols. Crucially, our inclusion criterion—restricting enrollment to patients within 1 month of onset—combined with a median treatment delay of only 8 days, indirectly substantiates the therapeutic window hypothesis wherein early immunotherapy optimizes long‐term outcomes including relapse reduction. Furthermore, the systematic deployment of rituximab salvage therapy for initial non‐responders, which demonstrates established efficacy in relapse reduction [23, 58], underscores the value of proactive therapeutic algorithms in modifying LGI1e disease trajectories.

However, key limitations warrant acknowledgment. The observed benefits of IVIg+IVMP require cautious extrapolation to corticosteroid‐contraindicated populations, particularly given diabetes‐associated AE risks and unresolved pathophysiological interactions. Methodological constraints include potential confounding from prolonged corticosteroid tapering in IVMP‐containing regimens—unlike IVIg monotherapy lacking maintenance phases—which may introduce bias in efficacy comparisons, particularly regarding the observed mRS advantage with IVMP. The moderate cohort size (n = 131) limits comprehensive subgroup analyses, while cognitive assessments relying on MMSE may underdetect hippocampal‐mediated deficits despite clinical utility, compounded by absent longitudinal neuroimaging for atrophy quantification. Finally, the reduced relapse rate (4.2%) may reflect protocol‐specific factors including early intervention, rituximab salvage, or differential treatment durations rather than natural disease history.

Building upon our findings of long‐term cognitive sequelae, future research must ‌integrate neuroprotective strategies alongside immunomodulation.‌ Addressing persistent cognitive impairment may require dual approaches: (1) Augmenting immunotherapy with hippocampal protectants (e.g., Brain‐derived Neurotrophic Factor enhancers) targeting atrophy mechanisms; (2) Developing comorbidity‐adapted protocols—such as corticosteroid‐sparing regimens using rituximab for diabetics—to mitigate exacerbating factors. Validating neuroimaging biomarkers (e.g., quantitative hippocampal volumetry) and domain‐specific cognitive batteries will facilitate precision interventions during therapeutic windows. Multicenter collaborations should focus on integrated trials combining these strategies to transform cognitive outcomes.

In conclusion, combined IVIg and corticosteroid therapy is recommended as first‐line treatment for LGI1e, demonstrating superior efficacy over monotherapies. Importantly, IVMP at 1000 mg—whether administered as monotherapy or in combination—showed no significant efficacy advantage over 500 mg dosing in acute or long‐term outcomes but increased the risk of AEs, particularly serious events. Therefore, IVIg combined with IVMP 500 mg constitutes the optimal regimen, especially for elderly patients with chronic comorbidities, requiring strict AE monitoring during infusion and comprehensive comorbidity screening (with emphasis on diabetes). These findings warrant validation through multicenter randomized controlled trials to optimize clinical protocols.
