A rare case of upper airway obstruction due to laryngeal leech infestation in a 1-year-old child
Authors: Tsion Haile Woldemariam, Fitsum Alemayehu Siyum, Robel Tibebu Kasaye, Mahlet Minwuyelet Dagne, Michael Akalu Geleta, Mahlet Tesfaye Abebe, Kibruyisfaw Weldeab Abore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeech infestation of the upper airway is a rare but potentially life-threatening event, particularly in young children from rural areas exposed to untreated water sources. We report a case of a 1-year-old boy presenting with acute upper airway obstruction due to laryngeal leech attachment. Diagnosis was confirmed by direct laryngoscopy, and the leech was successfully removed under general anesthesia. The patient had a full recovery following the intervention. This report highlights the diagnostic challenges and management considerations in pediatric airway leech infestation and underscores the importance of public education regarding the use of safe water.Keywords: Leech, Upper airway obstruction, Pediatrics, Infestation, Ethiopia

Introduction

Leech infestation of the upper airway, though extremely rare, poses a severe and acute threat to pediatric patients, especially in rural and resource-limited areas where untreated water is used for drinking or recreation [[1], [2], [3]]. Leeches are blood-sucking parasitic annelids found in natural freshwater sources such as ponds, streams, and wells, and can inadvertently be introduced into the human body through consumption or exposure to contaminated water [4,5]. The prevalence of such infestations remains low globally but may be underreported in endemic regions, with most documented cases arising in tropical and subtropical countries [6,7].

Children are particularly at risk due to their increased susceptibility to environmental exposure and thinner, more reactive airways, which heighten the danger of rapid airway compromise [1]. Reported cases highlight a spectrum of symptoms depending on the size and location of the leech, ranging from mild throat discomfort and hemoptysis to severe stridor, respiratory distress, and even fatal airway obstruction [5,6,8,9] The larynx and proximal trachea represent especially ominous sites of attachment, given the risk for complete occlusion and hypoxic injury [2,3,7].

Although the first reports of leech-related airway emergencies date back decades, awareness among clinicians remains limited due to rarity and nonspecific early symptoms [3]. This underscores the importance of considering leech infestation in the differential diagnosis of acute airway obstruction, especially in children from rural backgrounds with a history of recent freshwater exposure [[1], [2], [3]].

A review of the literature shows that timely diagnosis and intervention, often via direct laryngoscopy, are vital for patient survival and full recovery. Challenges in pediatric airway management, risk of parasite migration, and the need for gentle extraction further complicate treatment [[6], [7], [8]] This case report aims to contribute to current knowledge by detailing the presentation and successful management of laryngeal leech infestation in a 1-year-old boy, and to reinforce the importance of education on water safety and preventive public health measures in at-risk communities.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 1-year-old boy from a rural community presented to the emergency department with a 24-h history of blood-stained saliva, increasing restlessness, and progressive respiratory distress. There was a history of recent drinking from an untreated pond. On examination, his vital signs were: temperature, 36.8 °C; heart rate, 154 bpm; respiratory rate, 48/min; oxygen saturation, 91 % on room air. Stridor and suprasternal retractions were noted, along with mild cyanosis and audible gurgling on inspiration. Auscultation revealed reduced air entry over both lungs without wheezing.

Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 8.8 g/dL, indicating mild anemia; a white blood cell count of 8.6 × 109/L with neutrophils at 60 % and lymphocytes at 29.2 %, reflecting a normal differential; and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) elevated at 28 mm/hr, suggesting an inflammatory process.

Given the acute onset and severity of symptoms, the differential diagnosis included foreign body aspiration, infectious epiglottitis, and parasitic infestation. After adequate pre-oxygenation, patient was put on high flow intranasal oxygen. Subsequently, the patient was given ketamine, propofol and succinylcholine. Urgent direct laryngoscopy was performed with intermittent apnea, relaxed vocal cords, and open glottis, while carefully monitoring the airway and prepared for advanced airway management.

On laryngoscopy, a blackish, mobile leech was visualized firmly attached to the proximal trachea, just below the vocal cords (Video 1). Gentle traction with blunt forceps was employed to avoid rupturing the parasite or causing it to migrate deeper. Minor oozing was encountered but controlled with suction. No adjunct imaging was performed prior to the procedure due to imminent airway compromise.

The leech was successfully extracted whole, measuring 2cm in length (Fig. 1). The airway remained patent post-removal, and the child was observed in the pediatric ICU for 24 hours. He was discharged on day 3, with his caregivers counseled about avoiding untreated water and practicing safe drinking habits.

Discussion

Leech infestation of the upper airway, though rare, is a medical emergency posing diagnostic challenges due to nonspecific symptoms and rapid risk of respiratory compromise [[6], [7], [8]]. Pediatric patients are especially vulnerable given their smaller airway anatomy and greater exposure to contaminated natural water sources in rural or resource-limited settings [1,2]. Most reported cases occur in tropical and subtropical regions where untreated freshwater consumption is common, though underreporting is likely due to limited awareness and diagnostic difficulty [10].

In this case, the classic triad of hematemesis or blood-tinged saliva, unexplained anemia, and acute or progressive respiratory distress was evident, consistent with previous pediatric aerodigestive tract infestations [1,5,10]. The child's hemoglobin of 8.8 g/dL likely resulted from ongoing local blood loss exacerbated by leech saliva anticoagulants like hirudin [11]. The elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate in this patient reflects a nonspecific inflammatory response secondary to local tissue irritation from the parasite. Differential diagnoses for acute upper airway symptoms in children in endemic areas include foreign body aspiration, infections like epiglottitis or croup, and rare parasitic infestations. Blood-stained saliva and anemia can help differentiate leech infestation, but clinical suspicion should remain high when freshwater exposure is reported [[1], [2], [3]].

Definitive diagnosis depends on direct visualization via laryngoscopy, the gold standard especially when airway patency is compromised. Imaging is generally reserved for stable cases as it may delay urgent intervention [7,8]. Pediatric airway management for laryngoscopy requires careful preparation and expertise to mitigate risks during induction. Facilities must have appropriate pediatric equipment, and teams should be ready for advanced airway maneuvers. Gentle, controlled extraction is critical; fragmentation or rough manipulation can cause bleeding, airway edema, aspiration, or leech migration, worsening obstruction or complicating removal [7]. Post-removal, patients require monitoring for airway edema, secondary infections, and anemia correction as needed [1,7].

Communities reliant on untreated water should be provided health education about the risks of consuming or swimming in natural water bodies harboring leeches and other parasites. While this report describes a single case and thus may not capture the full clinical spectrum, it adds valuable insight to the limited literature on pediatric laryngeal leech infestations. Raising clinician awareness, especially in endemic regions, is essential for prompt diagnosis, timely airway management, and improved outcomes.

Conclusion

Laryngeal leech infestation is a rare but serious cause of airway obstruction in children exposed to untreated water. Early suspicion and prompt removal via direct laryngoscopy are crucial for recovery. Effective management requires skilled teams and highlights the need for public health measures promoting safe water and community education. Increased awareness and case reporting will improve prevention and treatment of this uncommon condition.
