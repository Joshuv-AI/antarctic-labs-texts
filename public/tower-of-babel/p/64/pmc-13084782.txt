Efficacy and Safety of preoperative Controlling Heart Rate in Patients with Acute Type A Aortic Dissection (ESCORT): protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Guangyu Liu, De Wang, Yanmin Yang, Tao Zhang, Baodong Xie, Jia Hu, Xiang Wei, Jun Chen, Su Yuan, Cuntao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency with a high mortality rate, and acute Stanford Type A AD is the most dangerous form, often requiring surgical intervention. Factors contributing to the progression of AD include increased heart rate, blood pressure and the rate of ventricular contraction (dP/dt). While heart rate control is a cornerstone of preoperative management for AD per existing guidelines, the recommended target (eg, ≤60 bpm in American Heart Association 2010) is consensus-driven (Level C) and thus not grounded in high-level evidence. The ESCORT (Efficacy and Safety of preoperative Controlling heart Rate in patients with acute Type A aortic dissection) trial is designed to address this critical gap by providing high-level evidence from a randomised controlled trial to define the optimal preoperative heart rate target.Methods and analysisThe study is a multicentre, single-blind, RCT involving adult patients diagnosed with acute (Stanford Type A) AD, scheduled for aortic arch prosthetic vascular replacement and elephant trunk stent implantation surgery. 680 patients will be randomised into two groups: low heart rate target group (55–65 beats per minute) and standard heart rate target group (75–85 beats per minute). The intervention involves protocol-directed medication to achieve the target heart rates, with esmolol as the primary recommended agent. The primary outcome measure is the rate of major adverse cardiovascular event within 30 days after surgery, and secondary outcomes include various clinical, economic and biochemical measures.DiscussionThe ESCORT study is the large-scale RCT to investigate the optimal preoperative heart rate control in patients with acute type A AD. The results of this study have the potential to fill the evidence gap in current clinical guidelines and provide evidence-based support for clinical management. The findings may influence the standard of care by either endorsing lower heart rate targets or providing alternative guidance for managing heart rate in this high-risk patient population. The study’s results will be disseminated through publications and presentations at both national and international conferences, ensuring that the results are accessible to the medical community and relevant patient organisations.Ethics and disseminationThis study, including the study protocol (version 1.3, 3 December 2022), was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, CAMS and PUMC (approval number: 2022-1886). Additional approvals were obtained from the ethics committees of participating subcentres (approval numbers are listed in the main text). The findings will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at scientific conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2300067811.

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) is a lethal cardiovascular emergency with a high mortality rate.13 The annual incidence of AD is estimated to be between 3 and 16 cases per 100 000 people worldwide.47 A higher frequency of hospital admissions for AD has been observed in the cold season, indicating a seasonal variation in its incidence.8 9 A study found that the annual incidence of AD among middle-aged adults in China is 2.35 per 100 000 person-years, with the rate among men being significantly higher at 3.97.10 Based on this rate and China’s population, the estimated number of new annual cases in this demographic is approximately 8000 to 10 000 people.10 The true incidence of acute type A AD is almost certainly underestimated for two key reasons: first, the substantial number of patients (21%–37%) who die before reaching a hospital,11 and second, studies indicate that nearly 40% of cases are initially misdiagnosed on arrival,12 13 leading to underascertainment. The diagnostic rate of AD has shown a clear upward trend, attributable to its growing clinical recognition and continual refinements in imaging techniques.14 AD can be classified using several systems, including the Stanford, DeBakey and Fuwai classifications.1517 The Stanford system, which categorises dissections based on the involvement of the ascending aorta, is the most prevalent in current clinical use. Acute Stanford Type A AD (ATAAD) is a critical condition with a high mortality rate, estimated at 1%–2% per hour during the initial phase following symptom onset, and it typically necessitates surgical intervention.18 This understanding of the high mortality and time-sensitive nature of ATAAD is largely derived from registries like the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection,19 which highlight the crucial window for early intervention. It is within this critical preoperative phase that heart rate control represents a modifiable yet unoptimised factor.

The progression and outcomes of AD are profoundly influenced by haemodynamic forces. Elevated heart rate, blood pressure and the rate of ventricular pressure rise (dP/dt) collectively increase shear stress on the aortic wall and aortic wall tension, thereby potentiating the propagation of the false lumen, extension of the dissection and the risk of catastrophic complications such as rupture or organ malperfusion.14 Beta-blockers form the pharmacological cornerstone of preoperative management, primarily by attenuating these forces.20 Their negative chronotropic and inotropic effects reduce heart rate and myocardial contractility, which in turn lowers blood pressure (afterload) and myocardial oxygen consumption.21 Esmolol, a short-acting, cardioselective beta-1 antagonist, is particularly suited for this unstable clinical scenario due to its rapid onset of action, short half-life (approximately 9 min), titratability and metabolism via erythrocyte esterases, which is independent of renal or hepatic function.20 Consequently, it is recommended as a first-line agent in guidelines and expert consensuses for acute aortic syndromes.22 However, a critical gap persists regarding the optimal intensity of heart rate control. The 2010 American Heart Association guidelines,23 for instance, recommend a target heart rate of ≤60 beats per minute (class I recommendation), but explicitly note that this is based on level C evidence, derived from consensus opinion rather than prospective clinical studies.24 The foundational review cited by these guidelines itself provided no primary clinical research evidence to support this specific target. Other contemporary reviews25 or guidelines22 have suggested a more lenient target of 60–80 beats per minute, further highlighting the prevailing uncertainty. Supporting this pathophysiological rationale, our team’s previous research26 demonstrated a significant inverse relationship between preoperative heart rate and clinical outcomes, wherein patients with higher heart rates experienced significantly poorer postoperative courses. Compounding this evidence gap, real-world clinical practice, particularly in China, often falls short of these consensus recommendations due to insufficient awareness and the lack of standardised protocols. This results in a significant proportion of patients not achieving any form of stringent rate control preoperatively.

To solve these problems, we designed the ESCORT trial, in which patients with acute type A AD will be randomised to different preoperative heart-rate targets so that the impact on outcome can be rigorously compared. This large-scale, multicentre, randomised controlled trial aims to establish the optimal preoperative heart-rate target for these patients and to provide high-level evidence for future guidelines.

Methods and analysis

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting of this research. This is due to the acute, life-threatening nature of acute type A AD, which necessitates rapid intervention and limits feasibility for such involvement during the protocol development phase. However, we are committed to disseminating the main findings of this trial to the participating patients and the wider patient community through lay-language summaries on completion of the study.

Trial Oversight An independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC), independent from the sponsor and with no competing interests, will periodically review unblinded accumulating trial data for safety and efficacy. The Trial Steering Committee (TSC) provides overall trial supervision. A blinded Clinical Endpoint Adjudication Committee will independently verify all primary and key secondary outcome events. Day-to-day coordination and data management are handled by the coordinating centre at Fuwai Hospital.

Study setting

The study is a multicentre, single-blind, randomised controlled trial conducted across eight major cardiovascular centres in China. Participating centres include: Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (Beijing); Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan); West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Chengdu); Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Guangzhou); Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai); The Second Hospital of Shandong University (Jinan); The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin); and The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Urumqi). Ethical approval for the remaining four centres (Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Guangzhou; Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, Shanghai; The Second Hospital of Shandong University, Jinan; and The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, Urumqi) is pending and will be obtained prior to participant enrolment at those sites.

Site and investigator eligibility

Eligible clinical sites must be high-volume cardiovascular centres in China performing ≥50 open aortic arch procedures annually for acute type A AD, supported by a dedicated 24/7 multidisciplinary aortic team and institutional capacity for protocol-directed administration of intravenous beta-blockers and vasoactive agents in critical care settings. The heart rate control intervention must be delivered or directly supervised by physicians specialised in cardiovascular anaesthesia, critical care or cardiology, all of whom must be trained and approved for this study protocol.

Study aims

To study whether heart rate control can reduce the incidence of adverse outcomes in the perioperative period for patients with ATAAD. By comparing the prognosis of different heart rate states in acute type A AD, we aim to explore the optimal target heart rate for these patients. To establish a standardised protocol for heart rate control in patients with acute type A AD, and to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of this protocol, providing guidance for clinical practice.

Intervention and control

After the patient’s visit, the selection for inclusion will begin, and after agreeing to participate and signing the informed consent form, 1:1 random grouping will be conducted. After grouping, the intervention will begin until the patient enters the operating room for anaesthesia or withdraws from the study. The intervention measures for different groups are as follows:

Low heart rate target group: The target heart rate is controlled between 55 and 65 beats per minute using medication. It is recommended to first use esmolol to control the heart rate, generally at a dosage of 0.15 mg/kg/min. After administration, heart rate monitoring and recording are performed, with a plan to reach the target heart rate in about 3 hours. The dosage is adjusted hourly according to the patient’s heart rate. If the heart rate is still not reached after using 0.3 mg/kg/min, it is recommended to increase the use of diltiazem and other medications to control the heart rate. If the heart rate significantly decreases, the clinical situation should be analysed for the cause, and the dosage of the heart rate-lowering medication should be reduced accordingly. Different research centres can choose the appropriate medication according to their own circumstances.

Standard heart rate target group: The target heart rate is controlled between 75 and 85 beats per minute using medication. It is recommended to first use esmolol to control the heart rate, generally at a dosage of 0.1 mg/kg/min. After administration, heart rate monitoring and recording are performed, with a plan to reach the target heart rate in about 3 hours. The dosage is adjusted hourly according to the patient’s heart rate. If the heart rate is still not reached after using 0.3 mg/kg/min, it is recommended to increase the use of diltiazem and other medications to control the heart rate. If the heart rate is below 75 beats per minute, the clinical situation should be analysed for the cause, and the dosage of the heart rate-lowering medication should be reduced accordingly. Different research centres can choose the appropriate medication according to their own circumstances.

Safety management measures: After the intervention, medication is given, the patient is visited every hour, and the patient’s blood pressure and heart rate are recorded. When the patient’s blood pressure drops to more than 30% below the baseline or below 80/50 mmHg, it is suggested that the clinician handle it according to the situation, which may include adjusting the dosage/speed of vasoactive drugs. The drugs that can be used include dopamine, norepinephrine and epinephrine, etc. To account for potential blood pressure discrepancies caused by the dissection, blood pressure will be measured in both arms at the time of enrolment, and the arm with the higher reading will be used for all subsequent hourly measurements during the preoperative period. If the blood pressure is more than 20% above the baseline or higher than 140/90 mmHg, it is suggested that the clinician handle it according to the situation, which may include adjusting the dosage/speed of vasoactive drugs. The drugs that can be used include sodium nitroprusside, urapidil and nitroglycerin, etc.

It is recommended that the research centre control the intervention time, that is, from the start of medication to the time of entering the operating room, within 3–24 hours. After entering the operating room, anaesthesia is administered according to the clinical routine, and the relevant surgery is performed.

Adherence monitoring

To ensure adherence to the intervention protocol, all site investigators and relevant intensive care unit (ICU) staff will receive standardised training. Research nurses will hourly document heart rate and drug dosage. Protocol adherence will be reviewed centrally by the Data Management Team, and feedback will be provided to sites periodically.

Harms

Definition

An adverse event (AE) is defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant administered a study intervention, which does not necessarily have a causal relationship with this treatment. A serious AE (SAE) is any AE that results in death, is life-threatening, requires inpatient hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation, results in persistent or significant disability/incapacity, or is a congenital anomaly/birth defect.

Assessment and collection

All AEs and SAEs will be systematically collected from the time of randomisation until the 30-day postoperative follow-up. Investigators will actively solicit and document AEs at each patient contact and will also record any spontaneously reported events. All AEs will be assessed for severity and relationship to the study intervention by the site investigator.

Reporting

All SAEs must be reported to the principal investigator and the independent DMC within 24 hours of awareness. The DMC will periodically review accumulating safety data, including all SAEs and specific AEs of interest (eg, severe bradycardia, profound hypotension, bronchospasm).

Sample size

Using PASS V.11.0.8 for calculation, based on reference literature and previous studies, the incidence rate of MACE in patients with acute type A AD is about 48%. Assuming that the low heart rate control group can reduce the incidence rate to 35%, with a type II error rate (β) of 0.1, and a power of 0.90, the O’Brien Fleming method29 is used to calculate the testing level, with one interim analysis planned, and a final statistical analysis α level of 0.048. The required sample size per group is 304 cases, and considering a dropout rate of 10%, 338 subjects per group are needed, totalling 676 subjects. For convenience in grouping, the sample size is rounded up, with 340 subjects per group, totalling 680 subjects.

Interim analysis and stopping guidelines

One interim analysis for efficacy and safety is planned when 50% of participants complete the 30-day follow-up, using the O'Brien Fleming method with a statistical significance level of α=0.005. The analysis will be performed by an independent statistician, and the results will remain strictly confidential and accessible only to the DMC to prevent bias. The DMC may recommend early termination for overwhelming efficacy, futility or safety concerns, with the final decision resting with the TSC after considering the DMC’s recommendation.

Recruitment

To ensure timely recruitment of the 680 target participants, all participating aortic centres will implement a coordinated strategy. This includes continuous 24/7 screening of emergency departments and inpatient referrals for immediate identification of eligible patients, supported by dedicated research personnel at each site responsible for patient approach and consent. A streamlined consent process tailored to this time-sensitive condition will clearly present the study’s potential benefits. The principal coordinating centre (Fuwai Hospital) will monitor recruitment rates monthly and implement corrective actions through the steering committee if needed. This target is considered feasible given the collective annual case volume across the eight centres far exceeds the required sample size.

Assignment of intervention, randomisation and blinding

Randomisation

After qualification confirmation and the signing of informed consent forms, participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the low heart rate target group or the standard heart rate target group. The random allocation sequence will be generated electronically by the central randomisation system using a computer-generated pseudo-random number algorithm. The sequence generation will be performed by an independent statistician who is not involved in participant recruitment or enrolment. A stratified, block randomisation method will be used. Randomisation will be stratified by the participating clinical centre to ensure balance between the two intervention groups within each centre. Randomly permuted block sizes will be used to help maintain allocation concealment.

Allocation concealment

The allocation sequence will be concealed within the central data and allocation website. Physicians and researchers will enrol participants and will only be able to view the assigned group through the website after entering the participant’s baseline data, thus ensuring concealment.

Method of blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, it is not possible to blind participants, physicians, researchers and others. However, the outcome assessment for participants will be conducted by a researcher who is unaware of the group assignments.

The blinding of the outcome assessor will be achieved by ensuring that all data related to the preoperative heart rate control phase (including drug dosages and hourly heart rate records) are stored in a separate database. The assessor will only have access to the postoperative clinical course and outcome data from the follow-up period, which will be stripped of any information indicating the assigned intervention group.

Unblinding procedure

A formal unblinding procedure is not applicable to this trial. Since the treating clinicians are already aware of the group assignment during the acute phase, unblinding the outcome assessor would not provide any additional information to guide the emergency management of a participant. The integrity of the blinded outcome assessment is therefore maintained throughout the trial without exception.

Data management

The study will use a combination of paper and electronic case report forms (CRFs) for data collection. Researchers will record data promptly, completely and accurately in paper CRFs based on original observations. These data will then be transcribed into an electronic data capture (EDC) system by trained site personnel. To ensure data quality, a double data entry procedure will be implemented for all key variables, with discrepancies resolved by referring to source documents. The EDC system will be programmed with automatic range and logic checks to identify implausible or inconsistent values at the point of entry, and queries will be automatically generated and sent to sites for resolution. AEs will be coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, and concomitant medications will be coded using the WHO’s Drug Dictionary.

Electronic data will be stored on secure, password-protected servers with role-based access control, encrypted during transmission and backed up regularly. Paper CRFs will be stored in locked cabinets with restricted access at each investigational site. All data will be retained for a minimum of 15 years after trial completion, in accordance with regulatory requirements. The final trial dataset will be under the custodianship of the principal investigator at the coordinating centre (Fuwai Hospital). Monitors will oversee trial conduct and CRF completion, and all data management processes will be supervised by the clinical research ethics committee. Detailed data management plans and standard operating procedures (SOPs) are maintained by the coordinating centre and available on request.

Data collection methods

Data will be collected using a combination of paper and electronic CRFs. The CRFs have been designed specifically for this trial to capture all variables outlined in the ‘Outcomes’ section. (1) All research personnel involved in data collection (eg, research nurses, outcome assessors) will undergo centralised training on the protocol, SOPs and CRF completion to ensure consistency across all sites. (2) Monitors will perform source data verification for a proportion of CRF entries against original medical records to ensure accuracy. (3) All laboratory tests (cTnI/hs-cTnI, NT-proBNP) will be performed according to each participating centre’s standardised clinical laboratory protocols. (4) Clinical outcomes (eg, stroke, myocardial infarction) will be defined using internationally accepted diagnostic criteria (eg, Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 definitions where applicable) to ensure consistency in adjudication. Additionally, from the hourly haemodynamic recordings obtained during the preoperative intervention period, the peak heart rate and peak systolic blood pressure will be extracted for each participant as exploratory endpoints.

The CRF templates are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Participant retention and follow-up

Given the acute nature of the condition and the short-term primary endpoint (30 days), participant retention is anticipated to be high. The following strategies will be employed to maximise complete follow-up for secondary outcomes up to 1 year: (1) At enrolment, multiple contact details (eg, participant, next of kin) will be collected to facilitate long-term follow-up; (2) Designated research personnel will be responsible for ensuring accurate follow-up assessments at the predefined timepoints (1, 3, 6 and 12 months); (3) We are committed to an intention-to-treat analysis and will make every reasonable effort—including telephone contact and queries of national household registration systems—to ascertain at least the vital status (all-cause mortality) at key follow-up timepoints for all randomised participants, including those who discontinue the intervention or are lost to follow-up. Confidentiality

The confidentiality of all potential and enrolled participants will be strictly protected throughout the trial and thereafter. On enrolment, each participant will be assigned a unique study identification (ID) number. All data collected for the study, including those in paper CRFs and the electronic database, will be identified only by this study ID to ensure that personally identifiable information (eg, name, identity card number, contact details) is kept separate from the research data. Access to the list linking study IDs to personal information will be restricted to authorised research personnel at the respective site and will be stored securely separately. All electronic data will be stored on secure, password-protected servers with encrypted transmissions, and paper records will be kept in locked cabinets. During the sharing of data between sites and with the coordinating centre or monitors, only the deidentified dataset will be used. After trial completion, all records will be archived securely for the required retention period in accordance with regulatory requirements.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are represented by summary statistics (mean, SD, median, IQR, or minimum and maximum values), and qualitative variables are represented by frequency counts.

The effectiveness analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle and per-protocol (PP) principal analysis. ITT analysis will include all subjects who were randomised, regardless of whether the drug dosage was changed, and analyse the subjects according to the initial randomised dosage group. To determine whether a participant fully adhered to the assigned heart-rate target, we will apply a PP analysis using the following procedure. From the start of the intervention until the participant enters the operating room, research nurses will record heart rate and blood pressure every hour (1 min rolling average after artefact removal).

Categorical variable inter-group differences will be assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test; continuous variables will be analysed using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test; time-to-event variables will be estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and inter-group differences will be compared using the Log-Rank test. In the final statistical analysis, a two-sided p value less than 0.048 will be considered statistically significant.

For the primary outcome (30-day MACE), we anticipate minimal missing data due to the short follow-up period and acute care setting. For participants lost to follow-up before 30 days, a sensitivity analysis will be performed using worst-case scenario imputation (assuming they experienced a MACE event) to assess the robustness of the primary results. For missing data in secondary continuous outcomes, we will use multiple imputation if the proportion of missingness is substantial (>5%). In addition, peak heart rate and peak systolic blood pressure during the preoperative intervention period will be compared between groups using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Interim analysis will be conducted when the number of enrolled patients reaches half of the sample size, to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of the trial. At the time of the interim analysis, statistical analysis will be conducted according to the O'Brien Fleming method with α=0.005. If the interim analysis shows any of the following situations, consideration will be given to terminating the study: (1) The research hypothesis has been proven; (2) Based on the current results, it is expected that even if the study is completed, the research hypothesis cannot be proven, or (3) There are significant abnormalities in safety-related hard endpoints (such as all-cause mortality, etc), and continuing the study would violate ethical principles.

Subgroup analysis will be conducted based on whether the patient had renal insufficiency before surgery, whether the patient was sedated and analgesic before surgery, whether different organs had ischaemic conditions before surgery, whether the dissection involved the coronary arteries, and whether the dissection involved the aortic valve, etc.

The trial protocol, statistical analysis plan and individual de-identified participant data can be accessed by contacting the corresponding author.

Informed consent

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants or their legally authorised representatives prior to any trial-related procedures. The consent process will be conducted by a qualified principal investigator, subinvestigator or a trained research coordinator at each clinical site. Given the acute nature of the condition, the process will be conducted as soon as feasible after presentation. The investigator will provide a full explanation of the trial’s nature, purpose, potential risks and benefits, and alternative treatments, using the ethics committee-approved informed consent form. An example of the participant consent form is provided in the online supplemental file 1. Participants or their LARs will be given adequate opportunity to ask questions and will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they can withdraw at any time without affecting their standard medical care.

This trial does not involve the collection or storage of biological specimens for future ancillary studies. The informed consent form covers the use of participant data for the primary and secondary outcomes defined in this protocol.

Trial registration

The ESCORT study is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry under the identifier ChiCTR2300067811.

Data monitoring committee

An independent DMC will be established without conflicts of interest. The DMC will operate under a dedicated charter and is responsible for periodically reviewing unblinded accumulating trial data on participant safety and efficacy. This committee, independent of the sponsor and funder, will provide recommendations to the TSC regarding trial continuation, modification or termination based on their review of the data.

Trial conduct monitoring

The conduct of the trial will be actively monitored by clinical research associates from the coordinating centre to ensure adherence to the protocol and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Monitoring visits will be conducted at least annually at each site, with additional visits triggered by high enrolment rates or data issues. The monitoring process will include verification of the informed consent process, source data verification for accuracy of CRF entries, assessment of protocol adherence and review of AE documentation. All findings will be documented in monitoring reports shared with site investigators to ensure prompt resolution of any identified issues.

Protocol amendments

Any amendments to the study protocol that may impact the scientific validity, participant safety, or the overall conduct of the trial will be considered substantial. All substantial amendments will be submitted for approval to the relevant research ethics committees before implementation. Once approved, these amendments will be promptly communicated in writing to all participating site investigators, the DMC and the trial registry. The site investigators are responsible for implementing the changes and, if applicable, obtaining re-consent from participants already enrolled in the trial. Non-substantial amendments (eg, administrative corrections) will be documented and shared with the investigators and the DMC for information.

Discussion

To our knowledge, ESCORT is the first multicentre, randomised controlled trial specifically designed to determine the optimal preoperative heart-rate target in adults with ATAAD scheduled for surgical repair. The findings from this study are poised to address a significant evidence gap in current international guidelines.

As noted earlier, current guideline recommendations for preoperative heart rate control in ATAAD remain grounded in level C evidence. ESCORT was specifically designed to address this evidence gap by providing high-quality, prospective data to either support or refine these consensus-driven targets. A recent large-scale observational study from China (Sino-RAD) highlighted this practice gap, demonstrating that only a minority of patients achieve this target preoperatively, and that failure to do so is associated with worse outcomes.30 ESCORT directly tests the clinical efficacy of this consensus-driven recommendation by comparing it against a more lenient, yet commonly encountered in practice, heart rate target.

The choice of comparator—a standard heart rate target of 75–85 bpm—was deliberate and grounded in both clinical practicality and methodological considerations. While prevailing consensus suggests the benefit of targeting a lower heart rate for patients with ATAAD, a target of 75–85 bpm represents a common, yet often unstandardised, real-world practice, particularly in settings where aggressive beta-blockade is cautiously applied due to concerns of precipitating hypotension and end-organ hypoperfusion. Furthermore, to robustly test the hypothesis that stricter rate control improves outcomes, a sufficient contrast between the intervention and control groups was essential. A modest difference might fail to demonstrate a statistically significant separation in outcomes. The 20-bpm separation between the low (55–65 bpm) and standard (75–85 bpm) targets was therefore chosen to provide adequate power to detect a potential clinically important difference, should one exist.

The trial employs rigorous methodology to ensure the validity of its results. Central randomisation and concealed allocation minimise selection bias. Crucially, the single-blind adjudication of all primary and secondary endpoints by an independent evaluator, unaware of treatment group assignment, significantly reduces ascertainment bias. Furthermore, all eight participating centres are established sites employing standardised perioperative management protocols, including defined anaesthetic and transfusion strategies. While acknowledging minor procedural variations, these real-world differences serve to enhance the external validity and generalisability of our findings.

A legitimate concern among clinicians is that aggressively lowering the heart rate might precipitate peri-operative hypotension and end-organ hypoperfusion. To mitigate this risk, ESCORT mandated hourly haemodynamic reviews. Protocol-defined rescue therapy with vasoactive agents such as norepinephrine was permitted.

If the primary hypothesis is confirmed, ESCORT will provide level A evidence to support revising international guidelines toward a lower, more stringent preoperative heart-rate target for ATAAD. Conversely, should the results demonstrate equivalence or even harm associated with the lower target, this would justify a more permissive heart rate strategy. Such a finding would importantly redirect future research toward alternative haemodynamic goals, such as the pulsatile index or diastolic perfusion time. In summary, the ESCORT trial is designed to address the limitations of previous studies and to fill critical knowledge gaps, thereby delivering actionable, high-quality evidence for clinicians managing this lethal cardiovascular emergency.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol (version 1.3, 3 December 2022), CRFs and informed consent documents have been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (approval number: 2022-1886). Additional approvals were obtained from the ethics committees of all participating sub-centres, including: the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval number: 2023-446); the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval number: TJ-IRB20230633); and the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval number: IRB-AF/SC-08/06.0). Any protocol amendments will be submitted for approval to these committees prior to implementation. Ethical approval for the remaining four participating centres (Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Guangzhou; Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, Shanghai; The Second Hospital of Shandong University, Jinan; and The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, Urumqi) is currently under review and will be obtained prior to initiating enrolment at those sites. Any protocol amendments will be submitted for approval to these committees prior to implementation. The findings will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at scientific conferences.

The protocol and results will be published in open access journals and presented at conferences both nationally and internationally. The publication of results will not be contingent on the outcomes. A summary of the results will be disseminated to the study participants, the Vascular Surgery Association and other relevant patient charities. Briefings will also be circulated to the National Health Commission of China and local health and service departments for major vascular surgery.
