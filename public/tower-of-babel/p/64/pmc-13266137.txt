Locally injectable visible light-cured carboxymethyl chitosan hydrogels with indocyanine green-methyl-β-cyclodextrin for breast cancer phototherapy
Authors: Sun-Jung Yoon, Sae Hyun Kim, Wheemoon Cho, Gun-Jae Jung, Dong Nyoung Heo, Su-Hak Heo, Youngbum Yoo, Suk-Ho Moon, Heung Jae Chun, Dae Hyeok Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy is the main therapy for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) but causes severe systemic toxicity. A hydrogel system that can be injected into the tumor along with photothermal/photodynamic combination therapy can potentially overcome systemic toxicity. Herein, we developed visible light-cured carboxymethyl chitosan methacrylate (CMCSMA) hydrogels containing an indocyanine green-methyl-β-cyclodextrin inclusion complex (ICG-ic-CD) as an injectable therapy for photo-triggered TNBC therapy. In vitro cell viability results from the inclusion complex of ICG and Me-βCD, which improves photostability and protects against light-induced degradation, revealed that 200 μg/mL ICG-ic-CD (ICG200-ic-CD) hydrogel possessed good biocompatibility and had a photo-triggered TNBC therapeutic effect. In addition, various CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels demonstrated superior photothermal/photodynamic combination therapy in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest that hydrogels composed of CMCSMA and 200 μg/mL of ICG-ic-CD (CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD) possess an optimal crosslinking density that enables facile injection and effective diffusion of the ICG-ic-CD into tumor tissue. Moreover, the visible light-cured CMCSMA hydrogel system described here holds promise as a versatile local drug delivery platform for various cancer treatments, including chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and gene therapy, as the loading and release of drugs and biomolecules can be effectively controlled using this system.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, Visible light-cured carboxymethyl chitosan methacrylate, Indocyanine green-methyl-β-cyclodextrin inclusion complex, Crosslinking density, Photothermal therapy, Photodynamic therapy

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression and is mainly treated with chemotherapy, which causes severe systemic toxicities and limits dose intensity [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Local drug delivery (LDD) offers site-restricted exposure and higher intratumoral drug levels than systemic delivery. Among LDD platforms, injectable hydrogels enable minimally invasive administration and in situ solidification for sustained local therapy [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Although photo-curable hydrogels have been previously established, they offer well-recognized advantages such as rapid gelation under visible light and precise spatiotemporal control, making them highly suitable for localized drug delivery and intraoperative applications [10,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. In this study, we employed photo-curable hydrogels as an injectable local drug delivery platform that combines rapid gelation with precise spatiotemporal control under visible light. This approach provides minimally invasive administration, tunable crosslinking density for controlled release, and improved intratumoral retention of therapeutic agents, features that are particularly advantageous for LDD [20,21] (see Scheme 1).

Injectable CMCSMA/ ICG200-ic-CD hydrogel for localized PTT/PDT therapy in TNBC.

Methacrylated carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCSMA) forms visible-light-cured networks with riboflavin and offers aqueous processability and biocompatibility suitable for biomedical use [20,[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Indocyanine green (ICG), an FDA-approved dye for diagnostic imaging, has been widely investigated as a photothermal agent in experimental cancer therapies and is commonly utilized in photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) due to its near-infrared optical properties and biocompatibility, but its clinical utility is limited by aggregation, photobleaching, and rapid clearance. Host-guest complexation with methyl-β-cyclodextrin (Me-βCD) mitigates these issues by reducing ICG self-aggregation and improving photostability and fluorescence, thereby enhancing phototherapy performance and tumor retention [26,27]. Recent studies have demonstrated that host-guest complexation with cyclodextrin derivatives effectively improves the photostability, fluorescence intensity, and tumor targetability of ICG. Methyl-β-cyclodextrin and sulfobutyl ether-β-cyclodextrin are widely used to stabilize ICG by reducing its aggregation and autoquenching effects, thereby enhancing photothermal and imaging efficacy both in vitro and in vivo [28,29]. Moreover, cyclodextrin-based ICG complexes have shown improved tumor accumulation and reduced photobleaching during photothermal therapy [[30], [31], [32]]. These findings support the rationale of employing ICG-CD inclusion complexes for cancer phototherapy.

Here, we develop an injectable, visible-light-cured CMCSMA hydrogel loaded with an ICG-Me-βCD inclusion complex (ICG-ic-CD) for localized PTT/PDT in TNBC. We hypothesize that a locally injectable, visible light-cured CMCSMA hydrogel incorporating ICG-ic-CD can provide dual-mode PTT and PDT for TNBC, while offering precise spatiotemporal control, tunable release through crosslinking density, and improved intratumoral retention to minimize systemic toxicity. We therefore (i) verified inclusion complex formation by ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy; (ii) identified a biocompatible and effective ICG-ic-CD range in vitro; (iii) tuned CMCSMA concentration and curing time to modulate crosslinking density and mechanical performance; and (iv) evaluated antitumor efficacy in a 4T1 xenograft model. Our results indicate that hydrogels composed of CMCSMA and 200 μg/mL of ICG-ic-CD (CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD) are a promising local drug delivery (LDD) platform for combined photothermal and photodynamic therapy in TNBC.

Materials and methods

Preparation of ICG-Me-βCD complex and CMCSMA hydrogel

Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) was chemically modified with methacrylic anhydride to obtain CMCSMA following a previously reported procedure (Degree of substitution: approximately 55%) [20]. To improve the stability of ICG, an inclusion complex with Me-βCD was prepared by co-dissolving ICG and Me-βCD at an optimized molar ratio, followed by purification. The inclusion of ICG into the Me-βCD cavity was confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopy, which showed characteristic absorption shifts compared with free ICG. Precursor solutions containing CMCSMA (4 - 8 wt%), ICG-Me-βCD (ICG-ic-CD), and riboflavin (24 μM) as a photoinitiator were crosslinked by visible light irradiation (430 - 485 nm, peak at 450 nm) for 10 - 120 s to obtain hydrogels. Inclusion complex formation was further characterized by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy.

In vitro photothermal effect

To evaluate the photothermal effect, ICG-ic-CD solutions and CMCSMA/ICG-ic-CD hydrogels with varying ICG concentrations were irradiated with an 808 nm Near-Infrared (NIR) laser (10 min). Temperature changes were recorded using an infrared thermal imaging camera at 1-min intervals. The maximum temperature increase and heating profiles were analyzed to determine the photothermal efficiency of each formulation.

Characterization of hydrogels

Swelling ratios were calculated by immersing hydrogels in PBS at 37 °C until equilibrium was reached. The rheological behavior of hydrogels, including storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus (G″) was determined using oscillatory rheometry. Mechanical properties were further assessed by compressive strength testing. The injection force was performed at a load speed of 10 mm/min, and the force required for injection was recorded. Release kinetics of ICG-ic-CD from CMCSMA hydrogels were investigated by immersing hydrogel disks in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, aliquots of the supernatant were collected and analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy to quantify the cumulative release of ICG.

In vitro cell studies

Murine 4T1 TNBC cells were cultured under standard conditions and treated with free ICG, ICG-ic-CD, or CMCSMA/ICG-ic-CD hydrogels at various concentrations. Following NIR (808 nm) irradiation, cell viability was quantified by CCK-8 assay. Intracellular ROS production was assessed by DCFH-DA staining and visualized with confocal microscopy.

Animal ethics

All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Catholic University of Korea, and were approved under protocol number CUMC-2023-0300-04.

In vivo animal studies

Female Balb/c mice (6 weeks old) were inoculated with 4T1 cells, and tumors were allowed to reach ∼200 mm3. Mice were intratumorally injected with CMCSMA/ICG-ic-CD hydrogels (50 μL total, divided into four doses), followed by NIR laser irradiation (808 nm) at defined time points. To confirm the tumor localization and retention of ICG-ic-CD, in vivo NIR fluorescence imaging was performed at various time intervals post-injection using a small animal imaging system. The distribution and persistence of fluorescence signals were quantified to assess intratumoral retention. In addition, real-time photothermal imaging was conducted during NIR laser irradiation to monitor temperature elevation within tumor tissues. Tumor volumes and body weights were recorded during the treatment period. At the study endpoint, tumors were excised, weighed, and analyzed histologically by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to evaluate both antitumor efficacy and the in vivo fate of the injected hydrogels.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Significance levels are indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Exact p-values are reported in the main text where relevant.

Results and discussion

In vitro evaluation of photo-triggered TNBC therapy with hydrogels with various ICG-ic-CD concentrations

ICG, an amphiphilic photothermal agent, is unstable in aqueous environments due to dimerization and photodegradation, reducing its PTT/PDT efficacy [26,33]. To overcome these limitations, we encapsulated ICG in the hydrophobic cavity of Me-βCD to form an inclusion complex (ICG-ic-CD) (Fig. 1A) [28,34]. In this host-guest pairing, the hydrophobic polyaromatic regions of the ICG molecule are accommodated inside the Me-βCD cavity [32,35], while ICG's hydrophilic sulfonate groups remain outside - a configuration that prevents ICG molecules from stacking into dimers. The UV-Vis spectrum of free ICG showed a dominant absorption peak at ∼710 nm, attributed to the H-aggregated (dimeric) form of ICG, and a secondary peak at ∼780 nm for the monomeric form. Upon complexation with Me-β-CD (Fig. 1B), the ∼710 nm peak decreased while the ∼780 nm peak became more prominent, indicating disaggregation of dimers into monomers. This shift supports the inclusion of ICG into the Me-β-CD cavity, reducing π-π stacking and self-quenching effects, consistent with previously reported host-guest complexation behavior [32]. This shift indicates that Me-βCD's cavity successfully binds ICG and inhibits its self-aggregation, consistent with previous reports of cyclodextrins reducing ICG dimerization and improving its photostability [28]. To further verify the formation of ICG-ic-CD beyond UV-Vis spectroscopy, 1H NMR and FTIR analyses were performed (Fig. S1A). In the 1H NMR spectra, the characteristic aromatic proton signals of free ICG (6.0 - 9.0 ppm) were markedly attenuated in ICG-ic-CD, while the backbone proton signals of Me-βCD (3.0 - 5.0 ppm) were retained with slight variations. These changes suggest restricted molecular mobility of ICG and its interaction with the Me-βCD cavity. FTIR analysis further supported complex formation (Fig. S1B). Compared with free ICG, the characteristic bands of ICG in the 1500 - 1400 cm−1 region were reduced and partially broadened in ICG-ic-CD. Meanwhile, the O–H stretching band of Me-βCD (∼3400 cm−1) exhibited subtle broadening, indicating altered intermolecular interactions associated with complex formation. To evaluate photothermal performance, ICG-ic-CD solutions (0 - 400 μg/mL) were irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser (15.3 W/cm2) for 10 min (Fig. 1C, D and Fig. S3). An increase in the temperature was observed in a concentration-dependent manner below 100 μg/mL. At concentrations of 150 μg/mL or higher, the temperature rapidly increased during the first 4 min of 808-nm NIR irradiation and then plateaued between 59.7 and 63.7 °C for the remaining 6 min. This saturation behavior is attributed to self-aggregation of ICG at higher concentrations, which diminishes its photothermal conversion efficiency due to reduced absorption [36]. A decrease in temperature was observed in low-concentration ICG-ic-CD solutions during NIR irradiation (Fig. 1D), which can be attributed to photobleaching and heat diffusion effects that become more significant when the number of photoabsorbers is limited. In contrast, at higher ICG-ic-CD concentrations, stable photothermal performance was maintained due to enhanced molecular stabilization by Me-βCD encapsulation and intermolecular coupling between adjacent ICG molecules [37]. In Fig. 1C, thermal images of 10, 100, and 150 μg/mL were selected to illustrate the range leading up to and including this plateau region. Complete thermal images for all tested concentrations (0 - 400 μg/mL) are provided in Fig. S3. Overall, ICG-ic-CD maintained efficient photothermal conversion, in agreement with prior ICG-Me-βCD studies [31]. In addition, to further verify the photostability of the representative formulation, free ICG and ICG-ic-CD at 200 μg/mL were subjected to repeated NIR irradiation in vitro (Fig. S2). Although both groups exhibited rapid heating during the first irradiation cycle, free ICG showed a markedly reduced temperature increase during the second irradiation cycle, whereas ICG-ic-CD maintained a substantially higher reheating profile. Consistently, the optical images showed a more pronounced color change in free ICG after repeated NIR exposure, indicating that complexation with Me-βCD improved the photostability of ICG and preserved its photothermal performance. Considering therapeutic thresholds (>42 °C for cell damage, >50 °C for rapid necrosis) [38], ICG-ic-CD concentrations above 100 μg/mL are suitable for PTT. ROS generation was assessed in 4T1 TNBC cells treated with ICG-ic-CD and irradiated at 808 nm (Fig. 1E, F, and Fig. S4). ROS was negligible at 1 min but increased with irradiation time, reaching a plateau above 200 μg/mL. Cell viability assays showed more than 80% viability up to 200 μg/mL without irradiation (82.2 ± 4.2% at 200 μg/mL), confirming biocompatibility. With irradiation, viability decreased dose-dependently below 200 μg/mL and stabilized above this concentration (Fig. 1G and H). These findings agree with previous reports of ICG-βCD complexes improving stability, tumor accumulation, and selective cytotoxicity [30,31]. Thus, 200 μg/mL ICG-ic-CD was identified as biocompatible without irradiation and effective with irradiation. Concentration of 200 μg/mL ICG-ic-CD was selected for subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies.

Swelling ratio, rheological properties, compressive strength, injection force, release profiles of CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogel

TNBC, an aggressive subtype lacking therapeutic targets, requires localized strategies [1,39]. We developed an injectable, visible light-cured CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogel as a local drug delivery platform for combined PTT/PDT [26]. In this system, methacrylic groups are introduced into CMCS via epoxy ring-opening reactions [20], and riboflavin serves as a visible light photoinitiator [25]. Riboflavin (24 μM) was selected as a biocompatible photoinitiator concentration, as it maintained over 90% cell viability after visible light exposure (Fig. S5). Evaluating the swelling ratio, rheological property, compressive strength, and injection force is critical, since hydrogel mechanics regulate injectability and drug release [40]. Gelation of precursor solutions was confirmed by tilting and rheological analysis (Fig. S6). As shown in Fig. 2A and B, swelling ratio decreased with higher CMCSMA concentration and longer irradiation, consistent with denser crosslinking. Rheological analysis using frequency sweep tests demonstrated that both storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) increased with higher CMCSMA concentration and prolonged curing time, indicating denser crosslinking and enhanced elasticity (Fig. 2C). This trend likely reflects increased crosslinking density at higher polymer concentrations and longer curing times [41,42]. Denser networks reduce swelling due to limited water uptake [43]. Compressive strength and injection force increased with CMCSMA concentration and irradiation time, consistent with higher crosslinking density (Fig. 2D and E). In addition to mechanical characterization, we evaluated the release profile of ICG-ic-CD from CMCSMA hydrogels under physiological conditions (PBS, 37 °C). Hydrogels were prepared with varying CMCSMA concentrations (4 - 8 wt%) and visible light curing times (10 - 120 s), and the cumulative release of ICG-ic-CD was monitored using UV-Vis absorbance at 780 nm over 48 h. As shown in Fig. 2F, higher CMCSMA concentrations and longer curing times significantly suppressed ICG-ic-CD release, indicating that crosslinking density modulates release kinetics. These results support the hypothesis that photoinitiated crosslinking effectively regulates therapeutic agent availability by altering hydrogel crosslinking density.

In vitro evaluation of photo-triggered TNBC therapy with CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels

Visible light-cured CMCSMA hydrogels were assessed as localized carriers for PTT/PDT in TNBC. CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels were prepared with varying CMCSMA concentrations and curing times (10 - 120 s), and their effects were examined with or without 808 nm NIR irradiation (Fig. 3). Temperature analysis showed that hydrogels cured for up to 60 s reached temperatures greater than 56 °C within 3 min, while those cured for 120 s only reached 49 - 53 °C, indicating reduced photothermal efficiency (Fig. 3A). Thus, curing for less than 60 s ensures sufficient PTT. In addition to its photothermal effect, ICG also acts as a photosensitizer [26]. ROS generation was evaluated by DCFH-DA staining in 4T1 cells treated with hydrogel extracts. No ROS was detected before NIR irradiation, but irradiated samples showed ROS levels depending on CMCSMA concentration and curing time (Fig. 3B and C). A higher CMCSMA concentration or longer curing time produces a more densely crosslinked network which in turn slows the diffusion/release of the ICG-ic-CD complex from the hydrogel. Consistently, we observed that less crosslinked hydrogels released more ICG-ic-CD (yielding greater ROS formation and cell death), whereas highly crosslinked hydrogels restricted ICG-ic-CD release, resulting in lower ROS generation. This trend is in line with previous reports linking higher crosslinking density to slower drug release from hydrogels [44]. Cell viability assays confirmed these findings. Without NIR irradiation, viability remained more than 80% across all groups, confirming biocompatibility. With irradiation, viability dropped sharply, particularly in hydrogels cured for 10 s (<15%). Longer curing times and higher CMCSMA concentrations reduced therapeutic efficacy, with 120 s-cured hydrogels showing more than 40% viability, indicating diminished PTT/PDT activity. Overall, hydrogels cured for 10 s provided the strongest therapeutic effects, balancing photothermal efficiency, ROS production, and cytotoxicity in 4 - 8 wt% CMCSMA formulations. Notably, the variations in ROS formation observed under different CMCSMA concentrations and curing times (Fig. 3B and C) were consistent with the corresponding trends in cell viability (Fig. 3D), supporting a ROS-mediated contribution to the overall phototherapeutic effect. These findings indicate that the in vitro therapeutic mechanism of the CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels is based on the combined photothermal effect and ROS-mediated photodynamic effect of ICG-ic-CD under NIR irradiation [45,46]. In this system, lower-crosslinked hydrogels provide greater functional availability of ICG-ic-CD, resulting in enhanced ROS generation and cytotoxicity, whereas highly crosslinked hydrogels restrict ICG-ic-CD availability and attenuate the overall phototherapeutic response. To further verify crosslinking-dependent release behavior under in vitro conditions, 4T1 cells were indirectly exposed for 24 h to CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels formed in transwell inserts and prepared with different CMCSMA concentrations and curing times (Fig. S7). The fluorescence intensity of released ICG-ic-CD in the cells decreased progressively with increasing CMCSMA concentration and prolonged curing time, indicating that denser hydrogel networks restricted ICG-ic-CD release and reduced cellular exposure. This trend was consistent with the corresponding changes in ROS generation and cell viability.

In vivo PTT evaluation of TNBC using CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels

The photothermal effect was evaluated in vivo by measuring tumor temperatures in mice injected with CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels and irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser at 24 and 48 h (Fig. 4A). ICG-containing groups showed higher tumor temperatures than Sham up to 48 h. Quantitatively, all CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD groups (4 - 8 wt%, 10 - 60 s curing) exhibited greater heating than free ICG or ICG-ic-CD at both time points (Fig. 4B). Temperatures were lower at 48 h than 24 h, reflecting gradual release of ICG-ic-CD. The enhanced retention of ICG-ic-CD within the photo-cured CMCSMA matrix explains the more sustained photothermal effect compared with free ICG formulations.

In vivo NIR imaging and biodistribution of CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD

The retention of ICG-ic-CD in tumor tissues was confirmed by in vivo NIR imaging (Fig. 5A). ICG and ICG-ic-CD-treated groups showed fluorescence only at 24 h, whereas CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD groups retained tumor fluorescence at 48 h, with intensity varying by CMCSMA concentration and curing time. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 5B) showed a gradual signal decrease in 4 - 6 wt% hydrogels (10 - 60 s curing), while 7 - 8 wt% samples maintained relatively stable fluorescence, indicating slower release. These results demonstrate that hydrogel crosslinking density, controlled by CMCSMA concentration and curing time, regulates ICG release. Ex vivo biodistribution at 48 h (Fig. 5C and D) confirmed strong fluorescence in tumors across all hydrogel groups, particularly those with higher CMCSMA concentrations and longer curing times. In addition, intestinal accumulation was observed in 4 - 6 wt% groups irradiated for 10 s, suggesting faster release under lower crosslinking conditions. Overall, both in vivo and ex vivo analyses indicate that CMCSMA crosslinking density modulates ICG retention and systemic distribution. To further assess systemic distribution, organ-level fluorescence was quantified at 48 h post-injection (Fig. S8). The intestinal fluorescence observed in the 4 - 6 wt% CMCSMA hydrogels cured for 10 s is attributable to the limited early diffusion of ICG-ic-CD from the loosely crosslinked network, rather than broad systemic distribution. A small fraction released under these low-density conditions can exit the tumor microenvironment because 4T1 tumors possess highly permeable vasculature and active lymphatic drainage [47], allowing the fluorophore to enter systemic or lymphatic circulation and subsequently reach the gastrointestinal tract through physiological transport pathways. In contrast, higher CMCSMA concentrations or longer curing times generate denser hydrogel networks that effectively confine ICG-ic-CD within the tumor, which aligns with the absence of detectable fluorescence in other major organs. Together with the rapid clearance characteristics of free ICG [26], these findings underscore that hydrogel crosslinking density is a key determinant of local retention and minimized systemic exposure. To further support the release-related interpretation of the in vivo fluorescence results, time-course in vivo dual-fluorescence imaging was performed after subcutaneous injection using FITC-labeled CMCSMA (green) and ICG fluorescence (red) (Fig. S9). The two fluorescence signals showed spatially associated retention patterns over time, and both signals decreased more rapidly in hydrogels prepared at lower CMCSMA concentrations and/or shorter curing times, whereas prolonged co-retention was observed in higher-crosslinking groups. These findings support that lower-crosslinking hydrogels provided weaker retention of ICG within the hydrogel depot, consistent with faster release-related loss in vivo. In addition, hemolysis analysis of CMCSMA/ICG-ic-CD hydrogels with different CMCSMA concentrations and curing times showed that all tested groups exhibited hemolysis values below 5%, with most remaining around or below 2% (Fig. S10), indicating favorable hemocompatibility regardless of formulation condition.

Anticancer effect of CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels

The therapeutic efficacy of CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels was evaluated in a 4T1 TNBC xenograft model with or without 808 nm NIR irradiation according to the treatment schedule shown in Fig. 6A. Representative tumor-bearing mouse photographs and quantitative tumor growth curves are shown in Fig. 6B and C, respectively. In the PBS group, tumors increased continuously, while in the ICG and ICG-ic-CD groups tumor volume decreased until day 2 but increased again thereafter (Fig. 6C). In contrast, CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels cured for 10 s and irradiated showed complete disappearance of tumors by day 2 (Fig. 6B and C). At 30 and 60 s curing, 4 - 5 wt% hydrogels also led to complete tumor elimination, whereas 6 - 8 wt% groups exhibited partial regression followed by regrowth (Fig. 6B and C). For example, in the 6 wt% group, tumor volume decreased to ∼65 mm3 on day 2 but increased again by day 6, and higher concentrations (7 - 8 wt%) showed faster regrowth (Fig. 6C). These outcomes are consistent with earlier observations that higher crosslinking density limits ICG release, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy. While higher CMCSMA concentrations enhanced the retention of ICG-ic-CD within the tumor, excessive crosslinking may restrict the functional availability and spatial distribution of photoactive ICG during NIR irradiation. Consequently, an intermediate crosslinking density appears to provide a more favorable balance between retention and effective therapeutic coverage, leading to improved tumor growth inhibition. To further visualize these condition-dependent therapeutic responses, representative photographs of tumor-bearing mice at days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6 after NIR treatment (specifically from groups in which residual tumor lesions remained) are provided in Fig. S11, qualitatively complementing the quantitative tumor growth data shown in Fig. 6B and C. Tumor weights measured on day 6 were consistent with the tumor volume results, as shown by the representative images of excised tumors (Fig. 6D) and the quantitative tumor weights (Fig. 6E). Non-irradiated hydrogel groups yielded tumors of ∼0.5 - 0.6 g, while irradiated 4 - 5 wt% hydrogels showed no measurable tumors (Fig. 6D and E). In this study, the term ‘complete tumor elimination’ was defined as the absence of visible and measurable tumors at the end of the 6-day observation period in each group, as assessed by caliper measurement and visual inspection. This short-term disappearance does not imply long-term cure and should be interpreted within the defined timeframe. Body weight monitoring (Fig. 6F) showed no significant differences among groups during the treatment period. To further evaluate systemic safety, blood samples collected 2 weeks after subcutaneous injection of CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogels prepared with different CMCSMA concentrations and visible-light curing times were analyzed for AST (GOT) and ALT (GPT) (Fig. S12). AST and ALT values in the hydrogel groups were generally comparable to, or lower than, those of the control group and did not show a consistent progressive increase across the formulations. In addition, gross observation of the injection sites 2 weeks after subcutaneous injection showed formulation-dependent persistence of the hydrogel depot: in several lower-crosslinking groups, no grossly visible hydrogel residue remained, whereas some higher-crosslinking groups still exhibited grossly observable residual depot material (Fig. S12). These findings indicate no obvious progressive systemic toxicity during the 2-week observation period and support crosslinking-dependent in vivo persistence and clearance-related loss of the hydrogel depot. Histological analysis (Fig. 7) further supported these results. Whole-section H&E images are additionally provided in Fig. S13. Non-irradiated tumors displayed large viable areas with necrotic cores, whereas irradiated tumors exhibited extensive necrosis and fibrosis. Taken together, these in vivo results suggest that local delivery of ICG-ic-CD by CMCSMA hydrogels enhances tumor treatment through the combined photothermal and photodynamic actions of ICG-ic-CD [45,46], while hydrogel crosslinking density governs therapeutic efficacy by regulating the local availability of the photoactive agent. Compared with previously reported injectable ICG-based local hydrogel systems, the present visible-light-cured CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD platform shows several practically relevant distinctions. A previously reported thermosensitive ICG hydrogel was designed to stabilize ICG and enable NIR-modulated gel-sol transition and combinatorial photothermal/photodynamic therapy, offering temperature-responsive release control and strong local phototherapeutic efficacy in a 4T1 model [48]. A pH-responsive supramolecular hydrogel loaded with ICG was reported for synergistic chemo-photothermal/photodynamic therapy, with the advantage of microenvironment-responsive drug release and combined therapeutic functionality [49]. In addition, an in situ fabricated ICG-alginate hydrogel was developed for localized tumor ablation, highlighting the utility of ion-triggered local hydrogel formation for photothermal treatment [50]. In comparison, the present CMCSMA system enables on-demand in situ curing after injection by visible light, and its crosslinking density can be directly modulated by both polymer concentration and curing time. This formulation-level tunability allows simultaneous control over mechanical properties, intratumoral retention, and ICG release behavior within a single hydrogel platform. At the same time, the current results also reveal a formulation-dependent trade-off, in which excessive crosslinking improves local retention but reduces the functional availability and spatial distribution of photoactive ICG during NIR irradiation. Therefore, rather than claiming broad superiority over previously reported systems, our results suggest that the visible-light-cured CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD hydrogel represents a distinct and tunable local delivery strategy that may be particularly advantageous for superficial TNBC phototherapy.

Conclusions

In this study, a locally injectable CMCSMA/ICG-ic-CD hydrogel system was developed and evaluated for photo-triggered TNBC therapy. In vitro, 200 μg/mL ICG-ic-CD induced significant photo-triggered cell death, and increasing CMCSMA concentration or curing time led to lower swelling, higher rheological properties, greater compressive strength, stronger injection force, and lower ICG release profiles. In vivo, photothermal efficacy was dependent on CMCSMA concentration and curing time, while in vitro studies confirmed the additional contribution of PDT through ROS generation. We conclude that CMCSMA/ICG200-ic-CD formulations with CMCSMA concentrations of 4 - 6 wt% and visible light curing times of 10 - 60 s exhibited the most favorable combination of injectability, mechanical strength, and therapeutic efficacy, and are thus promising injectable platforms for photothermal and photodynamic TNBC therapies. The therapeutic performance of this hydrogel system is tunable through formulation design, because crosslinking density controls the release kinetics and local availability of ICG-ic-CD. These findings indicate that precise control of polymer concentration and curing time is critical for optimizing injectable photo-curable hydrogel systems for TNBC therapy. While the therapeutic hydrogel system demonstrated efficacy in subcutaneous TNBC models, the limited tissue penetration depth of visible and NIR light must be considered for clinical translation. In particular, visible light (∼450 nm) penetrates only superficial tissues, and 808-nm NIR light reaches a depth of ∼5 - 10 mm in soft tissue. Therefore, this system is most suitable for superficial tumors, post-surgical tumor beds, or lesions accessible via interstitial fiber optics.
