Evaluation of a Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR) based balance sensor for objective fall risk assessment in older adults: a study protocol
Authors: Huanran Liu, Liqing Cai, Xin Ma, Jiaming Chen, Cindy Lo Kuen Lam, Jacqueline Kwan Yuk Yuen, Chin Ping Chan, Hon Ho Sze, Hua Li Wang, Ning Xi, Vivian Weiqun Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical fall risk assessments, such as the Fall Risk Assessment Scale for the Elderly (FRASE), often rely on subjective clinician observation. This protocol described the evaluation of “iBalance”, an AI-enabled device utilizing Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR) to objectively measure dynamic balance and predict fall risk. This study aims to validate the screening accuracy of the iBalance device and explore its feasibility for clinical integration.Methods412 adults aged 60 years and above and 10 clinical professionals will be recruited from outpatient clinics for this mixed-method study. Older adults’ fall risk will be assessed by the iBalance and clinical assessments (e.g., FRASE scale) separately, and their fall incidents will be recorded during a three-month follow-up. The iBalance captures high-resolution plantar pressure at 30 Hz. The system processes data via: (1) a machine learning model trained on extracted 3D Center of Pressure (CoP) and Center of Gravity (CoG) features; (2) a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for direct video data analysis; and (3) a statistical method optimized for maximum Area Under the Curve (AUC). To ensure participant safety, all the assessments will be conducted with side handrails and staff guarding to prevent falls. Individual interviews with clinical professionals will be conducted to gather insights on improving the device’s practicality and the application of clinical workflows. The primary outcome is sensitivity, specificity, and AUC of iBalance compared to the clinical assessments. Logistic regression will adjust for key confounders (age, gender, and BMI) for both fall risk factor identification and 3-month prospective fall prediction. Qualitative interviews will undergo thematic analysis.DiscussionThis protocol outlines a comprehensive evaluation for validating an AI-driven fall risk assessment tool. By addressing both technical screening accuracy and practical clinical utility, the study seeks to establish a standardized, objective framework for early fall intervention in geriatric care.Ethics and trial registrationThis study has been approved by the IRB of The University of Hong Kong/Hong Kong Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (IRB reference number: UW 24–540). Introduction

Hong Kong’s aging population already accounted of 20.5% from the latest 2021 Population Census in Hong Kong [1], highlighting the importance of addressing older adults’ health challenges in this aging society. Fall is defined as an event that results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or floor or other lower level, which is the second leading cause of accidental injury globally, especially for adults older than 60 years old [2]. These falls, particularly among older patients, often result in serious adverse outcomes, including injuries, prolonged hospitalizations, and increased healthcare costs [3], leading to a global public health concern. In Hong Kong, the majority of fallers aged 65 and above during hospitalization reported injuries [4]. Comprehensive fall risk assessments are especially recommended for older patients, alongside the interventions to prevent fall risks effectively [5].

Fall Risk Assessment Scale for the Elderly (FRASE) is an effective tool in predicting fall risk [6], adapted from the Falls Risk Assessment Tool (FRAT) for older adults [7]. It assesses multiple dimensions of fall risk, such as gait, sensory, medical history, and mobility. While it demonstrates a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 66% in predicting fall risk and is widely used in clinical practice [8, 9], its efficacy is inherently limited by the subjectivity of clinician observation and scoring. Similar performance-based tests, such as 4-Meter Walking and Five Times Sit-to-Stand [10], and Functional Reach Test [11], often exhibit rater bias and fail to capture the subtle and dynamic balance fluctuations indicative of early-stage fall risk. This subjectivity highlights a critical need for objective, data-driven assessment modalities in geriatric care. Encouragingly, Artificial Intelligence (AI) offers a promising solution. By processing complex clinical data, AI-driven models can significantly enhance the predictive accuracy of fall risk assessments [12].

However, the predictive power of AI fundamentally depends on the objectivity and granularity of the input data. While AI provides the analytical framework, recent evidence underscores that integrating sensor-derived biomechanical markers with non-accelerometric clinical factors through machine learning can achieve superior predictive accuracy for falls in older populations [13]. Within this framework, the emergence of Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR) technology offers a paradigm shift towards objective biomechanical measurement. Unlike traditional accelerometer-based sensors, FTIR-based markers, specifically Center of Pressure (CoP) and Center of Gravity (CoG) trajectories, provide a more sensitive and reliable non-accelerometric measure of balance that subjective scales cannot replicate [14, 15].

The iBalance device was developed to address this gap by utilizing high-resolution coupled with AI-driven analytical pathways. Although previous iterations of the iBalance have shown promise in assessing human balance and sarcopenia [16–21], its screening accuracy as a clinical screening tool for fall risk has not yet been validated against established clinical tests. Therefore, its performance must be evaluated alongside widely implemented reference measures, such as the FRASE scale, 4-Meter Walking, Five Times Sit-to-Stand, and Functional Reach test, to determine its utility in geriatric clinical practice.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a direct comparison between the iBalance device and the established clinical assessment tool (e.g., FRASE scale). We hypothesize that the iBalance device may demonstrate a significantly higher Area Under the Curve (AUC) for fall risk classification relative to the clinical assessment tool (e.g., FRASE scale).

Methods

Study design

This study employs an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, wherein qualitative data are collected after quantitative data to explain and elaborate on the initial results [22]. To begin with, a quantitative study is designed to compare the sensitivity and specificity of fall risk screening accuracy using iBalance against the established clinical comparator, such as the Fall Risk Assessment Scale for the Elderly (FRASE) and other physical performance tests (4-Meter Walking and Five Times Sit-to-Stand, and Functional Reach Test). Fall history, encompassing both retrospective data at baseline and prospective incidents recorded via a daily diary and 3-month telephone follow-up, will serve as the reference standard for determining fall risk status. The second part is a qualitative study to explore the application of using the device in clinical scenarios. We will select ten clinical professionals in the form of individual interviews to collect and analyze their opinions on enhancing the application of the device in clinical scenarios. Data will be integrated after data collection and analysis (see Fig. 1). Following the independent analysis of both studies, the quantitative screening accuracy results and the qualitative findings on clinical application will be synthesized narratively. This integration aims to provide a holistic evaluation of the iBalance device’s clinical utility, where the technical performance metrics are contextualized by the practical feasibility insights provided by clinical professionals and research assistants. Qualitative findings from clinical professionals will be used to explore the application of device in the clinical setting. The SPIRIT guideline ensures rigorous transparency in study design and reporting (Additional file 1) [23].

Setting

All the participants will be recruited and undergo baseline experiments at two General Outpatient Clinics (GOPCs) and one Nurse and Allied Health Clinic (NAHC) within the Hong Kong Western Cluster of the Hospital Authority in Hong Kong SAR. To ensure inter-rater reliability and prevent cognitive fatigue or environmental bias, the order in which data is collected from the three sites will be determined using a computer-generated random number sequence. Upon completion of the baseline session, the research assistant will provide each participant with a standardized daily fall diary. Telephone follow-up will be conducted online. The interviews will be held in person.

Sampling

In quantitative study, the sample size was calculated following the method by Buderer (1996) to ensure precision in estimating both sensitivity and specificity. The calculation was based on the following parameters: a 95% confidence level, an expected sensitivity (SN) of 74% and specificity (SP) of 66% [9], a latest fall prevalence (P) 21.6% among Chinese older adults [24], and a margin of error (W) of 10%, following the established methodological examples in Buderer (1996) for the clinical validation of screening tools [25]. The calculation for specificity required 87 non-fallers.

(4) Calculate the Sample Size Required for Specificity, N (Specificity).

Thus, the calculation for specificity required a total of 111 participants.

(5) Select the Final Sample Size, N.

N is the larger of N (Sensitivity) and N (Specificity). To ensure adequate precision for both key metrics, we adopted the larger of the two estimates, which was 343 participants. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the minimum sample size required is 412.

The selection of this sample size is informed by the systematic review by Hennink and Kaiser (2022), which demonstrates that in studies with a relatively homogeneous study population and a narrow research objective, code saturation is typically achieved at least 9 interviews [26]. Given that our participants are clinicians sharing similar professional backgrounds and clinical workflows, they constitute a highly homogeneous group, making a target of 10 interviews scientifically robust for identifying core themes. To address potential drop-out, we will adopt an over-recruitment strategy by issuing 12 invitations to ensure a minimum of 10 completed interviews are available for analysis.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Individuals who meet the following criteria will be invited to our quantitative study: (a) aged 60 years old and above, (b) able to communicate with experiment operators in Cantonese, Mandarin, or English, and (c) current patients at the clinics. Individuals will be excluded from the study if they are: (a) unable to provide written informed consent, for example, illiterate or with cognitive impairment. Older adults’ cognitive status will be verified through a review of medical records. Or (b) unable to stand for 30 s without any assistance.

Eligible participants for the qualitative study must meet the following criteria: (a) clinical professionals (e.g., physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists) currently working in a hospital, General Outpatient Clinics, or Nurse and Allied Health Clinic, and (b) have experience in fall risk assessment or managing older adults at risk of falls. Those who lack clinical experience in fall risk assessment or do not regularly communicate with older patients will be excluded from this qualitative study.

Recruitment

After getting a computer-generated random number sequence, the research assistant (RA 1) will start participant recruitment. The total sample of 412 older adults will be distributed across the sites as follows: 137 older adults from Site 1, 137 from Site 2, and 138 from Site 3. This structured allocation ensures that the recruitment burden is balanced, and the data collection process remains uniform across different clinical environments.

A designated Research Assistant (RA 1) will be stationed at the waiting areas of the clinic. RA 1 will approach older adults and provide a standardized verbal briefing and a study information sheet. Potential participants will undergo immediate screening. RA 1 will verify eligibility, specifically the exclusion of older adults with documented cognitive impairment or illiteracy, by reviewing the participants’ electronic medical records.

Procedure

To ensure a streamlined and double-blind assessment, three Research Assistants (RAs) are assigned specific and blinded roles. RA 1 will oversee administration, participant recruitment, and support RA 3 in clinical testing, RA 2 will serve as the iBalance device technician, and RA 3 will act as the clinical assessor. A strict double-blind protocol will be enforced to eliminate interpretation bias. RA 2 will conduct iBalance measurement in a separate area (away from RA 1 and RA 3), remaining entirely blinded to each other’s assessment results until the final data integration phase.

Physiological characteristics collection and clinical testing

This study will employ a mixed-method design (Fig. 1). After obtaining informed consent from participants at the clinics (Additional file 2), our RA 1 will assign a unique ID to each participant, which will serve as their sole identifier throughout our study. Following this, physiological characteristics, including blood pressure, pulse, height, weight, and lower leg length, will be collected by RA 1 and RA 3. Subsequently, clinical fall risk assessments, such as the 5 Times Sit-to-Stand test, 4-Meter Walking, and Functional Reach Test will be administered by RA 1 and RA 3.

Standard operating procedure for iBalance measurement

Then, the participant will be escorted to a separate area for iBalance device testing, which will be conducted by RA 2.

iBalance is a balance sensor [17] designed for fall risk assessment that enables users to monitor changes in their balance ability and detect potential fall risks through daily balance measurements. The balance sensor utilizes the Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR) principle as shown in Fig. 2.

a Balance Sensor Hardware; b Principle of the Balance Sensor: Frustrated Total Internal Reflection

To maximize reproducibility and minimize measurement noise, iBalance testing will follow a strict environmental protocol in a dedicated room. To prevent interference with the FTIR sensor and maintain a consistent 30 Hz optical baseline, windows will be shaded, and overhead lights will be closed during the 30-second measurement period. Additionally, RA 2 will perform daily calibrations to verify that sensor sensitivity remains within established operational parameters.

iBalance calibration will be performed by characterizing the relationship between optical pixel data and physical pressure using a pneumatic compression system to ensure a uniform interface across the sensing surface [19]. To account for inherent signal noise and optimize regression accuracy, we implement the Hybrid Analytical and Network (HAN) architecture. This framework synergizes theoretical physical principles with deep learning, providing superior measurement stability and higher precision than standard data-driven or purely analytical methods, even when operating with smaller training datasets [17].

Participants will stand barefoot with each foot centered on its respective sensing plate, maintaining a clear distance from all boundaries to ensure unconstrained capture of the entire plantar pressure profile. RA 2 will be responsible for the iBalance testing, with the following instruction: “Please step barefoot onto the center of each glass plate without touching any of the boundaries, then stand as still as possible looking straight ahead for 30 seconds. We will repeat this twice, first with your eyes open, and then with them closed. If you are ready, let’s begin.”

During a standardized 30-second standing test, the sensor records dynamic foot pressure data at a sampling frequency of 30 Hz. The primary output of the device is a set of CoP-based metrics. These metrics are subsequently analyzed by machine learning and deep learning algorithms to generate a probability of fall risk. To protect participants’ safety during the experiment, two handrails will be placed next to the device, and RAs will closely supervise the participant at all times.

Survey data collection

After the iBalance assessment, participants will complete a survey covering health-related quality of life, the Fall Risk Assessment Scale for the Elderly (FRASE), reasons for falls, and other relevant factors. Upon completion, the RA 1 will provide participants with incentives, and RA 2 will deliver a personalized fall risk report based on their balance trajectory data from the iBalance. Additionally, during the three-month follow-up period, the RA 3 will conduct telephone interviews to collect information on participants’ fall incidents and reasons for any falls that occurred since the baseline assessment.

Qualitative interview

After the baseline quantitative data collection, clinical professionals will be invited to attend a 45 to 60-minute personal interview to discuss the better application of the device in clinical settings by RA 3.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes will be the sensitivity and specificity results of falling risk screening accuracy from our device (iBalance) and the clinical assessments (e.g., FRASE). Participants’ health-related factors will also be collected.

Fall risk assessment by iBalance

The data from iBalance is processed through three methods: (a) Machine learning method. It extracts three-dimensional features, including center of pressure (CoP)-based measures and center of gravity (CoG)-based measures, which are then used to train a machine learning model to calculate fall probability. (b) Deep learning method. It directly feeds the video data into a convolutional neural network (CNN) to generate a learning model to produce fall probability. (c) Statistical method. It optimally selects the CoP and CoG features to calculate the maximum AUC and then provides fall classification.

Fall Risk Assessment Scale for the Elderly (FRASE)

FRASE will be utilized in this study as a primary clinical comparator. It includes various fall risk variables, including gender, age, gait, sensory deficit, fall history, medication, medical history, and mobility. Each fall risk variable is weighted between 0 and 3 representing the degree of each variable contributes to an older adult’s risk of falling. The higher the numerical score the higher the risk of falling. Based on the sum of the score, three categories will be identified: low risk (3 to 8), medium risk (9 to 12), and high risk (13 and above) [6, 8]. A comparison of the sensitivity and specificity of our iBalance and the FRASE will be conducted using participants’ fall history as the reference standard.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will encompass the identification of clinical predictors for fall risk status and the 3-month prospective fall incidence to explore the device’s utility. Additionally, the ideas from clinical professionals on the better application of the device in clinical settings will also be regarded as the secondary outcome. These themes include ease of use, time efficiency, space requirements, barriers, and improvement suggestions.

Health-related characteristics

Demographics

Age (years), gender (female vs. male), and educational attainment (years) will be collected during the survey.

Physiological characteristics

Blood pressure, pulse, height (cm), weight (kg), and lower leg length (cm) will be collected by our research assistant using the machine in the clinics. Height and weight will be standardized during our data analysis.

Fall-related factors

Fall-related factors, serving as the reference standard in this study, will be assessed using a standardized definition from the World Health Organization (WHO), which defines a fall as an event resulting in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground, floor, or other lower level [2].

Retrospective data (12-month fall history) will be used to evaluate the device’s performance in identifying older adults with a known history of falls, while prospective data (3-month follow-up) aims to examine the correlation between initial screening results and subsequent fall incidents. This approach focuses on the device’s feasibility and utility as a rapid risk-stratification tool in primary care settings. Both retrospective and prospective fall-related factors will collect the number of falls, the severity, and the reason for falling incidents.

Medical history of the participants will also be collected in the retrospective data (e.g., the history of the emergency, surgery, and diagnosed disease in the past three years).

The 5-level EQ-5D version (EQ-5D-5 L)

The EQ-5D-5 L is a widely recognized generic multi-attribute health utility tool designed to evaluate five key dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is assessed using a five-level response scale, ranging from “no problems” to “extreme problems”. The resulting scores range from 0 to 1, with higher scores reflecting better health-related quality of life. A self-rated health score for the day, assessed on a scale from 0 to 100, follows the evaluation of these five dimensions. Higher scores indicate better self-rated health for the day.

Five Times Sit-To-Stand

To align with standard geriatric clinical practice, we will also record supplementary physical performance measures, which will serve as complementary indicators of the participants’ physical performance alongside the FRASE assessment. During the Five Times Sit-to-Stand test, participants will be instructed to sit on a standard-height chair with knees flexed at 90° and arms crossed over the chest. They will be asked to stand up fully and sit down five times consecutively “as quickly as possible” without upper-limb assistance. A completion time exceeding 12 s was classified as having fall risk [27, 28].

4-Meter Walking

Participants will be asked to walk a 4-meter distance at their usual pace. Time will be measured from the first foot movement to when both feet crossed the finish line. Gait speed (m/s) < 1.0 indicates elevated fall risk in older adults [27].

Functional Reach Test

The Functional Reach Test (FRT) is a valuable method for detecting frailty and a high risk of falls among elderly individuals [29]. A hip strategy can evaluate anterior-posterior (AP) stability by determining the maximum distance a participant can reach forward with their arms extended while standing on a stable base. The test includes both single-hand and double-hand conditions. On the other hand, an ankle strategy is also incorporated into the assessment. In this approach, participants are instructed to perform the FRT by leaning forward as far as possible using only movement at the ankle joint, while keeping the rest of the body straight and avoiding bending at the hips or knees. This method specifically evaluates the contribution of ankle joint control to AP stability during the FRT. Two practice and 2 recorded trials are completed, with the average being the overall score.

Data analysis

The quantitative analysis will evaluate the screening accuracy of the iBalance device relative to the reference standard (fall history), with the FRASE scale as a primary clinical comparator, and other physical performance tests (such as 4-Meter Walking, Five Times Sit-to-Stand, and Functional Reach Test) as supplementary clinical comparators. iBalance device will be evaluated through separate and pairwise comparisons with each clinical measure (e.g., iBalance vs. FRASE; iBalance vs. 4-Meter Walking) rather than as a combined index. Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses will be conducted between these measures and the FRASE score to contextualize the tool’s performance within the broader clinical framework.

The sensitivity and specificity for iBalance and clinical assessments (e.g., FRASE scale) will be calculated alongside their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves will be plotted, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) will be computed with 95% CI using R (version 4.2.2). To address potential multicollinearity in subsequent multivariable modeling from correlated measures (e.g., CoP and CoG), Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) diagnostics will be applied prior to modeling. Variables yielding a VIF > 10 will be managed through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or the prioritization of the most clinically relevant metric to ensure model stability and the integrity of the screening results.

The ROC and AUC comparison is intended to objectively quantify which tool (iBalance device or clinical assessment) exhibits greater potential to discriminate between fall risk by evaluating their trade-off between the true-positive rate and the false-positive rate across all decision thresholds [30, 31]. Covariate-adjusted ROC curves will be fitted using logistic regression models, incorporating age, gender, and body mass index (BMI). These covariates were selected as they represent critical fall-related confounders known to independently influence older adults populations [32, 33]. To mitigate the risk of overfitting and ensure the generalizability of the analytical models, a stratified 10-fold cross-validation approach will be employed [34]. The difference between these adjusted AUCs will be tested for significance using a bootstrap approach with 5000 replicates. Missing data will be analyzed for its pattern, and if data is deemed missing at random and exceeds 5% for any key variable, multiple imputation will be employed through Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) to handle the incomplete datasets with five imputations [35], incorporating all key demographics and outcome measures. The primary analysis will be conducted on the pooled imputed dataset, with a sensitivity analysis performed on the complete cases to verify the robustness of the findings.

Secondary analyses will first utilize binary logistic regression to identify physiological and medical predictors (e.g., blood pressure, medical history) associated with the iBalance-derived fall risk categories (high vs. low). Additionally, the predictive potential of these categories will be evaluated against 3-month follow-up data using logistic regression for fall occurrence (Yes/ No) and Poisson regression for fall frequency.

Additionally, this study will employ qualitative analysis to gather insights from ten purposely selected clinical professionals regarding the device’s implementation in clinical settings. Data will be collected through semi-structured interviews, guided by a pre-defined interview guideline (see Additional file 3). The interview guideline will include open-ended questions, including (a) perceived utility of the iBalance device, (b) integration into clinical workflow, (c) potential barriers and facilitators, and (d) suggestions for improvement. All interviews will be audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and managed using qualitative data management NVivo software.

Following the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ), this qualitative study will undergo independent double-coding by two researchers to establish inter-coder reliability through consensus meetings [36]. These two independent researchers (RA 1 and RA 3) will follow six steps of thematic analysis, including iterative familiarization, coding, theme development, rigorous validation against the data at multiple levels, analytical refinement, and final reporting to convey the identified patterns meaningfully [37]. To enhance rigor, member checking will be conducted, allowing professionals to provide feedback on the findings to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the thematic framework [36].

The initial quantitative phase will evaluate the screening efficacy of the iBalance device through sensitivity, specificity, and AUC analysis. This will be followed by a qualitative phase involving semi-structured interviews with professionals to explore the device’s clinical application and feasibility. Integration occurs at the interpretation level, where technical performance metrics and implementation insights are synthesized to assess the overall clinical utility of device in primary care settings.

Safety considerations

This study involves minimal risk, comparable to daily life activities. Participants may experience a little bit tired during testing but are encouraged to request breaks at any time. Their participation is voluntary, and they may withdraw at any time without penalty.

A rigorous adverse event reporting procedure has been established. Any incident, including actual falls, near-falls, or significant physical distress, will be immediately documented in a standardized incident report by the research assistant. Such events will be reported to the Principal Investigator and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) within 24 h to ensure participant safety and study integrity.

To avoid fall risks during the experiment, all assessments will be conducted with safety handrails and supervised by RAs trained in standardized fall prevention. RAs will be present with participants during testing to provide immediate support without disrupting the experiment.

Data management, monitoring, and access

After data entry, hardcopies of anonymized data forms will be stored in a locked cabinet at the University of Hong Kong. Electronic data will be saved on a password-encrypted university server, with a cloud-based backup. Participants will be informed that the institutional review board (IRB), ethics committee, and designated investigators may access source data for monitoring. The principal investigator will oversee data safekeeping during and after the study. Personal and study data will be retained for 10 years, after which personal data will be permanently deleted. Also, participants will be informed that results will be shared through publications, registries, conferences, and participant summaries, following all data-sharing guidelines.

Discussion

This protocol outlines a comprehensive mixed-methods approach to evaluate the screening accuracy of the iBalance device. Unlike existing studies on body-fixed sensors and subjective measurements that often lack rigorous validation, this study evaluates screening accuracy by benchmarking sensor-derived Center of Pressure (CoP) and Center of Gravity (CoG) measures directly against the established FRASE scale.

A primary methodological strength is the exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, which extends beyond technical validation to encompass clinical feasibility. By integrating the perspectives of clinical professionals, the findings aim to provide evidence-based insights into the potential for objective sensor technology to be practically integrated into multidisciplinary primary care workflows. Furthermore, the pre-specification of this protocol enhances research transparency and minimizes potential reporting bias by outlining the analytical framework prior to data collection.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The recruitment from clinics may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to acute care or community-dwelling populations with different risk profiles. Additionally, while the use of a 12-month retrospective fall history is a standard benchmark for screening validation, it primarily reflects concurrent risk rather than long-term prognosis. To address this, the inclusion of a 3-month prospective follow-up serves as an exploratory step toward understanding the device’s predictive potential.

Future research should focus on multicenter validation across diverse clinical environments to further examine the device’s robustness. The findings from this study have the potential to provide a critical foundation for refining objective fall risk stratification and guiding the implementation of gerontechnology in primary care settings.

Conclusion

This protocol outlines the plan for a mixed-methods study designed to rigorously evaluate the iBalance device, a sensor-based measurement for assessing fall risk in older adults. Its primary objective is to determine the screening accuracy of iBalance against the established clinical fall risk assessments (e.g., FRASE scale) in the clinics. Its methodological innovation lies in the integration of quantitative sensitivity and specificity testing with qualitative interviews into clinical implementation. The study is designed to generate robust evidence regarding the device’s performance and practical application. To conclude, this study will provide comprehensive evidence to inform future decisions on the adoption and implementation of objective fall risk assessment tools in clinical settings.
