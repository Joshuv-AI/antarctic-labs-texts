Ramadan fasting is associated with reduced penetrating trauma and increased peptic ulcer perforation: a 10-year retrospective analysis
Authors: Merve Tokocin, Turan Pehlivan, Hakan Yiğitbaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Ramadan fasting alters neuroendocrine rhythms, nocturnal gastric acid secretion, and population-level social behaviour. Its concurrent impact on acute gastrointestinal complications and on community-level violent trauma in high-volume urban settings remains poorly quantified.OBJECTIVES: To evaluate the impact of Ramadan fasting on the frequency of surgical admissions for penetrating trauma, blunt trauma, peptic ulcer perforation (PUP), and acute appendicitis over a 10-year period.DESIGN: Retrospective chart reviewSETTINGS: İstanbul Bağcılar Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences Türkiye (Tertiary Academic Referral Hospital).MATERIALS AND METHODS: Emergency admissions requiring surgical intervention between 2016 and 2025 were reviewed. Ramadan periods were compared with equivalent non-Ramadan periods using Poisson regression with robust standard errors, reporting incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals. Matched calendar-month, ±30-day adjacent-window, and season-stratified sensitivity analyses were performed. Bonferroni-adjusted P values are also reported; primary conclusions are based on uncorrected values (threshold P<.05).MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Comparative frequencies of penetrating trauma and PUP admissions during Ramadan versus non-Ramadan periods; secondary outcomes were blunt trauma and acute appendicitis admissions.SAMPLE SIZE: 20 968 emergency cases meeting the inclusion criteria (12 162 males, 8806 females).RESULTS: Cases comprised 1549 penetrating trauma, 3619 blunt trauma, 776 PUP, and 2920 acute appendicitis. Penetrating trauma admissions were significantly lower during Ramadan [9.7 (2.8) vs 13.2 (2.6) cases per month; IRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.58–0.93; P=.049], predominantly among males (P=.021) and during evening and early morning hours. PUP admissions were significantly higher during Ramadan [10.5 (4.6) vs 6.1 (2.3); IRR 1.72, 95% CI 1.24–2.39; P=.041], particularly in males (P=.048), peaking in the early morning. A significant pandemic-period interaction was identified for PUP [P=.031 (interaction test)], the excess being larger in 2022–2025. No significant differences were observed for blunt trauma [26.4 (3.2) vs 30.5 (3.1); P=.201] or acute appendicitis [22.5 (3.1) vs 24.5 (3.0); P=.392].CONCLUSION: Ramadan fasting is associated with a reduced admission frequency of penetrating trauma and an increased admission frequency of PUP, predominantly in male patients, highlighting opportunities for targeted prevention and resource planning. Multicentre prospective studies incorporating individual fasting status are warranted.LIMITATIONS: Single-centre design. Potential unmeasured confounders, including changes in referral patterns or the COVID-19 pandemic, cannot be excluded.Keywords: Ramadan, fasting, penetrating trauma, peptic ulcer perforation, emergency surgery

INTRODUCTION

Ramadan fasting involves abstinence from food and fluids from dawn to sunset, leading to significant behavioural and physiological changes. Alterations in daily activity patterns, sleep cycles, hydration status, and medication use during Ramadan may influence the frequency and timing of emergency surgical admissions.

Previous studies evaluating emergency department use during Ramadan have reported inconsistent findings, particularly regarding trauma-related admissions. While overall emergency visits may not increase, specific mechanisms of injury—especially those related to interpersonal violence—may decline due to reduced social activity and heightened religious observance.1,2

Conversely, peptic ulcer perforation (PUP) has been consistently reported to increase during Ramadan, potentially related to prolonged fasting,3,4 dehydration,3–5 nocturnal acid hypersecretion,6,7 increased NSAID consumption, and concentrated smoking after iftar.3–7

Our study was designed as a condition-specific epidemiological survey, simultaneously examining conditions plausibly linked to Ramadan through distinct behavioural (penetrating trauma) and physiological (PUP) pathways, alongside negative controls (blunt trauma and acute appendicitis) that are not expected to be influenced by fasting. This approach, used in analogous published studies,2,8 allows demonstration of mechanistic specificity rather than a non-specific calendar effect. Acute appendicitis, whose pathophysiology is largely unrelated to short-term dietary changes, is generally unaffected by fasting and may serve as a useful negative control.9,10

This study's primary objective was to assess the effect of Ramadan fasting on the frequency of surgical admissions for penetrating trauma, blunt trauma, PUP, and acute appendicitis over a 10-year period, with additional subgroup analyses stratifying the cohort by gender and precise time of clinical presentation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective chart review was conducted at İstanbul Bağcılar Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences Türkiye, a high-volume tertiary academic referral hospital in an urban centre in Türkiye serving a predominantly Muslim population. All emergency admissions requiring operative intervention or surgical observation and management between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2025 were screened. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Bağcılar Training and Research Hospital (approval no. 0509-028). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Patients were included if they presented acutely with: penetrating trauma caused by firearm or stab wounds; blunt trauma (including motor vehicle accidents, falls, or assaults without penetrating injury); perforated gastric or duodenal ulcer confirmed radiologically or intraoperatively; or acute appendicitis confirmed histologically after appendicectomy. Patients were excluded if they were aged <18 years, had incomplete medical records, were managed non-operatively without objective diagnostic confirmation, presented with injuries related to self-harm or suicide attempt, or had non-perforated peptic ulcer disease or incidental appendicitis discovered during surgery for another indication.

Exposure definition

Ramadan dates for each year were obtained from official Islamic calendars published by the Directorate of Religious Affairs of Türkiye. The 29–30 days of Ramadan were defined as the exposure period. For comparison, case counts from the remaining 335–336 days of the same year were averaged and normalised to 30-day equivalents to allow direct comparison across years despite varying Ramadan duration.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records and included monthly normalised admission counts, patient gender, and time of emergency department presentation, categorised into three 8-hour periods: 00:00–07:59 (pre-dawn/early morning), 08:00–15:59 (daytime), and 16:00–23:59 (evening/post-iftar).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Primary analyses used Poisson regression with robust standard errors to account for overdispersion. Results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Because four primary outcome variables were pre-specified in this hypothesis-generating study, we report both uncorrected P values (primary significance threshold P<.05) and Bonferroni-adjusted P values (α=0.0125, i.e., raw P×4); the latter are provided for transparency but were not used to alter primary conclusions, consistent with the exploratory and descriptive nature of this analysis. Independent samples T-tests are also provided for comparability with prior literature. Categorical data (sex and time periods) were compared using Chi-square tests.

Three sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary exposure definition: (a) matched calendar-month comparisons, in which the non-Ramadan month with the closest calendar start to Ramadan in each year was used as comparator; (b) ±30-day adjacent-window comparisons; and (c) season-stratified analyses (spring/summer vs. autumn/winter), to account for the migration of Ramadan across seasons over the 10-year observation period.

Formal interaction tests were performed to compare Ramadan-associated effects across three periods: pre-pandemic (2016–2019), pandemic (2020–2021), and post-pandemic (2022–2025). Interaction P values are reported using standard notation [P=xxx (interaction test)], and interaction terms were modelled within the Poisson regression framework. Post-hoc power calculations confirmed >80% power for the primary overall comparisons of penetrating trauma and PUP, but not for all time-of-day subgroup analyses; potential Type II error in subgroup analyses is acknowledged. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical baseline characteristics

Over the 10-year study period (2016–2025), a total of 20 968 emergency cases meeting the inclusion criteria were analysed, comprising 12 162 male patients (58%) and 8806 female patients (42%). These included 1,549 cases of penetrating trauma (7.4%), 3619 cases of blunt trauma (17.3%), 776 cases of PUP (3.7%), and 2920 cases of acute appendicitis (13.9%).

For penetrating trauma, the 18–40-year age group predominated in both Ramadan (n=112, 73.2%) and non-Ramadan (n=1022, 73.2%) periods, with no significant between-period difference in age distribution (P=.43). For PUP, the combined 41–60 and >60 age groups were highly prevalent in both Ramadan (n=115, 69.7%) and non-Ramadan (n=426, 69.7%) cycles, with no significant between-period difference (P=.67).

Overall admission frequency and temporal trends

Penetrating trauma admission frequency occurred significantly less frequently during Ramadan than during non-Ramadan periods [mean 9.7 (2.8) vs 13.2 (2.6) cases per normalised month; IRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.58–0.93; P=.049, Bonferroni-adjusted P =.196] (Figure 1).

Annual frequency of penetrating trauma admissions (firearm and stab wounds) during Ramadan and non-Ramadan periods (2016–2025). Data are presented as mean cases per normalised 30-day month. The shaded area highlights the consistent Ramadan deficit, with a mean difference of approximately 3.5 cases/month (IRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.58–0.93; P=.049).

In contrast, PUP admission frequency was significantly more common during Ramadan [10.5 (4.6) vs 6.1 (2.3) cases per normalised month; IRR 1.72, 95% CI 1.24–2.39; P=.041, Bonferroni-adjusted P=.164] (Figure 2).

Annual frequency of peptic ulcer perforation admissions during Ramadan and non-Ramadan periods (2016–2025). The shaded area emphasises the Ramadan excess, which became markedly more pronounced from 2021 onwards [P=.031 (pandemic-period interaction test)].

No significant differences in surgical volumes were observed for the negative control conditions: blunt trauma [26.4 (3.2) vs 30.5 (3.1) cases per normalised month; IRR 0.87, 95% CI 0.71–1.05; P=.201] or acute appendicitis [22.5 (3.1) vs 24.5 (3.0) cases per normalised month; IRR 0.92, 95% CI 0.77–1.10; P=.392] (Table 1). Post-hoc power for the blunt trauma comparison was 62% and for acute appendicitis 48%, indicating potential Type II error for these outcomes.

Pandemic-period interaction

For penetrating trauma, no significant interaction between pandemic period and the Ramadan effect was detected [P=.38 (interaction test)], supporting consistency of the behavioral effect across years. For PUP, a statistically significant period interaction was identified [P=.031 (interaction test)]; the Ramadan-associated excess in PUP was modest in 2016–2019 (mean difference ∼2.1 cases/month), attenuated during the pandemic years 2020–2021, and markedly increased in 2022–2025 (mean difference ∼7.3 cases/month). Sensitivity analyses

All three sensitivity analyses (matched calendar months, ±30-day adjacent windows, season-stratified) yielded directionally consistent results for both penetrating trauma (IRR range 0.69–0.78) and PUP (IRR range 1.58–1.89). No significant season × Ramadan interaction was detected (all P>.15), indicating that the observed effects are not primarily attributable to seasonal confounding. Results are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

Time-of-day distribution

Penetrating trauma showed significant structural reductions during Ramadan in the early morning (00:00–07:59; 4.1 vs 6.3 mean cases; P=.032) and evening (16:00–23:59; 8.6 vs 12.1 mean cases; P=.028) periods, with no significant difference during daytime hours (08:00–15:59; P=.289) (Table 2).

PUP demonstrated a significant volume increase during Ramadan in the early morning period (00:00–07:59; 5.2 vs 2.9 mean cases; P=.041), whereas differences in daytime (P=.912) and evening periods (P=.073) did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Sex-specific findings

The reduction in penetrating trauma admission frequency during Ramadan was confined to male patients [7.1 (2.0) vs 11.4 (2.3) cases per normalised month; P=.021], with no significant difference among females [2.6 (1.1) vs 2.8 (1.0); P=.418]. Similarly, the increase in PUP during Ramadan was statistically significant only in males [7.4 (3.1) vs 4.5 (2.0); P=.048] and not in females [3.1 (1.4) vs 2.6 (1.2); P=.291] (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This 10-year retrospective chart review demonstrates condition-specific effects of Ramadan fasting on emergency surgical presentations in a cohort of over 20 000 patients. The most notable findings were a significant and consistent reduction in penetrating trauma volume and a significant increase in PUP admissions during Ramadan, while blunt trauma and acute appendicitis remained unaffected.

The significant reduction in penetrating trauma during Ramadan, with a consistent absolute difference of approximately 3–4 cases per normalised month throughout the decade (Figure 1), suggests a stable behavioural effect rather than a transient or worsening phenomenon.2,10 This pattern may reflect sustained cultural and religious influences during the holy month, including decreased nighttime social activities, reduced interpersonal conflict, and lower alcohol consumption—factors previously associated with violence-related injuries.2,11 The effect was confined to male patients and was most pronounced in early morning (pre-sahur) and evening/post-iftar hours, further supporting a behavioural mechanism tied to altered daily routines and heightened spiritual observance during Ramadan.

The increase in PUP during Ramadan showed temporal heterogeneity: the magnitude became more pronounced from 2022 onwards, with a significant pandemic-period interaction [P=.031 (interaction test)] (Figures 2 and 3). This escalating post-pandemic trend warrants attention and may reflect evolving risk factor profiles in the urban Turkish population, including changes in NSAID use patterns, concentrated post-iftar smoking, or alterations in dietary behaviour after the pandemic. These remain hypotheses requiring prospective investigation with individual-level exposure data.

The male predominance may reflect higher baseline rates of smoking and NSAID use in this specific population. Future prospective studies should systematically capture individual NSAID use, PPI co-prescription, H. pylori status, and smoking behaviour during Ramadan to quantify the exact relative contribution of each factor.

Our findings align broadly with the international literature. Studies from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Jordan have similarly reported increased PUP admission rates during Ramadan.3,4 The reduction in penetrating trauma is consistent with reports of reduced interpersonal violence during Ramadan from predominantly Muslim countries.2,8 In contrast, blunt trauma from motor vehicle accidents has shown inconsistent patterns across regions, with some studies reporting increases and others null effects.11,12 The post-2021 escalation of PUP excess observed in our data has not been systematically reported in prior international studies and may reflect population-specific trends in urban Turkey that warrant multicentre validation.

The absence of significant Ramadan-associated changes in blunt trauma and acute appendicitis serves as an important internal validity control. Acute appendicitis, whose aetiology is largely independent of short-term dietary modification, remained stable, reinforcing the specificity of the observed effects.

Strengths of this study include the long observation period, large sample size, inclusion of negative controls, and detailed subgroup analyses by gender and time of day, which provide robust and internally consistent evidence of differential impacts. The use of normalised monthly admission counts and consistent diagnostic criteria minimises seasonal and calendar-related bias.

Limitations include the single-centre design, which may limit generalisability to other regions with different fasting durations, sociocultural practices, or baseline risk profiles. Individual fasting status was not recorded; our findings represent a calendar-period comparison rather than a verified individual fasting-status comparison. However, this is not merely a limitation; it reflects an important conceptual point: Ramadan exerts effects at the societal level that extend beyond the individual act of fasting. Even individuals who do not fast observe altered social norms during Ramadan, including restaurant closures during daylight hours, reduced evening alcohol availability, diminished nightlife activity, and widespread changes in meal timing and social interaction. These population-level behavioural shifts may themselves drive the observed reduction in penetrating trauma independently of individual fasting compliance. The calendar-period design therefore captures the aggregate public health effect of Ramadan rather than the physiological effect of fasting alone—a distinction that future studies incorporating community-level exposure data should address explicitly. Unmeasured individual-level confounders, including NSAID use, smoking prevalence, and H. pylori status, cannot be excluded. The pandemic-period interaction for PUP, while statistically significant, must be interpreted with caution given the multiple potential confounders affecting care-seeking and case-mix in 2020–2021. Subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously given post-hoc power limitations.

CONCLUSION

Ramadan fasting is associated with a consistent reduction in the frequency of penetrating trauma admissions and an increased frequency of PUP admissions, predominantly among male patients. These findings suggest distinct behavioural and physiological effects of fasting on emergency surgical presentations. Anticipating these patterns may support targeted prevention strategies and informed resource planning during Ramadan. Future multicentre prospective studies incorporating individual fasting status, medication use (NSAIDs, PPIs), smoking behaviour, and community-level mobility data are warranted to elucidate the specific mechanisms and to assess generalisability across different populations and fasting durations.
