Mast cells release DNA Extracellular Traps (DETs) in response to SARS-CoV-2 and its spike protein

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfection by SARS-CoV-2 is associated with an uncontrolled and damaging inflammatory response during severe COVID-19 disease, during which immune cells, such as neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages, release pro-inflammatory mediators leading to the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome. Mast cells may also contribute to the pathogenesis of COVID-19, as increased serum levels of their proteases are associated with the severity of the disease. Mast cells are strategically located in tissues that interface with the external environment, such as the skin, respiratory tract, and gastrointestinal mucosa, exhibiting microbicidal activities, including phagocytosis and the release of DNA embedded with granular proteins, known as DNA extracellular traps (DETs).Methods and ResultsWe show that the inactivated SARS-CoV-2 virions and the SARS-CoV-2 recombinant Spike protein induce DET formation in the human mast cell line HMC-1, with participation of TLR2 and TLR4 recognition. Using pharmacological inhibitors, we demonstrate the involvement of reactive oxygen species (ROS), NF-кB, peptidyl arginine deiminase (PAD), calcium, and serine proteases in the formation of DETs by either stimulus. DETs were toxic to pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells, with tryptase, H3 citrullinated histone, and DNA scaffold contributing to cytotoxicity by apoptosis. Exposure of infectious virions to DETs reduced SARS-CoV-2 infection in Calu-3 cells, suggesting that the viral particles were trapped and killed by DETs. Furthermore, we also detected DET structures, characterized by colocalization of tryptase, citrullinated histone H3, and DNA, in the lung biopsies of COVID-19 patients.ConclusionTaken together, our results suggest a dual role for mast cell DETs during SARS-CoV-2 infection, as they damage both pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells while capturing SARS-CoV-2 virions and inhibiting viral infection. Our findings are consistent with the assumption that mast cell DETs control the viral load by reducing SARS-CoV-2 infectivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02947-4.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has had a significant global impact due to its high transmissibility and the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe disease. Approximately 15–20% of patients with COVID-19 develop a severe infection characterized by a cytokine storm, which contributes to morbidity and mortality more than any direct viral cytotoxicity [1, 2].

During its widespread dissemination in affected organs and tissues, SARS-CoV-2 infects and interacts with immune cells, including mast cells [3, 4]. Mast cells are innate immune cells widely distributed in tissues exposed to the external environment, such as the skin, respiratory, and gastrointestinal tracts [5]. They act as sentinel cells, detecting pathogens via various pattern-recognition receptors and rapidly releasing a wide range of mediators stored in their granules, including histamine and proteases such as tryptase and chymase [5]. Upon recognizing a virus, mast cells are activated and release chemokines and cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and CXCL8, which play central roles in the cytokine storm typical of severe COVID-19 cases [5, 6]. This array of responses positions mast cells as potential amplifiers of the systemic inflammation characteristic of the disease [5, 7]. The involvement of mast cells in COVID-19 is evidenced by elevated serum levels of their proteases, which correlate with disease severity [8–12]. Moreover, histological examinations of lung tissue from COVID-19 patients have revealed increased perivascular and septal mast cell density [11, 13], suggesting a potential contribution to tissue damage or to the aggravation of lesions caused by viral infection.

We and other authors have reported that SARS-CoV-2 induces the release of extracellular traps (NETs) from neutrophils, which are cytotoxic to pulmonary epithelial cells in vitro [14, 15]. In addition, NET levels are elevated in the plasma, tracheal aspirates, and lungs of patients with severe COVID-19 [15]. Although NETs can be beneficial due to their virucidal effect or by trapping and preventing virus entry into target cells, deregulated production or defects in their clearance are detrimental to bystander cells and tissues [16]. The damaging effects of NETs were reported in patients with COVID-19 and correlated with the hallmarks of severe disease [17, 18].

While NET release is primarily described in neutrophils, other cells, such as mast cells, can also form DNA extracellular traps (DETs). The formation of DETs by mast cells shares similarities with NETs, including morphology and release mechanism. Moreover, mast cell DETs are equally formed by a DNA scaffold associated with histones and other mast cell-specific proteins, like tryptase [19]. Various stimuli have been identified as able to induce DET formation in mast cells, including bacteria and their products, protozoan parasites (Leishmania donovani and L. tropica), fungi (Candida albicans), endogenous mediators (IL-23, IL-1β, glucose oxidase), as well as chemicals [19–26]. However, to date, no studies have examined whether viral stimuli can induce DET formation in mast cells. Moreover, while NETs have been extensively implicated in the adverse outcomes of COVID-19 [15, 27–29], the potential contribution of mast cell–derived DETs to SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis remains unexplored.

To investigate replication-independent sensing mechanisms, we employed β-propiolactone–inactivated SARS-CoV-2 particles (BPL-SARS-CoV-2), which preserve viral structural integrity while abolishing infectivity. Here, we demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 and its Spike protein can induce DET formation in the human mast cell line HMC-1, the cellular origin of the DNA scaffold, and the molecular pathways involved in this process. We demonstrate that both BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and the recombinant Spike protein trigger DET release through TLR2/4-associated signaling pathways involving NF-κB, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, peptidyl arginine deiminase (PAD) activity, calcium mobilization, and serine proteases. Furthermore, we show that purified DETs exert cytotoxic effects on pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells while simultaneously reducing SARS-CoV-2 infectivity. Finally, we provide observational evidence of DET-like structures in lung biopsies from patients with COVID-19. Together, our findings identify mast cell–derived DETs induced by SARS-CoV-2 and the spike protein, suggesting that they may contribute to antiviral defense and immunopathology during SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Materials and methods

Culture of the human mast cell line HMC-1

The HMC-1 cell line, derived from a patient with mast cell leukemia, was cultured in RPMI-1640 GlutaMax (GIBCO) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, LGC) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma) at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and subcultured every 3–4 days. For the assays, HMC-1 cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Lonza) at 400×g for 10 min, resuspended in RPMI-1640 (Gibco), and used in all experiments.

Hoxb8-immortalized mast cell precursors (iMCps) from C57BL/6J mice, generated as previously described [30], were isolated by limiting dilution and cultivated in RPMI supplemented with 20% FBS, antibiotics, 2 ng/mL rmIL-3 with 100 nM 4-OHT. To induce differentiation into mature mast cells, 4-OHT was removed from iMCps and cells were cultivated at 1.5-3.0 × 105 cells/mL with full medium changes every other day. Mast cell differentiation was confirmed by the acquisition of metachromatic granules and surface expression of FceRI and CD117 after 6 to 8 days.

Culture of human pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells

The Calu-3 human pulmonary epithelial cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and were subcultured every 5–6 days. Upon reaching 90% confluence (~ 2.4 × 10⁶ cells), the cells were detached using 3 mL of trypsin (5 mg/mL, Sigma) in combination with EDTA (2 mg/mL, Sigma).

The A549 cell line was maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, and subcultured every 3 days. Upon reaching 90% confluence (~ 2.4 × 10⁶ cells), the cells were detached using 3 mL of trypsin (5 mg/mL, Sigma) in combination with EDTA (2 mg/mL, Sigma).

Human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC) kindly supplied by Dr. Julio Scharfstein (Instituto de Biofísica Carlos Chagas Filho, Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 90% confluence (~ 8.5 × 10⁵ cells), the cells were detached using 3 mL of trypsin/EDTA and subcultured every 2 days for up to 6 passages after thawing.

SARS-CoV-2 virus

The SARS-CoV-2 was kindly provided by Dr. Thiago Moreno L. Souza (Laboratory of Immunopharmacology, Instituto Oswaldo Cruz/Fiocruz, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil). The virus sample was expanded in Vero E6 cells from an isolate obtained from a confirmed case of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil (GenBank accession no. MT710714, lineage B.1 clade 20 C) and inactivated with β-propiolactone (diluted 1:10000, BPL, Sigma-Aldrich). Viral titers were determined as plaque-forming units (PFU/mL), as described below, and viral stocks were stored at -80 °C. The virus inactivation was confirmed by the absence of plaque-forming units in Vero E6 cells. The mock control consisted of BPL-treated Vero E6 cells supernatant prepared under the same viral inactivation conditions [31]. All viable virus-related procedures were performed in biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities in accordance with WHO guidelines. Inactivated SARS-CoV-2 isolates are hereinafter denominated as BPL-SARS-CoV-2.

SARS-CoV-2 spike protein synthesis

The trimeric SARS-CoV-2 spike protein was kindly provided by Dr. Leda Castilho (Laboratory of Cell Culture Engineering, Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) and purified as described [32]. Briefly, the SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein was produced in HEK293-3F6 cells (NRC) that stably express the soluble ectodomain (amino acids 1–1208) of the spike protein in its prefusion trimeric conformation [33]. Cells were cultured in HEK-GM medium (Xell AG) at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ under agitation (180 rpm, shaker with a 5 cm stroke). The protein was purified using a StrepTrap XT affinity chromatography column (Cytiva) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration, purity, and identity of the eluted protein fractions were confirmed using a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher), silver-stained SDS-PAGE, and Western blot analyses [32]. The absence of endotoxins in our spike protein samples was determined using the Pierce™ Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit. The spike protein was dissolved in PBS, which was also endotoxin-free.

DET quantification and inhibition assays

HMC-1 mast cells (2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were incubated with culture medium or with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 (MOIs 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.5), Spike protein (0.1, 0.5, and 1 µg/mL), or PMA (100 nM, Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Then, supernatants containing DETs were removed through extensive pipetting, centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min to remove residual cells, and DET DNA was quantified by the Quant-iT Picogreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 485/538 nm excitation/emission on a SpectraMax Paradigm microplate reader (Molecular Devices). The retrieved DETs were preserved at − 80 °C until further use.

DETs were also evaluated in HMC-1 cells (2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) seeded in 48-well plates and treated with or without BPL-SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 0.5), Spike protein (0.5 µg/mL), or PMA (100 nM, Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. DETs were stained with 1 µM SYTOX-Green (Molecular Probes) and analyzed in a Leica AF-DMI 6000 B epifluorescence microscope.

Assays of inhibition of DET formation were done by pre-treating HMC-1 cells for 30 min with the following reagents: diphenyleneiodonium (DPI, inhibitor of ROS production; 32 µM, Sigma-Aldrich), N-acetylcysteine (NAC, antioxidant;1 mM, Sigma-Aldrich), chloramine (ClA, PAD4 inhibitor; 12 µM, Cayman Chemical), GSK-484 (15 µM, Cayman Chemical), BAPTA-AM (10 µM, Calbiochem), nafamostat (10 nM, Cayman Chemical), NF-кB inhibitor IKK16 (5 nM, Cayman Chemical), TLR2 inhibitor C29 (10 nM, Invivogen) and TLR4 inhibitor TAK242 (10 µM, Cell Signaling) followed by stimulation with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or Spike protein as above. The viability of HMC-1 cells (1 × 10⁵) treated with the aforementioned pharmacological inhibitors was assessed using the CytoTox96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescence microscopy

HMC-1 cells (5 × 10⁵) were adhered to glass coverslips previously treated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated with or without BPL-SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 0.5) or Spike protein (0.5 µg/mL) for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4 °C, washed 3 times with PBS, and blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA (Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated for 1 h with anti-SARS-CoV-2 serum (1:800, COVID-19-positive patient serum kindly provided by Dr. André Macedo Vale (Instituto de Biofísica Carlos Chagas Filho, UFRJ), anti-citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit; 1:200, Invitrogen), anti-tryptase (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-LL-37 (1:50; Santa Cruz), and anti-β-hexosaminidase (1:50; Santa Cruz) antibodies. After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/546 secondary antibodies against human, mouse, or rabbit IgG (1:500, Invitrogen) for 1 h. DNA was stained with DAPI (10 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min, followed by 3 PBS washes. The coverslips were mounted with ProLong Gold antifade containing DAPI (Thermo Fisher). Controls were performed to show that the HMC-1 cell line expressed tryptase prior to stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 or Spike (Suppl. Figure 2 A), and secondary antibody controls were also included (Suppl. Figure 2B). Images were acquired using the Nikon C2 confocal microscope.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) assay

HMC-1 cells (1 × 10⁵) were pre-treated with or without apocynin (1 µM) in RPMI without phenol red at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ for 30 min, and subsequently stimulated with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 0.5), Spike protein (0.5 µg/mL), PMA (100 nM), or left untreated (control). ROS generation was detected using dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR, 2 µM, Sigma-Aldrich) and monitored for 3 h at 37 °C with excitation/emission at 500/536 nm on a SpectraMax Paradigm microplate reader.

DET isolation

DET-rich supernatants from HMC-1 mast cells (1 × 10⁶), obtained as described, were purified by centrifugation at 18,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C [34]. The DNA pellet was resuspended in RPMI, and the DNA concentration was determined by Picogreen as described, with a standard curve generated from salmon DNA (Sigma-Aldrich).

Cytotoxicity assays

Calu-3 cells (2 × 10⁴) were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ until confluence (~ 2 days), and then, treated or not with different concentrations of DETs induced by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or Spike protein (10, 50, 100 ng/mL) for 24 h, and maintained as above. Cell viability was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega). Cells lysed with the kit’s lysis solution were used as a positive control for cell death.

TUNEL assay

HMC-1 cells were plated on LabTek slides, fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and then fixed again with 4% formaldehyde. Cells were washed twice with PBS for 5 min before and after each fixation and permeabilization step. Fragmented nuclear DNA was detected using the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation in equilibration buffer, a reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) and fluorescein-dUTP was applied for 1 h at 37 °C, protected from light. The reaction was stopped by washing twice with the kit’s stopping solution for 15 min each. To confirm cell death, nuclear DNA fragmentation was induced by adding DNase I (10 units/mL) after fixation and permeabilization. Following TUNEL staining, cells were incubated with phalloidin 647 (1:400; Invitrogen) and Hoechst (1:1000; Invitrogen) for 15 min, and coverslips were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen) and stored at 4 °C overnight in the dark. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with LasX software.

Semi-quantitative PCR

DETs, obtained as described, were immediately treated with EDTA (0.5 mM) and proteinase K (1 mg/mL). The DNA was extracted from the supernatant using a DNA extraction kit (QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit, Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA extracted from THP1 cells was used as a positive control for PCR amplification. The concentration and purity of the isolated DNA were measured using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop One). The origin of the extracellular DNA was determined by amplifying two nuclear genes: (Fas)- 5’-TCACCACTATTGCTGGAGTCAT-3’, (Fas)-5’-TAAACATCCTTGGAGGCAGAAT-3’, Ras homolog gene family, member H (Rhoh)- 5’-ATGCTGAGTTCCATCAAGTGCGTGTTG-3’, (Rhoh)-5’- TTAGAAGATCTTGCACTC-3’; and two mitochondrial genes: ATP synthase subunit 6, (Atp6)-5’-;ATACACAACACTAAAGGACGAACCT-3’; (Atp6)-5’-GAGGCTTACTAGAAGTGTGAAAACG-3’; cytochrome oxidase c subunit 1 (Co1)-5’-GGAGTCCTAGGCACAGCTCTAA-3’; Co1- 5’- GGAGGGTAGACTGTTCAACCTG-3’ [68]. A total reaction volume of 25 µL consisted of PCR buffer (20 mM Tris-HCL, pH 8.4, and 50 mM KCL), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 200 µM dNTP (dNTP mix, Invitrogen), 100 nM of each primer, 1.0 U taq DNA polymerase recombinant (Invitrogen), and 3 µl of DNA template (~ 60 ng). After an initial denaturation step of 5 min at 95 °C, 35 cycles were carried out, each consisting of 30 s at 95 °C for denaturation, 30 s at 60 °C for annealing, and 30 s at 72 °C for extension, followed by an additional extension step of 5 min at 72 °C. The amplified PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on a 1.4% agarose gel. DNA was monitored under UV light after staining with ethidium bromide.

Evaluation of DET effects on SARS-CoV-2 infection in Calu-3 cells

Uninfected Calu-3 cells were initially seeded at a density of 6 × 10⁶ cells/well in 48-well plates and cultured for 48 h at 37 °C. At the time of the experiment, SARS-CoV-2 Delta samples (GISAID: 8433592; MOI 0.1) were treated for 10 min with 5 µg of purified DETs released by HMC-1 cells, obtained as described above. For some experiments, DETs were pre-incubated with DNase (10 U) or anti-tryptase (1 µg/mL – 1:200) for 1 h at 37 °C before mixing with the native virus. Subsequently, Calu-3 cells were inoculated with untreated native virus (MOI 0.1) or with DET-virus mixtures. After 1 h for virus adsorption, cells were washed and then supplemented with fresh medium containing 10% FBS. At 24 h post-infection, supernatants were collected for quantification of infectious particles by plaque assay.

Quantification of infectious particles by plaque assay

Titers of SARS-CoV-2 infectious particles in virus stocks and culture media from experimental assays were quantified by plaque assay using Vero E6 cells, as described [35]. Briefly, cells were incubated with 10-fold dilutions of the samples for 1 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ to allow viral adsorption. Next, the inoculum was removed and replaced with fresh DMEM with 1% FBS and 1.5% carboxymethylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich). The cultures were then maintained for 3 days at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Then, monolayers were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet to visualize and count viral plaques. Viral titers were expressed as plaque-forming units/mL (PFU/mL).

Study subjects

Lung tissue samples employed in this study were obtained from the Hospital das Clínicas, Faculty of Medicine, Universidade de São Paulo (USP), SP, Brazil, including both COVID-19 patients (n = 5) and control individuals (n = 3). Control samples consisted of individuals who died from non-pulmonary causes prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, with no previous history of lung disease or smoking. All samples were collected and provided in collaboration with the Department of Pathology at USP and processed under identical experimental conditions. For the COVID-19 group, the diagnosis was confirmed by detection of SARS-CoV-2 using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Tissue collection followed an ultrasound-guided minimally invasive autopsy protocol [36]. All specimens were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded for histological processing.

Tissue immunofluorescence

Slide sections were dewaxed and rehydrated using the xylene-ethanol series to remove paraffin and then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed using Tris-EDTA (pH 9) and citrate (pH 6) buffers at elevated temperature and pressure for 20 min each. Samples were then incubated for 1 h with a blocking solution (PBS with 3% albumin), and sections were stained with primary antibodies anti-tryptase (1:200; sc59587, Santa Cruz) and anti-histone H3 antibody (1:200; NBP3-11316, Novus Biologicals) for 18 h at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies (1:600; Goat anti-Mouse IgG1 Alexa Fluor 647, A21240; Thermo Fisher, Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 750, A21039, Thermo Fisher) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. For nuclear counterstaining, slides were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium (Aqueous, Fluoroshield, ab104139, Abcam). Image acquisition was performed using the Axioscan 7 slide scanner microscope (Zeiss, Germany), and images were obtained with Zen Desk software (Zeiss).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The student’s t-test was used to compare two groups. Comparisons among more than two groups were made using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-test. The quantitative analysis of DNA+TRYPT⁺ H3cit⁺ cells was performed in three regions of interest (ROIs) per sample, selected based on the presence of mast cell infiltrates, using QuPath software [37]. Outliers were identified using the ROUT test and data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were carried out using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

DETs triggered by β-propiolactone (BPL)-SARS-CoV-2 and spike protein

Over the past decade, the structure, function, and relevance of DETs in mast cells have been investigated in models involving interactions with bacteria, fungi, and parasites [38]. However, the ability of viruses to induce these structures in mast cells remains unknown. Thus, we investigated whether BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and its Spike protein could induce DET release from the human mast cell line HMC-1. Our results show that HMC-1 cells release DETs in response to either BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or the Spike protein at different MOIs and concentrations, respectively (Figs. 1A, B). The PMA (100 nM), used as a positive control, increased DET formation by around 3-fold relative to the control. DET extrusion was not detected in unstimulated control HMC-1 cells, which ranged from 6,829,732 to 5,191,810 arbitrary units [AU], or in mock-stimulated cells exposed to BPL-VERO E6 supernatant, which ranged from 7,418,936 to 5,483,972 AU. The release of DETs by both stimuli occurs with HMC-1 death, as measured by lactate dehydrogenase release (Figs. 1C, D). The presence of DETs was confirmed after stimulation with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 0.5) or Spike protein (0.5 µg/mL) through SYTOX-Green staining (Fig. 1E). The virus and the spike protein also induced DET release in primary HOXB8-immortalized murine mast cells and DET release by mouse mast cells also occur with cell death (Suppl. Figures 1 A-D). Murine mast cell DETs presented similar morphology after Sytox green staining as the HMC-1 DETs (Suppl. Figure 1E).

Characterization of DET components

The formation of DETs by mast cells involves the association of granular and nuclear proteins with DNA, followed by their release into the extracellular milieu [19]. Confocal microscopy analysis of the structures released by mast cells stimulated with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or Spike protein revealed a characteristic NET-like structure (Fig. 2), with DNA decorated with H3 citrullinated histone, tryptase, β-hexosaminidase and LL-37 (Figs. 2A-F). Interestingly, the virus and the Spike protein were associated with the DNA scaffold, suggesting their entrapment by DETs (Figs. 2A-F). Unstimulated HMC-1 cells stained with antibodies to both protein markers confirmed the presence of H3 citrullinated histone and tryptase prior to stimulation with virus or Spike protein (Suppl. Figure 2). Similar morphology was also observed in DETs extruded by HOXB8-immortalized murine mast cells in response to SARS-CoV-2 and Spike, whose DNA is observed decorated with β-hexosaminidase and tryptase (Suppl. Figure 1 F).

It has been shown that the DNA scaffold of DETs released by neutrophils is of mitochondrial origin [39]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was employed to investigate the origin of the DNA scaffold in DETs stimulated with BPL-SARS-CoV-2. Amplification of two mitochondrial and two nuclear genes from the DETs demonstrated the presence of both mitochondrial and nuclear genome sequences (Suppl. Figures 3 A, B).

TLR2 and TLR4 are involved in the release of DETs from mast cells stimulated by SARS-CoV-2 and the spike protein

Several studies have demonstrated that Toll-like receptors 2 (TLR2) and 4 (TLR4) are involved in cellular activation during SARS-CoV-2 infection [40–44]. Therefore, their potential role in DET release by HMC-1 mast cells stimulated with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or the Spike protein was evaluated. Our results show that pre-treatment of mast cells with the TLR2 inhibitor C29 or the TLR4 inhibitor TAK242 reduced DET release induced by either stimulus to levels comparable to those of control cells (Figs. 3A-B). Ligation of TLR2 and TLR4 triggers NF-кB activation, and inhibition of this transcription factor decreases NET formation [45, 46]. Accordingly, pre-treatment of HMC-1 cells with IKK16, to inhibit NF-кB, decreased DET extrusion induced by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike (Fig. 3C). The concentrations of C29, TAK242 and IKK16 used in the experiments were not toxic to the mast cells (Fig. 3D). Moreover, SYTOX-Green staining further confirmed that DET release was suppressed when mast cells were pre-treated with TLR2, TLR4 and NF-кB inhibitors, as demonstrated by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3E).

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) contribute to DET release by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and spike protein

Previous studies have reported that DETs released by mast cells can occur in both ROS-dependent and -independent mechanisms [19, 23, 25]. Therefore, ROS production was assessed in the present model, and mast cells were stimulated with BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or the Spike protein. ROS levels were measured at various time points using the dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) probe. Our results show that both stimuli significantly increased ROS production in HMC-1 cells (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, when the mast cells were pretreated with apocynin, a NADPH oxidase inhibitor, the ROS production induced by both stimuli was reduced (Fig. 4A, B). To evaluate the role of ROS in DET release induced by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein, mast cells were pre-treated with diphenyliodonium (DPI, a flavoprotein inhibitor) or N-acetylcysteine (NAC, an antioxidant), and DETs were measured. Our results show that both DPI and NAC blocked DET release induced by either stimulus (Figs. 4C, D), evidencing that DET triggered by the virus and by the Spike protein is a ROS-dependent mechanism. As expected, the release of DET triggered by PMA was blocked by both inhibitors. Mast cell viability assays showed that DPI and NAC were not toxic to HMC-1 cells (Fig. 4E). Sytox Green staining confirmed that DET release stimulated by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein was inhibited by pre-treatment with DPI and NAC (Fig. 4F).

Peptidyl Arginine Deiminase (PAD) contributes to DET release induced by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 and spike protein

PAD is a calcium-dependent enzyme that citrullinates histones, contributing to chromatin decondensation, a key process for NET production [47]. Thus, we investigated whether DET release induced by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein depends on mast cell PAD activity. Pretreatment of HMC-1 cells with chloramidine (Cl-A), a PAD pan-inhibitor, significantly inhibited DET release in response to both stimuli (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the PAD-4-specific inhibitor GSK-484 (GSK) blocked DET extrusion in response to both stimuli (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that DET release induced by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein depends on PAD enzymes. Cell viability assays demonstrated that the inhibitory concentrations of Cl-A and GSK-484 were not toxic to mast cells (Fig. 5C). SYTOX Green staining, visualized by fluorescence microscopy, and further confirmed the role of PAD enzymes in DET formation triggered by both stimuli mast (Fig. 5D).

Mast cells stimulated by SARS-CoV-2 and spike protein require calcium and serine protease for the generation of DETs

Given that PAD is implicated in DET release and is calcium-dependent, we examined whether DET induction by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein requires calcium mobilization. Pre-treatment of mast cells with BAPTA-AM, a calcium chelator, significantly reduced DET extrusion induced by both stimuli (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate that calcium is required for DET release triggered by SARS-CoV-2 and the Spike protein. To further clarify the signaling pathways involved, the role of serine proteases was evaluated using the nonspecific inhibitor nafamostat. This inhibitor completely abolished DET release, thereby confirming the involvement of serine proteases in DET formation in mast cells activated by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike protein (Fig. 6B). The concentrations of BAPTA-AM and nafamostat used in these assays were not toxic to mast cells (Fig. 6C). DETs induced by PMA, used as a control, were decreased by both inhibitors (Figs. 6A, B). Additionally, SYTOX-Green staining confirmed the requirement for calcium and serine proteases in DET formation induced by all three stimuli, as observed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 6D).

Cytotoxicity of DETs induced by BPL-SARS-CoV-2 or spike protein on host cells

Next, we investigated whether the DETs induced by SARS-CoV-2 or the Spike protein could damage lung epithelial cells. For this, Calu-3 cells were treated with different concentrations of isolated DETs for 24 h, and then, the LDH release was evaluated to assess toxicity. We found that SARS-CoV-2-DETs and Spike-DETs were toxic to Calu-3 cells at 50 ng/ml and 10 ng/ml, respectively (Figs. 7A, E). Of note, the recombinant Spike protein alone, at the concentration used to induce DETs, was not toxic to HMC-1 cells (Fig. 7E). To identify DET components potentially responsible for cytotoxicity, we first cleaved the DET scaffold with DNase for 1 h, and then exposed Calu-3 cells to the DNase-treated DETs for 24 h. Our results show 68% and 48% reductions in SARS-CoV-2- and Spike-DETs toxicity in Calu-3 cells following DNA degradation (Figs. 7B, F). A similar toxicity of both DETs was observed for A549 and HBMEC cell lines (Suppl. Figures 4 A, B and 5 A, B), as well as a reduction in cell toxicity when DNase-treated DETs were tested in A549 and HBMEC cells (Suppl Figs. 4B, F and 5B, F). Histones are known to be toxic to different cells, so we neutralized citrullinated H3 histones, and observed decreased toxicity for the three cell lines tested (Figs. 7C, G; Suppl. Figures 4 C, G and 5 C, G). Then, because tryptase was identified in both DETs, we also investigated whether serine proteases contributed to DET toxicity. Thus, DETs were pre-treated with nafamostat and then added to the cell lines. We found that inhibition of serine protease decreased Calu-3 cell death induced by SARS-CoV-2- and Spike protein-DETs by 47% and 37%, respectively (Figs. 7D, H). A similar reduction in cell toxicity was observed when nafamostat-treated DETs were added to A549 and HBMEC cells (Suppl. Figures 4D, H and 5D, H). Our results highlight the importance of the integrity of DETs’ DNA scaffold, histone and of tryptase in inducing toxicity in endothelial and epithelial cells.

DET effects on SARS-CoV-2 infection

Based on previous studies demonstrating that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) restrict HIV-1 replication in CD4 T cells [48] and macrophages [49], we next investigated whether SARS-CoV-2-induced DETs influence viral infectivity in Calu-3 cells. Native SARS-CoV-2 isolates were treated with purified SARS-CoV-2-DETs at 5 ng/mL for 10 min prior to addition to Calu-3 cells. The release of infectious virus particles was quantified after 24 h using plaque assays. Exposure to DETs resulted in a 60% reduction in SARS-CoV-2 titer (Fig. 8A). To determine which DET components contributed to this reduction in viral infectivity, viable SARS-CoV-2 particles were exposed to DETs pre-treated with DNase or a neutralizing antibody targeting tryptase. Cleavage of the DNA scaffold or neutralization of tryptase nearly eliminated the inhibitory effect of DETs on SARS-CoV-2 infectivity (Fig. 8B). In contrast, neither DNase nor the anti-tryptase antibody alone affected SARS-CoV-2 infectivity.

DETs in COVID-19 lung biopsies

The occurrence of NETs in the lungs of COVID-19 patients [15] prompted an investigation into whether DETs could also be detected in this context. Accordingly, lung biopsies from COVID-19 patients (n = 5) revealed DET structures, characterized by the colocalization of tryptase, citrullinated histone H3, and DNA (Figs. 9A, B). In the control group composed of individuals who died from non-pulmonary causes prior to the pandemic, with no history of lung disease or smoking, mast cells were observed stained by tryptase only. The quantitative analysis of DNA+TRYPT⁺ H3cit⁺ cells revealed a statistically significant increase in DETs within the lung tissue of COVID-19 patients (Fig. 9C). This observation extends previous reports of DET formation in atherothrombosis [50] and psoriasis [20].

Discussion

The initial infection with SARS-CoV-2 activates innate immune cells, including mast cells, located in the airways and nasal mucosa, stimulating the secretion by these cells of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as histamine, tryptase, IL-1β, CCL2, IL-6, GM-CSF, and TNF-α, thus contributing to amplify the detrimental tissue effects of the COVID-19 cytokine storm [6, 51]. Although it is known that the number of mast cells is increased in the lungs of COVID-19 patients [7–11] and that their mediators, tryptase, chymase, and carboxypeptidase A3, are associated with disease severity [51, 52], the role of these cells in COVID-19 pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Notably, none of these studies analyzed the mast cells DNA extracellular traps (DETs) in this disease.

It is well established that NETs exacerbate damage in several tissues affected by SARS-CoV-2 infection, thereby playing a key role in COVID-19 pathogenesis [15, 53, 54]. Because other cells release DNA extracellular traps (DETs) upon stimulation with the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein [55], we investigated whether mast cells could extrude DETs upon contact with SARS-CoV-2 or the Spike protein. Our results show that mast cells release DNA extracellular traps (DETs) in response to both stimuli, suggesting that these DETs may contribute to the pathogenesis of COVID-19.

The mechanisms by which host cells recognize and respond to viruses and their components are crucial for controlling virus dissemination and preventing tissue damage. However, host cell overreaction, such as macrophage activation syndrome and the massive formation of NETs during COVID-19, contributes to a strong proinflammatory response and severe tissue lesions [56, 57]. Therefore, stimulation of mast cells by SARS-CoV-2 or its viral components would coincide with a broad activation of innate immune cells, thereby expanding the source of inflammatory mediators in tissue lesions during COVID-19 [58].

In contrast to neutrophils, in which SARS-CoV-2–induced NET formation requires active viral replication [15], we found that mast cell DETs can be triggered by inactivated virus and by purified Spike protein. In addition, we evidenced that toll-like receptors (TLR) 2 and 4, previously implicated in the recognition of SARS-CoV-2 and Spike [40–44], were involved in this process, as DET extrusion was reduced upon treatment with TLR2 and TLR4 inhibitors. It has been demonstrated that ligation of TLR2 and TLR4 triggers NF-кB activation, and inhibition of this transcription factor decreases NET formation [45, 46]. We found that DET levels released by mast cells drop significantly when NF-кB is inhibited. This suggests that stimulation through membrane receptors TLR2 and TLR4 contributes to DET release, thereby offering a mechanistic explanation for the replication-independent pathway we observed. It has been shown that the classical NET release mechanism is reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent for most of the stimuli and ends with neutrophil death [59]. Similar to classical NET release, SARS-CoV-2 and the Spike protein induce DET extrusion in mast cells in a ROS-dependent manner, leading to cell death during this process.

During NET formation, histones undergo modifications that contribute to chromatin decondensation. In neutrophils, depending on the activating stimulus, the enzyme peptidyl arginine deiminase (PAD) plays a role in this process by citrullinating arginine residues into citrulline on histones, with PAD-4 being the most studied isoform [60]. It has also been reported that PAD inhibition decreased NET release induced by SARS-CoV-2 in neutrophils [15]. Here, we used two PAD inhibitors and found that they reduced the release of mast cell DETs when stimulated by SARS-CoV-2 and the Spike protein. Thus, our results provide evidence of PAD involvement in mast cell DET release. These results are consistent with the inhibition of DETs’ extrusion following calcium chelation, as the activity of these enzymes is calcium-dependent [61, 62].

Mast cells contain various serine proteases, such as tryptase, which has been identified as a granule component associated with mast cell DETs [19]. However, their role in DETs’ extrusion remains unknown. Therefore, we evidenced the participation of serine proteases in DET formation, showing that nafamostat inhibited DET release induced by SARS-CoV-2 and Spike.

During the NET release process, elastase migrates from azurophilic granules to the nucleus, where it cleaves histones, contributing to chromatin decondensation [63]. Notably, tryptase has been identified in the nuclei of murine mast cells and human primary skin mast cells, and it has been shown to cleave histones and to be stabilized by DNA [64]. Although other serine proteases cannot be excluded, we hypothesize that nafamostat targets tryptase, as it is the most abundant enzyme in human mast cells [65], and propose that this enzyme may play a role similar to elastase in histone cleavage and chromatin decondensation in mast cells.

Interestingly, it has been reported that some neutrophil stimuli induced NETs composed of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA [39, 66, 67]. DETs cast by mast cells stimulated by SARS-CoV-2 present both DNAs, as evidenced by the amplification of two mitochondrial and two nuclear genes present in the DETs. Mitochondrial DNA, owing to its similarity to bacterial DNA, serves as a significant inflammatory damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) implicated in a variety of diseases [67, 68].

Histological examinations of lung biopsies from COVID-19 patients revealed NETs implicated in various tissue-damaging effects, including toxicity to endothelial and alveolar cells [69–71], and direct NET damage to A549 lung cells was shown in vitro [15]. Here, we revealed that DETs induced by the virus and its Spike protein damage in A549, Calu-3, and HBMEC cells. We further demonstrated that the integrity of DETs’ DNA scaffold is responsible for some of this harmful effect, as DNase treatment decreased toxicity in those cells. Also, the antibody neutralization of citrullinated H3 histone and tryptase reduced DETs’ injury effects in all the cells tested; ascribing toxicity to DETs’ associated tryptase. Although we did not test the toxicity from other histones present in these DETs, they probably contribute to the host cell toxicity since it was reported that they induce epithelial and endothelial cell death in a mouse model of LPS-induced acute lung injury and damage bronchial epithelial and endothelial cells, causing alveolar and capillary injury leading to ARDS [72].

We also demonstrated that DETs trapped SARS-CoV-2, and that this interaction decreased its infection in Calu-3 cells, a process mediated by DNA scaffold integrity and DETs’ tryptase. SARS-CoV-2 trapping by NETs had also been previously reported, and virus or PMA–induced NETs exhibited a virucidal effect, inhibiting both intracellular viral replication and viral infectivity [73]. Similarly, we have shown that NETs restrict HIV replication in macrophages and that their antiviral activity is abolished by DNase or protease treatment [49]. Although our data support a direct effect of DETs in reducing virion infectivity, we cannot exclude the possibility that DETs may also act directly on target cells, modulating viral replication through additional, still unidentified pathways that are apparently not related to cell death. Overall, our findings support the idea that mast cell DETs are important mediators in viral load control by reducing viral infectivity (Fig. 10).

Importantly, we identified higher DETs in COVID-19 than in non-COVID-19 lung biopsies. While they may not be as extensive as NETs, they are similar to those already described in atherothrombosis [50] and psoriasis [20]. DETs play a significant role in virus infectivity, and an overproduction of these structures can result in tissue damage. Advancing infection control and boosting effective immunity could benefit from understanding mast cell responses to viral infections, as the ability to induce DET extrusion and the mechanisms underlying the harmful host response will be important for developing more effective therapies [74].

We recognize that our study has limitations, such as the use of a human mast cell line. Although this cell line shares similarities with primary human mast cells, we acknowledge that further investigations using primary human mast cells would enhance the robustness of our results and provide translational relevance of our findings. Because SARS-CoV-2 recognition by immune cells may occur through many receptors [75], the potential involvement of some of them in DET induction by the virus and the Spike protein warrants further investigation. We also understand that our in vitro results need to be consolidated in an animal model of SARS-CoV-2 infection, which will confer translational significance to our findings.
