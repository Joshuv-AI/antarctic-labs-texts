Prediction of 180-day post-admission’s GOS in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage based on explainable machine learning technology
Authors: Cuijie Yuan, Mengyao Wang, Yafang Jiang, Hongwu Li, Chengpeng Zhan, Lijuan Xu, Xiafei Zhou, Guofeng Yu, Xinjiang Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Effective outcome prediction is crucial in the management of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (SICH). This study developed machine learning models to predict the 180-day Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score using clinical parameters and identify key prognostic factors. All patients with SICH were randomized into training and internal validation cohortsin a 7:3 ratio. After the most relevant variables were selected by the SVM-RFE and LASSO algorithms, the predictive efficiency of the gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), XGBoost, random forest (RF), and lightgbm models was evaluated through an exhaustive suite of performance indicators. The Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) methods were engaged to explain the top-performingmodel. The model was developed for 215 patients. The GBDT model showed stable performance in predictive power in both the internal and external validation cohorts, with the following metrics assessed in the internal test set: AUROC (0.865 ± 0.069); AUPRC (0.834 ± 0.090); accuracy (0.822 ± 0.065), F1 (0.716 ± 0.114); precision (0.787 ± 0.147); recall (0.676 ± 0.138); sensitivity (0.902 ± 0.070) specificity, (0.787 ± 0.147). The GBDT model was assessed in the outer test set with the following metrics: AUROC: 0.785 (0.752, 0.832), AUPRC: 0.693 (0.649, 0.762), accuracy: 0.770 (0.739, 0.807), F1: 0.769 (0.738, 0.806), precision: 0.769 (0.738, 0.806), recall: 0.770 (0.739, 0.807), sensitivity: 0.826 (0.793, 0.863), Specificity: 0.710 (0.662, 0.761). We found that the blood platelet count, serum calcium level, and hemorrhage in the left occipital lobe, right temporal lobe, and right parietal lobe were the 5 most important features for GOS prediction in the GBDT and RF models. The outcomes of SHAP and LIME were consistent with those of previous studies. The GBDT model exhibited the best prediction performance. Moreover, it has the potential to aid clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and guiding clinical decision making.

1. Introduction

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (SICH) is a nontraumatic rupture of a blood vessel in the brain parenchyma that results in blood accumulation within the brain parenchyma. It is the second most common stroke subtype after ischemic stroke and accounts for 10 to 15% of all stroke cases. The disease is highly dangerous, progresses rapidly, and has a poor prognosis, with an acute-phase fatality rate of 40 to 50%. Approximately 80% of patients develop severe neurological deficits, leading to long-term loss of independence, which imposes a significant economic burden on society and families.[1–4] Early diagnosis and timely intervention can reduce mortality rates. Owing to poor lifestyle habits and dietary patterns, an increasing number of individuals develop underlying diseases, contributing to a marked increase in SICH incidence, even among younger populations. Therefore, exploring more effective prevention and treatment strategies for hemorrhagic stroke is critical for public health.

The Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) is a scoring system used to assess patient recovery after brain or other neurological injuries.[5,6] It consists of multiple scales ranging from death to satisfactory recovery, with each scale reflecting the patient’s functional status and quality of life. The primary purpose of the GOS is to provide a standardized tool for physicians and patients to evaluate recovery progress, guide treatment decisions, and plan rehabilitation.[7,8] This system assesses not only physical function but also the patient’s ability to resume work, study, and self-care. Through the GOS, healthcare professionals can monitor recovery and adjust treatment or rehabilitation plans to optimize outcomes. Thus, the GOS serves as a quantitative tool for prognostic evaluation in this study.

Machine learning has emerged as a powerful artificial intelligence tool that has been widely adopted in healthcare data analysis to improve clinical strategies. Machine learning algorithms can automatically extract patterns from input data to predict outcomes and identify trends.[9,10] A statistical review of machine learning in medical diagnosis demonstrated that these techniques help medical professionals reduce diagnostic errors, improve healthcare delivery, and lower treatment costs. Many researchers have made significant breakthroughs by applying machine-learning algorithms to SICH.[11,12] However, previous studies were limited by the use of finite databases for model construction, which constrained the generalizability of the model. Real-world studies,[13] which are more clinically relevant, inherently feature large datasets and strong external validity. Therefore, the incorporation of real-world clinical data is crucial for the development of superior predictive models.

The objective of this study was to develop a clinically practical prediction model for reliably estimating 180-day GOS outcomes in patients with SICH adhering to modern best practices. We compared the performance of multiple machine learning algorithms in terms of their predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. Ultimately, we seek to provide clinicians with valuable references to facilitate the early identification of patients with SICH at a high mortality risk, enabling timely implementation of targeted preventive measures.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of data from patients admitted to the Quzhou Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between April 1, 2021, and October 1, 2023. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with protocol approval from the hospital’s ethical board (approval number: LW2023-163; the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Quzhou City People’s Hospital waived the need for informed consent). The study posed no risk to the participants. Patient selection followed strict criteria: first-ever stroke, SICH confirmed by cranial CT, hospitalization within 24 hours of symptom onset, and age ≥ 18 years. We excluded patients with secondary hemorrhage due to coagulation disorders, hemorrhagic transformation of cerebral infarction, Moyamoya disease, vascular malformations, or tumors; primary intraventricular hemorrhage; preexisting neurological conditions (brain tumors, severe head trauma); or severe systemic diseases, including malignancies, immunodeficiency syndromes, or major organ dysfunction.[14]

2.2. Data collection and data processing

All 215 patients presenting to the emergency department underwent a head CT scan confirming SICH diagnosis, performed according to the radiology department protocol. Radiologists interpreting the scans were blinded to the clinical information. To ensure real-world data accuracy, we collected extensive baseline patient characteristics (51 total, detailed in S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q657) and imaging features (assessed at 6H, 24H, 72H, etc). Two senior physicians reviewed all CT scans until hematoma and edema were fully evaluated. S1 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q657) summarizes the 38 imaging characteristics.

Two methods were used to calculate the hematoma and cerebral edema volumes: For cerebral hemorrhage CT images, the hematoma boundary was outlined using an image browser measurement software. This software automatically calculated each slice’s hematoma area, summed all layers to determine the total hematoma volume, and then outlined the edema boundary. The same method was applied to calculate the combined volume of the hematoma and edema, with the surrounding brain edema volume obtained by subtracting the hematoma volume. We analyzed hematoma and cerebral edema using 3D Slicer (v4.2.2; https://www.slicer.org/) with 2 coauthors blinded to the clinical data. Semi-automatic outlining of edema and hematoma was performed using a level tracing tool to generate 3-dimensional datasets. The threshold ranges were 44 to 100 Hounsfield units (HU) for hematoma and 5 to 33 HU for edema. Absolute volumes were assessed at each time point. The primary outcome measure for edema growth was prespecified as >6 mL absolute increase or >33% relative increase in edema volume between baseline and repeat imaging (<72 hours).[15–18]

Two senior physicians assessed each patient’s GOS 180 days post-admission. In cases of scoring discrepancy, a third physician independently evaluated the case, and the final score was determined by consensus among all 3 evaluators. For data preprocessing, missing data patterns were first analyzed across the dataset. Variables containing missing values underwent imputation using k-nearest neighbors (KNN) and random forest (RF) algorithms, where target variables served as supervisory labels. Models were trained on complete data subsets to predict and impute missing values. The imputed dataset was then randomly stratified into the training and validation cohorts at a 7:3 ratio.

2.3. Feature selection

We employed a rigorous variable selection approach to identify the most relevant predictors for building a predictive model using a training cohort to avoid data leakage. First, we assessed the collinearity of clinical variables using pairwise Pearson correlation matrices. Collinearity occurs when 2 or more predictors exhibit a strong correlation (R > 0.8), which complicates the evaluation of each variable’s unique contribution to the outcome. Consequently, we removed more readily available variables from the collinear pairs. Next, we applied a two-step variable selection process using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithms. LASSO is a regularization technique that performs variable selection and coefficient estimation by constraining the sum of the absolute values of model parameters. This process shrinks some coefficients to zero and effectively excludes them from the final model. The SVM-RFE algorithm was then used for further refinement. SVM-RFE iteratively reduces the number of features, evaluates the model performance, and ultimately identifies the optimal feature subset. This step yields another set of important predictors. Finally, we retained only the intersection of the predictors identified by both LASSO and SVM-RFE to ensure that our predictive models included the most relevant and robust variables. This combined approach enhances model accuracy and generalizability, while minimizing overfitting and the inclusion of irrelevant predictors.[19,20]

2.4. Construction of the predictive model

We utilized 4 machine learning classifiers – extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), RF, LightGBM, and gradient boosting decision trees (GBDT) – to construct predictive models for the 180-day GOS outcomes. These algorithms have been explained in detail elsewhere, and a brief summary is provided below. XGBoost, a widely used ensemble technique, employs a gradient boosting framework to combine predictions from multiple weak learners (primarily decision trees), thereby producing more accurate and robust models.[21] Similarly, RF aggregates predictions from numerous distinct decision trees through bootstrapping to improve the accuracy.[22] LightGBM is a gradient-boosting framework that uses tree-based learning algorithms and offers faster training speeds and higher efficiency through its distributed design.[23] The GBDT model iteratively reduces the residuals by calculating each step in the negative gradient direction of the residual reduction. GBDT has become popular because of its powerful, flexible, and accurate predictive capabilities for processing complex data.[24]

2.5. Model validation and Interpretability

To ensure consistency, all the models incorporated identical input variables. We performed grid and random searches to identify the optimal hyperparameters for each model using training data. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, F1 score, precision, recall, sensitivity, and specificity. Finally, we applied Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)[25] and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME)[26] to obtain consistent and locally accurate feature importance values from the best-performing model, providing deeper insight into the model behavior.

2.6. Hyperparameters tuning

Bayesian optimization was employed for model parameter tuning. The core concept involves continuously updating the posterior distribution (modeled as a Gaussian process) of an objective function through iterative sample point additions. This objective function requires only input-output specifications without knowledge of its internal structure or mathematical properties. This process continues until the posterior distribution adequately approximates the true distribution.

2.7. Statistical analysis

We performed the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to assess the normality of the distribution. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed continuous variables as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Continuous variables were compared between groups using independent sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on normality. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests, whereas paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for pre- and post-follow-up comparisons. The sample size satisfied the rule of 10 events per variable. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Python (version 3.9.0; Python Software Foundation).

4. Discussion

Hemorrhagic stroke is a life-threatening condition characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis, with acute-phase mortality rates of 40% to 50%. Patients often develop severe neurological deficits that may result in long-term disability, imposing significant economic burdens on both families and society. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment can reduce mortalitys. Therefore, we developed and validated machine learning models to predict the 180-day GOS outcomes. Using 4 algorithms (GBDT, LightGBM, RF, and XGBoost), 20 key predictors were identified. Internal and external validation demonstrated that the GBDT model performed optimally, offering clinically actionable insights.[27–29] To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate GOS prediction with machine learning algorithms incorporating hematoma and cerebral edema volume calculations, resulting in a model with enhanced clinical applicability.

Our importance analysis identified hemorrhage location (left occipital lobe, right temporal lobe, and right parietal lobe) as the most significant predictor in the 180-day GOS prediction model for patients with SICH. Previous studies[30,31] have shown that bleeding sites affect both clinical presentation and in-hospital mortality. Lobar SICH is associated with more frequent headaches, early seizures, and hemianopia than deep cerebral hematoma. Early seizures predominantly occur in parietal and temporal lobe hemorrhages, known epileptogenic zones.[32] Occipital lobe hemorrhages or lesions affecting optic radiations (which traverse the temporal and parietal lobes) commonly cause hemianopia. Headaches result from local parenchymal distention and inflammation at the hematoma site, with occipital hemorrhages specifically involving the trigeminovascular system activation. These location-dependent neurological manifestations directly impact GOS scores, confirming hemorrhage location as the model’s most critical predictive factor.

Our analysis further identified the following key predictors in the 180-day GOS prediction model: serum calcium, platelet count, prothrombin time, hypolipidemic therapy, thrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, diastolic blood pressure, triglyceride levels, and hypertension history. These findings align with those of prior studies establishing diastolic blood pressure, admission serum calcium, and platelet count as significant prognostic factors for SICH.[32–34] Elevated blood pressure and triglyceride levels contribute to vascular pathology through 3 mechanisms: structural vessel wall changes, blood cell abnormalities, and hemodynamic alterations, all potentially triggering thrombotic or hemorrhagic events. In SICH, hypocalcemia and platelet dysfunction promote aggregation at sites of vascular damage, critically affecting thrombosis. Serum calcium, a key secondary messenger in platelet signaling pathways, mediates platelet activation, aggregation, and thrombus formation, ultimately exacerbating hematoma expansion and worsening outcomes. Reduced platelet activity is strongly correlated with increased hematoma volume and poorer functional recovery. Our results specifically demonstrated associations between lower serum calcium levels, impaired platelet function, and reduced GOS scores. Consequently, rigorous monitoring of diastolic blood pressure, admission calcium levels, and platelet function coupled with timely intervention is a critical clinical priority.

Importance analysis demonstrated that serum sodium levels significantly influenced the 180-day GOS prediction model. Serum sodium serves as a key prognostic marker for cerebral edema, which is a critical prognostic factor for ICH.[35,36] Existing evidence suggests that perilesional edema evolves through distinct phases: early vasogenic edema, followed by potential late-stage cytotoxic edema. Vasogenic edema results from blood-brain barrier disruption with plasma component extravasation (particularly Na+ and K+ ions), whereas cytotoxic edema develops when cytotoxic substances impair cellular energy metabolism and disrupt the extracellular ion gradient. This triggers Na+-K + ATPase-mediated ion exchange across the blood-brain barrier, ultimately increasing intracellular cations. Given these mechanisms, cerebral edema severity strongly correlates with GOS outcomes, underscoring the clinical significance of monitoring serum sodium levels.

Importance analysis identified decompressive craniectomy, CK levels, stroke history, and intracranial pressure reduction as significant factors in the 180-day GOS prediction model. Patients requiring decompressive craniectomy, intracranial pressure management, or those with a history of stroke typically present more severe baseline conditions. As CK is primarily cardiac-associated, elevated CK levels often reflect poor systemic status, correlating with a worse prognosis and lower GOS scores. These clinical indicators indirectly validate the reliability of the model.

The GBDT model developed in this study demonstrated robust and consistent discriminative performance and calibration across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. Clinically interpretable results enable physicians to effectively identify high-risk patients and guide clinical decision making.

The GBDT model’s clinical utility resides in 3 actionable domains: emergency triage prioritization, ICU progression monitoring, and rehabilitation resource planning. However, deployment constraints include: limited generalizability from single-center spatiotemporal biases, and inability to translate predictor significance (e.g., parietal hemorrhage) into threshold-based interventions due to mechanistic opacity – necessitating causal inference and clinical knowledge embedding for future clinical adoption.

5. Conclusions

The GBDT model demonstrated optimal performance in predicting 180-day post-admission GOS outcomes across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. This model shows the potential to assist clinicians in the early identification of at-risk patients for poorer functional outcomes and to guide targeted preventive interventions.

5.1. Strengths

Our study has several key strengths: strong consistency between the included variables and real-world physiological conditions, comprehensive tracking of cerebral hemorrhage volume until complete hematoma resolution with no recurrent bleeding during follow-up, and rigorous model development and validation employing multiple machine learning algorithms to identify the optimal predictive model. Diverse evaluation metrics and interpretability methods (SHAP and LIME) ensured transparent and clinically actionable results.

5.2. Limitations

However, our study had several limitations. First, the sample size was not validated across multiple centers. The retrospective design and missing data may have excluded the potentially relevant predictors.
