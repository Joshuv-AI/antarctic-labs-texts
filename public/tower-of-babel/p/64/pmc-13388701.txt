Changing landscape of 16 S-RMTase-mediated aminoglycoside resistance in clinical Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from Chile between 1997 and 2023
Authors: Maximiliano Matus-Köhler, Rocío Rivas-Carrasco, Luis Amsteins-Romero, Mario Quezada-Aguiluz, Francisco Astorga-Ríos, Felipe Maurelia, Celia A. Lima, Néstor Herrera-Chávez, Andrés Opazo-Capurro, Gerardo González-Rocha, Felipe Morales-León, Helia Bello-Toledo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence of 16S rRNA methyltransferase (16S-RMTases) poses a major therapeutic challenge, as they confer high-level resistance to all clinically relevant aminoglycosides. In Chile, data on these enzymes remain scarce. This study investigated the epidemiological distribution and genetic relatedness of Klebsiella pneumoniae strains carrying 16S-RMTase genes. Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed using the Kirby-Bauer method and complementary techniques. Gene detection was performed via conventional PCR, and genotyping via ERIC-PCR. Among 1,200 K. pneumoniae clinical strains collected between 1997 and 2023; 233 (19.4%) exhibited high-level resistance to both amikacin and gentamicin, suggesting 16S-RMTase gene carriage. All presumptive strains carried 16S-RMTase genes, with rmtG being the most prevalent (n = 174), followed by armA (n = 30), rmtC (n = 24), and rmtD (n = 5). Genotyping revealed 19 distinct clusters. The rmtG gene was widely distributed, whereas rmtC displayed regional clustering, particularly in Concepción, and was associated with broader resistance profiles, including resistance to carbapenems. In contrast, rmtG-positive strains retained partial susceptibility to these agents. Fisher’s exact test revealed associations (p < 0.05) between specific 16S-RMTase genes and non-susceptibility antibiotic profiles. The spread of 16S-RMTase genes since 2007, from Iquique to Puerto Montt, highlights their growing clinical relevance. Introduction

Aminoglycosides are antibiotics commonly used to treat infections caused by Gram-negative bacilli, particularly in combination with β-lactams1. However, the emergence of 16S rRNA methyltransferase enzymes (16S-RMTases), such as ArmA, RmtA-RmtH, and NpmA-NpmC, has introduced a significant therapeutic challenge, as these enzymes confer high levels of resistance (MIC > 256 µg/mL) to all clinically relevant aminoglycosides2–4. These enzymes exert their effect by methylating specific nucleotides within the A-site of the 16S ribosomal RNA, located in the 30S subunit of the bacterial ribosome, thereby preventing the binding of aminoglycoside antibiotics to their target. Based on their methylation target site, 16S-RMTases are classified into two groups: ArmA and RmtA–RmtH target the G1405 residue, blocking the binding of 4,6-disubstituted aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin (GEN), amikacin (AMK), tobramycin, and kanamycin); in contrast, NpmA-C modify the A1408 residue, affecting both 4,5- and 4,6-disubstituted aminoglycosides, and conferring a broader resistance profile known as pan-aminoglycoside resistance2–4.

The 16S-RMTases has emerged as a global concern for three main reasons: (i) It confers high levels of resistance (MIC ≥ 256 µg/mL) to most clinically relevant aminoglycosides, including plazomicin, a last-resort antibiotic developed to treat infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, carrying aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (AMEs)4,5; (ii) 16S-RMTases genes are often co-located with other clinically relevant resistance genes, such as blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaCTX−M6; and (iii) These genes exhibit a high potential for dissemination, as they are commonly embedded in mobile genetic elements primarily transposons within mobilizable plasmids facilitating horizontal gene transfer4.

Since the first description of a 16S-RMTase in 2003, reports on these enzymes have increased considerably. Currently, armA, rmtB, and rmtC are the most frequently detected genes among members of the Enterobacteriaceae family4,7. The first gene identified was rmtA, found in a P. aeruginosa strain isolated in 19978. Subsequently, armA was detected in a K. pneumoniae strain isolated in 20009. However, sequencing of the pCTX-M3 plasmid from a Citrobacter freundii strain isolated in 1996 revealed the presence of the armA gene, suggesting that these genes were circulating before 200010. This hypothesis was further supported by the detection of the armA in an A. baumannii strain isolated in 1931 (BioSample: SAMN18636604)11.

A recent genomic epidemiology study of 16S-RMTase genes in Gram-negative bacteria from the Americas identified armA as the most prevalent gene (77.9%), followed by rmtB (9.1%), mainly in K. pneumoniae, E. coli, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa. Less frequently detected genes included rmtF (7.1%), rmtE (2.0%), rmtC (1.9%), rmtD (0.9%), and rmtG (0.8%)11.

In Chile, regarding aminoglycosides, only AMK and GEN are available for treating infections in clinics. In addition, epidemiological information on the carriage of genes encoding 16S-RMTase is scarce. To date, four reports have included strains isolated in Chile. The first study described an Enterobacter cloacae strain (2006) and a K. pneumoniae strain (2005), both of which carried the rmtD gene1. The second, identified a K. pneumoniae strain carrying rmtG, which also carried the blaCTX−M−212. The third and fourth reports describe K. pneumoniae and C. freundii strains carrying armA and carbapenemase-encoding genes13,14. As previously noted, no nationwide studies have characterized the epidemiology of 16S-RTMases in K. pneumoniae clinical strains. Given the limited available data on this concerning resistance mechanism, the present study aimed to determine the prevalence and types of 16S-RMTase genes in K. pneumoniae strains resistant to AMK and GEN, isolated between 1997 and 2023 from hospitals across Chile.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

A total of 1,200 clinical strains of K. pneumoniae isolated between 1997 and 2023 from 47 healthcare centres across Chile were investigated. The study included healthcare centres in the following locations: Iquique, Antofagasta, Valparaíso, Viña del Mar, Santiago, Talca, San Carlos, Chillán, Los Ángeles, Lirquén, Concepción, Talcahuano, Coronel, Lota, Arauco, Temuco, and Puerto Montt. All strains were stored at -80 °C in the biorepository of the Laboratorio de Investigación en Agentes Antibacterianos (LIAA), Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, Universidad de Concepción, Concepción, Chile.

In vitro antimicrobial susceptibility studies

A total of 1,200 K. pneumoniae strains were screened for high-level aminoglycoside resistance. Susceptibility to AMK and GEN was assessed using two complementary methods: Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion (AMK: 30 µg; GEN: 10 µg) and antibiotic-enriched agar (AMK and GEN: 128 µg/mL). Strains that exhibited no inhibition zones or showed minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) ≥ 128 µg/mL for both AMK and GEN were classified as presumptive of 16S-RMTase producers.

For presumptive 16S-RMTase producer strains, minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for aminoglycosides were determined (16 to 1024 µg/mL). Additionally, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on the same set of strains using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. The antibiotics tested included cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), imipenem (IPM, 10 µg), meropenem (MEM, 10 µg), ertapenem (ETP, 10 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 25 µg). E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain in all assays. Interpretive criteria and methodological procedures followed the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)15–17.

Genomic DNA extraction and detection of 16S-RMTase genes by PCR

For presumptive 16S-RMTase producer strains, 16S-RMTase genes were screened by conventional PCR using specific primers targeting armA, rmtA, rmtB, rmtC, rmtD, rmtG, and rmtE (Supplementary Table 1). Genomic DNA was extracted using a 5% Chelex® matrix (Bio-Rad®). PCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 10 µL, containing 5 µL of GoTaq® G2 Master Mix, 0.2 µL of each primer (25 mM), and 3.6 µL of nuclease-free water. Amplification products were visualized by electrophoresis in 1% agarose gels stained with SafeView and imaged using a UVIdoc HD5 transilluminator (Uvitec, Cambridge).

The following strains were used as positive controls: Enterobacter cloacae UCO-400 (armA), K. pneumoniae UCO-399 (rmtB), K. pneumoniae P88 (rmtC), P. aeruginosa UCO-397 (rmtD), E. coli UCO-401 (rmtE), and K. pneumoniae UCO-398 (rmtG).

Molecular genotyping by ERIC-PCR of K. pneumoniae strains carrying 16S-RMTase genes

K. pneumoniae strains carrying 16S-RMTase genes were genotyped using ERIC-PCR. Amplification was performed using the ERIC-2 primer (25 mM) (5’-AAGTAAGTGACTGGGGGGTGAGCG-3’)18. DNA concentrations were measured using the TAKE3 module on the EPOCH microplate reader and adjusted to a final concentration of 50–200 ng/µL.

PCR reactions were conducted in a final volume of 20 µL, containing 10 µL of GoTaq® G2 Master Mix, 0.8 µL of primer, 0.4 µL of MgCl₂ (25 mM), and 4.8 µL of nuclease-free water. Amplification products were separated by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels at 90 V for 3 h and 40 min. Visualization was performed using SafeView staining and a UVIdoc HD5 transilluminator (Uvitec, Cambridge).

Banding patterns were analysed using GelJ v2.0 software19, applying the unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) and the Dice–Sørensen coefficient (DICE), with a band position tolerance of 2%20. A dendrogram was constructed to assess genetic relatedness, and strains were considered genetically related when they clustered from a node with ≥ 90% similarity. The resulting dendrogram output from GelJ was subsequently visualized using iTOL v7.2.121.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test (one-tailed) was applied to evaluate the association between the presence of 16S-RMTase genes (armA, rmtC, rmtD, and rmtG) and antibiotic non-susceptibility (NS) profiles (including both resistance and intermediate categories). This test was selected due to the small sample sizes and the categorical nature of the data, for which parametric assumptions such as normality are not applicable. For each comparison, “n” corresponded to the total number of isolates analysed in each gene group. Associations were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical Considerations

All experimental procedures were conducted exclusively using bacterial isolates and were performed in accordance with all relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. This study was approved by Comité Ético Científico del Servicio de Salud Concepción (Chile), under approval number CEC-SSC:23-07-26.

Results

Molecular identification of 16S-RMTase genes

A total of 1,200 K. pneumoniae clinical strains collected in Chile between 1997 and 2023 were screened for high-level aminoglycoside resistance. Based on presumptive phenotypic criteria, 233 isolates (19.4%) were classified as possible 16S-RMTase carrying strains. The MIC50 and MIC90 values for both AMK and GEN were 512 µg/mL and > 1024 µg/mL, respectively, indicating high-level resistance. All 233 presumptive 16S-RMTase-producing strains were positive for at least one 16S-RMTase gene and showed distinct geographic (Fig. 1A) and temporal (Fig. 1B) patterns across Chile between 2007 and 2023.

The rmtG gene was first identified in strains isolated in 2007 in Concepción. Between 2007 and 2013, rmtG was the predominant 16S-RMTase gene, detected in 174 strains (73%) in 10 cities. However, its prevalence declined markedly in subsequent years, with only 4 rmtG positive strains (1.7%) identified between 2015 and 2023, suggesting a significant reduction in its circulation.

The oldest armA-carrying strain was isolated in Santiago in 2008. This gene showed a progressive increase specially from 2012 onwards, with a total of 30 (12.9%) positive strains identified across 5 cities. The 24 (10.3%) rmtC-positive strains were found exclusively in Concepción and were isolated between 2020 and 2023.

The rmtD gene was detected sporadically throughout the study period, with only 5 (2.1%) positive strains isolated in 3 cities. The first rmtD-positive strain was recovered in 2013 in Los Ángeles.

Resistance profiles associated with 16S-RMTase genes in K. pneumoniae strains

The antibiotic susceptibility test of the putative strains of K. pneumoniae carrying 16S-RMTase genes revealed a high level of multidrug resistance. Specifically, 211 (91%) strains were resistant to both CTX and CRO, 197 (85%) to CAZ, 139 (60%) to ETP, 77 (33%) to MEM, and 39 (17%) to IPM. In addition, 222 (95%) of the strains were resistant to CIP and 131 (56%) to SXT (Fig. 2).

Subsequently, Fisher’s exact test revealed associations between specific 16S-RMTase genes and antimicrobial NS profiles. The presence of armA was significantly associated with NS to CRO, MEM, ETP and SXT (p < 0.05). The presence of rmtC was linked to NS to CAZ, MEM, IPM, ETP, and SXT (p < 0.05); and rmtG was associated with NS to CTX, CAZ, MEM, IPM, ETP, and SXT (p < 0.05). In contrast, rmtD showed no statistically significant associations with NS to any of the tested antibiotics.

Genetic relatedness of K. pneumoniae strains carrying 16S-RMTase genes

Genetic relatedness analysis grouped 168/233 K. pneumoniae strains carrying 16S-RMTase genes into 19 distinct clusters (Fig. 3). The largest group, Cluster I, comprised 40 strains (17%) isolated between 2007 and 2008 from Antofagasta (n = 4), Santiago (n = 19), and Concepción (n = 17). Cluster II included 19 strains (8.1%) collected between 2007 and 2012 from Viña del Mar (n = 2), Santiago (n = 7), Arauco (n = 1), Concepción (n = 7), and Temuco (n = 2). Cluster III consisted of 18 strains (7.7%) isolated between 2007 and 2013 from Santiago (n = 5) and Concepción (n = 13), while Cluster IV grouped 14 strains (6%) recovered between 2009 and 2010 from Antofagasta (n = 1) and Viña del Mar (n = 13). Cluster V comprised 5 strains (2.1%) isolated between 2008 and 2010 from Santiago (n = 4) and Viña del Mar (n = 1).

Clusters VI, VII, and VIII each comprised 4 strains (1.7%), all isolated in 2008. Cluster VI included strains from Puerto Montt; cluster VII from Santiago (n = 2) and Concepción (n = 2); and Cluster VIII from Santiago. Cluster IX included 3 strains (1.3%), also isolated in 2008 from Santiago. Cluster X comprised 7 strains (3%) collected between 2012 and 2019 from Santiago (n = 1), Talca (n = 2), Los Ángeles (n = 3), and Temuco (n = 1). Cluster XI included 3 strains (1.3%) collected in 2012 from Puerto Montt (n = 1) and in 2021 from Concepción (n = 2). Cluster XII was composed of 12 strains (5.1%), all isolated in 2013 from Puerto Montt. Cluster XIII comprised 4 strains (1.7%), all isolated in 2008 from Santiago. Cluster XIV included 3 strains (1.3%), also isolated in 2008 from Santiago.

Cluster XV included 11 strains (4.7%), all isolated in 2023 from Concepción. Cluster XVI comprised 7 strains (3%), isolated in 2013 from Santiago (n = 1), in 2019 from Antofagasta (n = 1), and from Temuco (n = 2), and in 2023 from Concepción (n = 3). Cluster XVII included 3 strains (1.2%), all isolated in 2022 from Talcahuano, while Cluster XVIII included 3 strains (1.2%), all isolated in 2023 from Concepción. Finally, Cluster XIX included 4 strains (1,7%), all isolated in 2022 from Talcahuano.

Genetic relatedness analysis grouped 134 rmtG positive strains into 13 clusters (I–VII and IX-XIV) composed of isolates from Santiago (n = 48), Concepción (n = 39), Puerto Montt (n = 17), Viña del Mar (n = 16), Antofagasta (n = 5), Temuco (n = 3), Los Ángeles (n = 3), Talca (n = 2), and Arauco (n = 1). The remaining 40 strains (17.2%) were unclustered and recovered between 2007 and 2017 from 6 cities, including Iquique (n = 1), Antofagasta (n = 4), Santiago (n = 13), Concepción (n = 11), Temuco (n = 1), and Puerto Montt (n = 10) suggesting a heterogeneous genetic background and possible independent acquisition events.

Of the 30 armA positive strains (12.9%), 15 were grouped into 4 clusters (VIII, XVI, XVII and XIX), while Cluster XVI included 7 strains, 4 carrying armA from Antofagasta (n = 1; 2019), Santiago (n = 1; 2013), and Temuco (n = 2; 2019). The remaining 15 (6.4%) armA positive strains were not assigned to any defined cluster and were recovered between 2008 and 2022 from Antofagasta (n = 1), Santiago (n = 9), Concepción (n = 2), and Talcahuano (n = 3).

Regarding the 24 rmtC positive strains, 17 (7.3%) were grouped into 3 clusters (XV, XVI, and XVIII), with Clusters XV and XVIII exclusively comprising rmtC-positive strains, while cluster XVI included both rmtC- and armA-positive strains. All clustered rmtC strains were isolated in 2023. The remaining 7 unclustered rmtC-positive strains were also recovered in Concepción between 2020 and 2023.

Finally, among the 5 strains positive for rmtD, 2 isolates were grouped within cluster XI, while the remaining 3 strains, from Antofagasta, Los Ángeles, and Concepción, remained ungrouped. The oldest case, identified in 2013 in Los Ángeles, showed no genetic relationship to any other strain. The remaining 4 strains were isolated between 2019 and 2021 in Antofagasta and Concepción. Of these, 2 belonged to cluster XI, which also included a rmtG positive strain (K949) from Puerto Montt, isolated in 2012, suggesting a possible common genetic origin.

Discussion

The first 16S-RMTase encoding-gene, armA, was identified in an A. baumannii strain in 1931, followed decades later by rmtD1 in P. aeruginosa in 199712. In South America, sporadic reports have documented armA, rmtB, rmtC, and rmtD in Enterobacteriaceae and non-fermenters such as A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa11,22,23.

This study provides the first nationwide epidemiological assessment of 16S-RMTase genes in clinical K. pneumoniae strains from Chile. Notably, we observed a high prevalence of rmtG between 2007 and 2013, preceding its first known report in Brazil (2010–2011)12. The earliest rmtG-positive strain in our collection dates to 2007.

One of these strains, K921 (ST11), co-harboured blaCTX−M−2 and exhibited colistin resistance, with rmtG located on an IncA/C plasmid12. Later, rmtG was also detected in E. cloacae, from poultry imported to Uruguay, carried on IncQ1 plasmids alongside mcr-9.1 and blaCTX−M alleles24.

Across all isolates analysed, rmtG was the most prevalent 16S-RMTase gene (n = 174/74.7%), with the widest geographic distribution (~ 1,900 km). This finding contrasts with global data, where armA predominates in the Americas, accounting for 77.9% of reported sequences, while rmtG represents only 0.8%11. This discrepancy may reflect limited genomic surveillance in South America. Interestingly, rmtG detection declined after 2013, while armA cases increased progressively (13.3% to 0.9% and 0.4% to 4.3%, respectively), reflecting a dynamic shift in the prevalence of 16S-RMTase genes across Chile during the 2007–2023 period.

The grouping into 19 clusters and the co-existence of unclustered strains demonstrate a heterogeneous population structure. The oldest clusters (I–IV), formed between 2007 and 2013, include strains from multiple cities, suggesting an early and sustained dissemination of rmtG-carrying lineages. In contrast, the most recent clusters (VIII, XV–XVIII), mainly associated with rmtC and armA, are concentrated in Santiago, Concepción and Talcahuano between 2019 and 2023, which may indicate the emergence of new clones adapted to local hospital environments. The coexistence of different genes (rmtG, armA, rmtC) within some clusters, such as XVI and XI, together with the presence of 16S-RMTase-encoding genes in unclustered strains (17.2%), reinforces the importance of studying mobile genetic elements such as plasmids as the main genetic vectors of resistance determinants.

Furthermore, since 2019, armA has been detected in multiple regions, covering around 1,560 km. It is now the second most prevalent 16S-RMTase in Chile. Internationally, armA is frequently associated with high-risk clones (e.g., ST14, ST231, ST147) and carbapenemase genes such as blaNDM−1, blaOXA−48, and blaKPC−225–28. In Chile, armA was reported in K. pneumoniae ST25 strains co-harboring blaKPC−2/NDM−1 and blaKPC−3/NDM−713,14.

Concurrently, rmtC emerged in 2020 in Concepción, with a notable increase in detection during 2023, although its geographic distribution has remained limited. Globally, rmtC is less frequently reported but is often associated with blaNDM−1 and IncC-type plasmids25,28–30.

Among the 16S-RMTase genes identified in this study, rmtD was the least prevalent. The first rmtD-positive strain was identified in 2013 in Los Ángeles, and only 5 strains (2.1%) were detected throughout the study period. Internationally, rmtD was first described in P. aeruginosa isolates from Brazil, co-carrying blaSPM−131, and later reported in K. pneumoniae, E. cloacae, and C. freundii strains from Chile, Argentina, and Brazil, often associated with mobile genetic elements and carbapenemase genes1,8,30,32.

The susceptibility profiles of K. pneumoniae strains harboring 16S-RMTase genes were consistently associated with resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, including β-lactams. This pattern reflects a well-documented phenomenon in which 16S-RMTases co-occur with other resistance determinants, such as genes encoding extended-spectrum β-lactamases and, to a lesser extent, carbapenemases, frequently located on mobile genetic elements like plasmids6,10,12–14,33,34. Such elements contribute to a multidrug-resistant phenotype, highlighting the clinical significance of 16S-RMTase resistance and its linkage to a broader antimicrobial non-susceptible profile. These findings align with previous reports and underscore the role of 16 S-RMTase-carrying strains as reservoirs of complex resistance profiles with implications for therapeutic management and infection control.

A limitation of this study is the lack of genomic context (e.g., plasmid backbones and mobile genetic elements), which limits mechanistic interpretation of dissemination. Future genome- and plasmid-level analyses are needed to better understand the mobility of these resistance determinants.

Conclusions

K. pneumoniae strains carrying 16 S-RMTase genes exhibit high-level resistance to AMK and GEN, with MIC90 values exceeding 1024 µg/mL. These genes have been emerging in Chile since 2007 and, from 2008 onward, have been identified in clinical isolates from cities spanning Iquique to Puerto Montt. Notably, a greater diversity of 16 S-RMTase genes has been observed over the past decade. Currently, armA and rmtC are the most prevalent genes, having replaced rmtG as the dominant 16 S-RMTase genes in Chilean K. pneumoniae isolates.

Projections

The rising prevalence of aminoglycoside- and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae represents a significant public health concern, as it markedly limits available therapeutic options. A comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology of strains carrying genetic platforms that confer multidrug resistance is therefore essential to inform effective surveillance and containment strategies. Given the clinically relevant associations reported in the literature, particularly between rmtC and blaNDM−1, as well as armA and blaKPC−2 it is imperative to continue genomic surveillance studies to elucidate the structure, dynamics, and dissemination potential of these resistance platforms.
