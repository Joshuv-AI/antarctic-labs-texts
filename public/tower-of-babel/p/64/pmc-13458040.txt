Prenatal sonographic diagnosis of fetal right aortic arch with isolated left innominate artery and bilateral ductus arteriosus: a case description
Authors: Xuelu Feng, Xiaxia Cheng, Pujuan Jia, Yuwei Dong, Bin Ma
Published: 2026

Introduction

Isolated left innominate artery (ILIA) is an extremely rare branching anomaly of the aortic arch, typically characterized by the absence of a direct origin of the left innominate artery from the aortic arch, with abnormal connection to the pulmonary artery via the ductus arteriosus or an abnormal vessel. Most cases of ILIA are associated with a right aortic arch (RAA) (1-3). According to the literature, ILIA cases are usually identified postnatally when affected infants present with clinical symptoms or are incidentally detected on imaging examinations; prenatal ultrasound diagnosis is rare.

ILIA may be associated with other cardiovascular anomalies and may have important postnatal hemodynamic implications, including insufficient blood supply to the left upper limb, pulmonary overcirculation, or respiratory symptoms related to vascular compression. In addition, inadequate collateral circulation may lead to abnormal cerebral perfusion (4).

The pathogenesis of ILIA has not yet been fully elucidated. It is generally considered to result from abnormal embryonic development of the supra-aortic vessels. Although genetic data on ILIA are limited, previous reports have suggested possible associations with chromosomal microdeletions such as 4q25 (2), and RAA has been reported to be associated with 22q11.2 microdeletion. In this study, we report a rare fetal case of RAA combined with bilateral ductus arteriosus (BDA) and ILIA, and, with reference to the literature, analyze its prenatal sonographic features, key diagnostic points, and clinical implications.

Case presentation

The patient was a 27-year-old gravida 2, para 1 (G2P1) who had become pregnant spontaneously. She had been previously healthy and denied a family history of hereditary disease, as well as any illnesses or exposure to toxic or harmful substances during the current pregnancy. Her spouse was also apparently healthy. No obvious fetal structural abnormalities were detected on first-trimester ultrasound screening. At 23 weeks of gestation, suspected abnormalities of the aortic arch were identified on anomaly screening, and the patient was referred to Gansu Provincial Maternity and Child-Care Hospital for fetal echocardiography. The fetal cardiac position, chamber size, and intracardiac relationships were normal. On the three-vessel-and-trachea view (3VT), both the aortic arch and ductus arteriosus were located to the right of the trachea, and the descending aorta was located to the right of the spine (Figure 1A). On targeted para-transverse, para-coronal, and off-axis views, the aortic arch was seen to give rise only to the right common carotid artery and right subclavian artery; the left common carotid artery and left subclavian artery did not originate from the aortic arch. Dynamic assessment in multiple views demonstrated that the ILIA connected to the main pulmonary artery via a second left-sided ductus arteriosus (Figure 1B-1D). High-definition flow imaging (HD-Flow) and spatiotemporal image correlation (STIC) provided a clearer and more intuitive depiction of the connections and spatial relationships of the great vessels and their branches (Figure 2A,2B). No extracardiac or additional intracardiac abnormalities were identified. The major venous structures, including the superior vena cava, bilateral brachiocephalic veins, internal jugular veins, and subclavian veins, were also evaluated and demonstrated normal anatomical configuration. The patient consented to amniocentesis for chromosomal analysis, and the karyotype result was normal. At 39 weeks of gestation, a male infant was delivered by cesarean section with a birth weight of 3,250 grams. The 1- and 5-minute Apgar scores were 9 and 10, respectively. After birth, the neonate had stable vital signs, with no obvious cyanosis or respiratory distress. Cranial ultrasound was performed postnatally and revealed no obvious structural abnormalities; however, imaging data were not preserved. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed 2 days after birth. On the suprasternal notch view with cranial scanning in two-dimensional and color Doppler modes, the RAA was seen to give rise only to the right common carotid artery and right subclavian artery, with no left innominate artery originating from it (Figure 3A,3B). On the parasternal short-axis view of the aorta, both left and right ductal structures were shown to be closed (Figure 3C). Computed tomography angiography (CTA; Figure 3D-3F) further delineated the vascular anatomy, which was consistent with the prenatal diagnosis. During the available early postnatal evaluation, no obvious cyanosis, respiratory distress, or feeding difficulty was observed. However, the child was subsequently lost to follow-up and could not be reassessed. Therefore, detailed functional evaluation, including assessment of upper-limb arterial pulses, oxygen saturation, inter-arm blood pressure differences, carotid/subclavian/vertebral arterial flow, and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes, was not available.

Discussion

ILIA is an extremely rare anomaly of the aortic arch branches, defined as a condition in which the left innominate artery does not arise directly from the aortic arch but is abnormally connected to the pulmonary artery via a ductus arteriosus, or collateral pathways. Previously, most cases have been detected in childhood or adulthood because of associated congenital heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, or abnormal blood supply to the upper limb. Reports of prenatal diagnosis are extremely rare. ILIA is often associated with RAA, which is defined as an aortic arch located to the right of the trachea that crosses over the right main bronchus and descends to join the descending aorta. Common patterns of supra-aortic arch branching in RAA include mirror-image branching, aberrant left subclavian artery, and isolated left subclavian artery (1-3). BDA is also an extremely rare fetal vascular anomaly. In previous case reports, BDA has often been associated with RAA, double aortic arch, or complex intracardiac malformations.

According to the double-arch developmental model proposed by Edwards and subsequent studies, and based on the sonographic findings in this case, the embryologic development can be inferred as follows (Figure 4A,4B): the right fourth pharyngeal arch and right dorsal aorta are preserved and form the RAA, whereas the proximal segments of the left fourth pharyngeal arch and left dorsal aorta undergo abnormal regression, resulting in the loss of a direct connection between the left innominate artery and the ascending aorta. The distal portions of the sixth pharyngeal arches on both sides fail to regress as usual and are retained to form the left and right ductus arteriosus. The left innominate artery establishes an abnormal connection with the pulmonary artery through the left ductus arteriosus and is therefore supplied, in utero, anterogradely via the pulmonary artery (5). Postnatally, after closure of the left ductus arteriosus, the left innominate artery may be perfused retrogradely through collateral pathways involving the circle of Willis.

In prenatal ultrasound, the conventional 3VT view is a key view for screening aortic arch abnormalities. Normally, the aortic arch is located to the left of the trachea. When the aortic arch is identified on the right side of the trachea, an RAA should be suspected, and the side of the trachea on which the ductus arteriosus is located, whether it is unilateral or bilateral, and whether a “U”- or “O”-shaped vascular ring is formed, should be further assessed.

The aortic arch long-axis view, ductal arch long-axis view, and coronal view of the descending aorta are then used to assess the course of the ascending aorta, the position of the descending aorta, and the specific pulmonary artery branch from which the ductus arteriosus arises, as well as its pattern of connection to the descending aorta, in order to determine whether there is a left ductus arteriosus, right ductus arteriosus, or BDA. Subsequently, the origins of the head and neck vessels are systematically traced, sequentially identifying the right common carotid artery, right subclavian artery, and the left innominate artery or the left common carotid artery and left subclavian artery. If no left innominate artery is seen arising from the aortic arch and the left innominate artery is instead abnormally connected to the pulmonary artery through a left-sided ductus arteriosus, ILIA should be strongly suspected. Finally, the relative positions of the arch, BDA, and the trachea and esophagus are evaluated to determine whether a vascular ring is present and to estimate the risk of airway compression. In the present case, this prenatal examination protocol was followed, leading to the diagnosis of RAA combined with ILIA and BDA.

In this study, HD-Flow and STIC were fully utilized to acquire cardiovascular volume data. Multiplanar reconstruction and surface rendering were performed to provide an intuitive three-dimensional (3D) depiction of the spatial relationships among the aortic arch, ductus arteriosus, and head and neck vessels, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy (6). In addition, the combination of HD-Flow and STIC can provide 3D visualization of the RAA and the ILIA, particularly intuitive 3D images of ILIA connected to the left ductus arteriosus. These findings can provide valuable information for clinicians and also deepen sonographers’ understanding of ILIA, thereby sharpening their acuity in its prenatal diagnosis.

From a genetic perspective, numerous studies have shown that RAA is significantly associated with chromosomal abnormalities, particularly 22q11.2 microdeletion, and some authors have suggested that the risk of genetic abnormalities may further increase in the presence of complex arch anomalies (2).

Genetic data on ILIA are limited, but case reports have indicated that it may be accompanied by microdeletions such as 4q25 (4). Therefore, once prenatal ultrasound detects RAA combined with ILIA, even in the absence of obvious intracardiac structural malformations, the importance of chromosomal microarray (CMA) and targeted testing for 22q11.2 should be fully explained to the pregnant woman to facilitate risk assessment and reproductive decision-making. In the present case, invasive prenatal diagnosis was performed in the second trimester, and the results of conventional karyotype analysis were normal, with no obvious numerical or structural abnormalities, which to some extent alleviated concerns about prognosis. However, more subtle genetic syndromes cannot be completely ruled out. Thus, despite normal genetic test results, neurodevelopmental follow-up remains necessary.

BDA was identified prenatally in the present case. From an anatomical perspective, according to the classification proposed by Malakan Rad et al. (7), this condition can be categorized as a triple-steal type. In fetal life, the left innominate artery was supplied anterogradely through the left ductus arteriosus from the pulmonary artery. However, after postnatal closure of the ductus arteriosus, this ductal-dependent perfusion pathway would be interrupted, potentially resulting in marked changes in hemodynamic status.

Under these conditions, perfusion of the left head, neck, and upper limb may depend on collateral circulation. Cerebral blood flow may be redistributed through the circle of Willis from the contralateral carotid system to the left side. In addition, the vertebrobasilar system may contribute to collateral supply, and retrograde flow may develop through the left vertebral artery toward the distal left subclavian artery. Therefore, after ductal closure, both cerebral redistribution through the circle of Willis and collateral supply through the vertebrobasilar-subclavian pathway may coexist, forming a possible combined carotid-subclavian steal physiology (Figure 4C). This collateral mechanism may partly explain why no obvious ischemic manifestations were observed during the available early postnatal evaluation, although this remains speculative because postnatal functional vascular assessment was not performed.

During this compensatory process, the direction of blood flow in the left internal carotid artery, left common carotid artery, and left vertebral artery may be altered. However, in the present case, no systematic assessment of upper-limb perfusion, inter-arm blood pressure differences, oxygen saturation, carotid, subclavian, or vertebral arterial flow was performed, and Doppler evaluation of cerebral hemodynamics was not conducted. Therefore, these hemodynamic considerations remain speculative and require confirmation by functional vascular assessment, such as Doppler ultrasound, cranial ultrasound, or CTA. Previous studies have suggested that cranial ultrasound may be a useful noninvasive tool for evaluating cerebral hemodynamics, particularly in detecting steal phenomena and assessing their impact on cerebral perfusion (4).

Although some infants with an ILIA may show no obvious symptoms during the neonatal period, the absence of early clinical symptoms should be interpreted with caution. As the child grows and physical activity increases, insufficient perfusion may gradually lead to fatigue, weakness, or claudication of the left upper limb. In addition, altered hemodynamics or potential airway compression may be associated with delayed respiratory symptoms, recurrent pneumonia, feeding difficulty, circulatory abnormalities, or neurodevelopmental concerns. Therefore, the absence of obvious symptoms during the available early postnatal evaluation should not be regarded as sufficient evidence to postpone or rule out the need for surgical evaluation or repair.

Long-term structured follow-up and dynamic functional evaluation remain essential. Such follow-up should include clinical assessment of respiratory symptoms, feeding difficulty, upper-limb perfusion, inter-arm blood pressure differences, oxygen saturation, and neurodevelopmental status. When feasible, Doppler evaluation of carotid, subclavian, and vertebral arterial flow, as well as cranial ultrasound assessment of cerebral hemodynamics, may provide useful information for postnatal decision-making. In the absence of surgical revascularization between the aortic arch and the ILIA, there may be a long-term risk of cerebral hypoperfusion and related hemodynamic abnormalities. Although there is no universally accepted age for surgical intervention, successful repair has been reported from as early as day 3 of life to 7 months of age. More urgent surgical evaluation may be required in the presence of pulmonary overcirculation, impaired cerebral or upper-limb perfusion, airway compression, or clinically significant neurodevelopmental, respiratory, or circulatory symptoms (8).

A major limitation of this case report is the lack of extended postnatal functional follow-up. Although neonatal echocardiography and CTA confirmed the prenatal anatomical diagnosis, the child was lost to subsequent follow-up and could not be reassessed. Therefore, the long-term functional impact of this vascular malformation could not be determined, and further evaluation of upper-limb perfusion, inter-arm blood pressure differences, oxygen saturation, carotid, subclavian, and vertebral arterial flow, airway compression, cerebral hemodynamics, and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes was not available.

Conclusions

By performing a comprehensive evaluation involving conventional prenatal ultrasound combined with HD-Flow and STIC, we successfully diagnosed a rare anomaly of RAA combined with ILIA and BDA. This type of case has been rarely reported in the literature and suggests that, when a right-sided aortic arch is identified on the conventional 3VT view, 3D reconstruction with HD-Flow and STIC to further trace the course of the arch, the origins of its branches, and the morphology of the ductus arteriosus has important anatomic value. Prenatal examination should not only identify the position of the aortic arch but also emphasize the 3D spatial relationships of the brachiocephalic vessels and the ductus arteriosus, thereby providing support for prenatal counseling, delivery management, and postnatal management and follow-up. However, the clinical significance of this anomaly should not be limited to prenatal diagnosis and postnatal anatomical confirmation. Strict postnatal surveillance is essential because the functional impact of altered cerebral and upper-limb perfusion may not be fully evident during the neonatal period. In similar cases, long-term structured follow-up is needed to monitor neurodevelopmental, respiratory, and circulatory outcomes, and surgical evaluation or repair should be considered if related symptoms or hemodynamic abnormalities develop. In the present case, because the child was subsequently lost to follow-up and could not be reassessed, the long-term functional impact of this vascular malformation could not be determined.
