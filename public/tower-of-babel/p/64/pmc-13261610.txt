Influence of Dilinoleic Succinate Segments on the Biodegradation of Poly(butylene succinate-co-dilinoleic succinate) Copolymers under Composting and Anaerobic Digestion Conditions
Authors: Konrad Walkowiak, Malwina Niedźwiedź, Joanna Rydz, Khadar Duale, Joanna Rokicka, Krzysztof Gorący, Agnieszka Piegat, Maria L. Focarete, Judit E. Puskas, Frederick C. Michel, Miroslawa El Fray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing environmental and human health concerns associated with the persistence of nondegradable polymeric materials have intensified the global demand for sustainable alternatives. Consequently, research has increasingly focused on the development of biobased and biodegradable polymers as a means to mitigate plastic pollution. In this context, the present study investigates the biodegradation behavior of poly­(butylene succinate) (PBS) and poly­(butylene succinate-co-dilinoleic succinate) (PBS–DLS) copolymers under mesophilic anaerobic digestion (AD) and thermophilic aerobic composting conditions. After 99 days of anaerobic incubation, the tested materials exhibited negligible mineralization and no significant mass loss, indicating limited biodegradability under oxygen-deficient conditions. In contrast, all materials underwent substantial degradation during thermophilic composting (97 days), with PBS–DLS 70–30 demonstrating the highest extent of mineralizationnearly twice that of PBSand approximately 30% greater mass loss. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis revealed a decrease in molecular weight for all samples, with the most pronounced reductions observed under composting conditions. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed distinct structural alterations in both PBS and PBS–DLS following exposure to both environments, particularly for PBS–DLS 70–30. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis indicated measurable shifts in phase transition temperatures, consistent with polymer chain scission and morphological reorganization induced by degradation. Surface morphology analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), optical microscopy (OM), and macroscopic imaging showed minimal surface alteration after AD, while pronounced erosion and fragmentation were evident following composting. Collectively, these results underscore the limited anaerobic degradability of PBS and the markedly enhanced compostability of PBS–DLS copolymers with increasing DLS segment content. The findings highlight the potential of PBS–DLS copolymers as promising candidates for environmentally benign materials in sustainable packaging and biomedical applications.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, the rapid development and expansion of polymer manufacturing have established polymeric materials as indispensable components of a modern society. According to Plastics Europe, global plastic production reached 430.9 million tons in 2024. This extensive growth stems from the remarkable versatility of polymers, which enables their widespread application across numerous sectors including packaging, construction, automotive, agriculture, and biomedicine. Despite their technological and economic advantages, the escalating production and consumption of conventional synthetic polymers have raised substantial environmental concerns. The increase in polymer production is directly correlated to a concomitant rise in plastic waste generation, most of which originates from single-use products. The absence of efficient end-of-life management strategies exacerbates the accumulation of persistent plastic waste in terrestrial and marine ecosystems. , In 2024, only 19.4% of the total plastic products in Europe were derived from recycled materials, an insufficient proportion to counterbalance the continuous growth of plastic waste. The environmental implications of this imbalance include microplastic formation, which poses risks to aquatic and terrestrial organisms and has emerging implications for human health. ,

In response to these challenges, global efforts have intensified toward the design and implementation of sustainable “green” plastics that are both biobased and biodegradable. Among the various candidates, biobased aliphatic polyesters have attracted particular attention from both academia and industry owing to their favorable mechanical performance, processability, and intrinsic biodegradability. Within this class, poly­(butylene succinate) (PBS) has emerged as a promising biodegradable thermoplastic polymer distinguished by excellent melt processability and biodegradation potential. Moreover, PBS exhibits mechanical properties comparable to those of polypropylene, thereby offering opportunities for direct substitution in conventional applications. ,,

Commercial production of fossil-based PBS was first initiated in 1993 by Showa Denko (Bionolle) and later by Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation in 2003 (GS Pla). More recently, biobased PBS has been industrially produced since 2015 by PTT MCC Biochem Company Limited, a joint venture between Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation (MCC) and PTT Global Chemical Public Company Limited (PTTGC), under the trade name BioPBS. The favorable thermal and mechanical characteristics of PBS enable its utilization in diverse applications, including mulch films, food packaging, compostable bags, textiles, and agricultural nets. ,, Its nontoxicity and biocompatibility with numerous biological hosts , further support its adoption in biomedical applications such as tissue engineering scaffolds and drug delivery systems. Nevertheless, PBS exhibits certain drawbacks, including a relatively slow crystallization rate and low melt viscosity, which limit its performance in specific end-use scenarios.

To overcome these limitations, various strategies have been employed to tailor the PBS properties, including polymer blending, filler incorporation, and copolymerization. Such modifications not only enhance targeted physical and thermal characteristics but also may reduce production costs through partial substitution with less expensive components. Among the most studied systems is the PLA/PBS blend, in which PBS acts as a plasticizer. This combination improves the elongation at break and facilitates the cold crystallization of PLA. For instance, a PLA/PBS ratio of 80:20 yields an elongation at break of approximately 230%, although it is accompanied by a modest decrease in tensile strength to around 17 MPa. Another effective approach involves copolymerization, exemplified by the synthesis of poly­(butylene succinate-co-dilinoleic succinate) (PBS–DLS) copolymers. , In these materials, PBS constitutes the hard segments, while dilinoleic succinate (DLS) forms the soft segments, imparting enhanced flexibility and tunable thermal behavior. The soft segment precursor, dilinoleic diol (DLD), is a biobased glycol derived from the dimerization of C18 linoleic acid. Prior studies have reported the synthesis and detailed characterization of PBS–DLS copolymers, highlighting their nontoxicity and absence of leachable contaminants, thus reinforcing their suitability for biomedical applications. , Increasing DLS content typically results in higher elongation at break and lower melting temperature (T m), as well as altered hydrolytic degradation in the presence of enzymes and biodegradation rates. ,

According to ASTM D883-17, polymer biodegradation is defined as the breakdown of polymeric materials through the metabolic action of naturally occurring microorganisms, such as bacteria, fungi, and algae. This process can occur under either aerobic or anaerobic conditions. Composting represents a form of aerobic biodegradation and can be classified as either home or industrial composting. Home composting typically proceeds under ambient conditions (20–30 °C) and requires at least 90% biodegradation within 12 months, although no international standard currently exists for this method. In contrast, industrial composting as defined by DIN EN 14855 should be performed at 58 °C and is completed within approximately six months, conditions that favor thermophilic microbial activity. The biodegradation of PBS under industrial composting (58 °C, 90 days) was previously reported by Zhao et al., who demonstrated morphology-dependent (bio)­degradation rates: PBS powders degraded by ∼71.9%, while PBS films achieved ∼60.7% mineralization after 90 days.

Anaerobic digestion (AD), in contrast, proceeds in oxygen-free environments and results in the generation of biogas composed primarily of methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2). As a result of this and other benefits of AD, such as the processing of other biodegradable organic waste, for example food waste, which can account for up to 45% of solid waste, this process attracts more attention. The AD is performed at two temperatures, at 37 °C, which is better suited for mesophilic bacteria, while the temperature of 55 °C favors thermophilic bacteria. Numerous articles have discussed the AD of PBS, with many authors concluding that PBS degrades minimally or not at all when subjected to mesophilic anaerobic conditions. − The study by Dvorackova et al. revealed that during AD under thermophilic conditions, microorganisms were concentrated in the amorphous regions of PBS.

Given the growing societal and environmental demand for sustainable polymers, the exploration of novel biobased and biodegradable materials is imperative. While the biodegradation of PBS has been extensively documented, the (bio)­degradation behavior of PBS–DLS copolymers with varying hard-to-soft segment ratios (e.g., 90–10 and 70–30) has primarily been examined under enzymatic conditions. Further investigation into their biodegradation under realistic AD and composting environments is, therefore, warranted. The incorporation of soft DLS segments and the resulting reduction in crystallinity may enhance their susceptibility to microbial attack, making PBS–DLS copolymers promising candidates for controlled degradability in both environmental and biomedical applications.

In this study, PBS, PBS–DLS 90–10, and PBS–DLS 70–30 were subjected to anaerobic digestion and composting conditions. The extent of biodegradation was quantified by monitoring carbon mineralization (CO2 and CH4) and mass loss. Complementary analyses, including gel permeation chromatography (GPC), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), were employed to assess molecular, structural, and thermal changes in the materials throughout the degradation process.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Succinic acid (SA) ≥99% purity (Alfa Aesar, Karlsruhe, Germany), 1,4-butanediol (BD) (Merck, Germany), dilinoleic diol (DLD) (Pripol 2033, Cargill Bioindustrial, Gouda, The Netherlands), and a titanium dioxide/silicon dioxide coprecipitate catalyst (C-94) (Huntsman, The Woodlands, Texas, USA) were used for the synthesis. PBS and a series of copolymers, PBS–DLS, with two different DLS contents (10 and 30 wt %) were synthesized following the methodology previously described by Stępień et al. The chemical structures are shown in Figure a. After synthesis, the materials were injection-molded into thin plates of specified dimensions (Figure b) with a mass of 0.8 g. To serve as a positive control, toilet paper was used for composting experiments, while microcrystalline cellulose with an average particle size of 50 μm (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was used for AD.

Chemical structure of PBS–DLS (a) and shapes and dimensions of injection-molded thin plates (b).

Biodegradation of Materials

The compostability of polyester and copolyesters was evaluated under conditions simulating a commercial-scale industrial composting facility, following ASTM D5338-98 guidelines, with the experiment duration extended from 50 to 97 days. Each vessel contained the test material mixed with 502 ± 2 g of mature compost at a moisture content of 79 ± 0.2%, resulting in a total wet mass of approximately 510 g per vessel. The compost inoculum was obtained from a full-scale windrow composting facility with a concrete surface at the Ohio Agricultural Research and Development Center (OARDC), The Ohio State University, OH. Detailed characteristics of the compost mixture can be found elsewhere. Compost and test specimens were incubated in 4-L (working volume) vessels constructed from poly­(vinyl chloride) (PVC) pipes measuring 30 cm in length and 15 cm in diameter. These vessels were placed in a 55 °C incubator (BioCold Environmental Inc., MO, USA). To maintain aerobic conditions, the reactors were aerated from below at a flow rate of 1100 mL/min. To prevent drying during the experiment, the incoming air was saturated by bubbling it through water bottles maintained at the incubator temperature. To condense moisture from the off-gas, the air exiting the vessels was passed through flasks immersed in a separate water bath set at 9 °C. The off-gas was analyzed for CO2 concentration using a Vaisala GMT 220 infrared gas analyzer with a range of 0–20%. CO2 data from each vessel were automatically recorded every hour using a Campbell Scientific 23XL data logger. Each composting vessel was also equipped with a K-type thermocouple to monitor the temperature of the compost mixture near its center, with measurements recorded every 12 min. A more detailed description of the laboratory-scale composting system is available elsewhere.

AD was conducted in accordance with the standard protocol ASTM D5511-02. The experiment lasted 99 days at a constant temperature of 37 ± 1 °C and was shaken at 115 rpm on an orbital shaker, with three control points monitored monthly in triplicate. These tests simulate biologically active landfills and anaerobic digesters, measuring the conversion of samples to CO2 and CH4. Each test material sample, averaging 6 g, was thoroughly mixed with 200 g of wet, methanogenically active sludge obtained from a full-scale (3000 m3) anaerobic digestion system treating food waste and municipal sewage sludge. The ratio of total solids/volatile solids in the methanogenically active sludge was 6.44%/57.51%. This mixture was then placed into laboratory-scale batch reactors with a working volume of 250 mL. As a blank control, a reactor containing 200 g of active methanogenic inoculum without any added substrate was used. A positive control consisted of 200 g of inoculum with the addition of 5 g of cellulose. The produced biogas was collected in gas sampling bags, and the gas composition was analyzed using a BIOGAS 5000 analyzer (Landtec, Dexter, MI, USA). The total volume of CO2 and CH4 was then converted to moles of carbon using the ideal gas law: n=pV/RT 1 where n is the amount of substance (mol), p is the pressure (Pa), V is the volume (m3), R is the gas constant, 8.314 m3·Pa·K–1·mol–1, and T is the temperature (K) at which the volume was measured (310.15 K = 37 °C).

To determine the mass of emitted carbon, we multiplied the calculated moles of carbon by the atomic mass of carbon. The final carbon emission values were corrected by subtracting the amount of carbon released in the blank control to account for the background emissions from the methanogenically active sludge.

Analytical Methods

The mineralization of PBS and its copolymers under composting and AD conditions was quantified by assessing the mean carbon release in the form of CO2 and/or CH4. The carbon evolved from each treatment was corrected by subtracting the carbon emission measured in the blanks, which comprised only inoculum and organic substrates. The carbon release was then normalized to the total initial carbon content of the polymer samples, providing a measure of the mineralization efficiency. Furthermore, the masses of the samples were measured before the experiment and at each control point to monitor changes resulting from sample degradation. Prior to weighing after degradation, the samples were dried at 50 °C for 24 h.

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was used to determine the number-averaged and weight-averaged molecular weights (M n, M w) and dispersity (Đ). Measurements were performed with a NEON Multiangle Light Scattering (MALS) system equipped with four detection systems. The samples were dissolved in chloroform (CHCl3, HPLC grade, Alfa Aesar) at a concentration of 15 mg/mL. Before injection, the samples were filtered using a 0.20 μm SRP 20 filter, and the volume of the injected sample was 50 μL. The analysis was conducted with the ASTRA version 8.1.2 software, assuming 100% recovery from the columns.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the samples were recorded using a Bruker ALPHA Platinum spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with a diamond attenuated total reflectance accessory (ATR). All measurements were performed at room temperature in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm–1, with a spectral resolution of 2 cm–1 and 32 scans accumulated per spectrum. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using OPUS software (Bruker, Germany).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were performed using a Q2500 DSC calorimeter (TA Instruments, New Castle, Delaware, USA). Approximately 5 mg of the sample was weighed into an aluminum pan and hermetically sealed prior to analysis. The thermal program consisted of the following segments: cooling to −90 °C, followed by heating to 200 °C after a 3 min isothermal stage. The heating and cooling rates were set to 10 °C min–1. Data analysis was conducted using TRIOS software (TA Instruments, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies were performed using a Quanta 250 FEG (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, US) high-resolution environmental SEM instrument operated at 5 kV acceleration voltages. The PBS–DLS specimens were observed without coating under a low vacuum (80 Pa) by using a secondary electron detector (large field detector).

Optical microscopy (OM) was performed using an i4 Infinity (LW Scientific, Lawrenceville, GA, USA). The optical microscope images were taken at magnifications of 4, 10, 40, 100, and 120×.

Results and Discussion

Biodegradation Studies

The mineralization of PBS and its copolymers under AD conditions, assessed by total CO2 and CH4 production, was evaluated over 99 days at 37 °C (Figure a). During this time, PBS exhibited a mineralization rate of only 6.9%, indicating that mesophilic anaerobic conditions are not favorable for its biodegradation. Similar findings were reported by Cho et al., Dvorackova et al., and Yagi et al. While PBS is not suitable for AD under mesophilic conditions, promising results have been reported under thermophilic conditions. For example, Dvorackova et al. observed approximately 22% total degradation of PBS in the form of a thick film under thermophilic AD. The incorporation of DLS segments into the PBS structure did not enhance its degradability under anaerobic conditions. The cumulative carbon loss for the copolymers ranged from 7.1 to 7.3%, indicating that the addition of DLS segments had a negligible effect on AD behavior.

Cumulative carbon loss as a percentage of initial carbon for tested materials during (a) 99 days of AD and (b) 97 days of composting.

More promising results were observed under composting conditions, as assessed by total CO2 production over a 97 day period at 55 °C. The results are presented in Figure b. The mineralization of PBS reached approximately 10%, which is notably higher than the value obtained under AD conditions. Comparable results for PBS films were reported by Yang et al., where approximately 12% biodegradation (biodegradation index defined by DIN EN 14855) was observed after 45 days of composting. According to the literature, during the initial stages of PBS degradation, random hydrolytic cleavage of ester bonds occurs within the polymer matrix. Extracellular enzymes and abiotic agents, such as oxidation, photodegradation, and hydrolysis, depolymerize long-chain polymers to create shorter chains (oligomers), which then become accessible to the microbial attack and subsequent degradation. ,,

The incorporation of DLS segments resulted in an improved biodegradation during composting. Specifically, the addition of 10% DLS led to mineralization values of approximately 15%, while PBS–DLS 70–30 reached ∼17%. Thus, the incorporation of DLS into PBS enhances its biodegradation under composting conditions. However, since the plateau phase of mineralization was not reached, the ultimate biodegradation percentage of the tested polymers could not be determined.

The mass loss of PBS and PBS–DLS copolymers was also assessed following both anaerobic digestion and composting. Under AD conditions (which were also at lower temperature compared to composting), all materials exhibited minimal mass loss, ranging from 1.3 to 1.9%, indicating limited susceptibility to anaerobic degradation (Figure a). The incorporation of DLS segments did not lead to any noticeable improvement in degradability. The small reduction in mass is likely attributable to nonenzymatic hydrolysis rather than microbial activity. A related study by Kanemura et al. investigated PBS degradation in water at 25, 50, and 75 °C for up to 1500 h, reporting a slight reduction in M w at 25 °C and over a 50% reduction at 50 °C. While the exact temperature threshold for accelerated hydrolysis was not identified, the mass loss observed during AD at 37 °C in this study is likely related to hydrolytic degradation, as will be further supported by M w analysis presented later in this manuscript.

Mass remaining of the tested samples after (a) AD and (b) composting.

Under composting conditions, PBS exhibited a mass loss of approximately 40% after 77 days, being almost constant for up to 97 days, indicating a slowdown in the degradation rate (Figure b). In contrast, PBS–DLS 90–10 and PBS–DLS 70–30 exhibited significantly higher mass loss values. After 77 days, the mass loss values for PBS–DLS 90–10 and 70–30 were 49 and 62%, respectively, being almost 9 and 22% higher than those for the neat PBS. After 97 days, PBS–DLS 90–10 and 70–30 reached mass loss values of 57 and 67%, respectively. Although the rate of degradation slowed in all samples, the decline was less pronounced in the copolymers than that in PBS. These results confirm that PBS–DLS copolymers not only achieve a higher total mass loss but also maintain a faster degradation rate, indicating that DLS incorporation enhances the compostability of PBS.

The molecular weights of PBS and copolymers decreased gradually with each measurement period, confirming the effect of AD on the molecular chain scission.

PBS exhibited a greater decrease in M n of approximately 35% after 99 days of AD, whereas the M n value for PBS–DLS 70–30 decreased by only 12%. It can therefore be concluded that an increase in DLS content reduces the hydrolytic degradation ability by AD. Đ results during AD do not exhibit significant changes with the longer degradation time, which is a result of the uniform degradation across the molecular chains and in the bulk. After 97 days of composting, all materials exhibited a significant reduction in molar mass, as indicated by both M n and M w, with M n decreasing by more than 80%. In addition, the change in Đ fluctuates around 0.1. Compared to AD, composting resulted in a significantly greater decrease in M n and M w values, confirming the hypothesis that composting microorganisms and higher test temperature have a greater impact than AD microorganisms and the lower temperature at which the experiment was performed. The degradation of PBS and PBS–DLS chains due to hydrolysis and microorganisms through the cleavage of molecular chains, not only by surface erosion but also in a bulk, which resulted in the lower values of M n and M w, will be further analyzed using FTIR by monitoring changes in characteristic functional groups.

FTIR Spectroscopy Analysis of Chemical Structure Changes

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural changes occurring in PBS and its copolymers following degradation under AD conditions (Figure ) and composting conditions (Figure ). The obtained spectra provide insights into the chemical modifications taking place during the (bio)­degradation process, particularly in relation to hydrolysis and microbial enzymatic activities.

FTIR spectra before and after 99 days of the AD.

FTIR spectra before and after 97 days of the composting.

The pristine FTIR spectra of PBS and its copolymers exhibit well-defined vibrational modes characteristic of aliphatic polyesters. The absorption band at 1720 cm–1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of carbonyl (C=O) groups in ester linkages, is a key marker of polymer backbone integrity. The bands in the region of 1150–1100 cm–1, assigned to C–O stretching vibration, and those at 2945 and 2865 cm–1, attributed to asymmetric and symmetric CH2 stretching, confirm the presence of the polyester structure. A broad, low-intensity absorption band at approximately 3400 cm–1, associated with O–H, suggests a limited number of hydroxyl-terminated polymer chain ends in pristine materials.

Degradation under both composting and AD conditions resulted in significant spectral modifications, particularly in the ester and hydroxyl regions, indicative of polymer chain scission and the formation of the degradation byproducts. The progressive reduction in the intensity of the carbonyl stretching band at 1720 cm–1 indicates hydrolysis of the ester bond, leading to chain cleavage. This effect is particularly pronounced in composted samples, suggesting a higher degradation rate under aerobic conditions, where microbial and thermal factors accelerate the polymer breakdown. Among the copolymers, PBS–DLS 70–30 exhibits the most substantial reduction in carbonyl absorption, which can be attributed to the increased promoting susceptibility to the microbial and enzymatic attack.

Concomitant with the decrease in carbonyl absorption, the broadening and intensity of the O–H stretching band at 3400 cm–1 were observed, suggesting the formation of hydroxyl-terminated oligomers as a result of hydrolytic chain scission. This trend was particularly evident in PBS–DLS 70–30, in agreement with its lower crystallinity and higher content of soft segments, which may enhance polymer–water interactions and facilitate enzymatic hydrolysis. In addition, changes in the C–O stretching region (1150–1100 cm–1) were detected, further supporting ester bond cleavage. The changes in this region indicate the progressive breakdown of the polymer backbone, with a more pronounced effect in PBS–DLS copolymers compared to that in neat PBS.

Structural modifications were identified in the CH2 stretching region with a broadening of the bands at 2945 and 2865 cm–1. This implies increased disorder in the polymer matrix due to degradation, leading to changes in the aliphatic backbone. The extent of these spectral changes followed the degradation trend: PBS–DLS 70–30 > PBS–DLS 90–10 > PBS, indicating that the incorporation of DLS increases the biodegradability of PBS-based materials under both composting and AD conditions.

The degradation profile also depended on various environmental conditions. Composting at 55 °C resulted in extensive cleavage of ester bonds, as evidenced by the significant reduction in the carbonyl peak and a marked increase in hydroxyl uptake. These findings underscore the role of both the test temperature and thermophilic microbial activity in accelerating polymer degradation under aerobic conditions. In contrast, anaerobic digestion at 37 °C led to more moderate spectral modifications, with a slight reduction in the absorption bands associated with esters. This suggests that microbial enzymatic activity in AD is less effective in degrading PBS-based polymers, especially in the absence of oxidative degradation pathways.

FTIR spectroscopy (Figure ) provides direct evidence of structural degradation of PBS and its copolymers under both composting and anaerobic digestion conditions. The degree of degradation correlates with the DLS content, with PBS–DLS 70–30 showing the most pronounced spectral changes.

Thermal Properties

The effects of AD and composting on the phase transition temperatures and degree of crystallinity (X c) of the tested materials were analyzed by using DSC (Figure ). The results are summarized in Table . The influence of the incorporation of DLS units into PBS was previously described in depth by our research group. In this section, we summarize the key points for a better understanding of the degradation data. Incorporation of the DLS units lowers glass transition (T g) values up to 10 °C due to increased chain flexibility. The copolymers exhibit only one T g, T m, and crystallization temperature (T c), indicating the homogeneous structure of PBS–DLS copolymers and miscibility between PBS and DLS. Therefore, with the increase of the more flexible DLS units, one can also observe the shift toward lower temperatures of T cc (cold crystallization), T m, and T c values. The incorporation of DLS units also affects X c; with a higher DLS content, X c decreases. This results from increased irregularity of the copolymer chains upon DLS incorporation, which reduces the crystallizability of the copolymers.

Under AD, changes in T g were marginal for all tested materials. PBS before degradation and after 99 days of AD exhibits only a slight decrease in T g value (ΔT g = 0.7 °C), and no change is observed in the specific heat capacity (ΔC p) values. The copolymers exhibit similar observations; the T g values decreased up to 2.0 and 3.2 °C, respectively, for PBS–DLS 90–10 and PBS–DLS 70–30, while ΔC p values remained almost identical. Although modest, the composition trend (larger T g reduction at higher DLS content) is consistent with chain scission into shorter, more mobile chains. This interpretation aligns with GPC observations that degradation under AD reduces molar mass, particularly in the DLS-containing copolymers. Cold crystallization and melting temperatures of materials were unaffected by AD. After 30 days of AD, the T cc of PBS increased by about 2.2 °C and by only 0.5 °C further by day 99, while the change for PBS–DLS 90–10 was negligible over the same period. Across all samples, the shift in T m values was negligible from 1.1 to 1.7 °C. Among the phase transition temperatures, the most affected by the AD is T c, a clear shift toward a higher temperature range is observed just after 30 days of AD for all tested materials. The T c value of PBS after 99 days of AD increased by about 10.0 °C, while for the copolymers, it increased by 7.1 and 18.0 °C for PBS–DLS 90–10 and PBS–DLS 70–30, respectively. The increase in the T c values results from a decrease in molar mass that was observed during GPC measurement. The reduced chain length increases mobility, facilitating rearrangement into a crystalline structure. Oreski et al. reported a correlation between a decrease in M w values and an increase in T c values in case of PET degradation. This assumption is also confirmed in our tests by observed increase in the X c values after AD. The degree of crystallinity was calculated using the equation Xc=ΔHm−ΔHccΔHm0 , where ΔH m is the melting enthalpy of sample, ΔH cc is the cold crystallization enthalpy of sample, and ΔH m 0 is the theoretical melting enthalpy of 110.3 J/g for 100% crystalline PBS. PBS has a higher X c value of about 5.9% after 99 days of AD, and for copolymers, the increase in X c value is about 1.2 and 2.8% for PBS–DLS 90–10 and PBS–DLS 70–30, respectively.

Samples after 97 days of composting exhibit pronounced changes in the values of phase transition temperatures. For the copolymers, the T g value decreased with the increasing content of the DLS segment. This aligns with the results from GPC, where PBS–DLS 70–30 has the lowest values of M n and M w. Lower molecular weight polymers exhibit increased chain mobility of the materials, resulting in the highest decrease in T g values. The T m values were minimally influenced by composting, while T c of copolymers increased by up to 17.8 °C, consistent with higher crystallizability explained earlier. This is also supported by the higher degree of crystallinity compared to the X c values of copolymers before degradation. Moreover, two peaks are visible during melting of PBS–DLS 90–10, which can be associated with changes in the crystalline structure.

The most notable shift in the thermal properties occurs for PBS. The T g value after 97 days of composting increased by about 4 °C, and PBS no longer exhibits cold crystallization. At the same time, T m and T c values changed, and X c value declined. Although degradation typically initiates in the amorphous phase, after prolonged time of degradation, the crystalline regions become more exposed to hydrolysis and microorganisms in the late period of degradation. Thus, a plausible explanation is the selective degradation of crystallites during prolonged composting, which exposes the crystalline regions to hydrolysis and microbial action in the later stages, with a preferential attack on the thinner crystallites, resulting in a decrease in the X c value, as described by Wu et al. for PBS film after 10 weeks of degradation at 30 °C by composting microorganisms. As a consequence, an increase in T m is observed mainly due to the presence of thicker crystals in the crystalline phase. , The increase in the T g value of PBS may be linked to the decrease in X c as the rigid amorphous phase (RAF) could become more prominent, which restricts segmental mobility. The correlation between the increase in the RAF and the decrease in the X c was reported. , Confirmation of this theory requires further comprehensive research on the issue, which exceeds the scope of this study.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the biodegradation behavior of poly­(butylene succinate) and poly­(butylene succinate-co-dilinoleic succinate) copolymers containing 10 and 30 wt % DLS segments under two contrasting environmental conditions: mesophilic anaerobic digestion at 37 °C for 99 days and thermophilic aerobic composting at 55 °C for 97 days. The extent of biodegradation was assessed through carbon mineralization (CO2 and CH4 evolution), gravimetric weight loss, and complementary physicochemical analyses. Under anaerobic conditions, all tested materials exhibited limited degradation. Both mineralization and mass loss remained low, indicating that PBS and its DLS-containing copolymers are not effectively biodegradable under mesophilic AD conditions. The introduction of DLS soft segments did not significantly enhance microbial accessibility or enzymatic hydrolysis, suggesting that the dense, hydrophobic polymer structure and limited microbial activity in oxygen-deprived environments restrict polymer breakdown. These findings align with previously reported results for PBS, confirming its limited anaerobic degradability at 37 °C.

In contrast, composting under thermophilic aerobic conditions led to markedly accelerated degradation for all samples. After 97 days, neat PBS achieved approximately 10% mineralization and a mass loss of 40%, while both PBS–DLS copolymers showed significantly enhanced degradability. The degree of degradation in compost correlated positively with the DLS content: PBS–DLS 70–30 reached a mineralization level of 17%nearly double that of PBSand exhibited a mass loss of 67%, corresponding to a 26% increase compared to neat PBS. The enhanced degradation is attributed to the incorporation of flexible, amorphous DLS segments that reduce crystallinity, thereby improving water and microbial penetration. GPC confirmed polymer chain scission in all materials, with a substantial decrease in M n and M w values under composting conditions compared to AD, reflecting more extensive backbone cleavage in aerobic environments. FTIR spectroscopy further substantiated the occurrence of (bio)­degradation, revealing significant reductions in ester (≈1720 cm–1) absorption bands and broadening of hydroxyl (≈3400 cm–1) peaks. These spectral changes were most pronounced in PBS–DLS 70–30, indicating that higher soft segment content enhances susceptibility to the hydrolytic and microbial attack. Thermal analyses corroborated the molecular-level degradation. DSC revealed only minor variations in glass transition and melting temperatures, suggesting preservation of crystalline domain stability. However, a notable increase in crystallization temperature was observed for all materials following degradation, consistent with amorphous phase degradation, chain shortening, and enhanced segmental mobility that promote secondary crystallization. Morphological and visual assessments provided further insight into degradation mechanisms. SEM and OM revealed negligible surface changes after AD, with only minor cracking visible after 99 days. In contrast, composted samples exhibited extensive surface erosion, fissuring, and cavity formation, and biofilm formation, indicative of biodegradation. The density and depth of surface defects increased with DLS content, with PBS–DLS 70–30 showing the most pronounced deterioration. Macroscopic photographs clearly evidenced material disintegration, visually confirming degradation in compost. Overall, these findings demonstrate that PBS and PBS–DLS copolymers possess minimal biodegradability under mesophilic anaerobic conditions, rendering them unsuitable for AD-based waste management at 37 °C. However, thermophilic composting conditions substantially enhance their degradation performance, with the extent of degradation strongly dependent on DLS segment content. The superior biodegradation of PBS–DLS 70–30 underscores the beneficial effect of incorporating flexible, biobased DLS segments in promoting hydrolytic and microbial decomposition. The results highlight the potential of PBS–DLS copolymers as sustainable, biodegradable materials suitable for controlled degradation (depending on segmental composition) in industrial composting environments. Their tunable (bio)­degradation rates, coupled with favorable mechanical properties and biocompatibility with numerous biological hosts, make them promising candidates for eco-friendly applications, particularly in sustainable packaging, agricultural films, and biomedical devices where end-of-life biodegradability is essential.
