The relationship between mental health problems and risk of infectious diseases: A Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Ling Wang, Meilin Fang, Cunze Wang, Jinglin Li, Shasha Huang, Wen Li, Boyang Zhuang, Shurong Gong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The causal effects of mental health problems on the risk of infectious diseases remain vague. Investigating them via observational study is challenging as it presents possible confounding factors. Therefore, the objective of this study was to utilize Mendelian randomization (MR) techniques to evaluate the causal relationship between mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases. Multivariable MR analyses were performed using genome-wide association data for sleep disorders (N = 216,700), depression (N = 500,199), anxiety (N = 290,361), nervous feelings (N = 450,700), unspecified mental disorder (N = 218,792), pneumonia (N = 486,484), skin and subcutaneous tissue infection (SSTI; N = 218,792), intestinal infectious diseases (IIDs; N = 218,792), urinary tract infection (N = 463,010), and central nervous system (CNS) infections (N = 218,792) among individuals of European ancestry. Independent genetic variants significantly (P < 10−8) associated with each exposure were considered instruments. The primary analysis used an inverse variance-weighted method, followed by a series of sensitivity analyses. Genetically predicted sleep disorders were associated with an increased risk of SSTI (odds ratio [OR], 1.29 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.05–1.59]; P = .017). Genetically predicted depression was linked with an increased risk of CNS infections (OR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.00–2.53]; P = .049) and SSTI (1.24 [95% CI, 1.03–1.49]; P = .024). Genetically predicted anxiety was associated with IIDs (OR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.03–1.37]; P = .017) and SSTI (OR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.02–1.43]; P = .029). There was no significant causal evidence for genetic prediction of nervous feelings and unspecified mental disorders in IIDs, CNS infections, SSTI, pneumonia, or urinary tract infection. Sensitivity analyses showed that the above causal association estimates were robust. In this MR study, we demonstrated a causal relationship between sleep disorders, depression, anxiety, and the risk of infectious diseases. However, no evidence was found to support causality between nervous feelings, unspecified mental disorders, and the risk of infectious diseases.

1. Introduction

Infections are a major global public health problem, accounting for one-fourth to one-third of all deaths worldwide.[1,2] The annual death rate from infectious diseases in the United States is ≈1.7 million, adding to the global burden of disease.[3] The infection can potentially trigger an exaggerated response from the immune system to mistakenly attack healthy cells in the body, resulting in dysfunction of multiple vital organs and, in the most critical scenarios, cardiac failure and fatality. Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a frequently encountered infectious disease in adults, with a prevalence in women over the age of 65 years, which is approximately twice as high as the overall prevalence in women.[4] A recent national study in the United States found that intestinal infections are one of the most common infectious diseases among hospitalized patients.[5] Therefore, it is necessary to identify potential risk factors for infection in order to improve global public health.

Comprehensive research into the relationship between mental health and infectious diseases is a complex area. The prevalence of mental health problems among individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds is on the rise globally. Data from the World Health Organization indicate that around 20% to 25% of adults experience mental disorders. Research suggests that conditions such as anxiety and depression can compromise the immune system, making individuals more susceptible to infectious diseases. In patients with depression, there is often a rise in inflammatory markers, which may be attributed to inadequate sleep duration.[6] In addition, mental health problems can lead to unhealthy lifestyle choices that further elevate the risk of contracting infectious diseases. Further investigation is necessary to fully understand the relationship between specific infectious diseases and various populations. Conversely, studies have shown that contracting an infectious disease can negatively impact mental health. Mental disorders resulting from a combination of factors such as reduced life expectancy, weaker resilience, lower immunity, and heightened vulnerability to infections may increase the likelihood of developing other illnesses and experiencing poor physical health and treatment outcomes.[7,8] Conditions such as UTI and cystitis have been linked to symptoms of depression, anxiety, and neurotic personality traits, potentially complicating the treatment of infections.[9] However, the causal relationship between mental health problems and infection risk remains uncertain, as observational findings are subject to influence from other confounding variables and reverse causation.

Randomized controlled trials, considered the gold standard for establishing causal relationships in epidemiology, have faced challenges due to ethical constraints. In response, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as an alternative method to leverage existing genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for investigating causal links between risk factors and outcomes using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs).[10] Wang et al[11] discovered that genetic predisposition to waist circumference and hip circumference was associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism, aligning with findings from observational studies. Moreover, MR analysis confirmed a link between a diagnosis of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and a higher risk of hospitalization for novel COVID-19, supporting the established connection between severe COVID-19 and schizophrenia.[12] The versatility and efficacy of MR analysis have been demonstrated in diverse fields such as cardiovascular and psychiatry, further solidifying its utility. In the current study, we aimed to employ MR methods to determine the potential causal effects of mental health problems (sleep disorders, depression, anxiety, nervous feelings, and unspecified mental disorders) on the risk of 5 common infectious diseases: pneumonia, skin and subcutaneous tissue infection (SSTI), intestinal infectious diseases (IIDs), UTI, and central nervous system (CNS) infections. We hypothesized that there would be a directional causal effect between mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

We conducted MR analysis based on the publicly available summary-level data from GWASs to evaluate the causal relationships between mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases. MR studies should meet 3 essential assumptions of association, independence, and exclusivity: the IV should be highly correlated with the exposure variable, the IV was independent of observed or unobserved confounders, and the IVs affected outcome only via exposure.[13]

Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained from participants for all original studies. Because all analyses in this article were based on publicly available abstract data, this study did not require ethical approval from an institutional review board. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki.[14]

2.2. GWAS summary data for mental health problems

The exposure variable mental health problems were extracted from 3 GWAS pooled datasets: FinnGen, Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, and UK Biobank. Our study included 5 phenotypes related to mental health problems, sleep disorders (N = 216,700), depression (N = 500,199), anxiety (N = 290,361), nervous feelings (N = 450,700), and unspecified mental disorder (N = 218,792).

2.3. GWAS infectious disease summary data

Genetic determinants of pneumonia (ieu-b-4976; N = 486,484), SSTI (finn-b-L12; N = 218,792), IIDs (finn-b-AB1; N = 218,792), UTI (ukb-b-8814; N = 463,010), and CNS infections (finn-b-AB1; N = 218,792) were obtained from summary-level GWAS results in the UK Biobank and FinnGen, publicly available in the Integrative Epidemiologic Unit GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study with >500,000 participants from the United Kingdom.[15] Table 1 summarizes the data source, year, population, gender, and sample size of these GWAS.

2.4. Selection of genetic instruments

Based on the above GWAS summary data, a rigorous process was implemented to identify eligible single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs. First, we selected genetic variation SNP sites that hold genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) with regard to infectious diseases for pooling. Second, the threshold for the linkage disequilibrium parameter (r2) was set at 0.1 and the genetic distance at 1000 kb to select SNPs independently and eliminate the influence of linkage disequilibrium on the results. Finally, the F-statistic of each SNP was calculated using the formula β2SE2, where β and SE denote the estimation error and standard error of the effect allele, respectively.[16,17] In this study, SNPs with F-statistics ≤10 were excluded from the MR analysis, ensuring significance and avoiding weak IV bias.

2.5. MR analysis

MR was a novel analytical technique that employs genetic variations, typically SNPs, as IVs to investigate the causal association between modifiable risk factors and disease.[18] In the present study, we employed 5 complementary approaches to estimate the causal effects of mental health problems on the risk of infectious diseases, including the random-effects inverse variance-weighted (IVW),[19] MR-Egger regression,[20] weighted median (WM),[21] simple mode,[22] and weighted mode methods.[23] The odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of the above 5 methods were also visualized. This study primarily utilized IVW as the main method for this analysis, as it was the most widely used method in MR research. The other 4 methods were used as additional approaches for MR analysis. The IVW method had a strong ability to detect cause and effect, but it specifically requires that genetic variation only affects the outcome through exposure in the study. The other 4 approaches were utilized as supplementary methods for MR analysis.[24] MR-Egger did not rely on nonzero mean pleiotropy, as it can reduce statistical efficacy.[25] The WM approach complements MR-Egger regression, providing consistent estimates when at least 50% of the weights come from valid IVs.[26] Simple mode is a model-based evaluation method that offers both effectiveness and robustness.[27] In terms of pattern evaluation, the WM approach proves to be highly sensitive in handling complex data collection processes.[23] As an additional step, we used weighted mode and simple mode to enhance accuracy and stability.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of considering body mass index (BMI) and employment status as significant confounding factors when examining the relationship between mental health problems and infectious diseases.[28,29] BMI, a measure of obesity and overall health, is closely linked to various health issues, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and certain types of cancer. Mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression can influence an individual’s eating habits and physical activity levels, thereby impacting their BMI. Similarly, employment status plays a crucial role in mental health and infection risk, as job loss or dissatisfaction can lead to increased psychological stress and higher chances of mental health problems. Moreover, employment status can also affect an individual’s financial situation and social support network, further influencing their risk of infection. To assess the independent causal effects of mental health problems on infectious disease risk, a multivariable MR analysis was conducted using the multivariable IVW method, with adjustments for BMI and employment status.

2.6. Sensitivity analyses

In this study, sensitivity analyses comprised tests for heterogeneity and genetic pleiotropy, leave-one-out analysis, and a funnel plot. First, to estimate the heterogeneity of the IVW approach, we used the Racial Mendelian Randomisation R package to perform the Cochran Q test on SNPs that met the 3 hypotheses for assessing heterogeneity among individual genetic variants.[30] Heterogeneity exists if P < 0.05 for the Cochran Q test. Second, we used the MR-Egger intercept as an indicator to assess directional pleiotropy, with a P < 0.05 indicating horizontal pleiotropy.[31] Third, to assess whether MR estimates might be driven by single SNPs with particularly large horizontal pleiotropic effects, we performed a leave-one-out test.[32] Fourth, we applied funnel plots for visual inspection of asymmetry, which may indicate a violation of the MR assumption through horizontal pleiotropy.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Causal estimates were displayed as an OR and 95% CI. All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.2.2 software using the R packages “Mendelian Randomisation” and “Two Sample MR.” A 2-tailed P < .05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of the genetic instruments

An overview of the IVs included in each MR analysis is provided in Tables 1 to 5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N422. Following the instrument selection steps, 13 index SNPs were selected to genetically predict anxiety, 46 index SNPs were used to genetically predict depression, 46 index SNPs were chosen to genetically predict nervous feelings, and 3 index SNPs were chosen to genetically predict unspecified mental disorders. The F-statistics for these genetic instruments were all over 10, suggesting that no weak instruments were employed.

3.2. Multivariable MR analysis

After adjustment for BMI and employment, Figures 1 to 5 show the MR analysis estimates of the causal effect of mental health problems on the risk of infectious diseases using different methods. Genetically predicted anxiety was associated with a higher risk of IIDs (OR, 1.19 [95% CI, 1.03–1.37]; P = .017) and SSTI (OR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.02–1.43]; P = .029). However, it was not associated with a higher risk of CNS infections (OR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.57–1.67]; P = .920), pneumonia (OR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.80–1.17]; P = .735), and UTI (OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 1.00–1.00]; P = .947). Genetically predicted depression was associated with a higher risk of CNS infections (OR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.00–2.53]; P = .049) and SSTI (OR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.03–1.49]; P = .024), but it was not associated with a higher risk of IIDs (OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 0.99–1.31]; P = .063), pneumonia (OR, 1.13 95% CI 0.99–1.29]; P = .060), and UTI (OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 1.00–1.01]; P = .092). Genetically predicted sleep disorders were associated with a higher risk of SSTI (OR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.05–1.59]; P = .017). However, there were no significant differences with IIDs (OR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.74–1.23]; P = .711), CNS infections (OR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.45–1.49]; P = .517), pneumonia (OR, 1.06 [95% CI, 0.90–1.24]; P = .504) and UTI (OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 1.00–1.00]; P = .971). In addition, no evidence was found to support causal effects between nervous feelings, unspecified mental disorder, and the risk of IIDs (OR, 1.39 [95% CI, 0.71–2.72]; P = .340; OR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.90–1.13]; P = .906), CNS infections (OR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.06–5.64]; P = .647; OR, 1.11 [95% CI, 0.68–1.81]; P = .674), SSTI (OR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.33–1.60]; P = .425; OR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.83–1.15]; P = .815), pneumonia (OR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.25–0.93]; P = .030; OR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.87–1.12]; P = .839), and UTI (OR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.98–1.00]; P = .230; OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 1.00–1.00]; P = .617). 3.3. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted, including the Cochran Q test, the MR-Egger intercept test, leave-one-out analysis, and funnel plots. Table 6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N422, shows the results of the MR-Egger intercept test and the Cochran Q test. There was no horizontal pleiotropy between IVs and outcomes (all P values for the MR-Egger intercept test were >.05). However, heterogeneity was observed in the Cochran Q test analysis between anxiety and risk of pneumonia (Q = 24.62390; P = .0103445), depression and risk of IIDs (Q = 65.92373; P = .0226340), depression and risk of pneumonia (Q = 67.31678; P = .0171919), depression and risk of UTI (Q = 66.09056; P = .0171957), nervous feelings and risk of IIDs (Q = 65.95598; P = .0283348), and nervous feelings and risk of pneumonia (Q = 84.32264; P = .0009341). Although heterogeneity was detected in the above results, it did not invalidate the MR estimates because we used the random-effects IVW method as the main analysis, which compensated for combined heterogeneity. Furthermore, leave-one-out analysis indicated that the causal estimates of mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases were not driven by any single SNP (Figs. 6–10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N422). Finally, funnel plots showed that individual estimates of causal effects were symmetric on either side of the pooled point estimate, indicating that there was no horizontal pleiotropy in the results (Figs. 11–15, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N422).

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between 5 mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases. Our study showed that genetically predicted anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders were associated with an increased risk of SSTIs. In addition, no evidence was found for an association between nervous feelings and unspecified mental disorders, and the risk of infectious diseases. Taken together, our findings provided a better understanding of the role of mental health problems in infectious diseases, suggesting that reducing anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders may have beneficial health outcomes.

Mental health problems are a serious public health problem. Mental health problems and infectious diseases are linked in both directions. As mentioned above, some studies suggested that mental health problems increase the incidence of infections, while many other studies found that infections also have a significant impact on mental health problems. Studies showed that people with depression were at increased risk of subsequent autoimmune diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease).[33] A prospective study based on a Danish population found that the presence of depression may increase the risk of infection and that there was a relationship between the risk of infection and the number of depressive episodes, with a relative risk of infection of 64%.[34] Studies in humans focused on the association between sleep and infection risk rather than infection outcomes. Some studies showed that sleep affects various immune parameters, was associated with a reduced risk of infection, and can improve infection outcomes.[35] Individuals with mental disorders may have a higher risk of infection and poorer clinical outcomes due to a variety of mediating factors, including lower overall resilience and immune function.[7]

The mechanisms underlying the association of anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders with an increased risk of SSTIs are not known. In a state of anxiety, the autonomic nervous system was disturbed, mainly in the form of an increase in sympathetic activity and a decrease in parasympathetic activity. This disorder allowed the regulation of the immune system to be compromised, leading to a decrease in immunity. A related report suggested that the link between depression and infection risk may be mediated by depression-induced changes in the immune system. Depression can regulate a variety of internal physiological processes by triggering neuroendocrine stress signaling pathways (e.g., sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis), which may affect immune gene expression.[36] Depression produced a long-term stimulation of the neuroimmune connection, and this ongoing stimulation can alter the functioning of the immune system, making a person more susceptible to causing infections.[37,38] Evidence from experimental studies and epidemiological surveys in recent years has demonstrated that sleep disorders lead to an increased release of inflammatory mediators in the body, which activates the immune system and triggers an inflammatory response. In the case of SSTI, the most likely mechanism was that mental health problems lead to reduced immunity, loss of skin barrier integrity, and increased bacterial entry into the subcutaneous tissue. Further research is required to elucidate the underlying reasons why mental health problems are associated with a higher susceptibility to infections.

Our findings have significant implications for the analysis of mental health problems. Traditional models do not adequately capture the causal relationship between mental health problems and the risk of infectious diseases. In addition, the results of this study indicate that clinicians could potentially monitor the risk of infectious diseases in patients with anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders. These patients face an increased risk of various infectious diseases due to their anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders.

The strengths of this study include, first, that the MR design study can simulate a randomized controlled trial in an observational setting. The greater time and cost savings compared to a randomized controlled trial give MR analysis an advantage in assessing and screening for potential causal associations. MR studies reduced confounding bias and avoided reverse causality. Second, the pooled data included in this study were the most recent and largest GWAS studies available, providing sufficient sample size for high statistical power. Finally, MR studies based on IV theory can avoid reverse causation and unknown confounding bias.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this study focused on individuals of European origin, and it was not clear whether the conclusions obtained could be generalized to other populations; therefore, caution should be taken when applying the results to other races, and future results from other populations would be needed to confirm our conclusions.[39] Second, MR analyses can only investigate causal relationships and cannot investigate specific biological mechanisms. Finally, this study lacked subgroup data stratified by age and gender to compare differences in causal effects between subgroups.

5. Conclusion

In this MR study, we demonstrated a causal relationship between anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, and the risk of infectious diseases. However, no evidence was found to support a causal relationship between nervousness, mental disorders, and the risk of infectious diseases.
