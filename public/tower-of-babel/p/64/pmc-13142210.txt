Radiation Effects on Normal Brain in Subjects With Recurrent Glioblastoma by Spectroscopic MRI
Authors: Abram Abdou, Sulaiman Sheriff, Kelly Rojas, Miah Nicholls, Eric A Mellon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults and almost always recurs. In recurrent GBM, conventional MRI has limited ability to detect microscopic tumor infiltration and distinguish progression from treatment‐related change. Spectroscopic MRI (sMRI) provides quantitative metabolic maps across much of the brain, including choline (Cho) and N‐acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of healthy tissue. Because Cho/NAA is commonly used to define metabolically abnormal tissue, and prior radiation therapy (RT) can alter metabolite levels, we evaluated whether prior RT shifts baseline Cho/NAA in recurrent disease. We retrospectively studied 20 patients with recurrent GBM previously treated with maximal safe resection, RT, and temozolomide who underwent whole‐brain sMRI at recurrence. The median interval from RT completion to sMRI was 8.99 months. T1‐weighted images were co‐registered to sMRI in MIDAS and aligned with planning CT in MIM to generate radiation dose maps. MIDAS generated tissue‐water‐referenced Cho, NAA, and creatine (Cr) maps, and Cho/NAA was normalized to contralateral normal‐appearing white matter. Voxel‐wise linear regression was performed between prior radiation dose and metabolite values at recurrence. Higher prior dose was associated with reduced NAA (−0.26%/Gy, r 2 = 0.011, p < 0.001) and Cr (−0.18%/Gy, r 2 = 0.004, p < 0.001), while Cho changed minimally (−0.023%/Gy, r 2 = 0.001, p < 0.001). Accordingly, normalized Cho/NAA increased with dose, with a mean slope of 0.0018/Gy (p < 0.001). A standard Cho/NAA threshold of 2 corresponded to a median of 2.39 (range, 1.91–2.72) in tissue previously receiving 60 Gy. These findings suggest prior RT modestly elevates baseline Cho/NAA, primarily through NAA reduction, and that dose‐corrected Cho/NAA maps may improve tumor delineation in recurrent GBM.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) remains the most aggressive primary central nervous system tumor in adults, characterized by rapid proliferation, extensive infiltration, and a dismal prognosis [1, 2]. The standard treatment regimen for GBM typically includes maximal safe surgical resection followed by concurrent chemoradiation with temozolomide and adjuvant chemotherapy [3]. Despite these interventions, recurrence is almost inevitable. Conventional anatomical imaging, primarily through T1‐weighted postcontrast (T1PC) and T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (T2‐FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plays a central role in initial diagnosis, therapy planning, and posttreatment surveillance [4, 5]. However, these techniques are limited by their inability to detect microscopic tumor infiltration and to differentiate between viable tumor, treatment‐induced changes, and radiation necrosis [6, 7].

Spectroscopic MRI (sMRI) has emerged as a promising tool alongside conventional imaging. sMRI is the evolution of traditional single‐voxel MR spectroscopy and earlier magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging. It extends these techniques to provide near whole brain coverage with higher resolution [7, 8]. These large volume maps uniquely allow investigators to link metabolite changes to prior therapies. Metabolites such as choline (Cho), which is indicative of membrane turnover and cellular proliferation, and N‐acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal viability [9], can be quantified throughout the brain using sMRI [10, 11]. The ratio of Cho to NAA (Cho/NAA) has been extensively studied as an indicator of tumor, with elevated levels associated with GBM cellularity and recurrence [12, 13].

Radiation therapy (RT) is a critical component of GBM treatment with conventional fractionation doses of 60 Gy in 30 fractions [14]. Multiple studies have documented that radiation can lead to metabolic changes, most notably a reduction in NAA levels that is interpreted as neuronal injury, and alterations in Cho levels that may reflect cellular turnover or inflammation [15, 16]. These metabolic injuries can be detected by sMRI and offer insights into underlying brain changes beyond conventional T1PC and T2‐FLAIR [17, 18]. Given that RT dose varies across the brain during GBM treatment, we sought to determine the magnitude of variability in metabolite levels after RT.

Current literature focuses on primary GBM; however, the metabolic consequences of radiation‐induced injury remain at recurrence. Recurrent GBM presents a unique clinical challenge due to the confounding effects of previous treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation. In recurrent disease, distinguishing between tumor progression and posttherapeutic changes is particularly difficult using conventional MRI [19, 20]. This diagnostic uncertainty has spurred interest in integrating metabolic imaging into the evaluation of recurrent GBM [21].

In this study, we hypothesized that increasing local brain radiation dose during primary treatment alters metabolite ratios in a dose dependent fashion. While MR spectroscopy has been used in recurrent GBM, sMRI is a novel technique to analyze whole brain maps from no dose to full dose as opposed to single voxels or partial brain magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging. As such, adjustments in radiotherapy planning based on sMRI at time of recurrence could be necessary due to prior RT.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This IRB‐approved retrospective study was conducted on 20 patients with rGBM diagnosed at the University of Miami. All patients underwent the same treatment regimen for their initial primary GBM; maximal safe surgical resection followed by radiation to 60 Gy in 30 fractions with concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide. These patients subsequently developed probable recurrence and underwent further evaluation including whole brain sMRI. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

MRI and sMRI Data Acquisition

Imaging in the recurrent setting was performed on a 3 T system (Skyra or Vida, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). Anatomical imaging included high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted imaging, acquired using a Magnetization Prepared Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) sequence before and after gadolinium‐based contrast agent administration with a voxel resolution of 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.7 mm3, TR/TE/TI of 2300/2.41/930 ms, a flip angle of 9°, and a matrix size of 320 × 216 × 192.

Whole‐brain sMRI data were acquired using a spatial‐spectral echo‐planar readout with spin‐echo excitation (TR/TE = 1551/50.0 ms) and nonselective lipid inversion‐nulling (TI = 198 ms). The field‐of‐view for the sMRI acquisition was 280 × 280 × 180 mm3 with a matrix of 50 × 50 across 18 slices, elliptical k‐space encoding, echo train length of 1000 points, and bandwidth of 2500 Hz. After processing in MIDAS interpolated the metabolite maps, yielding an effective voxel size of 4.4 mm × 4.4 mm × 5.6 mm. The total acquisition time for sMRI was 15 min.

Image Registration and Metabolite Quantification

Contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted images were acquired at recurrence and were co‐registered with the sMRI data using the MIDAS software suite (University of Miami, Miami, Florida, USA) [22]. A matched, interleaved, EPSI water reference acquisition was used as the spatial reference for sMRI registration and processing [23]. Voxel‐water referenced metabolite maps of Cho, NAA, and creatine were generated [24]. Cho/NAA ratios were computed and then normalized to the contralateral normal‐appearing white matter (termed Cho:NAA) to minimize scanner and patient specific intersession and intersubject variabilities [25, 26]. The co‐registered sMRI data were subsequently aligned with the original planning CT scans using rigid multimodality image registration in MIM software (MIM Software Inc., Beachwood, Ohio, USA). The CT data, which contained the radiation dose distribution, were used to generate radiation dose maps by applying the CT to MRI transform so that the dose grid was spatially aligned to the sMRI scans. Data Processing and Analysis

After image registration and metabolite quantification, the DICOM images were exported for further analysis. To remove the effect of recurrent disease, the original treatment planning tumor volume from time of first treatment and any areas of elevated Cho:NAA ratio suspected to be related to disease were removed from the analysis. Small numbers of voxels at or close to prescription dose (60 Gy) remain in the analyses that were adjacent to the original planning tumor volume due to imperfect conformity inherent to radiation therapy planning. The sMRI scans were first cleaned with 3D Slicer (Version 5.6.2) [27]. Irrelevant or noisy voxels were removed from Cho:NAA maps, with the focused data then being processed using Python across all scans on a per patient basis. Voxels with dose values below 0.5 Gy or excluded from the metabolite maps by automated MIDAS spectral quality checking were excluded from analysis. The remaining data points were used to compute the linear correlation between radiation dose and metabolite intensities via linear regression, yielding slope, intercept, and R 2 values. Scatter plots with the fitted regression line were generated.

Univariate Analysis

Univariate correlation analyses were performed to assess whether factors listed in Table 1 such as age at recurrence and days between treatment were associated with changes in Cho, Cre, and NAA values. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. These analyses aimed to determine whether clinical factors were predictive of metabolic shifts observed on sMRI at recurrence.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 20 patients with recurrent GBM were included in this analysis (Table 1). The median age at the time of recurrence was 52.7 years. The cohort consisted of 11 females (55%) and nine males (45%). MGMT promoter methylation status was unmethylated in 14 patients (70%), hypermethylated in five patients (25%), and unknown in one patient (5%). Most patients (85%) had not undergone surgical resection for their recurrence, while three patients (15%) had a resection.

An exemplary patient's NAA, Cho, Cr, and Cho:NAA levels are plotted compared to the primary radiation course in Figure 2. NAA intensity decreased with dose at −0.66% per Gy. Cho showed only negligible change with dose (0.049%/Gy). Creatine exhibited a decline (−0.10%/Gy). The Cho:NAA ratio increased with dose as well (1.36%/Gy), likely driven by the decrease in NAA.

These trends align with cohort‐wide findings.

NAA Levels

A dose‐dependent reduction in NAA was observed in 18 of 20 patients (Figure 3). Normalized analysis showed that NAA decreased by a mean −0.26% per Gy (r 2 = 0.011, p < 0.001). Individual slopes ranged from −0.66% to 0.24%/Gy.

Linear regression analysis of metabolite intensities versus radiation dose across normal‐appearing brain tissue in all patients. All metabolite intensities were water normalized on a per patient basis. (a) NAA levels demonstrated a significant dose‐dependent decline, with an average slope of –‐51.41 a.u./Gy (r 2 = 0.012, p < 0.001). (b) Choline (Cho) levels showed minimal change, increasing by an average of −3.45 a.u./Gy. (c) Creatine (Cr) levels exhibited a modest decrease, averaging −32.40 a.u./Gy.

Cho Levels

In contrast to NAA, Cho levels exhibited only minimal changes with increasing radiation dose (Figure 3). The regression analysis revealed an average decrease of −0.02%/Gy (r 2 = 0.0008, p < 0.001), with slopes varying from −0.62% to 0.46%/Gy among patients. These modest alterations indicate that the cellular proliferation measured by Cho is less affected by radiation doses.

Cr Levels

Creatine levels also showed only slight changes in response to radiation exposure (Figure 3). The regression analysis indicated a mean decrease in Cr of −0.18%/Gy (r 2 = 0.004, p < 0.001) with a range from −0.59% to 0.35%/Gy.

Cho:NAA Ratio

Linear regression analysis of normal‐appearing brain tissue revealed a statistically significant, dose‐dependent increase in the Cho:NAA ratio (Figure 4). On a normalized scale, the regression yielded an average slope of 0.0018/Gy (r 2 = 0.0043, p < 0.001). In absolute terms, the Cho:NAA ratio exhibited a mean slope of 0.0027 a.u./Gy, with individual patient slopes ranging from −0.0015 to 0.012/Gy (Figure 5). These findings indicate that modest increases in radiation dose are associated with a measurable elevation in the Cho:NAA ratio.

Group Analysis

When aggregating data across all 20 patients, the overall trends remained consistent with the voxel‐level findings. Regions exposed to higher radiation doses consistently exhibited elevated Cho:NAA ratios and reduced NAA levels. Scatter plots of each of the metabolites plotted against dose for a sample patient are shown in Figure 2. The statistical significance of these associations (p < 0.001) across the cohort confirms the presence of a weak, yet reliable, dose‐dependent metabolic effect.

Univariate Analysis

Univariate analysis revealed no statistically significant correlations between age at recurrence or days between treatment across any of the metabolic values. Pearson correlation coefficients were low across all pairings, and no associations met the threshold for significance. These findings suggest that neither patient age at recurrence nor the interval between treatments had a measurable impact on sMRI‐detected metabolic alterations at the time of recurrence.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that radiation therapy induces dose‐dependent alterations in sMRI metabolite ratios that become evident when planning RT for rGBM. Voxel‐wise linear‐regression analysis showed that the Cho:NAA ratio of normal‐appearing brain increases with dose, a change primarily driven by reductions in NAA. Although dose‐dependent reductions in NAA after irradiation have been reported previously, the contribution of this work is the demonstration that this effect produces a planning relevant baseline shift in Cho:NAA across previously irradiated brain when sMRI is used for tumor delineation.

The observed decline in NAA likely occurs because NAA is a marker of neuronal integrity [28]. This suggests that radiation‐induced neuronal injury occurred from treatment in the primary setting is the driving factor causing the increase in the Cho/NAA ratio. In contrast, Cho levels showed minimal alterations, and creatine (Cr) demonstrated a smaller change. These findings correlate with previous work emphasizing the ability of NAA as a biomarker for neuronal health after the delivery of radiation [29].

Importantly, because Cho:NAA is frequently used to identify abnormal tissue, dose‐related NAA suppression creates a predictable risk of elevated Cho:NAA in nontumor tissue within a high dose region.

Due to the prevalence of treatment after‐effects changing metabolite concentrations, a dose correction map is proposed to adjust the Cho:NAA threshold for clinical interpretation. The analysis estimates that a Cho:NAA threshold of 2 at 0 Gy may correspond to an average threshold of approximately 2.11 at 60 Gy, meaning that the threshold for defining tumor in rGBM should be slightly higher than for primary GBM in a previously radiated area. Figure 6 displays a metabolite map of Cho:NAA ratios on a patient with a larger gradient based on dose, with a dose‐corrected map implying that a threshold of 2 at 0 Gy corresponds to 2.22 at 60 Gy. This mapping suggests that incorporating dose corrections into Cho:NAA thresholds may improve the delineation of high‐risk tumor regions in recurrent GBM, potentially enhancing the accuracy of radiotherapy planning.

Our work is consistent with earlier spectroscopy work that explicitly considered normal brain metabolic changes before and after radiotherapy. Prior single‐voxel region of interest 1H‐MRS studies have repeatedly shown that radiation injury suppresses NAA in tissue that can otherwise appear healthy [30, 31]. Moreover, a dose‐stratified analysis demonstrated that NAA/Cr decreases were shown in not just higher dose areas, but even in relatively low dose regions [32]. Other groups have previously demonstrated that global NAA loss is detectable after RT, supporting NAA as a sensitive marker of radiation injury [33]. This study builds upon the prior work to show a linear relationship between prior RT dose and metabolite alterations and generates metabolite correction maps used for rGBM irradiation planning. Incorporating a patient‐specific dose correction using normal‐appearing brain as an internal control may improve the specificity of sMRI delineation, whereas a fixed threshold may be skewed by prior radiation.

Previous MR‐spectroscopy studies have shown that Cho:NAA ratios are an effective way of distinguishing between radiation necrosis and previously treated brain [34]. Our results reproduce this pattern and confirm that metabolic shifts are distinguishable after radiation. The contribution of this work is voxel level dose adjustment, in which, by scaling the Cho:NAA threshold according to the dose, this map can be embedded in the reirradiation planning to better delineate tumor. This is particularly relevant for recurrent GBM, where conventional imaging often falls short in distinguishing between tumor progression and treatment‐related changes [35, 36].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and sample size of 20 patients may limit the generalizability of our findings. The low r 2 values observed in our regression analyses suggest that while radiation dose is statistically significant, it accounts for only a modest portion of the variability in metabolite levels. Additional factors such as other components of the primary treatment, different baseline levels in different regions of the brain, patient‐specific physiology, and other variations likely contribute to the observed metabolic profiles.

As clinical trials are underway and planned for sMRI‐guided radiotherapy in rGBM (NCT05284643), implementing correction maps to sMRI after primary GBM treatment may improve tumor delineation. Histologic validation of sMRI in rGBM in comparison to primary GBM may also help better define the correct Cho:NAA ratio for high‐risk tumor targeting.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that radiation therapy for GBM induces a dose‐dependent decline in NAA that can be mapped with sMRI. This leads to a baseline increase in Cho:NAA used to define GBM for RT targeting. Dose‐correction maps are proposed to account for this Cho:NAA alteration in radiotherapy planning. Such maps may be useful when incorporated into sMRI‐guided rGBM RT trials.
