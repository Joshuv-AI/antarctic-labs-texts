Thermally stable 2D YMnO3 enabling blue visible camouflage with mid-infrared transparency
Authors: Ziyuan Zhu, Hanyuan Zhang, Rong Hu, Yonggang Yao, Weilin Xu, Jun Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blue coloration is highly desirable for visible camouflage in aerospace environments, while effective mid-infrared camouflage requires surface layers that do not disturb the underlying radiative boundary under thermal detection. Achieving these two functions simultaneously at elevated temperatures remains fundamentally challenging, because the electronic processes responsible for blue visible appearance are intrinsically coupled to lattice polarization and phonon-mediated optical responses, which commonly destabilize the mid-infrared transparency window. Here, this long-standing constraint is overcome by realizing a two-dimensional hexagonal YMnO3 system through microwave-shock synthesis that integrates thermal robustness, a blue visible appearance, and mid-infrared transparency. The non-equilibrium synthesis kinetically stabilizes a low-dimensional oxide architecture in which dimensional confinement suppresses long-range polarization contributions, while rigid Mn-O polyhedral units reinforce short-range bonding. These cooperative effects reduce the splitting between longitudinal and transverse optical phonons and suppress Reststrahlen-band expansion, enabling visible coloration to coexist with mid-infrared camouflage even under high-temperature conditions. This work establishes a structure-based, non-equilibrium strategy for decoupling visible and infrared optical constraints in thermally stable oxides, offering a general paradigm for high-temperature, cross-spectral camouflage materials.

Introduction

The exterior surfaces of modern aircraft and advanced engineering systems are simultaneously interrogated by two fundamentally different observation modalities: optical contrast in the visible spectrum and thermal radiation in the mid-infrared (MIR). The former governs visual detectability against natural backgrounds, whereas the latter dictates the apparent signature in infrared imaging and radiometric sensing. A blue appearance provides robust visual camouflage in maritime and sky-dominated environments, while the 2.5–20 μm spectral range encompasses the dominant window of thermal emission. These optical constraints are further compounded by severe thermal loads arising from aerodynamic heating, exhaust radiation, and external thermal shocks, which impose stringent requirements on high-temperature stability. Consequently, an ideal surface functional layer would preserve a blue visible appearance while remaining effectively transparent in the MIR, allowing the radiative boundary condition to be dictated by the underlying metallic structure. This goal, however, has long been regarded as nearly unattainable. The fundamental obstacle does not lie in fabrication or geometric control, but rather in the fact that, across the vastly separated visible and MIR frequency regimes, electronic transitions, lattice polarization, and phonon-mediated optical responses are governed by the same intrinsic physical couplings, such that modifying visible appearance almost inevitably perturbs MIR radiative behavior.

A blue appearance does not originate from absorption of blue light itself, but from a non-uniform distribution of spectral reflectance across the visible range, which, when weighted by the human eye response, yields a blue color perception. In inorganic crystalline materials, such selective reflectance is commonly realized through wavelength-dependent attenuation associated with specific electronic transitions. Critically, these electronic processes are often accompanied by local charge redistribution and enhanced electron-lattice coupling, which amplify lattice polarization and increase the Born effective charge1. In polar crystals, the resulting polarization manifests directly in lattice dynamics through the longitudinal-transverse optical (LO–TO) phonon splitting2. The magnitude of this splitting reflects a competition between two contributions: long-range Coulomb interactions, governed by the Born effective charge and the high-frequency dielectric constant, which non-analytically elevate the longitudinal optical mode3; and short-range chemical bonding forces, together with effective mass, which define the intrinsic frequency of the transverse optical mode4. When polarization enhancement causes the long-range term to dominate, LO–TO splitting increases substantially, broadening the Reststrahlen band5 and driving the real part of the dielectric function negative in the MIR6. This, in turn, induces strong reflection and suppresses MIR transmission (Fig. 1a)7. Under realistic service conditions, the requirement of high-temperature stability further narrows the range of viable material systems. To withstand sustained thermal shock and oxidative environments, candidate materials must possess robust inorganic crystal frameworks, which in principle excludes organic pigments and weakly coordinated networks8–10. Within this constraint, prior efforts have largely followed two routes. One approach seeks rare material exceptions that combine a blue appearance with partial MIR transparency, while another relies on engineering strategies such as thinning, dispersion, or composite design to reduce effective MIR losses. However, the former remains limited by polarization-driven LO–TO splitting and the associated expansion of the Reststrahlen reflection band11, whereas the latter does not alter the phonon-defined radiative boundary and therefore mitigates losses only superficially12. From a physical standpoint, overcoming this limitation requires simultaneously suppressing long-range polarization effects and strengthening short-range bonding rigidity within the same lattice. Two complementary strategies emerge: dimensional confinement can suppress out-of-plane polarization fields, thereby weakening the non-analytic long-range Coulomb contribution near the Γ point and narrowing LO–TO splitting (Fig. 1b)13; rigid local structural units can elevate the intrinsic transverse optical phonon frequency14, compressing the splitting from the short-range side and suppressing MIR reflection (Fig. 1c)15,16. Under thermodynamic equilibrium, however, these two structural characteristics are typically incompatible in thermally stable inorganic crystals, as low-dimensional softness and high bonding rigidity tend to reside in distinct structural motifs.

Here, this structural incompatibility is resolved by employing microwave-shock synthesis to realize a hexagonal two-dimensional (2D) YMnO3 system that simultaneously exhibits high-temperature stability, a blue visible appearance, and high MIR transparency. The ultrafast volumetric heating and extremely short high-temperature dwell time inherent to the microwave-shock process establish a strongly non-equilibrium synthesis window, enabling low-dimensional structural motifs to be kinetically frozen before densification and phase transformation can occur. The resulting hexagonal 2D YMnO3 architecture suppresses long-range polarization through dimensional confinement while maintaining rigid Mn–O polyhedral units that reinforce short-range bonding, collectively compressing LO–TO splitting and inhibiting the expansion of the Reststrahlen band. As a consequence, the spectral modulation required for a blue appearance no longer inevitably triggers collapse of the MIR transparency window, but instead coexists stably with high MIR transmission under elevated temperatures. Systematic structural modulation further reveals a well-defined correspondence between blue color depth and MIR window retention, establishing a threshold criterion for achieving tunable visible coloration while preserving MIR transparency. These results demonstrate that, by harnessing non-equilibrium synthesis to cooperatively engineer long-range polarization and short-range bonding in thermally stable oxides, a long-standing conflict between visible camouflage and MIR radiative constraints can be transformed into a predictable and designable materials problem, opening a new physical paradigm for high-temperature, cross-spectral functional interfaces.

Results

Shock confinement locks the 2D framework and Mn–O coordination rigidity

Establishing a structurally well-defined 2D nanosheet architecture together with rigid structural units is a prerequisite for decoupling visible-blue absorption from MIR transparency limitations17,18. Figure 2a schematically summarizes a proposed formation pathway for the YMnO3 nanosheets under microwave shock conditions using a precursor system composed of YCl3, MnCl2, NH4Cl, and C6H12O6. During microwave-assisted synthesis, rapid energy uptake induces a sharp temperature rise to approximately 850 °C. Under this rapid thermal input, glucose is converted into a carbonaceous polymeric network that provides a spatially continuous matrix for homogeneous anchoring of Y and Mn species and constrained mass transport, thereby steering the early-stage morphology evolution from particle agglomeration toward the development of continuous lamellar domains. In parallel, NH4Cl decomposition releases gas and induces bubbling; the resulting bubble-templated porosity and associated stress perturbations facilitate interfacial enrichment of the solid phase and delineate the nanosheet contour. The short-duration, high-temperature pulse characteristic of microwave shock further suppresses coarsening and densification, thereby enabling the formation of a stable 2D nanosheet architecture. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that the YMnO3 is dominated by continuous two-dimensional lamellar domains (Fig. S1), while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging further resolves the overall sheet contour and edge continuity at the nanoscale (Fig. 2b). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements of independently sampled nanosheets show apparent edge step heights of approximately 0.6–0.9 nm (Fig. S2), consistent with an ultrathin nanosheet morphology. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping (EDS mapping) reveals strong spatial overlap of Y, Mn, and O across the sheet domains (Fig. S3); within the spatial resolution of the measurement, no resolvable elemental enrichment or phase segregation is observed, supporting compositional uniformity at the mapping scale. SAED displays discrete diffraction spots (Fig. 2c, inset), indicating a clear in-plane orientation and high crystallographic ordering. Lattice-resolved HRTEM further shows continuous fringes (Fig. 2c and Fig. S4), yielding a representative interplanar spacing of d (110) ≈ 0.306 nm that matches the (110) spacing of hexagonal YMnO3. To extend the local crystallographic signatures to the bulk-averaged structure, powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) coupled with Rietveld refinement is performed, showing good agreement between the calculated and experimental patterns over the full angular range (Rwp = 6.9%, Rp = 5.43%, χ2 = 2.49) (Fig. 2d). All reflections can be consistently indexed to hexagonal YMnO3; notably, the prominent peak cluster at 2θ = 30–33° can be assigned to the (110), (111), (004), and (112) reflections, among which the (110) reflection is consistent with the HRTEM-derived d (110) ≈ 0.306 nm across the local-to-bulk length scales19. Collectively, these results indicate that the rapid thermal history associated with microwave processing promotes the crystallization of hexagonal YMnO3, with no obvious crystalline secondary phase detectable by XRD. Repeated XRD/Rietveld refinements from independent microwave-shock syntheses further confirm the reproducible formation of hexagonal YMnO3 without detectable crystalline secondary phases (Fig. S5).

With long-range ordering and an ultrathin morphology established, vibrational spectroscopies are further employed to constrain local bonding configurations and lattice dynamics, thereby assessing phonon-involved loss channels relevant to MIR transmittance. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) resolves the expected lattice-vibration bands in the 400–700 cm−1 region (Fig. 2e). Features near 502 and 597 cm−1 are attributed to Y–O and Mn–O vibrations, respectively, with an additional contribution near 430 cm−1 assignable to O–Mn–O bending; no additional broad absorption is observed, supporting an oxide coordination framework without prominent extrinsic species20,21. Raman spectroscopy resolves multiple phonon modes over 100–1000 cm−1 and shows a pronounced A₁(TO) mode at 681 cm−1 (Fig. S6), providing a quantitative anchor for subsequent discussion of TO-mode stability and phonon-enabled MIR attenuation. In the extended Raman region covering the carbon D/G bands, no discernible features are observed near 1350 and 1580 cm−1 (Fig. S7), suggesting that no substantial sp2-rich carbonaceous phase is retained in the final product.

XPS is used to bind surface chemical states and defect-associated species relevant to extrinsic MIR loss (Fig. S8): Y 3d shows a spin–orbit doublet at 155.8 and 157.9 eV consistent with Y3+, while Mn 2p main features at 641.7 and 652.8 eV fall within the reported Mn3+ window and are therefore treated as constraints supporting predominantly trivalent Mn, given possible multiplet splitting and mixed-valence contributions20. The O 1s envelope can be deconvoluted into a dominant lattice-oxygen component at ~529–530 eV (Olat) and higher-binding-energy components that are commonly associated with surface hydroxyl adsorbate species and other non-lattice oxygen environments. EPR shows only a weak resonance at g = 2.003 (Fig. S9)22, and together with the restrained non-lattice O 1s components, this indicates a low detectable density of vacancy-related paramagnetic centers, consistent with minimized defect-assisted loss channels during MIR propagation.

X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements provide a local-structure-sensitive constraint on Mn coordination integrity and disorder, which is central to the short-range-bonding branch of the decoupling framework. Mn K-edge XANES of 2D YMnO3 closely overlaps with the bulk YMnO3 reference and is clearly distinct from Mn foil (Fig. 2f)23, indicating a similar oxidized Mn environment to the hexagonal YMnO3 lattice. In the EXAFS Fourier transforms, the first-shell Mn–O contribution remains pronounced for the 2D sample (Fig. 2g), evidencing preserved nearest-neighbor coordination. Quantitative fitting yields an essentially identical Mn–O distance (R = 2.052 Å for 2D vs 2.059 Å for bulk) with a σ2 value that is comparable to, or lower than, the bulk reference under the same fitting protocol, indicating no evidence of increased first-shell disorder in the nanosheet architecture23. Wavelet-transform maps show k–R intensity features consistent with Mn–O and Mn–Mn backscattering contributions (Fig. 2h, i), supporting the path assignments used in the EXAFS analysis24. Collectively, these local constraints substantiate the “short-range bond-retention rigidity” route in Fig. 1 by showing that dimensional confinement does not compromise Mn–O coordination integrity. DFT-based Bader charge and electron localization function analyses further support the electronic-structure aspect of this confinement effect. Compared with bulk YMnO3, 2D YMnO3 exhibits slightly reduced net Bader charges for Y, Mn, and O, indicating that the ultrathin configuration does not enhance static charge separation within the polar oxide lattice (Fig. S10 and Table S1). Meanwhile, ELF maps plotted on the same 0–1 scale show largely preserved local electron localization around the Y–O/Mn–O coordination framework. These results complement the XAFS evidence and provide electronic-structure-level support for the proposed decoupling picture, in which reduced static charge separation and preserved local oxide coordination are considered favorable for suppressing MIR lattice-loss channels.

To further substantiate the phonon-optical aspect of this decoupling mechanism, polarized infrared reflectance spectra of bulk and 2D YMnO3 were fitted using an effective factorized dielectric-function model25, with the detailed fitting results provided in the Supplementary Information. The representative TO and LO phonon frequencies were extracted from the fitted dielectric function rather than directly assigned from apparent reflectance extrema, enabling a quantitative comparison of LO–TO splitting between the two systems. The extracted results show reduced representative LO–TO splitting in 2D YMnO3 relative to bulk YMnO3, with reductions of 15.06–45.03% for the selected fitted polar modes (Fig. S11 and Table S2). The dielectric functions obtained from the same fitting further reveal a compressed negative-ε1 Reststrahlen response and weakened ε₂ phonon-loss features in 2D YMnO3, providing dielectric-function evidence for restrained Reststrahlen-band expansion (Fig. S12). In addition, the lower oscillator-strength-derived mean dynamic effective charges of 2D YMnO3 further support a reduced dynamic polarization response under dimensional confinement (Table S3).

Film formation defines a low-radiance boundary with cross-scale invariance

The geometric dimensions and crystallographic ordering of the 2D nanosheets provide the structural preconditions for interfacial optical regulation. Whether this capability can be effectively expressed at the device scale depends on the extent to which the nanosheet architecture can be stably converted into a processable dispersion that forms a continuous coating. Accordingly, Fig. S13 compares the viscosity-shear rate relationships of the YMnO3 ink and a commercial blue ink to benchmark the process feasibility for subsequent coating fabrication. The results show that the 2D YMnO3 ink exhibits a similarly stable viscosity response over a broad shear-rate range, evidencing favorable flow characteristics suitable for spray deposition26.

To establish a device-level reference boundary with a controllable and low radiative background, 2D YMnO3 was directly coated on stainless steel (SLS) substrates featuring a smooth surface and spectrally stable, low-emissivity radiative background. This design enables characterization of the effective spectral response of the coating-substrate composite while minimizing uncertainties arising from substrate-to-substrate variations. The simulated spectrum (Fig. S14) indicates that SLS maintains a low emissivity (<0.1) within the 8–13 μm atmospheric window27,28, thereby suppressing substrate-induced radiative interference and improving the reliability of optical evaluation for the coating. Figure 3a presents the UV–vis–NIR and MIR reflectance spectra of 2D YMnO3 on SLS, which define the cross-spectral boundary conditions linking solar input and thermal-radiation output28. Built on these spectral constraints, Fig. 3b shows a consistent trend of infrared apparent response across different substrates (glass, silicon, and SLS), indicating that the low-radiance appearance is primarily governed by the effective-emissivity boundary imposed by the coating and is therefore transferable29. Moreover, the skin-mounted comparison in Fig. 3c shows that a 2D YMnO3-coated PE film exhibits an apparent temperature essentially identical to that of pristine PE, indirectly confirming under conformal skin-contact conditions that the coating does not introduce resolvable additional radiative losses in the mid-infrared. Figure 3d reveals a weak increase in apparent radiation temperature with increasing film thickness: when the thickness increases from 7 to 35 μm the brightness temperature rises only from 52.2 °C to 56.7 °C. This behavior suggests that thicker films add an incremental equivalent radiative absorptive contribution30, yet the magnitude remains limited and does not drive the brightness temperature toward the actual hot-plate temperature (~93 °C). Therefore, within the thickness range examined, the low-radiance boundary established by the coating exhibits strong thickness robustness. Consistently, in Fig. 3e, a 25 μm film maintains a stable apparent temperature level and infrared-image contrast during prolonged exposure at 100 °C, demonstrating time-domain reliability of this radiative boundary within the operational temperature window.

For more stringent hot-side conditions, a thermal-endurance boundary was established by annealing at 900 °C, and the retention of the low-radiance appearance after thermal treatment was evaluated by benchmarking 2D YMnO3 against Prussian blue (PB). The infrared thermographs in Fig. 3f show that after 0–8 h of annealing, when samples are placed on a 100 °C hot plate, the apparent radiation temperature of 2D YMnO3 remains stable in the range of 48.5–53.9 °C with only a gradual drift. In contrast, the PB reading increases rapidly after short-time annealing and approaches the hot-plate setpoint, indicating that high-temperature treatment induces failure of its radiative-regulation capability31. Quantitative extraction of the thermographic readings in Fig. 3g further corroborates this trend: YMnO3 preserves the low-brightness-temperature regime, whereas PB rapidly converges to 100 °C, highlighting the superior retention of infrared appearance regulation in 2D YMnO3 after high-temperature processing. To further assess prolonged high-temperature oxidative stability, the YMnO₃-coated SLS sample was annealed in air at 900 °C for 24 h. The broadband reflectance and infrared thermography results show that the optical response and low-apparent-temperature behavior are largely retained, with the apparent temperature changing only slightly from 53.6 °C to 53.8 °C under identical conditions (Fig. S15 and Fig. S16). These results indicate that the YMnO3 coating retains its infrared-regulation function under the thermal conditions examined in this work, with no obvious evidence of rapid intrinsic degradation. The composition-phase boundary sets the visible-tuning space and the MIR-window threshold

Color modulation is quantified by CIE 1931 chromaticity coordinates calculated from tristimulus values (X, Y, Z) derived from the reflectance spectra34. The coordinates follow the standard definitions in Eqs. (4) and (5) (Fig. 4d). All points remain in the blue region while migrating continuously within the same hue family, confirming controllable lightness and saturation within a single blue gamut rather than a cross-hue transition. These CIE coordinates describe the visible appearance under the present coating/substrate configuration and measurement geometry; however, without wavelength-dependent optical constants or angle-resolved measurements, possible viewing-angle, interference, and substrate effects should be considered under different application conditions.

Structural evolution is resolved by XRD coupled with Rietveld refinement (Fig. 4e), with pristine Y2O3 included as an MIR-window oxide reference35. With increasing xMn, peak positions and profiles evolve continuously. At xMn = 0.1, the pattern remains largely Y2O3-like but shows discernible shifts consistent with Mn-induced lattice perturbation. At xMn = 0.2, additional diffraction contributions appear, indicating a transition from a doped-host regime to multiphase coexistence. The enlarged 28°–32° region (Fig. 4f) and the refined phase weight fractions (GSAS-II; Fig. S17) further resolve the phase partitioning among Y2O3, YMnO3, and Mn3O4. At xMn = 0.2, YMnO3 becomes quantifiable with a refined fraction of 20.6 wt.% (with Y2O3 as the balance), whereas Mn3O4 emerges at xMn = 0.6 (28.5 wt%), establishing a structural boundary that constrains the MIR window36.

Broadband reflectance and infrared thermography corroborate the MIR consequences of this boundary. Coatings with xMn = 0.1–0.5 retain a stable high-reflectance plateau in the MIR with an average reflectance of approximately 0.89 and limited intra-group variation (Fig. 4h), accompanied by an apparent radiation temperature of approximately 50 °C on a 100 °C hot plate (Fig. 4g). The xMn = 0.5 curve is shown in Fig. 3 and is nearly indistinguishable from xMn = 0.4 in the MIR, so it is not replotted in Fig. 4h for clarity. In contrast, at xMn = 0.6, the MIR average reflectance drops to 0.568, and the apparent temperature rises to 55.9 °C. Together with the emergence of Mn3O4 in XRD, this assigns window degradation to the introduction of a highly absorbing phase that collapses the MIR reflectance plateau.

An MIR-transparent layer preserves the metallic IR boundary to close the dual-band strategy

To address the visible and MIR dual-band camouflage requirement for aerospace exterior surfaces, a device-level implementation route is established based on “decoupling visible coloration from the MIR radiative boundary condition”17,33,37. The central premise is that YMnO3 imparts a blue appearance in the visible via selective reflection, while maintaining low-loss transmission over 2.5–20 μm. This low-loss MIR response arises from the 2D YMnO3 layer itself, whereas its device-level consequence in the coating/substrate geometry is the preservation of the low-emissivity radiative boundary of the underlying metal. Within the MIR range relevant to thermographic detection, the highly reflective, low-emissivity boundary condition of the metallic skin can therefore remain the dominant determinant of the effective emissivity of the overall system, thereby suppressing the apparent radiative signature (Fig. 5a)38,39. The coating response to rapid flame impingement is further examined using an ethanol-flame test40. Short exposure (t = 5 s; ~750 °C) is used to examine whether irreversible loss channels emerge under rapid heating and strong convective forcing that could dominantly alter the radiative boundary condition. When the exposure is extended to 53 s, a small amount of carbon deposition is observed; this extrinsic absorber may introduce additional MIR losses. This observation suggests that the changes observed after prolonged flame exposure are more plausibly attributed to surface carbon deposition and the associated extrinsic MIR losses, rather than to rapid intrinsic degradation of the YMnO3 phase itself. Accordingly, the long-duration condition is used only to flag a potential contamination pathway and is not taken as a basis for extrapolating intrinsic performance (Fig. 5b). Under identical thermography settings, the apparent temperatures of the bare surface, the spray-coated YMnO3 surface, and the YMnO3 surface after short-duration flame impingement are 46.6, 44.0, and 44.3 °C, respectively. The 2.6 °C reduction in apparent temperature upon coating indicates that the coating does not mask the low-emissivity metallic background, while the mere 0.3 °C difference before and after flame impingement further suggests that short-duration thermal shock does not introduce additional extrinsic losses sufficient to dominate the radiance in the measurement band (Fig. 5c, d). A steady-state hot-plate series systematizes this effect by defining ΔT = T (uncoated) − T (coated). As the set temperature increases from 100 to 350 °C (50 °C increments), ΔT evolves to 9.6, 17.0, 27.3, 42.8, 52.3, and 64.4 °C, exhibiting pronounced amplification at the high-temperature end. According to the standard radiometric principle of infrared thermography, the apparent temperature is obtained by converting the band-integrated infrared radiance received by the camera into a blackbody-equivalent temperature41. Beyond the spray-coating route, Fig. 5f outlines a scalable, large-area fabrication strategy based on roll-to-roll carbon thermal shock (CTS)42. In this process, mixed powders are delivered onto a conductive carbon cloth and subjected to an ultrashort Joule-heating pulse, which provides a high-temperature shock and enables one-step co-consolidation into a continuous composite surface layer on a metallic substrate. The inset in Fig. 5f shows a representative CTS-processed composite sheet together with IR thermography, qualitatively consistent with a metal-reflection-dominated IR boundary. Functionally, the Al phase serves as a highly reflective infrared background that suppresses effective emissivity, whereas the YMnO3 phase primarily imparts visible coloration while remaining MIR-transmissive, thereby avoiding radiative “masking” of the metallic boundary condition. This co-design integrates visible appearance and the governing infrared radiative boundary within a single manufacturable building block. Taken together, these demonstrations establish the materials-level feasibility of the YMnO3-based visible-MIR spectral design under controlled thermal conditions. However, they should not be interpreted as a full aerospace coating qualification, and future application-level evaluation will be required under coupled mechanical loading, thermal cycling, particle/rain erosion, interfacial or adhesion fatigue, and oxidative/environmental aging under representative aircraft-surface conditions.

Discussion

This work addresses a long-standing incompatibility between blue visible coloration and MIR camouflage in materials required to operate under high-temperature conditions. By tracing this conflict to lattice polarization-driven phonon–optical coupling, the work establishes that the loss of MIR transparency accompanying visible color modulation is not an incidental materials limitation but a structurally imposed physical constraint. Through microwave-shock synthesis, this constraint is overcome in a hexagonal 2D YMnO3 system, where non-equilibrium structural stabilization enables simultaneous suppression of long-range polarization contributions and reinforcement of short-range bonding rigidity within a thermally stable oxide lattice. Beyond demonstrating a specific material realization, the results reveal a general mechanism for decoupling visible and infrared optical responses by cooperatively engineering dimensional confinement and local bonding stiffness. The identification of a well-defined correspondence between visible blue coloration and retention of the MIR transparency window further provides a predictive criterion for structural tuning rather than empirical optimization. More broadly, this work shows that non-equilibrium processing can access optical–phononic regimes that are inaccessible under thermodynamic equilibrium, transforming cross-spectral camouflage from a trade-off-limited problem into a designable physical framework. These insights open new opportunities for the development of high-temperature functional surfaces in aerospace and related technologies where simultaneous control of appearance, thermal radiation, and structural stability is required.

Methods

Method and synthesis

All starting materials were of analytical grade, obtained from commercial suppliers in China, and used as received without any additional purification. The reagents and precursors included ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, CAS: 12125-02-9), anhydrous glucose (CAS: 50-99-7), yttrium chloride hexahydrate (YCl3·6H₂O, CAS: 10025-94-2), and manganese (II) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H₂O, CAS: 13446-34-9). For the synthesis of 2D YMnO3 nanosheets, 0.4 g glucose, 0.5 g NH4Cl, 0.12 g YCl3·6H2O, and 0.078 g MnCl2·4H₂O were weighed and thoroughly ground for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous precursor mixture. The resulting mixture was transferred into a clean, dry crucible and subjected to microwave irradiation at 650 W for 10 min in ambient air. During a representative microwave treatment, the sample temperature was monitored in real time using a Raytek M13 infrared detector, and the temperature rapidly exceeded 850 °C under the present reaction conditions. After microwave irradiation, the product was naturally cooled to room temperature in air, collected, and gently ground into powder for subsequent structural, spectroscopic, and coating fabrication experiments.

Characterization and measurements

Phase identification and structural analysis of YMnO3 were performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean, The Netherlands) over a 2θ range of 10°–80°. Morphological and microstructural features were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JSM-7800F, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F30, USA). The sample thickness was determined by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany). When inks were involved, rheological behavior was evaluated using a rotational rheometer (AR-2000ex).

The spectral reflectance r(λ) in the solar-relevant range (0.3–2.5 μm) was measured using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600 Plus/SolidSpec 3700) equipped with an integrating sphere, with BaSO4 used as the reflectance reference. In the MIR region (2.5–20 μm), reflectance and emittance spectra were collected on an FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Vertex 80 v) coupled with a diffuse gold integrating sphere to better account for scattering contributions. Reaction temperature was monitored in real time using a Raytek M13 infrared detector (USA).
