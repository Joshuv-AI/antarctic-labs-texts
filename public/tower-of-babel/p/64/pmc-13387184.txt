Mechanistic Basis and Translational Potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine in the Treatment of Cholestasis: A Narrative Review
Authors: Shuai Yuan, Shuang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholestasis results from impaired bile formation, secretion, or flow and contributes to heterogeneous hepatobiliary disorders, including primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, cholestatic drug-induced liver injury, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis. Persistent bile acid retention drives hepatocyte and cholangiocyte injury, immune activation, gut–liver axis disruption, and fibrogenesis. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been investigated as a multi-component therapeutic approach, but its evidence strength and translational readiness remain uncertain.ObjectiveTo synthesize mechanistic, clinical, and translational evidence on TCM-based interventions for cholestasis and identify evidence gaps relevant to future development.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed were searched for publications from 1 January 2016 to 25 January 2026, supplemented by backward citation chasing. A sensitivity search of CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed was performed to reduce language bias. Eligible studies were mapped by cholestatic condition or model, intervention type, study design, mechanistic domain, clinical relevance, and evidence strength.ResultsEighty studies were included in the core evidence set. The evidence base was dominated by preclinical animal, in vitro, omics, pharmacokinetic, quality-control, and toxicology studies, with limited mature clinical evidence. Recurrent mechanisms involved FXR-centered bile acid homeostasis, transporter regulation, inflammatory and immune pathways, oxidative stress and regulated cell death, fibrogenic signaling, and gut–liver axis modulation. Clinical evidence was most developed for intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, where adjunctive TCM plus ursodeoxycholic acid was associated with improvements in bile acids and pruritus. However, heterogeneity in formula composition, trial quality, endpoint selection, and safety reporting limits inference, particularly for maternal–fetal outcomes. Evidence for other cholestatic indications remains limited or largely preclinical.ConclusionTCM provides pathway-linked therapeutic hypotheses and candidate compounds for cholestasis, but high-confidence translation requires standardized products, exposure–response characterization, causal target validation, robust safety and herb–drug interaction assessment, and adequately powered disease-specific trials using clinically meaningful endpoints.

Introduction

Cholestasis is a pathophysiological state rather than a single disease entity. It occurs when bile formation, canalicular secretion, or biliary flow is impaired by hepatocellular transporter dysfunction, cholangiocyte injury, ductal obstruction, hormonal disturbance, metabolic stress, immune-mediated cholangiopathy, or drug-induced injury.1,2 Retained bile acids can directly injure hepatocytes and cholangiocytes and also act as signaling molecules that regulate hepatic metabolism, inflammation, and immune responses; in TCM-related cholestasis studies, these processes are most often linked to FXR- and PXR-centered bile acid signaling.3–6 Persistent cholestasis may therefore promote hepatocyte stress, ductular reaction, oxidative injury, gut-liver axis disruption, immune activation, and progressive fibrogenesis.7–10 Clinically, these mechanisms contribute to primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), cholestatic drug-induced liver injury, cholestatic pruritus, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis.1,11,12

Although ursodeoxycholic acid remains a key therapy for selected cholestatic disorders, important unmet needs persist. Secondary evidence syntheses in ICP and cholestatic hepatitis suggest that adjunctive TCM-containing regimens may improve selected biochemical or symptomatic outcomes, but the strength of evidence differs substantially by disease context.11,12 Broader reviews also emphasize that most TCM evidence in cholestatic liver disease remains preclinical, heterogeneous, and insufficient for broad clinical claims.1,2,13 In ICP, maternal biochemical and symptomatic improvement must be distinguished from hard perinatal outcomes, for which evidence remains less certain.11 These limitations support the exploration of adjunctive strategies that are mechanistically plausible, clinically testable, and safe.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has long been used for jaundice-related and hepatobiliary symptom complexes, including damp-heat jaundice models and Artemisia capillaris-based anti-jaundice strategies.14,15 In TCM theory, cholestasis-like presentations are commonly interpreted through patterns such as jaundice, liver-gallbladder damp-heat, impaired bile flow, toxin accumulation, and blood stasis. Treatment is usually guided by syndrome differentiation and multi-herb formula compatibility, in which different herbs are combined to target the principal pattern, enhance efficacy, reduce toxicity, or support organ-specific actions.1,2 These traditional concepts are not direct equivalents of modern molecular pathways; however, they provide a historical framework from which testable biomedical hypotheses can be generated. From a modern perspective, TCM is relevant to cholestasis because multi-component interventions may simultaneously affect bile acid synthesis and transport, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, fibrogenesis, intestinal barrier function,10,16–18 and the gut microbiota-bile acid-liver axis.19–22

Previous reviews have summarized individual formulas, herbal constituents, or selected mechanisms in cholestatic liver disease.1,2,13 The present review differs by mapping the evidence according to cholestatic condition or experimental model, intervention type, study design, mechanistic domain, evidence strength, and translational readiness. This distinction is important because the evidence base is uneven: clinical evidence is most developed for ICP, whereas evidence for PBC, PSC, cholestatic drug-induced liver injury, cholestatic pruritus, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis remains limited, heterogeneous, or largely preclinical.1,11,12 Accordingly, this review aims to synthesize mechanistic, clinical, and translational evidence on TCM-based interventions for cholestasis; distinguish clinical evidence from preclinical and hypothesis-generating evidence; and identify the key requirements for future development, including product standardization, pharmacokinetics, causal target validation, safety assessment, herb-drug interaction evaluation, and disease-specific clinical endpoints. Methods

Review Design

This study was designed as a narrative, mechanism-centered review of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)–based interventions for cholestasis and cholestasis-related conditions. We used a structured evidence-mapping framework to integrate clinical, preclinical, mechanistic, pharmacokinetic, quality-control, safety, and translational evidence. Because the included studies were highly heterogeneous in disease entity, experimental model, intervention type, comparator, and outcome reporting, quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. The purpose of the review was not to generate pooled effect estimates, but to map the evidence base, distinguish clinical evidence from preclinical and hypothesis-generating evidence, and evaluate translational readiness.

Literature Search

We searched PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection for publications from 1 January 2016 to 25 January 2026. The final search was conducted on 25 January 2026. The search strategy used three concept blocks: cholestasis-related terms, TCM or herbal medicine–related terms, and mechanism or clinical evidence–related terms. Synonyms within each block were combined using OR, and the three concept blocks were combined using AND. The PubMed search strategy was: (“Cholestasis”[Mesh] OR cholestasis[tiab] OR cholestatic[tiab] OR “bile acid”[tiab] OR “bile acids”[tiab] OR “intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy”[tiab] OR “primary biliary cholangitis”[tiab] OR “primary sclerosing cholangitis”[tiab] OR “cholestatic drug-induced liver injury”[tiab] OR “cholestatic hepatitis”[tiab] OR “cholestatic liver injury”[tiab]) AND (“Medicine, Chinese Traditional”[Mesh] OR “Drugs, Chinese Herbal”[Mesh] OR “traditional Chinese medicine”[tiab] OR TCM[tiab] OR “Chinese herbal”[tiab] OR “Chinese herb”[tiab] OR phytotherapy[tiab] OR “herbal formula”[tiab] OR decoction[tiab] OR extract[tiab] OR granule[tiab] OR “Chinese patent medicine”[tiab]) AND (mechanism[tiab] OR pathway[tiab] OR transporter[tiab] OR FXR[tiab] OR BSEP[tiab] OR “bile salt export pump”[tiab] OR inflammation[tiab] OR fibrosis[tiab] OR microbiota[tiab] OR “gut-liver axis”[tiab] OR efficacy[tiab] OR safety[tiab] OR pharmacokinetic*[tiab] OR randomized[tiab] OR trial[tiab] OR “systematic review”[tiab] OR meta-analysis[tiab]).

The Web of Science Core Collection search used the same three concept blocks in Topic fields: TS=(cholestasis OR cholestatic OR “bile acid” OR “bile acids” OR “intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy” OR “primary biliary cholangitis” OR “primary sclerosing cholangitis” OR “cholestatic drug-induced liver injury” OR “cholestatic hepatitis” OR “cholestatic liver injury”) AND TS=(“traditional Chinese medicine” OR TCM OR “Chinese herbal” OR “Chinese herb” OR phytotherapy OR “herbal formula” OR decoction OR extract OR granule OR “Chinese patent medicine”) AND TS=(mechanism OR pathway OR transporter OR FXR OR BSEP OR “bile salt export pump” OR inflammation OR fibrosis OR microbiota OR “gut-liver axis” OR efficacy OR safety OR pharmacokinetic* OR randomized OR trial OR “systematic review” OR meta-analysis).

To reduce language bias in this TCM-focused review, we also performed a sensitivity search of Chinese-language databases, including CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed, over the same time window. Chinese search terms were organized using the same three concept blocks: (胆汁淤积 OR 淤胆 OR 胆汁淤积性肝损伤 OR 妊娠期肝内胆汁淤积症 OR 原发性胆汁性胆管炎 OR 原发性硬化性胆管炎 OR 药物性胆汁淤积 OR 胆汁淤积性肝炎) AND (中医 OR 中药 OR 中草药 OR 中药复方 OR 方剂 OR 汤剂 OR 颗粒 OR 提取物 OR 单体成分 OR 中成药) AND (机制 OR 通路 OR 转运体 OR 法尼醇X受体 OR FXR OR 胆盐输出泵 OR BSEP OR 炎症 OR 纤维化 OR 肠道菌群 OR 肠肝轴 OR 疗效 OR 安全性 OR 随机 OR 临床试验 OR 系统评价 OR Meta分析). Where database interfaces did not support the full Boolean string, the three concept blocks were combined in simplified searches and records were manually screened against the same eligibility criteria.

Backward citation chasing was performed by screening the reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews or evidence syntheses. Studies cited only for general background, theoretical context, or pathway interpretation were not counted as part of the core evidence set unless they met the eligibility criteria below.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (i) they addressed cholestasis or a cholestasis-related phenotype, including intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, cholestatic drug-induced liver injury, cholestatic hepatitis, cholestatic pruritus, cholestatic liver injury, or cholestasis-associated fibrosis; (ii) they evaluated a TCM-based intervention, including a classical or modified formula, proprietary Chinese medicine, single-herb extract, processed herbal material, herb pair, decoction, granule, or defined bioactive constituent derived from Chinese materia medica; (iii) they reported cholestasis-relevant clinical, biochemical, histological, mechanistic, pharmacokinetic, quality-control, or safety outcomes; and (iv) they provided sufficient information on the intervention, model or population, comparator, and outcomes to allow evidence classification.

Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, non-randomized clinical studies, observational human studies, systematic reviews or meta-analyses used for clinical contextualization, in vivo preclinical studies, in vitro mechanistic studies, omics-based studies with experimental validation, pharmacokinetic studies, quality-control studies, and safety or toxicology studies.

Studies were excluded if they were non-empirical commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts without sufficient data, duplicate publications, off-topic reports, studies without an interpretable comparator or control, or studies with unresolvable reporting deficiencies. Network pharmacology-only or molecular docking-only studies without experimental or clinical validation were not treated as standalone evidence of efficacy or mechanism.

Study Selection and Classification

After deduplication, 583 records were retained for screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Full texts were retrieved when eligibility was uncertain or when the abstract contained insufficient information for classification. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus, with consultation of a senior author when necessary. Ultimately, 80 studies were included in the core evidence set. Before data extraction, each included study was assigned to one of the following predefined evidence categories: clinical/human evidence, preclinical in vivo evidence, in vitro mechanistic evidence, secondary evidence synthesis, omics or network pharmacology with experimental validation, pharmacokinetic or quality-control evidence, or safety/toxicology evidence.

A study was classified as clinical/human evidence only when the intervention was administered to human participants and patient-level or trial-level cholestasis-relevant outcomes were reported. Animal experiments, cell-based studies, rat or mouse metabolomics studies, pharmacokinetic studies, network pharmacology studies, and toxicology studies were not classified as clinical evidence, even when they investigated clinically named conditions such as intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy or cholestasis-associated fibrosis.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized extraction form. Extracted items included study category, disease entity or experimental model, population or species, intervention, comparator, dose, route, duration, main outcomes, mechanistic readouts, safety findings, and major methodological limitations.

For clinical studies, additional extracted items included diagnostic criteria, sample size, randomization, blinding, follow-up duration, background standard therapy, outcome definitions, and adverse-event reporting. For preclinical studies, additional items included animal species, sex, sample size, cholestasis model, timing of intervention, positive control, biochemical and histological endpoints, transporter or receptor assays, and target-validation experiments where available. For pharmacokinetic, quality-control, and toxicology studies, extracted items included analytical method, chemical markers, exposure measurement, batch or processing information, dose relevance, and safety endpoints.

Evidence Appraisal and Synthesis

Because the included studies covered diverse clinical, preclinical, omics, pharmacokinetic, quality-control, and safety designs, a single universal risk-of-bias tool was not applied. Instead, we used a study-design-specific appraisal framework. Clinical studies were assessed according to randomization, comparator appropriateness, baseline comparability, blinding, outcome completeness, endpoint relevance, follow-up duration, and safety reporting. Preclinical studies were assessed according to model relevance, sample-size reporting, randomization, blinding, dose justification, positive and negative controls, endpoint validity, and reproducibility. Omics and network pharmacology studies were assessed according to data-source transparency, pathway prioritization, biological plausibility, and experimental validation. Pharmacokinetic, quality-control, and toxicology studies were assessed according to chemical characterization, analytical validation, exposure measurement, batch consistency, dose relevance, and safety endpoints.

Mechanistic claims were categorized as causal, supportive, correlative, or hypothesis-generating. Causal evidence required direct target or pathway perturbation, such as receptor agonism or antagonism, inhibitor studies, knockdown, knockout, overexpression, rescue experiments, or target-engagement assays. Supportive evidence referred to consistent pathway changes accompanying improvement in cholestatic phenotypes but without direct target perturbation. Correlative evidence referred to association-level findings such as altered gene or protein expression, metabolomic signatures, pathway enrichment, or microbiome shifts. Computational, docking-based, or network-prediction findings without experimental validation were considered hypothesis-generating.

Evidence was synthesized narratively by mechanistic domain and clinical indication. Clinical evidence was discussed separately from preclinical evidence. Preclinical findings were not interpreted as evidence of clinical efficacy unless supported by human data. No journal-level metric, including impact factor, was used to determine eligibility, screening priority, data extraction priority, evidence classification, or evidentiary weight.

Results

Research Landscape and Trends

Among the 80 publications included in the core evidence set, the evidence base was dominated by preclinical and mechanistic studies rather than mature clinical trials, a pattern consistent with animal-study syntheses and broader reviews of TCM in cholestatic liver disease.1,2,13 According to the revised evidence map in Supplementary Table S1, the included studies were reclassified into predefined evidence categories: clinical/human primary evidence, secondary evidence synthesis, preclinical in vivo evidence, in vitro/mechanistic or omics-supported evidence, pharmacokinetic or quality-control evidence, and safety/toxicology evidence. This reclassification was performed to avoid conflating clinical efficacy, preclinical mechanism, computational prediction, exposure characterization, and safety evidence.

Publication output increased overall in recent years, particularly after 2021, indicating growing interest in TCM-based interventions for cholestasis. However, the 2026 count represents only records identified up to 25 January 2026 and should not be interpreted as a full-year trend. The publication pattern therefore reflects increasing research activity, but not necessarily increasing clinical maturity.

The majority of primary experimental studies used animal models of cholestasis. ANIT-induced models were represented by studies of swertiamarin, Yinchenzhufu decoction, gentiopicroside, and paeoniflorin,10,16,28,29 whereas BDL-induced cholestatic fibrosis models were used to evaluate Bie Jia Jian pill, MP-40, Glechoma hederacea, and Ger-Gen-Chyn-Lian-Tang.8,24,25,30 DDC-induced chronic cholangiopathy or cholestasis models were used for Huangqi decoction and Da-Huang-Xiao-Shi decoction.7,9 Estrogen- or pregnancy-related cholestasis models included Yinchenhao decoction/rhein, Calculus Bovis, and Calculus Bovis Sativus.31–33 These models were mainly used to evaluate TCM formulas, herbal extracts, herb pairs, and defined bioactive constituents for effects on bile acid homeostasis, inflammation, oxidative stress, cell death, fibrogenesis, intestinal barrier function, and the gut microbiota-bile acid-liver axis.

Primary human evidence was limited. Clinically relevant evidence was concentrated mainly in ICP, largely through secondary evidence syntheses comparing TCM-containing integrative regimens with conventional therapy.11 Evidence related to cholestatic hepatitis or liver injury was supported mainly by secondary synthesis and selected clinically oriented studies rather than by multiple disease-specific, high-quality trials.12,23 Evidence for PBC, PSC, cholestatic drug-induced liver injury, cholestatic pruritus, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis was sparse, indirect, or predominantly preclinical.1,2 Accordingly, human clinical findings and preclinical mechanistic findings are discussed separately in the following sections.

Across the evidence map, recurrent mechanistic themes included FXR-centered bile acid homeostasis,3–5,34 bile acid transporter regulation,9,16,32,35 inflammatory and immune signaling,18,36–38 oxidative stress and cell-death responses,39–42 fibrogenic pathways,19,24,26,27 and gut-liver axis modulation.17,20–22 Omics and network pharmacology studies contributed useful hypothesis-generating information,43–46 whereas pharmacokinetic, quality-control, and safety/toxicology studies were treated as translational-support evidence rather than direct evidence of efficacy.47–50

Mechanistic Evidence: Preclinical and Translational Signals

Most mechanistic evidence on TCM-based interventions for cholestasis is derived from animal models, cell-based experiments, omics-supported studies, and network pharmacology studies with varying degrees of experimental validation.13,43–45 Therefore, mechanistic findings in this section are interpreted according to evidence strength. Causal evidence refers to findings supported by direct target or pathway perturbation, such as receptor activation or inhibition, knockdown, knockout, overexpression, rescue experiments, or target-engagement assays. Supportive evidence refers to consistent phenotypic and pathway changes without direct causal testing. Correlative evidence refers to association-level findings, including altered gene or protein expression, metabolomic signatures, pathway enrichment, or microbiome changes. Computational or docking-based findings without experimental validation are considered hypothesis-generating.

Regulation of Bile Acid Synthesis, Transport, and Excretion

Restoration of bile acid homeostasis is the most frequently investigated mechanism in TCM-related cholestasis research.3,10,16,51 In ANIT-, DDC-, estrogen-, and other bile acid-related cholestasis models, several formulas, extracts, and defined constituents have been associated with reduced intrahepatic bile acid accumulation, improved cholestatic biochemistry, and attenuation of liver injury.9,28,32,33 Mechanistically, these effects are commonly linked to FXR-centered signaling, inhibition of bile acid synthesis pathways, and regulation of hepatobiliary transporters such as BSEP, MRP2, and related efflux systems. Representative interventions include Yinchenzhufu decoction, Alisma orientale extract, swertiamarin, and JiaGaSongTang.3,10,16,51 Yinzhihuang formula, SanWei GanJiang Powder, gentiopicroside, and PL-S2 further illustrate bile-acid-related regulation across cholestasis or bile-acid-relevant models.17,28,35,52

Some TCM-derived constituents provide stronger target-oriented evidence. For example, studies of FXR-active or bile-acid-pathway-related compounds such as licraside, emodin, physcion, and alisol derivatives suggest that defined herbal molecules may directly modulate bile acid receptor signaling.4,5,53 PXR activation by Schisandra lignans and FXR/BSEP activation by Dan-shen Yin further support the relevance of nuclear receptor pathways in bile acid regulation.6,34 Scoparone and genipin have also been discussed as liver-directed candidate molecules with translational potential, although their cholestasis-specific evidence remains less direct than that of validated FXR-active compounds.54,55 However, many studies still rely on downstream changes in transporter or receptor expression rather than direct target-perturbation experiments. Therefore, the overall evidence for bile acid homeostasis regulation is relatively strong at the preclinical level, but clinical confirmation remains limited. Anti-Inflammatory and Immunomodulatory Pathways

Inflammation is a major amplifier of cholestatic liver injury. Preclinical studies suggest that TCM interventions may attenuate cholestatic inflammation by suppressing canonical inflammatory signaling, reducing cytokine release, and modulating innate and adaptive immune responses.7,10,18,36 Frequently reported pathways include NF-kappaB signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, NOD1/RIPK2 signaling, Kupffer-cell activation, neutrophil recruitment, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and T-cell infiltration.18,25,37,38

In ANIT- and DDC-induced models, several TCM formulas or constituents were associated with lower levels of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, and IL-6, together with improved liver injury markers and histology.7,10,36,37 Chidan Tuihuang granule provides an example of a gut microbiota-innate immunity mechanism, in which reduced bacterial dipeptide translocation and inhibition of hepatic NOD1/RIPK2 signaling were linked to improvement in cholestatic injury.18 Yinchenzhufu decoction has also been associated with reduced neutrophil extracellular trap formation and attenuation of cholestatic liver injury and fibrosis.26,38 Additional liver-injury models involving inflammation and bile acid metabolism, such as Sanhuang Xiexin decoction in colitis-associated secondary liver injury, were treated as mechanistically relevant but not as direct cholestasis efficacy evidence.56

Overall, the anti-inflammatory evidence is mainly supportive rather than definitively causal. Stronger claims require studies showing that blockade or restoration of a specific inflammatory pathway changes the therapeutic effect of the TCM intervention. Oxidative Stress, Mitochondrial Injury, and Regulated Cell Death

Oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and regulated cell death contribute to hepatocyte injury during cholestasis. Several preclinical studies report that TCM interventions reduce oxidative damage, improve antioxidant defenses, and limit hepatocyte apoptosis.33,39–41 Nrf2-related antioxidant signaling, SIRT1 activation, glutathione metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and Bcl-2/Bax-caspase pathways are among the most commonly reported mechanisms.39–41,57

For example, Xiaoyan Lidan formula and related constituents have been linked to Nrf2-associated antioxidant responses, improved mitochondrial function, and measurable exposure of multiple bioactive components in cholestatic injury models.49,58,59 Ginsenoside Rg1 has been reported to alleviate ANIT-induced cholestatic injury through SIRT1-associated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, with stronger mechanistic support when SIRT1-dependent loss-of-function approaches are used.41 Other studies suggest that TCM interventions may reduce bile acid-induced apoptosis in hepatocytes or intestinal epithelial cells, thereby preserving liver and gut barrier integrity.22,33,57

Ferroptosis has emerged as a related but more complex mechanism. Some herbal exposures may contribute to cholestatic or herb-induced liver injury by disrupting bile acid transport, FXR-related signaling, oxidative stress responses, or other cell-death pathways.42,48,50,60 Geniposide-related studies also illustrate how defined iridoid constituents may affect inflammatory and stress-response pathways in liver-relevant disease contexts.61 Thus, ferroptosis and related regulated cell-death mechanisms should be discussed as both potential therapeutic targets and safety-relevant mechanisms. At present, most evidence in this domain remains preclinical and should be interpreted as supportive or correlative unless direct pathway rescue experiments are available.

Anti-Fibrotic Mechanisms in Cholestatic Injury

Evidence for anti-fibrotic effects of TCM in cholestasis is predominantly preclinical.24–26,30 Animal models such as BDL-induced cholestatic fibrosis and DDC-induced chronic cholangiopathy have been used to evaluate effects on hepatic stellate cell activation, extracellular matrix deposition, collagen accumulation, and pro-fibrogenic signaling.8,25,27,30 These models are useful for mechanism discovery but should not be interpreted as clinical evidence of anti-fibrotic efficacy.

Several studies suggest that TCM interventions may suppress TGF-beta1/Smad signaling, reduce alpha-SMA and collagen I/III expression, inhibit hepatic stellate cell activation, and promote extracellular matrix remodeling. Yajie Shaba and Ger-Gen-Chyn-Lian-Tang have been associated with reduced pro-fibrogenic signaling and collagen deposition in cholestatic fibrosis models.27,30 MP-40 mitigated BDL-induced hepatic fibrosis by inhibiting NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, suggesting a link between inflammatory cell death and fibrogenesis.25 Quantitative proteomics studies further suggest that Yinchenzhufu decoction may protect against cholestatic liver fibrosis through modulation of the PDGFRbeta/PI3K/AKT pathway.26 NET inhibition by Yinchenzhufu decoction and bile-acid-metabolism-related effects of Huangqi decoction further support inflammation-fibrosis and bile-acid-fibrosis links.38,62 Cholestasis-adjacent anti-fibrotic work involving total astragalus saponins and mesenchymal stromal-cell-based strategies was treated as translational context rather than direct cholestasis evidence.63

These findings support fibrosis-related mechanisms as an important preclinical research direction. However, human studies using validated fibrosis endpoints are still lacking. Therefore, anti-fibrotic claims should be framed as preclinical mechanistic evidence rather than clinical efficacy evidence. Gut-Liver Axis, Microbiota, and Intestinal Barrier Regulation

The gut-liver axis is a major mechanistic theme in recent TCM cholestasis research. Preclinical studies suggest that TCM interventions may reshape gut microbiota, improve intestinal barrier integrity, regulate microbial bile acid metabolism, reduce translocation of microbial products, and promote fecal bile acid excretion.17–19,21 These effects may secondarily reduce hepatic inflammation and bile acid burden and may overlap with effects on intestinal barrier protection and microbial bile acid metabolism.20,22,64,65

Yinzhihuang formula has been reported to modulate the microbe-gut-liver axis and bile acid excretion in cholestatic liver injury.17 Chidan Tuihuang granule was linked to gut microbiota remodeling and inhibition of NOD1/RIPK2-mediated inflammatory signaling.18 In an ICP mouse model, Yinchenhao decoction and rhein were associated with improvement of cholestasis-like features through modulation of intestinal flora.31 Other studies involving Si-Ni-San, Zhuyu pill, Shentao Ruangan granule, and related interventions suggest that microbiota-dependent mechanisms may contribute to anti-cholestatic or anti-fibrotic effects.20,21,66,67 Mechanistic analyses of Zhuyu pill, including target-gene and miRNA-related work, provide additional support for microbiota-metabolism-gene regulatory links in cholestasis models.68,69

Nevertheless, microbiome findings are often correlative. Stronger evidence requires microbiota-depletion, fecal microbiota transplantation, defined bacterial supplementation, metabolite rescue, or barrier-function intervention experiments. At present, the gut-liver axis should be regarded as a promising but still incompletely validated translational mechanism.

Omics-Supported Target Discovery and Network Mechanisms

Multi-omics, network pharmacology, serum pharmacochemistry, and pharmacokinetic studies have increasingly been used to explore the multi-component and multi-target features of TCM interventions. Examples include metabolomics- and network pharmacology-based studies of Huzhang Erjin decoction, Yin-Chen-Si-Ni decoction, Huaganjian decoction, and Yinchenzhufu decoction in ANIT-induced or related cholestatic injury models.43–46 Sequencing- or omics-supported work has also implicated the IDI1/SREBP2 axis, Artemisia capillaris-related networks, Da-Huang-Xiao-Shi decoction, Rabdosia serra, and paeoniflorin in cholestasis-relevant pathways.14,29,70–72 Huangqi decoction and Gentiana radix studies further illustrate metabolomic approaches in cholestasis- or jaundice-related models.15,73 Experimentally validated network pharmacology studies of cucurbitacin B and multi-omics studies in cholestasis-adjacent liver disease models were treated as target-discovery or translational-context evidence rather than direct clinical efficacy evidence.74,75

These approaches are valuable for generating mechanistic hypotheses and prioritizing candidate compounds, targets, and biomarkers. However, network pharmacology, docking, or omics enrichment alone cannot prove mechanism or efficacy. Their translational value depends on subsequent validation using pharmacodynamic experiments, target perturbation, exposure measurement, dose-response analysis, and clinically relevant endpoints. Therefore, omics and network-based findings in this review are treated as hypothesis-generating or supportive evidence unless experimentally validated.

Clinical Evidence and Therapeutic Strategies

This section summarizes human and clinically relevant evidence by cholestatic disease context. Preclinical animal studies are not treated as clinical efficacy evidence, even when they model clinically named conditions such as ICP, cholestatic hepatitis, or cholestasis-associated fibrosis. Instead, such studies are discussed in the mechanistic and translational sections according to their primary contribution.

Intrahepatic Cholestasis of Pregnancy

Among the cholestatic conditions covered in this review, ICP has the most developed clinical evidence for TCM-containing regimens. A network meta-analysis including 38 randomized controlled trials and 3841 pregnant women with ICP compared ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) alone with several UDCA-plus-TCM combinations and reported that integrative regimens were generally associated with greater reductions in total bile acids and pruritus.11 Modified Yinchenhao decoction-based regimens appeared favorable in the comparative ranking.11

However, this evidence should be interpreted as a clinical signal rather than definitive proof of benefit. The included trials varied substantially in formula composition, dose, treatment duration, diagnostic criteria, background therapy, comparator design, and endpoint reporting.11 Improvements in maternal symptoms and biochemical markers, such as total bile acids and pruritus, should also be distinguished from hard perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal distress, stillbirth, and neonatal complications. Safety reporting was not sufficiently standardized across all included studies, which is particularly important for herbal interventions during pregnancy.

Therefore, current evidence suggests that adjunctive TCM plus UDCA may improve maternal biochemical and symptomatic outcomes in ICP, but higher-quality, multicenter trials are needed to confirm efficacy, pregnancy safety, optimal formula selection, dose standardization, and effects on clinically meaningful maternal-fetal outcomes.

Cholestatic Hepatitis and Cholestatic DILI-Like Syndromes

Clinical evidence for TCM in cholestatic hepatitis and cholestatic drug-induced liver injury-like syndromes remains limited and heterogeneous. Secondary evidence synthesis has evaluated Yinchenhao decoction-related strategies in cholestatic hepatitis, whereas clinically oriented metabolomics work has examined Dachaihu decoction in septic liver injury rather than strictly defined cholestatic DILI.12,23 Additional translational studies of jaundice or cholestasis-like liver injury phenotypes provide mechanistic signals but often rely on preclinical or mixed-model evidence.14,43,45,71 These studies provide preliminary clinical or translational signals, but they often include mixed liver injury populations rather than strictly defined cholestatic DILI cohorts.

For this reason, evidence from cholestatic hepatitis or mixed liver injury studies should be interpreted cautiously. Apparent improvements in bilirubin, total bile acids, liver enzymes, or hospital stay may reflect combined effects of stopping the offending drug, spontaneous recovery, conventional therapy, and adjunctive TCM. Future studies should use standardized cholestatic DILI definitions, formal causality assessment tools, clear background therapy reporting, predefined biochemical endpoints, and systematic adverse-event monitoring. Herb-drug interaction assessment is also essential because patients with DILI or cholestatic liver injury may be receiving multiple concomitant therapies.

Primary Biliary Cholangitis and Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis

Robust human evidence supporting TCM as a disease-modifying therapy for PBC or PSC was not identified in the core evidence set. Although several preclinical and mechanistic studies suggest that TCM formulas or constituents may regulate bile acid transport, nuclear receptor signaling, inflammation, fibrosis, and gut-liver axis pathways relevant to cholangiopathies, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical efficacy in PBC or PSC.1–3,51

For PBC, future clinical studies should clearly define whether TCM is being evaluated as adjunctive therapy in patients with incomplete biochemical response or intolerance to standard treatment. Relevant endpoints should include alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, validated biochemical response criteria, pruritus, fatigue, quality of life, and safety outcomes. For PSC, short-term biochemical improvement alone is insufficient to support disease-modifying claims. Future studies should specify PSC phenotype, inflammatory bowel disease status, biliary complications, imaging or ductal outcomes, transplant-free survival where feasible, and long-term safety.

At present, TCM-related evidence in PBC and PSC should be regarded as mechanistically plausible but clinically immature.

Cholestatic Pruritus

Direct clinical evidence for TCM in cholestatic pruritus is limited. Pruritus outcomes are most often reported in the context of ICP studies, where some integrative regimens were associated with symptom improvement.11 However, dedicated trials in cholestatic pruritus using validated itch scales, sleep measures, and quality-of-life instruments are lacking.

Mechanistic hypotheses linking TCM to pruritus relief include modulation of bile acid signaling, gut microbial metabolism, inflammatory mediators, and intestinal barrier function.17,18,22,64 These hypotheses are biologically plausible but remain insufficiently validated in human cholestatic pruritus. Future trials should include patient-centered endpoints such as numeric rating scale itch scores, sleep disturbance, quality of life, rescue medication use, and durability of symptom control.

Cholestasis-Associated Fibrosis and Cirrhosis

Evidence for TCM in cholestasis-associated fibrosis is currently predominantly preclinical. Several animal studies using BDL, DDC, or related cholestatic fibrosis models have reported anti-fibrotic effects through inhibition of hepatic stellate cell activation, extracellular matrix deposition, inflammatory cell death, TGF-beta/Smad signaling, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, NET formation, gut microbiota dysbiosis, or PDGFRbeta/PI3K/AKT signaling.24–27 Ger-Gen-Chyn-Lian-Tang and Huangqi decoction studies further support bile-acid- or fibrosis-linked mechanisms in preclinical liver fibrosis settings.30,62 These studies are useful for identifying mechanistic hypotheses and candidate interventions, but they do not constitute clinical evidence of anti-fibrotic efficacy.

Accordingly, claims regarding cholestasis-associated fibrosis should remain conservative. Future clinical studies would need to distinguish cholestatic fibrosis secondary to PBC, PSC, biliary obstruction, DILI, or other causes; define background standard therapy; use validated fibrosis assessments; and evaluate long-term clinical outcomes rather than relying only on short-term biochemical improvement.

Summary of Clinical Evidence Strength

Overall, the clinical evidence base for TCM in cholestasis remains limited and uneven. The strongest clinical signal is in ICP, where adjunctive TCM plus UDCA has been associated with improvements in maternal biochemical and symptomatic outcomes, but uncertainty remains regarding formula standardization, trial quality, safety reporting, and perinatal endpoints.11 Evidence for cholestatic hepatitis or cholestatic DILI-like syndromes is preliminary and requires more rigorous disease-specific trials.1,12 Evidence for PBC, PSC, cholestatic pruritus, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis is largely absent or preclinical.1,2

Therefore, the current literature supports TCM as a source of adjunctive clinical hypotheses and mechanistically linked candidate interventions, rather than as a clinically established treatment for most cholestatic diseases. Translation will require disease-specific trial designs, standardized products, exposure-response characterization, validated clinical endpoints, and systematic safety and herb-drug interaction assessment.

Translational Pathways and Development Roadmap

The translational value of TCM-based interventions for cholestasis should be interpreted according to the type and strength of available evidence. At present, most mechanistic signals are derived from preclinical models, whereas clinically mature evidence is limited and concentrated mainly in ICP.1,2,11,13 Therefore, translation should not proceed from mechanistic plausibility alone, but should require standardized products, exposure characterization, causal target validation, disease-specific endpoints, and systematic safety assessment.

Prioritizing Candidate Constituents for Target-Oriented Development

Defined TCM-derived constituents provide a practical entry point for target-oriented drug discovery because their chemical identity, exposure, and mechanism can be characterized more readily than those of complex formulas. Compounds such as scoparone, genipin/geniposide-related constituents, emodin, and physcion have been investigated as liver- or cholestasis-relevant candidate molecules.5,54,55,61 Other defined or semi-defined candidates include rhein, alisol derivatives, swertiamarin, gentiopicroside, licraside, quercetin 7-rhamnoside, cucurbitacin B,16,28,31,53 and isoastragaloside I.4,22,36,74 Many of these studies implicate FXR-centered bile acid signaling, transporter regulation, inflammatory suppression, antioxidant responses, or gut-liver axis modulation.

However, most candidate-constituent evidence remains preclinical. To advance these molecules toward translation, future studies should define target engagement, dose-response relationships, pharmacokinetics, tissue exposure, therapeutic window, and safety margins. Mechanistic claims should be strengthened by receptor binding assays, pathway inhibition or rescue experiments, genetic knockdown or knockout models, and replication across disease-relevant cholestasis models. Only compounds with reproducible pharmacodynamic effects and acceptable safety profiles should be prioritized for formal drug-development pipelines.

Modernizing Formulas Through Standardization and Exposure–Response Characterization

Multi-herb formulas are central to TCM practice and may be attractive for cholestasis because they can act on multiple interconnected pathways. Nevertheless, formula complexity creates major translational barriers. The same named formula may vary in herb source, processing method, component ratio, extraction protocol, dose, and quality-control standards. Without chemical standardization and exposure assessment, it is difficult to compare studies or reproduce therapeutic effects.2,47,49,76

Future formula development should therefore include botanical authentication, standardized processing, chemical fingerprinting, quantitative marker assays, batch-to-batch consistency testing, and pharmacokinetic profiling of major bioactive or quality-marker constituents. Formula optimization should be driven by both pharmacological evidence and clinical relevance. For example, formula ratios proposed by network meta-analysis or network pharmacology should be treated as hypotheses and validated through pharmacodynamic experiments, exposure-response analysis, and, where appropriate, clinical feasibility testing.11,12,14,43

Omics-Guided Biomarkers and Causal Target Validation

Metabolomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, serum pharmacochemistry, and network pharmacology have helped identify candidate pathways involved in TCM effects on cholestasis, including bile acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, fibrogenesis, intestinal barrier function, and microbial metabolism.43–46 These approaches are valuable for hypothesis generation and biomarker discovery, as illustrated by metabolomics studies of Huangqi decoction, Da-Huang-Xiao-Shi decoction, Rabdosia serra, and paeoniflorin.29,71–73 Cholestasis-adjacent studies involving Cistanche deserticola, Gentiana radix, PL-S2, gypnosides, Qingtong principle, and Huanglian-Wuzhuyu herb pair were used only as contextual support for pathway prioritization rather than as direct cholestasis efficacy evidence.15,52,75,77–79

However, omics enrichment and network prediction alone cannot establish mechanism or efficacy. Translational use of these methods requires a stepwise validation strategy. First, candidate compounds and pathways should be linked to measurable exposure and pharmacodynamic changes. Second, key targets should be validated using pathway inhibition, gene perturbation, receptor assays, or rescue experiments. Third, candidate biomarkers should be connected to functional outcomes such as bile acid burden, transporter activity, histological injury, fibrosis progression, pruritus severity, or clinically meaningful biochemical endpoints. This approach can help convert broad “multi-component-multi-target” claims into testable translational hypotheses.

Microbiome– and Gut–Liver Axis–Oriented Strategies

The gut-liver axis is one of the most active translational themes in recent TCM cholestasis research. Preclinical studies suggest that several formulas may reshape gut microbiota, improve intestinal barrier function, regulate microbial bile acid metabolism, reduce microbial product translocation, and promote fecal bile acid excretion.17–19,21 These effects may secondarily reduce hepatic inflammation and bile acid accumulation, with related fecal-metabolism and microbial-diversity evidence from Si-Ni-San, Zhuyu pill, Coptis-Tetradium combinations, and other gut-directed interventions.20,65–67

Despite this promise, microbiome findings are often correlative. Future studies should determine whether microbiota changes are required for therapeutic effects by using microbiota-depletion models, fecal microbiota transplantation, defined bacterial supplementation, metabolite rescue experiments, and barrier-function assays. If causality is confirmed, microbiome-oriented TCM strategies could be developed as adjunctive approaches, particularly for conditions in which bile acid metabolism, intestinal barrier dysfunction, inflammation, and pruritus overlap. Site-directed intestinal delivery may also be explored to maximize gut-local effects while limiting systemic exposure and off-target toxicity.

Disease-Specific Clinical Translation

Clinical translation should be disease-specific rather than mechanism-only. For ICP, current evidence provides a preliminary clinical signal that adjunctive TCM plus UDCA may improve maternal bile acid levels and pruritus, but future trials must standardize formula composition, dose, gestational timing, pregnancy safety monitoring, and perinatal outcome reporting.11 For cholestatic hepatitis and DILI-like syndromes, secondary evidence and mixed liver-injury clinical studies should be interpreted cautiously because they do not always use strict disease-specific definitions.12,23 Maternal biochemical improvement should be analyzed separately from fetal and neonatal outcomes.

For PBC, future trials should define whether TCM is being tested as adjunctive therapy in patients with incomplete biochemical response or intolerance to standard treatment. Endpoints should include alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, validated biochemical response criteria, pruritus, fatigue, quality of life, and safety. For PSC, short-term changes in alkaline phosphatase should not be overinterpreted; studies should incorporate phenotype definition, biliary imaging, inflammatory bowel disease status, cholangitis events, transplant-free survival where feasible, and long-term safety monitoring. For cholestatic DILI-like syndromes, standardized causality assessment and clear separation of spontaneous recovery, culprit-drug withdrawal, conventional therapy, and adjunctive TCM effects are essential.

Overall, the translational pathway for TCM in cholestasis should proceed from standardized product -> exposure characterization -> causal mechanism validation -> disease-specific endpoint selection -> early-phase safety and pharmacokinetic studies -> adequately powered clinical trials. This staged approach would make future TCM research more reproducible, clinically relevant, and acceptable to international hepatology audiences.

Toxicity, Safety, and Quality-Control Considerations

Safety is a prerequisite for the clinical translation of TCM-based interventions in cholestasis. This is particularly important because cholestatic patients may already have impaired bile acid handling, reduced hepatic reserve, pruritus, pregnancy-related vulnerability, autoimmune cholangiopathy, or exposure to multiple concomitant drugs. Therefore, TCM should not be assumed to be safe simply because it is traditional or natural. Safety-relevant evidence includes reports of herb-induced cholestatic injury or hepatotoxicity mechanisms and quality-control studies addressing exposure characterization.42,47–50,60,76

Herb-Induced Liver Injury and Cholestatic Toxicity

Some herbs or herbal products can cause hepatocellular, mixed, or cholestatic herb-induced liver injury. Preclinical toxicology studies have shown that certain herbal exposures may interfere with bile acid transport, FXR-related signaling, oxidative stress responses, mitochondrial function, intestinal barrier integrity, or gut microbiota composition.40,42,48,50 These mechanisms are relevant not only to toxicity but also to cholestasis itself, making careful safety assessment essential. Processing- or detoxification-related studies involving Psoraleae Fructus and Forsythiae Fructus further illustrate the importance of bile-acid- and microbiota-linked toxicology evaluation.60,80

Safety concerns are especially important for herbs with known or suspected hepatotoxic potential, products with variable processing, and formulas used in patients with existing cholestatic disease. Processing methods may reduce toxicity for some herbs, but detoxification claims require chemical characterization, dose standardization, mechanistic toxicology, and clinical pharmacovigilance.5,47,76,80 The possibility that a TCM intervention may both protect against cholestatic injury in one setting and contribute to liver injury in another should be explicitly considered.

Pregnancy Safety and Vulnerable Populations

ICP is the area with the most developed clinical signal for TCM-containing regimens, but pregnancy also represents a highly vulnerable population.11 Future ICP trials should report maternal adverse events, fetal and neonatal outcomes, gestational age at treatment initiation, preterm birth, fetal distress, stillbirth, neonatal intensive care admission, and postpartum follow-up. Herbal use during pregnancy should be evaluated with stricter safety thresholds than those applied to non-pregnant adults, especially because pregnancy-related cholestasis models do not substitute for human pregnancy safety evidence.31

Other vulnerable populations include patients with cirrhosis, advanced cholestatic disease, autoimmune cholangiopathies, renal dysfunction, older age, and polypharmacy. These groups require dose caution, biochemical monitoring, and careful exclusion or stratification in early clinical trials.

Herb–Drug Interactions

Drug-herb interactions are a major translational concern. Patients with cholestatic disorders may receive UDCA, antipruritic agents, immunomodulatory drugs, antibiotics, lipid-lowering drugs, bile acid-related therapies, or emerging disease-specific agents. TCM constituents may influence drug metabolism, transporters, enterohepatic cycling, intestinal absorption, microbiota-mediated metabolism, and nuclear receptor signaling. Potential interaction pathways include CYP enzymes, P-glycoprotein, OATP transporters, BSEP, MRP2, NTCP, FXR, PXR, and CAR, which are relevant to both efficacy and interaction risk.1,6,49,64

Future clinical studies should document background therapy, concomitant medications, liver biochemistry, renal function, and adverse events. For candidate formulas or monomers intended for combination with conventional therapy, in vitro transporter/enzyme assays, animal interaction studies, and early-phase clinical pharmacokinetic assessments should be considered.

Quality Control and Reproducibility

Quality control is essential for both efficacy and safety. Future studies should report herb species, plant part, geographic origin, processing method, extraction procedure, formulation, dose, storage conditions, and chemical markers. Modern analytical methods such as UPLC-MS/MS, chemical fingerprinting, serum pharmacochemistry, and Q-marker-based evaluation can support batch consistency and exposure assessment.15,47,49,76

Contamination, adulteration, substitution, pesticide residues, heavy metals, and undeclared pharmaceuticals should also be considered. These issues are particularly important for multi-herb formulas and proprietary preparations. Without rigorous quality control, negative or positive findings cannot be reliably reproduced, and safety signals cannot be properly attributed. Comparative processing studies, such as those involving Forsythiae Fructus, reinforce that raw-material status and processing can alter bile-acid- and microbiota-related safety profiles.80

Minimum Safety Reporting Standards for Future Studies

Future clinical studies of TCM in cholestasis should include predefined adverse-event monitoring, liver biochemical surveillance, causality assessment for liver injury, documentation of concomitant drugs, pregnancy-specific safety endpoints where relevant, and follow-up after treatment discontinuation. Preclinical studies should include dose justification, toxicology endpoints, sex reporting, exposure assessment, and assessment of bile acid transport and hepatobiliary injury markers.1,2

In summary, safety and quality control should be integrated from the earliest stages of TCM development rather than added after efficacy claims are made. A credible translational program for TCM in cholestasis must combine therapeutic hypothesis generation with standardized manufacturing, exposure-response analysis, interaction assessment, and robust pharmacovigilance.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, it is a narrative, mechanism-centered review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis; therefore, the synthesis remains partly interpretive. Second, most available evidence is preclinical, whereas primary human evidence is limited and concentrated mainly in intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy. Third, many mechanistic claims are based on pathway marker changes, omics findings, or microbiome associations without direct causal validation. In addition, heterogeneity in formula composition, processing, dosing, quality control, pharmacokinetics, and safety reporting limits reproducibility and clinical translation. Although Chinese-language databases were searched as a sensitivity analysis, language and publication bias cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

TCM-based interventions provide multi-component therapeutic hypotheses and candidate compounds for cholestasis, with the most consistent preclinical signals involving bile acid homeostasis, inflammation, oxidative stress, fibrogenesis, and the gut–liver axis. However, clinical evidence remains limited, with the strongest signal currently in intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy as adjunctive therapy with ursodeoxycholic acid. Evidence for PBC, PSC, cholestatic DILI, cholestatic pruritus, and cholestasis-associated fibrosis is still insufficient for firm clinical recommendations. Future translation requires standardized products, pharmacokinetic characterization, causal target validation, systematic safety and herb–drug interaction assessment, and adequately powered disease-specific clinical trials.
