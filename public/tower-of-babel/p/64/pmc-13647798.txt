Ferritin-ApoE nanocarrier for targeted therapy of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder in mice
Authors: Xin Zhao, Qian Liang, Kangqi Lin, Shihe Jiang, Tian Yang, Mengjiao Sun, Run Song, Junqiang Yan, Sara Palma-Tortosa, Zaal Kokaia, Xiyun Yan, Ganqin Du, Kelong Fan, Wei-Na Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by anti-aquaporin 4 (AQP4) antibody-mediated damage to astrocytes, resulting in subsequent demyelination. Our prior work identified the protective effects of the apolipoprotein E130−149 (ApoE130−149) peptide in NMOSD mice by promoting astrocyte-microglia intercellular communication. However, its therapeutic potential is restricted due to the limited penetration of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) with systemic administration. Here, we designed a heavy-chain ferritin (HFn)-based nanocarrier containing the ApoE130−149 peptide (HFn-ApoE130−149), specifically engineered for CNS delivery.MethodsHFn-ApoE130−149 was constructed through genetic engineering by fusing the coding sequence of HFn with that of the ApoE130−149 peptide in a recombinant plasmid. An acute NMOSD mouse model was induced by transcranial co-injection of AQP4-IgG and human complement (hC) into the brain. The distribution of Cy5.5-labeled HFn-ApoE130−149 post intravenous injection was tracked using in vivo fluorescence imaging to confirm its presence in the brain and peripheral organs. Lesions in the brain were quantified using T2-weighted 7 Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (7T-MRI). Neuropathological features of NMOSD were evaluated by immunostaining of brain sections. Neuroinflammation and immune cell infiltration were analyzed via flow cytometry. The key signaling pathways regulated by HFn-ApoE130−149 were investigated through Western blot (WB) analysis. The interaction between HFn-ApoE130−149 and its receptors was validated through co-immunoprecipitation and visualized on microglia using proximity ligation assay (PLA). Finally, the therapeutic effect on spatial learning and memory was evaluated using the Morris water maze (MWM) test.ResultsThe HFn-ApoE130−149 effectively crossed the BBB, attenuated lesion progression and demyelination, as well as preserved AQP4 expression and astrocytic integrity in NMOSD mice. The treatment induced a spatial and phenotypic restructuring of the astrocytic response, notably reducing excessive astrocyte accumulation around lesions while encouraging a proliferative and reparative phenotype. Furthermore, HFn-ApoE130−149 influenced microglial polarization towards an anti-inflammatory state, reducing infiltration of peripheral immune cells. Mechanistically, HFn-ApoE130−149 exerted its anti-inflammatory effects through the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) -nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling axis in microglia. Functional binding of HFn-ApoE130−149 to LRP1 suppressed inhibitor of NF-κB (IκBα) phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Knocking down LRP1 reversed these effects, highlighting the importance of the LRP1-NF-κB signaling axis in the nanotherapeutic’s efficacy. Treatment with HFn-ApoE130−149 improved spatial learning and rescued memory deficits in NMOSD mice.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the engineered nanodrug HFn-ApoE130-149 is a promising targeted therapy for alleviating NMOSD pathology by enhancing BBB penetration and suppressing neuroinflammation through the LRP1-NF-κB signaling axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03935-8.

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an autoimmune disease that causes recurring episodes of optic neuritis and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis, leading to significant neurological disability [1]. The aquaporin-4 immunoglobulin G (AQP4-IgG) mediates astrocyte damage through complement-dependent cytotoxicity and pro-inflammatory processes, resulting in demyelination and neuronal loss [2, 3]. Reactive microgliosis and astrocyte-microglia crosstalk further amplify complement component signaling pathways, exacerbating the inflammatory response and tissue damage [4, 5]. Current therapeutic approaches, including corticosteroids, B-cell depletion therapies, and complement inhibitors, mainly focus on suppressing peripheral immune responses [6–8]. However, they show limited efficacy in reversing central nervous system (CNS) damage or preventing long-term disability. There is an urgent need for therapies that directly target specific neuroinflammatory pathways within the CNS.

Astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (ADEVs), containing various proteins with neurotrophic and neuroprotective properties, contribute to remyelination and CNS repair by modulating microglial activity [9–11]. In NMOSD, ADEVs serve as crucial mediators of intercellular communication [12]. Our previous research established that ADEVs facilitate astrocyte-microglia communication and exert a therapeutic effect in NMOSD, identifying the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) protein as a key functional mediator [13]. Direct intracranial delivery of the ApoE peptide (ApoE130-149) is neuroprotective in NMOSD murine models [13]. However, its therapeutic effectiveness is hindered by the limited penetration of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) following systemic administration.

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems offer a promising solution to this challenge. Nanocarriers, with their optimal size, modifiable surface, ability to cross the BBB, and favorable pharmacokinetics, are powerful tools for treating brain diseases [14, 15]. Ferritin is a naturally occurring spherical protein nanocage responsible for iron storage. It can self-assemble into a 24-mer structure and be surface-modified via genetic engineering. In mammals, it is composed of two distinct subunit types, heavy-chain ferritin (HFn) and light-chain ferritin (LFn) [16, 17]. Recent studies have shown that the human HFn exhibits specific binding affinity for transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), a receptor abundantly expressed on the endothelial cells that form the BBB. This receptor-ligand interaction facilitates the transcytosis of cargo across the BBB, enabling targeted drug delivery to the CNS [18, 19]. Our recent research has shown that HFn nanocarriers can selectively deliver drugs and iron oxide nanoparticles to glioma tumors in mice [20]. These features position HFn as an ideal vehicle for delivering ApoE peptides to the brain.

In this study, we designed an HFn-based nanocarrier containing the ApoE130−149 peptide (HFn-ApoE130−149) and evaluated its BBB-penetrating capacity and therapeutic efficacy in the NMOSD mouse model.

Results

Engineering and pharmacokinetics of HFn-ApoE130−149 nanoprodrug

ApoE130−149, the peptide segment of ApoE extending from amino acid 130 to 149, plays a vital role as the N-terminal receptor binding domain necessary for its physiological functions [21]. Using genetic engineering, the ApoE130−149 peptide was conjugated to the amino terminus of HFn via a flexible linker, enabling its oriented display on the outer surface of the HFn nanocage. The HFn-ApoE130−149 peptide was expressed using an Escherichia coli (E. coli) system and purified to homogeneity. Each HFn subunit was linked to one ApoE130−149 peptide, resulting in the display of 24 ApoE130−149 peptides on the surface of the HFn nanocage (Fig. 1A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that the presence of ApoE130−149 did not disrupt the intact spherical structure of the HFn nanocage (Fig. 1B). The elution volume of HFn-ApoE130−149 by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) closely matched that of the 24-mer HFn cage, confirming its successful self-assembly. The purified product assessed by High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) demonstrated a high purity (97%) (Fig. 1C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 13.53 ± 2.197 nm (Fig. 1D).

To assess the BBB-penetrating ability of HFn-ApoE130−149, an acute NMOSD mouse model was induced by co-injecting AQP4-IgG and human complement (hC) transcranially [22]. This model recapitulates key NMOSD pathological features in the ipsilateral hemisphere, including decreased AQP4 expression, reduced glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes, as well as neuroinflammation and demyelination [23, 24]. Cy5.5-conjugated HFn-ApoE130−149 was administered intravenously to both sham mice and NMOSD mice (Fig. 1E). In vivo fluorescence imaging showed rapid accumulation of Cy5.5-HFn-ApoE130−149 in the brain of both groups (Fig. 1F). The fluorescent signals were evident within 3 h, reached a peak at 6 h post-injection, and persisted thereafter, demonstrating their efficient BBB penetration (Fig. 1G). Mice were euthanized at the peak fluorescence signal (6 h post-injection), and major organs were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. The results demonstrated pronounced accumulation of Cy5.5-HFn-ApoE130−149 in the brains of mice from both groups (Fig. 1H). Confocal microscopy analysis of brain sections further confirmed the accumulation of HFn-ApoE130−149 within the brain parenchyma (Fig. 1I), while peripheral distribution was mainly observed in the liver and kidneys (Fig. 1J).

HFn-ApoE130−149 at 3 mg/mL attenuates NMOSD pathology with a favorable safety profile in mice

The safety and maximum tolerable dose of HFn-ApoE130-149 were assessed by intravenous administration at concentrations ranging from 1 to 9 mg/mL in NMOSD mice. Mice survived the 28-day observation period at 1 mg/mL or 3 mg/mL doses. However, mice receiving higher doses (6 and 9 mg/mL) exhibited severe toxicities, including reduced locomotor activity, anorexia, diarrhea, and mortality. At 6 mg/mL, the 7-day survival rate decreased to 30%, and all mice died within 6 days at 9 mg/mL (Fig. 2A). Compared with the sham group, all NMOSD mice exhibited significant body-weight loss (p < 0.05 for each comparison; Fig. 2B). No additional reduction in body weight was observed in the 1 mg/mL and 3 mg/mL treatment groups compared with vehicle-treated mice (p > 0.05, Fig. 2B), suggesting that these doses did not exacerbate systemic weight loss under disease conditions. T2-weighted 7-Tesla Magnetic resonance imaging (7T MRI) revealed a significant reduction in lesion volume in both 1 mg/mL and 3 mg/mL treatment groups compared with vehicle-treated controls (p < 0.05 for each comparison; Fig. 2C, D). Moreover, the 3 mg/mL group showed a greater reduction than the 1 mg/mL group (p < 0.05; Fig. 2C, D), indicating a dose-dependent therapeutic effect of HFn-ApoE130-149. Based on these findings, the 3 mg/mL HFn-ApoE130-149 dosage was selected for subsequent therapeutic investigations, with equivalent doses of HFn alone and PBS used as controls.

We next evaluated the therapeutic impact of HFn-ApoE130−149 on myelin integrity, AQP4 expression, and astrocyte responses in NMOSD mice. Treatment with HFn-ApoE130−149 significantly attenuated the loss of myelin basic protein (MBP) (p < 0.0001 for each comparison; Fig. 2E, F) and AQP4 (p < 0.001 for each comparison; Fig. 2G, H) compared to the vehicle controls. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that vehicle-treated mice exhibited prominent perilesional accumulation of GFAP-positive astrocytes, consistent with robust perilesional astrogliosis. In contrast, HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment significantly reduced the density of GFAP-positive astrocytes in the perilesional area (Fig. 2I, J). Notably, remaining astrocytes displayed hypertrophic morphology with thickened processes (Fig. 2K), a common feature of reactive astrocytes [25]. The flow cytometric analysis of whole-brain tissues confirmed an increased proportion of GFAP-positive astrocytes in HFn-ApoE130−149-treated NMOSD mice compared to control (Fig. 2L, M). HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment boosted the proportion of proliferative astrocytes (GFAP+Ki67+), suggesting that the rise in GFAP+ cells is partly due to enhanced astrocyte proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). In addition, HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment increased the proportion of GFAP+S100A10+ astrocytes and decreased that of GFAP+C3+ astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1C-F). These findings indicate that HFn-ApoE130−149 promotes astrocyte proliferation and induces a shift toward a neuroprotective phenotype, thereby remodeling the astrocytic response in NMOSD.

HFn-ApoE130−149 alleviates neuroinflammation by polarizing microglia towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype in NMOSD mice

ApoE130−149 has been shown to facilitate astrocyte-microglia communication and reactive microgliosis in NMOSD injury [13], we next investigated the impact of its prodrug HFn-ApoE130−149 on microglia in the NMOSD model. Immunofluorescence analysis of the perilesional area revealed that HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment significantly reduced microglial density and altered their morphology compared to vehicle-treated controls. The HFn-ApoE130−149 group exhibited a significant decrease in the number of ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1) positive microglia adjacent to the lesion site compared with the control group (p < 0.0001 for each comparison; Fig. 3A, B). Microglia in the control groups displayed typical reactive features, characterized by enlarged cell bodies and hypertrophied, shortened processes (Fig. 3C, D). In contrast, the microglia in the HFn-ApoE130−149-treated group had fewer and longer branches, suggesting a more regulated phenotype (Fig. 3C, D).

Flow cytometric analysis of microglial populations in NMOSD mice revealed that administration of HFn-ApoE130-149 resulted in a significant reduction of pro-inflammatory (CD86+) microglia, alongside a concurrent increase of tissue-reparative (CD206+) microglia (Fig. 3E, F). Additionally, HFn-ApoE130-149 treatment led to a significant reduction in the recruitment of T cells and macrophages into the brain (Fig. 3G, H), while the infiltration of B cells, neutrophils and eosinophils remained similar between the treatment and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). These results indicate that HFn-ApoE130-149 effectively mitigated microgliosis, promoted a polarization shift of microglia towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and attenuated the infiltration of peripheral immune cells.

HFn-ApoE130−149 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects via the LRP1-NF-κB signaling axis

The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, a central regulator of innate and adaptive immunity, orchestrates inflammatory responses in NMOSD by amplifying the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [26, 27]. HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment significantly reduced the protein levels of phosphorylated NF-κB (p-NF-κB) and phosphorylated inhibitor of NF-κB (p-IκBα) in NMOSD mice compared to HFn-treated controls (p < 0.05 for each comparison; Fig. 4A, B). Consistent with the suppression of upstream NF-κB signaling, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) showed a significant decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), in the brains of NMOSD mice (Fig. 4C). To further assess pathway involvement, we used PS-1145 [28], a selective inhibitor of inhibitor of kappa B kinase β (IKKβ) that acts as a critical upstream activator of IκBα [29]. PS-1145 treatment suppressed NF-κB phosphorylation and subsequent cytokine production to a similar extent as HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment (Fig. 4D-F). Importantly, co-administration of HFn-ApoE130−149 with PS-1145 did not result in a further significant suppression of NF-κB pathway activation or cytokine levels compared to either treatment alone (Fig. 4D-F), suggesting that HFn-ApoE130−149 acts through the canonical NF-κB pathway.

Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) is well recognized as a high-affinity receptor for ApoE and modulates anti-inflammatory signaling in microglia [30, 31]. We next explored whether LRP1 mediates the anti-inflammatory action of HFn-ApoE130−149. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed the interaction between HFn-ApoE130−149 and LRP1 in brain lysates (Fig. 5A). This specific interaction was further visualized in situ by proximity ligation assay (PLA) performed on microglia from both wild-type (WT) and ApoE knockout (ApoE−/−) mice (Fig. 5B). Quantification revealed comparable levels of PLA signals per microglia in ApoE−/− mice and WT controls (Fig. 5C), indicating that the binding of HFn-ApoE130−149 to LRP1 is independent of endogenous ApoE.

To validate the functional role of LRP1 in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of HFn-ApoE130−149 via the NF-κB pathway in microglia, we performed in vivo knockdown of LRP1 using LRP1-targeting siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Microglia were subsequently isolated from brain tissue by magnetic bead sorting to obtain a purified population for subsequent protein analysis. Western blot analysis showed that HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment significantly suppressed NF-κB activation. This suppression was attenuated by LRP1 knockdown, which partially restored NF-κB signaling and cytokine production (Fig. 5D-F). In addition, structural modeling based on AlphaFold 3 predictions suggested potential binding interactions between key residues of the ApoE130−149 peptide (e.g., Glu131, Arg146) and specific domains of LRP1 (e.g., Asn3059, Asp3600), supporting the specificity of the molecular interaction (Fig. 5G). Collectively, these findings support that HFn-ApoE130−149 mediates its anti-inflammatory effects in NMOSD primarily through the LRP1-NF-κB axis in microglia (Fig. 5H).

HFn-ApoE130−149 rescues spatial learning and memory deficits in NMOSD mice

To evaluate the potential functional benefits of HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment, cognitive function was assessed in NMOSD mice at one month after treatment using the Morris water maze (MWM) test. During the five-day acquisition phase, NMOSD mice treated with HFn exhibited significantly prolonged escape latencies to locate the hidden platform compared to sham controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, NMOSD mice treated with HFn-ApoE130−149 showed a significant reduction in escape latency compared to the HFn-treated group by the end of training (p < 0.01; Fig. 6A, B). No significant differences in swimming speed were observed among all groups (p > 0.05 for each comparison; Fig. 6C), indicating that motor performance did not affect the learning assessment. Analysis of the probe trial conducted 24 h post-training revealed that the HFn-treated group exhibited a significantly prolonged latency to first cross the platform location compared to the sham group (p < 0.01; Fig. 6D). This latency was significantly shortened by HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment (Fig. 6D). Compared to sham controls, the HFn-treated group spent less time in the target quadrant (p < 0.001) and made fewer crossings over the target platform location (p < 0.001; Fig. 6D). Treatment with HFn-ApoE130−149 significantly improved these deficits compared to the HFn group, indicating effective rescue of spatial learning and memory impairments in NMOSD mice (Fig. 6D). Western blot analysis on brain tissues at the endpoint of behavioral testing revealed a notable decrease in postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95) levels in NMOSD mice compared to sham controls, which was restored by HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). These findings suggest that HFn-ApoE130−149 helps maintain synaptic integrity, potentially contributing to the enhanced cognitive function in NMOSD mice.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that HFn-ApoE130−149 functions effectively as a nanoprodrug, crossing the BBB to alleviate neuroinflammation and tissue injury in NMOSD mice. Its therapeutic effects are mainly attributed to targeting the LRP1-NF-κB axis in microglia, thereby suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These results underscore the potential of HFn-ApoE130−149 as a targeted treatment approach for NMOSD.

The BBB poses a significant challenge for delivering therapeutics to the CNS, limiting the efficacy of standard NMOSD treatments [32]. Ferritin-based nanocages offer a distinct advantage due to the intrinsic ability of the HFn to bind TfR1, a receptor highly expressed on BBB endothelial cells. This binding facilitates transcytosis while maintaining BBB integrity, ensuring efficient and safe drug delivery [33]. The preferential recognition of HFn over LFn by TfR1 underlies this selectivity [34]. Unlike high-affinity anti-TfR1 antibodies, which can trigger lysosomal degradation, the lower affinity of HFn allows the nanocage to remain intact during transport, avoiding enzymatic degradation [35]. Consistent with this mechanism, we show that HFn-ApoE130−149 rapidly accumulates in the brain of both healthy and NMOSD mice, with peak levels at 6 h post-administration [20]. The prolonged presence in diseased brains may be due to increased BBB permeability in pathological conditions. Additionally, HFn-ApoE130−149 is stable and easily scalable through simple self-assembly, offering a translational advantage over more complex synthetic nanocarriers.

Our findings demonstrate that HFn-ApoE130-149 confers significant protection against the loss of MBP and AQP4 in NMOSD mice. ApoE, a crucial apolipoprotein involved in lipid metabolism and neuroinflammation, exerts downstream effects by binding to various receptors [36]. Prior studies indicate that ApoE can reduce pro-inflammatory responses by promoting an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype, inhibiting the NF-κB pathway and complement cascade, and supporting BBB integrity [37–39]. The ApoE130-149 peptide maintains these essential functions of full-length ApoE while addressing the limitations of the native protein for therapeutic purposes [40]. Noteworthy advantages of the peptide include a simplified structure suitable for large-scale production through genetic engineering or chemical synthesis, as well as the potential for modifications to improve stability, prolong circulation time, and reduce immunogenicity.

Astrocyte-microglia interactions form a self-perpetuating pathological loop that critically drives neuroinflammation, BBB disruption, and tissue injury in NMOSD [5, 13]. In this cycle, activated astrocytes release cytokines, chemokines, and other immune mediators that attract and activate microglia, further fueling neuroinflammation and tissue damage [41, 42]. Our findings suggest that HFn-ApoE130−149 effectively reduces microglial accumulation and promotes a shift from the pro-inflammatory phenotype to the anti-inflammatory phenotype. This microglial state modulation is critical, as activated microglia release cytotoxic mediators that not only propagate inflammatory signaling and drive demyelination, but also exacerbate astrocytic damage and direct the reactive response of adjacent astrocytes [5]. In response to injury, astrocytes undergo reactive remodeling characterized by elevated GFAP expression, cellular hypertrophy, and functional and metabolic polarization [43]. While perilesional astrocyte accumulation has traditionally been viewed as inhibitory to regeneration [44], more recent studies suggest that reactive astrocytes can also contribute to inflammation resolution and tissue repair [45]. The phenotypic balance of these reactive astrocytes is malleable and influenced by intercellular cues, particularly from microglia [5]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms that dictate the functional specification of distinct astrocyte subsets, especially in the context of lesion evolution, are not fully understood. Our data show that HFn-ApoE130−149 limits aberrant perilesional astrocyte accumulation and promotes a phenotypic shift towards a reparative state. This indicates that HFn-ApoE130−149 reshapes the reactive astroglial landscape, favoring the establishment of a microenvironment conducive to neural protection and recovery. Given the diverse roles of astrocytes across disease stages, further studies will be required to determine the precise mechanisms and temporal dynamics of astrocyte remodeling in response to treatment.

The NF-κB signaling pathway is a master regulator of immunity and inflammation [26, 46], and it has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple autoimmune and neuroinflammatory disorders [47–49]. In NMOSD, AQP4-IgG has been shown to activate canonical NF-κB signaling in astrocytes, contributing to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 [50]. Prior research has demonstrated that LRP1 can suppress NF-κB activation in macrophages, and LRP1 deficiency leads to heightened inflammatory responses [46]. In the present study, we identified a physical interaction between HFn-ApoE130-149 and LRP1 and observed that inhibition of NF-κB signaling by HFn-ApoE130-149 was attenuated following LRP1 knockdown in vivo. These findings support the involvement of LRP1 in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of HFn-ApoE130-149. Importantly, our data suggest that this effect is associated with modulation of the NF-κB pathway in microglia, as demonstrated using microglia-enriched cell populations. Therefore, we propose a signaling axis whereby HFn-ApoE130-149, upon binding to LRP1 on microglia, initiates a signaling cascade that inhibits the NF-κB pathway. Collectively, our work defines a novel, specific mechanism of action for HFn-ApoE130-149 and identifies the microglial LRP1–NF-κB axis as a critical therapeutic target in NMOSD.

Behavioral testing revealed spatial memory deficits in NMOSD mice, which were ameliorated by HFn-ApoE130−149 treatment. The precise mechanism underlying this cognitive improvement merits further investigation. Cognitive impairments are common in NMOSD patients but are often overlooked in clinical studies [51, 52]. The hippocampus, crucial for spatial memory, relies on synaptic plasticity and circuit integrity [53, 54]. Neuroinflammation, astrocyte and microglial activation, along with BBB disruption, can allow serum components, such as complement, to enter the hippocampal parenchyma, disrupting circuit homeostasis and contributing to cognitive deficits [55, 56]. Neuroinflammation leads to downregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)/tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) signaling and reduced dendritic spine density in the CA1 and DG regions [57]. Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines further exacerbate hippocampal injury and cognitive dysfunction [58]. We propose that HFn-ApoE130−149 inhibits microglial activation and cytokine release, thereby indirectly preserving hippocampal function. Furthermore, HFn-ApoE130−149 may promote synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis by directly engaging neuronal LRP1, potentially improving cognitive function. Future studies employing region-specific analyses should provide more insight into the hippocampal mechanisms involved in this process.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the NMOSD mouse model does not fully recapitulate the complexity and progression of human disease, which may affect translational applicability. Second, this study focused on the therapeutic effects of HFn-ApoE130−149 in the acute pathological stage. Future studies involving longer treatment and observation periods are needed to evaluate its sustained benefits. Third, while a dose of 3 mg/mL was identified as effective and well-tolerated, administration of higher doses led to significant toxicity and mortality. The precise mechanisms underlying this dose-dependent toxicity require further investigation. Potential adverse effects could arise from the disruption of systemic iron homeostasis by H-ferritin, which may increase labile iron and oxidative stress [59], and off-target accumulation of nanocarriers in peripheral organs [60]. Administration of supratherapeutic doses may also provoke excessive immune activation or a systemic inflammatory response [61]. Fourth, while HFn-ApoE130−149 clearly modulated microglial activation, its effects on other CNS cell types and the broader neurovascular unit require further clarification. Finally, although our findings provide initial mechanistic insights, the downstream signaling pathways and cellular states regulated by HFn-ApoE130−149 remain incompletely defined. Future studies incorporating detailed toxicological assessment, biodistribution analysis, and multi-omics approaches will be essential to better define both the therapeutic potential and safety profile of this strategy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, HFn-ApoE130−149 represents a promising nanotherapeutic candidate for the treatment of NMOSD. The HFn nanocage serves as an efficient delivery vehicle to facilitate the BBB penetration of ApoE130−149 peptide, thereby enhancing its therapeutic efficacy. This study establishes a novel strategy that integrates nanomedicine with neuroimmunology and highlights the considerable translational potential of HFn-ApoE130−149 for the targeted therapy of NMOSD.

Study design

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence created by an independent researcher not involved in animal handling, procedures, or data assessment. This ensured allocation concealment and prevented selection bias. Throughout the experiment, all investigators were blinded to group assignments during animal care, treatment, measurement, and analysis. The blinding protocol was strictly followed until all data collection and statistical analyses were completed. Sample sizes per group were determined in advance based on effect sizes from similar previous studies, ensuring statistical power of over 80% [13, 20]. Exclusion criteria outlined in the Methods section were applied consistently, and no data points were excluded beyond these criteria.

Animal models and ethics

AQP4-IgG was purified from the plasma of AQP4-IgG-seropositive NMOSD patients using IMMUSORBA TR-350 plasma immunoadsorption columns (JIMRO, Japan). Antibody specificity and concentration were confirmed by a cell-based indirect immunofluorescence assay (EUROIMMUN AG, Germany) and ELISA. Samples with AQP4-IgG titers > 80 U/mL were used, and the final IgG concentration was adjusted to 35–40 mg/mL. The complement was provided by QuidelOrtho (A113). Female C57BL/6J mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) and C57BL/6J ApoE−/− mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:002052) (8–10 weeks old; Sipeifu Biotechnology, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 23 °C, in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH). Female mice were selected to reflect the higher prevalence of NMOSD in human females and their robust immune responses in autoimmune models [13]. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Beijing Neurosurgical Institute and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines (Approval No. BNI202305006).

An NMOSD model was established by transcranial co-injection of AQP4-IgG and hC, simulating key pathological features of the human disease [22]. Mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (20 µL per gram of body weight) and secured in a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, China). A midline scalp incision was made to expose the bregma, and a burr hole was then drilled 2.5 mm lateral to the bregma on the right side using a high-speed drill (RWD Life Science, China). A 30-gauge needle was inserted to a depth of 3 mm to administer 15 µL of a mixture containing AQP4-IgG and hC at a 3:2 ratio. The mixture was infused intracerebrally at a rate of 1 µL/min using a microinjection pump (Aoran, China). The needle was left in place for 5 min post-injection to prevent backflow and then slowly withdrawn over 5 min. Sham-operated control mice underwent the same surgical procedure but received an injection of an equivalent amount of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Mice were monitored daily for body weight, neurological deficits, and signs of pain or distress. This model typically results in acute inflammation, astrocytopathy, and demyelination within 3–5 days post-injection, as previously characterized [13].

Preparation of HFn-ApoE130−149

The gene encoding HFn-ApoE130−149 was designed as a fusion protein, comprising the HFn sequence (SEQ ID NO.1: MTTASTSQVRQNYHQDSEAAINRQINLELYASYVYLSMSYYFDRDDVALKNFAKYFLHQSHEEREHAEKLMKLQNQRGGRIFLQDIKKPDCDDWESGLNAMECALHLEKNVNQSLLELHKLATDKNDPHLCDFIETHYLNEQVKAIKELGDHVTNLRKMGAPESGLAEYLFDKHTLGDSDNES) linked to the ApoE130−149 peptide (TEELRVRLASHLRKLRKRLL) via a flexible (GGSG)3 linker. The synthesized gene was digested with NdeI and BamHI restriction endonucleases and cloned into the corresponding sites of the E. coli expression vector pET-22b(+) to generate the recombinant plasmid pET-22b(+)/HFn-ApoE130−149 for recombinant protein production. The resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. A single transformed colony was inoculated into LB medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Protein expression was then induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 30 °C for 8 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min, and resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). Cells were lysed using high-pressure homogenization. The supernatant was subjected to heat treatment at 72 °C for 15 min to precipitate host proteins, followed by a second centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min to collect the clarified supernatant. Subsequently, the HFn-ApoE130−149 protein was purified using an anion exchange column (Q-Sepharose Fast Flow), and finally purified by Superdex 200 (10/300 GL, GE Healthcare) size-exclusion chromatography. The concentration of HFn-ApoE130−149 was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pierce) with bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Drug administration

All therapeutic formulations (HFn-ApoE130−149, empty HFn nanocages, and PBS) were administered intravenously via the tail vein. The initial dose was administered immediately after the intracerebral injection of AQP4-IgG/complement (or PBS for sham controls). This dosing schedule was designed to coincide with the initiation of neuroinflammation, based on previous pharmacokinetic data for ferritin-based nanocarriers and the established pathological time course in this NMOSD model [13, 20]. For tail vein injections, mice were gently restrained in a designated mouse restrainer, and a 1 mL insulin syringe (BD Biosciences) was used for the injection. A standard volume of 200 µL per 20 g of body weight was administered slowly over 10–20 s. For LRP1 knockdown, a pool of validated LRP1-specific siRNAs (RiboBio, SiBDMV002) was used. 0.5 nmol of LRP1 siRNA was dissolved in 2 µL of ribonuclease-free water. Twenty-four hours before NMOSD induction, the siRNA solution was infused into the right lateral ventricle (stereotactic coordinates relative to bregma: anteroposterior, -0.5 mm; mediolateral, + 1.0 mm; dorsoventral, -2.5 mm) at a rate of 0.2 µL/min. Control mice received an equivalent volume and concentration of a non-targeting scrambled siRNA sequence. For IKK inhibition, PS-1145 (TargetMol, T3094) was administered intraperitoneally. A stock solution was prepared by dissolving PS-1145 in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which was then diluted with sterile 0.9% saline to the final working concentration. PS-1145 (50 mg/kg) or an equivalent volume of the vehicle control (10% DMSO in saline) was administered once daily for three consecutive days, with the first injection given 24 h before NMOSD induction. The injection volume was standardized to 100 µL per 20 g of body weight.

Cy5.5-conjugated HFn-ApoE130-149 and fluorescence imaging

HFn-ApoE130−149 was conjugated with the fluorescent dye Cy5.5 for fluorescence imaging. Cy5.5 NHS ester (50 µg in 10 µL DMSO) was added to a solution of HFn-ApoE130−149 (2 mg/mL) in 10 mL of sodium carbonate buffer. The reaction mixture was protected from light and incubated at 4 °C overnight with constant mixing on a rotary shaker to facilitate conjugation via the formation of stable amide bonds with lysine residues on the protein nanocage. After purification by size-exclusion chromatography using a PD MiniTrap G-25 column (GE Healthcare, America), the concentration of the Cy5.5-HFn-ApoE130−149 conjugate was determined by UV-Vis spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein concentration was assayed using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pierce) with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Anesthetized mice received an intravenous injection of Cy5.5-HFn-ApoE130−149 (3 mg/mL in PBS) via the tail vein at a dose of 200 µL per 20 g of body weight. Then, fluorescence imaging was performed using the Xenogen IVIS Lumina II system (PerkinElmer, America), and the fluorescence intensity within each ROI was quantified as mean radiant efficiency (p/s/cm2/sr) using the Living Image software (PerkinElmer). To ensure accurate quantification, background fluorescence, measured from ROIs in adjacent tissue regions without signal, was subtracted from all measurements. All image sets were normalized to an identical color scale to enable direct comparison of fluorescence intensities across different time points and experimental groups.

7 Tesla magnetic resonance imaging

On day 4 post-model induction, MRI was performed using a 7T scanner equipped with a 31-cm ultra-shielded magnet and BioSpec Avance III technologies (BioClinScan, Bruker, Germany). The maximum gradient amplitude applied was 290 mT/m, with a switching rate of 1160 T/m/s. The MRI scanner was equipped with an actively decoupled four-channel surface coil. T2-weighted images were acquired using a spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: repetition time/echo time = 3080/31 ms; matrix size = 192 × 320; field of view = 24 × 30 mm²; slice thickness = 0.5 mm. Lesion volume and borders were analyzed by manually tracing the hyperintense area on each T2-weighted slice using ITK-SNAP software (Version 3.8, NYU Tandon School of Engineering).

Immunofluorescence staining

On day 4 post-model induction, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (25 µL per gram of body weight). The thoracic cavity was opened to expose the heart, followed by transcardial perfusion through the left ventricle with 20 mL of ice-cold 1× PBS and subsequent 20 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for tissue fixation. Brains were carefully dissected and postfixed in the same 4% PFA solution for 24 h at 4 °C, and cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions at 4 °C until they sank. The samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek), rapidly frozen on dry ice, and stored at -80 °C until sectioning. Coronal Sect. (16 μm thickness) were collected using a cryostat at -20 °C (Leica CM1950, Germany). For immunofluorescence staining, sections were permeabilized and blocked by incubation with a solution containing 5% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-AQP4 (1:200, Abcam, ab46182), rabbit anti-MBP (1:500, Cell Signaling, 78896s), rabbit anti-GFAP (1:500, Abcam, ab7260), mouse anti-GFAP (1:500, Cell Signaling, 3670), Rat anti-C3 (1:100, Abcam, ab11862), Rabbit anti-S100A10 (1:100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-95505), Rat anti-Ki67 (1:100, Thermo Fisher Scientific,14-5698-82), and rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:400, Fujifilm Wako, 019-19741). Sections were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with a fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Invitrogen, A-11034), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21202), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21209), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21207). After incubation with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Abcam, ab104139), fluorescence images were acquired using the PerkinElmer Vectra Polaris automated quantitative pathology imaging system. For each mouse, three coronal sections (spaced 300 μm apart) and three non-overlapping fields of view per section within the lesion boundary were imaged. Image analysis was performed using FIJI/ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) software.

Flow cytometry

On day 4 post-model induction, mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 40 mL of ice-cold 1× PBS via the left ventricle. The entire brain was carefully dissected, mechanically dissociated and enzymatically digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. The homogenate was passed through a 70-µm cell strainer (Falcon) to obtain a single-cell suspension, which was then centrifuged over a 30% Percoll solution (Cytiva, 17089101) at 700 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove the myelin layer. After lysing red blood cells (RBCs), the cell viability was assessed using Trypan Blue, and the total cell count was determined using a hemocytometer. For surface marker staining, cells were stained with an optimized cocktail of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. For intracellular staining (CD206, GFAP), cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer (BioLegend, 421002) after surface staining. Cell viability was assessed using Zombie Aqua viability dye (BioLegend, 423113) or 7-AAD (BioLegend, 420440). The antibody panel included: AF-700-conjugated anti-CD11b (BioLegend, clone M1/70, 101222), APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD45 (BioLegend, clone 30-F11, 103116), PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-CD86 (BioLegend, clone GL-1, 105028), FITC-conjugated anti-CD206 (BioLegend, clone C068C2, 141703), PE-conjugated anti-GFAP (BD Pharmingen, clone 1B4, 561483), PE-conjugated anti-CD3 (BioLegend, clone 17A2, 100206), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD19 (BioLegend, clone 6D5, 115520), BV605-conjugated anti-Ly6G (BioLegend, clone 1A8, 127639), PE-conjugated anti-Siglec-F (eBioscience, clone 1RNM44N, 12-1702-82) and FITC-conjugated anti-F4/80 (BioLegend, clone BM8, 123108). Appropriate isotype controls and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were included in every experiment to ensure accurate gating and compensation. Data were acquired on a BD FACSAria Ⅲ (BD Biosciences) equipped with five lasers (405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, 633 nm). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Biosciences). At least 100,000 live single-cell events were recorded per sample. Live cells were identified by gating on Zombie-negative or 7-AAD-negative populations. Cell populations were defined as follows: microglia (CD11b+ CD45int), astrocytes (CD45− GFAP+), macrophages (CD11b+ CD45+ F4/80+), neutrophils (CD11b+ CD45+ Ly6G+), eosinophils (CD11b+ CD45+ Siglec-F+), T cells (CD11b−CD45+ CD3+) and B cells (CD11b− CD45+ CD19+). Microglia activation states were further characterized based on the expression of CD86 (M1 phenotype marker) and CD206 (M2 phenotype marker).

Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)

On day 2 post-model induction, brain CD11b-positive cells (enriched for microglia) were isolated using anti-mouse CD11b MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-093-636) and MACS with an MS Column. Briefly, freshly harvested whole-brain tissue was mechanically dissociated in cold MACS buffer and filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer. After centrifugation (300 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), the cell pellet was resuspended in 90 µL of MACS buffer and incubated with 10 µL of CD11b MicroBeads for 15 min at 4 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed by adding 2 mL of buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of MACS buffer. A pre-equilibrated MS Column (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-201) was placed in the magnetic field of a MiniMACS Separator (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-090-312) and pre-rinsed with 500 µL of MACS buffer. The cell suspension was then applied to the column. After the sample had passed through, the column was washed three times with 500 µL of buffer. Following the final wash, the column was removed from the magnetic field, and magnetically retained CD11b-positive cells were eluted by applying 1 mL of buffer and firmly pushing the plunger. The eluted cells were collected for subsequent protein extraction and Western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis and co-immunoprecipitation assay

On day 2 post-model induction, the ipsilateral brain hemisphere adjacent to the lesion site was rapidly dissected, weighed, and homogenized on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, including 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Abcam, ab65621) and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78441). Homogenization was performed using a bead-based homogenizer (Servicebio, China) at 70 Hz for two cycles of 60 s each at 4 °C. After incubation on ice for 30 min, the lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pierce).

For Co-IP, protein samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation with 5 µg of either rabbit anti-LRP1 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-31959) or mouse anti-ApoE antibody (Proteintech, 66830-1-Ig). Normal rabbit IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC-2027) and normal mouse IgG (SC-2025) served as isotype controls. Then, 25 µL (0.25 mg) of Pierce protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88804) were added to each sample, followed by incubation at room temperature with agitation for 1 h. The immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted for further Western blot analysis.

Total protein lysates (15 µg per sample) or co-immunoprecipitated samples were denatured in loading buffer (Beyotime, P0015) at 95 °C for 5 min. Denatured samples along with a prestained protein molecular weight marker (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26616) were loaded onto 12% precast polyacrylamide gels (Beyotime, P0468S). Electrophoresis was conducted at a constant voltage of 80 V through the stacking gel and 120 V through the separating gel until the dye front reached the bottom of the gel. Proteins were then transferred from the gel to a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane (Amersham, 0.45 μm) using the electrophoretic transfer system (Springen, China) for 12 min. After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies.

The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-LRP1 antibody (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-31959), mouse anti-ApoE antibody (1:3000, Proteintech, 66830-1-Ig), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, #3033), rabbit anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, #8242), rabbit anti-phospho-IκBα (1:1000, Cell Signaling, #2859), mouse anti-IκBα (1:1000, Cell Signaling, #4814), rabbit anti-PSD-95 (1:250, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 51-6900) and mouse anti-β-Actin (1:5000, TransGen Biotech, HC201). Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:5000, Cwbio CW103s; goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:5000, Cwbio CW102s), the membranes were visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34579) and imaged with a minichemiluminescent imaging system (SINSAGE). Band intensities were quantified using FIJI/ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

On day 2 post-model induction, the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) in brain homogenates were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience: E-EL-M0037 for IL-1β, E-EL-M0044 for IL-6, E-EL-M3063 for TNF-α) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fresh brain tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Abcam, ab65621), followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Standards, blanks, and samples (100 µL) were added in duplicate to the pre-coated plates and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. After washing, 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by 100 µL of HRP-conjugated streptavidin (30 min at 37 °C). The reaction was developed with 90 µL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (15 min at 37 °C in the dark), stopped, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a TECAN Infinite M200 microplate reader (Switzerland). Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve and the sample dilution factor.

Proximity ligation assay

On day 2 post-model induction, the in situ interaction between HFn-ApoE130−149 and its putative receptor, LRP1, was detected in brain sections using the Duolink PLA kit (Sigma-Aldrich, DUO92101). Fresh-frozen brain Sect. (16 μm thickness) were blocked with Duolink blocking buffer (1 h, 37 °C) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-LRP1 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-31959) and mouse anti-ApoE antibody (Proteintech, 66830-1-Ig). After washing, sections were incubated with species-specific PLA probes (1 h, 37 °C), followed by ligation (30 min, 37 °C) and rolling-circle amplification (100 min, 37 °C) to generate detectable signals. The reaction was stopped by washing with buffer B, and nuclei were counterstained with Duolink DAPI. For immunofluorescence co-staining, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-Iba1 antibody (1:400, Fujifilm Wako, 019-19741) and an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Invitrogen, A-11034). PLA signals (distinct red dots, each representing a protein interaction within 40 nm) were visualized by confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 880). Negative controls (Blank) were performed by omitting one primary antibody (anti-ApoE) to assess nonspecific signal. For quantitative analysis, three non-overlapping random fields within the ipsilateral lesion area were captured per mouse. The number of PLA puncta and Iba1+ microglia were counted using FIJI/ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) with unified threshold settings. The number of PLA puncta per Iba1+ microglia was determined for each field. These values were then averaged per animal, with data expressed as the mean puncta count per cell.

Morris water maze tests

On the 28th day after model induction, spatial learning and memory were assessed through the MWM test. The test involved a 120 cm diameter circular pool filled with water (35 cm deep, 24–25 °C). A hidden platform was positioned 1 cm below the water surface in the target quadrant, surrounded by visual cues. During the acquisition phase, mice underwent four trials daily for five consecutive days, starting from random points, with each trial lasting a maximum of 60 s. A probe trial was conducted 24 h after the last acquisition trial to evaluate spatial memory retention. Mice were released from the quadrant opposite to the platform location and allowed to swim freely for 60 s. Memory retention was assessed by analyzing the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of crossings over the platform location. Parameters such as swimming speed, escape latency, time in the target quadrant, and platform crossings were measured and recorded using a video tracking system (Version 14, EthoVision XT).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified in the figure legends. The sample size (n) for each experiment represents biological replicates, defined as data points obtained from distinct animals. The specific n value for each experiment is detailed in the corresponding figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (9.5.1). Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for comparing two groups under a single experimental condition. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for comparisons among more than two groups. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for data with repeated measurements over time. Survival curve comparisons between groups were analyzed using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Detailed statistical information is provided in the figure legends.
