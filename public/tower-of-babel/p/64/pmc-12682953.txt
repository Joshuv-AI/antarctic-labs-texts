Durable left ventricular assist device pump stoppage due to rotor dysfunction
Authors: Sarah Madira, Mehran Rahimi, Erin Schumer, Matthew Schill, Mary Sullivan, Justin Hartupee, Gregory Ewald, Kunal Kotkar, Muhammad F Masood, Amit Pawale
Published: 2025

HeartMate 3 (Abbott) pump rotor with scratches consistent with rotor-to-housing contact.

Central Message

Magnetic levitation failure from the pump's computer cell corruption led to a rare HeartMate 3 (Abbott) pump stoppage. Rapid recognition and emergency intervention is essential for patients with LVAD.

The HeartMate 3 (Abbott) is a third-generation, durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implanted in patients with refractory end-stage heart failure. Its magnetically levitated centrifugal pump minimizes shear stress and hemolysis while offering improved durability over earlier models.1 The LVAD includes an inflow cannula, a pumping chamber with a rotor operating at 5000 to 6000 rpm (delivering 3-6 L/minute), an outflow cannula connecting to the ascending aorta, a percutaneous driveline, and an external controller that monitors system performance.2,3

Malfunctions in newer-generation devices remain exceptionally rare but are catastrophic. Pump stoppages are an uncommon etiology of documented LVAD failures.4 This report details a previously unreported mode of sudden HeartMate 3 pump stoppage due to rotor levitation failure. Patient written consent was waived because the case report was determined not human subject research (IRB#202311178; November 28, 2023).

Case Presentation

A 64-year-old with a HeartMate II (Abbott) implanted 9 years ago presented with persistent device low-flow alarms, fatigue, and exertional dyspnea. History included stroke, gastrointestinal bleeds, and chronic driveline infections. Imaging revealed pump thrombosis and he underwent HeartMate II to HeartMate 3 exchange with bivalirudin anticoagulation.

Five days postexchange, he became acutely hypotensive with pulseless electrical activity. The HeartMate 3 emitted a grinding noise, displayed 0 RPM, 0 L/minute flow, and failed to restart despite 20 W power draw. The patient required cardiopulmonary resuscitation, epinephrine, and femoral venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

The patient underwent HeartMate 3 to HeartMate 3 exchange. After reopening incisions and initiating bypass, outflow grafts were divided, confirming good flow. The previous HeartMate 3 was removed with no thrombus found. A new HeartMate 3 was inserted and started at 5000 rpm, achieving 4 L flow. During weaning from bypass, the patient developed refractory hypoxemia and right ventricle dysfunction requiring a percutaneous, oxygenated right VAD. The oxygenator was removed after 11 days, and the right VAD was decannulated 4 weeks later. His course was complicated by gastric erosions, bleeding, and dialysis-dependent renal failure. He was discharged home 2 months later in New York Heart Association functional class II and remains dialysis-dependent.

Manufacturer analysis of LVAD components found no thrombus or electrical discontinuity. The controller functioned normally. However, the rotor and rotor housing had circumferential scratches with failed levitation. Software analysis identified computer random access memory (RAM) cell corruption in the rotor angle control module. The patient's event log showed escalating warning signs of high current, magnetic centering errors, and rotor displacement faults.

Discussion

This case illustrates an exceedingly rare and, to our knowledge, previously unreported instance of HeartMate 3 rotor dysfunction from RAM cell corruption. The more common culprits of LVAD malfunction include inflow or outflow obstruction and pump thrombosis. Outflow obstruction and thrombosis generally present with elevated power due to increased resistance, whereas inflow obstruction (eg, suction events) can result in low flow and low power. These scenarios typically preserve partial rotor motion. In contrast, our case involved complete pump stoppage and 0 rpm, indicating intrinsic pump failure and a mechanical grinding noise that was particularly concerning for rotor-to-housing contact.

The HeartMate 3 rotor is suspended via electromagnetic levitation. The RAM stores real-time rotor position, speed, and magnetic field data used by the microcontroller to maintain magnetic rotor suspension. In this patient, microcontroller RAM corruption interfered with rotor angle computation, preventing magnetic stabilization and leading to physical rotor-to-housing contact as evidenced by circumferential scratch marks and failed levitation in mock loop testing (Figure 1). Because flow is directly proportional to rotor speed, progressive rotor instability resulted in a critical loss of pump efficiency precipitating hemodynamic collapse rescued by emergency surgical intervention.

Conclusions

This case describes complete HeartMate 3 pump stoppage due to software-related failed rotor levitation. Timely diagnosis of complete irreversible pump stoppage is crucial because an LVAD-dependent patient will likely need venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and pump exchange.
