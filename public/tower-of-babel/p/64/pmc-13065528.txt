System Preparedness for Rising Kidney Replacement Therapy Demand: Late-Stage CKD Drives Costs, Healthcare Resource Utilisation and Environmental Impact in Switzerland
Authors: Thomas Campbell-James, Stefan Buff, Giliane Nanchen, Andrew Hall, Thomas Rosemann, Lindsay Nicholson, Stacey Priest, Anthony Zara, Luke Hubbert, Caterina Vecchio Rodriguez, George Wharton, Menno Pruijm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, affecting one in ten Swiss adults. Patient numbers are expected to increase due to demographic shift and increasing comorbidity rates, necessitating strategic healthcare planning. Holistic burden remains unknown and detailed projections—including expectations of prospective economic and environmental impact—are lacking. To inform Switzerland’s public health strategies and support its 2050 net-zero healthcare goal, this study aims to forecast the multidimensional burdens of CKD.MethodsThe IMPACT-CKD microsimulation model was utilised to project CKD-progression and clinical, healthcare resource utilisation, economic, societal, and environmental outcomes in Switzerland over 10 years (2023–2032). Modelled individuals were assigned CKD-relevant characteristics—including kidney function, comorbidities and clinical events—based on real-world data. Individuals were categorised into non-CKD or one of six CKD stages and progressed through the disease, diagnosed or undiagnosed. CKD would account for 4.6% of the Swiss healthcare budget, incur CHF 12.9b in lost productivity, 43.2m missed workdays and CHF 606m in lost tax revenue. Greenhouse gas emissions are projected to increase by 12.9%, driven by rising dialysis demand.ConclusionIMPACT-CKD projects substantial increases in the multidimensional burdens of CKD in Switzerland. Impact could be mitigated via earlier detection and broader uptake of guideline-directed medical therapy. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for a minimum of 3 months, with implications for health [1]. Representing a significant public health concern, CKD affects 850 million persons globally [2] and is expected to become the world’s fifth leading cause of mortality by 2040 [3]. In Switzerland—where potentially avoidable hospitalisations (PAHs) due to noncommunicable diseases are forecast to increase by up to 51% until 2032 [4]—CKD affects one in ten adults, though patient numbers may rise, driven by an ageing population and increasing rates of morbidity, including diabetes and hypertension [5–8]. Switzerland’s healthcare expenditures related to CKD are already estimated to have increased by 46.7% to 646 million Swiss francs (CHF) between 2012 and 2017, driven by outpatient care and a 36.6% increase in spending per patient [9]. However, the true treated disease prevalence for CKD in outpatient care—and therefore also the related expenditure estimates—is likely significantly underestimated by the underlying methodology.

While diagnosis of CKD is simple, via blood, urine or imaging performed during a check-up or in case of intercurrent illness [1], and national primary care guidelines support early detection and staging in Switzerland [10], CKD remains underdiagnosed. Early‑stage CKD is often asymptomatic and there is insufficient screening of at risk-patients in Switzerland, which may provide a potential explanation for this underdiagnosis [1, 11]. Patients progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) require life‑sustaining kidney replacement therapy (KRT; i.e. dialysis or kidney transplant), which is highly resource-intensive for patients, healthcare systems, and the environment [12–14]. Notably, while KRT represents approximately 1% of Switzerland’s overall CKD population [5, 15, 16], it is responsible for 12.8% of CKD-related spending [17–20]. Further, depending on modality, one dialysis treatment consumes roughly 500 L of water, produces 3.8 kg of plastic waste and uses more electricity than an average Swiss family per day. This is amplified over a lifetime of treatment with an average of three sessions per week [21, 22]. As demonstrated in two recent studies, including an international life cycle assessment (LCA) quantifying the environmental burden of CKD care across multiple countries and identifying KRT as a major driver of emissions, these resource demands highlight the substantial environmental impact of KRT [13, 23]. Given these environmental demands, the increasing need for KRT poses a direct challenge to Switzerland’s commitment of achieving net-zero, climate-resilient healthcare by 2050 [24].

Given the multidimensional burdens of CKD, population‑level strategies are required to facilitate effective identification and timely intervention, thus mitigating progression to later CKD stages [25, 26]. While prior studies have described the current burden of CKD in Switzerland, evidence gaps exist in projecting its future, long-term multidimensional burdens across public health domains. The IMPACT-CKD model is an individual-level microsimulation designed to quantify the clinical, healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU), economic, societal and environmental burdens of CKD [27]. Informed by a conceptual framework for CKD developed by the London School of Economics, IMPACT-CKD was developed in consultation with over 60 global cross-functional experts, extensively validated and subsequently applied to project 10-year burden across eight countries [26–28]. It provides valuable population-level insights beyond future prevalence, including stratification of burden by CKD stage and KRT, as well as broader impacts on society and environment. Notably, IMPACT-CKD analyses for other European countries projected variable 10-year burden increases related to KRT, with dialysis rates projected to rise by 4% to 96%, resulting in KRT-related costs jumping by 20% to 95% and environmental burdens by 12% to 94% [28]. This study aimed to utilise the IMPACT-CKD framework to project Switzerland’s population-specific, multidimensional 10-year burdens of CKD, based on current patterns of diagnosis and treatment, to inform public health strategies and planning.

Methods

Model Structure

IMPACT-CKD is a validated individual-level microsimulation model simulating CKD development and progression to estimate the multidimensional burdens across the clinical, HCRU, economic, societal and environmental domains [27]. Here, the model was employed to generate a Swiss population, including individuals with and without CKD, using prevalence distributions. One million individuals were simulated over a 10-year time horizon (baseline [2022] and ten subsequent annual simulations [2023–2032]). Disease burden projections were aggregated and extrapolated to the national level (Fig. 1).

At baseline, individuals were generated at random from Swiss population-specific distributions for the following characteristics: age, sex, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR), and KRT (i.e. dialysis and kidney transplantation). IMPACT-CKD defines CKD stages and associated prevalence via eGFR and UACR, according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [1]. Disease states included non-CKD, CKD stages 1 through 5 (higher stages corresponding to lower kidney function), dialysis, kidney transplantation, and death. Additionally, comorbidities were assigned at baseline, including heart failure (HF), hypertension, diabetes and a history of stroke or myocardial infarction (MI). These were assigned based on age, sex, and CKD stage distributions. All individuals could also be assigned as a fast disease progressor to reflect a proportion of patients experiencing more rapid eGFR decline than average; however, only patients with CKD could experience fast eGFR decline over each model cycle.

In each 1-year cycle, stage progression, transplantation, initiation of dialysis and clinical events could occur. Due to limited health system capacity and organ availability, caps on dialysis and transplantation growth were considered. Clinical events that could occur included acute kidney injury (AKI), cardiovascular (CV; MI, stroke, and hospitalisation for heart failure [HHF]) and mortality. Parameters for all simulated individuals were updated at the end of each cycle, including age advancing by 1 year, development of a comorbidity (HF, hypertension, and diabetes) and annual eGFR decline. Annual eGFR decline occurred differentially based on CKD status each cycle. In patients with CKD, eGFR was informed by slope equations with fast progressors modelled separately [29]. A constant linear age-based rate for eGFR decline was assumed for individuals without CKD. Updated eGFR values informed CKD stage reclassification and associated prevalence estimates each cycle.

Following completion of one million individual simulations, outcomes were aggregated and extrapolated to the Swiss population, reflecting births and net migration in the population. Epidemiological outputs included CKD, clinical events, comorbidities, KRT and mortality. Epidemiological projections were then leveraged to estimate the HCRU, economic, societal and environmental burdens of CKD.

This study takes a societal perspective. Direct, stage-specific healthcare costs, including medication, outpatient care, dialysis, transplant surgery and maintenance, were taken from Germany and converted to Swiss costs using a recognised three-step process: correction for different levels of resource utilisation, for different prices of healthcare services, and for change in the level of resource utilisation and prices over time [17–20, 30]. This methodology was developed and validated by the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health to estimate healthcare expenditures associated with noncommunicable diseases in Switzerland [20] and has since been replicated in other research [31, 32]. Costing inputs and methodology are summarised in Supplemental Table S36. Societal costs, including productivity losses, missed workdays and lost tax revenue, capture broader economic consequences on patients, caregivers and the national economy.

Model Inputs

Demographic and clinical data were derived from literature searches and Swiss national databases and supplemented by consultation with clinical and health economic experts (Supplemental Tables S1–S43). Ethical approval was not applicable as only publicly available aggregated data were utilised, making individual identification impossible. Data from the Medical Statistics of Hospitals is available upon request from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office (FSO). Data from the FIRE project is available upon request from the Institute of Primary Care, University of Zurich. Key inputs included general population epidemiology data from the Swiss FSO [15, 33–38], population-representative eGFR and UACR distributions from the FIRE project (Family Medicine International Classification of Primary Care-Research using Electronic Medical Records; specifically for this project, renal function test data from a Swiss primary care population of over 226 thousand patients were analysed [39]), and historical dialysis and transplant incidences from the European Renal Association [40–42]. When Swiss data were unavailable, proxy data from other countries with similar healthcare systems or expert opinion were leveraged. It was assumed that the incidence of kidney transplantation would grow at 0.9% (aligning with population growth, thus reflecting increased organ availability [15]) and dialysis capacity would grow at 3.0% per year (reflecting historical trends [40–42]). Input cost data, derived from Germany and converted as explained above, are outlined in Supplemental Table S36. Inputs informing societal outcomes are outlined in Supplemental Tables S37–S40.

An LCA was used to characterise the environmental impacts, in terms of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, freshwater consumption, and fossil fuel depletion, associated with CKD across all disease stages, following ISO 14040/14044 international standards. An existing LCA framework, previously published via comparison of CKD-related environmental outcomes in ten countries, was applied and adapted to the Swiss context [13]. The framework comprises a series of disease-agnostic and CKD stage- and KRT-specific modules representing key healthcare resource components, including specialist care visits, hospitalisation events, and KRT, stratified by modality, as validated previously in other contexts [13]. Swiss-specific data inputs, such as energy mix, travel distance, heating, cooling, and lighting requirements, were used, where applicable (Supplemental Table 41). The environmental impact for each module was calculated using ReCiPe 2016 v1.1 impact assessment methodology, designed to be globally applicable [43]. Environmental impacts for each module were first scaled according to annual HCRU and then aggregated to estimate the annualised environmental burden for each CKD stage, including patients receiving KRT (Supplemental Tables S42–S43).

Calibration and Validation

Calibration and validation were also conducted in line with guidelines to confirm that projected model outcomes aligned with historical Swiss data and to reduce analytic uncertainty [44–47]. Key external validation targets for Switzerland included age, sex and KRT at baseline, as well as annual AKI, CV, and mortality events. They are summarised in Supplemental Table S44.

Clinical Impact

Between 2022 and 2032, the number of patients with CKD was projected to increase by 6.7% from 961 thousand [k] to 1.03 million [m] (Fig. 2). A large contributor to this increase was a rise in late-stage CKD (i.e. stages 3 to 5, according to KDIGO classification [1]) from 437k to 516k (Fig. 2), representing a change of 18.1%, while early-stage CKD (i.e. stages 1 to 2) were forecast to decrease by 2.8% (525k to 510k; Fig. 2). Increases in late-stage CKD were also associated with a projected 57.2% increase in patients undergoing KRT, from 8366 in 2022 to 13,153 in 2032 (Fig. 2). This was due to a rise in both dialysis by 51.4% (3896 to 5898) and transplants by 62.3% (from 4469 to 7255; Fig. 2). This represents an overall shift towards a majority of later, more advanced disease stages, from 44.5% of all cases in 2022 to 50.3% in 2032.

Projected change in total CKD, late-stage CKD, and KRT from 2022 to 2032. CKD chronic kidney disease, k thousand, m million, KRT kidney replacement therapy

A 49.7% growth in CKD-related annual mortality was projected by 2032 (from 26.4k per year to 39.6k per year; Fig. 3). Averaged over the 10-year time horizon, patients with CKD were estimated to experience an annual mortality rate of 3.4%, compared with 0.6% for the non-CKD population. CV events associated with CKD were projected to increase by 69.5%, comprised of a 149.2% increase in HHFs (5765 to 14,369; Fig. 3) alongside a 46.9% increase in cases of stroke (3121 to 4584; Fig. 3) and 5.0% for MI (6021 to 6321; Fig. 3); the last of these percentages reflects the relatively stable rates of MI across CKD stages compared to rates of HF. Cases of AKI were forecast to increase by 44.9% (50,742 to 73,512; Fig. 3).

HCRU

By 2032, population growth in Switzerland was projected to result in approximately 3.7m emergency room (ER) visits, 2.9m hospitalisations and 47.1m outpatient visits per year. Approximately 4.1% of ER visits were attributed to patients with diagnosed CKD, increasing from 126k visits in 2022 to 155k in 2032 (22.8% increase). Diagnosed patients with CKD accounted for 10.3% of projected hospitalisations, rising from 238k in 2022 to 298k in 2032 (25.2% increase). Accounting for 2.8% of all outpatient visits, projected outpatient visits were forecast to grow by 25.0% in patients with diagnosed CKD, from 1.1m in 2022 to 1.3m in 2032. This overall rise in HCRU was driven by the shift towards late-stage CKD.

Economic Impact

Growing clinical burden and its associated HCRU demonstrates significant (macro-)economic impact. Annual healthcare expenditures related to CKD and KRT were projected to rise by 27.6% (CHF 3.3 billion [b] to CHF 4.2b; Fig. 4). Costs related to patients with CKD not undergoing KRT were projected to increase by 20.3% (CHF 2.9b to CHF 3.5b; Fig. 4) while KRT-related costs were projected to increase by 77.3% (CHF 424.2m to CHF 752.0m; Fig. 4). When stratified by KRT modality, costs were expected to rise by 51.5% for dialysis (CHF 285.2m to CHF 432.0m; Fig. 4) and more than double for kidney transplantation (CHF 139.0m to CHF 320.0m; Fig. 4). As a result, CKD was projected to account for 4.6% of the Swiss healthcare budget, with the share of KRT increasing from 13% to 18% of total CKD-related costs [48].

Societal Impact

Over the study’s 10-year time horizon, CKD was projected to result in 43.2m missed workdays for patients and caregivers due to absenteeism and presenteeism (breakdown provided in Table 1), equating to 212k lost full-time equivalent workers. These missed workdays were estimated to result in CHF 36.0b in lost GDP, CHF 12.9b in lost productivity and CHF 606m in lost tax revenue.

Environmental Impact

Simulated CKD patient growth between 2022 and 2032 contributed to projections of exacerbated environmental outcomes across three domains. GHG emissions were projected to rise by 12.9% (from 229.7m carbon dioxide equivalents [CO2eq] to 259.4m CO2eq; Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 43). Freshwater consumption was forecast to rise by 17.3% (3.9m cubic metres [m3] to 4.6m m3; Fig. 5). Fossil fuel depletion was projected to increase by 14.0% (101.1m kilograms [kg] to 115.2m kg; Fig. 5). The anticipated shift towards KRT is a driver of these environmental forecasts. KRT-related environmental burden was expected to increase by 56.6% from 12.7m CO2eq to 19.9m CO2eq for GHG emission (Fig. 5). Freshwater consumption attributable to KRT was forecast to grow by 55.7% from 405k m3 to 631k m3 (Fig. 5). Fossil fuel depletion linked to KRT was projected to increase by 56.6% from 6.2m kg to 9.8m kg (Fig. 5).

Discussion

IMPACT-CKD is the first model to holistically evaluate the multifaceted burdens of CKD, leveraging a disease framework that reflects its care pathway in Switzerland [26]. This analysis quantified the impact of CKD in 2022 and projected it to 2032, providing insights into the consequences of disease progression and its negative impact on clinical, HCRU, economic, societal and environmental outcomes. The results presented herein highlight the need for evidence-based, system-level policies tailored to the needs of patients, caregivers and Swiss healthcare to mitigate CKD’s projected impact.

Between 2022 and 2032, Switzerland is projected to see a 6.7% rise in CKD prevalence, with the majority (50.3%) expected to be in advanced stages. Projections indicate a 57.2% rise in demand for KRT, affecting both dialysis (51.4% increase) and transplantation (62.3% increase). In parallel, annual mortality is projected to jump substantially (49.7%), with the CKD population experiencing nearly six-fold higher mortality compared with the non-CKD population. Increased clinical burden will also drive projected increases in HCRU, including ER visits (22.8% increase) and outpatient appointments (25.0% increase). Exacerbations of CKD-associated CV events are reflected in the projected hospitalisations: CKD-related hospital admissions are expected to increase by 25.2% to 298k overall, while HHFs among patients with CKD are expected to triple to 14,369 in 2032. Switzerland’s CKD-related annual healthcare costs are also projected to rise 27.6%, from CHF 3.3b to CHF 4.2b, driven largely by a 77.3% increase in KRT (CHF 424.2m to CHF 752.0m). By 2032, CKD will absorb 4.6% of Switzerland’s healthcare budget, reflecting a substantial and growing allocation of national health expenditure. Highlighting the anticipated burden increase related to ESKD, kidney transplantation costs are expected to more than double (130.3% increase from CHF 139.0m to CHF 320.0m), reflecting the projected 62.3% rise in transplants. The societal burden will be marked by 43.2m missed workdays, equating to 212k lost full-time equivalent workers and CHF 606m in lost tax revenue.

Environmental

Healthcare already has a substantial environmental footprint, though Switzerland has committed to achieving net-zero, climate-resilient healthcare by 2050 or earlier [24]. However, projected KRT-related increases in GHG emissions (56.6%), freshwater consumption (55.7%) and fossil fuel depletion (56.6%), driven by the growing number of patients requiring dialysis, may challenge this ambition. The magnitude of these environmental increases merits emphasis. The projected rise in GHG emissions is equivalent to adding approximately 629 transatlantic flights between Zurich and New York annually [49], while the anticipated growth in freshwater consumption would fill roughly 280 Olympic-sized swimming pools [50]. The increase in fossil fuel depletion alone is comparable to powering the entire city of Zurich for 3 weeks [51]. These projections indicate the importance of integrating sustainability into kidney care strategies, minimising environmental impact while optimising patient care delivery as Switzerland moves toward its net-zero healthcare goals.

Comparison to Other Countries

Validating the broader implications of these findings, the results from this analysis for Switzerland align with trends projected across other European countries, including Germany, Spain, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom (UK) [28]. Specifically, Switzerland’s projected increase in CKD prevalence is most comparable to the UK (7.7%) and generally consistent with the Netherlands and Spain. In contrast, patient numbers in Germany are projected to decline slightly over the 10-year time horizon, likely driven by forecasts of decreasing population age and size [28]. Across all five countries, projections indicate a concerning shift toward later-stage CKD, driving an unprecedented rise in KRT demand [28]. Among these countries, Switzerland is forecast to see the most significant increase in patients living post-transplant (a 62.3% increase to 7255 patients), ahead of the UK (58.7%) and the Netherlands (50.2%) [28]. Switzerland’s projections of future dialysis (51.4%) also exceed those of the Netherlands, where higher rates of living donor transplantation and kidney exchange programmes may reduce this need [28, 52].

Environmental projections mirror these clinical trends, where dialysis, specifically in-centre haemodialysis, is expected to drive substantial increases in healthcare‑related GHG emissions over the coming decade due to its high electricity demand, water use, and single‑use consumables [21, 22]. In Switzerland, GHG emissions attributable to the KRT population are projected to rise by 56.6%, a greater increase than in the Netherlands (17.1%), but lower than the UK (75.1%) [28].

Economically, all countries are projected to face substantial increases in CKD- and KRT-related costs [28]. However, Switzerland’s absolute healthcare costs are comparatively high, considering its population size, which may be attributed to factors such as health workforce size, high salaries, healthcare service availability, and quality of care delivery [9, 20, 28, 53, 54]. Costs of CKD, excluding KRT (CHF 2.9b) already surpass those reported in the Netherlands (€683m) and the UK (£1.89b), despite their populations being two and eight times larger than Switzerland’s, respectively [28]. KRT, which currently affects approximately 1% of Switzerland’s CKD population [5, 15, 16], is projected to undergo a 77.3% increase in costs, accounting for 17.8% of total CKD-related healthcare expenditures by 2032, up from 12.8% in 2022.

Due to differences in population characteristics, dynamics, and healthcare systems, there was expected variation in CKD’s trends across countries. This microsimulation model incorporates numerous interacting inputs to influence epidemiological changes, disease development, progression and KRT patterns. Consequently, results are not easily attributed to a few causative factors. Notwithstanding, several critical inputs significantly influence model outcomes: these include age distribution, eGFR and UACR distribution (key determinants of CKD stage), eGFR decline, population growth, mortality rates, comorbidity prevalence, as well as dialysis and kidney transplantation rates [28]. In Switzerland, CKD’s prevalent count is projected to increase, driven by an ageing population and rising rates of diabetes and hypertension [5–8]. The shift towards the later-stage CKD may reflect gaps in early detection and limited uptake of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) [55] and underutilisation of standardised chronic disease management programmes [56, 57]. The projected decrease in early-stage CKD is likely influenced by demographic changes, including progression of current early-stage patients to later stages in line with population ageing, combined with immigration of predominantly young, healthy individuals and the 10-year time horizon of the model, during which many individuals may not yet reach the age at which early-stage CKD typically manifests.

Additionally, previous sensitivity analyses for IMPACT-CKD varied three other key inputs including changes in population growth (birth and immigration), mortality and eGFR decline in the general population, with resulting changes to the model’s projections aligning with expectations (i.e. consistent trends with differing magnitudes) across all countries [28]. These findings are expected to be similar in the present analysis for Switzerland.

Limitations

The limitations of the IMPACT-CKD model have been detailed previously and are inherent to complex microsimulation models [27, 28]. These include the need to make assumptions and the requirement for substantial amounts of population-level data, often sourced from multiple studies that may differ in population characteristics, quality, and methodology [27]. Consequently, the projections presented here reflect current knowledge of the disease pathway and available data and should not be interpreted as definitive. To address gaps in Swiss-specific data, proxies were applied from Germany and other countries with similar patient demographics and healthcare systems. While this is a methodologically accepted practice, it may introduce some uncertainty into the modelled outcomes as proxy data may not be fully reflective of Switzerland’s population demographics and clinical pathway. Compared to other countries included in the IMPACT-CKD study, such as UK and Germany, the Swiss model relied more heavily on proxy data, particularly for some epidemiological and cost parameters. This reflects current limitations in data infrastructure in Switzerland—both general and CKD-specific—and underscores the opportunity to strengthen national surveillance and data collection efforts. Further, considering the model structure reflects the best available understanding of CKD, any gaps in current knowledge may also increase uncertainty. Lastly, although the objective of this work was to estimate the future burden of CKD under a status quo scenario—meaning without increases in screening, diagnosis, or treatment—greater uptake of CKD interventions driven by policy changes and nephroprotective GDMT (including new drug classes such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 [SGLT2] inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide 1 [GLP-1] agonists and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [MRAs]) is plausible. However, evaluating the impact of such treatment improvements on disease burden was outside the scope of this analysis. Accordingly, the projections presented here reflect current patterns of diagnosis and treatment and may overestimate the ultimate burden that will materialise if care practices improve over time.

Solutions to Mitigate Burden

New drug classes such as GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors and MRAs, which are now integral to GDMT, have demonstrated potential to protect the kidneys and mitigate kidney function decline, potentially changing the course of events for patients with CKD [1]. Australian data estimates optimal use of SGLT2 inhibitors (defined as 75% uptake) to prevent progression to ESKD in more than a thousand patients per year [58]. At system-level, optimised GDMT implementation demonstrates system-level impact for HCRU in Switzerland, reducing PAHs by up to 31% in heart failure and 14% in COPD respectively [4]. In Spain, CKD-related healthcare expenditure is largely driven by hospital admissions (77.1%) rather than drug spend (6.6%) [59]. The drug spend estimate for CKD is also comparable for Switzerland (8.1%) [9]. Greater implementation of GDMT may thus present a viable solution to mitigating CKD’s multidimensional burdens. Although SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended in most chronic nephropathies, they remain largely underprescribed, with uptake ranging from 4.1% to 8% in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and CKD [55]. Further, though Swiss physicians’ general attitudes towards clinical practice guidelines are positive [60], research indicates that national guidelines lack standardisation and are underutilised, with 30% to 40% of patients not receiving treatments of proven efficacy [56, 57]. Substantial kidney function impairment in patients with T2D is associated with markedly increased HCRU, including primary care interactions and inpatient stays, highlighting the urgent need for prevention and early nephroprotective intervention in this population [61]. Thus, innovations in structured chronic care management, such as disease management programmes in CKD and its comorbidities, may provide viable solutions to maximise GDMT implementation. Such programmes often also include processes managing early referral to nephrology, which is associated with significant mortality benefit [62]. Structured comorbidity management also has positive impact on HCRU and healthcare expenditures, reducing hospital admissions and ED visits by up to 40% and 30% respectively. Correspondingly, per person per month costs can be reduced by up to 17% [63]. Local Swiss GDMT-adoption rates for CKD were not available for this study. Thus, future research should investigate the anticipated system-level impact of increased uptake of GDMT and the provision of structured disease management programmes for CKD and its comorbidities in Switzerland.

Overall, the IMPACT-CKD model projects a shift toward late-stage CKD in Switzerland, driving increased demand for KRT, alongside substantial clinical, HCRU, financial, societal and environmental implications. Crucially, these forecasts reveal an impending public health challenge for Swiss healthcare, necessitating urgent policy interventions to improve patient well-being and societal outcomes. This aligns with the recent EU Kidney Health Alliance Manifesto, which advocates for integrated, patient-centred strategies across Europe to enhance early detection, treatment, and sustainable kidney care [64, 65]. Emerging evidence indicates policies favouring early diagnostics via screening and uptake of GDMT to result in significant burden reductions [66]. In particular, annual screening in high-risk populations demonstrates significant cost-effectiveness due to prevention of progression to dialysis, reducing CV events and incidence of ESKD by up to 40% [62]. In the UK, population-level interventions to improve CKD management and avoid disease progression, such as early diagnosis and increased SGLT2 inhibitors usage, have demonstrated high cost-effectiveness [67]. In Switzerland, data from 2012 suggests UACR screening to be cost-effective at regular intervals in patients with T2D and hypertension or at risk of CKD and 10-year intervals in the general population above 50 [68]. However, this may require reassessment considering likely higher current cost-effectiveness due to population ageing, disease burden increases and more effective therapies. Finally, the model was constructed to allow for flexible adaptation in case of input data availability. Thus, adaptation of the model to other regions with differing demographics and healthcare systems is of interest to further provide valuable insights for global health policy.

Conclusion

The IMPACT-CKD model provides a comprehensive evaluation of the clinical, HCRU, economic, societal, and environmental impacts of CKD in Switzerland. This analysis projected the holistic burden of CKD from 2022 to 2032, highlighting an intensification of disease impact across domains faced by Swiss society, healthcare institutions and its population. These findings demonstrate the need for coordinated policies to combat the significant consequences of CKD progression on patient well-being, resource utilisation, and the environment to uphold Switzerland’s 2050 net-zero healthcare targets. Future research should investigate the impact of timely diagnosis through screening for CKD among high-risk populations and increased uptake of guideline-directed renoprotective therapies to mitigate CKD burden.
