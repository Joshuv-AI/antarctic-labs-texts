Trimbody with rigid AI-designed scaffolds enables atomic-resolution cryo-EM structure determination of small proteins
Authors: Jinyang Song, Lei Qi, Yongyue Li, Xue Zhang, Yushu He, Zhengshi Zhang, Dongfang He, Mengjun Gu, Yuyao Guan, Hao Fang, Xuben Hou, Zengpeng Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) single-particle analysis faces significant challenges in resolving the structures of small proteins due to low signal-to-noise ratios and insufficient structural features. Here, we present Trimbody, a simple yet robust method that leverages rigid AI-designed scaffolds to overcome these limitations. Trimbody comprises two components: a trimeric scaffold (H3-PrAC-5350A) and a reformatted nanobody fusion (Nb-TAIL). The de novo 3-helix bundle (H3) and TAIL domains, engineered via RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN, stabilize the interactions between Nbs and the scaffold and enhance the overall rigidity of the system, thereby enabling high-resolution cryo-EM analysis. Using Trimbody, we resolve the atomic structures of four sub-50 kDa test proteins: human Gal10 (2.62 Å), Aequorea coerulescens GFP (2.29 Å), the IgV domain of human Nectin4 (2.43 Å), and membrane protein Escherichia coli LacY (2.50 Å), demonstrating the method’s versatility. Trimbody’s simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and compatibility with standard cryo-EM workflows position it as a universal tool for structural studies of small proteins, further advancing nanobody-based drug development and life science research.

Introduction

With advanced direct electron detectors and image-processing algorithms, single-particle analysis (SPA) of cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as the predominant technique for elucidating the structures of biological macromolecules. Theoretically, the minimum molecular weight (MW) threshold for cryo-EM is approximately 38 kDa1. However, in practice, obtaining high-resolution structures of small proteins (<100 kDa) using SPA remains challenging. In a limited number of instances, the specialized approaches or equipment have facilitated the high-resolution reconstruction of small proteins2. For example, the streptavidin tetramer (52 kDa) was determined at a resolution of 3.2 Å with the aid of a Volta phase plate, making it the smallest protein determined by cryo-EM to date3. Two primary factors hinder SPA in the structural determination of small proteins, particularly those important therapeutic targets below 50 kDa: the lower signal-to-noise ratio for smaller particles in micrographs4,5 and the lack of distinctive structural features that confound three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction algorithms6.

To make cryo-EM, such a powerful tool, applicable to small proteins, a general strategy involves increasing the MW of the protein of interest (POI) to facilitate the visualization of larger particles in micrographs7. Recently, several methodological studies have been conducted to develop a size-increase system, typically comprising two components: (i) a binder specific to the POI and (ii) structurally well-defined scaffold protein(s), either fused to or complexed with the binder. The selection of binder has predominantly concentrated on antibody-like proteins, such as Design Ankyrin Repeat Proteins (DARPins), antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) and nanobodies (Nbs)7. High-affinity binders for various POIs can be efficiently obtained through in vitro library-screening or animal-immunizing technologies. Aside from the region that binds to the epitope of the target protein, the conserved framework of the binder has been engineered to be fused or bind to scaffold protein(s). Consequently, this combination of modular binder and unchangeable scaffold(s) aims to establish a universally applicable method. The key challenge in obtaining a high-resolution structure of POI lies in ensuring the rigidity of the entire system, as even slight flexibility can lead to smeared densities in corresponding regions during 3D reconstruction.

DARPins are engineered antibody mimetic proteins comprising four or five ankyrin repeat motifs. Synthetic DARPin libraries can encompass over 1012 variants with randomized interaction residues8. The Yeates Group developed a two-subunit tetrahedron cage as a scaffold, with an anti-GFP DARPin fused to one subunit. The earlier version of the DARPin-containing cage, T33-21, determined the structure of the cargo protein (GFP) at a local resolution of 3.8 Å but failed to provide a reliable atomic interpretation due to the flexibility of the single-helix linker between DARPin and scaffold9,10. The later version, adopting the cage T33-51 and a sequence-optimized linker, established a more rigid DARPin-fusing scaffold. It successfully resolved cryo-EM structures of GFP (3.1 Å) and three oncogenic KRAS mutants bound to GDP or the inhibitor AMG510 (2.9 ~ 3.2 Å)11. Since DARPins are niche and antibodies are more readily available commercially, most MW-increasing methodologies have been developed based on Fabs or Nbs. In 2012, the Fab fragment ( ~ 50 kDa) was introduced as a binder to resolve cryo-EM structures of small membrane proteins, leveraging its characteristic elbow construction as a fiducial marker12. Although a few successful precedents have been reported13–15, this strategy has not been widely adopted because the hinges between the variable and constant immunoglobulin (Ig) domains in Fab are generally flexible, complicating the identification of a stable and rigid Fab. Moreover, the expression and purification of certain Fab is not trivial, and the medium MW of a single Fab may still be inadequate for determining the structures of very small proteins. In contrast, the Nb, derived from camelids or sharks, is a rigid, single-domain mini-antibody (12 ~ 15 kDa) that utilizes three complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) to effectively recognize smaller epitopes, as opposed to the relatively flexible heterodimeric Fab with six CDRs. Nbs can be quickly obtained via in vitro library-screenings and are readily purified using the prokaryotic recombinant expression system, with the advantages of a short production cycle and cost-effectiveness. Recently, three approaches utilizing Nb as a POI-specific binder have been developed. The Locher Group has screened a NabFab, which efficiently binds to Nb’s conserved distal loops (DL; Fig. 1a) opposite the CDRs. Additionally, another specific Nb can bind to the hinge of the NabFab’s light chain, thereby stabilizing the conformation of the scaffold complex16. Using the NabFab method, cryo-EM structures of the bacterial transporters VcNorm (50 kDa) and ScaDMT (50 kDa) have been resolved at overall resolutions of 3.5 Å and 3.8 Å, respectively. The Steyaert Group has developed the Megabody method17, wherein the scaffold protein HopQ (45 kDa) or YgjK (86 kDa) is fused to the DL of an Nb by extending two conserved β-strands. This approach has been employed to determine the structures of hedgehog acetyltransferase (HHAT, 62 kDa) at 2.7-Å resolution18 and the Na+-taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP, 38 kDa) at 3.3/3.7-Å resolution19. The Rapoport Group introduced the approach of Legobody, consisting of a heterodimeric Fab8D3, an Nb, and a fused MBP-PrA/G protein20. These components form interfaces between each other to assemble a stable quaternary complex. Utilizing this system, the structural determinations of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain (RBD, 22 kDa) at 3.6-Å resolution and the small membrane KDEL receptor (23 kDa) at 3.2-Å resolution have been achieved. The aforementioned methods employing Nb as a binder in combination with the designed MW-increasing scaffold indeed provide available toolkits for resolving cryo-EM structures of small proteins. Nonetheless, certain limitations have been identified based on our experience. In the NabFab method, the sequence of Nb’s non-CDR region is strictly confined to the TC-Nb4 framework, which is effectively recognized by NabFab. However, for some Nbs derived from in vitro library screenings, the reconstructed chimeric Nbs could not bind the POI. For Megabody, the Nb, which only depends on two β-strands fused to the scaffold, still exhibits a degree of flexibility, thereby impeding the acquisition of high-resolution structures of POI/Nb complex. Here, we present a straightforward yet robust method, termed Trimbody, which facilitates cryo-EM analysis of small proteins at high resolution (within 3 Å) in a time-saving and cost-effective manner. The Trimbody system comprises only two components: a fusion protein of Nb-TAIL as a binder and a fused H3-PrAC-5350A trimer as a scaffold. These fusion proteins can be readily purified following recombinant expression in E. coli. The de novo TAIL and H3 domains, designed using RFdiffusion21 and ProteinMPNN22, are crucial for forming a highly stable and rigid Trimbody complex. In this work, we demonstrate the application of Trimbody (including the initial version) by determining the cryo-EM structures of four test proteins: the 13 kDa human Galectin10 (hGal10) at 2.62-Å resolution, the 26 kDa Aequorea coerulescens GFP (AcGFP) at 2.29-Å resolution, the 13 kDa IgV domain of human Nectin4 (IgV_hNectin4) at 2.43-Å resolution, and the 46 kDa membrane protein Escherichia coli Lactose permease (EcLacY) at 2.50-Å resolution. To our knowledge, the Trimbody system represents the most simplified universal binder/scaffold complex method developed so far, which can significantly advance the application of cryo-EM SPA in the structural analysis of full-sized proteins.

Results

Initial design of Trimbody (iTrimbody version)

The PrAC/Nb subcomplex, derived from the Legobody20, provides a suitable starting point for developing methodologies. The 3-helix bundle PrAC from Staphylococcus aureus binds to the conserved mainbody region (MR) of Nbs without disturbing CDRs’ interaction with the POI (Fig. 1a)20,23,24. Similar to Legobody, PrAC can be fused to a structurally rigid scaffold with an α-helix at either the N- or C-terminus. A critical design consideration is to ensure that the space surrounding PrAC remains as open as possible to accommodate the Nb/POI complex (Fig. 1a). In this study, the chosen scaffold was the 5350A subunit from the computationally designed icosahedral complex, I53-5025, which has been widely applied in drug delivery research and vaccine development25–27. Compared to its prototype, 2-keto-3-deoxy-6-phosphogluconate aldolase from Thermotoga maritima28, the PrAC-fused 5350A offers the advantage of potential self-assembly with the 5350B into high-symmetry particles, akin to the tetrahedral cage in the DARPins-based methods9–11. Structurally, 5350A adopts a classic α/β barrel fold and assembles into a rigid and stable trimer (Fig.1b). The α-helix (α0 or α8) at the N- or C-termini is firmly lodged on the α/β barrel, which facilitates the design of a PrAC-fused scaffold (Fig. 1b).

Using manual modeling to merge α-helices followed by structural prediction via AlphaFold29, we designed five candidate scaffold constructs: three PrAC-5350A fusions (labeled N1–N3) and two 5350A-PrAC fusions (labeled C1–C2) (Supplementary Fig. 1). These fusion scaffolds were readily purified after recombinant expression in E. coli. We then used two Nbs (anti-AcGFP and anti-hGal10) to test whether the POI, Nb and scaffold could assemble into a stable complex in vitro. AcGFP was chosen as a representative monomeric test protein30, rather than eGFP, which exists in a monomer-dimer equilibrium31. The Nb against AcGFP originates from an anti-eGFP Nb (PDB ID 3OGO)32, in which all interacting residues with eGFP are identical to those with AcGFP (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Experimentally, this Nb was confirmed to bind AcGFP (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) analyses showed that three scaffold constructs (N1, N2 and C2) could form relatively stable complexes with AcGFP and NbAcGFP. hGal10 is a classic dimer that recognizes cell surface glycans, and its dysfunction has been implicated in inflammation and certain myeloid leukemias33. The anti-hGal10 Nbs were obtained via phage display and ELISA screening in our laboratory. Among the scaffold constructs, only the N1 PrAC-5350A fusion formed a stable complex with hGal10 and NbhGal10 in vitro. Since only the N1 construct was suitable for complex-assembly of either AcGFP or hGal10 (Supplementary Figs. 2c, 3a), the purified ternary complexes were further loaded onto a Tundra cryo-TEM (100 kV) for preliminary analysis. Two-dimensional (2D) class averages of the AcGFP dataset revealed clover-like particles consistent with the trimeric nature of 5350A (Supplementary Fig. 2d). In contrast, 2D class averages of the hGal10 dataset predominantly showed long hollow rod-like particles, suggesting the presence of two 5350A-containing trimers (Fig. 2a). These observations indicate that the 5350A trimer can serve as a distinguishable fiducial marker for particle localization and alignment during SPA. However, 3D classification and refinement of the AcGFP dataset revealed only densities corresponding to the 5350A trimer, with no detectable features attributable to PrAC, Nb, and AcGFP (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Upon collecting a larger dataset using a Krios cryo-TEM (300 kV), we successfully resolved a 3D density map at an overall 2.62-Å resolution following routine SPA processing (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3b). No particle orientation bias was observed, enabling high-quality map reconstruction, except for a slight decrease in resolution at the periphery (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). After straightforward de novo model building and refinement, the final model revealed a characteristic hexameric conformation, in which two PrAC-5350A trimers, mediated by six Nbs, sandwich three hGal10 dimers (Fig. 2c). Most amino acid side chains of PrAC-5350A, Nb and hGal10 are clearly visible, particularly the key interacting residues at the interfaces of PrAC/Nb and Nb/hGal10 (Fig. 2d). A detailed structural analysis of the Nb/hGal10 interaction will be presented elsewhere.

A natural question emerges: why was the scaffold PrAC-5350A effective in resolving the hGal10 structure at such high resolution but not that for AcGFP? We speculated that hGal10 dimers bridge the two Nb/PrAC-5350A trimers, locking them into a rigid conformation. In contrast, the monomeric AcGFP lacks the bridging function. The single helical connection between PrAC and 5350A likely fails to provide sufficient rigidity, further preventing high-resolution reconstruction of PrAC/Nb/AcGFP subcomplex, similar to the earlier version of DARPin-containing cage9. Superimposing of PrAC-5350A trimer from the experimental hGal10 dataset with the AlphaFold-predicted N1 model revealed that PrAC rotated ~10o upon hGal10 binding (Supplementary Fig. 4f). The structural alignment results indirectly confirmed the flexibility of the single α-helix linker. Therefore, the initial design of Trimbody (referred to as iTrimbody hereafter), composed of Nb and PrAC-5350A, requires further optimization. While iTrimbody enabled high-resolution cryo-EM structure determination for specific targets like hGal10, it may not be broadly applicable without additional modifications.

Optimized design of Trimbody

Maintaining the rigidity of the entire binder/scaffold system is the most challenging aspect of developing size-augmentation approaches. To address the high flexibility issue of the projecting PrAC in iTrimbody, we aimed to design an additional domain on the side opposite the Nb-binding site of PrAC to stabilize it in a rigid conformation. The optimal anchoring position was identified within the cavity formed by the PrAC-5350A trimers (Fig. 3a). To this end, we employed RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN to perform the de novo design of an α-helical bundle domain, which could be fused to the N-terminus of PrAC. In addition to interacting with PrAC, this designed domain was configured as a trimer to comply with the trimerization property of 5350A (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Based on an integrated assessment of larger interface size, minor steric hindrance, and highest pLDDT value of predicted structure, we selected the optimal design of a 3-helix bundle (termed the H3 domain) and synthesized the fusion protein H3-PrAC-5350A. SEC analysis showed that the purified H3-PrAC-5350A retained the ability to bind NbAcGFP (Supplementary Fig. 6a). However, quantitative analysis of the band intensities from the purified H3-PrAC-5350A/Nb complex yielded a molar ratio of 7:1, in contrast to the theoretical expectation of 1:1. Similar result was observed during the purification of the PrAC-5350A/Nb/AcGFP complex (Supplementary Fig. 2c). It was hypothesized that the Nb was prone to dissociating from PrAC, regardless of the presence of the H3-5350A domains. To investigate this hypothesis, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis to examine the binding kinetics. Although the binding affinity was sufficient to form the Nb/scaffold complex (KD = 3.857 × 10⁻6 M), the kinetic curve revealed rapid dissociation of NbAcGFP from H3-PrAC-5350A (kd = 1.434 × 10−3 s−1) (Fig. 3c). To address this instability, we initially attempted to optimize the PrAC/Nb interface. Our strategy involved preserving the key interacting residues at the interface while mutating seven surrounding residues to enhance Nb/PrAC stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 6b). However, these mutations failed to significantly reduce Nb dissociation from PrAC after SEC analysis. Moreover, certain mutations on Nb’s framework (such as S85V) severely compromised protein expression levels. We then proposed an alternative strategy: fusing an additional domain to the C-terminus of the Nb to interact with the 5350A scaffold, thereby enhancing the system stability via two interfaces. The interface between 5350A and 5350B in the I53-50 icosahedral particle25 provides the ideal hotspots for such design (Fig. 3b). Using RFdiffusion in combination with ProteinMPNN for sequence optimization, we designed a TAIL domain to be fused to the Nb’s C-terminus (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 5b). The optimal design was selected for further SPR analysis, which showed that the Nb-TAIL fusion protein exhibited markedly improved binding affinity to H3-PrAC-5350A (KD = 1.435 × 10−10 M). More importantly, the kinetic curve demonstrated reduced dissociation (kd = 9.729 × 10−5 s−1), confirming that the TAIL domain can significantly stabilize the Nb/scaffold complex (Fig. 3c). Additionally, the molar ratio of the purified AcGFP/Nb-TAIL/H3-PrAC-5350A ternary complex by SEC was validated to be approximately 1:1:1 (Fig. 4a). Trimbody used for resolving cryo-EM structure of small soluble proteins

The purified complex of AcGFP, Nb-TAIL, and H3-PrAC-5350A was further analyzed by cryo-EM (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 7). The characteristic clover-shaped morphology and suitable particle size facilitated efficient data processing, including particle picking, 2D classification and 3D reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 7). Representative particles from 2D class averages clearly revealed the clover-shaped 5350A trimer bound by three copies of the POI (Fig. 4b). By applying C3 symmetry, the final reconstructed density map was determined at an overall resolution of 2.29 Å (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 8a–e). Notably, the densities of H3-PrAC-5350A in complex with Nb-TAIL were well-resolved to better than 3 Å, allowing for accurate atomic model building (Fig. 4d). As anticipated, the de novo designed H3 domain also formed a trimer, with its threefold symmetry axis coinciding with that of the 5350A trimer (Fig. 4e). H3 predominantly uses αx and αz helices for internal trimerization, while αy and αz helices mediate strong interaction with αa and αb of PrAC (Fig. 4e). These interfaces are stabilized primarily by hydrophobic interactions among helices, whereas the solvent-exposed regions are enriched with hydrogen bond networks formed by polar residues (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Although H3 does not contact 5350A directly (Fig. 4e), the trimeric interactions within H3 and its interface with PrAC are sufficient to rigidify the projecting PrAC conformation. The TAIL domain fused to C-terminus of the Nb adopted a fully α-helical folding pattern, in which α5’ and α7’ form a hydrophobic interface with 5350A (Fig. 4f), mimicking the binding mode of the native 5350B subunit in I53-50 (Supplementary Fig. 9b). A four-helix bundle linker (α1’-α4’) connects the Nb and the 5350A-binding motif, interacting with both but not directly contacting 5350A. Notably, α1’-α2’ of the linker interacts with the Nb framework (Fig. 4f). We speculated that this structurally rigid linkage, featuring a rigid four-helix bundle rather than a flexible loop, is critical for stabilizing the Nb-TAIL/scaffold interaction, contributing to the four-order-of-magnitude improvement in binding affinity (Fig. 3c).

Although the density maps and atomic models of Trimbody were well-defined, the AcGFP component was manifested by discontinuous density and lower local resolution at the periphery (Fig. 4c), probably due to the intrinsic flexibility of the POI or slight variations in the attached Nb orientation. To address this issue, focused refinement was performed on the Nb/AcGFP region. The resolution of the Nb/AcGFP subcomplex had been substantially improved to 2.80 Å (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). The densities for most side chains were well-resolved, and the fluorescent chromophore along with its surrounding reactive residues was visualized at the level of atomic detail (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Furthermore, the side chains of key residues at the AcGFP/Nb interface were well fitted into the electron density (Fig. 4g). Superposition with the crystal structure of the eGFP/Nb complex yielded a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.451 Å (Supplementary Fig. 9d), indicating that the Trimbody-assisted cryo-EM structure of the POI is virtually indistinguishable from that obtained by X-ray crystallography.

To further demonstrate the general applicability of the Trimbody method, we investigated previously unsolved Nb/POI complexes. As a representative case, we selected the extracellular IgV domain of hNectin4 (~13 kDa), a cell adhesion molecule overexpressed in various cancers and serving as a biomarker linked to poor prognosis and multiple tumor-promoting processes34. This protein has been validated as a therapeutic target of enfortumab vedotin (EV), an FDA-approved Nectin4-targeting antibody-drug conjugate34,35. We utilized phage display to screen a naive nanobody library against the extracellular domain of hNectin4 (residues 32–351; containing one IgV and two IgC2 domains). After three selection rounds, eleven positive clones were obtained. SEC analysis identified two Nbs capable of forming stable complexes with the IgV domain of hNectin4 (IgV_hNectin4). Two Nb-TAIL fusion proteins were constructed, and SEC analysis was also performed on a mixture of H3-PrAC-5350A, Nb-TAIL, and IgV_hNectin4, where the latter two components were present in excess. This step was essential, as certain Nbs binding to specific epitopes of the POI could induce steric hindrance, potentially obstructing scaffold binding and impairing stable complex formation. Only one Nb was confirmed to assemble into a stable ternary complex, as indicated by SEC peak profiles (Supplementary Fig. 10a), and was subsequently subjected to cryo-EM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10b). The cryo-EM dataset was processed similarly to that of AcGFP. The final refined 3D map was determined at an overall resolution of 2.43 Å, with the focused portion of the Nb/IgV_hNectin4 subcomplex further refined at a resolution of 2.96 Å (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 11a-g). Compared to the compact β-barrel of AcGFP, the IgV_hNectin4 structure contained more flexible loops, resulting in relatively lower resolution, consistent with higher RMSD values (1.085 and 1.016 Å) upon comparison with the crystal structures (PDB IDs 4JJH and 4FRW). Importantly, nanobody binding yielded an improved density for otherwise unresolved flexible regions, such as a β-strand (residues 2–6) that was absent in both reference crystal structures (Supplementary Fig. 11h). AcGFP primarily interacts with the CDR3 of the Nb and is positioned on the MR side of the Nb (Fig. 4d). In contrast, IgV_hNectin4 engages all three CDRs of the Nb, with the POI located predominantly above the Nb (Fig. 5b). AcGFP and IgV_hNectin4 exemplify the two most common modes of Nb/POI interactions, both of which can be amenable to high-resolution structural determination using the Trimbody system (Figs. 4g, 5c). Application of Trimbody in determining cryo-EM structures of small membrane proteins

The structural determination of small membrane proteins poses significantly greater challenges compared to soluble proteins. Particularly, the POI without classic extramembrane domains is surrounded by a micelle or lipid bilayer environment that generally lacks inherent high-contrast features, complicating particle alignment in cryo-EM analysis. This limitation highlights the potential utility of fiducial markers. Here, we selected E. coli Lactose permease (EcLacY) to test whether Trimbody applies to membrane proteins. LacY functions as an H⁺-galactoside symporter, catalyzing the coupled translocation of a proton and a galactoside across the membrane36. Structurally, the protein comprises 12 transmembrane helices organized into two pseudo-symmetrical bundles, each containing six helices. The residues critical for substrate recognition are predominantly located within the N-terminal bundle, whereas those essential for H⁺ translocation are primarily found in the C-terminal bundle. Two bundles form a large hydrophilic cavity that is open normally to the cytoplasm, constituting an inward-facing conformation37. This outward-facing conformation, characterized by the cavity open towards the periplasmic space, represents the rate-limiting step in the H⁺-galactoside transport cycle. Previous studies have shown that this high-energy state can be captured either by the G46W/G262W mutation or by binding specific nanobodies (Fig. 6a)38,39. In this study, an EcLacY variant with 10 amino acid mutations was selected for investigation: eight Cys mutations were introduced to facilitate high-yield recombinant expression40, while the double-Trp mutation was included to stabilize the outward-facing conformation38 (Supplementary Table 1). The Nb-TAIL was reformatted from the previously reported crystal structure of Nb9039/EcLacYG46W/G262W (PDB ID 5GXB)39. Similar to the Trimbody assembly of soluble proteins, a high-quality ternary complex was obtained by mixing H3-PrAC-5350A, Nb-TAIL, and EcLacY10Mu at a molar ratio of 1:1:1.2, followed by SEC (Supplementary Fig. 12a). During the cryo-EM processing, representative particles from 2D class averages clearly revealed the characteristic micelle structures formed by the detergent coating the membrane protein, enabling reliable particle classification. In the reconstructed 3D map, the three LacY-bound micelles were well separated from each other and showed no steric interference with the Trimbody scaffold (Fig. 6b). After refinement, a high-quality electron density map was obtained at an overall resolution of 2.50 Å (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Figs. 12b, 13a–e). Local resolution assessment revealed that the Nb-bound C-lobe of EcLacY10Mu was resolved to higher resolution, whereas the N-lobe, located at the periphery of the complex, exhibited poorer resolution (Fig. 6c). Focused refinement on the Nb/EcLacY10Mu subcomplex yielded a map at 2.82-Å resolution, which significantly improved the local density for the N-lobe (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 13f–g), allowing clear visualization of side-chain densities for the majority of residues within the transmembrane helices of EcLacY10Mu (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Structural comparison between the Trimbody-determined EcLacY10Mu and the crystal structure of EcLacYG46W/G262W revealed a slight conformational difference (RMSD = 0.699 Å), which mainly originates from the N-lobe (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Given the absence of direct contact between the N-lobe and the Trimbody, this conformational shift is likely attributable to the introduced Cys mutations. The universality analysis of the Trimbody method

The Trimbody application hinges on the engineering Nb-TAIL fusion protein, which depends on two interfaces of PrAC/Nb and Nb/TAIL. Although these interacting residues are primarily located within the highly conserved framework, slight variations exist in the amino acid sequences of Nbs derived from different species. Therefore, the compatibility of these residues must be considered when constructing the Nb-TAIL fusion protein. Based on the structural analysis of four Trimbodies, we have identified 10 important residues of Nb that interact with PrAC (Fig. 6f). Previous studies on the Legobody method also analyzed the compatibility of PrAC-interacting residues from different native Nbs: NbKDELR and NbRDB were successfully assembled, whereas the original NbALFA from the crystal structure (PDB ID 6I2G) failed to interact with the PrAC-containing protein until nine amino acid mutations were introduced20,41. Sequence alignment of these Nbs revealed that the 10 PrAC-interacting residues are highly conserved (Supplementary Fig. 15a). For the original NbALFA, two residues, T75 and N89 (correspondingly S73 and S87 on NbAcGFP), are likely responsible for severely impairing PrAC binding, due to steric clashes (Supplementary Fig. 15b). We have also identified eight key residues involved in TAIL interaction by sequence alignment and structural analysis of the three Nb-TAIL constructs successfully assembled into Trimbodies. The five highly conserved hydrophobic residues on the Nb framework can be retained, whereas the three hydrophilic residues located at the C-terminus of Nb must be mutated (Q112L, T114A, S116V) as they participate in the AI-designed hydrophobic interface (Supplementary Fig. 15c). Since the three residues point toward the solvent-exposed region, these mutations do not affect the overall Nb structure. In summary, the successful assembly of Trimbody relies on the proper construction of the Nb-TAIL fusion protein. Based on the successful cases presented in this study, the aforementioned 18 residues are strongly recommended for nanobody reformatting. If alternative sequences must be used, complex assembly should be initially validated by biochemical assays (e.g., pull-down, SEC, or SPR) in combination with structural prediction and analysis.

Another potential limitation is steric hindrance between the POI and Trimbody, or intrinsic steric clashes among three POI copies binding to Trimbody. To assess this, we conducted structural superposition analyses using a set of Nb/POI crystal structures (MW < 100 kDa) from the PDB, excluding redundant entries with similar epitopes (Supplementary Table 2). Among 68 soluble POIs analyzed, three showed minor steric clashes. However, the inherent flexibility of CDRs may allow these possible clashes to be circumvented. Only two POIs exhibited clear steric conflicts with Trimbody. For 16 membrane proteins, irrespective of lipid components or nanodiscs, only three displayed notable steric conflicts with Trimbody. Further analysis of these five structures with significant steric hindrance revealed that their Nb-binding sites predominantly involve CDR2, a rare mode of the Nb/POI interaction, exemplified by the fimbrial adhesin AC (PDB ID 4W6W) and the mouse opioid receptor (PDB ID 5C1M) (Supplementary Fig. 16). The proximity of the PrAC/Nb interface to CDR2 likely leads to the obvious clashes with H3-PrAC-5350A. Notably, no structures were identified that clashed with Nb-TAIL or exhibited intrinsic steric clashes among the three POIs, while simultaneously avoiding conflict with H3-PrAC-5350A. This strongly suggests that the main source of steric hindrance arises from the H3-PrAC domain, an inherent limitation of all PrAC/Nb-based systems. Fortunately, Nbs that preferentially utilize CDR2 as the dominant interface are relatively rare. In conclusion, the initial design principle, orienting the CDR regions of Nbs toward the solvent-accessible space and away from the scaffold, ensures the broad compatibility of Trimbody with the majority of small proteins. Moreover, preliminary validation via SEC or pull-down assays provides an effective strategy for screening Nbs that avoid steric interference with the scaffold.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed a powerful two-component tool, Trimbody, for cryo-EM structure determination of small proteins at atomic-level detail. The overall design is based on a trimeric scaffold, so we propose the name Trimbody for the method (please avoid confusion with the unrelated TRIM(body)-Away technique42,43, a strategy for targeted protein degradation based on TRIM21 protein). In Trimbody, the Nb functions as a POI-binder, while the trimer of PrAC-5350A fusion proteins serves as a scaffold to provide size and a characteristic shape. Most importantly, the rigidity imparted by AI-designed domains enhances the local resolution of the Nb/POI complex within 3 Å. At such high resolution, the densities of most side chains can be clearly traced, as demonstrated by the resolved cryo-EM structures of hGal10, AcGFP, IgV_hNectin4, and EcLacY10Mu.

By analyzing our method alongside previously developed universal approaches, including DARPin-containing cage, Megabody, NabFab and Legobody, several advanced aspects of Trimbody are summarized as follows. (i) The rigidity and stability of the whole system facilitate high-resolution structural determination of Nb-bound target proteins. The AI-designed trimeric H3 domains interacting with PrAC compensate for the insufficient rigidity of the single α-helical linker to 5350A. The AI-designed TAIL domain significantly enhances the binding affinity of Nb-TAIL to H3-PrAC-5350A, overcoming the dissociation issue of the weak Nb/PrAC interface. Moreover, the rigid four-helix bundle linker, as opposed to a flexible loop linker, ensures that the Nb-TAIL fusion protein behaves as a single compact domain, which is crucial for system stability. (ii) Our system represents the simplest binder/scaffold complex explored thus far, composed of only two fusion proteins: Nb-TAIL and H3-PrAC-5350A. The Nb-fusion design involves straightforward N/C-terminal grafting, requiring the retention of a few key residues that interact with PrAC and TAIL domains during Nb framework engineering. Importantly, both fusion proteins can be readily purified from an E.coli recombination expression system, and the pure ternary complexes containing the POI can be easily assembled and purified in vitro in either a one-step (three-protein mixture) or two-step process. Thus, our approach is both labor-efficient and cost-effective. (iii) In Trimbody, the region where the Nb binds to the POI is oriented outward and away from the scaffold. The H3 domain is positioned inside the PrAC-5350A trimer and almost parallel to PrAC. This design minimizes steric hindrance between the POI and the scaffold. (iv) Our method can also be applied to high-throughput structural biology research in nanobody-drug development, eliminating the need for crystallization screening of Nb/POI complexes.

During application, we identified several considerations: (1) Particle orientation bias could exist in vitreous ice. Among the four structures resolved in this study, no obvious particle orientation bias was observed, indicating that Trimbody does not inherently introduce directional preference. However, when resolving the structures of human Tim3 in complex with two identified Nbs, particle orientation bias was encountered during sample preparation. This issue had been successfully addressed through protein PEGylation (a detailed structural and functional analysis of these anti-hTim3 Nbs will be published elsewhere). Thus, potential issues of particle preferred orientation, which depend on the nature of the POI, could be addressed by routine sample optimization strategies, such as adding glycerol, different detergents, or PEGylation reagents44. (2) The TAIL domain fused to the C-terminus of Nb does not enhance solubility. While most Nbs can be efficiently expressed in E.coli, some Nbs exhibit low expression levels due to specific CDR sequences. Appending the TAIL domain does not mitigate this limitation. Therefore, it is advisable to prioritize Nbs with intrinsically high expression levels in their unmodified forms when designing Nb-TAIL constructs to ensure optimal yield and stability. For those Nbs with low expression, it is advisable to optimize expression conditions prior to fusion with the TAIL domain. This may involve the use of specialized E. coli strains (e.g., SHuffle T7 cell), fusion tags (e.g., SUMO), or alternative expression system (e.g., Expi293 cell). (3) Owing to the resource-intensive nature, we do not recommend constructing an Nb-TAIL library at the outset. We advise starting with a conventional screening strategy to identify candidate Nbs that exhibit high expression, high affinity for the POI, and/or neutralizing activity. The selected candidates can subsequently be subjected to structural determination using the Trimbody method. (4) Following structural determination, it is essential to perform structural analysis and functional assays to verify that the POI conformation represents its physiological state, thereby ruling out a constrained conformation induced by potential contact between the POI and Trimbody. Despite these minor limitations, the Trimbody system demonstrates significant potential for widespread applicability. To date, we have successfully resolved six atomic-resolution structures of low-MW target proteins. The PrAC/Nb complex-based framework utilized in this study, further optimized through AI-assisted stabilization and rigidity enhancements, provides a versatile platform for advancing Cryo-EM methodologies.

Methods

Ethics

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Third Institute of Oceanography, Ministry of Natural Resources (Approval No. TIO-IACUC-02-2022-03-02), and complied with Fujian Provincial Department of Science and Technology’s experimental animal management requirements.

Nanobody generation and selection

Nanobodies targeting hGal10 were generated via immunization of Bactrian camels (Camelus bactrianus). For immunization-based screening, the animals were immunized with hGal10 according to standard protocols, followed by phage display-based screening. Nanobodies against hNectin4 were isolated from a naive phage display library constructed from Bactrian camels. Phage display panning was conducted for three rounds using immobilized hNectin4 as the target, and positive clones were subsequently identified by ELISA. The anti-AcGFP and anti-EcLacY nanobodies were derived from the previously reported sequences (PDB IDs 3OGO and 5GXB). These nanobodies, reformatted with/without the TAIL domain, were obtained by gene synthesis (Tsingke Biotech). All nanobody sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Construct, expression and purification of proteins

Genes encoding nanobodies, featuring a C-terminal 6×His tag and a Myc tag, were cloned into the pADL-23C vector. Expression was induced in E. coli WK6 cells cultured in Terrific Broth (TB) at 25 °C overnight, by adding 0.5 mM isopropylthio-β-galactoside (IPTG) at an OD600 of 1.0. Cell pellets of 1 L culture medium were collected after centrifugation, resuspended in 15 mL ice-cold TES buffer (0.2 M Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.5 M sucrose) and incubated with gentle shaking on ice for one hour. Subsequently, 30 mL of TES/4 buffer (a 1:4 dilution of TES in ddH2O) was added, and the mixture was agitated on ice for another 45 min. Cell debris was removed via centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was loaded onto Ni-NTA resins (MedChemExpress) pre-equilibrated with TES/4 buffer. After washing with a buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 50 mM imidazole, the target protein was fractioned by elution with 300 mM imidazole. Concentrated proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in Hepes-Buffered Saline (HBS; 25 mM Hepes at pH 7.4 and 150 mM NaCl) buffer. Chromatography fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE to assess purity.

DNA sequences encoding NbAcGFP-TAIL and NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL were synthesized and cloned into the pET-28a and pET-22b vectors, respectively, with each construct carrying a C-terminal 6 × His tag. Expression was carried out using E. coli BL21(DE3) cells in Luria Broth (LB) at 16 °C overnight after 0.3 mM IPTG at an OD600 of 0.6. Harvested cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Hepes at pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP), supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 1 × EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific), and 0.5% Triton X-100. Cells were lysed by sonication, and the clarified lysate was loaded onto a Ni-NTA column (MedChemExpress). The column was washed with lysis buffer supplemented with 50 mM imidazole, and eluted with 300 mM imidazole. NbAcGFP-TAIL was further purified by loading on a Superdex 75 Increase column in HBS buffer. The purification process of NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL was similar to that of NbAcGFP-TAIL except that 10% glycerol was added to the buffers. The plasmid pET-22b carrying NbEcLacY-TAIL with a C-terminal Strep II tag was transformed and expressed in E. coli SHuffle T7 cells. The recombinant protein was purified using Strep-Tactin resin (IBA), involving a wash with lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT) followed by elution with a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 50 mM biotin. It was then further purified by SEC on a Superdex 75 Increase column with HBS buffer.

DNA fragments encoding PrAC-5350A and H3-PrAC-5350A were synthesized and cloned into the pET-22b vector, while IgV_hNectin4 was cloned into the pET-21a vector. All constructs are featured with C-terminal 6 × His tags. These proteins were purified using the same protocol established for NbAcGFP-TAIL. Genes encoding AcGFP and hGal10, each with a C-terminal Strep II tag, were cloned into the pRSF-1b and pET-28a vectors, respectively. The corresponding recombinant proteins were purified in a similar way to NbEcLacY-TAIL.

The EcLacY10Mu protein, encoded by a lacY gene incorporating ten mutations (G46W, C117S, C148A, C154V, C176M, C234S, G262W, C333S, C353A, C355A), was cloned into a pET-28a vector with C-terminal Strep II and 10 × His tags. The plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, which were grown in 5 L of TB and induced with 0.3 mM IPTG for 20 h at 25 °C. The cells were harvested and lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer, and the debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min. Membranes were collected by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 90 min and subsequently solubilized for 2 h at 4 °C in HBS buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 1 × EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific), and 1.5% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM; Anatrace). After centrifugation at 150,000 × g for 30 min, the supernatant was loaded onto a cobalt affinity resin (Takara). The bound protein was washed with HBS buffer containing 20 mM imidazole and 0.1% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG; Anatrace) to exchange the detergent. Finally, the detergent-exchanged EcLacY10Mu was eluted with HBS buffer containing 300 mM imidazole and 0.05% LMNG, and the imidazole was removed using a desalting column (Cytiva).

Assembly and purification of the (i)Trimbody/POI complex

Purified PrAC-5350A, NbhGal10, and hGal10 were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1.5:2 in HBS buffer, and incubated on ice for 1 h. The complex was purified using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column in the same buffer. Elution fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and the identified iTrimbody/hGal10 complex was immediately used for cryo-EM preparation. The PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP/AcGFP and H3-PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP-TAIL/AcGFP complex were assembled and purified using the same procedure. For the H3-PrAC-5350A/NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL/IgV_hNectin4 complex, three components were mixed at a 1:1:1.2 molar ratio in the assembly buffer (25 mM HEPES at pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl and 10% glycerol), incubated at 4 °C for 1 h, and purified by loading on a Superdex 200 Increase column in HBS buffer. The H3-PrAC-5350A/NbEcLacY-TAIL/EcLacY10Mu complex was purified using a two-step SEC strategy. Initially, H3-PrAC-5350A was incubated with NbEcLacY-TAIL at a 1:3 molar ratio, and the resulting binary complex was resolved by SEC. The purified binary complex was then combined with a 1.2-fold molar excess of EcLacY10Mu in assembly buffer containing 0.015% LMNG. Finally, the assembled ternary complex was purified via SEC in HBS buffer supplemented with 0.01% LMNG and 0.5% glycerol.

Surface plasmon resonance analysis

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were performed on a Biacore T200 (Cytiva) to investigate the binding kinetics. The system was preconditioned with HBS-EP+ buffer (Cytiva) containing 3 mM EDTA and 0.05% (v/v) Surfactant P20. H3-PrAC-5350A was covalently immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva) via standard amine coupling. The ligand was diluted to 20 µg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0), and immobilization was stopped when the response reached approximately 2000 response units (RU). Two analytes, NbAcGFP (5000–156.25 nM) and NbAcGFP-TAIL (25–1.5625 nM), were prepared in HBS-EP+ buffer by twofold serial dilution. Analytes were injected at 30 µL/min and 25 °C. For NbAcGFP, the association and dissociation times were 120 s and 300 s, respectively; for NbAcGFP-TAIL, they were 90 s and 600 s, respectively. Data were collected at a rate of 10 Hz.

The kinetics of the H3-PrAC-5350A and NbAcGFP-TAIL interaction were analyzed using a 1:1 binding model in Biacore Evaluation Software (v3.2.1, Cytiva). Reference surface and blank injection subtractions were applied (double referencing). The fitted Rmax was 37.68 RU (χ2 = 0.702 RU2). The association and dissociation rate constants were ka = 6.782 × 105 M−1 s−1 and kd = 9.729 × 10−5 s−1, yielding a KD of 1.435 × 10⁻10 M. For the interaction between H3-PrAC-5350A and NbAcGFP, binding data were analyzed using a steady-state affinity model with double referencing. The fitted Rmax was 245.0 RU, with a χ2 value of 0.439 RU2. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was 3.857 × 10−6 M. To better illustrate the kinetics, the dissociation rate constant (kd) was also determined to be 1.434 × 10−3 s−1 by the 1:1 binding model. All fitting curves and results are provided in Source Data.

Design of the H3-PrAC-5350A and Nb-TAIL fusion proteins

The 5350A model was derived from the 5350A/5350B subcomplex structure in synthetic icosahedral particle I53-50 (PDB ID 6P6F)26, while the PrAC domain originated from the 3-helix bundle that interacts with the anti-KDELR Nb in the structure of the Legobody/KDELR complex (PDB ID 7RXC)20. In the mode of merging α-helices, three PrAC-5350A fusion models (N1-N3) and two 5350A-PrAC fusion models (C1–C2) were manually constructed using PyMOL [http://www.pymol.org] and Coot45.

RFdiffusion21 was employed to design the H3 domain, which is fused to PrAC-5350A and stabilize the projecting PrAC conformation. Based on the PrAC-5350A model, derived from our cryo-EM structure of the PrAC-5350A/NbhGal10/hGal10 complex, we generated 10 preliminary designs of the H3 domains, with 60 residues in length and C3 symmetry (the parameter settings are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 5a). These H3 candidates were then superposed with the model of PrAC-5350A/NbhGal10/hGal10 complex using ChimeraX46. The design with optimal PrAC interval occupancy and minimal steric clash with the scaffold was selected. Subsequently, ten amino acid sequences were computationally designed using ProteinMPNN22. Following 3D structural prediction by AlphaFold version 2.1.0 (AlphaFold2)29, the design with the highest pLDDT score was chosen for experimental validation.

We adopted a two-step design strategy for the de novo TAIL domain fused to the C-terminus of the Nb. Firstly, key residues (V83, I87, V91, A116, and V119) of 5350A at the 5350A/5350B interface from the synthetic icosahedral particle I53-50 (PDB 6P6F) were identified. Surrounding these hotspots, the 5350A-binding motif was designed guided by RFdiffusion software (the detailed parameter settings in Supplementary Fig. 5b). The 30 initial designs were evaluated by contact area with 5350A in ChimeraX, and the largest contacting construct was selected. Then, the linker motif that bridges Nb and the 5350A-binding motif was further designed (the detailed parameter settings in Supplementary Fig. 5b). Among the 30 resulting designs, a rigid 4-helix bundle interacting with both the C-terminus of Nb and the 5350A-binding motif was selected. The final designs of Nb-TAIL were performed through ProteinMPNN sequence generation and AlphaFold2 prediction, with the highest-scoring one selected for experimental validation.

Sample preparation and cryo-EM data collection

The assembled ternary complexes were prepared at the following concentrations: PrAC-5350A/NbhGal10/hGal10 at 0.7 mg/mL, PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP/AcGFP at 0.65 mg/mL, H3-PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP-TAIL/AcGFP at 1.1 mg/mL, H3-PrAC-5350A/NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL/hNectin4 at 1 mg/mL, and H3-PrAC-5350A/NbEcLacY-TAIL/ EcLacY10Mu complex at 0.6 mg/mL. A 3 µL aliquot of each sample was applied to a glow-discharged Quantifoil 300-mesh R1.2/1.3 gold grid for 50 s at 15 mA using a PELCO easiGLow (Ted Pella). The samples on Grids were vitrified using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under controlled conditions (4 °C, 100% humidity, blot force ranging from 3 to 9, and blot time 3 s).

Cryo-EM data collection of the PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP/AcGFP complex was conducted on a 100 kV Tundra Cryo-Transmission Electron Microscope (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Ceta F CMOS Detector (Thermo Scientific). Images were acquired at × 81,000 magnification, yielding a pixel size of 0.98 Å, and were dose-fractionated into 52 frames at a dose rate of 1 e/Å²/s. For the other four protein complexes, micrographs were collected on a 300 kV Krios G4 microscope (Thermo Fisher), utilizing a Falcon4i direct electron detector in EER mode with the EPU automated acquisition software. Data were recorded at × 130,000 magnification, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.92 Å. Movies were collected with a total dose of 60 e/Å² and a defocus range from −1.0 to −2.5 µm.

The workflow of cryo-EM data processing

All image processing operations for dose-fractionated movies were conducted using cryoSPARC v.4.6.147, including motion correction, contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation, particle picking, 2D classification, 3D reconstruction, and polishing. Initial particle selection was performed using the blob picker, followed by two rounds of 2D classification. An ab initio 3D reconstruction was then generated, which served as a reference to create 2D templates for template-based particle re-picking. The re-picked particles, combined with those from the initial 3D reconstruction, were subjected to heterogeneous refinement using default settings. Particles from class displaying well-defined secondary structure features were re-extracted for homogeneous refinement with C1 symmetry and CTF refinement.

For the PrAC-5350A/NbhGal10/hGal10 complex, the homogeneous refinement output was further processed using non-uniform refinements with D3 symmetry, resulting in a density map with a 2.62-Å resolution. For the H3-PrAC-5350A/NbAcGFP-TAIL /AcGFP complex, non-uniform refinement with C3 symmetry yielded a final resolution of 2.29 Å. Additionally, a local refinement was performed using a mask that selectively encompassed the regions of AcGFP and NbAcGFP, further improving the resolution to 2.80 Å. For the H3-PrAC-5350A/NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL /hNectin4 complex, data processing was conducted to reach the overall resolution of 2.43 Å, applying local refinement with C3 symmetry. A separate local refinement was carried out using a mask specifically targeting IgV_hNectin4/Nb complex, resulting in a final resolution of 2.96 Å. The global refinement with C3 symmetry applied on H3-PrAC-5350A/NbEcLacY-TAIL/EcLacY10Mu complex yielded an overall map resolution of 2.50 Å. The focused refinement was performed on the EcLacY10Mu/Nb region using a soft mask, which finally reached the 2.82-Å resolution. All reported resolutions were determined using gold-standard refinement procedures, with the Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) threshold set to 0.143. FSC plots were generated using the Orientation Diagnostics tool in cryoSPARC v.4.6.1.

Model building and refinement

The atomic models used for modular docking into cryo-EM density maps were derived as follows: (1) the components of (i)Trimbody were primarily the predicted models using AlphaFold2, including PrAC-5350A, H3-PrAC-5350A, NbAcGFP-TAIL, NbIgV_hNectin4-TAIL, and NbEcLacY-TAIL. Structural predictions were performed using default parameters without templates. Predicted models for key scaffolds, such as N1 PrAC-5350A, H3-PrAC-5350A, and Nb-TAIL, are available in Supplementary Fig. 17, with both pLDDT scores and PAE plots shown. (2) the POIs were mainly obtained from experimentally resolved crystal structures, such as hGal10 (PDB ID 1HDK), AcGFP (PDB ID 3LVA), IgV_hNectin4 (PDB ID 4JJH), and Nb9039/EcLacYG46W/G262W (PDB ID 5GXB). The structural models were initially fitted into the cryo-EM density maps using ChimeraX and subsequently refined manually using Coot. Further refinements were performed in PHENIX48, applying standard real-space refinement protocols. Data collection, refinement, and validation statistics for the four cryo-EM structures are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Structural figures were generated using PyMol (http://www.pymol.org) and ChimeraX.

Statistics & reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. Most experiments were not randomized except that the dataset was split into two independent randomized half sets in cryo-EM model validation. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Biochemical experiments (protein expression, purification, complex assembly, SEC, and SDS-PAGE) were performed at least twice independently with similar results. SPR analyses were performed once per complex with multiple concentrations. Cryo-EM grid preparation and screening were repeated multiple times per complex to optimize conditions. High-resolution data collection was performed once per complex, as standard in single-particle cryo-EM.
