Diffuse Infiltrating Perisellar Mass Mimicking Polyneuritis Cranialis
Authors: Paul Philipp Kastner, Pia Lena Niederau, Georgios Pantazis, Katharina Seystahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSubacute oculomotor dysfunction encompasses a broad differential diagnosis, including autoimmune, infectious, vascular, and neoplastic etiologies. Primary skull base diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a rare but treatable cause of cranial nerve dysfunction and may present subtly on conventional neuroimaging.MethodsWe report an 80‐year‐old man with subacute diplopia, dysfunction of multiple cranial nerves, left‐sided ptosis, and headache. Diagnostic workup included cranial imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis with cytology and flow cytometry, neurophysiological studies, serum diagnostics, and dedicated MRI of the skull base. Transsphenoidal biopsy was performed following identification of an infiltrative lesion.ResultsInitial investigations including cranial CT, CSF analysis, and serum diagnostics were unremarkable. Dedicated skull base MRI revealed a diffuse infiltrating mass involving the clivus, petrous bone, occipital condyles, and perisellar/posterior pituitary region, with anatomical correlation to multiple cranial nerves. Mildly elevated prolactin levels suggested a stalk effect. Histological analysis confirmed DLBCL (NOS, GCB type). Treatment with corticosteroids followed by dose‐adjusted R‐CHOP resulted in complete clinical remission and radiologically confirmed tumor regression.ConclusionsThis case illustrates a rare presentation of primary bony skull base DLBCL with perisellar infiltration, highlighting the diagnostic challenges of complex oculomotor dysfunction. Dedicated skull base MRI is essential in the workup of unexplained cranial neuropathy. CSF‐based molecular markers may complement the diagnostic approach in similar presentations.Keywords: lymphoma, ophthalmoplegia, pituitary gland, polyneuritis cranialis, skull base

Introduction

Subacute oculomotor dysfunction represents a broad differential diagnosis involving autoimmune, (post)infectious, and vascular etiologies, but also may be caused by local destructive or infiltrative processes.

Case Presentation

An 80‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with a 10‐day history of subacute vertical and diagonal diplopia, left‐sided ptosis, and left‐sided headache. The new‐onset headache was described as a dull pain radiating unilaterally from the neck and occipital regions to V1 and especially V2 areas, worsening in supine position. Further symptoms included transient sensation of a heavy tongue and numbness of the left ear. Clinical examination revealed bilateral sixth nerve palsy (left predominant), severe adduction and elevation deficit of the left eye, concomitant skew deviation, and ptosis. Tongue deviation suggested hypoglossal nerve involvement. Neurological and ophthalmologic examination did not show any further abnormalities. Medical history was unremarkable except for myeloproliferative disorder (i.e., essential thrombocythemia).

The presentation initially suggested various neurological entities, pointing towards polyneuritis cranialis as a common etiology of multiple cranial nerve dysfunction.

Cranial computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography revealed no acute pathology. Similarly, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed no pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, negative viral panel testing, and unremarkable cytology and flow cytometry. Neurophysiological conduction studies, as well as serum diagnostics including LDH were unremarkable. However, cranial MRI focusing on retroorbital regions, and the skull base identified a local diffuse mass involving the clivus, petrous part of the temporal bone, and occipital condyles, but also extending to the perisellar region, infiltrating the posterior pituitary region (Figure 1A,C,E).

The anatomical location of this infiltrative lesion was adjacent to several cranial nerves, namely the left oculomotor, trigeminus, and hypoglossal nerve, and bilateral abducens nerve. Irritation of the hypoglossal nerve was assumed to occur during passage of the hypoglossal canal in the occipital bone. Compression of both abducens nerves was noticed at the level of the Dorello canal bilaterally, while irritation of the left abducens, oculomotor, and trigeminus nerves could be observed mainly during passage of the wall of the left cavernous sinus.

Clinically, there was no evidence of pituitary dysfunction. However, mild elevations in prolactin levels were present, suggesting a stalk effect caused by infiltration of the sellar region and posterior pituitary gland.

Given the absence of an evident diagnosis derived from imaging modalities, transsphenoidal biopsy of the lesion was performed. Histologic analysis ultimately revealed a diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), NOS (IARC/WHO‐5th Ed., Vol.11), GCB Type (Figure S1).

This patient received oral prednisolone therapy followed by systemic treatment with dose‐adjusted rituximab, cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone. Symptoms resolved completely upon early corticosteroid administration, and tumor regression was confirmed by imaging after six cycles of systemic treatment (Figure 1B,D,F).

Discussion

This case is one of a few reports on the rare occurrence of an extranodal primary bony skull base DLBCL with infiltration of the sellar region. These DLBCLs are characterized by highly variable clinical and imaging characteristics [1]. Here, complex oculomotor dysfunction was caused by diffuse infiltration of the DLBCL in the midline skull and perisellar regions. While earlier case series reported DLBCLs mainly in the anterior and anterolateral skull base, diffuse midline lymphomas are rare [1, 2]. Although systemic lymphomas show involvement of the CNS in some cases, infiltration of the pituitary gland is seen very infrequently in primary skull base lymphoma [1, 3, 4]. In our case, histology confirmed the presence of DLBCL in the neurohypophysis. Our patient did not exhibit severe endocrinological disturbances, probably attributable to early initiation of treatment.

The clinical presentation in this patient mimicked polyneuritis cranialis but also warranted consideration of other differential diagnoses, including cavernous sinus fistula, myasthenia gravis, other intracranial neoplasms, and infectious processes. A biopsy of the lesion was performed to achieve precise etiologic differentiation. Diagnostic work up could have been supplemented by minimally invasive CSF‐based approaches not performed in this case, such as detection of MYD88 mutations, elevated IL‐10, or CXCL13, as they have shown diagnostic value in identifying lymphomatous etiology of CNS lesions [5]. Accurate and timely diagnosis is essential for selecting appropriate treatment and determining the timing of corticosteroid administration, which may alter histopathological features and mask or delay the diagnosis of lymphoma. The patient responded well to systemic therapy, with DLBCL regression confirmed clinically and radiologically.

In conclusion, this case illustrates a rare presentation of DLBCL of the bony skull base with infiltration of the sellar region, featuring subtle but intriguing MR imaging findings. Moreover, it highlights the challenges in differential diagnoses in patients with complex oculomotor dysfunction with immediate therapeutic consequences.

Supporting information

FIGURE S1: Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis of the neurohypophyseal lesion. (A) Hematoxylin–eosin staining (magnification 40×) showing diffuse infiltrates of large atypical lymphoid cells within the neurohypophysis. (B) CD20 immunostaining showing positivity in the atypical lymphoid cells (magnification 40×). Additional immunohistochemical analysis demonstrates a Ki‐67 proliferation index of approximately 90%, positivity for CD10, BCL2, and BCL6, with negativity for MUM1 and CD3; EBER in situ hybridization and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for IgH rearrangement, BCL2/IgH fusion, BCL6 rearrangement, MYC break‐apart, and MYC/IgH fusion were negative (data not shown). Overall findings are consistent with diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), NOS, according to IARC 2024 (WHO‐5th Ed., Vol.11) criteria.
