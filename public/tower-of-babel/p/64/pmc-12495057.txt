Machine learning and gene network integration reveal prognostic subnetworks and biomarkers in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Rana Salihoglu, Jesus Nieves, Gudrun Dandekar, Regina Ebert, Maximilian Rudert, Thomas Dandekar, Elena Bencurova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer has a high mortality rate and lacks early detection markers. Advanced methods, such as machine learning (ML) and network analysis, identify central cancer networks with potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, leading to improved tumor targeting strategies.MethodsWe systematically collected pancreatic cancer transcriptome datasets from the databases TCGA, GTEx, and GEO. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified gene modules associated with clinical traits. Multiple machine learning-based feature selection methods (Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, LASSO, ReliefF) and differential gene expression (DGE) analysis prioritized candidate genes. Functional enrichment (Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway database) examined biological processes involved in tumor progression and immune evasion. Survival analyses evaluated prognostic significance.ResultsWGCNA identified pancreatic cancer networks from key gene modules strongly associated with cancer stage and survival. Common biomarkers, including transcripts from genes ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, MUC1, and PHF20, demonstrated significant diagnostic and prognostic potential as shown by ML, WGCNA, DGE, and survival analyzes. In vitro validation was performed for proteins mucin1 and CD44 in patient samples and tissue models.ConclusionThis study identified novel regulatory cancer networks and associated biomarkers for pancreatic cancer prognosis and diagnosis by integrating WGCNA with ML, DGE, pathway, and survival analyses. An interactive web portal to explore the full results and visualizations is available at pc-biomarkers.de. Future work will further validate these biomarkers to improve early detection, prognosis, and treatment strategies.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, machine learning, biomarker, WGCNA, feature selection

Introduction

In recent years, discovering reliable biomarkers has become crucial for advancing early cancer diagnosis and treatment strategies [1]. Pancreatic cancer, particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), remains highly deadly due to its aggressive nature and the lack of effective early detection methods, such as dependable biomarkers [2], [3], [4]. Current diagnostic approaches, including imaging and non-specific biomarkers such as CA19–9 and CAE, often fail to identify early-stage disease [5]. Machine learning (ML) combined with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) has shown promise in uncovering cancer-related genes [6], [7]. The WGCNA identifies co-expressed gene modules likely involved in shared biological processes, including tumor formation, and has been used to detect disrupted networks in pancreatic cancer [8]. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis, along with Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment, further elucidates biomarker functions in cancer progression [9], [10]. These approaches highlight key cellular processes, such as cell cycle control and inhibition of apoptosis, which are critical for tumor development.

Recent studies using genome-wide RNA sequencing, microRNA profiling, bioinformatics, and ML have identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) in PDAC. Khojasteh-Leylakoohi et al. [11](2023) identified dysregulated genes such as Bcl2 Modifying Factor (BMF), FERM Domain Containing 4 A protein (FRMD4A), and Calcium Voltage-Gated Channel Auxiliary Subunit Gamma 3 protein (CACNG3), along with miRNAs including hsa-miR-642a and hsa-miR-153–1, which are linked to tumor progression and metastasis, highlighting Cathepsin W (CTSW) as a novel prognostic biomarker, validated by RT-PCR. Similarly, Yang et al. [12] (2024) identified Epiphycan (EPYC) as a high-risk factor promoting tumor growth via the pathway of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT pathway), emphasizing the importance of computational and experimental approaches in biomarker discovery.

ML algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests (RF) excel in biomarker identification, effectively managing high-dimensional genetic data [13], [14], [15]. Rouzbahani et al. [16] (2024) demonstrated that ML could identify PDAC biomarkers associated with cell cycle regulation and patient survival.

Pancreatic cancer’s molecular heterogeneity further complicates diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment. Sinkala et al. [17] (2020) explored this molecular diversity through integrative profiling of proteomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic data of two tumor subtypes. These multi-omic methods facilitated the identification of key genes, such as Solute Carrier Family 6 Member 14 (SLC6A14), involved in cancer proliferation and metastasis, laying emphasis on the need to integrate genetic and non-genetic factors [18]. Refined analysis to reveal novel tumor drivers and more precise tumor drivers requires complex tools, such as ML and WGCNA, which can assist in cancer network analysis and the discovery of novel biomarkers for pancreatic cancer [19].

This study reveals key cancer protein interactions and networks for pancreatic cancer including underlying genes. To achieve this, it integrates ML, WGCNA, and DGE transcriptome analyses on a large-scale pancreatic cancer dataset, including 178 TCGA-PAAD samples, 197 GTEx normal pancreatic tissues, and GEO datasets GSE62452 and GSE79668. Advanced ML algorithms (such as RF, SVM, and Gradient Boosting) were used for highly sensitive and specific gene classification and feature selection in these pancreatic cancer datasets, while WGCNA identified hub genes associated with clinical traits. Functional enrichment (GO/KEGG) and survival analyses further validated the importance of these genes. This multifaceted approach enhances cancer network detection and we suggest tumor drivers and biomarkers for early diagnosis, prognosis, drug target refinement, and the development of clinically relevant tissue models. All our results can be explored in detail with a new dedicated web-server on pancreatic cancer (www.pc-biomarkers.de) with key involved protein and gene networks, for the interested user, be it a scientist, a clinician or a clinical scientist.

Methods

Differential Gene Expression (DGE) analysis

We performed DGE analysis using RNA-seq data from 178 PAAD samples (TCGA) and 120 normal pancreas samples (GTEx). TCGA-PAAD data were retrieved and processed using TCGAbiolinks, [20] maintaining consistent formats. GTEx and TCGA datasets underwent normalization and filtering to retain high-quality data.

To minimize study-specific variation between TCGA and GTEx, we first examined potential batch effects using the TCGAbatch_Correction [21] function of TCGAbiolinks. Subsequently, count-level correction was performed with ComBat-Seq (sva package; [22]), a widely used method for balancing RNA-seq data across studies. Upper quantile normalization and quantile filtering were also applied to reduce technical noise and eliminate low-abundance genes. These steps ensured that the observed differences mainly reflected biological variation rather than technical artefacts.

DGE was performed using edgeR [23] with generalized linear models (GLMs) and likelihood ratio tests (glmLRT), with FDR < 0.01 and |logFC| ≥ 1 thresholds. Limma [24] with voom transformation [25] was applied to adjust mean-variance relationships. Gene annotations were retrieved via biomaRt [26] to map Ensembl IDs to HGNC symbols. Results from edgeR and limma were merged and validated based on statistical significance.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed on TCGA-PAAD expression data and clinical metadata [27]. The expression matrix was transposed, and samples were filtered for alignment. Genes with the highest 8000 variance were selected after normalization and quality control. Samples and genes were filtered to eliminate outliers. Hierarchical clustering was used to assess sample-trait relationships, and a soft-thresholding power was chosen to achieve scale-free topology.

A signed network was built with a minimum module size of 30 genes and a merge cut height of 0.25. The Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) measured the network's structure. Module-to-module relationships were examined using heatmaps. Missing gene symbols were replaced with Ensembl IDs for consistency.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of WGCNA modules were conducted using clusterProfiler [28] with the org.Hs.eg.db database. Gene symbols were converted to Entrez IDs. GO enrichment was assessed across Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function categories using a Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment (q < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment was performed similarly. Top pathways were visualized with bar plots, dot plots, and enrichment maps based on pairwise term similarity.

Machine learning analysis

We focus on machine learning (ML) techniques used to uncover novel cancer networks and subnetworks with implied biomarkers by combining different tools.

This study used 178 TCGA-PAAD samples, 197 normal pancreatic tissue samples from GTEx, and two GEO datasets: GSE62452 (61 healthy, 69 cancer samples) and GSE79668 (51 tumor samples) for model training. Data were transposed, labeled, and imputed using SimpleImputer (mean strategy) [29]. Features were normalized using StandardScaler.

Feature selection was conducted using RF, SVM (linear kernel), LASSO, ReliefF, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), and SelectKBest (ANOVA F-statistics). The chosen features were evaluated with Logistic Regression, SVM, RF, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and AdaBoost classifiers. GridSearchCV with 10-fold cross-validation was employed to optimise hyperparameters, and models were assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC metrics. Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)[26], [30] was applied to address class imbalance. In this dataset, healthy samples formed the minority class, and SMOTE was used to synthetically generate additional normal samples by interpolating between existing minority class instances. This ensured a balanced representation of both cancer and normal samples during model training.

The top 500 significant genes from ML results were analyzed using STRING to construct a gene interaction network, visualized in Cytoscape. Hub genes were identified through CytoHubba using Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) (see Supplementary Materials).

Correlation calculation for ML-WGCNA

The analysis combined ML-derived prominent genes with WGCNA module edge lists. The ML dataset included the top 500 genes ranked by SVM importance scores. Gene identifiers were standardised and matched with WGCNA edge lists of co-expressed gene pairs. Overlapping genes were extracted, and Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear and rank-order associations [31]. A minimum of two shared genes with different importance scores was required for analysis. This process again leads to network development and reveals new interactions and subnetworks.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed on the common genes identified from machine learning, WGCNA modules, and limma/edgeR DEG results. For each candidate gene, expression levels were divided into “high” and “low” groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves were generated using the survival [32] and survminer [33] packages. Statistical differences between survival distributions were assessed with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards re-gression models were used to quantify the relationship between gene expression and OS, providing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 % confidence intervals.KM survival curves were visualized with customized color palettes and confidence intervals using ggplot2 [34] and survminer. Median survival times were annotated, and violin plots were generated to compare gene expression distributions between tumor and control groups. Outlier detection was performed using interquartile range filtering, and expression distributions were displayed with over-laid boxplots.2.7 Validation of key markers in cell culture models.

Validation of key markers in cell culture models

To validate key protein markers, we utilized 2D cell cultures and 3D bioprinted models. The pancreatic cancer cell lines PANC-1 and CAPAN-1 were cultured under standard conditions. Primary human mesenchymal stromal cells (hMSCs), isolated from bone marrow with informed consent and ethical approval, were used at low passages. For 3D dynamic culture, tumor cells—either alone or in co-culture with hMSCs—were seeded on SISmuc scaffolds and maintained in flow bioreactors under continuous medium perfusion for 14 days. Subsequently, the 3D models were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on these FFPE sections following standard antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against MUC1 and CD44, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies and nuclear counterstaining. Images were acquired using a Keyence digital microscope under consistent exposure settings. Detailed protocols for cell culture, bioreactor setup, processing, and staining are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Our analysis combined multiple methods to identify key biomarkers, pathways, and pancreatic interaction networks involved in pancreatic cancer from publicly available datasets. DGE analysis detected significantly altered genes, while WGCNA connected gene modules to clinical traits. GO and KEGG enrichment highlighted important cancer-related pathways. Machine learning techniques (e.g., SVM, Random Forest) prioritised high-confidence biomarkers, interaction networks, and identified their hub genes. Venn diagram analysis showed shared genes common across WGCNA, DGE, and ML analyses and filtered out more robust cancer subnetworks. Survival analysis revealed significant prognostic markers with potential as therapeutic targets.

Differential Gene Expression (DGE) analysis

The DGE analysis using edgeR and limma yielded different results. With edgeR, 18,242 genes were analysed, identifying 1460 downregulated and 11,001 upregulated genes (p < 0.01; log fold-change >1). Conversely, the limma analysis examined 16,377 genes, with 1597 downregulated and 8862 upregulated, applying the same p-value and log fold-change thresholds (Supplementary Data 1).

Significance of the identified modules for pancreatic cancer

WGCNA identified gene modules with strong clinical relevance, which were validated by their enrichment for established pancreatic cancer markers (Table 1). Notably, the ME1 module contained key genes—including MUC1, MUC5AC, and CEACAM6—known to drive tumorigenesis (Table 2). Similarly, ME4 was enriched for tumor suppressors like SMAD4 and DAPK1, while the ME13 module included critical regulators such as TP53 (a tumor suppressor) and BIRC5 (an oncogene). The presence of these well-documented genes strongly validates our modules and suggests their potential utility as biomarkers for monitoring disease progression and treatment response.

Revealed key modules in pancreatic cancer can be further refined by gene ontology and pathway analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the ME1 module, which is significantly associated with AJCC stage (p = 3.2e-07), vital status (p = 0.001), and age (p = 0.03), revealed its critical role in key biological processes. These processes—including signal release, hormone secretion, and insulin secretion—are known to drive tumor growth and metastasis in pancreatic cancer (Figure S1A). Supporting these findings, KEGG pathway analysis further linked the ME1 module to endocrine pathways regulating insulin, glucagon, and cortisol, underscoring its role in reprogramming tumor metabolism. Additionally, pathways associated with the nervous system and environmental processing were enriched. The identification of metabolic pathways like N-glycan biosynthesis also suggests a potential role in facilitating cell adhesion and tumor progression.

Pancreatic cancer modules activated by and associated with risk factors

GO and KEGG analyses of modules ME2, ME19, and ME31 revealed a concerted activation of cancer networks linked to environmental factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption, which were associated with poorer survival (188 GO terms; Supplementary Data 2). A key finding was the significant enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation and aerobic respiration pathways (Figure S1C), accompanied by changes in mitochondrial gene expression indicative of metabolic reprogramming. KEGG analysis further underscored the role of oxidative phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated carcinogenesis (hsa05208). This metabolic dysregulation was also connected to disorders that elevate cancer risk, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and diabetic cardiomyopathy (Figure S1D). Notably, these modules also shared neurodegenerative pathways (Huntington’s, Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s), linking pancreatic cancer progression to broader mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic disruption (Supplementary Data 3).

Model performance tests by machine learning identifies key tumor genes and interactions

To identify the best model and training method, we assessed the performance of several ML models using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score (Supplementary Table 1). ML-based feature selection pinpointed 500 potential biomarker genes (Supplementary Data 4), including consistent examples such as glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1), anillin (ANLN), and plant homeodomain finger protein 20 (PHF20).

Models trained with RF-selected features achieved high test accuracies (94.7 %), with AdaBoost performing best (96.5 % accuracy), followed by XGBoost (95.6 % accuracy) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1).

Heatmap visualization of model performance metrics across different feature selection methods and machine learning models. Each cell displays the respective metric value, including Test Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score. Feature selection methods are indicated alongside the models for clarity. The heatmap highlights comparative model effectiveness based on multiple evaluation criteria.

SVM-selected features produced strong results, with Gradient Boosting and XGBoost achieving 96.5 % and 97.3 % accuracy, respectively, while maintaining high precision, recall, and F1-scores. LASSO-selected models showed slightly lower accuracies (92.0 %–93.8 %) but retained strong ROC-AUC values. ReliefF-selected features also performed well, with XGBoost and AdaBoost reaching 96.5 % and 94.7 % accuracy, respectively.

RFE-based models achieved test accuracies of 94.7 %–96.5 %, with Gradient Boosting and AdaBoost performing well. SelectKBest models achieved accuracies between 93.8 % and 94.7 %, with Logistic Regression and SVM maintaining high ROC-AUC scores.

Overall, precision, recall, and F1-scores exceeded 92 %, often surpassing 94 %. ROC-AUC values ranged from 0.967 to 0.995, with AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting demonstrating near-perfect discrimination between healthy and cancer samples. Genes selected using SVM and RF were prioritized for further analysis based on their superior performance.

Correlation between WGCNA modules and ML results points out new pancreatic cancer biomarkers

To identify robust pancreatic cancer biomarkers, we integrated machine learning (ML) predictions with WGCNA by analyzing correlations between their respective gene sets using Pearson and Spearman coefficients. This integration revealed a strong synergistic relationship, with several modules showing exceptionally high correlations with ML predictions. Notably, modules ME14, ME22 (darkgreen), ME30, ME35, and ME7 exhibited near-perfect concordance (Pearson ≈ 0.999, Spearman = 1). ME1—a module significantly linked to key disease phenotypes—also demonstrated a very strong correlation (Pearson: 0.895, Spearman: 1), further supporting its high value as a biomarker candidate.

The correlation strength varied across other significant modules, ranging from moderate to strong (e.g., ME16/ME26: Pearson 0.914–0.952; ME8/ME36: ∼0.733; ME3/ME11: ∼0.697–0.733); a complete summary is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

To biologically validate these correlated modules, we investigated their overlap with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified by limma and edgeR. This analysis confirmed the clinical relevance of the integrated gene sets. For instance, within the high-priority ME1 module, 42 overlapping genes were significantly associated with AJCC stage (p = 3.2 × 10⁻⁷), vital status (p = 0.001), and age at diagnosis (p = 0.03). This gene set included key players in cancer progression such as GPRC5A (retinoic acid-induced protein 3) and ETV1 (ETS translocation variant 1), linking the module to critical metabolic and oncogenic pathways (Fig. 3).

Venn diagrams illustrating the results of differential expression analysis (limma and edgeR) alongside biomarker genes identified through machine learning, organized by selected modules: (A) ME1 (turquoise) module, (B) ME10 (purple) module, (C) ME3 (brown) module, (D) ME4 (yellow) module.

The ME10 module, comprising 22 genes, was significantly associated with poor survival outcomes, showing strong correlations with vital status (p = 0.0002) and days to death (p = 0.011). Key genes within this module included TNFRSF12A and CEACAM1, which are implicated in apoptosis and immune evasion, as well as ANLN and FERMT1, known promoters of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis.

Similarly, the ME3 module was enriched for immune-related functions and correlated with vital status (p = 0.0004) and age (p = 0.008). It featured important regulators such as the transcription factor KLF6 and the RNA-binding protein IGF2BP2.

Finally, the ME4 module was linked to AJCC stage (p = 0.0015) and captured processes essential for tumor growth, including cytoskeletal dynamics, lipid metabolism, and RNA stability. This was reflected by the presence of genes including NPC1L1 (cholesterol transport) and MATR3 (RNA binding).

Survival analysis confirms importance of determined genes

Survival analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic significance of key candidate genes. Six genes—PHF20, MUC1, ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, and TSPAN1—were significantly associated with survival outcomes in pancreatic cancer across all subtypes (Fig. 4).

Survival and Gene Expression Analysis. (A) Kaplan–Meier survival curves for PHF20, MUC1, ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, and TSPAN1 using PAAD TCGA clinical data. (B) Gene expression levels of PHF20, MUC1, ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, and TSPAN1 in PAAD TCGA datasets, comparing normal tissue (blue) and tumor tissue (red).

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that high expression of MUC1, ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, and TSPAN1 was associated with shorter overall survival, identifying them as potential oncogenes (Fig. 4A-B). In contrast, PHF20 exhibited a tumor-suppressive role: it was highly expressed in normal tissues and correlated with increased survival but was significantly downregulated in tumors, where its loss aligned with poorer outcomes.

The prognostic relevance of all six genes was statistically confirmed: GPRC5A (p < 0.0001), MUC1 (p = 0.0047), TSPAN1 (p = 0.0044), KLF6 (p = 0.012), ANLN (p = 0.018), and PHF20 (p = 0.039). Notably, GPRC5A emerged as a particularly strong prognostic marker.

In summary, these results define two distinct prognostic groups: the overexpression of oncogenes (MUC1, ANLN, GPRC5A, KLF6, and TSPAN1) correlates with aggressive disease and poor prognosis, while the loss of the tumor suppressor PHF20 signifies a loss of protective function and worse survival.

Validation of key markers by staining in patient tissues

To validate the clinical relevance of our in silico findings, we analyzed the expression of MUC1 and CD44 in patient-derived PDAC tissues and in complex 3D in vitro models. These models were established on decellularized intestinal scaffolds cultured under flow conditions, which preserve the basement membrane and allow for the study of both non-invasive and invasive tumor growth (Supplementary Materials).

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed distinct expression patterns. CAPAN-1 models (both monoculture and co-culture with hMSCs) exhibited high MUC1 expression, whereas PANC-1 models lacked MUC1 but showed strong, homogeneous CD44 expression (Fig. 5A).

More differentiated tumor regions express strongly MUC1 whereas in invasive parts CD44 can be expressed. (A) Immunofluorescence staining of 3D tumor cell monocultures and co-cultures with stromal cells (hMSCs), with DAPI (blue) as nuclear counterstain show in more differentiated CAPAN-1 cells a high expression of MUC1 with no distinctive cell borders and no expression of CD44 in contrast to invasively growing PANC-1 cells which express CD44 but not MUC1. (B) Immunofluorescence staining of MUC1 and CD44 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tissue samples from three different patients, counterstained with DAPI (blue) demonstrate in all samples high apical MUC1 expression in the epithelial regions and a slight expression of CD44 in patient 1 in deeper layers of the tissue (white arrows, insert). Scale bars: 100 µm. MUC1: mucin-1, hMSCs: human mesenchymal stromal cells.

Interestingly, these models recapitulated key phenotypes observed in patient samples. The apical localization of MUC1 in patient tumors closely resembled the pattern seen in CAPAN-1 models (Fig. 5A, B). Conversely, while CD44 expression was pronounced and uniform in PANC-1 models, it was weaker and more heterogeneously localized to stromal regions in patient samples (Fig. 5B, insets and arrows).

These results suggest that while the PANC-1 model exhibits a pronounced CD44-positive invasive phenotype, CD44 expression in vivo is more heterogeneous. In contrast, MUC1 expression patterns were consistently maintained between the CAPAN-1 model and patient tissues, underscoring its reliability as a marker.

Discussion

Our pancreatic cancer sub-network-based approach identified key hub genes, including dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPYD), microtubule associated protein 6 (MAP6), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RAD18 (RAD18), fibrillin-1 (FBN1), and ankyrin-2 (ANK2), which are consistent with recent studies linking them to prognosis and disease progression [19], [35], [36]. Notably, DPYD was strongly associated with poor survival, highlighting its value as a biomarker and therapeutic target. Furthermore, WGCNA identified scaffold attachment factor B1 (SAFB) as another potential target [37].

The dysfunction of these networks is often driven by canonical PDAC mutations. KRAS mutations (∼90 % prevalence) fuel tumor growth and immune suppression, with targeted therapies now emerging [38]. TP53 mutations (50–90 %) drive proliferation and immune evasion [38], [39], [40], [41], while SMAD4 mutations (30–60 %) promote metastasis and therapy resistance through disrupted TGF-β signaling [38]. The convergence of our network analysis with these established mutational drivers underscores the critical need for targeted treatments in PDAC, as summarized in Table 3.

Beyond known targets for pancreatic cancer, we identify new key biomarkers (e.g., MMP9, FN1, CDH1, ANLN, TIMP-2, GPRC5A, KLF6, IGF2BP2, MUC1, TSPAN1, and PHF20) through integrative analyses of pancreatic cancer datasets and cancer subnetworks. As we used a really large-scale dataset of transcriptome data from exclusively regarding pancreatic cancer patients but different types, we are confident that these results are robust and relevant.

Our analysis identified key genes involved in the ECM remodeling that is critical for PDAC progression. Specifically, we found that Matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9) promotes invasion and metastasis by degrading the ECM and impairing NK cell function, correlating with poor prognosis [42], [43]; however, its therapeutic targeting remains challenging [44], [45]. In contrast, the Tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP-2) acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting invasion, and its downregulation is linked to aggressive PDAC [46], making exogenous TIMP-2 a promising therapeutic agent [47]. Furthermore, Fibronectin 1 (FN1), a regulator of cell adhesion and migration associated with poor prognosis, EMT, and activation of PI3K-Akt/MAPK signaling [48], [49], [50], plays a dual role. Its involvement in both ECM remodeling and immune suppression presents a significant challenge for therapeutic intervention [51], [52].

Our analysis revealed several genes with critical and complex roles in PDAC pathogenesis, offering insights into potential therapeutic vulnerabilities. A key finding was the identification of genes that drive EMT and metastasis. The loss of CDH1 (E-cadherin) was associated with increased aggressiveness and chemoresistance [53], [54], a process further enabled by the upregulation of GPRC5A, which promotes EMT and migration via cAMP-CREB-YAP1 signaling [57], [58]. Similarly, TSPAN1 was found to drive proliferation, migration, and autophagy through LC3 and MMP pathways, correlating strongly with poor prognosis [62], [63], [64].

We also identified genes with context-dependent dual roles, highlighting the complexity of PDAC signaling. For instance, GPRC5A and KLF6 can each function as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor depending on the cellular context [55], [56], [59], with KLF6 downregulation being linked to poor outcomes.

Notably, several genes emerged as promising therapeutic targets. IGF2BP2 enhances tumorigenesis through PI3K/Akt activation [60], [61], while PHF20 presents a particularly compelling mechanism. Although it functions as a tumor suppressor by stabilizing p53 to promote apoptosis [65], [66], its role in activating NF-κB and Akt signaling suggests it may also contribute to tumor progression [67], warranting further investigation into its complex regulatory network.

The immunofluorescence findings shown align with prior studies demonstrating MUC1’s high expression in epithelial cancers, supporting roles in tumor differentiation, immune evasion, and chemoresistance [68], [69], [70], [71]. In addition, the upregulation of CD44 upregulation in PANC-1 cells aligns with its known association with mesenchymal-like phenotypes, enhancing migration, invasion, and therapeutic resistance [72], [73]. Additionally, While MUC1 expression was consistent in PDAC samples, CD44 expression varied, suggesting heterogeneity in stromal involvement.

The observed nuclear polarization and columnar-like morphology in CAPAN-1 models co-cultured with hMSCs suggest higher cellular organisation compared to aggressive PDAC tissue [74]. This finding may indicate that CAPAN-1 cells retain certain epithelial characteristics in co-culture conditions. In PANC-1 cells, invasion and CD44 expression were not hindered by stromal co-culture. These experiments demonstrate the ability to reflect clinically relevant markers in vitro and establish a foundation for testing in silico-predicted new treatment strategies.

Conclusions

This study shows that combining different computer analysis methods is a powerful way to reveal pancreatic cancer protein interaction networks promoting cancer. Using WGCNA, machine learning, and survival analysis together helped us find important cancer networks and genes. We confirmed the role of key biomarkers with lab experiments: Using a 3D model, we showed that transcripts from gene MUC1 are a reliable marker for diagnosis and prognosis, and that CD44 transcripts are a marker for cancer invasion. These results matched what we saw in real patient samples. Our larger analysis found other important genes—including MMP9, FN1, CDH1, ANLN, TIMP2, GPRC5A, KLF6, IGF2BP2, TSPAN1, and PHF20. These genes play key roles in helping the cancer grow, spread, and resist treatment. They work together in complex networks, which is a major reason why pancreatic cancer is so difficult to treat. For better accessibility of our analysis to the interested reader, we deliver a dedicated web-server allowing examination of all data in detail.

In summary, we have successfully identified and validated new pancreatic cancer biomarkers and the networks they belong to, providing a strong foundation for future research and therapy development.
