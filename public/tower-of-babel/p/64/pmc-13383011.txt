Prostate cancer-associated Trousseau's syndrome presenting as stroke in a 42-year-old man
Authors: Yosuke Tsukii, Tomoki Okada, Gensuke Okazaki, Yuya Ota, Satoshi Kurokawa, Takahiro Yasui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a 42-year-old man presenting with acute left hemiparesis and multiple cerebral infarctions accompanied by markedly elevated D-dimer levels, suggesting cancer-associated thrombosis without an identifiable primary malignancy. Six months later, he was diagnosed with advanced prostate adenocarcinoma with multiple bone metastases and a markedly elevated prostate-specific antigen level. Treatment with apalutamide and degarelix was initiated. Despite early recurrent thromboembolic events, PSA and D-dimer levels gradually decreased. At 10 months, both markers were significantly reduced and no further thrombosis occurred. This case highlights prostate cancer as a potential cause of cryptogenic stroke and importance of cancer-directed therapy for Trousseau's syndrome.

Introduction

Trousseau's syndrome is a malignancy-associated hypercoagulable state characterized by recurrent arterial and venous thromboembolic events.1,2 It is most commonly associated with adenocarcinomas, such as pancreatic, gastric, lung, and colorectal cancers, whereas prostate cancer is a relatively uncommon cause.1, 2, 3, 4 Cancer-associated stroke often presents with multiple infarctions in different vascular territories and markedly elevated D-dimer levels, but the underlying malignancy may remain occult at initial presentation.5,6 We report a rare case of Trousseau's syndrome caused by advanced prostate cancer, initially presenting with multiple cerebral infarctions before the primary malignancy became clinically apparent.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old man with no significant medical history and no family history of prostate cancer or other malignant diseases was admitted because of acute left-sided hemiparesis. Neurological examination revealed left facial palsy and left hemiplegia. Electrocardiography, echocardiography, and vascular evaluation revealed no atrial fibrillation, intracardiac thrombus, or significant atherosclerotic disease. Laboratory testing showed a markedly elevated D-dimer level of 16.32 μg/mL, decreased fibrinogen levels (90 mg/dL), elevated fibrin/fibrinogen degradation product levels (30 μg/mL), preserved antithrombin III, and a mildly prolonged prothrombin time. Anti-cardiolipin antibodies and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies were negative. Trousseau's syndrome was therefore suspected.

A systemic search for malignancy, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography, echocardiography, upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, and lower-extremity venous ultrasonography, revealed no significant abnormalities. Aspirin was started on admission, and 60 mg/day edoxaban was added 1 week later. His neurological symptoms gradually improved, and he was discharged without major residual deficits.

Six months later, he presented to the urology department with gross hematuria. His prostate-specific antigen level was 1,709 ng/mL, and prostate magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a locally advanced prostate tumor (Fig. 2). Cystoscopy showed no apparent bladder tumor, and urine cytology was negative. Staging studies demonstrated cT3N0M1b disease with multiple bone metastases. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed a locally advanced prostate tumor, and bone scintigraphy demonstrated multiple bone metastases (Fig. 3). At the initial urological visit, prostate-specific antigen and D-dimer levels were 1,709 ng/mL and 6.2 μg/mL, respectively.Fig. 2T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging of the prostate at the initial urological presentation, demonstrating a locally advanced prostate tumor.Fig. 3Bone scintigraphy at diagnosis, showing multiple bone metastases.

The patient was diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer associated with Trousseau's syndrome and was treated with apalutamide plus degarelix. Around the time of treatment initiation, he developed deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremities, renal infarction, and peripheral arterial thrombosis. Thereafter, both prostate-specific antigen and D-dimer levels gradually decreased. At 5 months, they had improved to 26.6 ng/mL and 0.5 μg/mL, respectively, and a prostate biopsy revealed adenocarcinoma with degeneration. At 10 months, they further improved to 0.64 ng/mL and 0.5 μg/mL, respectively. No further thrombotic events occurred, and both the malignancy and the hypercoagulable state remained well controlled (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Clinical course after the initial urological presentation. Discussion

Trousseau's syndrome is a cancer-associated hypercoagulable condition that can manifest as cerebral infarction, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and visceral infarction.1,2 Multiple infarctions involving different vascular territories and markedly elevated D-dimer levels are characteristic findings in cancer-associated stroke.5,6 In the present case, the initial presentation strongly suggested cancer-associated stroke; however, no primary malignancy was identified despite extensive systemic evaluation. This course highlights the diagnostic difficulty of occult malignancy in patients with cryptogenic stroke and marked coagulation abnormalities.5,6 Elevated D-dimer levels have been reported to be a useful clue for detecting occult cancer in patients with acute ischemic stroke.6

The negative result for anti-cardiolipin and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies were clinically important because antiphospholipid syndrome is an important differential diagnosis in younger patients with unexplained arterial or venous thrombosis.7 In this case, these findings supported a malignancy-associated hypercoagulable state, rather than an autoimmune thrombotic disorder.7

The management of Trousseau's syndrome requires both anticoagulation treatment and effective treatment of the underlying malignancy.1,2,8 Although heparin-based anticoagulation has traditionally been the standard approach, direct oral anticoagulants are increasingly used in selected patients with cancer-associated thrombosis.8

Compared with gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas, prostate cancer is a relatively uncommon cause of Trousseau's syndrome 1, 2, 3, 4. Nevertheless, even advanced metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer may respond favorably to systemic therapy.9 In previously reported cases of prostate cancer-associated Trousseau's syndrome, thromboembolic events generally occurred in the setting of advanced disease and high tumor burden.3,4

In the present case, prostate-specific antigen and D-dimer levels declined in parallel after treatment with apalutamide plus degarelix, and no further thrombotic events occurred after the early treatment phase (Fig. 4). This course suggests that long-term control of Trousseau's syndrome depends not only on anticoagulation but also on adequate suppression of tumor activity.1,8

Although prostate cancer is a relatively uncommon cause of Trousseau's syndrome, it should be considered in male patients with cryptogenic stroke, markedly elevated D-dimer levels, and no obvious embolic source, even in relatively young individuals 3, 4, 5, 6.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of Trousseau's syndrome caused by advanced prostate cancer initially presenting as multiple cerebral infarctions. Prostate cancer should be considered as a potential underlying malignancy in male patients with cryptogenic stroke. Prompt cancer-directed therapy, in addition to anticoagulation, may be essential for long-term control of malignancy-associated hypercoagulability.
