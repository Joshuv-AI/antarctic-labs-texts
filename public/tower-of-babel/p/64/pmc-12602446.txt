Resurgent COVID‐19 Wave in India and South Asia: Is the NB.1.8.1 Variant a Renewed Global Health Concern?
Authors: Abir Hasan Pranto, Anika Tabassum Promi, Salsabil Islam, Md Rabiul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe re‐emergence of COVID‐19 in India and South Asia, driven by the Omicron variant (NB. 1.8.1), poses an urgent challenge to global pandemic preparedness amid waning citizen awareness and reduced monitoring. This review aims to examine the variant′s characteristics, assess its public health impact, and evaluate the regional and global responses to this resurgence. Here, we examine the characteristics of the NB.1.8.1 outbreak, the magnitude of its effect on public health, and analyze regional and global responses.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive literature search across important databases to extract information, followed by concentrating on recent insights regarding the dissemination and therapeutic implications of SARS‐CoV‐2 NB.1.8.1 variant from PubMed, Nature, and The Lancet Infectious Diseases, and genomic surveillance tools including GISAID and Nextstrain in detail.ResultsSARS‐CoV‐2 NB.1.8.1 variant showed mutations associated with higher transmissibility and evasion by immunity, which prompt fears among individuals with weakened immune system and poor uptake of the booster vaccine. The phenomenon of the emergence of this variant has resulted in more cases of infections and hospitalizations in South Asia, especially in the densely populated and poorly resourced regions. Although the actual deaths are not high, there is a threat of a faster spread and an overwhelmed health system. The reactive measures put in place by India and other neighboring countries include renewing vaccination campaigns, wearing masks, and genomic surveillance. However, there is still little response at the international level, partly due to the uncertainty of disease severity and low mortality rates by this variant.ConclusionThe article points to the relevance of novel therapeutics, a fast tool of diagnostics, and compliance with preventive measures among the population. Timely global cooperation, active communication, and responsive health‐related mechanisms are required to tackle the current wave and avoid further surges in the future.Keywords: COVID‐19, infectious disease, NB.1.8.1 variant, pandemic, public health, SARS‐CoV‐2

Background

The current outbreak of COVID‐19 in India and South Asia, caused by the Omicron subvariant NB.1.8.1, heralds a transitional point in the world as the pandemic emergency turns into an endemic management scenario. The sharp increase in the number of infections with the coronavirus confirms the constant instability of the development of SARS‐CoV‐2, as several countries have already lifted control measures and reduced medical responsibilities. As the world faces subsequent vaccination campaigns and waves that develop partial immunity in the population, the impact of novel variants with different mutations causes difficulty in containing them and providing healthcare resilience [1]. The outbreak that is in question has emerged under the circumstances when control over chronic illnesses, climate‐related infections, and antimicrobial resistance has turned into a top priority for the whole world. However, the emergence of NB.1.8.1 teaches us important lessons: new versions of familiar pathogens can figure out holes in protective immunity, challenge the performance of vaccines in circulation, and overwhelm health systems lacking financial resources, particularly in overcrowded settings such as South Asia.

South Asia is highly susceptible to COVID‐19 because of its densely populated environment, weakened healthcare infrastructure, and limited resources [2, 3]. The spread of the virus was enhanced by limited health infrastructure, poor testing capability, and, even more, delayed responses by the government [4, 5]. Technically, strict lockdowns were aimed at curbing the spread, but these usually did not line up with the epidemiological patterns and resulted in gross breakdowns on the economic front without controlling the number of cases [6, 7]. Conversely, adaptive and focused strategies worked better. The socioeconomic inequality of the region, the overcrowding of the cities, and the lack of preparedness against the pandemic resulted as a combination in the higher vulnerability of the region to massive outbreaks and adverse health outcomes [8]. This outbreak is notable not only in the local effects but also in its potential to shape health safety in the global arena. South Asia has significantly dense urbanized, inter‐global clinics, and unbalanced medical facilities, which may influence it as an escalation belt since viral outbreaks are reinforced. Additionally, minimized genomic surveillance in most countries increases the chances of a slow response regarding the emergence of subvariants. Therefore, this is not a rare occasion, and the outbreak is a manifestation of the greater hazards of the post‐pandemic transition period. It requires new international collaboration, active pandemic surveillance, and sustainable pandemic preparedness.

Methodology

The current study conducted a multidisciplinary review of literature and up‐to‐date epidemiological statistics. We searched the databases PubMed, Nature, and The Lancet Infectious Diseases, and genomic surveillance tools including GISAID and Nextstrain. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), were revised along with the one provided by the Indian Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW). They were searched using the terms such as NB.1.8.1 variant, COVID resurgence South Asia, Omicron subvariant India, and public health response 2025. Epidemiological trends were evaluated with the help of information published on the Internet sites, including Our World in Data or COVID‐related dashboards in India. Also, the following media resources, the Hindu, Scroll, and Al‐Jazeera, were used to identify the sociopolitical and health policy reactions. NB.1.8.1‐relevant genomic information was further matched with outbreak.info [9] and previous WHO reports. Preprint articles and professional remarks were assessed to learn about the lineage development and response tactics.

Historical Perspective of NB.1.8.1 Variant

NB.1.8.1 is a sublineage of the Omicron lineage, theorized to be divergent with the BA.2.86‐like cluster in early 2025 [10]. The first clinically documented emergence of it occurred at the end of March 2025 by genomic surveillance in southern India. Within 3 months by April, it had become the most prevalent circulating strain in some spots in India and an adjoining country, Bangladesh, which has shown high rates of community circulation.

The NB.1.8.1 variant was a descendant lineage of XDV, a recombinant lineage that originated in two SARS‐CoV‐2 lineages, XDE (a recombinant of GW.5.1 and FL.13.4, both derived from XBB) and JN.1. NB.1.8.1 is a late‐generation Omicron variant that carries this complex recombination. It is the sequel to JN.1 and its subvariants, KP.3, KP.3.1.1, and LP.8.1 [11]. NB.1.8.1 gained seven spike protein mutations and 23 non‐spike mutations relative to JN. NB.1.8.1 has four major substitutions compared to the spike of XEC (another JN.1‐based recombinant), G184S, K478I, A435S, and L1104V, which make it more infectious [11]. NB.1.8.1 was demonstrated to have 1.17 (95 percent credible interval, 0.83‐1.66) fold‐increased reproduction number (Re) compared with LP.8.1 based on Bayesian modeling of genome surveillance data in Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia, and the USA. This was suggestive of increased transmission. The pseudo‐virus shows 2.5‐fold greater infectivity than LP.8.1, whereas the antibody escape was equivalent among neutralization assays [11]. Thereby, NB.1.8.1 probably appeared and was disseminated because of its higher infectivity and ACE2 binding capacity and not because of a significantly greater immune escape, although its widespread mutations, including non‐spike, might determine its further evolution and should be examined closely in virological research [11].

The Omicron variant is quite different compared to the past variants of SARS‐CoV‐2 in terms of transmissibility and disease severity, as well as the symptoms of the disease. Omicron proved to be highly infectious, quickly displacing the dominant one during the wave in Eastern India. Nevertheless, it mostly led to less serious short‐term disease, and people were not hospitalized and died much less in comparison with Delta. Nonetheless, the research found that there is a sizable burden of Long COVID symptoms, including fatigue, breathlessness, and cognitive problems, even following mild or moderate infections. The multiple mutations of the spike protein caused Omicron to evade part of the immune response, which resulted in reinfections and breakthrough infections, even in vaccinated or naturally immunized patients. The dynamic nature of this variant, its rapid circulation, and post‐acute consequences created new barriers to the pandemic process and recovery. Accordingly, Omicron was a new stage of the pandemic, not as dangerous in its effects, but powerful in the long‐term perspective [12].

NB.1.8.1 is a novel variant of SARS‐CoV‐2 with huge transmission capability since it was a balanced mixture of a high ACE2‐binding affinity and moderate escape of the immune system (Figure 1). It is a relative of the XDV.1.5.1 lineage and harbors significant mutations in spike protein Gln493Glu, Ala435Ser, and Lys478Ile [13]. The receptor‐binding domain (RBD) of NB.1.8.1 subtype is most affine to human ACE2 among the variants isolated, and this is one of the factors that makes the variant infectious. It manifested strong infectivity in pseudo‐virus assays (although not as high as that of LP.8.1.1). Neutralization experiments with human plasma showed that NB.1.8.1 induces a 1.51.62‐fold decrease in neutralization titters relative to LP.8.1.1 and signal of stronger humoral immune escape than BA.3.2 but less extreme [13]. It is also not susceptible to several neutralizing classes of monoclonal antibodies, especially because of the K478I and A435S mutations that have made the difference in antibody binding. Overall, the high ACE2 binding capability and low immune escape of NB.1.8.1 indicate that the variant is positioned to displace other lineages and cause future outbreaks of COVID‐19 [13].

Transmission of NB 1.8.1 Sub variant of SARS‐CoV‐2.

In contrast to its predecessors, however, NB.1.8.1 was introduced under post‐pandemic conditions when booster participation had softened and when the general social load on the public had changed. This formed an ideal situation in silence, especially in city slums, transportation hubs, and health centers, where infection control measures were reduced. The variant made its way to South Asia by the middle of May and helped drive a significant increase in hospitalizations, particularly in the older populations with compromised immunity and unvaccinated people. Localized outbreaks were reported in Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, which in some cases were caused by a lack of existing hospital beds and a low uptake of boosters. Evidence Epidemiologists regard NB.1.8.1 as part of an increasing phenomenon of variant persistence in which the virus can evolve at a slow rate but still has a major enough difference to avoid existing immunity. The phenomenon has echoes of the endemic nature of influenza, i.e., without new vaccines and continued genomic surveillance, the waves of the COVID‐19 virus could become a yearly or a half‐yearly occurrence [14, 15].

Is There Any Reason for Public Health Threat by NB.1.8.1 Variant?

NB.1.8.1 is the variant that draws particular attention to the field of public health because of its genetic mutations, high transmissibility, and the likelihood of providing partial immune escape. It has many more mutations in the spike surface protein, compared with other variants of Omicron seen earlier, and some of them have been previously reported to confer an increased viral fitness.

Genetic Mutations

NB.1.8.1 was probably associated with convergent mutations in the spike protein, such as F486P, N460K, R346T, K444T/M, and E484A/R, which were observed in the related subvariants of XBB, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.16, and XBB.1.17 [16]. These mutations are found to: Improve the affinity of the ACE2 receptors; Escape class I–IV neutralizing antibodies and lessen protection secondary to vaccination or exposure to the infection; Conduct changes to critical antigenic residues of both the receptor‐binding domain (RBD) and NTD, and escape monoclonal antibody therapeutics. These mutations1 increase the resistance of NB.1.8.1 against the current immune defenses and to be neutralized by earlier mAb treatments [16].

Transmission Rate

The F486P, N460K, and R346T are mutations related to increased transmissibility and improved viral fitness, particularly in the face of immune pressure. Its parent lineage (e.g., XBB.1.5) already possesses a demonstrated ability to spread more globally (in need of confirmation), and NB.1.8.1 probably carries over the same, or improves it further [16]. The variant can become very contagious among vaccinated or previously infected people, like Omicron BA.1 and XBB.1.5, which rapidly displaced previous strains [16].

Clinical Features

Although there may still be a lack of hard clinical data on the NB.1.8.1, previous similar mutation patterns (such as BQ.1.1 and XBB.1.5) had exhibited breakthrough infections even among the triple‐vaccinated, and potentially less severe symptoms, but increased reinfection and breakthrough symptomatic rates. Cases of immune‐compromised patients facing prolonged infection or the failure of therapy caused by monoclonal antibodies escaping are worrying [16]. The impact of severe disease can be even more severe, but their reinfection, depleted vaccination‐induced immunity, and immune evasion pose significant clinical risks.

Mortality

There is so far no indication that NB.1.8.1 causes mortality rates to rise markedly when compared to previous Omicron subvariants. Nevertheless, high transmission may cause more infections and, as a result, more absolute deaths in the vulnerable groups. Immune escape and the lowered efficacy of monoclonal antibodies can result in a poorer outcome in high‐risk groups, and the possibility of overwhelming healthcare systems indirectly increases death rates. Although they may be mild in nature, extensive spread and immune evasion may end up causing excess deaths, particularly in low booster coverage or healthcare access [16]. Although NB.1.8.1 does not seem to be more deadly than its predecessors, its immune escape capabilities and rapid transmission render it a serious threat to public health at large, especially in communities with gaps in the immune system or pressure on the health services.

The Present Responses Against This Disease Outbreak

Indian and South Asian Response

India has started an all‐out plan to counter the NB.1.8.1‐induced explosion. The Integrated Disease Surveillance Program (IDSP) units, whose real‐time tracking of cases was revived by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), were reinforced. Airport advisories, hospital, and gathering advisories of the major cities in India, like Delhi, Mumbai, and Chennai, were reimposed against the use of masks [17].

The booster vaccination campaigns, mostly with updated Omicron‐adapted mRNA and protein subunit vaccines, were started amongst individuals in frontline services, the elderly, and individuals with weakened immune systems. The Serum Institute of India increased the pace of its manufacturing of newer formulations of Covovax, and awareness measures were done together with local NGOs. Some countries additionally implemented border screenings, hospital triage standards, and community closure within the high‐risk regions, such as Dhaka and Kathmandu. Nevertheless, a scant supply of antivirals paired with minimal use of boosters and the availability of resources have not enabled a substantial response across these countries. PPE, oxygen, and testing kits have been offered through the initiation of Public‐private partnerships. Telemedicine platforms were reopened to decongest the hospitals, and mobile health units were used to bridge the access system in rural areas. Even then, vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, and infrastructural weakness persist as barriers to the containment process [18].

Response of Global Health Authorities

NB.1.8.1 has been categorized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a variant under monitoring, and a technical guidance requesting member nations to increase genomic sequencing capacity and recover test‐related infrastructure has been issued [19]. The reaction is very cautious since there is little evidence of severity. The slow delivery schedule of international funding, bottlenecks in logistics of containment, and divergence of national priorities can impede prompt containment unless there is increased liaison globally.

Risk Assessment for Global Public Health

The outbreak of NB.1.8.1 is now a threatening situation both regionally and globally, especially in a world that is yet to stabilize out of the pandemic. Several of them are interconnected and increase the possibility of a threat: I. Immunity Gaps: Most low‐booster South Asian populations have incomplete protection, because they caught other versions of the coronavirus before the Omicron variant. In a situation where NB.1.8.1 can evade immunity, the risk of reinfection is increasing. Citizens of other parts of the world with weakened immune systems can be at risk as well. II. Healthcare Fragility: Weaker countries such as Bangladesh, Nepal, or some regions of India will experience system defects in health care provision, such as insufficient intensive care beds, oxygen availability, and poorly equipped rural facilities. A slight increment in severe cases can flood the facilities and intensify death. III. Population Density and Mobility: Cities in South Asia boast of some of the densely populated cities in the world. A high level of cross‐border mobility, both locally and internationally, provides fertile ground for the spread of the virus very fast. IV. Global Connectivity: Large cities of India are the world's connectors. The local outbreak can be a transcontinental one within several days, particularly when the detection and quarantine action is uneven across the borders. V. Genomic Surveillance Deficits: The erosion of COVID‐specific surveillance around the world implies that the emergence of new variants can remain unnoticed for weeks before they are identified. This late detection gives the viral mutations time to spread and makes it even harder to contain.

VI. Variant Evolution: NB.1.8.1 might be a precursor of more hazardous mutations, especially in spreading uncontrolled immunocompromised populations, where the evolution of viruses is known to speed up.

To conclude, although NB.1.8.1 does not directly cause catastrophic risk on the global level, it highlights the weaknesses in the global preparedness against pandemics. Unless a crisis is brought under control promptly and data shared, there is a possibility that the outbreak may overwhelm the health systems, give rise to misinformation, and drive people back against health governance.

Preventive and Treatment Methods

When it comes to NB.1.8.1, the countries need to implement a layered approach that includes vaccination, antiviral, testing, and community‐wide social health measures (Figure 2). New vaccines are based on Omicron derivatives whose results, in preliminary in vitro testing, are promising in generating neutralizing antibodies against NB.1.8.1. To ensure rapid scale‐up of booster campaigns against the above set of vaccines is crucial, particularly amongst the groups at high risk [20]. Therapies like Paxlovid and Molnupiravir are still effective despite the current variant, but it would be necessary to watch their usage because this will lead to resistance. The diagnostic kits should be modified so that they have high sensitivity to the mutations of NB.1.8.1. Early isolation and treatment can be achieved through widespread testing, particularly in symptomatically ill and high‐contact patients [21]. The pattern of public communication needs to be revitalized as a means of defeating misinformation and vaccine fatigue. An individual responsibility, i.e., masking, ventilation, and self‐isolation, is of importance.

Conclusion

The new coronavirus wave in South Asia, fueled by a mutation known as NB.1.8.1, is one more confirmation that viral evolution is still a global problem of health in the post‐pandemic transition period. The transmissibility, as well as partial immune escape, of this variant, and the weak healthcare systems of South Asia, require both immediate and thought‐out measures. The outbreak is not yet a global disaster, but given the risk posed in the region, its spread can be expected to be even greater due to the complacency of the global community. Enhanced surveillance, expedited use of boosted vaccination, and international cooperation are the most important tools of containment. The One Health approach provides a long‐term, viable solution, especially in resource‐limited contexts. To respond to the titular question: Yes, NB.1.8.1 might turn into a revived worldwide issue one more time, just in case the potential to contain the problem is squandered. Interventions should be both proactive and cooperative so that it does not become a global crisis.
