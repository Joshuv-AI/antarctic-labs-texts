Phenomenological research in the field of Infant Mental Health and Early Childhood (IECMH) ‐A mapping review
Authors: Minna Sorsa, Bente Dahl, Idun Røseth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfant Mental Health and Early Childhood (IECMH) is a field of study of infants and the developing relationship and the optimal development between infants and their caregivers. Phenomenological research within the well‐being of infants and caregivers has core importance in the comprehension of the subjectivity of the infant and the attachment relationship. We aimed to explore and analyze, in a mapping review, how phenomenology has been used in IECMH research. The mapping review is a rigorous and systematic approach to identify and categorize the quantity of available evidence on a particular topic. We conducted a wide systematic review, including original articles globally and without a time limit. A total of 780 records were identified, and 167 original studies were analyzed inductively with content analysis. The studies present global variation and diverse phenomenological traditions. We identified that mothers and infants are mostly studied, whereas the father and extended family perspectives have remained scarce. Many studies lacked clear grounding in phenomenological philosophy or showed inconsistencies between method and phenomenological principles, underscoring the need for philosophically and methodologically coherent research. Phenomenological insights may deepen and enrich the relationships between professionals, caregivers, and infants.Keywords: child, infant mental health, mapping review, mother, phenomenology

INTRODUCTION

The field of Infant and Early Childhood Mental Health (IECMH) brings together multiple disciplines concerned with the relational and developmental well‐being of infants and their caregivers. Within this field, phenomenological studies have offered valuable ways of accessing subjectivity and intersubjectivity beyond what can be captured through behavioral or biomedical frameworks. However, there is currently limited knowledge about how phenomenology has been applied in IECMH research and which areas remain underexplored.

Phenomenological research, broadly defined, is a qualitative approach that explores phenomena as they are lived, both individually and in relation to others. It may draw on participants’ own accounts as well as observations and written texts and seeks to describe or interpret the meanings of these lived experiences. This approach is particularly valuable as it allows researchers to access and understand phenomena from the perspective of those directly involved. In the context of IECMH, phenomenology provides unique insights into the lived experiences of infants and caregivers and their interactions, capturing subtleties of emotion and relational dynamics. Phenomenology can deepen our understanding of how phenomena are perceived, felt, and interpreted, which can inform both theory and clinical practice. Phenomenological researchers are, therefore, inherently aligned with the IECMH stance in that they don't assume they know, instead, they use an open, empathic, and reflective stance to access, understand, and describe meanings inherent in lived experiences from the perspective of those directly involved with the phenomenon.

Statement of relevance to infant and early childhood mental health

For IECMH practitioners, this review demonstrates how phenomenology can deepen understanding of the lived experience of caregiving and illuminate cultural meanings that shape early relational health. The findings reveal that although phenomenological methods are well suited to exploring intersubjectivity and empathy, they remain underutilized in studies of attachment and caregiver–child interaction. Expanding phenomenological inquiry in these areas can strengthen reflective practice, inform culturally responsive interventions, and advance relationship‐based care in IECMH.

The IECMH field

IECMH is the field of study concerned with infants’ emotional and social development, with particular emphasis on the evolving relationship between infants and their caregivers, as well as the conditions that support optimal development (Canin, 2023; Weatherston & Ribaudo, 2020). It focuses on the social and emotional well‐being of infants and their caregivers (Fitzgerald et al., 2011). Central to the IECMH perspective is the understanding of infant's behavior as expressions of their inner subjective experiences and that effective support requires attentiveness to the emotional states of both the child and the caregivers (Keren, 2022). Intersubjectivity in the parent‐infant relationship refers to the mutual sharing and coordination of emotions, intentions, and attention between the infant and caregiver (Balbernie, 2007; Trevarthen, 1979). The caregiver's prior experiences with relationships and trauma impact their ability to form a relationship and interact with infants (Canin, 2023).

Attachment Relationship (AR) is a key construct for understanding how interactions between infants and their caregivers shape their emotional and developmental lives. Attachment implies a strong bond between a child and their caregiver, often emerging early in life (Ainsworth, 1979; Bowlby, 1969). Attachment involves interactions that reflect the affective quality and organizational patterns of the relationship (Ainsworth, 1979; Letourneau et al., 2015). Sensitive caregiving behavior involves the ability to notice, interpret, and quickly respond to a child's signals of needs, allowing secure child–caregiver attachment (Madigan et al., 2024). Previous studies describe mental representations of the AR that may be classified as secure, insecure, or disorganized, reflecting the infant's sense of safety and support (Ainsworth, 1979; Letourneau et al., 2015; Madigan et al., 2024; Main & Solomon, 1986; O'Rourke, 2022).

The IECMH field contains areas related to the perinatal period, within specific cultural and family settings, and in multidisciplinary professional settings. The IECMH field comprises practitioners from various scientific and professional backgrounds, such as health, education, social work, psychology, nursing, pediatrics, and psychiatry, who collaborate with families to support the optimal development of infants (Fitzgerald et al., 2011; Weatherston & Ribaudo, 2020). The support of families may be offered as promotion or prevention during pregnancy, with the goal of supporting caregivers in developing confidence in their capacity to understand and meet the needs of the infant (Weatherston & Ribaudo, 2020). Supporting families may also be provided in therapeutic encounters, interventions, and intensive treatment, with the overall aim of improving the growth environment of the baby (Letourneau et al., 2015; Weatherston & Ribaudo, 2020). Positive parent‐infant relationships can be fostered when both maternal and paternal states of mind are supported, along with their ability to read and understand the infant (Canin, 2023). Such sensitive support, for example, can be provided at the NICU or in baby‐toddler intensive treatment, supporting emotional containment and reciprocity.

Caregiver sensitivity and reflective functioning are regarded as essential antecedents to infant attachment security (Letourneau et al., 2015). Promoting parental mental health during the perinatal period is important so that it helps expectant parents to view reality from the perspective of the infant. The first year of an infant's life is a critical time for development, as early experiences and the first years of life lay the foundations for essential life skills, such as the development of resilience. High caregiver resilience reduces mental health problems (Hajure et al., 2024); therefore, interventions that strengthen parents' resilience are valuable for supporting infant wellbeing. Resilience‐enhancing interventions have also been shown to reduce depressive symptoms in pregnant women both before and after childbirth (Walker et al., 2022), which is important considering that the mean global prevalence of perinatal depression, antenatal depression, and postnatal depression is 26.3%–28.5% (Al‐abri et al., 2023). The other common mental health problems in perinatal mothers are anxiety and post‐traumatic stress disorder (Hajure et al., 2024). IECMH deals with supporting vulnerable parents in their role.

The area of work with baby families, particularly the collaboration with vulnerable families, is very demanding, which is why reflective supervision is often a part of the work of professional skills development in IECMH (Canin, 2023; Weatherston & Ribaudo, 2020). The core skills in IECMH interventions involve building alliances and giving emotional support, supporting coping in daily life, facilitating social support, and offering counseling on basic needs, and promoting the infant's growth and development.

There is an imbedded emphasis on understanding cultural differences and their impact on infant development and parental practices (Bornstein, 2013; Fitzgerald, 2006). Cultures internalize familial values and everyday practices of families, since societal beliefs, values, and attitudes impact child rearing, caregiver‐infant interactions, and the infant's construction of sense of self and self‐identity (Bornstein, 2013; Fitzgerald, 2006). There are universal features across cultures: many developmental milestones, parenting strategies, family processes, how children feel loved, and the mechanisms through which parents likely influence children (Bornstein, 2013). The universal application of attachment theory is linked with a need to develop culturally competent practices that respect diverse developmental contexts and address the ethical concerns (O'Rourke, 2022). Consequently, studies conducted within different social‐cultural settings are essential, and research within early development should reflect the diversity of the world's population and cultures (Fitzgerald, 2006; Tomlinson & Swartz, 2003). There are existing and known cultural differences in caregiving, yet comparisons across cultures are still scarce (Bornstein, 2013). There is a cultural imbalance in the knowledge about infancy, since in previous reviews North America and Europe dominated the research within the field of IECMH (Tomlinson & Swartz, 2003). The imbalance of cultural perspectives may lead to misinterpretations of the nature of infancy, as well as the rearing practices and interventions. The basic misunderstanding may be due to a lack of engagement and knowledge. For example, psychoanalytically oriented authors have commented on the societal resistance to the idea that premature infants possess a mind and experience physical as well as psychological pain (Canin, 2023).

In a recent review, Canin (2023) examined English psychoanalytic journal articles on premature birth and infancy over the past 25 years and identified only 28 research papers addressing the therapeutic imperative of interacting with the infant as a person. She specially focused on the experiences of infants, parents, therapists or observers, the staff at NICU, and the parent‐infant relationship. Reviews of perinatal mental health interventions in Western countries have highlighted the heterogeneity of approaches in the field, and the complexity of multifaceted intervention programs makes outcome comparisons challenging (Newton et al., 2022). Tsivos et al. (2015) highlighted the poverty of interventions assessing outcomes for both parents and infants. The underrepresented groups in IECMH research are ethnic minorities and families in low‐income countries (Tsivos et al., 2015).

Tomlinson & Swartz (2003) recommend conducting reviews of research methodologies in the field of IECMH from a global perspective. When screening qualitative reviews and syntheses in the field of IECMH, we found no methodologically oriented qualitative reviews, indicating a significant gap in the existing research. Although numerous phenomenological studies have been conducted in IECMH, no review has systematically mapped this work to identify knowledge gaps.

Phenomenology as a research method

Our interest lies in qualitative research methodologies, which aim to study individual subjectivity and social phenomena in context by placing participants’ perceptions, feelings, and experiences at the center of inquiry (Liamputtong et al., 2023). Qualitative methods (e.g., interviews, observations, or diverse analysis methods) must be linked to specific theoretical approaches, which frame how the phenomena under study are interpreted and understood (Green et al., 2025). A challenge is that qualitative research methods draw on diverse methodological and theoretical traditions, each emphasizing different phenomena and contexts, while also employing similar concepts, such as subjective experience, but with different meanings and intentionality's.

A core insight in phenomenological philosophy is that consciousness is intentional, meaning that it is always directed towards something other than itself (Husserl, 1977). Our embodied consciousness is not a physical object that has measurable dimensions like depth and width, but the medium through which we experience the world and actively constitute its meaning. Phenomenology stresses that knowing the other is a dialogical, embodied process in which knowledge is co‐constituted through the researcher–participant interaction; our bodily presence, reactions, and intentions help shape the meanings that emerge (Røseth, 2025). Through empathy, we can bring the other's subjectivity into view (Stein, 1989).

Phenomenology consists of different methods that spring from philosophy, functioning both as a philosophical tradition and as a set of related but distinct methodological approaches. Edmund Husserl (1990) is widely regarded as the founder of both phenomenological philosophy and the phenomenological method. Students and followers of Edmund Husserl expanded upon and, in some cases diverged, from his foundational ideas. As a result, phenomenology today encompasses various strands, including the phenomenology of Heidegger (2010), Merleau‐Ponty (2012), de Beauvoir (2011), and Sartre (1993). Psychologist Amedeo Giorgi (2009) was the first to introduce Husserlian phenomenology into psychology and was a pioneer in developing qualitative research methods within the human sciences. Since then, several phenomenological methods have emerged, such as van Manen's phenomenological method (van Manen, 1990) and Dahlberg's reflective lifeworld approach (Dahlberg et al., 2008), each drawing on or integrating different branches of phenomenological philosophy.

Within phenomenological research in IECMH, it remains unclear which populations, phenomena, and phenomenological methods have been studied or remain underutilized, and which areas require further research. This study therefore aims to explore and analyze the application of phenomenology in infant mental health research and to identify existing research gaps.

METHODS

Design

In this mapping review, we focus specifically on phenomenological studies, which explore how phenomena are constituted and lived. We will map research contexts, participant characteristics, research questions, and the phenomena studied. We will examine the methods used, assess the clarity of theoretical or philosophical foundations, and evaluate the alignment between methods and their stated theoretical bases, the methodological rigor, and the consistency with phenomenological theory. The term “mapping review” has its origins in the natural sciences and was adopted by the Evidence for Policy and Practice Information and Co‐ordinating Centre (EPPI‐Centre) in an early publication (Grant & Booth, 2009). A Mapping review is a suitable method when one aims to gain an overview of a larger research topic or identify research gaps within the topic. The method is often used as a first step in a systematic review, and it creates an overview of a specific area of research.

The mapping review is a rigorous and systematic approach to identify or “map out” and categorize the quantity of available evidence on a particular topic. The method has considerable overlap with scoping reviews, but the focus of a mapping review is often broader than that of a scoping review, and a scoping review may focus on a specific and detailed research question in depth. As the mapping review uses predefined codes to extract descriptive information, the method is considered to follow an “aggregative” logic. This implies that findings or data from multiple sources are systematically collected and synthesized to build a comprehensive understanding of the topic, providing an overview of the existing evidence base rather than developing deep insight or generating new theories. This typically involves systematic collection of data, categorization and organization of data, and combining and summarizing the findings without significant reinterpretation, focusing on breadth, not depth.

The search for literature is rigorous and systematic, and clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria are required. Predefined codes are often applied, and the review is often accompanied by tables and graphs (Campbell et al., 2023). According to Campbell et al. (2023, p 5), these reviews aim to answer questions like “What do we know about a topic?” or “What and Where research exist on a particular area”. Others have described a mapping review as a review that involves a literature search to find out what sort of studies have been undertaken to address the review question and to locate where they have been published and in what databases they have been indexed. Further, it involves exploring what sort of outcomes they have assessed and in which populations (Petticrew & Roberts, 2006).

Since the aim of the mapping review is concerned with mapping the breadth and scope of existing research rather than critically evaluating the rigor or reliability of the literature, it is not common to perform a detailed quality assessment of the included studies. Mapping reviews have an exploratory nature; they are not designed to synthesize findings or develop policy recommendations. In the analysis and synthesis stage, the mapping review focuses on a visual synthesis of the data, which are often graphical and tabular. Furthermore, the analysis often includes characteristics and key features of the included literature that are relevant to the review questions (Grant & Booth, 2009).

The literature describes various steps in the process of conducting a mapping review (Christou et al., 2025; Fernández‐Sotos et al., 2018). Inspired by Christou et al.’s proposed steps for conducting mapping and scoping reviews, the following 7 steps guided the process in this review (Table 1).

Search strategy

We planned a search with an information specialist in February‐March 2023, using a wide set of concepts related to the study in interest in the search. After reviewing the results in Rayyan software, we decided to redo the search by restricting the concepts to those that would be more vital for the aim of our study. The search was completed on 28.2.2023 in Cinahl Complete, Psychinfo (OVID), SCOPUS, Web of Science by searching the title, abstract, and keywords and by combining words:

infant*or toddler* or young children or baby or newborn or neonate or pregnancy or (parent‐infant, mother‐infant, or father‐infant) interaction or attachment

and

and

infant mental health

In Web of Science, we additionally used the topic searches. For “infant mental health,” we extended the search in PsycInfo and Web of Science to all fields and in Cinahl to all text. Additionally, we asked an information specialist to use the same words to search the Maternity and Infant Care database. Next, a PRISMA diagram was created. PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) is an evidence‐based guideline that provides a standardized framework to ensure transparent, complete, and reproducible reporting of systematic reviews and meta‐analyses. We adhered to the PRISMA 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) and used its flow diagram (Figure 1) to ensure transparency and completeness throughout the review process. Because our initial searches—both by the information specialist and our own—missed some key articles in the field, we expanded the search to include relevant and well‐known references (Figure 1). All studies were subjected to the same review process and criteria.

Inclusion‐ and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table 2. We used an age limit from pregnancy to 5 years, which is the age used, for example, by international organizations focusing on infancy. We included references when the phenomenon occurred during pregnancy to 5 years, for example, studies related to attachment, relationships, connections, or interaction. Our interest included mother, father, parent, caregiver, and the extended family with any combination describing interaction related to infant mental health, as well as bereavement related to the death of a child. We required that in all cases of maternal or paternal studies, there had to be a link to infants within the study to be included. Any studies had to utilize a phenomenological method, also in the case of the study of interventions with parents and/or babies. The exclusion criteria were if the research domain was not aligned with the inclusion criteria, e.g., studies that focused solely on the mother, father or baby without addressing the relationship with infancy. We required that all children in the study be under five, which meant that studies with older children were not included in the review.

Selecting studies

We have used the Prisma diagram (Page et al., 2021) in the process of selecting studies (see Figure 1). In total the databases and manual search yielded 780 references. Doubles were removed automatically in EndNote by one of the authors, and in the next step we used the reference management system Rayyan software to identify sources without an English or Scandinavian language text by one author. Rayyan is an online platform designed to facilitate efficient screening and selection of studies for systematic reviews. Two authors then screened and blinded the titles and abstracts for inclusion. Discrepancies were solved in the study group. Additionally, we identified literature from other sources, such as references of the included articles. The additional articles went through the same process, and we added seven references at this stage of the study.

We checked the full texts of 283 studies so that the inclusion and exclusion criteria were met.

We shared the studies so that all authors searched and coded a third of the studies using the protocol we had developed (available as supplemental material). The full texts were used to identify the age of the children and whether a phenomenological method reference was used.

Analysis

Technically the analysis began in the reference management system Rayyan, where we worked inductively, continuously developing and adding a list of codes for mapping the results. In the next stage, Rayyan data with labels and comments were exported into Excel OneDrive (= Outlook). The list of categories has been included as supplemental material. The protocol was refined throughout the process.

RESULTS

The research question is to explore and analyze, how phenomenology has been used in infant mental health research, and to identify research gaps. We will first present the background of the studies, then review the type of research questions used, the phenomena and settings and finally present an analysis of the phenomenological methods used and discuss the results by identifying research gaps.

The participants in the studies

The phenomenological research articles of this study include 2471 participants in total, meaning that on average 15, and a median of 12 participants took part in the studies (Table 5). The study participants are presented in Table 5. It is most common to include mothers, pregnant women, or women with past memories of the perinatal time in studies, since 89 studies (53%) have solely women as participants (n = 1266). Two (1%) of the studies include pairs of mothers and babies, since the focus of the study is on the interaction between them. Most studies (126 studies, 75%) in this mapping review within infant mental health have included infants under age one.

The research questions

Most studies use the concept of “exploring” as the aim of the study, it appears in 71 studies (43%), the term “understanding” second most in 51 studies (31%), “describing” appears in 21 studies (13%). Additional verbs are “examining” in 11 studies (7%), “identifying” in 8 studies (5%), “investigating” in 8 studies (5%), and a few mentions with “giving a voice”, “creating meaning patterns”, “raising awareness”, “discovering”, “illuminating”, “making sense”, “analyzing”, and “contributing”. The authors use a rich language in describing the purpose of their study, by “fully capturing their experiences”, “elucidating experiences”, or “seeing phenomena”. The authors also want to “use that knowledge”, “to capture”, “to learn how it is manifest and to identify any factors”, “to report on” study results for a specific purpose for “highlighting the importance of”, or even “how these impact” on the practical level of service organization, as well as for development of systems of care. The ultimate usefulness in their aim is “to inform service transformation”, “to formulate guidelines (based on the results obtained)”, “to provide basic data for the development of perinatal education and nursing interventions”, or “to suggest how these findings can help practitioners develop family‐centered screenings and interventions”. (Table 1).

In half (n = 84, 50%) of the studies the concept “experience” is used to grasp the level of interest in the studies, and “lived experience” in 28 studies (17%), “meaning” in 11 studies (7%), “perceptions” in 8 studies (5%), “perspectives” in 8 studies (5%), “essence” in 7 studies (4%), “expectations” in 6 studies (3%) and “decision‐making” in 7 studies (4%). (Table 1.) The idea was that phenomenological studies could help in detailed comprehension of the phenomena apparent within infant mental health. Especially in non‐western countries, the aim was connected to broader societal contexts and service development.

The phenomena

Altogether 26 studies (16%) directed their study towards aspects of attachment or AR. Most studies (n = 13, 8%) originated from Europe, and five (3%) were from Asia. Most studies on attachment focused on the mother‐child relationship (n = 18, 11%), four studies (2%) on the father‐child relationship, three (2%) had a focus on both parents, and one study on caregivers (0.5%). The various phenomena identified in the included articles were divided into the following categories: caring experiences, the health challenges of women and men, the new parental role, the health challenges of the child, perinatal loss, and interventions.

Caring experiences

Caring experiences were the largest category and described a variety of caring experiences from mothers’, fathers’, parents’ and health care professionals’ (HCP) perspectives (n = 46, 27%). Experiences related to caring for an infant admitted to NICU were described in 10 studies, including parents (n = 7, 4%), mothers’ (n = 2, 1%) and HCPs’ (n = 1, 0.5%). Parents’ experiences of neonates in home care were explored in one study (0.5%), their experiences of caring after stillbirth in another (0.5%). Caring for premature infants/ preterm births (n = 9, 5%) included parents’ (n = 3, 1%), fathers’ (n = 1, 0.5%), women's (n = 2, 1%) and HCPs’ (n = 3, 1%) experiences. Breastfeeding was described in several studies (n = 5, 3%), whereas women's experiences with formula feeding were explored in one study (0.5%). Pregnancy and post‐natal care (n = 5, 3%) were described by women (n = 2, 1%), and in single studies (0.5%) by parents, fathers and midwives. Other topics that were highlighted in this category described mothers’ response to the baby's crying, singing, comforting and separation. Further, socio‐cultural factors, community healthcare and COVID maternity care were described. Finally, some studies elaborated the care relationship, the professional‐parent relationship and partner support.

The health challenges of women and men

The category describing women's health challenges was the second largest category (n = 39, 23%), and most studies explored women's experiences with different mental health challenges (n = 17, 10%). Six studies (4%) described women's experiences with post‐partum depression, postnatal distress (n = 4, 3%), perinatal anxiety (n = 2, 1%), and single studies (0.5%) explored depression in pregnancy, mental health, disturbed maternal affection, stress related to fetal death or invitro‐fertilization treatment decision making. Four studies (3%) explored women's experiences with drug use, three studies (2%) explored birth trauma, and single studies (0.5%) described intimate partner violence or eating disorder. In total 16 of all studies (10%) focused on the experience of men and fathers. A few studies explored depression in pregnancy from parents’ perspectives and single studies (0.5%) post‐partum depression or post‐partum psychosis from fathers’ perspectives. Women's experiences of the COVID pandemic were described in 5 studies (3%). The remaining studies in this category concerned high‐risk pregnancy, infertility, heart disease and C‐section.

The new parental role

Challenges related to the parental role were described in 31 studies (19%). The largest group of studies concerned experiences of motherhood/ mothering/ maternal identity (n = 8, 5%), including low‐income mothers, teenage mothers, adolescent mothers as well as mothers suffering from post‐partum depression or birth trauma and mothers who had given birth to a premature infant or were incarcerated. Studies concerned with fatherhood/ fathering (n = 7, 4%) constituted the second largest group and included fathers’ experiences after stillbirth or fathering a premature infant. The group of studies involving parenthood/ parenting comprised of 4 studies (3%), two (1%) of these described parenting after stillbirth.

The remaining studies in this category were mainly concerned with relationship and bonding, the mother‐child relationship (n = 3, 1%), mother‐infant attachment (n = 2, 1%), or single studies (1%) mothers’ experiences of bonding with a stillborn or the bodily connectedness between mother and child. This category also included studies (n = 3, 1%) on nurses’ experiences of mother‐child bonding and relational trauma as well as first time parents’ experiences of intimate relationship.

The health challenges of the child

The category describing children's health challenges involved 16 studies (10%) included in the data material. In this category we also found studies that described different perspectives, including mothers, parents, fathers, grandparents, children and HCPs.

The studies included a variety of different health challenges, including autism, hearing loss, burn injury, spina bifida, eating problems, diabetes, prematurity, congenital muscular torticollis and physical disabilities. Further, the studies described experiences related to having a critically ill child, or a child who has undergone surgery or being in need of bubble continuous positive airway pressure. One study (0.5%) explored children's experiences with being in a prison environment.

Perinatal loss

A total of 14 studies (8%) included in the review explored a variety of experiences related to perinatal loss (n = 8, 5%), mothers’ experiences of stillbirth (n = 3, 2%) and neonatal death (n = 3, 2%). There were single studies on bereavement and receiving a lethal diagnosis in pregnancy (n = 1, 0.5%). Other studies focused on parents’ experiences of perinatal death (n = 3, 2%), and there were single studies (0.5%) on infant death, receiving a lethal diagnosis in pregnancy, decision‐making during stillbirth, and fathers’ experiences of pregnancy termination.

Interventions

The sixth category included different interventions described in the primary studies (n = 13, 8%). Two studies (1%) were concerned with vulnerability/ high risk pregnancy, the remaining studies described experiences in single studies (0.5%) related to music therapy, baby theatre and toddler groups. There were single studies (0.5%) on experiences concerning use of Marte Meo, Mellow Bumps and Alexander technique, along with experiences related to parenthood programs and motor interventions programs. The use of mindfulness techniques during childbirth was also described.

The settings

The settings described in the data material were organized into 5 categories: primary and secondary healthcare, home, a combination of settings and other settings. The combination of settings refers to several settings within a single study.

Primary healthcare

The category of primary healthcare included 24 studies (14%) and was mainly related to public health nursing/ community health nursing (n = 17, 10%) and community mental health nursing (n = 5, 3%), and single studies (0.5%) from community maternal and health nursing or antenatal classes.

Secondary healthcare

The largest category (n = 64, 38%), secondary healthcare, included hospital settings, such as several studies stating NICU as the setting (n = 25, 15%). Other relatively large groups in this category were maternity hospital (n = 17, 10%) and hospital/ clinic (n = 13, 8%). In addition, the category included small groups of studies stating the setting to be hospital, a mother and baby unit (n = 2, 1%), and single studies (0.5%) from a hospital infant and toddler mental health specialist unit, a rehabilitation center, the specialist drug and alcohol services, additionally private mental healthcare was represented in four studies (2%).

Home

Home was the second largest category describing study settings (n = 39, 23%). These studies mainly focused on recent or lifetime stressors, pregnancy, the birth experience or current illness experiences in everyday life at home.

Combination of settings

The category including combinations of settings included 32 studies (19%). We located combinations of the hospital setting and home settings in eight studies (5%), the hospital in combination with primary healthcare in three studies (2%), diverse settings in combination with hospital in single studies (0.5%): mother and baby unit and home, the pediatric cardiac clinic and home, specialist toddlers’ unit, a burns unit, a medical Center and a specialist outpatient clinic. A variety of combinations were related to NICU hospital settings: home (n = 4, 2%), and single studies (0.5%) from postpartum unit, home‐based neonatal care, and home and community health nursing.

Combinations related to maternity hospital comprised of homes (n = 2, 1%), public health nursing (n = 2, 1%) and outpatient clinic (n = 1, 0.5%). Four studies described a combination of public health nursing with other settings: community mental health (1%) and single studies with homes or special clinics.

Other settings

The remaining settings are social services comprising of five studies (n = 5, 3%), two studies (1%) are from prisons, and a single study setting (0.5%) is formed by online social media networks.

METHODS

Phenomenological methods

We identified studies employing a wide variety of phenomenological methods, including Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) by Smith (Smith & Larkin, 2009), Colaizzi's method (Colaizzi, 1978), van Manen's method (van Manen, 1990), the descriptive phenomenological method by Giorgi (Giorgi, 2009), the lifeworld approach by Dahlberg (Dahlberg et al., 2008), and the hermeneutical phenomenological approach by Lindseth and Norberg (Lindseth & Norberg, 2022). Additional approaches include nursing phenomenological methods (Benner & Benner, 1994; Thomas, 2002), other phenomenological methods (Fleming et al., 2003), phenomenological NOS (not otherwise specified), and other Method. Philosophical and theoretical background and congruence with phenomenological method

Figure 2 shows the theoretical background of various studies. There is significant variation in the theoretical background for the different methods. Some methods, such as Smith and Phen NOS, have a high percentage of studies without a specific theoretical background (50% and 39% respectively). Other methods, such as van Manen and Dahlberg, have a higher percentage of studies grounded in phenomenological philosophy (39% and 80% respectively). Even among studies that specify a theoretical background, there is significant variation in the frameworks employed. Some methods, such as those by van Manen and Dahlberg, are strongly rooted in phenomenological philosophy, as expected given their origins. Other methods, like Smith's IPA and Phen NOS, show a more diverse grounding, incorporating elements from phenomenological and professional theories, or using methods described in qualitative research method handbooks. (Figure 2).

There is significant variation in the congruence between the phenomenological philosophy or theory and the methods used. For instance, Dahlberg's lifeworld approach has 100% congruence, while Other method only has 36%. It is interesting to note that Phen NOS, the most frequently used method in the material, only has 46% congruence. Most of the incongruence lies between the theoretical background and the method used, but in Phen NOS, 8 studies (29%) feature incongruent references within the method section itself.

Methods for data collection and use of software in data analysis

Interviews are the most commonly used data collection method across all ten methods. Out of a total of 167 studies, 138 (83%) used interviews as the data collection method. The studies employed open‐ended, semi‐structured, as well as structured ways of interviewing. Eight studies combined interviews with other data collection methods: with focus groups (n = 3, 2%), with observations (n = 3, 2%), or other type of data such as documents (n = 1, 0.5%). Six studies (4%) collected data solely by focus groups, two studies (1%) used drawings in data collection, and one study (0.5%) used poems. Several data collection tools were most common in type Phenomenological NOS studies (n = 8, 5%) and in studies utilizing Smith (n = 5, 3%). The use of software in analysis varies between methods. Colaizzi, van Manen, and Other method have the highest percentage of studies using software (13%, 13%, and 36% respectively), while Giorgi, Dahlberg, and Lindseth & Norberg have no studies reporting the use of software.

Distribution of phenomenological methods by geographical region

In the European countries, among the 65 studies (39%), most studies had used the methods by Smith (n = 25, 15%), van Manen was used in 7 original studies (4%), and Giorgi and Lindseth & Norberg were each used in five studies (3%). Additionally, nine European studies (5%) used the unspecified phenomenological NOS methods, which were the most popular (n = 9, 5%) among the Northern American 32 studies (19%). Other mostly used studies in North America were van Manen (n = 7, 4%), and Colaizzi (n = 4, 2%). In the studies from Asia, Colaizzi and Smith were popular.

DISCUSSION

We conducted a mapping review using a systematic search in the field of Infant and Early Childhood Mental Health (IECMH). We explored and analyzed how phenomenology has been used in IECMH research in 167 scientific studies, bringing together multiple scientific and professional disciplines across the globe. To our knowledge, such a review has not been conducted previously. We examined the phenomenological research tradition in greater depth to enhance understanding of the strengths and potential of this methodological tradition within IECMH research. Our work calls for broader perspectives that acknowledge cultural diversity and communalities in child‐rearing practices.

Cultural diversity and global outreach

We identified studies with a qualitative focus within the field of IECMH, with phenomenological studies from diverse cultural settings, thus IECMH has a global scope. Our review of phenomenological research methodologies may provide researchers knowledge about the global application of this methodological tradition. In contrast, previous reviews within the field of IECMH, research has been dominated by studies from North America and Europe (Fitzgerald, 2006; Tomlinson & Swartz, 2003). The cultural question is important, as an imbalance in cultural perspectives may lead to misinterpretations on the nature of infant mental health. We identified a broader global outreach than previous studies, as research from Asia, Africa and South America are represented alongside studies from Europe and America (Table 4). However, we did not identify studies making explicit cultural comparisons of caregiving, a scarcity also noted by Bornstein (2013). Furthermore, phenomenological studies may illuminate universal or more general features, such as attachment patterns across cultures. As most of the studies aimed at increasing knowledge and understanding of specific phenomena, with an emphasis on the necessity to study the lived experience, the articles from Asia and Africa connected this aim to a broader societal context, and the possibility of improving services with the help of lived experience.

Mothers the central focus of research

Phenomenological research offers ways of accessing lived experience, subjectivity, and intersubjectivity, and phenomenological research can grasp the lived worlds of infants and caregivers, or service providers. Phenomenological research offers the potential to capture the infant's experience. In her psychoanalytically oriented review, Canin (2023) had identified only 28 research papers addressing the therapeutic stance of interacting with the infant as a person. In our review, it was striking that few studies focused on the mother‐child relationship (n = 3), mother‐infant attachment (n = 2), or mothers’ experiences of bonding with a stillborn (n = 1), and bodily connectedness between mother and child (n = 1).

Research within a phenomenological tradition remains scarce. Most studies in this mapping review within IECMH (126 studies, 75%) included infants under age one. It was also common to focus on pregnancy, or the combined period of pregnancy and the first year of the infant, as 45 studies (27%) addressed this time frame. There were less studies of toddlers aged 1–3, or young children aged 3–5. Since the foundation of mental health begins early, in utero, at birth, it is essential to support these early bonds at the earliest stages of attachment to develop resilience and good mental health. Only two studies included mother‐baby pairs with a total of 20 participants, and no single studies focused solely on the infant perspective. Altogether 26 studies (16%) directed their study towards aspects of attachment or AR. It might be more common to use other types of research traditions in the study of bonding and attachment. The application of attachment theory is predominantly based on Western middle‐class perspectives, which may limit its relevance and applicability to culturally diverse populations and caregiving contexts. Most studies in our review with an attachment focus (n = 13, 8%) originated from Europe, and five (3%) were from Asia. Among studies on attachment, the majority focused on the mother‐child relationship (n = 18, 11%), four (2%) studies on the father‐child relationship, three (2%) on both parents, and one (0,5%) study on caregivers. These findings highlight the need for broader perspectives that acknowledge cultural diversity and on the other hand, communal child‐rearing practices.

Most studies used mothers as participants in the studies (n = 89, 53%), consisting of 1266 women. The majority explored women's experiences with various mental health challenges (n = 17, 10%), with post‐partum depression being the most common. A smaller number of studies explored post‐partum depression and post‐partum psychosis from fathers’ perspectives. The results of our review may reflect results reported in other studies indicating that depression is the most common mental health challenge in the perinatal time (Al‐abri et al., 2023; Hajure et al., 2024). Phenomenological research in other health challenges of mothers and fathers remains limited. However, nearly a quarter of the studies included couples or both mothers and parents as participants. A research gap in regard to men and the father perspective is still evident, as only 15 (7%) studies included men as sole participants in their studies, comprising a total of 151 individuals. These studies addressed both the attachment perspective and the emergence of the new parental role. The extended family, which plays an important role in many cultural settings, was represented by 56 grandparents or other persons close to caregivers in only 7 studies (4%) mainly from non—western countries. The limited research on the extended family in western settings may reflect a traditional focus on mothers as primary caregivers, while it also highlights a limited understanding of the role of fathers, partners, and the extended family in IECMH.

Hospitals as the main setting

We identified a wide range of settings, from early interventions to specialized hospital‐based treatments. It is noteworthy that only 24 studies (14%) were conducted in primary care settings, despite early support being considered the norm in policy contexts. In contrast, phenomenological research has predominantly focused on hospital settings, with 64 studies (38%).

The field of IECMH includes attachment‐based interventions, our results show a heavy emphasis on the caregiver's experiences, and a paucity of intervention studies within the phenomenological research tradition, with only 13 (8%) studies identified. Given that phenomenological research can provide valuable insight into participants’ perspectives, there is clearly a research gap, and limited attention to intervention studies, which is especially relevant in addressing intergenerational trauma. Our review identified only 17 (9%) studies explicitly addressing the professional work stance, which is strikingly few given the breadth and diversity of the scientific and professional disciplines. The evident research gap in professional perspectives means the field lacks studies that could help professionals and parents gain a deeper understanding of “phenomena” or lived experience. Such insights have the potential to enhance practice and relationship development.

Phenomenological study methodologies in the field of IECMH

When analyzing the research questions, it was surprising that phenomenologically oriented terms such as “lived experience” or “essence” appeared infrequently. Most studies used “exploring” as their stated aim (n = 71 studies, 43%), followed by “understanding” (n = 51 studies, 31%), and “describing” (n = 21 studies, 13%). This pattern may suggest a shift towards more general qualitative methodologies and a movement away from the historical roots of the phenomenological tradition. The research questions were primarily designed to give voice to the original experiences of individuals within their lived reality, based on the premise that these perspectives could provide valuable insights for, for example, service development.

We identify a strong reliance on interviews as the primary method for data collection, with 138 (83%) studies employing this approach. Only eight studies combined interviews with other data collection methods, such as focus groups, observations, or document analysis. Six studies collected data exclusively from focus groups, while two studies incorporated drawings and one study used poems as data sources. Multiple data collection tools were most common in Phenomenological NOS studies (n = 8, 5%) and in studies utilizing IPA (n = 5, 3%). The observation of caregiver‐child interaction is rarely used in the phenomenological studies within IECMH, which is somewhat puzzling. This is particularly notable given that central insights into intersubjectivity and empathy, core concepts in phenomenology, would seem to lend themselves to observational methods.

One of the most striking findings is the significant variation in the use of methods. IPA (Smith & Larkin, 2009) and Phen NOS are the most frequently used approaches. However, a notable number of studies employ other phenomenological methods, such as those developed by van Manen (1990), Colaizzi (1978), Giorgi (2009), and Dahlberg et al. (2008). This diversity reflects a field in development, with researchers exploring various phenomenological approaches. Additionally, during the inclusion process we noticed that several studies called “phenomenological” in the titles of the scientific articles did not utilize phenomenological references. Our findings reveal considerable variation between studies in the alignment of theory and method, with some studies demonstrating strong congruence and others showing significant discrepancies. This underscores the need for greater attention to the theoretical foundations of phenomenological studies in this field, especially in IPA and Phen NOS studies. Given the increasing use of both approaches over the past decade, our review highlights the importance of refining and grounding phenomenological studies in IECMH within robust and coherent theoretical frameworks, ensuring that methods remain consistent with the core principles of phenomenological philosophy.

We do not suggest that phenomenological researchers must become philosophers. Rather, our review emphasizes the importance of being mindful of the consistency between theory and method in phenomenological studies. This raises a critical question: can a qualitative data analysis method still be considered genuinely phenomenological if it is no longer connected to the central insights of phenomenological philosophy? Without this connection, there is a risk that the methods may lose their phenomenological identity. At a practical level this means studying individual subjective experiences and not meanings related to phenomena. This concern is particularly relevant for IPA and methods classified under Phen NOS, which often show a lack of clear alignment with phenomenological theory. Such disconnections may dilute the theoretical rigor and philosophical foundations essential to phenomenological research. As phenomenological research can reveal in‐depth contextual meanings of importance in the field of IECMH, strengthening methodological rigor in research is crucial to support the further usage of research in the practical development of the field of IECMH.

Additionally, phenomenology has not yet fully developed a systematic observational method grounded in its philosophical principles. Nevertheless, pioneering work by Daniel Stern (Stern, 1985) and Colwyn Trevarthen (Trevarthen, 1979) has laid important groundwork for integrating phenomenological insights into the observation of human interactions, particularly in the context of intersubjectivity and early caregiver‐child relationships. Insights from research can help professionals and parents gain a deeper understanding of ‘phenomena,’ or lived experience, which in turn can support and enhance relationship development.

We have not discussed the prevailing research methods in IECMH, as quantitative research methods still dominate the field, framing infancy from quantitative or medical paradigms. To move the field forward, more methodological studies and reviews are needed to diversify the research lenses applied in future IECMH work.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this review is that such an extensive mapping of phenomenological studies with a global scope has never previously been conducted in the field of IECMH.

A strength is the focus on the IECMH field and time period. The chosen inclusion criteria allowed us to maintain a clear focus for the mapping review. Many excluded studies involved mixed samples of infants, young children, toddlers, and older children. Including studies with older children would have increased the number of eligible publications, but it would have made it difficult to extract results specifically related to the IECMH time period.

A strength is that our inclusion criteria required that studies either mentioned infants or addressed intersubjectivity. As a result, a limitation can be that many studies focusing solely on the parental perspective or well‐being were excluded. Furthermore, we might have identified more studies related to clinical practice if the search strategy included additional keywords concerning clinical and professional work within IECMH.

In a mapping review the volume of included studies and the purpose of identifying gaps rather than conducting a formal synthesis makes assessing the quality of the studies less relevant. Nevertheless, had it been feasible, a quality assessment could have been interesting to highlight limitations in the included studies. The lack of a quality appraisal of the included studies can be seen as a limitation.

A strength is that we excluded a wide set of studies utilizing thematic analysis, even though our search had identified those as phenomenological studies, mainly because the terms “phenomenology” and “phenomena” had been used in the studies.

We inductively created a list of categories for analysis, which is included as supplemental materials. However, the Rayyan software tool was, at the current development stage, not suitable for conducting a detailed analysis. The categories could be further redefined according to the types of relationships each study focused on. It would also be interesting to analyze the categories from a cross‐cultural perspective. A more detailed analysis of the content of the phenomenological study results could provide deeper insights to inform both science and praxis. In future stages, analysis could also be conducted from a demographic perspective, as gender has so far only been identified in the participant descriptions.

In the analysis of the phenomenological methods (Figure 2), it is important to note that studies were assigned the label Phen NOS when it was unclear which phenomenological method had been used or when the study refers to multiple approaches in an eclectic or inconsistent manner. This means that researchers should aim at clarity in their studies. Similarly, some studies are categorized under Other Method when they reference phenomenological philosophy or theory but apply a non‐phenomenological method in practice. Studies categorized under these labels reveal inconsistencies in the application of phenomenological methods within IECMH research.

IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE AND/OR FURTHER RESEARCH

Methodologically, phenomenological studies could benefit from more diverse forms of data collection beyond various types of interviews, such as incorporating written narratives as well as observational methods. We identified substantial non‐congruence across phenomenological studies, meaning many lacked a clear connection to the philosophical traditions and foundational underpinnings of phenomenology. This was evident, for example, in the scarce use of the concept “lived experiences” in the research questions. Without the connection to philosophical roots ‐being with‐ the full potential of a phenomenological orientation is not fully understood and utilized in both research and practice development.

The implications for practice are that phenomenological traditions can bring added value to the comprehension of the optimal growth of infants in the delicate time of new parenthood and for deeper comprehensions of the attachment relationship. We consider it of utmost importance for researchers to deepen their understanding of the broader phenomenological traditions to ensure that the phenomenological study results and insights are empathically attuned and authentically reflect the participants lived world and meanings.

CONCLUSION

This mapping review explored and analyzed the diverse ways phenomenology has been used in IECMH research. The studies identified a global context and primarily focus on the subjective experiences of women and the growth and change into the parental role. Phenomenological research can grasp multiple, simultaneous levels of lived experience and add culturally sensitive meanings and knowledge about clinical practice in the field of IECMH. Phenomenological research gives valuable insight and in‐depth knowledge of the infants’ world, families, and care. Several research gaps were identified in the field, intervention research and professional perspectives were scarce. Studies focusing on fathers, partners, and extended family are limited. Additionally, research on attachment relationships or caregiver‐child interaction were scarce and often not central, despite the strong methodological potential of phenomenology to explore these areas of research. Our conclusion is that the potential of phenomenological insights of intersubjectivity and empathy has not yet been fully implemented in IECMH research.
