Highly Mesoporous Zr‐Based MOF‐Fabric Composites: A Benign Approach for Expeditious Degradation of Chemical Warfare Agents and Simulants
Authors: Mai O Abdelmigeed, John J Mahle, Gregory W Peterson, Gregory N Parsons
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research has demonstrated the degradation of organophosphonates through hydrolysis using microporous UiO–66–NH2‐fabric composites. Yet, challenges remain due to the limitations of organophosphonates accessing active sites in large, engineered granules. To address this, an innovative approach to integrate mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 onto various fabrics is provided, thereby overcoming previous mass transfer limitations. Mesoporosity in the UiO–66–NH2‐fabric is attributed to the amphoteric cocamidopropylbetaine (CAPB) surfactant which templates the mesochannel construction. Unexpectedly, because the synthesis is aqueous, benign, low temperature (60°C), and avoids strong acids and toxic solvents, it is compatible with fragile supports such as untreated cotton. The UiO–66–NH2‐fabric composite formed using treated polypropylene (PP) attains a BET specific surface area of 360 m2 g−1 comp. Remarkably, the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2‐composites exhibit a pore volume as large as 0.2 cm3 g−1 comp, 33% in the mesoporous range, which is higher than other previous reports. Practically, the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2‐treated PP composite enhances the rate of methyl paraoxon (DMNP) degradation, showing a t 1/2 value that is 15 times faster than microporous UiO–66–NH2 composites measured under the same conditions. Similar trends are observed in the degradation of actual nerve agents. These composites hold significant potential across diverse applications, including filtration, protection, and catalysis.Keywords: benign and mild synthesis, Mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 , MOF‐fabric composites, nerve agents’ degradation, organophosphonate detoxification

Introduction

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) represent a relatively new class of porous crystalline materials, consisting of metal clusters or secondary building units (SBUs) coordinated with organic linkers through strong bonds.[ 1 ] SBUs, inorganic polynuclear clusters, provide rigidity, directionality, and stability to MOF structures.[ 1 ] The chemical nature of SBUs makes MOFs versatile in applications such as gas and vapor adsorption, separation processes, and SBU‐mediated catalysis.[ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] Organic units, typically mono‐, di‐, tri‐, or tetravalent ligands, contribute to pore size and volume expansion in MOFs. Functionalization of organic ligands enhances applications in gas separation, energy storage, catalysis, and thermal energy conversion.[ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ]

MOFs can offer an enhanced catalytic activity for the hydrolysis of toxic organophosphonate vapor and liquid compounds, including the most toxic fluorophosphate chemical warfare agents (CWAs).[ 11 ] Among these, GD (Soman), VX (venomous agent X), and HD (Sulfur Mustard) can be toxic through oral, dermal, and percutaneous routes.[ 12 ] Remarkably, even a small amount, such as one drop of VX (6 to 10 mg) on the skin,[ 13 ] or 200 to 1700 mg for a 70 kg man of GA, GB, or GD, can be fatal.[ 14 ] In addition, organophosphonates are widespread, comprising ≈34% of the chemical pesticides currently in use.[ 15 ] Protection from CWAs and related compounds currently relies on physisorption in highly porous carbon.[ 16 ] However, these carbons are primarily microporous which impedes species diffusion. Another drawback of porous carbon is that toxic compounds can desorb under specific conditions introducing additional exposure risk.[ 17 ]

As a potential replacement for activated carbon, UiO‐type (University of Oslo) Zr‐based MOFs are of interest because they not only adsorb but also facilitate the decomposition of these organophosphonates into less toxic substances through hydrolysis, driven by Zr(IV) metal node centers acting as strong Lewis acid sites.[ 18 ] The two‐step hydrolysis mechanism involves the nucleophilic addition of OH groups to the nerve agent, coordinated to a Zr metal center, forming a phosphorus intermediate, followed by an elimination step.[ 19 ]

Despite their potential, the practical application of MOFs is limited by their hard‐to‐handle powder form unless they can be engineered into a successful configuration. To address this, recent research has focused on mechanically stable macroscopic MOF‐polymer composites, including UiO–66–NH2‐fiber structures.[ 20 ] Several fiber substrates were used such as polyamide‐6 (PA‐6), polypropylene (PP), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyethylene (PE), and spandex (also known as elastane or Lycra) to grow the UiO–66–NH2 on them solvothermally. Attempts to grow UiO–66–NH2 solvothermally on untreated cotton typically fail because the fiber cannot endure high temperatures in acidic environments. However, the cotton fiber remains intact during the growth of the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2, thanks to the mild synthesis conditions. The catalytic activity responsible for degrading organophosphonates is contained within the pores of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2, which prevents the glycosidic bonds from breaking and protects the cotton. Additionally, the MOF coating, possibly combined with TiO2, provides a protective layer that guards against hydrolytic damage. Given cotton's versatility,[ 21 ] developing a method for the solvothermal growth of UiO–66–NH2 on cotton is essential.

Regarding MOFs, improved catalytic performance can be achieved by increasing access to active metal sites, for example, by inducing missing‐linker defects or by designing various highly porous metal–organic frameworks (HP‐MOFs)[ 22 ] with adjustable pore sizes and volumes. During synthesis, missing‐linker defects can be intentionally introduced using a monocarboxylic acid modulator. The modulator serves a dual purpose: the carboxylic acid coordinates with the metal ion to form metal–oxo clusters and the alkyl chain introduces structural defects and additional pore space. However, these defective MOFs still fall within the microporous range (<20 Å).[ 23 ] Attempts to directly incorporate mesoporous MOFs onto fibers have had limited success. For example, mesoporous MIL‐101 (Cr) with a surface area exceeding 1900 m2 g−1 powder, was incorporated onto electrospun polyacrylonitrile (PAN) nanofibers, achieving a surface area of up to 300 m2 g−1 comp (»15% MOF loading only). However, the isotherm for this composite showed only microporosity,[ 24 ] suggesting the MOF‐fabric synthesis process led to either MOF structure degradation or pore blockage or even both.

This work describes a benign synthesis approach marking the initial creation of a mesoporous MOF‐fabric composite, where the mesoporosity within the UiO–66–NH2 enables enhanced performance compared to the more common microporous MOFs. Surprisingly, we find that MOF composites comprised of high‐performance mesoporous MOFs can be formed at 60°C in water, instead of the more common 100[ 25 ] ‐120°C[ 26 ] in organic solvents,[ 27 ] expanding options for practical use, including integration onto untreated cotton. This approach not only enhances environmental and performance aspects but also plays a crucial role in the successful growth of MOFs on various fabrics, including untreated cotton, which previously was incompatible with the synthesis condition of the solvothermal microporous MOF integration.[ 21 ]

To confirm the importance of pore structure, we examine the effect of MOF mesoporosity on the rate of organophosphonate degradation. We investigated mesoporous MIP‐206 and MIP‐206‐OH MOFs, (MIP: Institute of Porous Materials of Paris) created using a homometallic‐multicluster‐dot (HMD) method. These MOFs are sturdy and feature large, accessible mesopores of 26 Å diameter. By blending two distinct homometallic inorganic clusters into one dot structure, the HMD approach enables the efficient construction of robust mesoporous MOFs. For example, we used non‐toxic Zr6 and Zr12 oxo‐clusters and isophthalic acid as the linker to synthesize MIP‐206, a mesoporous Zr‐based MOF. The strategic positioning of functional groups within the isophthalic acid allows for fine‐tuning the chemical environment of the pores.[ 28 ]

For the synthesis of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2, the key design element involves using an amphoteric surfactant, such as cocamidopropylbetaine (CAPB), to template the mesopore formation, as illustrated in Scheme 1 . The interaction between MOF precursors and surfactants is crucial for directing the successful growth of mesoporous MOFs.[ 29 ] It's known that the surfactant concentration affects mesopore size, but we maintained a fixed concentration to ensure the mechanical stability and resilience of the MOFs, as larger mesopores could compromise structural integrity. Amphoteric surfactants, characterized by carboxylate functionality and quaternary ammonium as the hydrophilic head group, play a key role. The carboxylate functionality enables strong bonding with metal clusters in MOFs, while the quaternary ammonium group enhances solubility.[ 30 ] This dual functionality allows the surfactant to act as both a template and a coordinating agent, preventing phase separation of mesopore‐free MOFs. Additionally, using a sufficient surfactant concentration in the water‐based system, the reaction ensures the formation of rod‐shaped surfactant micelles, directing MOF growth.[ 30 ] This aqueous‐phase synthesis overcomes challenges with micelle formation in organic solvents, typically used for MOF synthesis. The subsequent dissolution of the surfactant in excess water during post‐synthesis washing and soaking in heated methanol/HCl leads to well‐defined and residue‐free mesopores.[ 31 ] This approach extends to mesoporous MOF‐fabric materials with high activity for nerve agent and organophosphonate degradation, offering a promising strategy for real‐world applications.

Results and Discussion

Our objective in this study is to understand the role of mesoporosity in Zr‐based MOF composites to enhance the rate of CWA and simulant hydrolysis, considering factors such as ease of synthesis, environmental friendliness, cost‐effectiveness, and scalability. We examined several Zr‐based MOFs formed via solvothermal synthesis, including MIP‐206, MIP‐206‐OH, UiO–66–NH2 (produced in typical microporous form) as well as “mesoporous UiO–66–NH2” described below. MOFs were fabricated in common powder form as well as in MOF‐fabric composites. Details regarding materials used and experimental procedures area available in supplemental information. All powder and composite materials were analyzed by X‐ray diffraction (XRD), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), rate of simulant (DMNP) hydrolysis, and CWA (GD, VX, and HD) degradation. The microporous UiO–66–NH2, referred to as “2X UiO–66–NH2” was produced using our previously published protocol,[ 32 ] except the precursor concentration was increased by a factor of two.

The information presented in Table 1 indicates that the mesoporous catalysts are improved substantially compared to the typical microporous MOFs. Specifically, the total porosity of the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 is increased by 50% compared to the microporous MOF (0.75 cm3 g−1 powder versus 0.5 cm3 g−1 powder), and the mesoporous MOF has nearly half of its porosity in the mesoporous range (pore size > 20 Å), compared to just 1 to 2% for the microporous version.

Figure 2 displays the nitrogen isotherms for the powders in Table 1 and illustrates the differences in pore width distribution for the MOFs studied. The adsorption isotherms for MIP‐206 and MIP‐206‐OH followed a Type IV isotherm with the initial part attributed to monolayer‐multilayer adsorption. Here, the Type IV isotherms exhibit reversible capillary condensation‐evaporation, which leads to the absence of hysteresis loops, which suggests that the pore openings are almost the same size as the pores themselves confirming having uniform pores and mesochannels. Our BET surface area tool can detect pores up to » 200 Å using Density‐functional theory model, so larger pores outside this range may also be present. The pore size for both MIP‐206 and MIP‐206‐OH was up to » 20 Å using DFT (Density‐functional theory), aligning with the average pore sizes values in literature of 23.8 Å for MIP‐206 and 26.1 Å for MIP‐206‐OH.[ 28 ] The isotherm for the 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 followed a Type I pattern, indicative of monolayer adsorption. The isotherm for mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 combined features of Type I and Type IV, suggesting the simultaneous existence of micropores and mesopores. Importantly, because the synthesis of the mesoporous material proceeded effectively at lower temperatures (60°C), we successfully achieved solvothermal growth of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on untreated cotton yielding a specific surface area of 110 ± 30 m2 g−1 comp. For this low‐temperature process, the MOF‐cotton composite retained the mechanical integrity of the original cotton. Specifically, the integrity of the composites was characterized by observing the sample cohesiveness and physical integrity after repeated manipulation in the lab by hand, including for example during sample weighing and BET analysis. The materials showed good integrity and maintained their original structure during laboratory handling, even after the samples had been stored in lab air for more than one year. However, when cotton substrates were processed at 90°C or 120°C to integrate microporous MOF, the mechanical properties were noticeably degraded, and the MOF‐fabrics readily fragmented under common laboratory handling. Additionally, using the lower temperature conditions mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 was cultivated on PET, yielding a specific surface area of 90 ± 40 m2 g−1 comp. The same approach using PE & spandex resulted in a specific surface area of 70 ± 50 m2 g−1 comp. To facilitate a comparison between the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 fabric‐composite and the 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 fabric‐composite, 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 was also grown on PP@TiO2, presenting a specific surface area of 120 ± 30 m2 g−1 comp. The adhesion of the MOF to the substrate is detailed in Table 2, where MOF‐fabric composites underwent testing through manual agitation, brushing, and exposure to a pressurized air stream. Remarkably, despite the lower synthesis temperature and the drastic change in the solvent used, outstanding adhesion of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 was shown on all fabric types. Furthermore, the mesoporous MOFs enabled substantially larger mass loading onto the fibers, achieving three times higher surface area than that of the microporous version. In addition, the mild synthesis conditions used in the preparation of the mesoporous MOF allow for more versatile substrates and environmental benefits.

These findings emphasized that the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 displayed superior surface area to the other MOF‐fabric composites synthesized in this study. This is attributed to the inherent propensity of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 to grow on fabrics, coupled with the anchoring effect of the 20 nm thick TiO2 coating. The inherent affinity of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 for fabric substrates could be facilitated by CABP, an amphoteric surfactant employed in an acidic water‐based MOF system. CABP possessed positively and negatively charged functional groups within its structure, enabling it to function effectively as a surfactant across various pH levels.[ 34 ] Under acidic conditions, the positively charged component of CABP could interact with negatively charged surfaces, behaving like a detergent or emulsifier.[ 35 ] When applied to fibers, CABP assisted in removing dirt and oils by reducing the surface tension of water, thereby facilitating deeper infiltration of MOF precursors into the fibers.[ 36 ] Furthermore, CABP's amphoteric characteristics allowed it to interact with both hydrophobic and hydrophilic surfaces, making it adaptable for cleaning and conditioning various fiber types, including natural and synthetic ones like cotton, wool, polyester, and nylon.[ 35 ] Additionally, the amino functional groups inherent in mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 enhanced its adhesion and compatibility with fiber surfaces, further enhancing the interaction between the two materials. Meanwhile, the TiO2 coating served as an anchor, aiding in the formation of a uniform, well‐attached thin film comprising small UiO–66–NH2 crystals on the coated PP substrate.[ 11 ] Knowing that, the more diminutive the MOF crystals are, the more efficient the mass transfer becomes, as discussed below.

At a temperature of 180°C, all fibers used for MIP‐fabric composites, including Kevlar, were fully degraded using the solvothermal technique due to the severe synthesis conditions involving elevated temperature (180°C), high pressure, and high acidity. Employing CAPB surfactant and reducing the synthesis temperature to 120°C facilitated the growth of MIP‐based MOFs on PP, producing a composite with a specific surface area of 70 m2 g−1 comp. However, PP lost its mechanical properties due to the rigorous synthesis conditions involving high temperature (120°C), high pressure, and high acidity.

The importance of the 20 nm thick ALD TiO2 coating becomes apparent when contrasting the SEM images and BET specific surface areas of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP with PP@TiO2. Even though the same MOF synthesis technique was used, the TiO2‐coated substrate exhibits an approximately twofold increase in surface area compared to the uncoated PP. The SEM images also illustrate the development of a dense and uniform thin film consisting of small crystals of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 exclusively on PP@TiO2, whereas only isolated crystals are seen on the uncoated PP.

The significance of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 is further highlighted through a comparison of SEM images and BET surface areas between mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 and 2x‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. Despite utilizing the same substrate, the mesoporous MOF exhibited an approximately threefold increase in surface area compared to its microporous counterpart. The SEM images further depicted the formation of a dense and uniform thin film composed of small crystals in the case of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2, contrasting with a less dense and uniform film consisting of relatively larger crystals in the microporous UiO–66–NH2.

Following the analysis of MOF powders and fabric composites, our subsequent aim was to assess their potential in degrading organophosphonate compounds. Consequently, the hydrolysis rate of methyl paraoxon (DMNP, GD simulant) was measured for mesoporous MOF powders and UiO–66–NH2 fabric composites. Additionally, live agent degradation tests were conducted using both mesoporous and 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. We determined the catalyst weights in the DMNP degradation experiments by consistently using 2.5 mg for MOF powder and 14 mg for MOF fabric, allowing for accurate comparisons with a previous study [ 32 ] using microporous UiO–66–NH2. For the tests using MOF‐fabric, materials are compared based on the total mass of the composite rather than the total mass of MOF because, in true field applications, the performance is expected to be benchmarked relative to the overall mass burden borne by the user.[ 20 ]

Kinetic analysis of DMNP hydrolysis to nitrophenol was conducted in buffered solution (pH = 10) using 2.5 mg samples of 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2, MIP‐206, MIP‐206‐OH, and mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 powders, and the results are presented in Table 4 and Figure 5 , where the lines in Figure 5 are fit to a pseudo‐first‐order reaction equation. The data points in Table 4 and in the inset in Figure 5 reveal that MIP‐206‐OH exhibited the fastest half‐life (15 min), followed by microporous UiO–66–NH2 (17 min), then mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 (20 min), and finally, MIP‐206 (43 min).

The fast degradation rate of DMNP observed in MIP‐206‐OH could be attributed not only to its mesopores, which were ≈20Å in size, four times larger than the size of DMNP molecules, but also to the inclusion of Zr metal clusters with accessible metal sites and linkers containing functional groups such as OH. The presence of the hydroxyl (OH) group enabled the donation or acceptance of protons in hydrolysis reactions, thereby aiding in proton transfer processes.[ 37 ] This participation in proton transfer reactions accelerated the cleavage of phosphorus‐oxygen bonds, resulting in increased hydrolysis rates. 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 demonstrated the second fastest degradation rates due to its higher surface area, which might compensate for its relatively smaller pore size, resulting in faster kinetics compared to its mesoporous version. The mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 exhibited the third fastest degradation kinetics due to its larger mesopores and relatively lower surface area compared to its microporous version. The rapid degradation of DMNP was attributed not solely to the mesopores and the high surface area of the MOF but also to the presence of NH2 functional groups in UiO–66–NH2. The amino (NH2) group acts as a nucleophile, initiating hydrolysis by attacking the phosphorus atom of the organophosphonate compound.[ 37 ] This group also facilitates bond cleavage by forming a covalent bond with the phosphorus atom, aiding in the cleavage of the P‐O bond, which is crucial for hydrolysis. Lastly, MIP‐206 lagged due to its lower surface area and the absence of functional groups.

These findings in Figure 6 were performed using the same protocol and the same amount of MOF‐fabric catalyst previously reported by our group[ 32 ] for analysis of microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2, so previously published values can be used as a control for comparison to the results presented here. The previously published 1X microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 control material showed a specific surface area of 65 m2 g−1 comp and a t 1/2 of 15 min,[ 32 ] with complete conversion in over 90 min. Therefore, the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 shows a t 1/2 that is 15 times faster than the 1X microporous UiO–66–NH2 and 3 times faster than the 2X microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. This comparison underscores the significant impact of employing mesoporous MOFs and increasing precursor concentrations on DMNP hydrolysis. Doubling the precursor concentrations of 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 nearly doubled its surface area in the fabric composite compared to the control 1X microporous UiO–66–NH2 fabric composite. The study also highlights that a single layer of mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 performed as effectively as three layers of non‐modulated microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2.[ 38 ] This further supports the hypothesis that a larger pore size and smaller UiO–66–NH2 crystals facilitate easier transport of the bulky DMNP molecules to active sites, thereby avoiding diffusion as a limiting step for the hydrolysis reaction. The combination of higher surface area and MOF loading enhanced the availability of active sites, leading to the more rapid degradation of DMNP molecules.

The mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 composites also showed good long‐term and mechanical stability. To analyze the long‐term stability of the composites, the DMNP degradation test was repeated using mesoporous UiO–66–NH2‐fabric samples that had been stored in the open‐air lab environment for more than 12 months. As shown in Figure 6, the repeated test showed a DMNP t 1/2 = 1.4 min and a complete conversion in 7.5 min, fully consistent with measurements performed on fresh samples, therefore demonstrating the stability of the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2‐fabric composites. As a test of mechanical stability, we subjected our composites to conditions that simulate machine washing, including mechanical spinning in pH 10 solution at 1100 rpm for at least 30 min. After treatment, the physical integrity and functionality of the MOF‐composites remain intact.

After conducting the simulant hydrolysis tests, a subsequent series of solid‐state tests were conducted on the degradation of live chemical agents. As indicated in Table 6 , degradation experiments for GD, VX, and HD were carried out using mesoporous and 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. Due to the high toxicity of these live agents, experiments were performed only once for each sample, so reported values were considered approximate. Even so, the results revealed that mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 achieves nearly complete conversion of GD in 24 h, with somewhat less degradation for VX and HD. Likewise, 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 exhibits 80% degradation for GD, >50% for VX, and less degradation of HD.

As observed, the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 demonstrated superior GD degradation compared to the 2x‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. This superiority could be attributed primarily to the presence of mesochannels and mesopores facilitating the easy access of bulky compounds like GD to the active sites. Additionally, its elevated surface area indicates a plentiful number of accessible active sites.[ 29 ] While the increase from 80% to 97% may seem moderate, it is significant in the context of the half‐life degradation time for chemical warfare agent simulants. Our findings indicate that the mesoporous version of the UiO–66–NH2 fabric composite reduces the half‐life by a factor of 15. This represents a substantial improvement in both protection and health effects and suggests a similar trend with actual nerve agents. Additionally, complete reaction conversion contributes to environmental remediation and filtration efforts. This signifies the catalyst's superior performance, indicating that even at very low concentrations of nerve agents, the mesopores effectively reduce mass transfer barriers, allowing contaminants to reach the active sites efficiently. Despite the low driving force, the catalyst is still capable of degrading nerve agents.

In contrast, the 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 exhibited a higher VX degradation percentage compared to the mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2. The VX hydrolysis reaction was known to produce ethyl methylphosphonate (EMPA) and diisopropylaminoethanethiol (DESH).[ 39 ] We hypothesize that the distribution of hydrolysis products is contingent on the structure of the MOF‐composite, so the accelerated hydrolysis of VX in the 2X‐microporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP@TiO2 composite may be due to a higher proportion of EMPA generated. This increased EMPA content could potentially serve as a catalyst, further expediting the hydrolysis of VX.[ 40 ]

Concerning the degradation of HD, both the microporous and mesoporous variants of UiO–66–NH2‐fabric composites exhibited a similar, relatively low degradation trend. The relatively slow degradation for HD was ascribed to a difference in reactant transport to the active catalytic sites, where GD was observed to infiltrate and diffuse through the fabric more readily compared to HD and VX, likely due to the presence of adsorbed water in these tests, which facilitates the transportation of partially soluble GD as opposed to insoluble HD.

Conclusion

A novel benign, low‐temperature synthesis approach has been developed enabling mesoporous MOF‐fabric composites that are mechanically resilient and show exceptional performance and long‐term stability for harmful organophosphate catalytic degradation. The mesoporosity within the UiO–66–NH2 enables substantially faster compound hydrolysis compared to the microporous version measured under identical conditions, reducing the degradation time from 15 min to <1 min for the agent simulant, and a corresponding increase in the extent of GD agent degradation within 24 h. The improvement is ascribed to the smaller crystals and mesoporous structure that effectively reduce mass transfer barriers, allowing contaminants to reach the active sites efficiently. This represents a significant advancement from a protection perspective that can greatly mitigate the severe consequences of exposure. Moreover, achieving full reaction conversion supports environmental remediation and filtration initiatives. The MOF composite synthesis is achieved at temperatures as low as 60°C using water as a solvent, as compared to common methods for microporous MOF composites requiring 90°C or higher in organic solvents such as DMF,[ 25 ] GVL,[ 41 ] or ethanol.[ 27 ] This approach reduces environmental impact and enhances functional performance, while also playing a crucial role in the successful growth of MOFs on a wide range of fabrics not previously suitable for mesoporous MOF integration, including untreated cotton.

Different mesoporous Zr‐based MOFs, such as MIP‐206 and MIP‐206‐OH, were investigated for their effectiveness in the organophosphonate degradation reaction. The results underscored the impact of crystal size, mesoporosity, and the extent of MOF growth in accelerating the kinetics of the hydrolysis reaction. It was found that mesoporous UiO–66–NH2 on PP coated with a 20 nm thick ALD TiO2 film exhibited the highest surface area and the shortest DMNP half‐life time. For future endeavors, different substrates, including macro‐architectures with 3D porous interconnected network structures, can be explored to further broaden the range of applications.
