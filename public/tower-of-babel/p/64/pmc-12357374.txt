An epigenome-wide association study in the case-control study to explore early development identifies differential DNA methylation near ZFP57 as associated with autistic traits
Authors: Ellen M Howerton, Valerie Morrill, Rose Schrott, Jason Daniels, Ashley Y Song, Kelly Benke, Heather Volk, Homayoon Farzadegan, Aimee Anido Alexander, Amanda L Tapia, Gabriel S Dichter, Lisa A Croen, Lisa Wiggins, Genevieve Wojcik, M Daniele Fallin, Christine Ladd-Acosta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuantitative measures of autism spectrum disorder (ASD)-related traits can provide insight into trait presentation across the population. Previous studies have identified epigenomic variation associated with ASD diagnosis, but few have evaluated quantitative traits. We sought to identify DNA methylation patterns in child blood associated with Social Responsiveness Scale score, Second Edition (SRS).MethodsWe conducted an epigenome-wide association study of SRS in child blood at approximately age 5 in the Study to Explore Early Development, a case-control study of ASD in the United States. We measured DNA methylation using the Illumina 450K array with 857 samples in our analysis after quality control. We performed regression of the M-value to identify single sites or differentially methylated regions (DMRs) associated with SRS scores, adjusting for sources of biological and technical variation. We examined methylation quantitative trait loci and conducted gene-ontology-term pathway analyses for regions of interest.ResultsWe identified a region about 3.5 kb upstream of ZFP57 on chromosome 6 as differentially methylated (family-wise error rate [fwer] < 0.1) by continuous SRS T-score in the full sample (N = 857; fwer = 0.074) and among ASD cases only (N = 390; fwer = 0.021). ZFP57 encodes a transcription factor involved in imprinting regulation and maintenance, and this DMR has been previously associated with ASD in brain and buccal samples.ConclusionsBlood DNA methylation near ZFP57 was associated (fwer < 0.1) with SRS in the full population sample and appears to be largely driven by trait heterogeneity within the autism case group. Our results indicate DNA methylation associations with ASD quantitative traits are observable in a population and provide insights into specific biologic changes related to autism trait heterogeneity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11689-025-09637-1.Keywords: Autism, Social Responsiveness Scale, DNA methylation, Quantitative trait

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that often manifests in early childhood [1]. Except for syndromic ASD in which variants in a single gene cause the disorder, most causes of ASD are unknown, though multiple lines of evidence suggest epigenetic mechanisms may be altered among individuals with ASD. Rare Mendelian disorders that affect epigenetic machinery such as Fragile X, Rett, and Angelman syndromes either cause ASD or share core symptom characteristics with ASD such as repetitive behaviors [2]. Additionally, rare genetic variants associated with ASD are more commonly found in chromatin remodeling genes, further implicating epigenetic processes in ASD etiology [3].

When comparing biological samples from people with and without ASD (i.e., cases and controls), differences in DNA methylation have been observed in brain tissue [4–7], buccal cells [8], paternal sperm [9, 10], placenta [11], and peripheral blood [12, 13]. Epigenetic findings in studies of blood tissue have been more modest, likely because they were underpowered or because comparison of individuals with and without ASD failed to capture the heterogeneity and variation in levels of ASD features. One study in peripheral blood at birth identified differential DNA methylation by polygenic risk for ASD, suggesting that some differences in DNA methylation are genetically driven [14].

ASD core features include impairments in social reciprocity and repetitive and restrictive behaviors and interests, both of which can vary in intensity among individuals with ASD and are measurable in the general population [15]. Like other neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, it has been hypothesized that ASD is on the end of a quantitative trait spectrum, where individuals with an ASD diagnosis have the greatest presentation of autistic features compared to those without [16, 17]. Additionally, family members of individuals with ASD often have elevated levels of autistic features compared to the general population even if they do not have an ASD diagnosis themselves, often referred to as the broader autism phenotype [18, 19].

Many studies of ASD to date, particularly epigenetic or other biological studies, have considered ASD as a dichotomous phenotype [12] rather than a trait continuum. Two studies thus far, both in the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC), have examined the association between DNA methylation and pragmatic communication traits as measured by the Social and Communication Disorders Checklist (SCDC). Massrali and colleagues performed an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) of cord blood DNA methylation at birth and scores on the SCDC at age 8 in 701 children. No CpG site associations were statistically significant; 19 sites reached suggestive significance (p < 1 × 10−4) [20]. Notably, only 5 participants in their study sample had autism. Rijlaarsdam and colleagues examined the prospective association between DNA methylation in cord blood at birth and peripheral blood at multiple time points between ages 8–17. They identified three CpG sites in cord blood at birth (nearest genes: A4GALT, APCDD1, SPSB4) associated with SCDC score trajectories as characterized by latent profile analysis. They performed a replication analysis using the Generation R Study and the social communication subscale of the short-form Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS); however, their results from ALSPAC did not replicate in the Generation R sample [21]. Few of the participants in these prior studies, largely conducted in European population samples, met autism diagnostic criteria and the assessment scores examined (SCDC and an SRS subscale) failed to capture restrictive or repetitive behaviors (RRBs), a key feature of ASD. To our knowledge, no studies have examined DNA methylation associations with the validated SRS instrument, which captures both social communication challenges and restricted or repetitive behaviors and interest traits in the US population. Furthermore, gaps remain in our understanding about epigenetic changes related to heterogeneity in quantitative autism trait phenotypes among ASD cases.

Therefore, to address these gaps in our knowledge and address prior study limitations, our goal was to identify DNA methylation patterns in child peripheral blood associated with autistic traits in the Study to Explore Early Development (SEED), a large multi-site case-control study of ASD in children in the United States, using the Social Responsiveness Scale Score, Second Edition (SRS).

Methods

Study population

The Study to Explore Early Development (SEED) is a case-control study of ASD in the United States and has been described in full detail elsewhere [22, 23]. Briefly, in SEED Phase 1 (SEED 1), children born between 2003 and 2006 who were ages 2 to 5 at the time of enrollment were recruited at study sites across six states (California, Colorado, Georgia, Maryland, North Carolina, and Pennsylvania). SEED used three groups for participant ascertainment: general population (POP), developmental delay (DD), and known ASD cases. Participants in the potential POP group were ascertained from state vital records, and potential DD and ASD participants were ascertained from schools and healthcare providers in the catchment areas. All participants recruited to SEED underwent screening using the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ). Participants were assigned to additional assessments to determine if they met diagnostic criteria for ASD if they scored SCQ ≥ 11, regardless of their initial ascertainment group. Final classifications in SEED consisted of ASD cases (ASD), developmental delay (DD), and population controls (POP).

Specific to the present study, SEED DNA methylation data were processed and available only for ASD cases and population controls. We pooled SEED participants with a final classification status of “possible case” with the ASD case designation, such that we had two comparison groups (case or possible case = 1 [N = 390], population control = 0 [N = 467]). ASD possible cases (N = 13) were children who had a mental age less than 24 months as measured by the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL), and for whom the clinician evaluating the child was unsure if they had ASD [23]. We conducted sensitivity analyses excluding the possible cases from the overall ASD case group (see Sect. 3.9). Demographic characteristics were primarily collected from the SEED Caregiver interview, a telephone-assisted interview with the child’s primary caregiver.

Social responsiveness scale (SRS)

The Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition (SRS) is a 65-item questionnaire that primarily measures the level of social impairment commonly present in people with ASD [24]. Raw SRS total scores range from 0 to 195, with higher scores indicating greater social impairment. The SRS total raw score consists of measurements in five domains: social awareness, social cognition, social communication, social motivation, and autistic mannerisms. In addition to SRS total raw scores, SRS total T-scores are standardized by rater type (parent or teacher), age (e.g., preschool, for ages 2.5–4.5 years, and school-age, for ages 4–18), and sex of the individual being assessed. In SEED, the SRS preschool or school-age form was completed by the parent or caregiver, typically the child’s mother.

DNA methylation measurement and quality control

DNA methylation data in SEED were available for a subset of children with a final classification status of ASD case or population control. Peripheral blood samples were collected at the SEED clinic visit at an average participant age of 59 months (IQR: 55–65] (Table 1). Samples were prepared using the QIAsymphony midi kit (Qiagen). DNA was treated using the 96-well EZ DNA methylation kit (Zymo Research) and methylation was measured using the Illumina Infinium 450K BeadChip (Illumina), with samples randomized across batch and plate by ASD diagnosis and participant sex.

Filtering steps for DNA methylation quality control were performed using the minfi R package, with most steps described in more detail in previous SEED publications [12, 25, 26], and further illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1. First, liver and placenta control samples were removed (N = 22), and within-sample normalization and background correction were completed using the preprocessNoob() function. Duplicate samples (N = 12) and low-intensity samples, defined as detection p-value > 0.01 in more than 1% of probes, or median methylated or unmethylated signal < 11, were removed (N = 7). Low-intensity probes with detection p-value > 0.01 in > 10% of samples (N = 702) and ambiguously mapping probes (N = 29,146) were removed [27]. Samples were also removed if their epigenetic age using the Horvath clock algorithm [28] was substantially different than their expected chronological age, which may indicate a sample switch with maternal samples (N = 1; sample was predicted to be the age of an adult and not a child). Additionally, samples were removed if their corresponding genotyping data failed a genetic relatedness check in PLINK software [29] during the genotyping quality control (N = 28). Samples were also removed if predicted sex did not match reported sex for DNA methylation and/or genotyping quality control (N = 5). Lastly, samples were removed if they were missing SRS score (N = 74) or were not the index child in the SEED study (N = 7). Our final analytic sample included 857 individuals, with 390 pooled ASD cases (ASD) and 467 controls (POP).

Genotyping measurement and quality control

Most participants in our analytic dataset had clean genotyping data (N = 757) in addition to DNA methylation data, and, among other explanatory variables, we used genetic ancestry principal components (PCs) to assess whether the surrogate variables from surrogate variable analysis (see Sect. 3.5 and 3.9) adequately captured variation due to genetic ancestry that may confound associations between DNA methylation and SRS scores. Briefly, whole-genome genotyping was measured using the HumanOmni1-Quad BeadChip (Illumina), the Affymetrix Axiom KP array (Affymetrix), or the Illumina Omni 5M array (Illumina), depending on genotyping wave. Quality control involved removing single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers with low call rate (< 95%), removing SNPs outside of Hardy Weinberg equilibrium, removing samples with low call rate (< 98%), removing samples with sex discrepancies and related pairs, and removing samples with excess heterozygosity or homozygosity. SHAPEIT software [30] was used for phasing, and SNP imputation was done using the Michigan Imputation Server [31] with the global 1000 Genomes Phase 3, Version 5 as the reference panel [32]. Genetic ancestry principal components were estimated using EigenStrat software [33, 34], anchored by the HapMap 3 ancestral populations of African, European, and East Asian descent [35].

Differentially methylated position (DMP) analysis

We used four regression frameworks to characterize the association between SRS scores and DNA methylation: (1) SRS T-score as a categorical variable, using established SRS cutoffs for social impairment symptoms (none: ≤59T, mild: 60T-65T, moderate: 66T-75T, and severe: ≥76T) [24, 36] (full sample only), (2) SRS T-score as a continuous variable (full sample), (3) ASD-case-only and population-control-only analyses for SRS T-score as a continuous variable, and (4) a model with an interaction term for continuous SRS T-score by ASD case status (case/control) (full sample only). Since SEED is a case-control study, we conducted our analysis with SRS T-score as a continuous variable in the full sample (adjusted for ASD status as captured by surrogate variables, see below), case-only, and control-only subgroups, both to account for the use of a secondary outcome in a case-control study, and to better characterize how DNA methylation patterns, and their associations with behavior, may differ among individuals with and without ASD. We included an interaction model for continuous SRS T-score by ASD case status (case/control) to statistically test if there were differences in the association between DNA methylation and SRS by ASD diagnosis.

We used linear regression of the M-value, which is the log2-transformed proportion of methylated to unmethylated signal [37], to test for association between SRS score and DNA methylation at each CpG site using the limma R package [38]. We adjusted for surrogate variables, estimated in the sva R package [39], to account for sources of measured and unmeasured confounding. To select the optimal number of surrogate variables to include in our models, we generated heatmaps with all estimated surrogate variables (all individuals: 29; case-only: 21, control-only: 18, interaction-term: 29) versus known sources of variation such as cell type proportion, batch, child sex, and genetic ancestry principal components, to visualize how known variables were captured by the surrogate variables (Supplementary Figures S2 – S6). Additionally, we ran linear regression models for the association of SRS score with DNA methylation that iteratively included all surrogate variables (e.g., 0–29). We calculated the genomic inflation factor (i.e., lambda value) of each model using median chi-square test statistics. We plotted the lambda value versus the number of surrogate variables included in the model (Supplementary Figures S7 – S11) and selected models where lambda ~ 1, adjusted for the fewest number of surrogate variables, which, for consistency, was approximately 17 surrogate variables across all models. Specific to the model with all participants and SRS T-score as a categorical variable, we used the limma function makeContrasts() to retrieve coefficients for each comparison of interest, using the lowest SRS T-score group as the reference (e.g., 60T-65T to ≤59T, 66T-75T to ≤59T, and ≥76T to ≤59T). Genomic annotations for the Illumina 450K array were attributed to each CpG using the getAnnotation() function in the minfi R package. Statistical significance for DMP analyses was defined using a Bonferroni-corrected p-value threshold of p < 1 × 10−7; suggestive significance was defined as p < 1 × 10−5. Thresholds were applied based on previous work by Andrews et al. [12] in SEED on the 450K methylation array.

Differentially methylated region (DMR) analysis

Following DMP analysis, we ran DMR analyses for SRS total T-score as a continuous variable in the full sample, case-only, control-only, and interaction-term analyses. We used the bumphunter() function in the minfi R package to identify differentially methylated clusters of CpG sites, specifying a maximum gap of 250 base pairs between clusters with 1,000 bootstraps [25, 26, 40]. All DMR analyses were adjusted for 17 surrogate variables to match the adjustment procedure for the DMP analyses.

Methylation quantitative trait locus (meQTL) mapping

Building upon previous work in SEED [12], we used maps of peripheral blood meQTLs to identify SNP-CpG associations for the CpG sites in our top-ranked DMRs. These meQTL maps were generated using genotyping and DNA methylation data from the same individuals in the present study. We flagged CpG sites within the DMR that were known SNPs or for which the methylation probe included a SNP using the getAnnotation() function in minfi [25, 26]. To identify how many CpGs in each DMR were under control of at least one SNP as determined by statistical significance, we used a Bonferroni-corrected p-value of p < 5 × 10−9 given 9,066,791 total SNP-CpG associations in our set.

Pathway analysis

We performed gene-ontology-term (GO-term) pathway analyses using the top 10, 50, and 100 nearest genes for our DMR results. We used the goregion() function from the missMethyl R package [41], specifying gene annotations for the Illumina 450K array so as to appropriately account for the differing number of probes per gene. As the DMR output from the bumphunter procedure included additional specifications for nearest genes, i.e., the CpG sites in some DMRs would map to an intergenic region, we first used entrezIDs to map the nearest genes to their genomic regions, then input the genomic ranges of those genes into the goregion() function in missMethyl.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed several sensitivity analyses. In one analysis, we directly adjusted for genetic ancestry PCs estimated from genotype data in addition to estimated surrogate variables from the DNA methylation data in the samples with both sources (N = 757). We performed this analysis to check whether the surrogate variables from DNA methylation were adequately controlling for variation by genetic ancestry. We also ran an analysis where we excluded ASD possible cases (N = 13) from our case group (total N = 844; ASD cases: N = 377; population controls: N = 467). As previously noted, ASD possible cases were children who had a mental age less than 24 months as measured by the MSEL, and for whom the clinician evaluating the child was unsure if they had ASD; we performed this analysis to be more limiting in our ASD case definition, requiring the child to have a confirmed ASD diagnosis based on the SEED network diagnostic protocol [23]. For both sensitivity analyses, we re-estimated surrogate variables to account for the changes in our model and/or the smaller sample size.

Results

Participant characteristics

SRS total raw score and T-score showed similar distributions by final classification status and child sex, aside from the peak at 90 T for the maximum value of SRS T-scores (Supplementary Figure S12). Some ASD cases had low SRS scores (N = 40 with SRS T-score ≤59T) and a few population controls had high SRS scores (N = 8 with SRS T-score 66T-75T; N = 1 with SRS T-score ≥76T), with higher scores indicating greater social impairment (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S12).

Given the similarity of the distributions of SRS raw and T-scores, we used SRS T-scores for our primary analyses because of the interpretability of established SRS T-score cutoffs for ASD-related social impairment (none: ≤59T, mild: 60T-65T, moderate: 66T-75T, and severe: ≥76T). We pooled SEED participants with a final classification status of “possible case” with the ASD case designation, such that we had two groups (case or possible case = 1, population control = 0). There was a higher proportion of female children with lower SRS T-scores compared to the total proportion of female children (female % ≤59T = 43.9%, including ≤10 cases and approximately 200 controls [exact counts masked due to small cell size]; female % total = 34.0%, 71 cases and 220 controls). Most participant SRS scores (93.0%) were assessed using the school-age form. By child race and ethnicity, there were proportionally more non-Hispanic white participants in the lowest SRS T-score group than the other groups. We observed some variation in SRS T-score by income status, study site, processing batch, and cell type proportion.

We did not find our results to differ appreciably in sensitivity analyses, i.e., adjusting for 16 surrogate variables plus direct adjustment for 0 or 10 genetic ancestry principal components in the subset of participants with genetic data (N = 757) (Supplementary Tables S11– S16), or excluding possible ASD cases in the case group (N = 844) (Supplementary Tables S17– S22). For example, when we excluded possible ASD cases, we found that when adjusting for 16 surrogate variables, which may be most appropriate given the smaller sample size, the DMR near ZFP57 was statistically significant in both case-only and all-participant analyses (case-only: fwer = 0.018, all-participant: fwer = 0.051) (Supplementary Tables S17– S19). When adjusting for 17 surrogate variables, the DMR was not statistically significant for all participants, but was statistically significant in case-only analyses (case-only: fwer = 0.02; all-participant: fwer = 0.259) (Supplementary Tables S20– S22). We interpret this result similarly to the full analyses with a less-stringent ASD case definition, in that methylation differences near ZFP57 were more pronounced in the ASD-only analyses, regardless of whether possible ASD cases were included in the ASD case group.

meQTL mapping

We examined the presence of meQTLs for the CpG target sites within the DMRs in Table 4. Supplementary Table S4 briefly summarizes the meQTL associations for each DMR, including the number of CpG sites in the DMR that are SNPs, the number of methylation probes that contain a SNP, and the number of CpG sites with methylation controlled by at least one SNP. By count, all CpGs in all top DMRs were under control of meQTLs as defined by a p-value threshold of p < 5 × 10−9, suggesting that the DMRs identified are influenced by genetic variation.

Pathway analyses

We did not identify any statistically significant pathways using gene-ontology-(GO)-term analyses for the top 10, 50, and 100 nearest genes from the DMR analyses for all, case-only, or control-only participant analyses. In viewing the top 20 GO-terms for the top 10 nearest genes for all participants, some activities related to smell (e.g., olfactory receptor activity, detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception of smell, and sensory perception of smell) were included (Supplementary Table S5), which could be relevant to sensory sensitivities in ASD, though we are cautious of over-interpretation.

Discussion

We performed an EWAS of SRS score using child peripheral blood samples in a case-control study of children with ASD and population controls. Given the case-control study design of SEED, and our desire to explore and compare autistic social impairment trait associations between those with and without ASD, we analyzed all individuals, ASD cases alone, and controls alone for each portion of our analysis, along with an interaction-term analysis for continuous SRS T-score by ASD case status (case/control).

We did not identify statistically significant associations in our single-site analyses, though some CpG sites of suggestive significance (p < 1 × 10−5) were in or near genes that have been previously implicated in ASD or in biological processes relevant to ASD. For example, cg11712936 in the promoter region of CNTNAP5 was identified as suggestively significant in our analysis of SRS total T-score as a continuous variable, and CNTNAP5 is a strong candidate gene per the SFARI Gene resource [43–45], encoding a transmembrane protein involved in intracellular communication and cell adhesion. Additionally, one top site that appeared in both the continuous and categorical analyses was cg26337020 near ST8SIA4, is implicated in neural cell adhesion [46]. Six of the top 10 CpGs overlapped between SRS total T-score as a continuous variable and by SRS total T-score categorically when comparing highest (≥76T) versus lowest (≤59T) groups, showing similarities in the findings between the two analyses. It is also possible that the association between SRS and DNA methylation may differ at the quantitative extremes for some CpGs, or that exclusion of the middle groups (e.g., 60-65T, 66-75T) resulted in some information loss in the categorical analysis due to a smaller sample size. There was less consistency in top sites associated with continuous SRS total T-score between ASD case-only and population control-only analyses, possibly because of the reduced sample size within each group compared to the total sample, or because there are true DNA methylation differences in autistic social impairment traits by ASD case-control status. In statistical testing of these differences with an interaction term, no sites were statistically significant, though one suggestively significant site in the interaction-term analysis, cg19590445 in the body of MSX2, is in an ASD candidate gene, with rare variants in this gene reported in the SFARI Gene resource [43, 47, 48].

When comparing our findings from DNA methylation and SRS to the work previously published by Andrews et al. [12], an EWAS of ASD in SEED, we did not identify overlaps in the suggestively significant (p < 1 × 10−5) CpG sites. This could be due to the fact that the SRS as an instrument measures a different outcome as compared to a dichotomous ASD diagnosis. The SRS is designed to capture population variance in quantitative autistic traits among individuals with and without ASD. Thus, given that the SRS reflects a range of autistic traits in a population, it may be more reflective of the heterogeneity present in ASD, rather than a dichotomous diagnostic outcome, resulting in different DNA methylation associations across these two outcomes.

Our DMR analyses identified a region upstream of ZFP57 on chromosome 6 as differentially methylated by SRS T-score as a continuous variable in both the full sample and case-only sample. Our stratified DMR result, where ZFP57 showed a significant change in methylation associated with SRS scores in the autism case group but was not detectable as a DMR in the control group, suggests that the association we detected in the full sample was driven by the autism case group. ZFP57 encodes a transcription factor that is involved in imprinting regulation and maintenance [49]. The same DMR has been previously identified as associated with ASD in post-mortem temporal cortex samples [5] and in buccal samples in a Mexican case-control study of ASD [50]. It should be noted, however, that the direction of effect in our study was opposite that of these two previously published findings. This may be due to differences in the outcomes analyzed– continuous trait measures compared to dichotomous diagnostic outcomes– or differences in tissue types analyzed (Supplementary Table S6). However, this is not an entirely contradictory finding as the strength of the association between ZFP57 DNA methylation and SRS scores was most apparent in the ASD case-only analysis. Further, it is not unlikely that the direction and/or magnitude of effect is different in our study from the two previously published papers that have a consistent direction of effect between them. This could be due to the fact that brain and buccal cells are both derived from the ectoderm during early embryogenesis and thus may share more similarities in their epigenetic patterns compared to blood (mesoderm derived) [51]. What is notable, however, is that there are consistently identified inter-individual differences in DNA methylation at this region that are associated with ASD and ASD-related outcomes, that may be important in furthering our understanding of the biologic changes related to autism and autistic trait heterogeneity. Additionally, this DMR falls within the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC), which is known to contain a number of meQTLs and indeed studies have identified changes in ZFP57 expression associated with underlying genetic variation [52, 53]. Therefore, future studies should examine whether the associations presented here and elsewhere may be driven at least in part by underling genetic variation. Nevertheless, the consistency with which methylation at this gene has been associated with ASD-related outcomes across multiple cohorts and tissue types is important, and this study adds to this body of literature.

Compared to other SRS T-score groupings, we found that the low-SRS T-score case group (≤59T) was hypermethylated compared to other groups and appeared to be driving the associations, particularly among the ASD cases. To further interrogate this group, we briefly examined average measures on other autism screening and diagnostic tools that were part of the SEED diagnostic pipeline (e.g., Social Communication Questionnaire, Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Autism Diagnostic Interview– Revised) and found that the lower SRS scores were generally consistent with the other measures– i.e., these participants had scores consistent with an ASD diagnosis, but appeared to have fewer impairments as measured by these instruments than other individuals with ASD in our study. Future SEED investigations could further explore differences in SRS scores and other measures among this group of low-SRS cases. Additionally, the SRS does not correlate perfectly with an ASD diagnosis [54], which suggests unique features of an ASD diagnosis that go beyond what the SRS captures; for example, we observed differences in methylation by SRS score near ZFP57 among cases, but not among controls. Given the findings in our study, we would recommend future studies evaluate SRS scores and ASD diagnosis in conjunction, when possible.

In regard to our quality control pipeline, we did not exclude extreme outlier methylation samples at specific probes. It is possible that such outlier probe values detected among a small number of samples represent a technical artifact. However, it is also possible that these represent rare de novo methylation changes [55] in a subset of participants which could be biologically relevant. Follow-up studies are needed in larger samples to better distinguish between these two scenarios.

The SEED case-control study design allows comparison of DNA methylation patterns and their association with ASD-related quantitative traits in both ASD cases and population controls, with a moderately sized sample. However, given the case-control sampling design, the full-sample analyses, adjusted for ASD status via surrogate variables, may not be fully generalizable to a general population sample. Furthermore, the design does not allow for prospective assessment of DNA methylation and later ASD trait presentation or change in DNA methylation with age. Additionally, as the DD group did not have DNA methylation data available, we do not know what DNA methylation patterns in SEED are unique to ASD or found in both children with ASD and children with DD. Given the unique nature of SEED as a relatively large, US population sample with both autism quantitative trait data and DNA methylation measurements, we were not able to perform replication analyses for the present study; however, we are encouraged that the ZFP57 locus has been previously identified in other studies of autism and DNA methylation. Future directions include replication of our findings in an external study with SRS measurements, exploration of methylation-SRS associations at additional timepoints, such as in adolescence, and further interrogation of the ZFP57 locus in human studies.

Conclusion

We conducted an EWAS of social communication deficits associated with ASD as measured by the SRS in the Study to Explore Early Development and compared epigenetic signatures in ASD cases and population controls. We identified a differentially methylated region upstream of ZFP57 on chromosome 6 that was hypermethylated in low-SRS ASD cases and was previously associated with ASD diagnosis in prior studies and other tissue types. Further characterization of this locus is recommended. Our findings show that autistic traits can provide additional insights into ASD underpinnings and heterogeneity in these traits among autistic individuals, in conjunction with an ASD diagnosis.
