A Reproducible Workflow for Modelling of 1H to 13C Polarization Transfer Kinetics Using Solid‐State NMR
Authors: D Jacob, X Falourd, C Deborde, M Lahaye, C Rondeau‐Mouro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQuantitative analysis of solid‐state NMR data, based on magic‐angle spinning with cross‐polarization experiments (CP‐MAS), often requires extensive signal processing, from the transformation of raw time‐domain data (FIDs) to the extraction of quantitative data and the modelling of signal intensity kinetics. Many current workflows rely on semi‐manual peak fitting and heterogeneous tools across laboratories for intensity curve modelling, limiting reproducibility and throughput. In this work, we propose a fully reproducible and open workflow combining two key methodological approaches: (1) an adaptive bucketing approach, extraction of relevant variables for analysis (ERVA), implemented in NMRProcFlow application, to automatically segment 13C spectra into chemically relevant spectral regions; and (2) an online modelling platform that allows users to fit intensity curves over contact time with multiple models, guided by objective indicators including fit quality scores and parameter sensitivity metrics. This integrated approach provides a fast, user‐friendly and transparent path from FIDs to kinetic model parameters, opening new perspectives for reproducible quantitative solid‐state NMR.Keywords: 13C, kinetics modelling, reproducibility, solid‐state NMR, VCT‐CP‐MAS

Introduction

Solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy provides unique insights into the structure and dynamics of complex materials in soft or solid states. One of its most widely used methods is magic‐angle spinning with cross‐polarization (CP‐MAS) and heteronuclear decoupling [1, 2], which increases the sensitivity of nuclei, such as 13C, by transferring the polarization from abundant 1H spins to the former 13C spins. The spectra thus produced, well resolved (< 0.5 ppm in linewidth), although sometimes they can exhibit linewidths greater than 2 ppm. By recording NMR spectra with variable contact times (VCT), one can explore the local molecular environments and molecular dynamics associated with different NMR signals. Accurate modelling of signal intensity as a function of contact time is therefore essential to obtain information, particularly on mobility diversity, structural and dynamic heterogeneity within the sample.

Although the VCT‐CP‐MAS approach has become a standardized method [3, 4], 13C data processing and modelling remain highly heterogeneous across laboratories and above all, reproducibility and robustness of 13C data processing remain a challenge. From the acquisition of free induction decays (FIDs) to the modelling of signal intensities, multiple processing choices can affect the final kinetic parameters.

Regarding kinetics, spectral intensities are generally obtained either by manual integration over regions of interest, by detecting peaks at local maxima, or by fitting the peaks using a line shape model (such as dmfit [5]). However, these approaches have several limitations in solid‐state NMR including a limited signal‐to‐noise ratio at short contact times and sensitivity to user choices in defining integration boundaries or line‐shape models. As a result, the early part of CP buildup curves, which is the most informative about transfer rates between 1H and 13C covalently linked, often exhibits large uncertainties or systematic biases.

The goal of this application note is to introduce a workflow based on open‐source tools that address these issues while being simple to deploy and fully reproducible. First, the workflow uses NMRProcFlow [6] to perform interactive yet standardized processing of 13C FIDs and to apply the extraction of relevant variables for analysis (ERVA) [7] bucketing strategy to define meaningful integration regions. This produces a small set of buckets that are tightly matched to the actual signals, providing robust intensity estimates across all contact times. Second, the workflow relies on an online modelling application that takes the ERVA‐derived intensities as input and fits them using several published CP kinetic models, implemented with constrained nonlinear least squares (nlsLM [8]). The tool provides both global fit scores and parameter‐centric diagnostics, such as sensitivity and uncertainty, thereby helping users to select the best model. Furthermore, the entire workflow is fully reproducible thanks to workbooks produced as output that compile all the necessary information and parameters.

Materials and Methods

This work is an extension of a previously published method [9] that focused more specifically on optimizing acquisition time. In the present article, the focus is on the complete processing of spectra and data, including kinetic modelling. We will therefore base our work on the same series of 460 spectra with 128 accumulations associated with a recovery time of 3 s, namely, acquired from cotton cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) from our laboratory collection, using an AVANCE III 400 spectrometer with a double resonance H/X CPMAS 4‐mm probe coupled with a high power‐level amplifier (Bruker, Wissembourg, France).

For each contact time, a single 13C spectrum was acquired in the time domain as an FID. Acquisition parameters such as spectral width, number of complex points and recycle delay were kept constant across the series. The FIDs were stored in the native vendor format.

Regarding spectrum preprocessing, using NMRProcFlow ensures that all raw spectra will be processed in the same way for a given series. This means that automatic phasing, which can be lengthy and tedious to perform manually, will be carried out independently of the operator and therefore perfectly reproducible. A short tutorial summarizing the main preprocessing tasks is available online [10].

Among the parameters, the choice of the apodization parameter (LB = 100 Hz in our case) is crucial because it influences the signal‐to‐noise ratio and influences the signal shape. Therefore, a value representing a compromise between these two aspects should be chosen. Furthermore, if necessary, baseline correction based on the airPLS algorithm [11] can be performed locally.

Regarding spectrum processing and curve/kinetics modelling steps, we propose a workflow that integrates two key elements: a relevant‐variable bucketing approach and a web‐based curve modelling tool.

The relevant‐variable bucketing approach is based on the ERVA method, implemented in the open‐source web application NMRProcFlow. Although the ERVA approach has been previously published in the field of metabolomics, its use in the context of solid‐state NMR data analysis is original and particularly suited to this application. The width of a bucket, that is, a peak integration zone, is estimated from the width of the corresponding peak plus one third of that width. The method enables the automated identification of spectral regions containing significant information, avoiding the drawbacks of both peak picking and complex peak fitting procedures. When the signal‐to‐noise ratio does not fall below 5, then the peak fitting approach gives excellent results. However, the signal‐to‐noise ratio can be equal to or less than 3, that is, for C4m (amorphous). As these are the first points of the kinetics, this strongly impacts the modelling. It should be noted, however, that the application of the ERVA bucketing method is only relevant if it is assumed that all strongly overlapping carbons have similar kinetics, or, for complex compounds such as plant cell walls or lignocellulosic biomass, if the peaks are not strongly overlapped, that is, if the distance between the central chemical shift values of two peaks is larger than the full peak width at half maximum.

The web‐based cross‐polarization kinetics modelling tool (simply called the CP modelling tool in the rest of this article) performs constrained nonlinear least squares fitting using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (nlsLM). Several kinetic models already published for CP dynamics are made available and discussed in [12]. The user can compare them based not only on standard fitting scores (adjusted R 2 or residual sum of squared) but also on indicators reflecting the quality and sensitivity of fitted parameters. Bucket intensities as a function of contact time (t) can be adjusted using these implemented models, implying kinetic parameters such as the 1H rotating‐frame spin–lattice relaxation time constant (T1ρH), one or two constants describing the rate of proton spin diffusion THH), the time‐constant describing the CP signal build‐up (TCH), as well as a parameter (λ) defining the proportion between the different spin diffusion times and Io the theoretical maximum intensity in absence of relaxation.

The application has been written in the R language along with the Shiny framework [16]. To implement new kinetic models, it will be possible to do so using a few lines of R code. An instance of the application is online (https://ssnmr.sk8.inrae.fr/) and the source code is available (https://forge.inrae.fr/sk8/sk8‐apps/transform/ssnmr).

Results and Discussion

To illustrate the proposed workflow, we applied the ERVA‐based bucketing approach to a set of 13C CP‐MAS spectra acquired from cotton cellulose nanocrystals (CNF), with contact times distributed heterogeneously between 5 and 10,000 μs using steps of 5 μs from 5 to 1000 μs, 10 μs from 1000 to 2000 μs and then 50 μs from 2000 to 10,000 μs. This distribution of contact times maximizes the number of points at the beginning of the kinetics while minimizing the total acquisition time. The objective was to model and analyse the proton‐carbon transfer kinetics using raw time‐domain data from solid‐state NMR experiments.

Spectra Processing

After FID processing and spectral chemical shift calibration in NMRProcFlow [10], the ERVA algorithm automatically identified the regions of the spectra exhibiting significant and reproducible variations across the series of contact times (Figure 1), focusing only on statistically relevant spectral zones, that is, free from noise zones.

One crucial point is that the ERVA bucketing strategy provides reliable intensity estimates at very short contact times, that is, early‐time, a region where traditional peak picking or deconvolution often fails. Short CP contact times typically yield low‐intensity, partially overlapping signals with reduced signal‐to‐noise ratio, making peak‐based quantification unstable and highly sensitive to fitting constraints. In contrast, ERVA extracts integrated variables that remain robust even when the spectral features are not fully resolved. As illustrated in Figure 3, the ERVA‐derived buckets for the crystalline and amorphous C4 regions yield consistent and well‐behaved intensity values for contact times below 120 μs. These early‐time points align smoothly with the fitted CP‐kinetic model, demonstrating that ERVA preserves the physical validity of the initial rise of the polarization transfer curve. This behaviour is essential for accurate parameter estimation because the early‐time regime carries significant information on local motional dynamics and heteronuclear dipolar coupling strengths.

13C CPMAS spectra of CNF in the 70‐ to 90‐ppm region (A). The colours represent the different contact time ranges. The 5‐ to 30‐μs range (red) exhibits rather noisy spectra, unsuitable for either deconvolution or peak picking. This is why some of these spectra have sometimes been excluded from kinetic analysis. With the ERVA method, however, the buckets (grey rectangles) for these spectra are identical to the others because their calculation is performed using all the spectra, knowing that spectra with low intensities have little weight in the calculation. Thus, these first points of the kinetics, crucial for modelling, are well estimated as shown on the curves on the right, both fitted with the extended Model 3 (B). Peak fitting, where each spectrum is decomposed into parametric line shapes, can mitigate overlap but at the cost of substantial user intervention and increased computational effort. In practice, the quality of the fits at short contact times is frequently limited by poor signal‐to‐noise ratios and by ambiguities in separating the components. Consequently, the corresponding kinetic curves may display erratic behaviour in the early regime. This is why the ERVA bucketing approach should be preferred whenever peaks belonging to different compounds are not too strongly overlapped. Indeed, the method involves integrating the area around the central value of a peak, which is preferable to a simple maximum intensity value that can be affected by noise. Besides, if the overlap of neighbouring peaks is partial, the integrated area will be minimally influenced by them.

Regarding the extraction of intensity data, the output is a matrix containing the areas of each bucket, with one sample (or spectrum) per row and one variable (or bucket) per column. In addition, a column corresponds to the contact time which is taken from the pulse sequence parameters (p15 in our Bruker pulse sequence with μs as the default unit) recorded in the acquisition parameter file provided with each spectrum. This matrix, exported in a simple tabular format (TSV), is fully compatible with the curve modelling tool. As discussed further below, it is also possible to export all the necessary information to reproduce the spectra processing.

Data Modelling

To model the polarization transfer kinetics, the CP modelling tool has been developed and made available to the community. The kinetic data to be modelled must be provided in matrix form in a tab‐separated values file, that is, the exported file resulting from the 13C spectra processing in the previous step. During import, each of the variable columns is normalized based on the maximum value being equal to 100. Next, we model each variable separately.

The CP modelling tool implements several established kinetic models for VCT‐CPMAS experiments, as mentioned above. For each variable, the user can select a kinetic model, then save the result to export it to a workbook. This workbook contains one tab per variable, grouping the results of all the models saved for that variable.

Each fit produces not only the best‐fit parameters and conventional quality metrics but also sensitivity and identifiability indices for the fitted parameters. While classical indicators such as adjusted R 2 or residual sum of squares (RSS) assess the performance of the regression model, sensitivity and identifiability indices quantify the extent to which small variations in data affect parameter estimates. In our modelling tool, identifiability is based on the collinearity, which is a measure of approximate linear dependence between sets of parameters. The higher its value, the more closely related the parameters are, which means that several parameter combinations may produce similar values of the output variables. Regarding sensitivity, the associated parameter sensitivities indicate that the parameters are well constrained by the data. A high sensitivity score indicates that the parameter is well determined by the data; conversely, a low sensitivity score reveals potential overparameterization or correlation among parameters. The modelling tool also offers indicators which penalize unnecessary complexity (AIC for Akaike information criterion and BIC for Bayesian information criterion [17]) for model comparison and selection, thus guiding the user towards the most appropriate kinetic description.

Let us illustrate this with an example based on the crystalline (cr) and amorphous (am) C4 regions. Table 1 shows the results provided by the CP modelling tool for the adjustments made for both Models 1 and 2. For the two variables C4cr and C4am, the adjusted R 2 fitting criteria have very close values for the two models (CV of 0.17% and 0.16%, respectively). Similarly, the parsimony criterion, here collinearity, is all below 10, which means that all parameters are identifiable and stable. However, this is not the case for the AIC and BIC criteria. For the C4cr variable, the two models differ with CVs over 12%, indicating that model 2 is better. The intensities corresponding to resonance for this variable have a good signal‐to‐noise ratio even at low intensities, that is, short contact times. In Figure 4, we see that Model 2 fits perfectly at the first few points. Regarding the C4am variable, the CVs for the AIC and BIC criteria are 2.7% and 2.2%, respectively. This is very close to the rule of thumb that states that when these criteria have CVs below 2%, we choose the least complex model. This is because at low intensities, the signal‐to‐noise ratio is quite low, and Models 1 and 2 do not fit perfectly at the first few points because they exhibit greater dispersion, as shown in Figure 4.

It should be noted that the number of kinetic points has a major impact on the quality of model fit. The more complex the model, the greater the number of points required. In a previous study [9], it was shown how to optimize acquisition time, thus allowing us to increase the number of acquisitions for different contact times. The good fitting of C4 permitted showing a faster polarization transfer in crystalline regions, with higher TCH compared to the amorphous phases (~42 μs for C4am and ~34 μs for C4cr). Subsequently, for contact times greater than 120 μs, the transfer is governed by spin diffusion, which is linked to strong H—H couplings and therefore to water interactions. Moreover, the amorphous cellulose indicates a shorter T1ρ time (~31,000 μs) than crystalline cellulose (~40,000 μs).

Parameter optimization of kinetic models is based on the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm for nonlinear least squares curve‐fitting problems [18], chosen for its robustness and speed. However, given the complexity of the models, it is necessary to perform several fits from randomly drawn initial values each time, to ensure that the overall minimum of the least squares criterion is obtained. The number of fits equal to 200 is quite sufficient for Models 1, 2 and 3. Regarding more complex models, such as the extended Model 3, convergence does not always reach the global minimum given the number of parameters (8) and trigonometric functions (sin/cos). In this case, it may be necessary to specify a much larger number of fits (> 2000) and to ensure that we obtain a collinearity of parameters close to or below 20.

Reproducibility and Community Impact

A central motivation for developing this workflow is to promote the reproducibility of data processing from the VCT‐CPMAS approach. Indeed, each stage—from raw FIDs to final kinetic parameters—is documented, exportable and reproducible. For each of the two main steps—spectral processing and then kinetic data modelling—a complete workbook is provided, including all the information and parameters necessary to reproduce the results, as well as information regarding the software versions and packages used, as shown in Figure 5 for the kinetic modelling application.

Example of a workbook provided by the kinetic modelling application, with only one variable (bucket) and the results of the fitting for two models in the same tab. [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

By relying on open‐source software, web‐accessible tools, and shared datasets, the workflow enables other researchers to re‐run the entire analysis pipeline and to verify or extend the results. This transparency is especially valuable when kinetic models are used to infer subtle physical properties, where small differences in data processing can lead to different interpretations.

Beyond reproducibility, the workflow also serves as a teaching and dissemination tool. Students and practitioners can use the provided datasets and scripts to learn how processing choices and modelling assumptions influence the final kinetic parameters. The modular design further allows the community to contribute new models, processing options or diagnostic tools, fostering a shared ecosystem around solid‐state CP data analysis.

Conclusions

In summary, the combination of ERVA bucketing and constrained nonlinear modelling offers a practical, efficient and fully reproducible methodology for the quantitative analysis of CP kinetics in solid‐state NMR. As illustrated on the C4 peak of cellulose, the method can help in interpreting signal variations even in case of low signal‐to‐noise ratio. Beyond its application, the workflow illustrates how open‐source, web‐based tools can transform data treatment practices in nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, enhancing both reproducibility and scientific trustworthiness. The workflow responds to the growing demand in the scientific community for open‐science, that is, transparent data processing and analysis practices.

Finally, the open and modular nature of both NMRProcFlow spectra processing tool and the CP modelling tool invites community participation. Users can contribute new models, share datasets and benchmark analyses. Such collaborative development fosters a transparent ecosystem of tools and data that benefits the entire NMR community.
