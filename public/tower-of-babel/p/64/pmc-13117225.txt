Ferroptotic neurodegeneration and diet: a narrative review
Authors: Ximaine R. van der Burg, Danielle V. Toen, Scott Ayton, Hanno Pijl, Louise van der Weerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe neuroprotective potential of various dietary interventions has emerged as a complementary approach to delay neurodegeneration. Ferroptosis, a mechanism of cell death, may be influenced by dietary interventions. Ferroptosis is driven by labile iron generating reactive oxygen species, resulting in lipid peroxidation and cellular death. Iron accumulates in the brain in various neurodegenerative disorders, and this correlates with disease progression.METHODSWe reviewed studies reporting effects of dietary interventions on brain iron accumulation and/or ferroptosis.RESULTSWe report indications that dietary interventions can impact ferroptosis in the brain. Anti‐ferroptotic effects involve reduced lipid peroxidation, enhanced antioxidant defense activity, and reduced labile iron through altered iron metabolism. Transcription factor Nrf2 may be the core regulator of dietary anti‐ferroptotic effects, connecting the three pathways of ferroptosis.DISCUSSIONTargeting ferroptosis through diet may offer a feasible approach to supporting brain health across neurodegenerative conditions, but dedicated studies are required to directly investigate this potential.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of neurodegenerative diseases is expected to increase rapidly because of increased life expectancy. In 2021, the World Health Organization estimated that worldwide 57 million people were affected by some type of dementia, with approximately 10 million new cases every year. 1 Even though more and more people will be suffering from neurodegenerative disease, no curative treatments are available today. 2 Lifestyle choices, especially diet and nutrition, have been proclaimed as a complementary method to delay the onset of Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and other diseases associated with neurodegeneration such as traumatic brain injury (TBI) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 3 Dietary changes may act as early therapeutics addressing biological processes that may be compromised before neurodegeneration progresses from MCI to cognitive failure. 4

The association between diet and neurological function has been extensively reported. Several dietary interventions, including a Western diet and diets rich in animal‐based products, have been associated with increased risks of developing both AD and PD. 5 , 6 , 7 In contrast, various dietary regimes have also been reviewed for their neuroprotective properties, including the ketogenic diet (KD), intermittent fasting (IF), the Mediterranean diet (MeDi), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), and Mediterranean‐DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diets. 3 , 8 , 9 , 10 The KD, in which the main source of calories is fats instead of carbohydrates, the MeDi, and the MIND diet were all linked to delayed cognitive decline in neurodegenerative disease in a recent systematic review. 11 However, separate dietary components such as antioxidants, vitamins, polyphenols, or fatty acids have also been linked to protective effects in neurodegenerative disease. 3 , 12 , 13 Next to antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects, several dietary components or regimes have been reported to decrease brain iron accumulation or ferroptosis, which is a relatively newly described form of iron‐dependent cell death occurring in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Of the available knowledge on ferroptosis and dietary effects on ferroptosis, most comes from the field of cancer research. 19 , 20 Here, ferroptosis is seen as a vulnerability of cancer cells that may be promoted by dietary interventions. 19 , 21 However, in neurological disease, ferroptosis should be limited to minimize cellular damage, which can potentially be achieved by dietary interventions. Although dietary interventions can delay neurodegeneration, the potential mechanisms of how these interventions affect neurochemistry, including pathways relevant to iron and ferroptosis, is underexplored. Thus, this review compares the relevant literature on in vitro, in vivo, and human studies that focus on ferroptosis in the brain and the influence of a selection of both dietary regimes and components (graphical abstract).

IRON AND FERROPTOSIS IN NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES

Brain iron accumulation may propel neurodegeneration through the mechanism of ferroptosis. Several studies have reported a correlation between iron deposition and neurodegeneration. For example, abnormally high levels of iron accumulation are observed in several neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and PD. 22 The cortical iron burden has even been reported to predict AD progression in several studies. 16 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Increased lipid peroxidation, which is an indispensable process within ferroptosis, has been detected in AD brain material. 29 However, even in early AD, lipid peroxidation has been shown to be increased. 30 Using gas chromatography/mass spectrometry on the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of AD patients, increased levels of isoprostanes, which are formed in vivo by peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), have been detected. Reduction of these lipid peroxides into lipid alcohols by antioxidant enzymes restricts the occurrence of ferroptosis. 13 These results are consistent with ferroptosis as a mechanism of neurodegeneration, since iron loading sensitizes to ferroptosis.

The discovery of the cystine‐glutamate antiporter system xCT (xCT [Slc7a11]) marked the first step in revealing the molecular basis of ferroptosis. 31 This system consists of the proteins Slc7a11 and Slc3a2 and forms the basis of glutathione (Gsh) production. This later led to the discovery that antioxidant supplementation prevented cell death. 32 However, the term “ferroptosis” was only introduced in 2012. 33 Since then, selenoprotein glutathione peroxidase 4 (Gpx4), the only protein able to detoxify lipid hydroperoxides in membranes, has been recognized as the main checkpoint for ferroptosis. Inhibition of this enzyme, or loss of Gsh, which it uses as a substrate, leads to a toxic build‐up of lipid peroxides and subsequent cell death. 34 , 35 , 36 Ferrous iron (Fe2+) can catalyze the formation of lipid hydroperoxides by the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mainly by mitochondria, through Fenton and Harber/Weiss reactions. 13 , 15 , 37 However, ROS can also be produced via enzymatic processes, such as those mediated by oxidases and lipoxygenases, which may also be dependent on iron. 38 This ROS formation occurs continuously, so the cell requires ongoing lipid repair by Gpx4. In line with this, ferroptosis can be blocked by iron chelators or by inhibitors of lipid peroxidation such as liproxstatin. 39

The main driver of this ferroptosis pathway is iron, which accumulates with age and is required for multiple physiological processes, including oxygen transportation, DNA synthesis, mitochondrial respiration, myelin synthesis, and neurotransmitter metabolism. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 More specifically, iron is a cofactor for several enzymes involved in these processes. As a cofactor, iron supports enzymes such as ribonucleotide reductase for DNA synthesis and tyrosine and tryptophan hydroxylases for dopamine and serotonin production, respectively. 44 Exactly when the physiological function of brain iron is overridden by the ferroptosis process remains unknown but may depend on the size of the labile iron pool. When iron metabolism is disrupted, for example, through altered expression of iron transporters or the iron‐storage protein ferritin, iron can accumulate in the brain and initiate ferroptosis. 45 Under physiological conditions, circulating iron is usually bound to transferrin, which can cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) via endocytosis or transporter systems on endothelial cells. Within the central nervous system (CNS), transferrin‐bound iron can be transported into the intracellular environment via the transferrin receptor (Tfr). Alternatively, the transferrin‐independent lactotransferrin receptor (Lfr) imports free iron into cells. 46 , 47 Intracellular iron levels tightly regulate the expression of both iron transporters and storage proteins, including ferritin, to maintain iron homeostasis. 48 As the BBB tightly regulates iron transport, peripheral iron overload does not automatically result in increased brain iron. 49 Additionally, the transcription nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) contributes to iron homeostasis by controlling expression of ferritin and other iron‐related genes, thereby limiting cytosolic free iron and oxidative stress. 50 Importantly, iron stored in ferritin does not contribute to ferroptosis, as it cannot react with oxygen to generate ROS. 51

Although brain iron accumulation is often described in the context of neuronal vulnerability to ferroptosis, it is critical to note that iron homeostasis differs substantially across CNS cell types. 52 Neurons maintain relatively low ferritin levels and possess a limited capacity to safely store excess iron. 53 As a result, even modest increases in the neuronal labile iron pool may enhance susceptibility to ROS formation and ferroptosis. In contrast, glial cells express higher levels of ferritin, enabling them to store excess iron more effectively. 53 Nonetheless, several studies demonstrate that iron accumulation within glial cells, specifically microglia 54 and astrocytes, 55 can itself drive neuronal death. This occurs even with limited neuronal iron accumulation, likely through the enhancement of pro‐oxidant and pro‐inflammatory cellular environments.

Overall, ferroptosis can be driven through iron by the formation of ROS, which causes lipid peroxidation, and can be prevented by the reduction of lipid peroxides by antioxidants. This connects not only iron content but also lipid and redox metabolism to ferroptosis. Prevention of ferroptosis can therefore be achieved through iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, or redox regulatory pathways, which may be influenced by dietary changes.

THE EFFECT OF DIETARY REGIMES ON COGNITIVE FUNCTION AND THE ASSOCIATIONS WITH FERROPTOSIS

Nutritional strategies can vary widely, whether they are pursued for weight management, overall health, or specifically to support the brain. Here, we do not consider weight‐loss diets but focus on isocaloric diets with supposed health benefits. Broadly, these can be classified into approaches that modify nutritional composition or meal timing. IF exemplifies the latter category, because food consumption is restricted to periods during the day or week. 56 , 57 Composition‐focused diets can either emphasize particular macronutrients, such as the KD's increased reliance on fats, 9 or promote overall dietary patterns, as in the MeDi, where unprocessed and nutrient‐rich foods are prioritized. 58 , 59 Despite their differences, these modes of dietary modulation share the potential to influence brain health and may help to protect against processes such as ferroptosis.

The ketogenic diet

The KD has been used for decades for weight loss, management of metabolic disorders, and reducing seizures in pediatric epilepsy. 9 The classic KD relies on fats for 80% to 90% of its caloric content and only 4% and 6% from carbohydrates and proteins, respectively. This high‐fat diet brings the body into a state of ketosis, in which elevated levels of ketone bodies are present in the blood. These ketone bodies may be the effective compounds of the diet, since supplementation with one of the main ketone bodies, beta‐hydroxybutyrate (BHB), alone improved clearance of amyloid beta (Aβ) in a human in vitro BBB model, improved cognition and reduced Aβ depositions in an AD mouse model and reduced motor deficits in a PD mouse model (Table 1). 60 , 61 , 62 It has been revealed that KD exerts neuroprotective effects against neurodegeneration, including AD, PD, and MCI. 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70

The neuroprotective effects of the KD in neurological disease were previously deemed to act by decreasing neuroinflammation, decreasing oxidative stress, and enhancing mitochondrial functioning or antioxidant effects in several reviews and studies. 71 , 72 , 73 For example, the KD decreased microglial content in the brain by 22% in a mouse model of AD, nearly normalizing it to the levels in wild‐type animals. 74 However, recent developments have also shown that KD has effects on brain iron deposition and metabolism in AD mouse models, directly relating the effects of the diet to ferroptosis. 15 , 40

Yang et al. investigated the effects of a 28‐day KD diet (46% fat, compared to 4% fat) in 12‐month‐old mice of a chronic sleep‐deprivation model, used to mimic AD. 40 The KD was started 7 days prior to a 21‐day sleep deprivation period and continued throughout this time. Following this period, behavioral tests were performed, and mice were sacrificed to determine the expression of several ferroptosis‐related proteins using histochemistry and Western blots. The short‐term KD alleviated AD‐related cognitive impairment, suppressed AD pathology, including Aβ and tau, and normalized hippocampal neurogenesis. Additionally, the total iron content of the hippocampus was reduced through the normalization of the expression of several iron‐homeostasis proteins. Furthermore, the KD ameliorated the expression of several lipid peroxidation markers and induced activation of the sirtuin 1 (Sirt1)/Nrf2 pathway. Yang and colleagues concluded that KD activated the Sirt1/Nrf2 pathway, thereby leading to reduced lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation and, thus, preventing ferroptosis.

In another recent study by Qin et al. (2023), the neuroprotective effects of a 12‐month KD were investigated in a genetically induced AD mouse model, namely, APP/PS1 mice. 15 In addition to performing behavioral tests to assess the cognitive function of the mice, several tests were performed to identify the mechanism of action of KD in preventing AD disease progression. The long‐term KD alleviated cognitive functional impairment, reduced hippocampal neuronal loss, and Aβ depositions, decreased hippocampal iron deposition, normalized lipid peroxidation, and relieved mitochondrial damage. These effects were related to decreased ferroptosis and hypothesized to be induced by BHB‐dependent activation of the Nrf2 pathway. In a complementary experiment, the role of the Nrf2 pathway in mediating the effects of a KD were further elucidated. Bilateral hippocampal injections of an adeno‐associated virus (AAV) inducing low Nrf2 expression effectively reduced Nrf2 levels and disrupted iron metabolism. Under KD conditions, the anti‐ferroptotic effects were markedly diminished in Nrf2‐deficient mice, with decreased levels of ferritin heavy chain (Fth), Tfr, ferroportin (Fpn), xCT (Slc7a11), and Gpx4 compared to KD mice with normal Nrf2 expression. Consistently, the lipid peroxidation marker 4‐Hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE) was elevated, indicating that Nrf2 was essential for the KD‐mediated protection against ferroptosis.

These two studies showed that both short‐ and long‐term KD could prevent neurodegeneration in AD mouse models, alleviating AD‐related disturbances of cognitive function, pathology, and ferroptosis. Ferroptosis has been shown to be regulated by Nrf2, which modulates the expression of proteins associated with iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation. 15 , 40 Although it was concluded in both studies that KD alleviated ferroptosis in AD, it can be questioned whether the used models correctly mimic human AD. While sleep deprivation may be a risk factor for AD, a model solely relying on sleep deprivation to induce AD may not encapsulate the complexity of the disease. Additionally, the APP/PS1 model induces amyloidosis, but not AD‐related tau pathology. Again, this model does not fully model the complexity of human AD, thereby decreasing the validity of the results. However, the studies still showed the general neuroprotective effects of KD, which can be studied further in the context of specific diseases.

Intermittent fasting regimes

Dietary regimes that cycle between periods of regular eating and fasting, also called IF regimes, have emerged as an attractive strategy to lose weight, improve overall health, and as a preventive treatment for neurodegenerative disease. 56 , 57 , 75 Similarly to KD, IF promotes the use of fat as an energy source, in the form of ketone bodies. 76 Following an IF regimen, cognitive function is improved in adults with MCI, 64 , 77 dopamine depletion and motor deficiencies are reduced in PD rodent 78 and macaque models, 79 multiple sclerosis (MS) progression is delayed in a mouse model, 80 and cortical neuronal loss and cognitive decline is reduced in a model of ischemic stroke (Table 1). 81 Although there is a prominent neuroprotective effect of IF regimes, there is no consensus on the mechanism of action. One of the main hypotheses involves IF improving mitochondrial function, since mitochondria are responsible for 90% of the cellular ROS production, and mitochondrial damage results in enhanced production of ROS. 82 , 83 Therefore, improving mitochondrial function limits ROS production and therefore oxidative damage, which has been extensively reviewed. 75 , 82

In addition to the mitochondrial effects of IF, 1 month of IF was shown to alter the expression of ferroptosis proteins in a TBI mouse model in a recent study by Yang et al. 14 Here, a 1‐month IF regimen decreased cortical iron accumulation, prevented ferroptosis, and improved TBI‐induced cognitive impairment. Expression of the redox‐related protein Gpx4 and heat shock protein beta‐1 (Hspb1), a regulator of iron import, was increased with the intervention. In contrast, the IF regime resulted in a decreased expression of the ferroptosis‐inhibitory genes Nrf2 and xCT (Slc7a11), which was attributed to a possible secondary effect upon initiation of ferroptosis.

Overall, IF regimes have proven to be neuroprotective in various neurodegenerative disorders, hypothesized to happen through improved mitochondrial function. Evidence suggests that IF can enhance mitochondrial metabolism and bioenergetics by improving insulin sensitivity, a process potentially mediated by increased levels of BHB. 84 , 85 Additionally, recent evidence suggests that modulation of brain iron homeostasis may also contribute to the neuroprotective properties of IF. 14 Although changes in iron metabolism have not been studied in the context of IF in neurodegeneration, its effects may parallel those observed with KD. In this way, ketone bodies induced by IF, or BHB specifically, may activate Nrf2 and thus prevent ferroptosis by regulation of iron, lipid, and/or redox metabolism. 15

Mediterranean and related diets

The traditional dietary pattern consumed in Mediterranean countries, namely, the MeDi, is characterized by a high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, and legumes. Fish, poultry, and alcohol, in particular red wine, paired with meals, are consumed moderately. Intake of red and processed meats is limited, and olive oil is used as the main fat source in the diet. 58 , 59 Adherence to the MeDi has been associated with decreased risk of developing cardiovascular disease, 86 but also with benefits in cognitive functioning (Table 1). 10

The cognitive benefits of the MeDi, investigated in human studies, include improved cognitive performance, 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 slowed cognitive decline, 91 , 92 , 93 decreased risk for cognitive impairment, 91 , 94 , 95 and decreased risk of developing AD 94 and PD. 96 , 97 , 98 However, findings are not entirely consistent; several prospective observational studies have reported no association between MeDi adherence and cognitive decline. 99 , 100 , 101 Emerging evidence also suggests that the neuroprotective effects of the MeDi may be sex dependent. For example, a 9‐year observational follow‐up study in older women reported no link between dietary patterns and cognitive decline, 102 while other observational studies observed cognitive benefits in men but not women. 103 , 104 Notably, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) outcomes may not fully align with other cognitive assessments, 105 underscoring the need for complementary testing.

The extent of MeDi's neuroprotective effects may also be dependent on specific dietary components. In the PREDIMED‐NAVARRA randomized controlled trial (RCT), the MeDi supplemented with extra‐virgin olive oil or nuts improved cognition compared to a low‐fat control diet over 6.5 years. 106 Yet, a subsequent analysis found that only the olive oil group consistently improved fluency and memory, 107 while both supplementations resulted in cognitive benefits in another study. 108

A key approach to studying brain iron content in humans is the use of iron‐sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, such as susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM). The relationship between diet and MRI‐derived brain iron has been examined in several observational studies, as reviewed by Zachariou et al. 109 In a cohort of healthy adults (mean age 43.2 years), SWI‐derived brain iron measures revealed sex‐dependent links to dietary patterns, with higher vegetable intake associated with increased brain iron in men but decreased levels in women. 110 In cognitively healthy older individuals, higher intake of nutrients characteristic of the MeDi (e.g., nuts, fish, and healthy oils) associated with lower QSM‐derived brain iron and better working memory, in both cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses. 111 , 112 While these studies did not examine the MeDi specifically, they highlighted that MeDi‐style nutritional components may be beneficially related to brain iron load.

Modifications of the MeDi, such as the DASH and MIND diets, have been developed to enhance specific health outcomes. Both have been linked to slower cognitive decline and reduced AD risk, 113 with the MIND diet demonstrating the strongest neuroprotective associations. 113 MIND adherence correlates with better cognitive performance, 114 , 115 , 116 reduced age‐related cognitive decline, 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 , 120 and lower risk of dementia 116 , 121 , 122 or PD. 97 , 123 Some studies even suggest superior neuroprotective efficacy compared to the MeDi, 122 though others report limited or absent effects. 116 , 124 For instance, de Crom et al. observed a reduced dementia risk only early in the follow‐up period of an observational study, potentially reflecting reverse causality, where neurodegenerative progression leads to deteriorating diet quality and further cognitive decline. 116 This highlights the importance of cautious interpretation when linking nutrition to neurodegenerative outcomes.

Previously, the neuroprotective effects of the MeDi were related to its anti‐inflammatory properties, and it has been suggested that the MIND diet decreases neuroinflammation or oxidative stress. 10 , 125 However, individual components of these dietary regimes have been associated with decreased ferroptosis, meaning that the neuroprotective effects of these diets may be partially explained by their anti‐ferroptotic properties. Both the MeDi and MIND are characterized by high intake of fruits, vegetables, and legumes, which are rich in the polyphenols galangin, 126 quercetin, 127 , 128 , 129 and kaempferol. 130 Additionally, fruits and vegetables are rich in vitamins E, 13 , 131 , 132 , 133 A, 134 and K 135 and sulforaphane. 136 , 137 , 138 The moderate consumption of red wine and fish in the MeDi and MIND diets result in increased intake of the polyphenol resveratrol 139 and the fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), 140 respectively. All these dietary components were previously described to decrease ferroptosis in the brain in preclinical studies, which showed a possible relation between the MeDi and MIND diets and neuroprotection through decreased ferroptosis (further specified in chapter 4).

Polyphenolic compounds as potent inhibitors of ferroptosis

Aside from complete dietary regimes, separate dietary compounds may also exert neuroprotective effects (Table 2). Polyphenols, for example, are a diverse group of plant‐derived molecules with a wide array of biological activities, among which antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects have been reported. Additionally, some polyphenolic compounds have been reported to be possible ferroptosis inhibitors in vitro and in vivo. 12

For example, the neuroprotective properties of the polyphenol galangin have been attributed to its ability to reduce ferroptosis. When galangin was used as a treatment after cerebral ischemia‐reperfusion (I/R) injury in gerbils, lipid peroxidation, Gsh depletion, and iron accumulation were ameliorated, and expression of the redox enzyme Gpx4 was increased. 126 This indicates that galangin acts on all three pathways involved in ferroptosis: lipid peroxidation, iron metabolism, and redox regulation. However, how exactly iron metabolism is altered by galangin has not yet been investigated.

Additionally, there is a polyphenolic compound that can be extracted from the skin of grapes and wine, called resveratrol, which has been linked to several components of the ferroptosis pathway. The neuroprotective effects of resveratrol have been studied in rodents modeling ischemic injuries, where pretreatment with resveratrol decreased ferroptosis. 139 , 141 Mice from a model for subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) that were given 30 mg/kg of resveratrol intraperitoneally in the 3 days prior to injury showed signs of decreased brain ferroptosis. 141 Resveratrol was argued to act on ferroptosis through the activation of Sirt1. This results in activation of Nrf2 and, therefore, upregulation of Gpx4 and the Gpx4‐independent lipid peroxide inhibitor ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (Fsp1), both averting lipid peroxidation. Similar effects of resveratrol treatment were found in a rat model for cerebral I/R injury. 139 The rats were given intraperitoneal injections with 30 mg/kg resveratrol daily in the 7 days prior to the injury. This treatment resulted in decreased ischemic damage, infarction volume, degenerative neurons, and ferroptosis. Furthermore, expression of Gpx4 and Fth was increased, which resulted in reduced ferroptosis and increased iron storage, thereby linking the effects to both the redox‐ and iron‐metabolism pathways of ferroptosis. Overall, resveratrol decreases the occurrence of brain ferroptosis, which may be related to the attenuation of lipid peroxidation, redox enzymes, and iron metabolism.

Curcumin, which is abundantly present in turmeric, is also a polyphenolic compound with neuroprotective properties through antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐ferroptotic effects. In a rat model for AD, dietary supplementation of curcumin decreased memory loss, Aβ deposition, synaptic loss, and oxidative damage. 142 This decrease of oxidative damage may link curcumin to ferroptosis, because iron can drive ferroptosis through the production of ROS. This has been confirmed in primary cortical neurons of the HT22 cell line, where both the presence of ROS and ferroptosis were decreased upon treatment with curcumin. 143 In mice, curcumin treatment resulted in the upregulation of both Nrf2 and Gpx4, connecting curcumin to the redox‐regulatory pathway of ferroptosis. 143 However, curcumin has several limitations, including poor oral bioavailability and limited ability to cross the BBB, which limits the use of curcumin as a dietary supplement for its neuroprotective properties. 144 The use of curcumin nanoparticles may overcome the hurdles of curcumin delivery. 143

Flavonoids, a subclass of polyphenolic compounds, have been associated with neuroprotection and the regulation of ferroptosis. For instance, baicalein is a flavonoid polyphenolic compound found in thyme and roots from Scuetellaria baicalensis Georgi that has been described to inhibit brain ferroptosis through the modulation of iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation. 128 , 145 When mice were subjected to TBI and received baicalein as a treatment 10 to 15 min after injury, both oxidation and ferroptosis were decreased, resulting in improved outcomes. 146 Here, baicalein was described as an inhibitor of arachidonate 12‐lipoxygenase (Alox12) and arachidonate 15‐lipoxygenase (Alox15), which are enzymes driving lipid peroxidation. Therefore, the reported protective effects of baicalein were attributed to its ability to reduce lipid peroxidation. However, baicalein has also been described to alter brain iron accumulation in a mouse model involving transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO). 145 Intraperitoneal administration of 10 mg/kg baicalein for seven consecutive days prior to occlusion only slightly reduced infarct volume. In contrast, a dose of 80 mg/kg produced marked effects on several components of the ferroptosis pathway. This higher dose resulted in significantly decreased infarct volume and decreased expression of the lipid‐peroxidation byproduct malondialdehyde (MDA). It also increased the levels of Gsh, the expression of the antioxidant enzyme Gpx4, and the expression of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xCT (Slc7a11). Additionally, ferroptosis and iron content were reported to be decreased. The decrease in labile iron content was attributed to the increased expression of iron‐storage proteins Fth and mitochondrial ferritin (Ftmt).

Catechins, which are flavonoids that can be isolated from green tea, are potent inhibitors of lipid peroxidation 147 , 148 and have been described as iron chelators. 149 In 2004, before ferroptosis had been described as a driver of neurodegeneration, Mandel et al. investigated the effects of (–)‐Epigallocatechin‐3‐Gallate (EGCG) in a PD mouse model. 150 Here, oral administration of EGCG resulted in neuroprotection and decreased iron accumulation in the substantia nigra. This was an early indication of a connection between EGCG and ferroptosis in neurological damage. More recently, catechins have been studied as a treatment that can be given after the occurrence of TBI in mice. 151 The compound (−)‐Epicatechin (15 mg/kg) was administered orally 3 h after TBI and subsequently once daily for seven consecutive days. This treatment reduced lesion volume, edema, neuronal cell death, and improved both short‐ and long‐term cognitive performance (at 3 and 28 days after TBI). Additionally, brain iron depositions were reduced, and Nrf2 was upregulated through the depletion of its inhibitor Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein 1 (Keap1). Although Nrf2 was previously identified as the key mediator linking neuroprotective dietary compounds and ferroptosis, this study describes the reduction in iron deposition as being driven by a Nrf2‐independent pathway. As a result of these promising effects, a phase III clinical trial (NCT02731495) has been set up in which TBI patients are given EGCG. 128 , 152

The flavonoid quercetin is found in fruits, vegetables, and medicinal plants. This compound was reported to decrease ferroptosis in HT22 cells and the Aβ infusion mouse model for AD, resulting in the amelioration of short‐term memory deficits. 127 Additionally, quercetin has been described as an iron chelator that limits ROS formation and upregulates both Sirt1 and Nrf2 expression. 128 In a MCAO rat model of ischemic stroke, pretreatment with quercetin improved neurological outcomes and reduced infarct volume. 129 These protective effects were attributed to activation of the Nrf2/Heme oxygenase 1 (HO‐1) pathway, in which HO‐1 acts as an antioxidant downstream of Nrf2. Notably, these effects were eliminated when Nrf2 was inhibited with ML385 in HT22 cells.

Other flavonoids involved in neuroprotection and ferroptosis modulation include kaempferol, carthamin, morachalcone D, luteolin, and fisetin. When primary mouse cortical neurons with induced ferroptosis were treated with kaempferol, ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and iron accumulation were reduced through activation of the Nrf2 signaling axis. 130 Additionally, kaempferol also allowed cells to retain normal mitochondrial structures. Carthamin, which is a flavonoid isolated from safflowers, attenuated ferroptosis through the amelioration of ROS production, lipid peroxidation, Gsh depletion, and iron accumulation when given for 2 weeks prior to cerebral I/R injury in rats. 153 Additionally, morachalcone D, a prenylated flavonoid isolated from mulberry leaf tea, activates the Nrf2 pathway in HT22 cells, leading to upregulation of Gpx4, xCT (Slc7a11), and Gsh, thereby preventing ferroptosis through antioxidant effects. Treatment with morachalcone D also reduced ROS formation and iron accumulation. 154 A recent study investigated the anti‐ferroptotic properties of luteolin, a flavonoid primarily found in plant leaves such as clover blossom and parsley. They reported that luteolin's anti‐ferroptotic effects were diminished upon co‐treatment with the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385, directly linking luteolin's action to Nrf2 activation. 155 Similarly, the flavonoid fisetin has been reported to decrease ferroptosis in the context of TBI via the Nrf2 pathway. 156

In conclusion, polyphenolic compounds, including flavonoids, have been proven to be potent ferroptosis inhibitors. Most of the previously mentioned polyphenols affect lipid peroxidation and the expression of antioxidant enzymes and iron depositions, thereby preventing ferroptosis via several mechanisms. Although these compounds are promising in in vitro and in vivo models, they are only sparsely investigated in a human setting.

Neuroprotective and anti‐ferroptotic potential of other dietary compounds

In addition to the broad range of polyphenolic compounds that have neuroprotective effects linked to anti‐ferroptotic mechanisms, other dietary components have also been shown to reduce ferroptosis in neurodegenerative diseases. These include vitamins, omega‐3 fatty acids, amino acids, and various bioactive molecules with distinct biochemical properties. The cognitive benefits associated with these compounds have been related to anti‐ferroptotic effects, particularly through the reduction of brain iron accumulation and oxidative stress. In what follows, we summarize the cognitive benefits and the associations with ferroptosis for a selection of dietary components (Table 2).

Vitamins

Vitamin E has eight naturally occurring chemical forms (alpha‐, beta‐, gamma‐, and delta‐tocopherol and ‐tocotrienol) with variable levels of biological activity. 157 Alpha‐tocopherol is the only chemical form that is recognized by the human body when ingested and is present in nuts, seeds, vegetable oils, green leafy vegetables, and fortified cereals. 158 , 159 The presence of a hydroxyl group allows for the neutralization of a variety of free radicals including ROS, explaining the antioxidant potential of vitamin E. 160 However, vitamin E has also been linked to neurological effects. Previous reviews reported that a vitamin E deficiency increases the risk of developing early‐onset neurological disease. 161 , 162 Although there is a link between neurodegeneration and vitamin E deficiency, studies investigating the neuroprotective effects of vitamin E supplementation have yielded inconclusive results. On one hand, studies conclude that vitamin E holds great potential in preventing or delaying neurodegeneration. 163 , 164 , 165 , 166 However, several studies have concluded that there is no neuroprotective benefit of vitamin E. 163 , 165 , 167 , 168 Even when vitamin E is reported to have neuroprotective potential, this conclusion may be based primarily on positive effects observed in a single questionnaire assessing cognitive function rather than a range of diverse outcome measures. 166 Despite contradictory results regarding the neuroprotective effects of vitamin E, a substantial number of studies have focused on unravelling the biological mechanism of the potential neuroprotective effects. Next to its antioxidant potential, 160 vitamin E has also been associated with anti‐inflammatory properties 169 , 170 and specifically the ability to suppress ferroptosis in the brain. 13 , 131 Several in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed vitamin E as an inhibitor of ferroptosis, which is mainly attributed to the upregulation of the antioxidant pathway. 132 , 133 However, vitamin E has also shown anti‐ferroptotic protection in mice depleted of Gpx4, which is one of the main proteins in the antioxidant pathway inhibiting ferroptosis. 133 This indicates that at least a part of the anti‐ferroptotic properties of vitamin E is independent of the antioxidant regulation within ferroptosis.

In addition to vitamin E, vitamins A and K have also been shown to suppress ferroptosis. Vitamin A, or retinol, is present in a wide variety of dietary components, including animal products, fruits, and vegetables. Retinol and its metabolites can cross the BBB and have been shown to protect against lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in cultured neuronal cells, likely via the mechanism of radical trapping. 134 In this process, retinoids directly react with lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby terminating the chain reactions of lipid peroxidation that drive ferroptosis. Beyond direct radical scavenging, the bioactive metabolite retinoic acid activates nuclear receptors (Nrf2, retinoic acid receptor [RAR] and retinoid X receptor [RXR]) that upregulate transcriptional programs involved in ferroptosis suppression (e.g., Gpx4, Fsp1) as well as lipid remodeling enzymes like acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 3 (Acsl3) and stearoyl‐CoA desaturase (Scd), which modulate membrane lipid composition to reduce susceptibility to oxidation. 171 Vitamin K is present in spinach, broccoli, kale, and brussels sprouts. Its reduced form blocks lipid peroxidation and therefore ferroptosis via Fsp1 in various cell models, including the neuronal HT22 cell line. 135 It contributes to ferroptosis suppression through its reduced hydroquinone form (VKH2), which is produced via the vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1L1 (Vkorc1l1) and acts as a potent radical‐trapping antioxidant. 171 , 172 Together, vitamins A and K act through complementary mechanisms of direct radical scavenging or activation of nuclear receptor‐mediated transcriptional programs to protect against ferroptosis. However, these results primarily come from cell culture studies, and confirmation of their mechanisms in the brain specifically will require further investigation using animal models or human biomaterials.

Folic acid or folate, a B9 vitamin, is naturally present in a variety of foods, including leafy green vegetables, legumes, beans, and beef liver. 173 As a known antioxidant, folic acid plays a role in ferroptosis by contributing to cysteine biosynthesis, thereby promoting the synthesis of Gsh. 174 A recent study by Wang et al. reported that folic acid supplementation inhibits ferroptosis by acting on the xCT (Slc7a11)‐Gsh‐Gpx4 antioxidant pathway. 175 Specifically, 3‐month‐old Sprague Dawley rats were exposed to a diet with increased folic acid (4.0 mg/kg, compared to a normal 2.0 mg/kg). After 22 months of a high folic acid diet, iron deposition in the cortex and hippocampus was decreased, and the expression of cysteine, xCT (Slc7a11), Gpx4, and Fth was ameliorated. Further investigation of the mechanism was performed in the HT22 neuronal cell line, with induced ferroptosis. Folic acid was found to bind the active site of xCT (Slc7a11), which resulted in decreased cell death, intracellular iron, and lipid peroxidation, while these effects were diminished in a xCT (Slc7a11) knockdown model. Nonetheless, the potential impact of folic acid on upstream or alternative modulators of ferroptosis, including the transcription factor Nrf2, remains to be thoroughly investigated.

Vitamin D, which is most commonly obtained from oily fish, has recently been shown to improve cognitive impairment and alleviate ferroptosis in the context of aging via activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in mice. 176 In this study, 3‐month‐old male C57BL/6J mice received daily D‐galactose (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) with or without vitamin D (1 µg/kg, subcutaneously every other day) for 8 weeks. Mechanistically, improvements in behavioral outcomes were associated with decreased expression of Acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (Acsl4), Alox15, and MDA. Acsl4 is involved in the preparation of PUFAs for incorporation into phospholipid membranes, thereby facilitating lipid peroxidation. 177 Alox15 catalyzes lipid peroxidation by producing lipid peroxides. 178 Alongside these changes, upregulation of Gpx4 and Gsh were reported, resulting in restored antioxidant defenses. Importantly, the protective effects of vitamin D were abrogated, and Nrf2 expression remained reduced when the vitamin D receptor was inhibited, highlighting the necessity of receptor‐mediated Nrf2 activation for neuroprotection.

Animal‐derived compounds

The long‐chain omega‐3 fatty acid DHA is the most prominent omega‐3 fatty acid in the brain, influencing numerous neuronal and glial processes. 179 , 180 Dietary sources of DHA include cold‐water fatty fish, such as salmon, fish oils, and seaweed. 181 DHA has proven to have protective effects against neurodegeneration 182 and even improved memory and reaction time in healthy young adults with a dietary DHA deficiency in a RCT. 183 Similarly, another RCT investigating the effects of 12‐month DHA supplementation through fish oil on cognitive function in MCI patients showed improved short‐term and working memory. 184 However, 18 months of DHA supplementation did not improve cognitive function of mild‐to‐moderate AD patients. 185 Overall, it might be concluded that DHA can be neuroprotective if supplementation precedes extensive neurodegeneration. Mechanistically, the neuroprotective effects of DHA may be linked to ferroptosis. A study in an AD model with rats showed that oral DHA supplementation reduced lipid peroxidation and ROS levels, both of which have been described as drivers of ferroptosis. 140 Future studies investigating multiple pathways that lead to ferroptosis, including iron metabolism, may clarify a correlation between DHA and this form of iron‐dependent cell death.

Taurine is a sulfur‐containing amino acid present in many dietary products, including beef, shellfish, dark‐meat poultry, and dairy. 186 Besides its general antioxidant effects, taurine has been reported to be neuroprotective and have specific anti‐inflammatory properties in the brain. 187 , 188 , 189 , 190 , 191 Additionally, the relationship between taurine and ferroptosis in strokes was previously reviewed. 192 Liu et al. investigated the neuroprotective potential of taurine in the context of SAH. 193 Taurine levels in the CSF of patients who had suffered from SAH were significantly decreased within 3 days of the injury. In a SAH mouse model, taurine supplementation improved neurobehavioral functions while reducing brain edema and BBB permeability. This showed a clear correlation between taurine levels and neuroprotective effects. Furthermore, the authors aimed to unravel the underlying mechanism of taurine's neuroprotective effects. For this, they used mice suffering from SAH and compared taurine‐induced changes, which may have contributed to previously reported neuroprotective effects. Taurine activated the protein kinase B (AKT)/glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK‐3)β/β‐catenin signaling pathway, a key regulator of cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism. Additionally, taurine supplementation resulted in the reduction of lipid peroxidation, increased expression of xCT (Slc7a11), the antioxidant enzyme Gpx4, and decreased iron content in the ipsilateral cortex. Therefore, these results indicate that taurine exerts its neuroprotective effects through inhibition of ferroptosis by the regulation of the AKT/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling pathway.

Plant‐derived metabolites

Another dietary compound that has excellent neuroprotective effects is the isothiocyanate sulforaphane, which is present in cruciferous vegetables including broccoli, cabbage, and kale. 136 Supplementation with sulforaphane appears to be protective against AD, 194 , 195 PD, 196 , 197 Huntington's disease (HD), 198 MS, 199 and SAH 200 in various cell and animal models. Additionally, it improved cognitive function and limited edema in a TBI rat model. 201 Upon sulforaphane supplementation, the transcription factor Nrf2 is activated by the removal of its inhibitor Keap1. 202 This activation of Nrf2 is believed to be the driver behind the neuroprotective effects of sulforaphane and may connect the effects of sulforaphane to ferroptosis. 137 , 138 , 196 , 197 , 198 , 200 , 202 , 203

Lastly, glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) has been described as a potential novel agent for preventing or treating ferroptosis‐related disorders. 192 GA is a bioactive component present in liquorice root, which is widely used as a flavoring agent in the food and pharmaceutical industries. The effects of GA on ferroptosis may be cell‐type dependent. Upon treating triple‐negative breast cancer cells with GA, ferroptosis is induced by the inhibition of the antioxidant defense system (Gsh, Gpx4, xCT [Slc7a11]) and increased ROS production. 125 However, GA has also been shown to prevent ferroptosis that has been induced by the well‐known ferroptosis inducer 56 (FIN56) in the neuronal cell line HT22. 204 The latter anti‐ferroptotic effect has been attributed to the inhibition of labile iron accumulation and the maintenance of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) levels in the neurons. The prevention of iron accumulation inhibits ferroptosis by limiting ROS formation, while maintenance of CoQ10 levels indirectly inhibits ferroptosis by preventing lipid peroxidation, independent of the antioxidant defense system. 205

Essential minerals

For neurons to respond to ferroptosis using the selenoprotein Gpx4, sufficient selenium must be available. Selenium is a dietary compound present in Brazil nuts and various meats that are proven to be anti‐ferroptotic in several stroke models. 206 , 207 A single dose of selenium increased Gpx4 expression, protected neurons, and improved behavioral outcomes in a hemorrhagic stroke model. 208 The upregulation of Gpx4 has been attributed to the ability of selenium to activate the transcription factors transcription factor activator protein 2 gamma (Tfap2c) and specificity protein 1 (Sp1). However, selenium not only alters Gpx4 expression. In rats with MCAO pretreated with sodium selenite (0.4 mg/kg/day for 4 weeks) before reoxygenation, ROS levels, lipid peroxidation, iron levels, and Acsl4 expression decreased, and in addition Gpx4 expression increased. 209 The potency of selenium to improve cognitive function has also been investigated in human studies. Selenium levels in the serum, erythrocytes, and nails of AD patients was lower compared to that of healthy individuals in an observational case‐control study. 210 Although peripheral measurements do not directly mirror selenium availability in the brain, evidence suggests the brain maintains preferential selenium homeostasis, and its selenium level maintenance is prioritized over that of other organs. 211 Observational studies have not consistently linked selenium status to cognitive performance. Although selenium supplementation, whether as sodium selenate 212 or Brazil nuts, 213 has improved cognitive function in AD patients 212 and individuals with MCI, 213 such associations have not been observed in cognitively healthy older adults. 214

THE MECHANISMS CONNECTING DIET TO FERROPTOSIS

By modulating ferroptosis, dietary interventions have been shown to have prominent neuroprotective properties. Rather than acting exclusively on isolated proteins, the interventions appear to affect a wide variety of targets across lipid peroxidation, antioxidant defense, and iron metabolism regulatory networks. Ferroptosis itself is a regulated form of cell death, characterized by iron‐dependent ROS formation and lipid peroxidation, but its regulation involves a complex network of signaling pathways extending beyond iron availability. 13 , 37 , 215 , 216 Here, we describe a regulatory network in which dietary interventions may affect lipid peroxidation, stimulate antioxidant defense, or modulate iron homeostasis (Figure 1). However, their impact varies with nutritional context, pathology, and disease model.

The expression of several ferroptosis‐related proteins is regulated by the transcription factor Nrf2, which is activated through Sirt1 and repressed by Keap1 (B). The antioxidant defense system (C), with its main contributor Gpx4, can inhibit lipid peroxidation (D), which is an indispensable process within ferroptosis. All these pathways are affected by dietary interventions (green arrows and inhibitor signs). Suppression of lipid peroxidation is repeatedly described for a range of dietary supplements including for catechins, 147 , 148 DHA, 140 taurine, 193 selenium, 209 and a variety of polyphenolic compounds including baicalein, 128 kaempferol, 130 carthamin, 153 and galangin. 126 Also, the KD has been reported to decrease lipid peroxidation in animal models. 15 , 40 Mechanistic insights reveal that dietary interventions may act by downregulating pro‐oxidative enzymes such as Acsl4 and Alox15, 176 both of which promote lipid peroxide generation and ferroptosis. In contrast, Acsl3, another lipid‐metabolizing enzyme, appears to confer protection by enriching membranes with monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) rather than PUFAs, thereby reducing their susceptibility to oxidation. Upregulation of Acsl3 has been described as an effect of vitamin A. 171 Dietary modulation also enhances the activity of non‐enzymatic defense systems. For instance, Fsp1 prevents lipid peroxidation by regenerating the antioxidant ubiquinol from CoQ10, maintaining membrane integrity and preventing ferroptotic cascade activation. Both Fsp1 40 , 135 , 141 , 171 , 205 and CoQ10 141 , 204 , 205 are increasingly reported in the context of dietary modulation studies.

To counteract lipid peroxidation, cells rely on antioxidant enzymes, which enzymatically convert oxidized lipids into non‐reactive forms. For this process, membrane transporter xCT (Slc7a11) imports cystine in exchange for glutamate, supplying the substrate required for Gsh synthesis. Within the Gsh‐dependent reaction cycle, the antioxidant enzyme Gpx4 reduces toxic lipid hydroperoxides (L–OOH) to non‐reactive lipid alcohols (L–OH). This process oxidizes Gsh to glutathione disulfide (Gssg), which can be recycled to maintain antioxidant capacity. Dietary interventions enhance this pathway through upregulation of its components. Both KD and IF increase Gpx4, 14 , 15 , 40 while KD additionally prevents the depletion of Gsh, 14 , 40 indicating improved cystine utilization and reduced oxidative stress. Polyphenols and antioxidant nutrients act similarly: folic acid, baicalein, and morachalcone D increase expression of xCT (Slc7a11), Gsh, and Gpx4 145 , 154 , 175 ; taurine upregulates xCT (Slc7a11) and Gpx4 193 ; and carthamin, galangin, and vitamin D enhance Gpx4 and Gsh. 126 , 153 , 176 Some compounds selectively activate one part of this axis; quercetin elevates Gsh, 127 while resveratrol, curcumin, and selenium primarily boost Gpx4. 139 , 141 , 143 , 207 , 208 , 209 Through regulation of this antioxidant axis, dietary interventions strengthen lipid peroxide detoxification.

The production of ROS, via either the iron‐dependent Fenton and Harber/Weiss reactions or enzymatic processes, drives the occurrence of lipid peroxidation, thereby initiating ferroptosis. 13 , 37 The cytosolic labile iron pool dynamically exchanges with mitochondrial iron. Within mitochondria – the main site of ROS production – iron imported from the cytosol participates in heme and Fe–S cluster synthesis but also exists as a mitochondrial labile iron subpool. 217 When unbuffered, this pool catalyzes intramitochondrial ROS formation, damaging mitochondrial DNA and phospholipids and amplifying lipid peroxidation. Mitochondria therefore act as major iron sinks, and dysregulated mitochondrial iron metabolism, through excess uptake or impaired ferritin‐like sequestration by Ftmt, can initiate ferroptosis‐like oxidative injury. 217 Additionally, the mitochondrial iron exporter mitoneet (CDGSH Iron Sulfur Domain 1: Cisd1) prevents iron accumulation and lipid peroxide formation in mitochondria; its loss enhances ferroptotic cell death, further emphasizing the link between mitochondrial iron handling and ferroptosis susceptibility. 217 Although mitochondrial iron is the main contributor to iron‐dependent ROS formation, often the cytosolic labile iron pool is investigated in the context of dietary interventions. This iron pool can be reduced through dietary interventions, as described in the KD 40 and IF 14 regimes, upon supplementation of baicalein, 145 kaempferol, 130 carthamin, 153 galangin, 126 morachalcone D 154 or catechins, 150 , 151 folic acid, 175 taurine, 193 selenium, 209 or GA. 204 This decrease in labile iron may be attributed to iron chelation, 128 , 149 , 171 which refers to the process of physical binding between a compound and free iron particles, thereby reducing the availability of labile iron that can participate in the catalyzation of ROS. 218 Alternatively, the labile iron pool can be decreased through altered iron metabolism, including an increase in iron storage through upregulation of Fth 15 , 40 , 139 , 145 , 175 or Ftmt, 145 upregulation of iron exporter Fpn 15 or decreased iron import via the Tfr, 15 , 40 and divalent metal transporter 1 (Dmt1). 40 These alterations in iron metabolism have been most extensively described in the context of the KD. 15 , 40 Regulation of these targets likely occurs at the transcriptional level via activation of Nrf2, which promotes expression of Fth and Fpn while repressing Tfr and Dmt1. 219 , 220 The transcriptional activation of Fpn by Nrf2 involves displacement of the repressor Broad‐Complex, Tramtrack and Bric‐a‐brac (BTB) domain and Cap ‘n’ Collar (CNC) homolog 1 (Bach1) from antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the Fpn promoter, allowing Nrf2 to bind and stimulate expression. 221 This mechanism might be similar for other targets of Nrf2. Alternatively, in cancer cell models, decreased expression of Tfr has been linked to the inhibition of its stimulator Hspb1, independent of Nrf2. 222 , 223 In some contexts, Nrf2 also induces HO‐1, 129 which contributes to antioxidant defense by catalyzing the degradation of pro‐oxidant heme. 224 This activity leads to the production of biliverdin and carbon monoxide, which are described to have antioxidant properties. However, this reaction also leads to the production of ferrous iron, thereby increasing the labile iron pool. Thus, while HO‐1 activity supports antioxidant defense, it can paradoxically increase the labile iron pool and sensitize cells to ferroptosis. 15 , 224 , 225

Nrf2 functions as a central regulator of ferroptosis, integrating control over antioxidant defenses, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism. By interacting with AREs in the promoters of target genes, Nrf2 can stimulate antioxidant defense by promoting expression of Gpx4 and xCT (Slc7a11). Additionally, it induces expression of Fth and Ftmt, promoting iron storage. Nrf2 enhances Fpn expression, facilitating iron export, while repressing iron importers Tfr and Dmt1. Some regulations involve the displacement of the repressor Bach1 from AREs, allowing Nrf2 binding and transcriptional activation. Dietary interventions such as KD and supplementation with polyphenols (curcumin, quercetin, luteolin, fisetin, morachalcone D, catechins, sulforaphane) upregulate Nrf2 activity. 15 , 40 , 128 , 129 , 136 , 137 , 143 , 151 , 154 , 155 , 156 , 202 , 226 Ketone body BHB, which is formed during the KD and IF, may directly activate Nrf2 by metabolic intermediates succinate and fumarate modifying inhibitor Keap1, reducing its ability to inhibit Nrf2. 72 , 227 , 228 Nrf2 itself is upregulated upstream by Sirt1, which can be induced by the KD, 40 resveratrol, 141 and quercetin. 128 Sirt1 is a histone deacetylase and can deacetylate Nrf2, which leads to increased binding of Nrf2 to AREs in the promoters of its target genes. 229 However, a potential negative feedback mechanism exists whereby elevated labile iron impairs the ability of Nrf2 to bind these AREs. 230

Dietary interventions have been shown to influence the expression of proteins involved in antioxidant defense, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism. While the detailed mechanisms linking these dietary changes to protein expression remain to be fully elucidated, Nrf2 frequently emerges as the key regulator. It activates the transcription of various target genes responsible for these effects. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which most dietary interventions stimulate Nrf2 activation are still not well understood.

CONCLUSION

In this review, recent studies investigating dietary influences on brain iron accumulation and ferroptosis in neurodegenerative diseases were compared. Several preclinical articles reported anti‐ferroptotic effects of dietary regimes or components in addition to their general neuroprotective effects.

Dietary regimes, including the KD, IF, the MeDi, and the MIND diet, have neuroprotective properties. However, these properties were only connected to reduced ferroptosis for the KD in AD and IF in TBI mouse models. This anti‐ferroptotic effect of the KD was attributed to the activation of Nrf2, resulting in the upregulation of the expression of antioxidant enzymes and altered iron metabolism. For IF, no connection with Nrf2 was described, but similarly to what was described in the KD, antioxidant activity was increased, and iron content was reduced. Both the MeDi and MIND dietary regimes are rich in dietary components like polyphenols, vitamins, and fatty acid DHA, which have been described to decrease ferroptosis in the brain. The high content of such compounds may, at least partially, explain the neuroprotective properties of these diets. The described polyphenolic compounds proved to be potent inhibitors of ferroptosis in pre‐clinical studies. Some polyphenols, including baicalein, resveratrol, curcumin and morachalcone D were reported to enhance the expression of the antioxidant enzymes Gpx4 and Gsh. The anti‐ferroptotic effects of polyphenols are linked to several mechanisms, including enhancing antioxidant defenses, reducing lipid peroxidation and lowering iron levels, either by modulating iron metabolism or by acting as iron chelators. Occasionally, these anti‐ferroptotic effects were reported in combination with Nrf2 activation. Other dietary components may also possess anti‐ferroptotic properties by activating Nrf2 or inhibiting lipid peroxidation, but these have not yet been as extensively studied in this context.

Most of the anti‐ferroptotic effects have been described in a preventive context. However, some polyphenolic compounds have also shown neuroprotective properties if they are administered after neuronal injury in models, including strokes. The anti‐ferroptotic properties of dietary interventions may therefore be most applicable as a complementary method to delay neurological disease but may also be investigated further for their potential as treatment agents.

Current research on dietary modulation of ferroptosis is limited by several factors. Most studies rely on cell or animal models, with human research largely restricted to cognitive outcomes correlated to diet. Specific outcome markers in preclinical studies, such as MDA or 4‐HNE for lipid peroxidation, provide evidence of altered lipid peroxidation activity. However, these measurements alone do not clarify the molecular drivers through which diet attenuates lipid peroxidation or ferroptosis. By investigating intermediate players of lipid peroxidation, the mechanism may be uncovered. This also extends to the current understanding of dietary modulation of antioxidant defense, iron metabolism, or ferroptosis in general. Future studies should prioritize elucidating these mechanisms by examining complete signaling pathways. Additionally, the frequent use of (cytosolic) iron levels as indicators of ferroptosis warrants reconsideration. Iron concentration does not follow a linear relationship with ferroptosis occurrence. Furthermore, mitochondrial iron plays a more central role in driving ROS formation, in contrast to cytosolic iron. Expanding outcome measures to include subcellular iron pools and outcome markers reflecting the process of ferroptosis will offer more accurate investigation of the effects of diet on ferroptosis. While dietary interventions clearly influence the antioxidant axis, it is not yet established whether standalone antioxidant supplementation can fully suppress ferroptosis‐linked neurodegeneration, nor how dietary signals precisely govern the expression and activity of antioxidant components. Importantly, translational research connecting preclinical findings with outcomes of human studies should incorporate specific ferroptosis markers in dietary intervention trials. Mechanistic insights from animal studies, particularly those identifying diet‐responsive ferroptosis pathways such as iron metabolism, antioxidant defense, or lipid peroxidation, can directly inform the selection of biomarkers for human studies. Designing human studies on dietary interventions and brain health should therefore involve the integration of mechanistic biomarkers in combination with in vivo readouts for brain iron content, such as iron‐sensitive MRI techniques. Together with other clinical outcomes, these measures would enable a comprehensive evaluation of both the clinical effects and the mechanistic pathways engaged in dietary modulation. Addressing these gaps, through mechanistic clarity, refined outcome markers, and translational integration, will enhance the mechanistic understanding and therapeutic potential of diet‐based ferroptosis modulation.
