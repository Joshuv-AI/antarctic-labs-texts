Catheter-related cerebral air embolism presenting with multifocal intracerebral hemorrhages
Authors: Juntao Yin, Wan Wang, Weiwei Wang, Yanan Jia, Jing Sun, Yu Wang, Tingting Liu, Haikun Luo, Yuqing Wei
Published: 2025

Introduction

Cerebral air embolism (CAE) is an uncommon cause of stroke, typically arising when intravascular air enters the cerebral circulation via trauma or iatrogenic events (e.g., catheter insertion) (1). Although rare, CAE carries a high risk of severe neurological injury, with significant morbidity and mortality (1). Common risk factors include central venous catheter manipulation, endovascular or cardiothoracic interventions, laparoscopic surgery, and neurosurgical procedures performed in the sitting position (2). Clinically, CAE can be challenging to recognize, as patients often develop non-specific neurologic symptoms (altered consciousness, seizures, or acute stroke-like deficits) in close temporal relation to a precipitating procedure (2). Given its often abrupt presentation and potential to rapidly deteriorate, prompt diagnosis is critical.

Neuroimaging plays a pivotal role in confirming CAE. Non-contrast cranial computed tomography (CT) may reveal intracranial air (gas) within cerebral vessels or parenchyma, typically in distal arterial territories or watershed zones, accompanied by acute ischemic changes such as edema. However, CT can be unrevealing in subtle cases, making magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) more sensitive for detecting CAE-related brain injury (3). MRI classically shows multiple acute infarcts in a bilateral cortical or border-zone distribution, with a combination of cytotoxic and vasogenic edema reflecting vascular occlusion and inflammatory injury (1,4). Susceptibility-weighted MRI sequences (T2*-weighted imaging) often demonstrate punctate hypointensities; these were historically presumed to represent intravascular air bubbles that would dissipate, but autopsy evidence now suggests they correspond to micro-hemorrhagic infarctions (5).

Despite these insights, hemorrhagic manifestations of CAE are exceedingly rare. Only isolated case reports have documented intracerebral hemorrhage or hemorrhagic transformation due to cerebral air emboli, highlighting a gap in the literature (6). In this context, the present case is notable for demonstrating multifocal intracerebral hemorrhages attributable to CAE originating from an intra-arterial catheter during carotid recanalization—a novel radiological finding that expands the recognized spectrum of CAE-related brain injury.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old man with hypertension developed sudden loss of consciousness and right-sided hemiparesis during an endovascular recanalization procedure of the left internal carotid artery via arterial catheterization for chronic occlusion. Non-contrast CT performed 30 minutes after symptom onset showed multiple hypodense areas in the left hemisphere (Figure 1A). CAE was diagnosed, and the patient underwent hyperbaric oxygen therapy. Follow-up CT 9 hours later showed a reduction in hypodense areas but new multifocal hemorrhages (Figure 1B). On postoperative day 4, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) showed multiple areas of hemorrhage (Figure 1C). MRI revealed gyriform diffusion restriction in the left cortical regions (Figure 1D,1E) with extensive vasogenic edema (Figure 1F). To further investigate the possibility of paradoxical embolism, a transthoracic echocardiogram and a transcranial Doppler bubble test were performed. Both tests did not reveal the presence of a patent foramen ovale, atrial septal defect, or ventricular septal defect. The patient showed significant improvement in symptoms at 90 days post-follow-up. Discussion

Our case uniquely highlights multifocal intracerebral hemorrhages as an imaging manifestation of catheter-related cerebral CAE, a phenomenon not previously described in the literature. While CAE typically presents radiologically with ischemic changes—including combinations of cytotoxic and vasogenic edema—hemorrhagic transformation or primary hemorrhagic presentation remains exceedingly rare (7,8). The underlying mechanism for hemorrhagic conversion in CAE is uncertain, but we hypothesize that it could result from a rapid, massive influx of air disrupting the vascular integrity and causing direct endothelial damage, followed by secondary reperfusion injury upon air resolution. Animal models and autopsy studies suggest microvascular endothelial disruption and inflammatory responses triggered by air bubbles may contribute to hemorrhagic events, as activated leukocytes release proinflammatory cytokines leading to blood-brain barrier compromise (1,2,7).

The identification of hemorrhage in this case posed significant challenges in the differential diagnosis. Other causes of multifocal cerebral hemorrhages, such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy, hypertensive encephalopathy, hemorrhagic metastases, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, or fat embolism syndrome, required careful exclusion. The temporal relationship to catheterization, distribution of lesions consistent with vascular territories affected by air emboli, and the absence of systemic signs associated with alternative diagnoses strongly supported CAE as the etiology.

From a clinical management perspective, the recognition of hemorrhagic CAE is crucial. Hemorrhagic transformation significantly impacts therapeutic decisions, particularly regarding the use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents, which are commonly considered in ischemic CAE scenarios. Prompt identification also facilitates early initiation of hyperbaric oxygen therapy, shown to be effective in reducing air emboli size and mitigating secondary brain injury. Follow-up imaging protocols should include SWI sequences to better detect hemorrhagic changes and guide prognosis.

In terms of prognosis, although multifocal hemorrhages theoretically indicate a more severe cerebral injury, our patient exhibited significant neurological improvement, suggesting that early and aggressive hyperbaric intervention may alter clinical outcomes positively. However, the long-term implications of hemorrhagic CAE remain unclear, highlighting the need for further observational studies and case reports to delineate its true clinical significance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, clinicians should be aware that CAE can present with multifocal hemorrhagic lesions. This case underscores the importance of rapid recognition, appropriate imaging evaluation, careful differentiation from other cerebrovascular pathologies, and tailored clinical management to optimize patient outcomes.
