Quantitatively detecting ground surface changes of slope failure caused by heavy rain using ALOS-2/PALSAR-2 data: a case study in Japan
Authors: Xuechen Wang, Hiroyuki Honda, Ibrahim Djamaluddin, Hisatoshi Taniguchi, Yasuhiro Mitani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany SAR images have been utilized for geologic disasters investigations with the continuous launch of new Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites such as ALOS-2/PALSAR-2. However, to proactively respond to transient slope failures caused by heavy rainfall, rapid extraction of areas of surface change accompanying slope failures is required. This study proposes two methods for quantitatively extracting slope failure areas using L-band SAR observations with slope units (SUs) as the evaluation units. The first method is based on the threshold method, which automates the selection of thresholds for various disaster-affected conditions, such as land use and topography. The second method is a machine-learning-based density ratio estimation method, which uses multi-temporal periodic observation data and pre- and post-disaster data to detect outliers through feature selection optimization. In the observation direction with the shortest satellite observation period, the F1 score (The F1 score is the harmonic mean of the precision and recall) of the threshold method for accuracy evaluation is 61.91%, and the F1 score of the density ratio method is 65.87%. Both methods can reduce the problem of low extraction accuracy caused by the effect of speckle noise. When slope failure occurs, both methods can extract the area of surface change within hours of a disaster. The method proposed in this study displays good applicability in supporting emergency rescue and the prevention of secondary disasters.Keywords: SAR, ALOS-2/PALSAR-2, Slope failure, Ground surface changes, Slope unit, Heavy rainSubject terms: Natural hazards, Geomorphology, Environmental impact

Introduction

Slope failure is a common geological hazard worldwide, causing substantial economic losses and human casualties every year1. Slope failure varies depending on the geometry of the slope, the type of slope surface, and the intensity of rainfall2. In many countries, slope instability due to heavy rainfall occurs every year. Slope failure due to heavy rainfall is a phenomenon that can cause severe damage to social infrastructure and loss of life3. Due to the mountainous terrain and subtropical monsoon climate in Kyushu, Japan, the intensity of summer rainfall can be high, and slope failure conditions due to heavy rain disasters have been present in recent years4,5.

To effectively implement emergency response and early recovery after a disaster, it is essential to quickly determine the distribution and number of areas with slope failure6. Remote sensing data, such as UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle), aerial photographs, and satellite data, can detect large-scale disaster conditions7–10. UAVs have flexible view angles and high resolution but are limited by scanning range, ground operator requirements, and weather conditions11. Detailed aerial photographs cover are costly, and the task is labor-intensive12. Artificial satellite remote sensing offers fast response, wide coverage, and low cost13, making it ideal for large-area surface monitoring14,15. In the last decade, the availability of optical satellite imagery in the disaster field has increased significantly16,17. However, heavy rainfall events often occur during periods of dense cloud cover, and optical images are not always available immediately after an event8. When an actual disaster occurs, it is not easy to obtain post-disaster data quickly in any of the above ways. Rapid extraction of the affected area after a disaster is essential for coordinating the emergency response and minimizing delays in relief. Therefore, researchers are increasingly exploring all-weather Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)18,19. SAR data processing technology has advanced in recent years, and new radar satellites offer greater data processing capabilities20. New space radar sensors have enhanced resolutions and revisit times, making them ideal for slope failure investigations21.

SAR-based products such as intensity and backscattering coefficients have been applied to identify natural hazards such as slope failure and landslides based on visual interpretation18,22. Only in recent years have quantitative methods been proposed23,24. However, SAR intensity change detection is mainly used to detect change states with a longer duration, such as ground settlement, surface classification changes, and landslides. The change detection of slope failure triggered within a short period of heavy rainfall is still in a less researched stage. Slope failures due to heavy rainfall cause many economic and infrastructural losses to countries every year, so related research is necessary. Most recently, proposed SAR-based change detection techniques utilize ratio or differential images to suppress background information and enhance change information25. In differential images, the pixel values associated with land cover changes differ significantly from those associated with unchanging areas23,26. At sites where surface changes were associated with slope failure, the difference in backscattering coefficients between two time points before and after a disaster was greater than the difference the points during normal periods. Researchers and engineers can set thresholds to extract the areas where slope failure occurs from satellite data before and after the disaster27,28. However, the factors that influence the extraction results of SAR data include land cover, satellite observation conditions, terrain conditions, etc29–31. Therefore, thresholds based only on changes in backscattering coefficients observed after a disaster have some geographic limitations.

To improve the limitation when setting change extraction thresholds based only on SAR data before and after a disaster, we developed a method for monitoring surface changes during slope failure due to heavy rainfall disasters. This method can extract surface change areas based on the effects of land cover, satellite observation conditions, and topographic conditions by using differences in backscattering coefficients observed periodically to set thresholds. Rapid response is made at the time of disaster.

Speckle noise affects SAR images, and their pixel values have large variances even in homogeneous regions. Noise hinders the accuracy of variation monitoring, and its removal is an important issue32,33. Ratio imaging helps to suppress the image background structure and improve the detection of potential changes in SAR data25. Therefore, some studies have used images of the ratios before and after the change in their parsing25,34. However, in the above-mentioned change detection method using ratio images, the threshold setting still needs to rely on the post-disaster image.

To reduce the impact of speckle noise on image classification and achieve a more comprehensive and accurate change detection, this paper proposes a surface change extraction method that combines the density ratio estimation method in machine learning. However, calculating the probability density distribution separately and then calculating the density ratio will produce a significant error, and the unconstrained least squares importance fitting (uLSIF) method can directly calculate the density ratio without density estimation, which is efficient and stable35. In addition, the parameter values can be adjusted objectively due to a natural cross validation procedure36,37. Therefore, uLSIF is considered a practical and excellent density estimation method. However, although the uLSIF method is relatively mature in other fields, its application in the direction of SAR data change detection has yet to be developed. This study combines the two to improve surface change detection accuracy and computational efficiency after a disaster. Unlike the traditional calculation of the difference of satellite data to detect changes, this study utilizes the difference of the regular-observed multitemporal SAR backscattering coefficients as a reference basis and the difference of post-disaster backscattering coefficients as anomalies according to the characteristics of the SAR data and detects the anomalies using the uLSIF density ratio estimation method38,39. Considering the integration of timeseries data, both reduce the error when a single dataset is used as the reference data to a certain extent and enhance the applicability and robustness of the method.

In addition, the pixel-based extraction method with SAR data is highly dependent on pixel size, and the resolution of results between two neighboring pixels may be independent. However, the area where slope failure occurs is seldom smaller than the area covered by one pixel, and there is a lack of correspondence between the extent of slope failure and the pixel size40. Pixel-related and “salt and pepper” effects caused by speckle noise can easily be mistaken for areas of slope failure41.To address this problem, previous studies divided natural slopes into slope units (SUs) to capture the area of surface change associated with slope failure30,41. An SU is a terrain unit bounded by drainage and demarcation boundaries. Geomorphological processes within SUs are characterized uniformly, and maps generated based on SUs are intrinsically more suitable for land use planning and disaster management than are others34. SUs can be mapped manually by hydrogeologists or by probing ridgelines and valley lines in high-resolution DEM data and creating the area enclosed by the ridgelines and valley lines as an SU with a GIS-based algorithm30,42. Nevertheless, the slope aspect and slope angle of the inclined plane are complex and diverse, and traditional hydrological methods is impossible to perform high-precision segmentation by setting a single threshold. Furthermore, to reduce the creation of the “salt and pepper” effects in the implementation of the above two methods, the disaster situation is discussed based on the natural terrain. We propose an improved method for creating SUs. This method performs multi-scale segmentation based on the quantized slope aspect and slope angle instead of setting a single threshold.

These methods are each combined into two multi-step detection methods, including a preprocessing and backward scatter difference calculation step for SAR data, an SU creation step, and the application of two different change detection algorithms. “Data and study area" section describes the data used and the study area. “Methodology” section shows the SU creation and two SU-based surface change detection methods. “Results” section shows the results and thresholds for change detection. “Discussion” shows the applicability of the two methods to other regions.

Data and study area

The study area is located near the village of Toho in North Kyushu, Japan. The area is approximately 10 km2 (Fig. 1), and it received heavy rainfall from July 5th to 6th, 2017, when multiple slope failures occurred38. We collected ALOS-2 data before (June 12, 2017) and after (July 10, 2017) the heavy rainfall event in the study area and obtained regular observations in the absence of disasters. A total of 19 scenes were obtained. To reduce the impact of an overly large time span on observations, a relatively short observation time interval was used in the study area, the observation orbit was descending, and the observation direction was the right side (DR). Pairs of data points with a time interval of 28 days were divided into one group, and the backscattering coefficient differences for the 12 groups (including data before and after the disaster) were calculated. Notably, the ALOS-2 satellite can observe a point maximum of four times per year under the same conditions unless there are special observation requirements. Therefore, to obtain more data to verify the feasibility of the method, the regular observation data used in this study were satellite data collected after July 10, 2017 (after the disaster). In addition, we also used 10 m2 DEM data to calculate the slope angle and slope aspect (Table 1). Also, an optical aerial image with a resolution of 0.5 m2 was utilized to produce a map of surface changes in the entire study area based on 1 m2 of DEM difference data before and after the disaster through visual interpretation. This map was used as the validation data to verify the extraction accuracy of the methodology of this paper (Fig. 2a). At the same time, we created composite RGB images from the images of the backscattering coefficient before and after the disaster (Fig. 2b). When the surface changes, because the intensity of the received reflected waves changes, the area where the surface changes will appear red and blue on the RGB image. Examples are shown in Fig. 2(c); we show the color composite results of optical aerial and SAR images of two areas where slope failure occurred. The blue line is the visual interpretation results shown in Fig. 2(a) (used as validation data for subsequent studies). The change pattern in the SAR image is the same as that in the verification data.

Methodology

As slope failures accompanying heavy rainfall disasters are often sudden and destructive, realizing rapid and quantitative extraction of surface changes due to heavy rainfall-associated slope failures is crucial for disaster emergency response and recovery efforts. The main body of this study consists of three parts that aim to improve the efficiency of systematically detecting and analyzing such sudden surface changes. First, we preprocessed the ALOS-2 dataset to minimize the impact of errors on the extraction results at the data level and computed the difference in backscattering coefficients as the primary input data for the proposed method (“Difference in backscattering coefficients calculations for the ALOS-2 dataset” section). Then, we improved the existing hydrological analysis to create SUs to make the created SUs more consistent with the actual terrain, thus improving the accuracy of the extraction results based on SU analysis (“Improvements to slope units” section). Finally, this study introduces the threshold and machine learning methods to propose two methods for extracting sites where slope failure occurs under heavy rainfall disasters. The extraction method based on the threshold method mainly considers the relationship between the objective conditions that may affect the surface changes and the SAR data when not affected by the disaster and sets the extraction threshold with the regular observation data, thus realizing fast extraction (“Threshold method based on impact conditions” section). The extraction method based on the machine learning method mainly considers the change in the density ratio between the regularly observed SAR data and the SAR data after the disaster. Since this method calculates the data density ratio before and after the disaster, it can more comprehensively capture disaster-induced changes, thus improving the accuracy and reliability of change detection (“Density ratio estimation method” section).

Threshold method based on impact conditions

This section analyzes the relationship between conditions that may affect surface change extraction and regularly observed SAR data. All of the periodic observations of SAR used in this study were obtained after the occurrence of the disaster. The difference in backscattering coefficients between the two time points differed significantly only when extensive changes in the ground surface occurred. Thus, the difference in backscattering coefficients obtained from regular observations after a disaster occurred was similar to the difference in backscattering coefficients obtained from regular observations before the occurrence of a disaster. We calculated the difference in backscattering coefficients for normal periods and used the difference as a threshold for extracting regions of surface change.

Figure 6 shows the workflows. Concerning previous studies, we consider the effects of the following factors: land use situation, elevation, slope angle, slope aspect, LIA (local incidence angle) and relative azimuth (slope aspect based on satellite observation direction). We calculate the curvature, slope angle, and slope aspect of the study area based on the DEM data; the relative azimuthal angle based on the slope aspect and the satellite’s orbital data; calculate the LIA based on the DEM data and the satellite’s orbital data; and the data for the land use were obtained from the MLIT (Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism), Japan. The results of the above influences are then assigned pixel by pixel to the 11 difference maps of the posterior scatter coefficients obtained by parsing according to Fig. 3 as periodic observation data. Finally, to identify the influencing factors that significantly affect the extraction of surface changes, reduce the interference of noise and irrelevant variables, and improve the accuracy and validity of the set thresholds. Two statistical methods, ANOVA (analysis of variance) and analysis of multiple comparisons, are utilized to clarify the conditions that affect the SAR observation data.

Workflow of the threshold setting method according to the influence conditions. (a) Compilation of data; (b) Characteristic analysis of periodic observation data; (c) Extraction of land surface change locations based on feature analysis results and evaluation of extraction accuracy.

ANOVA was used to identify the extent to which different factors influenced the observations. Each factor was categorized into classes (e.g., at slope angles categorized as 0°-10°, 10°-20°, ……, and 50°-60°) (Table 2). The different conditions are grouped, the difference in the backscattering coefficients in the normal period is calculated, and the data are extracted and used as samples for ANOVA. If the difference in the backscattering coefficient is the same for any grade, the condition has no effect on the difference in the backscattering coefficient, and the opposite condition has an effect. Based on the results of the ANOVA, factors affecting the observations were filtered and excluded, and multiple comparisons were analyzed for each category corresponding to the factors identified by the ANOVA that would affect the observations. Multiple comparison analyses were conducted to compare the differences between the factors further. In this study, multiple comparisons for each group of samples were performed to determine the differences in properties between grades for the same condition and to determine the minimum ranges of different properties for each condition. The study was conducted with an error of 1% and a reliability of 95% as criteria, and a final sample of 9604 was established. Density ratio estimation method

Density ratio estimation method is a method used to detect outliers contained in routine data. To detect outliers, the probability density distribution of regular data can first be denoted as , and the outlier data containing outliers can be denoted as . The density ratio is then divided by . is set between 0 and 1, with values close to 1 indicating regular data. Values close to 0 indicate data a potential outlier associated with a region where slope failure has occurred.

We used the backscattering coefficient difference images based on regularly observed data under DR observation conditions as a basis and the backscattering coefficient difference images based on the pre- and postdisaster data as outliers. As in Eq. (1), the absolute value image of the difference in backscattering coefficients is calculated, focusing only on the magnitude of the change. ArcGIS Pro was used to generate point data at the center of the 3 m2 grid. The difference in the posterior scatter coefficients of the 11 periodic observations was assigned to the corresponding point data. Then, based on the uLSIF method to calculate the density ratio of the regular data to the outliers43. The process is shown in Fig. 7. 5000 data were randomly selected from 11 sets of regularly observed data as normal values. The density ratio was calculated by comparing the difference of the backward scatter coefficient before and after the disaster with the normal value, and the density ratio estimation results with outliers (areas where surface changes occurred) were obtained. To fully describe the distribution characteristics of the density ratio, this study calculated the average, median, standard deviation, maximum, minimum, skewness, peak value, 3rd quartile, 1st quartile, and quartile range of the estimated density ratio in each SU.

Flow of applying the uLSIF procedure.

The density ratio extraction thresholds for extracting regions of surface change were determined based on the PR (Precision -Recall) curves of the ten statistics described above. In this study, we set the threshold by using the value of the density ratio corresponding to the best extraction accuracy for the location of the surface change of the North Kyushu heavy rainfall disaster as an outlier. To assess the extraction accuracy, we used the F1 score. We calculated the F1 score of each density ratio and used the density ratio corresponding to the best F1 score as the threshold value to determine whether surface changes occurred.

In the figure, area B is in the forest area south of Kurokawa, and some SUs that were not extracted using Method I were correctly extracted with Method III, indicating that Method III is more accurate and appropriate than Method I for extracting areas where surface changes occurred. These results suggest that for the case of the North Kyushu heavy rain, density ratio estimation of the difference in backscattering coefficients before and after disaster periods using conventional observation data can improve the extraction of the surface changes associated with slope failure.

Threshold setting using the density ratio estimation method

We considered ten basic statistics to set the thresholds. To qualitatively determine whether each statistic is suitable for identifying whether there is a change in the ground surface, we computed the probability density distribution of the occurrence of a ground surface change versus the absence of a ground surface change for each statistic. For these ten statistics, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) values of the precision-recall (PR) curves to quantitatively assess the discriminability of the binary classification51. The PR plot (Fig. 16) shows that the statistics for cases with high AUC values include a mean of 0.709, a standard deviation of 0.726, and a minimum of 0.715; additionally, the standard deviation and minimum exhibit higher discriminatory power than the commonly used mean when applied to the density ratio within SUs.

PR curves for SUs-based underlying statistics (observation condition: DR).

The minimum value is based on only one data point within an SU and can be strongly influenced by outliers. Therefore, to consider the minimum and the values around the minimum, we selected the smallest 32 values and calculated the mean as a new statistic to be examined, called the average of the low values. The reason for using 32 values is that the average area of slope failure during the North Kyushu disaster was approximately equal to 32 grids of 3 m2. The PR curve, including the mean of the average of the low values, exhibits an AUC of 0.743, which is greater than the values of the standard deviation and minimum (Fig. 17). This indicates that the accuracy of extracting the location of surface changes can be improved by aggregating the mean of the low values within each SU.

Discussion

Applicability of the method proposed in this study to other regions

This study area is located around Kure city, Hiroshima Prefecture, Japan, where heavy rainfall was particularly concentrated and slope failures were frequent during the July 2018 rainstorm disaster. We identified this study area as a 6 × 5 km area (Fig. 19). The dataset is from November 14, 2015, to August 29, 2018. In this case study, we used regular observations of SAR data in the normal periods before the occurrence of the disaster, and the observation conditions were the AR conditions, which yielded the largest amount of data in the region.

In terms of precision, Method I yields the highest value, but Method II and Method III produce higher recall values than Method I. If the results extracted by Method II and Method III are used for detailed investigations, such as field surveys, damage can be assessed with a relative margin of safety, even if there are some gaps in the results. Next, the F1 score of each method was compared, and the values for Method II and Method III were higher than that for Method I, indicating that Method II and Method III can extract damage with higher accuracy. Considering the trade-off between recall and precision, the inclusion of recall makes the F1 score sensitive to the imbalances among categories48. Therefore, when the correct extraction rate and detection rate of slope failure are considered simultaneously, Method II and Method III are found to effectively extract areas of surface change with higher accuracy than Method I. Based on these results, Methods II and III only require pre-disaster information to guarantee a precision of more than 60%. From this point of view, we believe that the proposed methods in this paper are efficient in cases with actual disasters and when post disaster information is not immediately available.

Moreover, although the precision of Method II is slightly higher than that of Method III, Method III performs better in terms of recall and F1 score. Also, Method III can reduce the error caused by noise, and thus, overall, Method III should be given priority when a disaster occurs.

Proposed method

Remote sensing techniques used to detect changes are mainly classified as supervised and unsupervised techniques52. The former requires the simultaneous acquisition of the pre- and post-change conditions, from which a training set is obtained. Only the original image under consideration is available for change detection in the latter approach. Although the accuracy of this method is lower than that of the supervised detection technique, the response rate is faster53. In the context of the rapid extraction of post disaster conditions proposed in this study, unsupervised techniques are necessary because information is not always available immediately after a significant disaster53.

The two methods proposed in this paper are based on parsing satellite data with well-established information to obtain thresholds for detecting possible future disaster conditions. The choice of the threshold value is crucial because the accuracy of the final change detection result depends mainly on the choice of the threshold value. Unlike the classical unsupervised change detection techniques, both methods proposed in this study are SU-based. They, therefore, include spatially contextual information that can improve the accuracy of the final extraction result30,41,54. Based on accuracy considerations, we used SUs as the units of analysis because storm-induced sediment disasters tend to occur over large areas. Furthermore, SUs can simultaneously reflect the overall disaster situation and isolated affected areas. Therefore, this approach is beneficial for assessing the corresponding disaster situation.

The two methods proposed in this study are based on SUs and combine slope and aspect information when creating SUs to explore typical slope types in Japan (Not a lot of manual development). However, special slope types also deserve attention. Reservoirs are usually built in river canyons with dense contour lines to ensure a large water catchment area and storage capacity. However, the construction of the reservoir will significantly change the geological conditions of the area, and water level changes are a leading factor in inducing geological disasters. In addition, rainfall is the most crucial factor that induces geological disasters in reservoir areas55,56. When the water level changes in the reservoir and rainfall work together, slope stability is greatly reduced, and the possibility of geological disasters in the reservoir environment will increase56. Therefore, when dealing with slopes with special terrains such as reservoir environments, method II of this study should consider the reservoir water level as one of the factors that influence the water level in conjunction with the primary factors proposed in this article. Method III should consider the changes in special terrain and adjust the required threshold for extraction according to this change when applied to special terrain.

Limitations and expectations

In this paper, since Method III does not consider the land use and satellite sensing conditions, further analyses under different conditions in the target area must be performed to assess the accuracy of results. Based on the analysis results of the two new methods, we believe that the probability density distribution trends of the backscattering coefficient differences between the pre- and post-disaster periods may differ due to variations in land use and satellite observation conditions. Therefore, in the future, we will consider integrating the two methods, dividing the study region according to the conditions, and then estimating the density ratio of each region under each condition to improve the extraction accuracy.

With the launch of SAR satellites such as ALOS-2/PALSAR-2 (JAXA), Sentinel-1 (ESA), and ENVISAT (ESA), the amount of SAR data is growing rapidly and will continue to increase in the future. Moreover, multiband datasets, such as the C-band and L-band, are available for studying sediment disasters57. In this study, only the ALOS-2 dataset, notably, SAR data, were used due to resolution considerations. Therefore, in the future, we will consider the effects of different satellites and wavebands on the proposed method, which will aid in detecting slope failure.

Conclusions

In this study, two new methods for extracting surface change areas caused by slope failure based on the improved slope unit are proposed, and the applicability of the methods is verified by conducting experiments on slope failure caused by two heavy rainfall disasters in different regions of Japan. In this study, the drawbacks of the difficulty of quickly obtaining optical data and manual probing after the occurrence of a heavy rainfall disaster were fully considered, and both methods can quickly extract the area where slope failure occurred based on the data collected in the usual way and the SAR data from the emergency observation, to quickly grasp the place where slope failure occurred and the extent of the damage in the aftermath of the disaster. Taking the Toho village area as an example, the following main conclusions were obtained:

i.Improvements to the beveled units are effective. The improved SU creation method can effectively reduce over segmentation while creating SUs with greater detail. The F1 score is 23% more accurate compared to the conventional maneuver.

Improvements to the beveled units are effective. The improved SU creation method can effectively reduce over segmentation while creating SUs with greater detail. The F1 score is 23% more accurate compared to the conventional maneuver.

ii.Both methods proposed in this study can achieve an accuracy of more than 60% of the disintegrated area quickly extracted within a few hours after the disaster.

Both methods proposed in this study can achieve an accuracy of more than 60% of the disintegrated area quickly extracted within a few hours after the disaster.

iii.We validated the parameter settings obtained in the Toho village area in the Hiroshima area, and F1 score of more than 70% were achieved. The results show that both methods are widely applicable and reproducible.

In the future, we plan to incorporate conditions affecting the extraction results, such as land use and satellite observation conditions, into the density ratio estimation method. The accuracy of the proposed method is expected to be further improved for the quantitative, accurate, and fast extraction of surface change areas caused by slope failure.
