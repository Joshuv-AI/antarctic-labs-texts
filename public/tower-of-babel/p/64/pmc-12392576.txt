Biological functions and clinical implications of the CMPK2 across multisystemic diseases
Authors: Jia Li, Qing Liu, Jie Yang, Ziyi Yang, Mingxin Zhang, Jie Zhang, Chao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytidine/Uridine monophosphate kinase 2 (CMPK2) is a crucial enzyme responsible for the phosphorylation of nucleotides, specifically converting cytidine monophosphate (CMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP) into their respective diphosphates, known as cytidine diphosphate (CDP) and uridine diphosphate (UDP). Recent studies have demonstrated that CMPK2 plays a pivotal role in multiple pathophysiological processes, such as cellular nucleotide metabolism, energy homeostasis, and inflammatory response. Dysregulation of CMPK2 has been implicated in a variety of pathologies, including viral infections, neurodegenerative diseases, and autoimmune disorders. Despite its biological significance and interventive potential, knowledge gaps remain with respect to CMPK2, in particular, the precise molecular mechanism and mechanical basis through which it contributes to the onset and progression of underlying diseases. In this regard, this narrative review aims to provide an overview concerning the biological functions of CMPK2, shed light on its involvement in disease pathogenesis alongside clinical relevance, and ultimately highlight potential therapeutic strategies to target CMPK2 across multisystemic disorders.Keywords: CMPK2, Nucleotide phosphorylation, Mitochondrial homeostasis, NLRP3

Introduction

Cytidine/Uridine monophosphate kinase 2 (CMPK2), a pivotal enzyme belonging to the nucleoside monophosphate kinase family, drives the phosphorylation of cytidine monophosphate (CMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP) to produce cytidine diphosphate (CDP) and uridine diphosphate (UDP)—essential precursors for nucleotide biosynthesis and metabolic regulation [1]. This mitochondrial-localized protein exhibits dual compartmentalization: approximately 30% of its cellular pool specifically associates with the outer mitochondrial membrane; on the other hand, it spatially co-distributes with translocase of the inner mitochondrial membrane, serving as a key mitochondrial membrane scaffolding protein to exert collaborative effects in the membrane-related processes. Evolutionarily conserved from mammals to lower eukaryotes, CMPK2 demonstrates ubiquitous expression in versatile human tissues with elevated metabolic activity, particularly the liver, brain, and skeletal muscle, sustaining mitochondrial nucleotide homeostasis and energy production via mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) replication and oxidative phosphorylation. CMPK2 is also an interferon (IFN)-stimulated gene (ISG) critical for antiviral immunity and inflammatory responses. Its expression is tightly regulated by pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs) and cytokine receptors through both IFN-dependent and IFN-independent pathways [2, 3]. The IFN-dependent Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway is the primary upstream signaling cascade. Upon binding to the IFN receptor, type I interferons (IFN-α/β) activate the receptor-associated protein tyrosine kinases Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2). These kinases then phosphorylate the latent cytoplasmic transcription factors signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and STAT2. Following tyrosine phosphorylation, STAT1 and STAT2 dimerize, translocate to the nucleus, and assemble with IFN-regulatory factor 9 (IRF9) to form a trimolecular complex known as IFN-stimulated gene factor 3 (ISGF3). This ISGF3 complex binds to interferon-stimulated response elements in the CMPK2 promoter to initiate transcription [3, 4]. Type II interferons and various other cytokines also signal through analogous mechanisms [5]. Reinforcing this regulatory axis, a recent study in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients identified the LY6E as an upstream regulator of CMPK2 within signaling pathways triggered by type I interferons and immune complexes [6]. Functioning as a mitochondrial stress sensor, CMPK2 plays a pivotal role in cellular injury or energetic crises by mediating the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol. This release activates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway, triggering type I interferon responses [7]. Additionally, the released mtDNA can interact with Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), leading to downstream consequences such as the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) [8]. Together, these pathways create a vital link between mitochondrial integrity and innate immunity. Dysregulation of CMPK2 has been linked to diverse clinical entities, including infectious diseases, autoimmune disorders, and other systemic conditions, highlighting its broad involvement and multifaceted functions in the context of cellular homeostasis. Further in-depth investigation regarding its regulatory mechanisms and disease-specific functions could enhance clinical utility as well as therapeutic targeting strategies.

Biological functions of CMPK2

The biological role of CMPK2 extends beyond the sole function of nucleotide phosphorylation. This enzyme is involved in various processes integral to distinct essential cellular functions as described below.

Metabolic pathways

Nucleotides, which constitute the fundamental building blocks of nucleic acids and serve as cellular energy currencies, are dynamically regulated to preserve genomic stability and support essential biological processes. Their metabolic equilibrium is essential for DNA replication fidelity, organelle function, and coordinated cell division. An imbalance in nucleotide pools can elicit catastrophic consequences, ranging from mutagenesis to organelle dysfunction [9]. This rate-limiting enzymatic step not only replenishes pyrimidine nucleotide pools but also directs CDP and UDP into divergent metabolic circuits, thereby maintaining integrative cellular architecture and signaling plasticity [10]. Mechanistically, CDP-derived intermediates (e.g., CDP-choline and CDP-ethanolamine) act as activated precursors for the biosynthesis of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine—structural lipids indispensable for membrane biogenesis. Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) is particularly important for mitochondrial membrane integrity [9, 11]. Concurrently, UDP functions as a key activated sugar nucleotide donor (e.g., UDP-N-acetylglucosamine [UDP-GlcNAc], UDP-galactose) closely linked to protein and lipid glycosylation. Of note, UDP-glucose harnesses glycogen synthesis, while UDP-sugar derivatives (e.g., UDP-GlcNAc) are essential precursors concerning glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, known as macromolecules vitally involved in the extracellular matrix organization and cell–matrix communication [12]. Moreover, O-linked β-N-acetylglucosamine modification, a nutrient-sensitive post-translational pattern mediated by UDP-GlcNAc, dynamically regulates mitochondrial enzymes by modifying serine/threonine residues. This delicate modification fine-tunes energy metabolism, mitigates oxidative stress, and modulates apoptosis through interactions with phosphorylation signaling [13]. Therefore, by interweaving nucleotide recycling with membrane biogenesis, glycosylation dynamics, and energy sensing, CMPK2 emerges as a metabolic linchpin coordinating spatial and temporal aspects of cellular homeostasis.

Mitochondrial homeostasis

Mitochondria are essential organelles in eukaryotic cells, playing a central role in cellular energy production and maintaining cellular homeostasis. Beyond their role as the primary ATP-generating "powerhouses" supporting cellular functions and survival, mitochondria are critical regulators of lipid and glucose metabolism, calcium buffering, phospholipid synthesis, programmed cell death, and immune responses [14]. Given these vital functions, cells employ intricate quality control mechanisms to regulate mitochondrial morphology, quantity, and integrity. This quality control system involves the coordinated regulation of dynamics (fission and fusion), biogenesis, and degradation (e.g., mitochondrial-associated degradation, mitochondria-derived vesicles, mitophagy and mitochondria-lysosome-related organelle)—processes essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis [14, 15]. Dysregulation of these mechanisms disrupts metabolic flux, redox balance, and signaling cascades, contributing to the pathogenesis of diverse diseases [16]. Emerging evidence highlights CMPK2 as a critical regulator of mitochondrial integrity and cellular energy metabolism. CMPK2 localizes to mitochondria and supports mtDNA synthesis—a process crucial for mitochondrial genome stability and respiratory chain function. Impaired mtDNA maintenance compromises oxidative phosphorylation and amplifies ROS generation, directly linking CMPK2 activity to mitochondrial metabolic fitness [15]. Notably, CMPK2 expression levels correlate with mitochondrial architecture, dynamics, and functional integrity. A comparative analysis revealed notable morphological differences, with CMPK2-knockdown (KD) and overexpressing (OE) cells displaying enlarged and more densely packed mitochondrial networks, alongside structural disorganization of the mitochondrial cristae [1]. Both KD and OE cells exhibited reduced levels of total dynamin-1-like protein (DNM1L) and its phosphorylated form, a key mediator of mitochondrial fission, but maintained stable expression of indicative fusion-related proteins [1, 17]. Notably, macrophages with either silenced or constitutively overexpressed CMPK2 exhibited hallmarks in relation to mitochondrial stress as indicated by significant membrane potential depolarization and extensive ROS generation [1]. These perturbations coincided with upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-18 (IL-18). Notably, sustained CMPK2 modulation induced a metabolic shift towards enhanced glycolytic flux, mirroring the metabolic reprogramming characteristic of classically activated M1 macrophages [1]. Genetic depletion of CMPK2 gives rise to severe mtDNA loss, mitochondrial function defects in addition to impaired oxidative phosphorylation, leading to ATP deficiency and pathological ROS accumulation [18, 19]. Collectively, CMPK2 constitutes a critical orchestrator of mitochondrial homeostasis, integrating nucleotide metabolism, redox balance, and ATP production (Fig. 1).

Inflammatory and immune response

The innate immune system constitutes the primary defense against pathogens and cellular damage. PRRs detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), initiating signaling cascades that upregulate type I interferons, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and chemokines. These responses prime adaptive immunity and suppress pathogen survival. Host-derived damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) similarly activate innate immune pathways [20, 21]. Furthermore, the interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation is fundamentally bidirectional. Mitochondrial damage releases components such as mtDNA and ROS into the cytosol or extracellular space. These molecules act as DAMPs that activate inflammatory pathways—including the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)—stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor 1 (STING1) axis (triggered by cytosolic mtDNA) and the NLRP3 inflammasome (activated by mtDNA/ROS)—thereby driving pathological inflammation [15, 21, 22]. Under physiological conditions, homeostatic clearance of damaged mitochondria occurs via mitophagy, a selective autophagic process. However, when the number of damaged mitochondria exceeds mitophagic capacity, accumulated ROS and mtDNA escape degradation, potently activating inflammatory cascades [22]. Critically, inflammation itself exacerbates mitochondrial damage through membrane permeabilization and mtDNA leakage, which further amplifies the DAMPs-driven signaling pathway. This self-perpetuating cycle establishes a pathological feedback loop that sustains chronic inflammation [20]. CMPK2 critically regulates inflammatory responses by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, which senses danger signals (including PAMPs and DAMPs) to drive inflammation and trigger pyroptosis—a programmed cell death process that exacerbates tissue injury [23]. During infection or sterile inflammation, PAMPs, such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or viral RNA, and DAMPs, such as high mobility group box-1, trigger the upregulation of CMPK2 through interferon (IFN)-dependent or IFN-independent pathways. This occurs via the binding of PAMPs or DAMPs to Toll-like receptors (TLRs) or retinoic acid-inducible gene-I (RIG-I)-like receptors, thereby strengthening the host’s innate immune defense [3, 10, 23, 24]. Once activated, CMPK2 translocates to the mitochondria, where it phosphorylates pyrimidine nucleotides to regulate mtDNA synthesis, a crucial process for maintaining mitochondrial genome integrity under normal conditions [25]. CMPK2-mediated mtDNA synthesis results in the generation of oxidized mtDNA (ox-mtDNA). This ox-mtDNA is then released from the mitochondria into the cytosol via mitochondrial permeability transition pores and voltage-dependent anion channels, pinpointing an activity designated as mtDNA leakage. Once in the cytosol, ox-mtDNA is recognized by the NLRP3 inflammasome [26]. The binding of mtDNA to NLRP3 induces its oligomerization to instigate the inflammasome assembly [24, 27]. This activity is further amplified by a spectrum of secondary signals such as potassium ion (K⁺) efflux, lysosomal rupture or ROS, destabilizing cellular membranes and enhancing NLRP3 activation [28–30]. The formation of NLRP3 inflammasome complex involves recruitment of the adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase activation and recruitment domain and procaspase-1, facilitating proximity-induced autocleavage of caspase-1 into its enzymatically active form [31–33]. Activated caspase-1 then executes two pivotal pro-inflammatory functions: First, it cleaves the precursor cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature, bioactive forms (IL-1β and IL-18), subsequently recruiting immune cells, stimulating endothelial activation, and exaggerating local and systemic inflammatory actions [26, 34, 35]; Second, it proteolytically processes gasdermin D (GSDMD), generating N-terminal fragments that oligomerize to form plasma membrane pores in the plasma membrane, essential for IL-1β/IL-18 release during pyroptosis —a lytic form of cell death to produce additional DAMP, and intracellular cytokines, eventually perpetuating the inflammatory cascade [31, 33]. The CMPK2-NLRP3 axis plays a dual role in immune homeostasis: although transient activation offers prompt protection against invading pathogens, dysregulated signaling may contribute to pathological inflammation. For example, in the context of viral infections, CMPK2-induced hyperactivation of NLRP3 amplifies cytokine release, resulting in the excessive secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. This triggers pyroptosis in both pulmonary and immune cells, contributing to the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multi-organ failure [25, 35]. Paradoxically, CMPK2 may also be attributable to feedback mechanisms to curtail overwhelming inflammatory injuries. During the resolution phase, autophagy pathways fostered by CMPK2-associated mitochondrial stress can degrade damaged mitochondria and inflammasome components [36, 37]. Taken together, these findings position CMPK2 as a key molecule at the mitochondrial-immune interface due to its regulation of the intensity of NLRP3-mediated inflammation (Fig. 2).

The association between CMPK2 and multiple systemic diseases

Infectious diseases

The immune system’s response to infections involves complex interactions between immune cells and different pathogens. CMPK2 is a potent interferon-stimulated gene in THP-1 macrophages and may contribute to interferon regulatory factor 3-mediated, type I IFN-dependent antibacterial and antiviral responses due to LPS and polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid challenges [10]. CMPK2 is significantly induced during infection caused by various viral pathogens, including hepatitis E virus (HEV) [38], human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [39], human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) [40], dengue virus (DENV) [3], Zika virus (ZIKV) [41], Nipah virus [42], duck tembusu virus (DTMUV) [43, 44], and spring viremia of carp virus [45]. Among aforesaid contagious models, the manipulation of CMPK2 activity either by genetic knockdown or chemical inhibition impacts the magnitude of viral replication and the levels of inflammatory cytokines, highlighting its contribution pertinent to controlling the virus expansion and harnessing the immune system. For instance, in a mouse and human cell-based model of DENV-2, CMPK2 was indispensable for DENV-induced antiviral cytokine production, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and the release of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol, all of which can be suppressed by CMPK2 depletion [3]. Furthermore, the precise role of CMPK2 in the circumstance of bacterial infections is equally significant, in particular, it is capable of regulating immune responses to combat and counteract pathogenic bacteria along with minimizing collateral tissue damage. Analyzing long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) expression profiles in responsive macrophages to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, Arumugam et al. identified a novel and highly expressed transcript uniquely incorporating the protein-coding gene CMPK2 [46]. CMPK2 is rapidly induced by virulent Mtb and LPS, but its absence or delayed expression in non-viable Mtb/BCG-infected cells unravels its marked influence on macrophage responses. The increased expression of CMPK2 in response to fast-growing bacteria further underscores the importance of these RNAs in alignment with incurring macrophage infection [46]. These findings suggest that CMPK2 is partially responsible for balancing the inflammatory response, preventing excessive tissue damage as well as retaining an effective defense against the pathogen attacks.

Autoimmune diseases

Autoimmune diseases are characterized by the immune system mistakenly attacking healthy cells and tissues, leading to chronic inflammation and persistent tissue injury. In this regard, CMPK2 has garnered attention for its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Tan et al. reported a significant upregulation of CMPK2 in CD4⁺ T cells from patients with SLE at both the gene and protein levels [47]. These T cells exhibited mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by increased accumulation of ROS, elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, and an increase in mtDNA copy number. Additionally, apoptosis and activation of CD4⁺ T cells were distinctly observed in SLE patients. Notably, these detrimental effects were significantly ameliorated upon effective suppression of CMPK2. Furthermore, Lai et al. confirmed that CMPK2 plays a crucial role in regulating IFN-α-induced ROS production and inflammasome activation in SLE patients. Specifically, reduced CMPK2 expression inhibited IFN-α-induced foam cell formation, which was linked to decreased expression of scavenger receptor class A [48]. A recent mechanistic insight was provided by Jenn-Haung Lai et al., who identified specify upstream molecules (e.g., STAT1 and LY6E) as key upstream regulators of CMPK2 in the pathogenesis of SLE. This finding further supports the relationship between CMPK2 and SLE [6]. Moreover, Yixi Li et al. demonstrated that a nine-protein biomarker panel, including CMPK2, exhibited strong performance in assessing SLE exacerbation, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.990 [49]. RA is a debilitating autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic synovial inflammation and progressive cartilage degradation. Using a combination of versatile in vitro and in vivo approaches, including fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) inhibition and gene KO, Jin et al. have elucidated the regulatory role of the FTO-CMPK2 pathway in synovial inflammation [50]. Mechanistically, this pathway affects chondrocyte homeostasis through the mtDNA-mediated cGAS/STING signaling pathway. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of dermatomyositis (DM), a rare autoimmune disease affecting the skin and muscle tissues. Based on weighted gene co-expression network analysis, CMPK2 was identified in both the muscle and skin tissues from patients with DM, highlighting CMPK2 to be associated with an immune infiltration context [51]. Acid-sensing ion channels (ASICs) belong to a family of cell membrane receptors in response to acidic conditions. Among the ASIC subunits, Acid-sensing ion channel 1a (ASIC1a) has been clearly addressed to allow calcium ion (Ca2⁺) permeability and involved in a range of pathological issues [36]. Dong et al. unveiled that the ASIC1a-CMPK2 axis positively regulates M1 macrophage polarization, responsible for osteoarthritis-associated chondrocyte senescence [36]. Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a chronic, slowly progressive autoimmune disorder marked by inflammatory lymphocytic infiltration of the salivary and lacrimal glands. Li et al. revealed that CMPK2 is significantly upregulated in clinical samples derived from pSS patients. The elevated CMPK2 expression was in close relationship to enhanced immune cell infiltration in salivary glands, dysregulation of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, and perturbations in key metabolic pathways (i.e. gluconeogenesis, tricarboxylic acid cycle, pyruvate/ketone/lipid/amino acid metabolism) [52]. Furthermore, CMPK2 upregulation in the context of allergic rhinitis triggers mtROS-dependent mtDNA release, activating the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis to amplify inflammation, which leverages the molecular basis of diverse autoimmune pathologies [53]. In conclusion, CMPK2 plays a crucial role in regulating immune responses and maintaining mitochondrial function, partially responsible for the onset, development and progression of specific autoimmune diseases.

Other systemic diseases

Emerging evidence emphasizes the multifaceted role of CMPK2 in the pathogenesis of multisystemic diseases across the cardiovascular, neurological, digestive, and respiratory systems. Regarding cardiovascular diseases, CMPK2 exacerbates inflammatory cascades critical to atherosclerosis progression. Wu et al. provided in vivo evidence directly dissecting the role of CMPK2-NLRP3 pathway in monocyte adhesion and migration in the circumstance of atherosclerosis [54]. Additionally, Natarajan et al. unravelled that vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) promotes mtDNA synthesis originating from macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques under inflammatory conditions in both human and mouse models. From a molecular perspective, VCAM-1 activates CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (C/EBPα), which binds to the promoters of key mitochondrial biogenesis genes, such as CMPK2 and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α). This gives rise to increased expression of CMPK2 and PGC-1α, ultimately stimulating mtDNA synthesis and bursting STING-dictated inflammation to exacerbate the progression of atherosclerosis [7]. Regarding the nervous system, CMPK2 deficiency disrupts mitochondrial integrity, as evidenced by another research highlighting familial brain calcification (FBC). Biallelic CMPK2 variants observed in patients and CMPK2-knockout (KO) mice exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction with reduced mtDNA copies, impaired cristae structure, and diminished ATP production, in parallel with features in patient-derived immune cells [18]. Similarly, Guan et al. reported that CMPK2 expression is upregulated in monocytes/macrophages and microglia in human beings and experimental mice with stroke-inducible models. CMPK2 depletion has been proven to inhibit brain inflammation, decrease infarct area, and improve neurological outcome in mice exposed to ischemic damage. From pathophysiological perspective, CMPK2 KO inhibits mtDNA synthesis and ox-mtDNA formation to repress the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome [55]. As for digestive disorders, CMPK2 emerges as a pivotal mediator of various metabolic and inflammatory hepatopathies. Zhu et al. observed a significant upregulation of CMPK2 in the hepatic tissues of obese individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), as well as in mice with diet-induced MASH. Notably, hepatocyte-specific deletion of CMPK2 notably alleviated liver injury, inflammation, and fibrosis. Additionally, the inhibition of CMPK2 suppressed the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent hepatic pyroptosis [56]. Luo et al. further verified CMPK2’s role in relation to hepatic ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, demonstrating that hypoxia/reoxygenation -induced upregulation of NLRP3, IL-1β, and TNF-α is abolished upon CMPK2 silencing [34]. Gao et al. confirmed that the expression of long non-coding CMPK2 (lncCMPK2) is markedly upregulated in the colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues, and is positively correlated with clinical stages and the presence of lymphatic metastasis. Overexpression of lncCMPK2 promoted CRC cell proliferation and cell cycle progression both in vitro and in vivo. Localization studies revealed that lncCMPK2 is confined to the nucleus of CRC cells, where it interacted with far upstream element binding protein 3 (FUBP3). This interplay is capable of stabilizing FUBP3 and facilitating its recruitment to the far upstream element of the c-Myc gene alongside further transcriptional activation, offering a novel insight into functional role of lncCMPK2 in the CRC progression [57]. Of note, several respiratory diseases also involve CMPK2-mediated inflammatory pathways. Tang et al. clarified CMPK2 as a conserved hub gene across viral pneumonias in the ways of fostering NLRP3 to amplify IL-1β/IL-18 release, as a consequence, the exacerbation of lung injury [58]. This phenomenon also extends to lung ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury [59], acute lung injury (ALI) [60], ARDS [25], and sepsis [35], positioning the CMPK2-NLRP3 axis as a potent participant of pulmonary inflammation. Besides, CMPK2 also underpins some renal and skeletal pathological entities. In urate nephropathy, exosome-mediated signaling involving long intergenic noncoding RNA-p21 (lincRNA-p21), allograft inflammatory factor 1 (AIF-1), and CMPK2 exacerbates tubular inflammation and apoptosis through the activation of NLRP3 [40], while glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis involves CMPK2-driven mitochondrial DNA leakage, inflammasome activation, and osteogenic senescence, all of which can be medicinally mitigated upon CMPK2 inhibition [61]. Collectively, CMPK2 orchestrates multi-stages pertinent to disease progression through convergent pathways, that is, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammasome activation, and immune-metabolic crosstalk. While its pleiotropic effects in systemic pathologies are increasingly recognized, translational gaps are supposed to be addressed with in-depth investigation and exploration. Future studies are required to prioritize tissue-specific targeting strategies, isoform-selective inhibitors, and clinical validation of CMPK2 as a surrogate biomarker across aforesaid interconnected disease spectra.

CMPK2 contributes to diverse diseases through distinct mechanisms: (1) Infectious Diseases: Functions as an interferon-stimulated gene, limiting viral replication (e.g., Dengue/Zika virus) and modulates macrophage responses to bacteria (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis). (2) Neoplastic Diseases: Exhibits context-dependent effects via nucleotide metabolism (e.g., lncCMPK2 promotes colorectal cancer; elevated CMPK2 correlates with poor melanoma prognosis). (3) Autoimmune/Inflammatory Disorders: Induces mitochondrial ROS and inflammasome activation in SLE; promotes synovitis and cartilage degradation in RA upregulated in pSS salivary glands and DM muscle/skin, linking mitochondrial dysfunction to inflammation. (4) Organ-Specific Injuries: Amplifies lung inflammation in ALI via NLRP3 activation; biallelic loss leads to mitochondrial deficiency and brain calcification. Clinical significance of CMPK2

CMPK2, a multifunctional enzyme integral to nucleotide metabolism, mitochondrial dynamics, and inflammatory responses, has emerged as a critical participant in diverse pathological processes. While its effects pertaining to immune modulation and metabolic regulation are well-established, accumulating evidence has underscored its clinical implications as a diagnostic surrogate and therapeutic target across distinct oncology and inflammatory disorders (Table 2). In diagnostic contexts, pan-cancer analyses revealed elevated CMPK2 expression as a high-accuracy diagnostic marker for as many as 16 malignancies, particularly skin cutaneous melanoma, related to poor prognosis as indicated by reduced overall survival and progression-free interval [54, 61]. Mechanistically in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), Zhou et al. identified the lncRNA NRIR as an instigator of immune escape, demonstrating its role in programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) upregulation via transcriptional activation of CMPK2. NRIR initiates CMPK2 transcription, responsible for modulating ATP-dependent signal transducer and STAT1 activation within the IFN pathway to enhance PD-L1 expression, positioning both NRIR and CMPK2 as diagnostic biomarkers alongside therapeutic targets for HCC [62]. In RA, neutrophil-derived CMPK2 expression predicted tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) responsiveness with remarkably discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.94), specifically forecasting biological therapy effectiveness [63]. Therapeutically, boric acid suppressed glioblastoma progression in xenograft models, presenting dose-dependent survival extension, tumor volume reduction, and hematological parameter normalization while modulating CMPK2 expression in agreement with proliferation marker Ki67 and astrocytic marker glial fibrillary acidic protein [64]. Small-molecule inhibitors (Z25/Z08) reduce the expression level of CMPK2 mRNA and protein, thereby inhibiting NLRP3 and alleviating the development of severe viral pneumonia [58]. MicroRNA (miRNA)-based strategies include: bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC-exosomes) carrying miR-202-5p attenuating pulmonary ischemia–reperfusion injury by suppressing CMPK2-mediated pyroptosis [59]; and miR-494-3p attenuating ALI-associated apoptosis and inflammation through CMPK2 downregulation [60]. Natural compounds demonstrate efficacy: cannabidiol accelerated oral ulcer healing by synergistic harnessing of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1), inhibiting CMPK2/NLRP3 inflammasome activation [65]. Dendrobium fimbriatum, a traditional Chinese herb, protected against gastric injury via targeting CMPK2 [66]; and Xixin Decoction (XXD) alleviated the pathology of Alzheimer’s disease in mice models through CMPK2/NLRP3 axis inhibition, reducing amyloid-β deposition while suppressing TNF-α and IL-1β [67]. Despite these advances, critical gaps include prospective validation of CMPK2 as a pan-disease biomarker, optimization of tissue-specific delivery systems for miRNA/exosome-based therapies, and mechanistic research elaborating immune-metabolic system crosstalk. Thus, CMPK2 represents a pathophysiological nexus requiring rigorous clinical investigation to translate its diagnostic and therapeutic potential.

Future perspectives

Despite significant advances in elucidating the pathological roles of CMPK2 across multisystemic diseases, key mechanistic and translational challenges remain unresolved. Foremost, deeper investigations into tissue-specific microenvironments—such as the synovium in RA—are essential, extending beyond the predominant focus on immune cells and hepatic/neuronal models. Techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics could delineate cell-type-specific regulatory networks governing CMPK2’s dual metabolic and immune functions, including post-translational modifications and non-coding RNA interactions. Concurrently, establishing causal links between CMPK2 genetic variants and disease susceptibility (e.g., SLE) via multi-omics approaches is necessary, while elucidating organ crosstalk pathways (e.g., the gut-liver-brain axis) in CMPK2-mediated inflammation will refine existing pathophysiological models. Furthermore, while CMPK2 is known to activate the cGAS-STING pathway and NLRP3 inflammasomes via mitochondrial dysfunction, critical feedback mechanisms within this bidirectional mitochondria-inflammation axis remain unclear. Specifically, how inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α) directly regulate CMPK2 expression and whether CMPK2 interacts with mitophagy adaptors to modulate DAMPs clearance remains unknown. Addressing these gaps requires therapeutic innovation, starting with the development of isoform-selective inhibitors that exploit structural differences between CMPK2 and its homologs (e.g., CMPK1), which can be accelerated by artificial intelligence (AI)-driven drug design. Additionally, combinatorial strategies that pair CMPK2 suppression with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., in HCC) or antifibrotic agents (e.g., in hepatic disorders) should be explored to counter compensatory pathways. Engineering tissue-targeted carriers (e.g., ligand-conjugated nanoparticles) for nucleic acid-based therapies (e.g., miRNA/exosomes) will also be essential to enhance spatiotemporal precision. For successful clinical translation, validating CMPK2 as a pan-disease biomarker through prospective multicenter cohorts—emphasizing its correlation with NLRP3 activation in autoimmune diseases—and advancing non-invasive detection methods (e.g., liquid biopsies for circulating mtDNA-CMPK2 complexes) are crucial priorities. Ultimately, the cross-disciplinary integration of structural biology, metabolomics, and AI will be pivotal in transforming these interconnected research priorities into effective therapies for intractable diseases.

Conclusion

CMPK2 is a key enzyme involved in various aspects of cellular nucleotide metabolism, functionally catalyzing the phosphorylation of CMP and UMP to CDP and UDP, respectively. Its potential medicinal effects in relation to nucleotide homeostasis, energy metabolism, and immune response underlie its clinical implication and practical relevance among both healthy situations and illnesses. Further insights into CMPK2’s molecular mechanism and its involvement in disease pathogenesis may offer invaluable clues to develop novel therapeutic strategies across multiple systems.
