Trajectories of health care contact days experienced by decedents who underwent curative-intent surgery for pancreatic cancer
Authors: Whitney V Johnson, Khalid Ishani, Amy A Gravely, Vishal R Patel, Christopher J LaRocca, David G Brauer, Eric H Jensen, Anders D Westanmo, Arjun Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with pancreatic cancer undergoing curative-intent surgery face incredible burdens, even long-term after surgery, but the amount of time spent in health care (ie, as health care contact days) was not hitherto quantified. In this cohort study of 649 patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing curative-intent surgery in the largest integrated health system in the United States, patients spent approximately 50% of days as contact days (and 50% as “home days”—without health care contact) in the first month after surgery. The percentage of home days only increased to approximately 60% even 1 year after surgery when including the approximate mortality 20% rate, and patients spent at least 20% of their days with health care contact even months/years after surgery. These data are important for counseling patients and caregivers (both to set expectations and to augment informed decision-making) and for health systems and policy makers to ensure appropriate supports.

Background

Health care contact days—the number of days spent receiving care outside the home—reflect how much of a person’s life is consumed by health care.1 Patients with metastatic solid cancers receiving chemotherapy spend approximately one in five days in contact with the health care system.2,3 However, contact days patterns among patients undergoing curative-intent surgery are unknown. Patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing surgery may face significant burden from the surgery itself, perioperative treatments, and longer-term health impacts and recovery.4 For patients considering this path, a key clinical question is: what will my daily life look like leading up to and after surgery?

Thus, we used a national database to characterize the intensity and trajectory of contact days for patients undergoing curative-intent surgery for pancreatic cancer.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis using the Veterans Affairs (VA) Clinical Cancer Registry and Corporate Data Warehouse to identify adult decedents with pancreatic adenocarcinoma diagnosed between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2023, who underwent curative-intent surgery (distal pancreatectomy or pancreaticoduodenectomy) (Supplementary Table S1). Since contact day patterns were expected to vary based on peri-operative chemotherapy receipt, we divided decedents into four sub-cohorts: received adjuvant chemotherapy only, neoadjuvant chemotherapy only, both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, and no chemotherapy. For each sub-cohort, we divided follow-up time (diagnosis to death) into clinical phases based on chemotherapy/surgery timing—eg, the adjuvant sub-cohort’s time was split into diagnosis to surgery, surgery to chemotherapy start, chemotherapy duration, and chemotherapy end to death.

Each day of follow-up was classified in a binary fashion as a contact day (in-person health care contact) or a home day.3 We calculated the percentage of contact days in each clinical phase as contact days divided by total days in each phase. We plotted weekly contact day rates as percentages and fit a cubic smoothing spline to the normalized observations within each phase. Additionally, to visualize time at home in the 12-month post-operative course, we plotted and fit with cubic splines the monthly home day (inverse of contact days) and contact day percentage, stratified by type of surgery (pancreaticoduodenectomy or distal pancreatectomy) while accounting for mortality.

We conducted analyses from October 1, 2024, to July 31, 2025, using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute LLC). The Minneapolis VA Medical Center Review Board approved this study.

Results

We included 649 decedents (Supplemental Figure S1). The median age was 69 years, 627 (96.6%) were male, and 413 (63.6%) underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy (Table 1).

Across the four sub-cohorts stratified by chemotherapy receipt, median overall survival ranged from 12.4 months (no chemotherapy) to 23.5 months (both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy), with median overall contact day percentages from diagnosis to death ranging from 27% (adjuvant chemotherapy) to 36% (neoadjuvant chemotherapy) (Supplementary Table S2). Figure 1A-D demonstrate the trajectories of weekly contact day percentages across each sub-cohort and their clinical phases. For example, for the adjuvant sub-cohort, the median follow-up time in each phase and the corresponding median contact day percentages were: diagnosis to surgery (approximately 1 month; 29%), surgery to chemotherapy start (approximately 2 months; 41%, with a sharp decline a few weeks after surgery), chemotherapy duration (approximately 5 months; 26%), and chemotherapy end to death (approximately 10.5 months; 22%). For the sub-cohort that received both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, contact day percentages were similar (median of 31%) during both chemotherapy periods. The lowest contact day percentage across sub-cohorts and phases was 22%. The surgery to death duration (incorporating multiple and different clinical phases across sub-cohorts) followed a U-shaped trajectory with a 100% health care contact day rate immediately after surgery, a trough rate of approximately 20%, and a rise to approximately 40% just prior to death.

Regarding the post-operative course detailing the 12 months post-surgery, only approximately 50% of days in the month after surgery were home days (Figure 1E, F). While the percentage of home days (relative to contact days) increased over time for patients who were alive, due to the competing risk of death (approximately 20% mortality at 12 months), the percentage of days spent as home days remained approximately 60% through the end of 12-months.

Discussion

In this national cohort study from the largest U.S. integrated healthcare system, Veterans undergoing curative-intent surgery for pancreatic adenocarcinoma experienced persistently high and intense contact days throughout the disease course. Previous work has demonstrated a 20%-40% decision regret rate among patients undergoing surgery, and we hypothesize that this costly time burden may play a part in this sentiment.5

Contact days were not lower when chemotherapy was delivered neoadjuvantly (vs. adjuvantly)—both across sub-cohorts, and within the sub-cohort that received chemotherapy both before and after surgery. Because the sub-cohorts were non-randomized (potentially confounded—eg, only patients who are fit enough for chemotherapy post-surgery may be included in the adjuvant chemotherapy cohort) and clinical issues other than actual chemotherapy receipt (eg, biliary obstruction, post-operative recovery, etc.) can influence contact days, it is difficult to comment on the true tolerability of neoadjuvant vs adjuvant chemotherapy using the contact days measure.6

The pattern of contact days from surgery to death mirrors the previously described U-shaped trajectory for patients with metastatic cancer from diagnosis to death.3,7,8 This may reflect the high recurrence risk and that many patients eventually died of relapsed metastatic disease. Such graphs can be used to communicate expected clinical courses to patients considering and initiating treatment as well as their caregivers, both to set expectations and to augment shared, informed decision-making.1 Health systems and policy makers can use these data to ensure supports for all patients and their care partners, and especially those more vulnerable to the expected multifaceted burdens of cancer care.9,10

Strengths of this study include the use of a national contemporary cohort over an 11-year period. We applied strict inclusion and exclusion criteria and identified all in-person health care contact. The predominantly male population, inclusion of only decedents, and possible care delivery changes in 2020 and beyond may limit generalizability and interpretability. We were unable to assess whether a contact day was clinically appropriate or not.
