Abnormal Sleep in Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy
Authors: Oliver L Steiner, Claudia Angela, Fabian Klostermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesChronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is a dysimmune disease leading to sensorimotor deficits due to peripheral nerve dysfunction, but recently additional non‐sensorimotor symptoms (NSMS) were increasingly recognized. In this context, we compared the sleep behavior in persons with versus without CIDP and discussed the results with respect to further NSMS.MethodsTwenty‐five CIDP patients and 27 controls took part in this prospective, cross‐sectional study. Clinically, sensorimotor disability (RODS), affective state (DESC‐I), and fatigue levels (FSMC scores) were assessed. Regarding sleep–wake behavior, they wore actigraphic devices over 14 consecutive days and completed sleep diaries, chronotype questionnaires, and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The actigraphic data were analyzed with respect to sleep efficiency, wake after sleep onset (WASO), sleep onset latency, total sleep duration, intradaily variability (IV), and interdaily stability (IS).ResultsPatients with CIDP reported significantly worse subjective sleep quality (p < 0.001). They also showed higher levels of fatigue (p < 0.001) and depressiveness (p = 0.046). The actigraphic results showed significantly reduced WASO (p bonf = 0.012), especially early in the morning and immediately preceding waking up (p = 0.010). The sleep abnormalities were not linked to the raised clinical data (p > 0.05).DiscussionBeyond subjectively reduced sleep quality, actigraphic data show that persons with CIDP have lowered sleep efficacy and experience increased nocturnal arousals. This suggests that sleep disturbance is a genuine aspect of CIDP adding to the underrecognized problem of NSMS in this condition.Keywords: chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, CNS manifestations, fatigue, neurodegeneration, sleep

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is a neurological disorder characterized by progressive demyelination of peripheral nerves caused by dysimmune processes against Schwann cells. The resulting impairment of nerve conduction leads to sensory impairments and weakness with mostly symmetrical, both proximal and distal distribution [1]. Next to the main phenotype, variants with pure motor or sensory symptoms and distal‐only or focal localization exist [2].

Beyond these lead deficits, CIDP goes along with various non‐sensorimotor symptoms (NSMS) such as fatigue [3, 4], depressiveness [3, 5], cognitive change [6], whose aggregate impact may even outweigh the sensorimotor burden of the disease. In this context, a further frequent complaint of persons with CIDP is disturbed sleep [7, 8], which, however, has never been studied using objective methods. This is remarkable, given the evident importance of sleep for health and the profound knowledge about sleep dysfunctions in other neurological conditions [9].

A practical, low‐cost, and noninvasive method to quantify sleep parameters is actigraphy. It has been shown to provide high accuracy data, even when compared to polysomnography, which is the current gold standard for sleep measurement and evaluation [10]. In this study, we assessed the sleep behavior of persons with versus without CIDP through actigraphy. The group results were contrasted with each other to identify potential disease‐related abnormalities. Further, the actigraphic data were related to the subjective sleep experience and to additionally assessed clinical data. This was done in order to come to a more comprehensive picture of NSMS in CIDP and to gain insights into the nature of subjective sleep dysfunction that is prevalent but underrecognized in this condition.

Methods

Participants

Primary inclusion criterion for participation in this study in the patient group was the definite diagnosis of CIDP according to the 2021EAN/PNS criteria and the absence of CIDP in controls. Exclusion criteria for both groups were neurological conditions apart from CIDP as well as psychiatric diseases, night‐shift work, and diagnosed insomnia disorders (persons with general sleep problems, but not diagnosed with a sleep disorder could participate in the study). Based on previous research in neurological disease [11] and on subjective sleep data in CIDP [7], 30 persons with definite CIDP and 30 control persons without CIDP were initially recruited for this study. From the persons with CIDP, five were excluded, because of extended off‐wrist time of the actigraphy device (n = 4), an additional neurological diagnosis (n = 1). Of the remaining 25 persons with CIDP, 15 had a typical phenotype with symmetric proximal and distal sensorimotor deficits and 10 had a CIDP variant. All received regular intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) maintenance treatment in the Neurological Outpatient Clinic of Charité, Campus Benjamin Franklin. Additionally, 30 healthy control participants without neurological or psychiatric conditions were recruited. Three controls were excluded due to excessive off‐wrist periods of the actigraphy device, resulting in 27 participants. Twelve were partners of the included CIDP patients (8 shared the bed with the CIDP‐affected partner). All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Charité (protocol number EA/165/16).

Fatigue, Depressive Symptoms, and Subjective Sleep

Fatigue was evaluated using the Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognitive Functions (FSMC) [12]. The FSMC consists of 20 items scored on a Likert scale (total scores ranging from 20 to 100), with higher scores indicating greater fatigue severity. The FSMC differentiates peripheral (motor) fatigue from central (cognitive) fatigue, characterized by reduced physical endurance and, respectively, deficient mental resilience with problems in concentration, memory, and cognitive functioning accompanied by a feeling of excessive exhaustion. Established thresholds classify total scores into mild (≥ 43), moderate (≥ 53), and severe fatigue (≥ 63) [12].

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Rasch‐based Depression Screening (DESC) [13]. Each of the 10 items is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always), resulting in total scores between 0 and 40, with higher scores indicating higher depressiveness. A clinical cutoff score of ≥ 12 has been established to indicate significant depression [13].

Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [14], including seven subdomains: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medications, and daytime dysfunction. Higher PSQI scores indicate poorer sleep and a value > 5 is the cutoff for the assumption of a clinically significant sleep disturbance [14].

Chronotype was measured with the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ), which assesses sleep timing behaviors on work and free days [15].

Sensorimotor Disability

All patients underwent a standardized neurological evaluation. The severity of polyneuropathic symptoms was assessed using the Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (R‐ODS), a standard tool for evaluating the impairment from sensorimotor symptoms in immune‐mediated peripheral neuropathies [16]. This instrument derives a composite score from the responses to 24 items that evaluate both fine and gross sensorimotor abilities. Each item is scored from 0 (indicating the task cannot be performed) to 2 (indicating it can be performed easily), with the total score ranging from 0 (complete disability) to 48 (no disability).

Actigraphy and Sleep Diary

Participants wore Condor ActTrust II actigraphy devices (Condor Instruments, São Paulo, Brazil) on their nondominant wrists for 2 weeks. Throughout the recording period, participants also completed daily sleep diaries, in which they reported daytime naps, main sleep period, activity, use of medication, use of electronic devices, and off‐wrist times. For the analysis, weekday data were included, whereas Fridays and Saturdays were not analyzed, in order to control for different sleep–wake routines during weekends. Periods when the devices were removed (e.g., for taking a shower), were excluded from the analysis. To describe sleep–wake and circadian behavior, we assessed the following parameters: Motor activity was quantified within the Proportional Integrative Mode (PIM) at 1‐min intervals. PIM is a movement intensity measure that shows a strong correlation with polysomnography for assessing sleep characteristics [17]. Ambient light was measured with a light sensor that is integrated within the actigraphy device. Skin temperature was measured with a sensor at the backside of the device. Main and secondary sleep (naps during the day) were identified first by the provided ActTrust II software algorithm (https://condorinst.com/en/actstudio‐software/, accessed March 10, 2025). Because, the algorithm uses basically only movement inactivity to determine sleep onset, it is widely recommended to adjust these values if possible based on sleep diary entries or available light protocols [18]. Accordingly, each individual report was screened by the two first authors (O.L.S. and C.A.) based on the diaries and additionally ambient light history was used to determine the sleep onset time.

Sleep parameters included sleep efficiency (%), wake after sleep onset (WASO, defined as intervals of wakefulness preceded and followed by sleep), total wake times, and total sleep duration. Circadian rhythm measures comprised interdaily stability (IS), intradaily variability (IV), mesor and circadian amplitude. All these values were again calculated by the ActTrust II software.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.4) within a Jupyter Notebook (version 7.0.8) environment. Prior to analysis, the Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted with respect to data distribution. For comparisons, for normally distributed data two‐sided t‐tests, for data not meeting the normality assumption Mann–Whitney U tests were used. We report effect sizes as Cohen's d [19] for normally distributed data and as r (calculated as Z/√N from the Mann–Whitney U test [20]) for non‐normally distributed data. We applied a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, for the subdomains of fatigue, subjective sleep, and actigrafic data (p bonf). To further validate the findings, we ran additional Bayesian statistics (t‐tests) for each variable with the penguin package [21]. We interpreted the Bayesian factor with the “Jeffreys' scale” [22]. Specifically, we computed BF10 for each comparison and classified the evidence for the hypothesis using the thresholds 1–3.16 (“weak”), 3.16–10 (“substantial”), 10–31.6 (“strong”), 31.6–100 (“very strong”), and ≥ 100 (“decisive”). Whenever BF10 was < 1, we inverted it to BF01 = 1/BF10 and applied the same cutoffs to grade evidence for the null hypothesis. To analyze WASO distributions for the main sleep after sleep onset and before sleep‐offset, the percentage of WASO was calculated for each 1‐min time‐bin across participants. For instance, if one out of 10 participants who previously had been asleep was awake during a specific minute, this corresponded to 10% WASO for that bin. These data were then smoothed with a 30‐min rolling median‐window, and cluster‐based permutation tests (1000+ permutations, cluster‐level p = 0.01) identified significant differences. These tests inherently correct for multiple comparisons across time points by evaluating clusters of temporally adjacent differences. For the cluster‐based permutation tests we employed the MNE‐Python package [23].

An explorative subanalysis was run additionally, implying the few persons from the patient and control groups sharing the same bed (8 patients with CIDP and 8 controls). This was done because of the high similarity of the environmental sleep conditions in these participants from both groups. Thus, group distinctions identified in the main analysis and confirmed in this subanalysis could hardly be explained by conceivable (yet unlikely) group differences in sleep hygiene.

To explore potential associations of CIDP symptoms and objective sleep metrics, correlations between depressive symptoms, fatigue, subjective sleep measures, sensorimotor disability values and objective sleep parameters were calculated using Pearson coefficients for normally distributed variables and Spearman coefficients for non‐normally distributed variables. p values were corrected for multiple comparisons with Benjamini–Hochberg (p BH) correction.

Results

In total, the behavior of participants was recorded for 13 days (range: 10–19 days). After excluding weekends, the average monitoring period was approximately 10 days (range: 7–13 days). There were no significant group differences with respect to age (Control: 60.11 ± 8.58 years; CIDP: 60.72 ± 11.60 years; p = 0.832, d = −0.06, BF10 = 0.284), sex distribution (Control: 15; CIDP: 11; p = 0.415, BF10 = 0.368), and recording times (Control: 9 ± 2 days; CIDP: 10 ± 2 days; p = 0.552) (Table 1).

Fatigue and Affective Symptoms

Regarding fatigue, total FSMC scores indicated that patients with CIDP were significantly more fatigued than controls, as evidenced by substantially higher scores (CIDP: 59.64 ± 22.79 vs. Control: 34.96 ± 11.26; p < 0.001, r = 0.656). This difference was observed in both fatigue subdomains (motor fatigue in CIDP/Control: 32.32 ± 11.13 vs. 17.15 ± 5.65; p bonf < 0.001, d = −1.74; cognitive fatigue in CIDP/Control: 27.32 ± 12.16 vs. 17.85 ± 6.19; p bonf = 0.003, d = −0.99). In addition, DESC‐I scores were higher in CIDP patients compared to controls (CIDP/Control: 7.44 ± 8.03 vs. 2.89 ± 2.97; p = 0.012, d = −0.76). Bayesian analysis provided strong to decisive evidence for fatigue difference (BF10 = 31.13–78,215.71), and substantial evidence for DESC‐I difference (BF10 = 4.73).

Fatigue difference also showed up between the CIDP and control participants sharing the same bed. Total and motor fatigue scores were substantially elevated in the CIDP patients (total fatigue in CIDP/Control: 60.88 ± 20.77 vs. 40.50 ± 13.17; p = 0.040, r = 0.625, BF10 = 2.23; motor fatigue in CIDP/Control: 32.88 ± 9.09 vs. 18.38 ± 6.99; p < 0.001, r = 0.797, BF10 = 12.47). No other statistically significant group difference was found.

Subjective and Objective Sleep Quality

CIDP participants reported significantly poorer overall subjective sleep quality, as indicated by higher PSQI Total Scores compared to controls (CIDP/Control: 9.00 ± 4.28 vs. 5.33 ± 3.16; p = 0.001, r = 0.54, see Figure 1). For the subjective sleep quality component, CIDP participants reported worse scores than controls (CIDP/Control: 1.76 ± 0.88 vs. 1.07 ± 0.73; p = 0.036, r = 0.42). Scores for Subjective Sleep Latency did not significantly differ between groups (CIDP/Control: 1.20 ± 1.04 vs. 1.00 ± 0.78; p = 1.00, r = 0.08). Similarly, scores for subjective sleep duration did not significantly differ (CIDP/Control: 0.92 ± 0.86 vs. 0.44 ± 0.75; p = 0.166, r = 0.333). Subjective sleep efficiency scores also did not significantly differ (CIDP/Control: 1.08 ± 1.08 vs. 0.70 ± 0.91; p bonf = 1.00, r = 0.201). Regarding sleep disturbances, CIDP participants reported higher scores than controls (CIDP/Control: 1.64 ± 0.70 vs. 1.15 ± 0.46; p bonf = 0.046, r = 0.375). Scores for the use of sleeping medication did not significantly differ (CIDP/Control: 0.44 ± 0.96 vs. 0.22 ± 0.80; p bonf = 1.00, r = 0.117). However, CIDP participants reported significantly greater daytime dysfunction (CIDP/Control: 1.80 ± 0.96 vs. 0.74 ± 0.86; p bonf = 0.002, r = 0.582).

For the secondary sleep, we did not observe a significant difference in sleep duration (CIDP/Control: 26.25 ± 43.01 min vs. 35.38 ± 52.52 min; p = 0.597; r = 0.074).

Bayesian analysis provided strong to decisive evidence that, compared to controls, CIDP participants had poorer subjective sleep quality (BF10 = 10.66), more sleep disturbances (BF10 = 9.10), greater daytime dysfunction (BF10 = 196.32), lower sleep efficiency (BF10 = 36.318), and increased WASO (BF10 = 7.775). Conversely, there was substantial evidence for a sleep duration indifferent between the groups (BF10 = 0.322).

Sleep‐related measures among patients and relatives sharing the same bed revealed significant differences in objective sleep parameters. Specifically, sleep efficiency was lower in CIDP (84.220% ± 6.240%) compared to healthy controls (90.830% ± 9.260%; p = 0.015, r = −0.719, BF10 = 1.045). Also, WASO was notably higher (63.420 ± 33.500 min vs. 36.150 ± 34.610 min; p = 0.028, r = 0.656, BF10 = 0.974) in the CIDP cohort. No other subjective or objective sleep parameters reached statistical significance.

Circadian Rhythm and Chronotype

Regarding chronotype, no group difference in MCTQ scores was identified (CIDP/Control: 3.56 ± 1.24 vs. 3.43 ± 0.88; p = 0.735, r = 0.055). Actigraphic parameters of circadian rhythmicity did not significantly differ between the groups either (IV scores in CIDP/Control: 0.86 ± 0.16 vs. 0.89 ± 0.20; p bonf = 1.000, d = 0.18; IS scores in CIDP/Control: 0.48 ± 0.11 vs. 0.46 ± 0.11; p = 0.504, d = 0.19; Mesor [mean activity level] in CIDP/Control: 1555.99 ± 728.43 vs. 1459.37 ± 83.6; p bonf = 1.000, d = 0.15; Amplitude in CIDP/Control: 1628.96 ± 67.50 vs. 1592.76 ± 83.60; p bonf = 1.00, d = 0.34).

Bayesian analysis provided substantial evidence for similar MCTQ Scores (BF10 = 0.30). There was weak evidence for the intactness of the circadian system based on Bayesian analysis.

Correlation Analyses

Within the CIDP group, significant associations were found between DESC‐I scores and FSMC motor fatigue (ρ = 0.66, p BH = 0.007) as well as cognitive fatigue (ρ = 0.74, p BH < 0.001; see also Figure 3). FSMC total scores were strongly correlated with FSMC motor (ρ = 0.78, p BH < 0.001) and cognitive scores (ρ = 0.80, p BH < 0.001), and FSMC motor and cognitive subscales were highly correlated as well (r = 0.91, p BH < 0.001). Sleep efficiency was positively associated with sleep duration (ρ = 0.59, p BH = 0.027) and negatively with WASO (ρ = −0.74, p BH < 0.001), while WASO was positively associated with total awakenings (ρ = 0.71, p BH = 0.002). No other correlations were significant.

Discussion

This study provides actigraphic evidence of sleep dysfunction in CIDP, adding objective data to the feeling of reduced sleep quality often reported by patients [7, 8, 24]. Specifically, the results showed reduced sleep efficiency and increased waking periods after sleep onset (WASO). Despite this fragmentation, the sleep duration was not significantly reduced and the overall circadian behavior remained intact.

Generally, the findings indicate that CIDP goes along with a disturbance of the sleep microarchitecture. In particular, so‐called micro‐arousals could underlie the increased WASO value in the patient group. They were found in various medical conditions [25] and describe sudden changes of brain and muscle activity of only a few seconds, resulting in sleep fragmentation and a reduction of its restorative function [25]. However, due to their limited influence on complete wakefulness they do not necessarily impact the overall circadian rhythm. Thus, micro‐arousals could explain the low sleep efficiency and quality without a significant impact on the general functioning of the circadian clock observed in the CIDP group.

Fatigue was significantly increased in CIDP patients. Although one could assume that this was an effect of unrestful sleep, as has been suggested for, e.g., multiple sclerosis [11], neither actigraphic nor PSQI abnormalities were found to correlate with this finding. Accordingly, fatigue and disturbed sleep appeared as independent phenomena in CIDP. Further, depressiveness, tendentially increased in the CIDP cohort, did not correlate with the determined sleep abnormalities. Although the values measured in the CIDP group did not imply cases of high‐grade affective disorders, this is worthwhile to note, because depression has been found to be mutually associated with fragmented sleep [26]. Finally, it deserves mention that no relation of sleep measures with sensorimotor disability was identified in the patient group. Thus, the data suggest that sleep problems in CIDP do not simply emerge as a reaction to the discomfort from polyneuropathic complaints or in the framework of psychological distress associated with the condition.

The specification of sleep abnormalities is of particular interest with respect to increasingly recognized NSMS in CIDP. In clinical settings, NSMS are less attended than typical polyneuropathic symptoms. Although this seems obvious given the primary nature of CIDP, it is important to note, since NSMS are thought to drive the health‐related quality of life reduction as much as sensorimotor complaints. This topic is also linked to the question of the origin of particular NSMS, that is, whether CIDP is an all‐out peripheral condition or if some disease sequelae reflect subtle CNS involvement. With respect to sleep disorders in CIDP, different findings are of interest in this context. For example, reminiscent of findings in multiple sclerosis as the main demyelinating CNS condition [27], CIDP‐related damage of the retinal ganglion cell layer has been identified in recent imaging studies [28]. The photosensitive neurons in this layer are critical for circadian entrainment and their loss has been associated with impaired sleep quality in other diseases [29]. Thus, altered retinal function is a conceivable factor of changes in the sleep behavior of persons with CIDP [30]. In a similar vein, recent data suggest that CIDP goes along with a hypofunctional retinal melanopsin pathway [7], which could reasonably contribute to sleep disturbances. However, in this framework one would have to explain the stable day–night rhythm in the CIDP group, for example, as a behavioral compensation strategy for problems to fall and stay asleep, potentially aggravated by a less regular schedule.

Further, the current results may be considered with respect to another interesting area of NSMS in CIDP, namely subtly decreased cognitive performances [6, 30]. In this regard, it has been proposed that a certain passage of the disease pathology via a leaky blood–brain barrier may impair sensitive CNS functions [7, 28, 31, 32]. However, an alternative or additional explanation could be that sleep fragmentation exerts a negative impact on cognitive functioning. Sleep is thought to be particularly relevant for cognitive integrity [33], for example, by consolidating memory contents or via glymphatic removal of metabolic waste in the central nervous system [34]. Its disturbance promotes neurodegenerative processes [35], also of interest given that in CIDP elevated serum neurofilament light chain levels biomarking axonal damage [36, 37] were found associated with low cognitive performance [38].

The findings suggest that sleep disruption is a prevalent, yet easily overlooked problem in CIDP, which deserves increased attention in the treatment routine. Notably, WASO values exceeding 31 min are considered indicative of clinically relevant insomnia [39]. In our cohort, 68% (17/25) of the patients met this criterion, calling for further investigation. To study the underpinnings of this actigraphic result, further polysomnographic examination would allow to identify potential abnormalities of the sleep architecture [40] and appears indicated in CIDP patients, who report sleep‐related complaints or show, if available, abnormal actigraphic results.

The study has several limitations. For example, the assessment of sleep behavior was conducted in real‐world settings, so that environmental factors influencing sleep hygiene were not controlled and theoretically could have been different between the groups. However, also in the small subgroup of participants sharing the same bed, reduced objective sleep abnormalities were present in the CIDP group, which can be understood as an indication that the observed effects were not driven by group differences of external conditions. Further, the absence of a link between fatigue and both subjective and objective sleep behavior should be considered with caution, because it seems to imply, for example, that the potential of sleep counseling to reduce fatigue in CIDP patients is low. However, it cannot be excluded that an association between fatigue and sleep behavior was missed due to the used cohort sizes and, accordingly, this issue might be readdressed in larger future studies. Methodologically, such trials would ideally use polysomnographic techniques, in order to gain insights into the physiology and potential stage‐dependency of sleep disturbance in CIDP, which were not approached by the current actigraphic measurements.

In sum, the raised data demonstrate the presence of sleep abnormalities related to CIDP, with increased night‐time arousals. These findings confirm the subjective feeling of unrestful sleep reported by affected persons and advocate the assessment of sleep quality in CIDP patients for diagnostic and therapeutic reasons. Future research could address the biological underpinnings of disordered sleep and its associations with other NSMS to gain a comprehensive picture of the disease beyond its peripheral lead symptoms.
