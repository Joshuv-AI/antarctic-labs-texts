Machine Learning‐Derived Neural Signatures of Itch and Pain That Reliably Distinguish the Two Sensations in Humans: A Proof‐Of‐Concept Study
Authors: Hideki Mochizuki, Elly Georgas, Odelia Schwartz, Gil Yosipovitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRecent advances in human neuroimaging combined with machine learning have enabled identification of neural signatures representing various internal states, providing a promising framework for developing objective biomarkers. However, no study has investigated neural signatures that can reliably identify and distinguish itch and pain. Such neural signatures were explored in the present study using functional MRI (fMRI) and support vector machine (SVM).MethodsWe measured brain activity in 33 healthy participants under cowhage‐induced itch, mustard oil‐induced pain, and control conditions using fMRI. We made seed‐based functional connectivity images (R‐images), where seed brain regions were the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and bilateral anterior insular cortex (aIC). We conducted a cross‐validated and bootstrapped SVM using R‐images to identify key brain regions with weights that were important to identify and distinguish itch and pain (threshold to identify these regions: p < 0.05). These neural signatures of itch and pain were applied to test sets of R‐images to examine classification performance (Itch vs. Control or Pain).ResultsThese signatures showed excellent classification capability, in particular when combining multiple signatures (area under the curve of receiver operating characteristic curve: > 0.9, accuracy: > 90%).ConclusionsThis is the first neuroimaging study to explore neural signatures that can reliably detect and distinguish itch and pain using machine learning. Our approach using seed‐based functional connectivity images combined with cross‐validated and bootstrapped SVM demonstrated high classification performance. The present study serves as a proof‐of‐concept demonstrating the feasibility of this approach to develop brain‐based biomarkers for assessing itch and pain.Significance StatementThis is the first study to identify neural signatures of itch and pain. These signatures reveal distinct brain network patterns representing itch and pain, enabling reliable detection and differentiation of these two sensations based on brain activity. These signatures hold strong potential for the development of objective assessments of itch and pain.

Introduction

Itch and pain are closely related somatosensory experiences, and their chronic forms are common symptoms in many diseases, affecting the quality of life of people worldwide, from infants to the elderly (Le Pors et al. 2021; Yosipovitch et al. 2024; Criado et al. 2025; Chambers et al. 2024; Zajacova et al. 2021; Dagnino and Campos 2022) Therefore, accurate assessment of itch and pain is essential for detecting their presence and evaluating therapeutic effects. However, current clinical assessments of itch and pain rely almost entirely on self‐report, which is often challenging for individuals who are unable to communicate or can communicate only unreliably, including infants, very young children, bedridden individuals with dementia or cerebral palsy, and unconscious patients (Le Pors et al. 2021; Wager et al. 2013; Booker and Herr 2016; Herr et al. 2019; Ikoma et al. 2020; Smith et al. 2024). It is also important to distinguish between itch and pain in these populations because itch and pain treatments differ (e.g., antihistamines for itch, and acetaminophen for pain). Mu‐opioid receptor agonists relieve pain but cause itch (Kim et al. 2022). To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has attempted to develop an objective tool capable of identifying and distinguishing itch from pain.

Recently, multivariate analysis using machine learning has been introduced in human neuroimaging, which is making it possible to elucidate unique features from brain activity that represent various internal states including emotions, interoceptions, and thoughts. These neural signatures offer a promising framework for developing objective biomarkers to assess various internal states (e.g., emotions, perceptions, mental health, etc.) (Al‐Zubaidi et al. 2019; Altmann et al. 2016; Bu et al. 2019; Callan et al. 2014; Frick et al. 2014; Gim et al. 2024; Månsson et al. 2015; Mochizuki and Yosipovitch 2024; Moll et al. 2014; Sato et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2006, 2007; Wager et al. 2013).

Thus, the present study aimed to identify distributed spatial patterns of brain activity associated with itch and pain. Functional MRI (fMRI) can non‐invasively measure activity across the entire brain. This whole‐brain coverage may increase the likelihood of identifying neural signatures that distinguish itch from pain. As MRI technology continues to become more affordable and widely available for clinical and diagnostic applications (Sarracanie et al. 2015; Kimberly et al. 2023; Kravchenko et al. 2025), the development of fMRI‐based neural signatures is both feasible and clinically relevant.

Based on these considerations, the present study was designed as a proof‐of‐concept investigation to identify neural signatures of itch and pain in healthy participants using fMRI and support vector machines (SVM). We used cowhage ( Mucuna pruriens ) and mustard oil (allyl isothiocyanate) to provoke itch and pain sensations, respectively, as established in previous human studies (Koltzenburg et al. 1993; Drzezga et al. 2001; Mochizuki et al. 2003, 2007, 2015, 2017; Namer et al. 2008; Kremeyer et al. 2010; Papoiu et al. 2011, 2012, 2014; Andersen et al. 2017). Our findings may provide a foundation for developing objective biomarkers of these sensations.

Methods

Participants

Thirty‐three healthy adult participants were enrolled in this study (12 males, 21 females; mean age ± standard deviation: 26 ± 5.7 years). The study was approved by the University of Miami Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Itch Stimuli

As in previous human itch studies (Andersen et al. 2017; Namer et al. 2008; Papoiu et al. 2011, 2012, 2014), cowhage spicules were used to induce itch sensations. Approximately 80–100 spicules were applied to a predetermined region of the right forearm (a circle with 3.5‐cm diameter) and gently rubbed the skin for 20 s.

Pain Stimuli

To induce pain, we used mustard oil (allyl isothiocyanate, AITC) that has been often used in human pain studies (Andersen et al. 2017; Koltzenburg et al. 1993; Kremeyer et al. 2010). A cotton pad soaked with 1 mL of AITC‐solution (40%–50% AITC dissolved in pure paraffin oil) was applied to a predetermined region of the right forearm (a circle with 3.5‐cm diameter).

Experimental Design

There were three conditions: itch, pain, and control. In each condition, an experimenter attached a cotton pad to the right forearm at approximately 8 s after the first fMRI scan and after that another location of the skin was rubbed for 20 s. In both the control and itch conditions, the cotton was infiltrated with only pure paraffin oil, whereas in the pain condition the cotton was infiltrated with AITC‐solution. Thus, pain was provoked only in the pain condition. In the itch condition, the fingers to rub the skin had cowhage spicules, whereas in the pain and control conditions these fingers had no spicules. Consequently, itch was provoked only in the itch condition.

At the end of rubbing, participants started rating the intensity of itch and pain in the itch and pain conditions, respectively, using a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (no itch/pain) to 10 (unbearable itch/pain). The ratings were made by selecting a number displayed on the screen by moving a cursor using a trackball holding in the left hand. Participants continuously conducted the NRS rating during fMRI scans. In the control condition, participants continuously moved the cursor to specific numbers following on‐screen instructions (e.g., ‘Move cursor to 3’) to control for motor activity.

MRI Measurements

Functional images were acquired on a 3‐T MRI scanner (Magnetom Vida, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a gradient‐echo echo‐planar imaging (EPI) sequence (repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle = 80°; field of view (FOV) = 220 mm; slice thickness = 3 mm; 37/39 slices; volumes = 121). High‐resolution structural images were obtained using a magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MP‐RAGE) sequence (TR = 2530 ms; TE = 3.3 ms; flip angle = 9°; FOV = 256 mm; 1 slab; 192 slices; voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm).

Preparation of Brain Activity Images

We focused on seed‐based functional connectivity patterns in the present study. For each participant, we generated a brain map (R‐image) representing the strength of correlations between the time series of each local brain region and that of a location within each of the three seed regions: the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and the right and left anterior insular cortices (raIC and laIC). These locations were selected based on previous reports indicating greater selectivity for itch over pain. The medial parietal cortex, including the PCC, has been repeatedly observed to be activated by itch stimuli and deactivated by pain stimuli (Herde et al. 2007; Mochizuki et al. 2003, 2007, 2009, 2019; Papoiu et al. 2012, 2014; Bergeret et al. 2011; Ishiuji et al. 2009; Wager et al. 2013; Coghill et al. 1999; Yelle et al. 2009). In particular, Mochizuki et al. (2007) directly compared brain activity between itch and pain and demonstrated the PCC selectively responds to itch stimuli. The aIC is activated bilaterally by both itch and pain stimuli. However, a previous study reported that the aIC adjacent to the prefrontal cortex is more selective for itch (Roberts et al. 2019). Each seed region was defined as a 3 × 3 × 3 voxel cube centred on Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinates based on these previous studies (Mochizuki et al. 2007; Roberts et al. 2019): PCC (0, −38, 26), raIC (40, 16, 4), and laIC (−46, 12, 2). For each condition (itch, pain, and control), correlation coefficients between the seed region time series and all other brain voxels were calculated. This yielded seed‐based correlation images, referred to as R‐PCC, R‐raIC and R‐laIC images. In total, nine R‐images (three seeds × three conditions) were obtained for each participant (Figure 1).

Weight Values and Bias

Data preprocessing and data analysis were conducted using Statistical Parametric Mapping (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/), Matlab (https://www.mathworks.com/products/matlab.html), CANlab neuroimaging analysis tools (https://canlab.github.io/). For machine learning, we used support vector machine (SVM), a high performance of classification in small sample size with high dimensional data such as brain images. SVM (a linear SVM with default regularization parameter [C = 1]) was conducted using CANlab. Simply, SVM identifies weights of voxels and bias (intercept) that best classify 2 different conditions (e.g., itch vs. control). Voxels with higher absolute weights indicate that these voxels have higher contributions to classification.

Using a cross‐validated SVM, we estimated weights of the whole grey matter and bias using R‐PCC images, R‐raIC images and R‐laIC images. As an example, we explain how we obtained weights and bias using R‐PCC images in the itch and control conditions. Participants were randomly divided into five groups: four groups served as the training set and the remaining group as the test set. We applied SVM to R‐PCC images in the itch and control conditions of the training groups to estimate weights of all voxels involved in the grey matter and the classifier bias term. Then, these parameters were applied to R‐PCC images of the test group to generate classification scores in the itch and control conditions in individuals in the test group. Details are described below. We repeated the above process by replacing participants involved in a test group until all participants were used as test data.

We calculated the following three types of classification scores:

Score Proportional to Distance From the Hyperplane (SDH)

SDH = ∑(w × x) + b, w: weights of voxels, b: bias, x: correlation coefficients in corresponding voxels in a R‐PCC image.

Decision Score (DS)

DS for itch using itch‐selective weight map for PCC = ∑(w itch × x itch). w itch: weights in voxels within an itch‐selective weight map for PCC, x itch: correlation coefficients in voxels corresponding to an itch‐selective map in a R‐PCC image in the itch condition. DS for control was ∑(w itch × x cont). x cont: correlation coefficients in voxels corresponding to an itch‐selective map in a R‐PCC image in the control condition. DS for pain was ∑(w itch × x pain). x pain: correlation coefficients in voxels corresponding to an itch‐selective map in a R‐PCC image in the pain condition. Because voxels used to calculate scores were limited to itch selective, a DS was expected to outperform a SDH in discriminating both itch versus control and itch versus pain. Similarly, DSs using pain‐selective weight map for PCC was calculated by using the same function (∑(w pain × x itch)). However, we used weights in voxels within a pain‐selective weight map for PCC (w pain). In the same way, we calculated DSs for control and pain were calculated. Detail about how we selected voxels for itch‐ and pain‐selective weight maps are described in another section.

Difference in Decision Scores (DifDS)

Defined as the difference between a DS computed using the itch‐selective weight map for PCC and that computed using pain‐selective weight map for PCC. For example, a DifDS for itch: ∑(w itch × x itch) − ∑(w pain × x itch), a DifDS for pain was calculated ∑(w itch × x pain) − ∑(w pain × x pain). Because weights in an itch‐selective weight map were determined to respond to itch and those in pain‐selective weight map were determined to respond to pain, a DS using an itch‐selective weight map should be higher than that using pain‐selective weight map when an itch‐related image (e.g., a R‐PCC image in the itch condition) was used, yielding a positive DifDS. Conversely, when a pain‐related image (e.g., a R‐PCC image in the pain condition) was used instead, a DS computed with an itch‐selective map for PCC should be lower than that computed with a pain‐selective weight map, yielding a negative DifDS. Thus, we estimated if individuals had itch or pain by seeing if DifDSs were positive or negative values.

As SDHs and DSs were calculated for each condition (itch, pain, and control), three SDHs and three DSs were obtained for each participant. For DifDSs, two DifDSs (itch and pain) were also calculated per participant. The same procedures were applied to R‐raIC and R‐laIC images to obtain SDHs, DSs, and DifDSs for each seed region. Thus, in total, nine SDHs, nine DSs, and six DifDSs were obtained for each participant.

Itch‐ and Pain‐Selective Weight Maps

We made 3 different itch‐selective maps as we had 3 seed brain regions such as the PCC, raIC and laIC: Itch‐selective weight maps for PCC, raIC and laIC. For example, to make an itch‐selective weight map for PCC, we conducted a bootstrapped SVM approach. First, 33 participants were randomly resampled with replacements across 5000 iterations. In each iteration, we applied SVM to R‐PCC images in the itch and control conditions of selected 33 participants to obtain weights. Voxels whose weights were stable across 5000 iterations (p < 0.05, bootstrap test) were retained, constituting the itch‐voxel set. The same bootstrapping procedure was applied to the pain versus control comparison to define the pain‐voxel set. After that, voxels present only in the itch set were defined as itch‐selective, and voxels present only in the pain set as pain‐selective. At each fold, we computed DSs for test participants in the control, itch, and pain conditions by multiplying the weights used to classify itch versus control within itch‐selective voxels (i.e., itch‐selective weight maps for the PCC obtained from training set) by the corresponding voxel‐wise r‐values and then summing across voxels. In the same way, we also computed DSs for test participants in the control, itch, and pain conditions using a pain‐selective weigh map for PCC. The same procedure was repeated for R‐raIC and R‐laIC. Thus, at each fold, we obtained itch‐ and pain‐selective weight maps for raIC, and laIC as well as DSs using these maps. Figure 2 show itch‐ and pain‐selective weight maps for PCC, raIC, and laIC. These are neural signatures to detect and distinguish itch and pain in the present study.

Itch and Pain Sensations

No statistically significant difference was found in AUC (Itch: 927 ± 530, Pain: 856 ± 480, p = 0.46), indicating that the overall magnitudes of itch and pain stimuli on study participants were similar between the itch and pain conditions.

Performances of Classifications

We compared classification performance using AUC‐ROC, specificity/sensitivity to itch, and discrimination accuracy (itch vs. control, itch vs. pain). Tables 1 and 2 show that DSs yielded an AUC‐ROC of 1 for all seed regions, whereas SDHs produced lower AUC‐ROCs (0.71–0.85). Combining SDHs across seeds (PCC + raIC, PCC + laIC, raIC+laIC, PCC + raIC+laIC) slightly improved AUC‐ROC but did not exceed 0.9. Specificity, sensitivity, and accuracy for DSs in itch versus control classification were very high (97–100) across all seed regions (Table 2). By contrast, SDHs showed lower specificity, sensitivity and accuracy (61–82). Overall, DSs outperformed SDHs in itch versus control classification.

Discussions

In the present study, we identified neural signatures that detect and distinguish itch and pain with high accuracy (> 90%) using fMRI combined with cross‐validated and bootstrapped SVMs.

Classification Performance of Weight Maps (Neural Signatures)

Among the three types of predictors, DSs and DifDSs consistently outperformed SDHs across all classifications. We consider this improvement to arise from two key factors. First, voxels with stable contributions across resampling were selected, making DSs more robust to sample variability. Second, voxel selection was refined to retain only itch‐ or pain‐specific weights. Because both itch and pain share features when compared with control, such as general somatosensory processing and increased saliency due to stimulation, voxels reflecting these shared components were minimized. Thus, voxels that contributed to both itch‐versus‐control and pain‐versus‐control discrimination were excluded, yielding itch‐selective and pain‐selective weight maps. As expected, DSs derived from the itch‐selective weight maps (DS‐itch) were greater than DSs from the pain‐selective weight maps (DS‐pain) for itch‐related R‐images, with the opposite pattern for pain‐related R‐images. Consistent with this logic, most participants showed positive DifDS values for itch and negative values for pain (Figure 4B).

Differences of Weight Maps Between Itch and Pain

Itch is a unique sensation provoking the urge to scratch, which is clearly distinct from pain. Despite this, itch and pain share common brain regions (Apkarian et al. 2005; Mochizuki et al. 2019). This similarity has puzzled researchers about how the brain distinguishes itch and pain. To date, only a limited number of neuroimaging studies have directly compared brain activity between itch and pain, reporting that the PCC and the anterior portion of the aIC are more responsive to itch than pain (Mochizuki et al. 2007; Roberts et al. 2019). Thus, our understanding of the neural differences between itch and pain remains limited. From this perspective, the present study discusses noticeable differences that may explain how the brain distinguishes itch from pain in some brain regions. For example, the functional coupling between the PCC and striatum was only seen in an itch‐selective weight map for PCC (blue dashed circles at the top of Figure 2). No previous itch studies have reported this functional coupling. The present study used cowhage to provoke the itch sensation. The itch sensation is transmitted by 2 major ascending pathways: the histamine‐dependent and ‐independent pathways. Cowhage‐induced itch is associated with the histamine‐independent pathway. While brain activity patterns are partially different between histamine‐ and cowhage‐induced itch (Papoiu et al. 2012), both itch modalities activate the PCC and striatum (Mochizuki et al. 2019). Given this overlap, the functional coupling between the PCC and striatum obtained from the present study may also be seen when histamine‐induced itch is provoked. Thus, future studies are needed to determine whether the itch signatures identified here can reliably detect itch across different itch modalities. The specific role of the PCC‐striatum functional connectivity in itch perception is currently unknown. However, considering that the PCC is selective for itch and the striatum is a key brain region related to the urge to scratch (Mochizuki et al. 2019; Leknes et al. 2007), this network may play an important role in itch perception. If this is the case, this network may be a potential therapeutic target for itch relief.

Functional coupling between the raIC and bilateral operculum, including the posterior insular cortex and secondary somatosensory cortex, was observed only in a pain‐selective weight map for raIC (yellow dashed square in the middle of Figure 2). A previous study reported that the aIC increases functional connectivity with the operculum during pain stimuli (Peltz et al. 2011). Thus, our finding is consistent with the previous study. Interestingly, the same study reported that functional coupling between the aIC and operculum is also increased during warm and cold stimuli. The operculum receives somatosensory‐related signals from the periphery most strongly (Dum et al. 2009; Baumgärtner et al. 2010; Craig 2010), and from there, these signals are transmitted to other brain regions (Peltz et al. 2011). Consistent with this anatomical pathway, clinical studies in epileptic patients have repeatedly shown that electrical stimulation of the operculum provokes various somatic sensations, including pain (Mazzola et al. 2009; Ostrowsky et al. 2002; Stephani et al. 2011). However, none of these studies reported that these patients perceived itch sensations. The aIC‐operculum network may not be engaged in itch perception.

Decreased functional connectivity between the laIC and medial parietal cortex was selectively observed during pain stimuli in the present study (yellow dashed square in the bottom of Figure 2). A previous meta‐analysis study reported that a similar reduction of functional connectivity between these brain regions is commonly observed in chronic pain patients (Lai et al. 2025). Although a specific role of this network in pain is unknown, the decreased functional connectivity may partly represent a ‘Pain’ state regardless of acute or chronic conditions.

The Generalizability to Chronic Itch and Pain Populations

Representative brain regions processing itch‐ and pain‐related neural signals, such as the somatosensory cortex, insular cortex, striatum and fronto‐parietal cortex, are conserved between healthy individuals and chronic itch and pain patients (Apkarian et al. 2005; Tanasescu et al. 2016; Ishiuji et al. 2009). Accordingly, we anticipate that the weight maps developed in this study may have the potential to detect itch and pain in clinical populations following further training and validation across diverse conditions. Furthermore, we observed similar characteristics in brain activity patterns between the weight maps derived from the present study and chronic itch and pain patients reported in previous studies.

For example, in the pain‐selective weight map for the PCC, we observed positive weights in the bilateral parietal cortex, indicating that functional connectivity between the PCC and bilateral parietal cortex is increased while the participants perceive the pain sensation (green arrows at the top of Figure 2). Increased functional connectivity between the PCC and the bilateral parietal cortex in chronic pain patients was observed in chronic pain patients (Bolwerk et al. 2013). The pain‐selective weight map for the left laIC showed negative weights in the precuneus, indicating that functional connectivity between the laIC and precuneus is reduced during pain (yellow dashed square in the bottom of Figure 2). A similar negative association in activity between the insular cortex and precuneus was also observed in chronic pain patients (Mayr et al. 2022; Lai et al. 2025). In the pain‐selective weight maps for the raIC and laIC, we observed positive weights in the supplementary motor area (SMA), indicating increased functional connectivity between these brain regions while the participants perceive the pain sensation (purple arrows at the middle and bottom of Figure 2). A meta‐analysis reported increased connectivity between the insular cortex and SMA observed in chronic pain patients (Lai et al. 2025). In chronic itch patients, functional connectivity between the PCC and the right parietal cortex has been reported to positively correlate with itch severity (Mochizuki et al. 2021). Similarly, our itch‐selective weight map for the PCC shows increased functional connectivity with the right parietal cortex (light blue arrows at the top of Figure 2). Our weight maps are not ready for clinical applications as they have not yet undergone sufficient training or independent validation. These maps also need evaluation in chronic itch and pain patients. However, such efforts may help identify the optimal seed brain regions for generating R‐images and facilitate the development of weight maps that share more neural characteristics and patterns with clinical itch and pain. Ultimately, this work may lead to robust, reliable, and generalizable brain‐based biomarkers for itch and pain.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Although SVM is suitable for small‐sample, high‐dimensional neuroimaging data, overfitting cannot be excluded. Future studies should validate the model using larger datasets to improve generalizability. Cross‐validation enables model training and testing, but the model may still learn dataset‐specific features. Therefore, validation using independent datasets is needed to strengthen the reliability of the weight maps as biomarkers. The generalizability of these weight maps should be further evaluated across different stimulus modalities (e.g., histamine itch, cold pain), other sensory and affective states. Evaluation in chronic itch and pain patients is also needed before clinical applications. In the present study, we focused on identifying weight maps that distinguish itch and pain. Future studies should also train models to capture itch and pain intensity (e.g., mild, moderate and intense).

Although we selected the PCC and aIC as seed regions based on previous studies, itch and pain engage widespread brain areas, and many regions could potentially serve as seeds for generating weight maps to identify itch or pain. Future studies should systematically evaluate classification performance using R‐images derived from different seed regions. This may reveal additional itch‐ or pain‐specific neural signatures that could serve as biomarkers.
