Harnessing cooperative sorbate-sorbent adaptation in a flexible metal-organic framework for switchable hydrocarbon separation
Authors: Han Fang, Meagan Mulcair, Jia-Peng Han, Bo Zhang, Brian Space, Shi-Qiang Wang, Mei-Hui Yu, Ze Chang, Xian-He Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pursuit of selective adsorption for separating molecules with similar shapes and interactions remains a formidable challenge. This work harnesses a powerful, previously unrecognized mechanism in a flexible metal-organic framework (Flex-Cd-MOF-3) to achieve switchable adsorption selectivity. Specifically, Flex-Cd-MOF-3, a descendant of Flex-Cd-MOF-2 with increased linker rigidity and functionalization, exhibits both local rearrangements and reversible framework expansion or shrinkage, showing phase-dependent propylene/propane selectivity. Considering propane, mutually beneficial conformational changes in the material and sorbate co-occur that are not possible with rigid propylene, reshaping the energy landscapes. These distinct interaction mechanisms, for otherwise very similar molecules, directly lead to pore swelling and shrinking at different pressures and phase switchable selectivity. An innovative modeling approach was developed that describes the full desorption pathway, explaining the relative binding energies and the sorbate-dependent mechanism of pore shrinkage. This work establishes cooperative sorbate-sorbent adaptation as a generalizable adsorption mechanism for achieving precise separation of similar molecules through distinct intermolecular interactions.

INTRODUCTION

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) represent a versatile class of porous crystalline materials with systematically tunable functionalities (1–5). Early efforts in developing selective adsorbents shifted from maximizing surface areas to engineering pore surface to enhance specific host-guest interactions, aiming for molecular recognition within rigid frameworks (2, 5). In recent years, a promising subset of flexible MOFs has been found to exhibit preferential sorbate selectivity through stimulus-responsive structural transformations (6–9). This framework flexibility enables structure-dependent host-guest interactions, leading to loading-dependent thermodynamics and selectivity (9, 10). The complex energy landscapes of flexible MOFs enable them to amplify subtle differences in host-guest interactions, endowing them with exceptional molecular discrimination, enhanced working capacities, and intrinsic thermal management properties (11–13). Consequently, flexible MOFs have achieved notable success in difficult separations, including CO/N2, C2 and C3 hydrocarbons, and benzene derivatives (14–19). Furthermore, global structural flexibility, manifested as breathing or subnetwork displacement, can produce a cooperative transformation across the entire crystal lattice, while local sorbate-induced distortions further reshape the pore geometry. Together, these deformation modes enable loading-dependent optimization of host-guest interactions. These structural evolutions can be revealed via in situ characterization techniques (9, 15, 20) and combined with modeling to provide molecular-level insight that supports rational material design (21).

Propylene (C3H6) is a critical industrial feedstock, but its separation from propane (C3H8)—a common impurity—is energy intensive due to their molecular similarity (22). Conventional cryogenic distillation processes underscore the urgent need for efficient alternatives, e.g., adsorptive separation, to enable sustainable production (23). Currently, C3H6/C3H8 separation using MOF adsorbents is achieved through equilibrium (thermodynamic) (24, 25), nonequilibrium (kinetic and molecular sieving) (26), framework flexibility–based mechanisms (23), or combinations thereof (27). However, C3H8 and C3H6 generally exhibit highly similar shapes and intermolecular interactions in their gas-phase geometries, which are typically unaltered upon sorption. While induced-fit mechanisms in flexible MOFs have been identified (28, 29) and sorbate structural changes have been reported even in essentially rigid MOFs (30), an important yet unrecognized mechanism is reported here in which both the framework and sorbate mutually adapt. By leveraging both global and local flexibility, C3H8 and C3H6 interact very differently with a new flexible MOF, Flex-Cd-MOF-3. Specifically, C3H8 and Flex-Cd-MOF-3 undergo cooperative conformational adjustments that are not possible with rigid C3H6 (Fig. 1). This joint adaptation increases the surface contact between Flex-Cd-MOF-3 and C3H8 and lowers the adsorption energy compared to C3H6, whose stronger electrostatic interactions and comparable dispersion energy persist in its unadapted, gas phase–like geometry. Ultimately, this cooperative conformational change drives differences in sorption, selectivity, and thermodynamics of pore expansion for C3H8 versus C3H6. Thus, this represents a powerful, previously underexplored strategy for achieving inverse selectivity with similar chemicals, such as C3 hydrocarbons. These effects are likely relevant more broadly to mixtures containing molecules with similar sizes or shapes but different conformational flexibility, and may already be occurring unnoticed, as they are not readily discernible from conventional thermodynamic measurements.

Specifically, building upon our previous discovery of supramolecular bilayers as ideal building blocks for flexible MOFs (31), we developed an isoreticular MOF, denoted as Flex-Cd-MOF-3, by judiciously replacing linear linkers in the parent Flex-Cd-MOF-2 with naphthalene-integrated pillars (32). This design strategy stems from the identified role of aromatic groups and their interactions with guest molecules as a key feature during the global structural transitions observed in Flex-Cd-MOF-2. The incorporation of extra benzene rings into the linker backbone enhances pillar rigidity while introducing new guest-binding sites. These modifications modulate the interplay between the framework deformation energy and sorbate-sorbent interactions, thereby enabling sorbate-mediated stabilization of distinct metastable states. As a result, multiple forms of flexibility are present in Flex-Cd-MOF-3 with small-amplitude local deformations and a threshold-triggered structural expansion and shrinkage occurring via sorbate loading and unloading. Notably, the nature of the adsorbed molecules produces distinct transformations. Crucially, reversed adsorbate selectivity is observed between the shrunken and swollen phases due to distinct guest accommodation, leading to opposite selectivities for C3H6 and C3H8 in each phase (Fig. 1). Using an innovative molecular modeling approach, the energy landscape of sorbate-dependent desorption is mapped over the entire unloading process from saturation to the evacuated material, providing an explanation for the relative binding energies and the sorbate-dependent mechanism of global pore shrinkage. The energy landscapes for C3H8 and C3H6 are shown to be substantially different, with C3H8 exhibiting an intrinsically abrupt expansion due to sorbate nucleation in the pore and C3H6 supporting a series of metastable states. Crucially, Flex-Cd-MOF-3 demonstrates a synergistic combination of local and global pore space adaptation, concurrently inducing sorbate conformational changes to produce preferential sorbate selectivity. While induced-fit–type mechanisms in flexible MOFs have recently shown promise for challenging separations (28), the mechanism identified here offers a previously unidentified approach to achieve superior separations of similar molecules in industrial processes. It is not uncommon for molecules with similar shapes to exhibit quite different intramolecular motions; for example, the presence of a single C═C bond in C3H6 leads to extremely different sorption and selectivity compared to C3H8. This work demonstrates that pairing rational synthetic strategies with detailed molecular modeling enables the design of materials with complex dynamics for energy-intensive separations.

RESULTS

Synthesis and structural determination

Transparent block single crystals of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) (sw = swollen phase) were yielded via a solvothermal reaction of Cd(NO3)2·4H2O, 1,3,5-tri(pyridin-4-yl)benzene (TPB) and 6-(4-carboxyphenyl)-2-naphthoic acid (H2L) in N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF). Single-crystal x-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis reveals that Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/c and features the desired pillared-bilayer network with the general formula [Cd2(L)(TPB)2(NO3)2]·x(solvent) (table S1). The structure comprises crystallographically independent Cd(II) cations in a distorted pentagonal bipyramid geometry, connected by neutral TPB linkers and terminal nitrate groups to form supramolecular bilayers in the bc plane. These bilayers were generated in situ and expanded into a three-dimensional supramolecular framework supported by pillar-supporting L2− linker. Despite increased steric hindrance from the L2− linker, PLATON calculations indicate a large solvent accessible volume of 38.3% for Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw), slightly lower than that for the previously reported analog, Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw) (42.0%) (33, 34).

Single crystals of the guest-free shrunken variant Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (shk = shrunken phase) were harvested after removing the DMF guest molecules and were found to be stable in this guest-free state (figs. S1 and S2). SCXRD revealed shrinking of the crystal along the pillar direction while maintaining the monoclinic space group P21/c. The dynamic flexibility of the framework originates from the “kneecap” connection forged between the Cd(II) cations and the carboxylic function of the bitopic linkers (Fig. 2). Compared to Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw), the lattice parameter a is reduced from 19.35 to 14.28 Å, resulting in a 22.5% decrease of the unit-cell volume from the initial swollen pore phase (Fig. 2C). Consequently, the bilayer-to-bilayer distance shrinks from 13.5 to 9.3 Å, and the node-to-node distance between the bilayers is reduced from 17.6 to 16.9 Å, with the linker intersection angle decreased from 53.0° to 33.1°. These structural transformations were produced by introducing the reduced swing of the pillar L2− linkers in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw), which are more rigid than those in the related Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw). This rigid pillar-mediated flexibility is explored, demonstrating the preservation of framework connectivity and the underlying network topology across structural transformations (29). The largest channel diameters (LCD) of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) and Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) were measured by the Zeo++ code as 6.26 and 5.14 Å, respectively (35). The swollen phase exhibited a decreased LCD compared to Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw) (6.40 Å), while conversely, the shrunken phase displayed an increased LCD in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (5.14 Å versus 4.59 Å). Linker rigidification reduces free volume in the swollen phase, and relative pore space changes from swollen to shrunken phase. This makes Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) a promising material for investigating the impact of subtle alterations in pore space on adsorption selectivity.

Characterization of the structural transformation

Powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns reveal the high purity of the targeted Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) (fig. S3). The reversibility of the framework shrinking and swelling driven by desolvation and resolvation was verified (fig. S4). Similar to Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw), the general shift to larger 2θ values, e.g., the main (100) reflection at 4.6° disappears while a new one appears at 6.2°, is suggestive of a reduction in bilayer-to-bilayer distance and consequently a substantial contraction of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) upon guest removal. The patterns of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) feature more minor shifts compared to Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw), suggesting a notable reduced degree of contraction. After immersing as-synthesized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) in more volatile solvents such as acetone, dichloromethane (CH2Cl2), acetonitrile (CH3CN), and methanol (CH3OH) and then dried in air, several peaks shift toward higher 2θ angles accompanied by pore shrinkage due to the evacuation of guest solvents (figs. S4 and S5). It was also found that these structural transformations are reversible upon immersion of the shrunken species in the same solvents. Meanwhile, in situ temperature-dependent diffraction patterns of freshly prepared Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) are shown in fig. S6. Pattern with a mixture of swollen and shrunken species is observed after heating to 70°C, likely due to incomplete desolvation. All samples readily transform to the shrunken form above 110°C, with crystallinity retained up to 270°C, indicating useful high-temperature stability of the materials. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) revealed a two-stage weight loss, accompanied by two endothermic events in the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve (figs. S7 and S8). No obvious structural collapse was otherwise observed. The first endothermic event (below 110°C in the DSC curve) was ascribed to the removal of solvent molecules and the shrinkage of the framework, whereas the second one corresponded only to the removal of solvent molecules in the shrunken phase, as attested by the temperature-dependent PXRD patterns. No thermal effects were observed in the cooling DSC curve, indicating that the phase transition is strongly related to the presence of guest molecules.

To elucidate the impact of linker rigidification on nanoscopic mechanical properties, we conducted nanoindentation measurements at varying loadings on Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) (figs. S9 and S10). Notably, the (00-1) crystal facet of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) exhibits less displacement during indentation and demonstrates more substantial elastic recovery upon unloading compared to Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw). Using dynamic mode load-displacement data at 4 mN, the Young’s moduli (E) for both frameworks were calculated, i.e., E = 4.17 and 5.05 GPa for Flex-Cd-MOF-2(sw) and Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw), respectively. Although both MOFs are relatively compliant and soft, the sterically rigid pillared linker in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) restricts its dynamic deformation (Fig. 2A), thereby imparting greater stiffness to the framework, as evidenced by its higher modulus value. The combination of high thermal stability and useful hardness is an important practical merit of the material.

Gas adsorption–dependent structure transition

The pore characteristics of desolvated Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) were evaluated by N2 sorption at 77 K (Fig. 3A). A substantial amount of N2 {~250 cm3 [standard temperature and pressure (STP)] g−1} was adsorbed, and a typical pore-expanding process occurred at ~11 mmHg (0.015 P/P0), showing a type F-II isotherm (36, 37). The observed uptakes below a P/P0 value of 0.015 can be attributed to gas adsorption in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk), which exhibits accessible porosity and well-defined channel structures. This adsorption behavior reflects the expansion of the pore aperture within the framework during adsorption, a characteristic commonly associated with “flexible-robust” structures (38–40). With respect to the N2 adsorption isotherm, experimental pore volumes were determined to be 0.10 cm3 g−1 at P/P0 = 0.015—the critical pressure preceding the second sudden increase of gas uptake—and 0.39 cm3 g−1 at P/P0 = 0.95. These pore volumes closely approximate the theoretical accessible volume (probe radius = 1.82 Å) of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (0.12 cm3 g−1) and Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) (0.35 cm3 g−1), respectively, indicating the subtle structural dynamics in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) triggered by N2 adsorption, leading to a marked structural transition from the shrunken pore to swollen pore.

The microporous triangle channels with aromatic side chains in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) are reminiscent of aromatic-based MOFs known for their excellent hydrocarbon separation performance, e.g., ZJU-120/121 and PCP-IPA (41, 42). Hence, hydrocarbon isotherms were collected for Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) at different temperatures and are shown in Fig. 3 and figs. S11 to S19. Type I isotherms were observed for C2 hydrocarbons at pressures below 1 bar and the measured temperatures, suggesting retention of the shrunken phase during adsorption without notable structural changes to the pore. C3 hydrocarbon isotherms showed evidence of sorbent- and temperature-dependent pore expansion, which occurred at distinctly different pressures for different sorbates and temperatures. Adsorption isotherms of C3H6 and C3H8 at 273, 298, and 308 K traced step-shaped adsorption profiles, and the sigmoidal desorption isotherms mirrored the adsorption behavior, indicating reversible structural transitions. It can therefore be deduced that the stepped sorption isotherms reflect isotherms of the individual phases of the material at specific pressure ranges. In the low-pressure region, Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) adopted a shrunken-pore form and displayed a steeper uptake of C3H6 over C3H8 (fig. S18). At intermediate pressure, Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) showed gate-expanding behavior with higher C3H6 threshold pressures than C3H8, indicating a reversal in adsorption selectivity. Considering 308 K, both C3 hydrocarbon displayed type I isotherms until gate-expanding pressures of 0.39 bar for C3H8 and 0.58 bar for C3H6, and total adsorption capacities approached 72.6 cm3 g−1 for C3H6 and 74.4 cm3 g−1 for C3H8 at 1 bar, respectively (Fig. 3B). The highly guest-specific threshold pressures in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) give rise to a C3H8/C3H6 uptake ratio of up to 2.31 (0.5/0.5 bar), making it among the best-performing MOF materials for this separation, inferior only to CdIF-13 to our knowledge (tables S2 and S4) (17, 43). At lower temperatures, the reduced thermal motion of gas molecules enables stronger host-guest interactions to initiate the pore expansion, resulting in lower threshold pressures. The reduced adsorption-desorption hysteresis in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (C3H6: 17%; C3H8: 18%) relative to Flex-Cd-MOF-2(shk) (C3H6: 27%; C3H8: 37%) demonstrates enhanced structural deformation recovery ability (Fig. 3C and table S5), which aligns well with the macroscopic elastic recovery analysis obtained from nanoindentation measurements.

Step-shaped adsorption profiles elucidated by in situ gas-dosed PXRD measurements (1 bar and 308 K) for C3H6 and C3H8 show that the first adsorption stage occurred in the shrunken pore, up to 0.5 bar for C3H6 and 0.3 bar for C3H8, where PXRD patterns of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) reflect a blend of diffraction peaks of the individual shrunken and swollen phases (Fig. 3, D and E). The peaks recorded, especially the one indexed as (100), gradually shifted to slightly lower diffraction angles, supporting the expansion of the shrunken framework with gas loading. A marked change in the PXRD pattern of the shrunken phase was observed beyond the threshold pressure, almost coincident with that of the swollen phase, verifying phase swelling as the cause of the observed stepped adsorption. Typically, either a continuous or a distinct phase transition was observed in flexible MOFs (6–9, 44). This observed phenomenon—continuous accumulation of small expansions leading to an abrupt transformation of framework at a specific pressure—is unusual, although a similar occurrence can also be achieved by a partial desolvation approach in SHF-61 (45). During desorption, only the swollen phase was present until the pressures of C3H6 and C3H8 dropped to 0.6 and 0.4 bar, respectively. Stark changes were again observed in the PXRD patterns as the pressure decreased, coinciding with structural shrinkage to the shrunken phase and indicating that pore expansion and shrinkage of the material is responsible for the observed reversible structural shifts.

To further elucidate the unique pore structure–related selectivity switching phenomenon, we investigated the adsorption of C4 hydrocarbons (i.e., n-C4H10 and n-C4H8) in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk), also revealing stepped isotherms (fig. S19). In contrast to the adsorption behavior of C3 hydrocarbons, the more complex C4 adsorption profiles can be attributed to the higher conformational flexibility of C4 hydrocarbons (30), which enables more diverse, loading-dependent interactions with the flexible framework. Notably, the n-C4H10 isotherm exhibits a single gate-expansion step, whereas n-C4H8 displays a two-step adsorption behavior. At low pressures, n-C4H8 is preferentially adsorbed in the initial Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) phase and likely stabilizes a metastable intermediate phase, as evidenced by the adsorption plateau observed at 0.03 to 0.05 bar. With increasing pressure, this intermediate phase undergoes a second pore expansion. In comparison, the threshold pressure for n-C4H10–induced pore expansion (0.04 bar) is lower than the second gate-expanding pressure of n-C4H8 (0.06 bar), leading to a pressure-dependent inversion of adsorption selectivity. At higher loadings, the adsorption selectivity switches back in favor of n-C4H8, which can be attributed to its more efficient packing within the swollen pores. Both C3 and C4 hydrocarbon isotherms exhibited steeper slopes at low pressures and lower threshold pressures in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) than in Flex-Cd-MOF-2(shk) under identical conditions, as more aromatic rings on the pillared linkers provide potential interaction sites with hydrocarbons (Fig. 3C and fig. S19). A key difference is that Flex-Cd-MOF-2(shk) did not exhibit selective switching behavior, with both the shrunken and swollen phases showing n-C4H8–selective adsorption. Aromatic ring modification enhanced the paraffin-selective adsorption behavior of the swollen pore in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk), facilitating the observed selectivity switching.

Kinetic adsorption

A noticeable difference in the diffusion rates of C3H6 and C3H8 by Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) was shown in the time-dependent adsorption profiles of C3H6 and C3H8 in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (Fig. 4A), obtained by a gravimetric method (308 K and up to 1 bar) with comparable equilibrium adsorption amounts for both light hydrocarbons. A higher diffusion rate was observed for C3H6 than for C3H8 in the initial stage, whereas a higher C3H8 diffusion rate was observed as the adsorption process progressed. Equilibrium uptake was reached after ~18.3 min for C3H8 and 33.0 min for C3H6, reflecting easier diffusion of C3H6 in the initial shrunken pore owing to its smaller kinetic size relative to C3H8 (C3H6: 4.68 Å and C3H8: 5.1 Å) until the pore size expands enough to reduce diffusion resistance. Afterward, greater sorbate and sorbent interactions between the material and the bulkier C3H8 molecules relative to C3H6 may allow C3H8 to reach the adsorption equilibrium more quickly. This demonstrated that preference for adsorption under kinetic conditions is consistent with thermodynamic performance, validating the cooperativity of diffusion ability and host-guest interaction as the trigger for switchable selectivity.

Dynamic mixed-gas separation

The selectivity reversal performance of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 was anticipated under dynamic mixed-gas conditions, as adsorption separation is governed by enthalpy-dependent thermodynamic equilibrium and diffusivity-dependent kinetic mechanisms. To evaluate this, we conducted breakthrough experiments under ambient conditions (figs. S20 to S26). Highly efficient separation of C3H6/C3H8 was realized for equimolar binary mixtures with a total flow rate of 10 ml min−1 at 308 K, as presented in Fig. 4B. C3H8 was first eluted at 5.4 min through the fixed bed, while C3H6 remained trapped in the column of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) until 6.3 min. Concentrations of both gasses were not close to equilibrium immediately after breakthrough, and a higher concentration of C3H6 was detected within 7 to 11 min, reaching 81%. The initial retention times could be gradually increased by raising the carrier gas content (Fig. 4B and figs. S24 to S26). The observed point of intersection in the relative concentration indicated a selectivity reversal, after which the adsorption column competitively adsorbs C3H8. Even when the partial pressure of C3H8 is below its gate-expanding threshold, the intersection still occurs because, from a thermodynamic perspective, the gate-expanding pressure for a multicomponent mixture is lower than that of any individual component (46). Therefore, C3H8 displaced the more weakly adsorbed C3H6 in the column, as the pore structure expanded from the shrunken form, causing an effect known as roll-up, where C3H6 exceeds its inlet concentration above 1.0 (figs. S20 to S26). A non–high purity concentration of C3H6 could be obtained, possibly due to the nonsimultaneous transition from shrunken to swollen phase in the adsorption column. For comparison, during the dynamic separation of C2H6/C2H4 mixtures, Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) exhibited exclusive C2H6-selective adsorption behavior, attributed to the inability of C2 hydrocarbons to induce the pore expansion under ambient conditions and the resulting absence of switching selectivity (fig. S27). This is reflected in the breakthrough curve, where C2H4 elutes before C2H6, followed by the gradual equilibrium of their concentrations. In addition, the crossover breakthrough curves for binary C3H6/C3H8 mixtures were consistently reproducible over three continuous cycles, confirming the durability of the MOF (fig. S28). The accumulated C3H6 from the previous step can be recovered immediately by this late reversal behavior, without energy input, e.g., from additional heating, helium purging, or vacuuming (Fig. 4C). Because C3H6 is the desired product, the switchable mechanism substantially reduces the energy consumption and simplifies the traditional C3H6-selective separation process.

The observed crossover behavior should not be attributed to the leakage or slipping-off effect in the separation process because the pore-expanding flexibility from the shrunken pore to the swollen one facilitates guest adsorption at low pressure and/or concentration and prevents later leakage in the separation process (15, 47, 48). The adsorption-induced structural expansion of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 proceeds through discrete and well-defined pore phases that switch from C3H6 selective to C3H8 selective, regardless of whether the transition is induced by increasing pressure or by prolonged adsorption time at a given pressure (Figs. 3B and 4A). This selectivity switching is confirmed by both thermodynamic and kinetic single-component isotherms, ensuring that the breakthrough behavior—governed by thermodynamic and/or kinetic factors—inherits the intrinsic phase-dependent adsorption preference. After structural transformation from the shrunken pore, the swollen pore geometry of Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) is advantageous for the adsorption of C3H8, which accumulates in the swollen pore channel, thereby excluding C3H6 competitors. These observations are strongly supported by the modeling studies presented next.

Molecular modeling

Molecular modeling was used to better understand the structural transition mechanism of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 as a function of gas sorption and to explore the underlying energy landscape responsible for the transformation and separations observed experimentally. The mechanical stability of the crystallographically identified vacant swollen [Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw)] and shrunken [Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk)] pore structures were validated by periodic density functional theory (DFT), which showed only minor cell contraction of the structures after optimization, corresponding to movement of the L2− linker (table S6). To evaluate gas sorption in the rigid structures, we performed a series of grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations at 298 K across pressures ranging from 0 to 1 bar to simulate the gas sorption of CO2 and a series of C2 and C3 light hydrocarbons in the rigid, DFT-optimized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) and Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) structures. Simulations of CO2 and C2 gasses in the shrunken and swollen phases were performed, and the results for Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) closely approximated experimentally obtained isotherms, indicating that Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) is not readily accessible for these smaller sorbates, while validating the C2 and C3 models (figs. S29 to S31). Larger molecular dispersion and polarizability led to increased binding interaction with especially Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk), demonstrated via the isosteric heat of absorption (Qst) (table S7). Direct comparison of empirically modeled CO2 Qst compared to those (23.5 kJ mol−1) obtained from the measured type I isotherms (figs. S11 to S15 and table S3) further validates the modeling and shows the strong preference for Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) seen for the smaller molecules.

In addition, analysis of saturated C3H6 and C3H8 sorption by GCMC simulations showed preferential binding interactions (increased relatively by >10 kJ mol−1) of the larger C3 sorbate molecules in both the rigid shrunken and swollen phases of the material compared to the smaller sorbates (table S7). Similar binding sites, driven by dispersion-dominated contacts, were identified for saturated sorption of C3H6 and C3H8, with slightly higher occupancy of the larger C3H8 molecules evident in the material (fig. S32). Switchable selectivity was identified in the bulk between C3H6 and C3H8, with preferential modeled binding of C3H6 (Qst: 54.3 kJ mol−1) over C3H8 (Qst: 51.7 kJ mol−1) in the shrunken pore and a reversed selectivity of C3H8/C3H6 (50.3/46.0 kJ mol−1) in the swollen pore (table S7), when the material and sorbate are both modeled as rigid.

However, to understand the performance of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 in distinguishing C3H8 from C3H6, the mutual flexibility and/or adaptation of the sorbate and sorbent must be accounted for. To explicitly demonstrate this, Fig. 5 shows the favorable binding site for C3H6 at near-zero loading in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) that was identified (60.51 kJ mol−1) relative to C3H8 (56.84 kJ mol−1) by calculating isosteric heats of sorption for single molecule loading (Qst0) of C3H6 and C3H8, modeled by periodic DFT optimizations, following simulated annealing of the sorbate in the rigid, DFT-optimized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) structure. C3H6/C3H8 selectivity at near-zero loading was determined to be as a result of better site occupancy of the smaller C3H6 molecule, with stronger electrostatic interactions, in the shrunken phase, aided by the increased planar character of the sorbate molecule relative to C3H8 (Fig. 5A). In stark contrast, Fig. 5B shows that comparable sorbate energies were observed for C3H6 (62.24 kJ mol−1) and C3H8 (62.70 kJ mol−1) upon full optimization of the binding site, allowing the material to move in response to sorbate inclusion. This full optimization resulted in considerable structural changes, with C3H8 altering its structure in a mutual adaptation with the MOF, leading to much stronger C3H8 binding than in the rigid structure. By allowing mutual sorbate and sorbent conformational change, the C3H8 binding energy drops by ~5.9 kJ mol−1. In contrast, C3H6 has only a small energy drop of ~1.7 kJ mol−1 and no notable change in either sorbate or sorbent structure. This mechanism to distinguish otherwise similar molecules using flexible MOFs has neither been appreciated nor widely explored. This is likely because there is no simple way to confirm this phenomenon, even if it is present in extant experiments; detailed modeling is necessary.

Thus, considering C3H8 sorption, the molecule and material mutually adapt to facilitate optimal dispersion contacts with the bulkier, more flexible sorbate, consistent with the observed transformation behavior; this propensity for pore expansion at even low C3H8 loading agrees with the lower C3H8 threshold pressure identified in experimental isotherms (Fig. 3B). Further details on simulated sorbate binding sites, structural changes, and energetics are available in fig. S33 and tables S8 to S11.

To further explore the structural features responsible for the phase transition of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 as a function of C3H6 and C3H8 gasses, respectively, we performed first GCMC simulations in the rigid swollen and shrunken phases of Flex-Cd-MOF-3. Experimentally observed gas sorption behavior (Fig. 3B) was captured by simulated isotherms at near-zero gas loading and saturation (fig. S31) for both gasses. However, as the material is held rigid in GCMC simulations, additional modeling is required to fully describe the intermediate states observed in the experimental isotherms, given the inherent flexibility of the sorbates and sorbents. Hence, a simulated desorption profile was constructed for both C3H6 and C3H8 in the material using an innovative computational method (21, 31) that combines periodic DFT optimizations and GCMC simulations to understand the mechanism and underlying energy landscape of the structural transition in response to C3 hydrocarbons. This approach allows for detailed validation and molecular resolution of the complex local and global phase transformations, which are both sorbate loading and identity dependent. This earlier-developed iterative approach, which combines empirical GCMC and periodic DFT methods, has several advantages when successfully applied (21). First, it confirms that both the empirical force-field model and the periodic DFT energy surface are compatible, as required to obtain meaningful results. This is also consistent with the observation of similar Qst0 values across the two methods. The substantial advantage is the ability to sample the configuration space of intermediate adsorbent structures using GCMC, while periodic DFT calculations handle the detailed structural changes. In principle, this could all be done using periodic DFT, but the computational cost would be prohibitive. This hybrid method provides a tractable alternative for mapping the complex energetic landscape without bias in selecting which guests to add or delete.

Furthermore, as shown in movies S1 and S3 and Fig. 6, the conformationally adapted binding site for C3H8 serves as a nucleation site for subsequent C3H8 sorption. This is in contrast to C3H6, where the second molecular binding site is not in contact with the first, as shown in Fig. 6 and movie S2. This leads to the observed phenomenology of abrupt shrinkage for C3H8 and a more gradual, stepwise energy landscape for C3H6 desorption, with stabilized intermediate states. To build this profile, the vacant swollen (Flex-Cd-MOF-3) structure, as elucidated by SCXRD (table S1), was optimized by periodic DFT and saturated with C3H6 and C3H8, respectively, using GCMC empirical simulations. The resultant, saturated system was then reoptimized by DFT. To simulate desorption, we then removed the most weakly bound sorbate molecule, and the structure was again reoptimized by DFT. This process was repeated until all gas molecules were removed, and an empty structure was optimized for each gas profile (Fig. 6). The underlying energy landscape shows a near single-step, abrupt cell contraction in the C3H8 desorption profile around two sorbate molecules per unit cell, consistent with a clear transition from swollen to shrunken pore structure in Fig. 3B. In contrast, a distinct, multistep phase transition with distinct intermediate states is observed in the C3H6 profile. This is supported by the desorption profile in Fig. 3B, where a desorption intermediate is visible even at 308 K at a loading of five C3H6. While Fig. 6 represents the underlying energy landscape, i.e., optimization represents very low temperatures, the thermal isotherms in Fig. 3 still echo the underlying structural mechanism identified in Fig. 6.

This difference in sorption behavior by the C3 gasses is also evident in experimental isotherms and PXRD patterns (Fig. 3). At low loading, a larger pore volume was observed in the simulated C3H6 sorption profile than was seen for C3H8, aligning with the C3H6/C3H8 selectivity observed experimentally and computationally determined binding energetics detailed above. With increased loading of C3H8, a marked increase in pore volume was observed, corresponding to a lower threshold pressure of C3H8 for distinct pore swelling relative to C3H6, where notable changes to the pore volume occur at higher loading. Similarly, this result is validated by experimental and analysis of binding-site energetics. The higher saturation uptake of C3H6 relative to C3H8 before pore expansion leads to a switch in selectivity by the material, which is reversed with increased gas uptake, making it C3H8 selective. Animations of the simulated flexibility mechanism and details of cell-structure changes as a function of sorption can be found in movies S1 to S3, which demonstrate the abrupt structural change for C3H8 compared with C3H6. This demonstrates the critical role the guest-host interactions play in materials transformations—the same material can show distinct behaviors even for ostensibly similar molecules. Furthermore, the detailed mechanism can be fully captured through careful simulation methods that efficiently combine empirical GCMC with DFT optimization.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that the structural flexibility of both sorbate and sorbent can be harnessed for switchable hydrocarbon separation via an unprecedented, powerful mechanism: cooperative sorbate-sorbent adaptation. Flex-Cd-MOF-3, a descendant of Flex-Cd-MOF-2, was modified to increase linker rigidity and functionalization, showing both sorbate-dependent local rearrangements and a global pore-phase transition with reversible shrinking or swelling. Rigid pillar linkers in Flex-Cd-MOF-3 constrain the flexibility of the shrunken phase. This constrained pore environment favors the occupancy of the C3H6 molecule, as its smaller kinetic and more planar geometry enhances electrostatic host-guest interactions. Upon further pore expansion and exposure of aromatic pore surfaces, the conformational adaptability of C3H8 leads to its preferential stabilization in the swollen phase, ultimately resulting in a switch in adsorption selectivity. Regarding C3H8, mutually beneficial conformational changes occur between the material and sorbate that are not possible with rigid C3H6, reshaping the energy landscape. Here, C3H8 has a substantially stabilized Qst0 due to the complementary rearrangement of the MOF and C3H8 molecules—typically, C3H6 has stronger intermolecular interactions. The nature of this mechanism was revealed by coordinated experimental and theoretical investigations. Modeling reveals distinct energy landscapes: abrupt changes for C3H8 and a more stepwise process for C3H6, consistent with the experimental desorption profile. In situ x-ray studies, coupled with a modeling approach, were both designed to map the structure/energy landscape of sorption and to validate the molecular modeling. The observed mutual confirmational changes for C3H8 lead to distinct loading and sorption sites for C3H6/C3H8, ultimately resulting in inverted selectivity, as demonstrated in the modeling animations. The entire desorption landscape was mapped for both sorbates, providing molecular-level resolution for the complex isotherms. These insights are consistent with breakthrough data showing the practical separation capabilities of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 for C3H6/C3H8. These studies demonstrate the power of rational insights derived from combining experimental and validated molecular modeling. Here, these joint efforts identified a powerful handle for separating similar molecules using flexible MOFs: cooperative sorbate and sorbent adaptation, leading to distinct intermolecular interactions and energetics that ultimately enable separation of similar molecules.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of materials

Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw): Cd(NO3)2·4H2O (0.10 mmol and 30 mg), H2L (0.05 mmol and 14.6 mg), TPB (0.05 mmol and 15 mg), and DMF (2 ml) were combined in a 20-ml scintillation vial. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min and then heated in an oven at 92°C for 48 hours. After washing with DMF several times and collecting colorless block-shaped crystals, the yield was about 52% (based on TPB).

Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk): Guest-free Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) was obtained by exchanging the lattice DMF molecules in Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) with acetone and activating in vacuo at 80°C. There was no apparent change in the crystals’ color. Colorless single crystals suitable for x-ray diffraction were obtained.

Characterization of materials

The crystal morphological images were taken by a Nikon LV100ND microscope. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were collected on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Phenom XL G2 desktop SEM. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurements were performed on a TENSOR 37 FT-IR spectrometer. TGA was conducted under a continuous argon flow and recorded with a Rigaku Thermo Plus EVO2 8121 analyzer, using a heating rate of 10°C/min and an empty Al2O3 ceramic as a reference.

The DSC measurement was conducted in a nitrogen atmosphere using a TA Instruments DSC-25 within the temperature range of 10° to 180°C at a heating rate of 5°C/min. The instrument was calibrated with standard reference materials.

The nanoindentation experiments were conducted at room temperature using a three-sided pyramidal cube-corner nanoindenter (Triboindenter, Hysitron, TI Premier, USA; tip radius = 50 nm). Before the mechanical tests, face indexing was performed on the as-synthesized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) crystals with a Rigaku XtaLAB diffractometer, indicating that the (00-1) natural facet is the most exposed and suitable for nanoindentation. The carefully selected single crystal was cold mounted in epoxy resin and polished thoroughly before testing. The loading and unloading rates ranged from 2 to 9 mN/s, with a 10-s hold at peak load. Several indents were made on the natural faces to determine Young’s modulus (E). The average E values were derived from the P-h curves using the standard Oliver-Pharr method.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD patterns were collected using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer over the range of 3° to 50° in an air atmosphere. In situ variable-temperature PXRD data were collected using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer over the range of 3° to 50°. The Anton Paar BTS 500 stage was used for temperature regulation. The PXRD patterns were obtained under an Ar atmosphere at various temperatures.

Variable-pressure PXRD patterns were recorded on a Rigaku SmartLab instrument operating at 40 kV and 40 mA, equipped with an Anton Paar TTK 600 accessory. The experimental setup included a combined control unit for temperature regulation, with the chamber connected to a turbo vacuum pump, gas injection lines, and pressure detectors to create a controlled environment for the samples. Before testing, the Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) samples were activated under vacuum. For the experiments, Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) samples were loaded into the chamber, ensuring a flat sample surface. An air refrigerator cryo-system, coupled with the temperature control unit in the Anton Paar TTK 600 stage, was connected to the sample chamber to regulate the temperature range. The sealed samples were vacuumed for 3 hours, and then PXRD data were collected at 0 bar. Subsequently, the samples were loaded with the corresponding hydrocarbon gas at a flow rate of 20 ml min−1, and pressure was monitored using pressure detectors. PXRD patterns were recorded after equilibration (1% of pressure changes within 10 min) at selected pressure points. In situ PXRD patterns were measured at each adsorption and desorption equilibrium point under controlled temperature and pressure conditions.

SCXRD studies

The single-crystal diffraction data for as-synthesized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) and guest-free Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) were collected at 298 and 100 K, respectively, using a Rigaku XtaLAB PRO MM007 DW diffractometer equipped with a hybrid pixel array charge-coupled device detector. The x-ray source is Cu Kα (λ = 1.5418 Å). The raw diffraction data were collected and reduced with the program “CrysAlisPro 1.171.40.84a (Rigaku OD, 2020),” and treated with multiscan absorption correction. The crystal structures were solved by direct methods and refined on F2 through full-matrix least-squares using the SHELXT package included in Olex2. Non-hydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement parameters during the final cycles. Hydrogen atoms were placed at geometrically calculated positions relative to their carrier atoms and refined with isotropic thermal parameters, which were included in the final stage of refinement. The contributions of heavily disordered solvent molecules were modeled as diffuse using the SQUEEZE procedure in the PLATON program.

Adsorption measurement

Low-pressure gas sorption isotherms were conducted using a Micromeritics 3Flex surface area and pore size analyzer at pressures up to 1 bar. Before gas sorption measurements, the as-synthesized Flex-Cd-MOF-3(sw) samples were soaked in acetone for 3 days, with fresh acetone changed three times daily. Vacuuming such a solvent-exchanged sample at 413 K for an additional 12 hours produced a fully activated Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) sample. The cryogenic temperature was maintained at 77 K using liquid nitrogen. The bath temperature for hydrocarbon sorption measurements was regulated using a recirculating bath containing an ethylene glycol/water mixture.

Adsorption kinetics

Kinetic adsorption profiles of C3H8 and C3H6 were performed using a Beishide Instrument Technology DVS analyzer at 308 K and 1 bar. It accurately measures the adsorption capacity of the adsorbate on the adsorbent using a gravimetric method and provides corresponding time-dependent gas-uptake profiles. About 100 mg of samples was initially loaded into the chamber and in situ activated at the specified temperature under high vacuum for 12 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the chamber was backfilled with N2 until the pressure reached 1 bar. Once the analysis began, C3H6 or C3H8 was introduced into the chamber at a total flow rate of 40 ml min−1. The mass of the sample with gas molecules was continuously recorded until it reached a stable value.

Breakthrough experiments

The breakthrough experiments were conducted in a homemade gas separation test system. The activated Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) (1.044 g) was packed into a column (6 mm of external diameter, 4 mm of internal diameter, and 15 cm long) to form a fixed bed. The gas flow rate was controlled using a mass flow controller (Alicat). Before the breakthrough measurements, the Flex-Cd-MOF-3(shk) sample was activated at 413 K under high vacuum for 12 hours. Then, the column was heated for 3 hours at 393 K, while helium (He) (purity ≥ 99.999%) was continuously purged at a flow rate of 5 ml (STP) min−1. After the separation column naturally cooled to 308 K, equimolar gas mixtures (C3H6/C3H8) at varying partial pressures were passed through the column at a flow rate of 10 ml min−1. The outlet composition was constantly monitored by a mass spectrometer (Hiden, HPR-20). Before starting the next experiment cycle, the fixed bed was regenerated for 2 hours at 393 K.

Molecular modeling

All optimizations of Flex-Cd-MOF-3 were simulated by periodic DFT at the TZVP/PBE-D3 (49, 50) level of theory with an energy cutoff of 900 rydberg to an energy convergence of 1.0 × 10–6 Hartree (4.36 × 10–24 J), using the CP2K open-source software (51). Atomic point partial charges were applied using the REPEAT method (52) for all atoms. Classical gas sorption simulations were performed on DFT-optimized unit cells in the GCMC ensemble with the Massively Parallel Monte Carlo (MPMC) software (53). C3 gasses were described by the TraPPE model (54). All models are available on the Space group MPMC GitHub site. All calculations were performed on High Performance Computing (HPC) clusters made available by NC State.
