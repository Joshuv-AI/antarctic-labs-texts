Effects of organic amendments and crop cultivation on soil organic carbon storage, phosphorus components, and microbial respiration in stratified soils
Authors: Xue Li, Joan Romanyà, Qi Fu, Xiumei Zhan, Shiyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic fertilization and planting interactively regulate soil carbon (C) storage, phosphorus (P) components, and microbial respiration, providing insights for optimizing fertilization regimes in farmland. This study employed a pot experiment with organic fertilization and crop planting as two factors, each at two levels, was conducted to comparatively analyze the changes in soil organic C, P fractions (total organic P/total inorganic P, Po/Pi), and microbial respiration (SIR, substrate-induced respiration) under different treatments (organic fertilizer application, planting crops or not, and their combinations). The results showed that long-term absence of organic inputs significantly reduced soluble C by 33–56% and enhanced organic P mineralization (indicated by a 27% decrease in Po/Pi ratio). Organic fertilizer application increased soluble C by 18–22% in the topsoil (0–5 cm), and this increase was greater than that observed in the absence of crops, stimulated microbial respiration (+ 30%), and led to a 3.6-fold accumulation of labile organic P. However, it exacerbated P availability stratification (40% decline in Po/Pi). Crop cultivation increased microbial biomass (+ 15–20%) and improved subsoil P speciation (50% increase in Po/Pi). Combining deep-rooted crop cultivation with surface organic fertilizer application synergistically enhances soil C sequestration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53127-w.

Introduction

Modern agricultural management practices, such as tillage methods and fertilization strategies, significantly influence the accumulation and decomposition of soil organic C, thereby regulating soil CO₂ emissions1. Among these, no planting and organic fertilizer application, as key sustainable agricultural practices, demonstrate notable advantages in improving soil structure, enhancing C sequestration, and reducing erosion2. However, the mechanisms underlying their effects on the dynamic variations in CO₂ respiration across different soil layers, require further investigation.

No planting maintains soil aggregate stability by minimizing mechanical disturbance, thereby reducing the mineralization rate of organic C and promoting soluble accumulation in surface layers2, 3. Studies indicate that long-term no planting increases microbial biomass and activity in topsoil but may restrict C translocation to deeper layers4. Additionally, the combined application of no planting and organic fertilizers (e.g., organic fertilizer or manure) can further enhance surface P bioavailability but may increase the risk of vertical P leaching5. Microbial metabolism plays a critical role in regulating P transformations (e.g., organic P mineralization, inorganic P fixation), thereby influencing plant P use efficiency6. Therefore, investigating the effects of no planting and organic fertilizer on soil C-P coupled cycling is essential for optimizing agricultural management strategies.

Organic fertilizer, as an organic amendment, not only supplies substantial degradable organic matter but also introduces active microbial communities, significantly influencing soil C cycling processes7. Organic fertilizer application can enhance microbial respiration, accelerate soluble decomposition, and increase CO₂ emissions, yet long-term application may promote carbon stabilization8. Moreover, the depth and method of organic fertilizer application differentially affect CO₂ respiration across soil layers. For instance, surface application may stimulate microbial metabolism in the 0–5 cm layer, whereas deep application may enhance carbon mineralization in subsoil (> 15 cm)9. However, the regulatory mechanisms of no planting combined with organic fertilizer on CO₂ respiration across different soil layers remain unclear, particularly regarding response variations under different climatic and soil conditions10.

This study compares CO₂ respiration rates in different soil layers (0–5 cm, 5–15 cm) under no planting and conventional tillage combined with organic fertilizer or inorganic fertilizer treatments. We hypothesize that: (1) no planting and organic fertilizer interactively affect soil CO₂ emissions; (2) microbial respiration contributes differentially to C cycling across soil layers; and (3) planting and fertilization alter soil organic C-P coupling via microbial regulation, with distinct driving mechanisms between surface and subsurface layers.

P cycling in agricultural soils is fundamentally distinct from C or nitrogen cycling, as P lacks a gaseous phase and its bioavailability is governed by sorption, mineral weathering, and enzymatic hydrolysis—processes that operate on different spatial and temporal scales. While C and nitrogen dynamics under organic amendments are well documented, the vertical redistribution and transformation of organic P remain poorly quantified, particularly in response to the combined effects of organic fertilization and crop presence. Nitrogen cycling is therefore intentionally excluded from this study to avoid confounding factors and to maintain a mechanistic focus on P stabilization and mineralization—a critical but often oversimplified component of soil nutrient cycling.

Based on this rationale, we propose three layer-specific, process-based hypotheses: (1) Organic fertilization increases the Po/Pi ratio in the 0–5 cm layer due to surface accumulation of organic P, but not in the 5–15 cm layer due to limited vertical P mobility; (2) Crop presence decreases the Po/Pi ratio in the 0–5 cm layer via enhanced phosphatase activity and shifts in P-solubilizing microbial communities; (3) Organic fertilization and crop presence synergistically reduce the Po/Pi ratio in the surface layer, reflecting coupled substrate supply and biological demand. By testing these hypotheses, this study moves beyond descriptive comparisons of P fractions and provides mechanistic insights into how management practices alter P cycling processes in a depth-dependent manner an advance over previous studies that have largely treated the plow layer as homogeneous or examined single-factor effects alone.

Methods

Preparation of soil for the pot experiment

The experimental soil was collected from an organically managed farmland in Mollet del Vallès, Catalonia, Spain (41°33′44.2″N, 2°11′43.3″E). The soil type was identified as a Luvisol, with samples taken from the 0–20 cm topsoil layer. The basic physicochemical properties of the soil were as follows: loam texture (42.3% sand, 35.6% silt, and 22.1% clay), negligible gravel content (> 2 mm particles < 1%), pH 8.01 (soil: water ratio 2.5:1), 4.0% calcium carbonate (measured by volumetric calcimeter), 0.87% organic C (determined by potassium dichromate oxidation), and 0.09% total nitrogen (measured by the Kjeldahl method). After collection, the soil was air-dried, passed through a 2-mm sieve, and stored in a light-proof environment.

Organic fertilizer used in the experiment

The experiment utilized mature organic fertilizer produced on-farm, which was derived from a mixture of horse manure and crop residues (wheat straw: corn straw = 3:1). The organic fertilizer Ing process involved six months of aerobic decomposition (with weekly turning). The basic properties of the organic fertilizer were as follows: organic matter content 19.0% (determined by dry weight method), total C content 9.5% (measured using an elemental analyzer), total nitrogen content 0.96% (Kjeldahl method), total P content 0.71% (molybdenum-antimony anti-colorimetric method), and a C-to-nitrogen ratio (C/N) of 9.9. Prior to use, the organic fertilizer was sieved through a 5 mm mesh and stored in a cool, shaded area.

Experimental design

The experiment employed a pot-based simulation method. The test soil was sieved through a 1 mm mesh, thoroughly homogenized, and then mixed with perlite (Premium Gramoflor, Germany, particle size 2–6 mm) at a 2:1 volume ratio to improve substrate aeration and drainage. The mixing process was completed using a professional substrate mixer to ensure uniformity. The prepared mixed substrate was loaded into sixteen 10 L plastic pots, while a portion of the initial soil samples was air-dried and retained for baseline measurements. The basin is a ceramic basin with a depth of over 30 cm.

A total of 4 treatments were established, including 8 pots with 1 L of organic fertilizer applied to the soil surface (organic fertilizer group) and 4 pots without organic fertilizer (control group), with 4 replicates per treatment. The organic fertilizer application rate followed the research standard of Kelly et al.11,, based on the nutrient requirements of a two-year crop rotation and the need for soil stratification studies. All treatments were maintained at near field capacity using an automated drip irrigation system. Treatments: Control group—no organic fertilizer and no crops, with spontaneous seedlings periodically removed to control plant residue input; Organic fertilizer without crops group—organic fertilizer applied but no crops planted, managed similarly to the control group; Organic fertilizer with crops group—organic fertilizer applied with crop rotation following the sequence: beet-tomato-tomato-cabbage-fallow-tomato. It presents the P accumulation data in potted crops (tomato, beet, and cabbage) and fallow soil over two years. In spring 2019, the aboveground P accumulation in beets was 14.44 ± 1.09 mg/pot, while root P accumulation was 8.83 ± 0.37 mg/pot, resulting in a total P accumulation of 23.27 ± 1.14 mg/pot. In summer 2019, tomatoes exhibited significantly higher aboveground P accumulation (308.80 ± 22.19 mg/pot), with fruit P accumulation at 15.03 ± 1.18 mg/pot, leading to a total accumulation of 323.83 ± 23.37 mg/pot. By autumn 2019, tomato aboveground P accumulation decreased to 140.39 ± 14.38 mg/pot, with fruit P accumulation at 2.62 ± 1.14 mg/pot, totaling 143.01 ± 15.52 mg/pot. In winter 2020, cabbage had an aboveground P accumulation of 7.51 ± 0.72 mg/pot, while no P accumulation was recorded for the fallow soil (spring-summer 2020). In winter 2021, tomato aboveground P accumulation was 91.28 ± 7.69 mg/pot, with fruit P accumulation at 18.75 ± 2.44 mg/pot, summing to 110.03 ± 5.07 mg/pot. Across all crops, the total aboveground P accumulation was 562.42 ± 9.21 mg/pot, while the combined P accumulation in beet roots/tomato fruits was 45.23 ± 1.03 mg/pot, yielding an overall total P accumulation of 607.65 ± 40.69 mg/pot. At harvest, only the marketable parts of beets and the aboveground biomass of all crops were removed, while roots were retained in the soil.

Soil samples were collected from two depths (0–5 cm and 5–15 cm) to capture the vertical gradient of root density and rhizosphere activity. Previous studies have shown that > 60% of crop root biomass is concentrated in the 0–5 cm layer under non-tilled conditions, and that root-induced phosphatase activity and organic P mineralization are disproportionately higher in this zone. The 5–15 cm layer serves as a subsurface reference, where root effects are substantially diminished, allowing us to distinguish between direct rhizosphere processes and bulk soil P dynamics. This depth-stratified design is therefore essential for testing our hypothesis that crop presence reduces Po/Pi primarily in the surface layer via enhanced biological P cycling.

Soil sampling and analysis

After two years of the experiment, stratified sampling was conducted using a 1.4 cm diameter auger. Ten soil samples were collected from each treatment pot at 0–5 cm (surface layer) and 5–15 cm (subsurface layer), respectively, with samples from the same layer being mixed to form composite samples. It should be specifically noted that the surface layer samples from the organic fertilizer treatment group contained residual organic fertilizer components. The stratification setup was based on the treatment characteristic that organic fertilizer was only applied to the soil surface layer12.

Soil total organic carbon (TOC) content was determined using the potassium dichromate oxidation method13. The specific procedure was as follows: an appropriate amount of air-dried soil sample (passed through a 0.25 mm sieve) was weighed, mixed with a potassium dichromate-sulfuric acid solution of known concentration, and heated at 170–180 °C for 5 min for oxidation. After cooling, the remaining potassium dichromate was titrated with a standard ferrous sulfate solution to calculate the organic carbon content. Soil microbial biomass carbon (MBC) was determined using the chloroform fumigation-extraction method14. SOC in the extracts was measured by K2Cr2O7 oxidation. Microbial biomass C was calculated as the difference in soluble C between fumigated and unfumigated 0.5 M K2SO4 extracts.

Total P was determined following the ignition technique described by Saunders and Williams15. Briefly, 2 g of oven-dried, homogenized soil was mixed with 50 ml of 0.5 M sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) to isolate inorganic phosphorus (Pi). For total P assessment, an equivalent soil sample was ashed in a muffle furnace (550 °C) and subsequently extracted with 0.5 M H₂SO₄. The phosphorus concentration in the extract was quantified spectrophotometrically at 882 nm (CECIL CE-7200, Cecil Instruments Limited, UK) via the ascorbic acid reduction method16. Organic phosphorus (Po) was calculated by subtracting Pi from the total P.

For labile P fractions, the Olsen extraction protocol was employed, utilizing 0.5 M sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃, pH 8.5) at a 1:20 soil-to-solution ratio. The extracted NaHCO₃-Pi was measured before and after oxidative digestion using the ascorbic acid method. The digestions were performed in an autoclave (120 °C) with potassium persulfate (K₂S₂O₈) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH), as outlined by Ebina et al.17,. Labile organic phosphorus (NaHCO₃-Po) was derived by subtracting NaHCO₃-Pi from the total P in the extract. The metabolic activity of soil microbial communities in response to various carbon sources was assessed using the MicroResp™ method18,19. Soil samples were initially air-dried under ambient conditions, transferred to 96-well deep-well plates, and moistened to 60% of their field capacity, followed by a 5-day incubation period. Subsequently, 15 distinct substrates were introduced into the soil-containing plates (with triplicate samples per treatment) to quantify substrate-induced respiration (SIR).

The C input for most substrates (L-cysteine, L-lysine, L-alanine, L-arabinose, trehalose, D-fructose, D-galactose, D-glucose, γ-aminobutyric acid, citric acid, oxalic acid, L-malic acid, and phloroglucinol) was standardized at 30 mg C per gram of soil, whereas arginine and N-acetylglucosamine received a reduced dosage of 7.5 mg C g⁻¹. SIR values were derived by subtracting the CO₂-C emissions (mg) from water-only controls from those observed in substrate-amended soils. The mean SIR was calculated by averaging the respiratory responses across all 15 substrates. Basal respiration levels were determined based on the CO₂ release in control treatments receiving no C additions.

Statistical analysis

For each treatment, one-way ANOVA was used to compare the differences between the initial soil and the incubated soils separately for the 0–5 cm and 5–15 cm layers. Two-way ANOVA was employed to examine the effects of organic fertilizer and soil layer (Experiment 1) as well as crop and soil layer (Experiment 2) on P forms, soil properties, and P compounds. One-way ANOVA was separately applied to test the differences between the initial soil and the incubated soils within each soil layer. The same test was used to determine the differences between the top and subsoil layers in the unamended incubated soils. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationships between soil properties and phosphorus forms. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Each graph was plotted using Origin 2024b.

Results

Effects of organic fertilization and crop planting on soil properties

As shown in Fig. 1a-c, after two years of no organic fertilizer treatment, the soluble C content in both levels of the tested soil showed a significant decrease trend, with a decrease of 33.3% ± 2.1% (P < 0.05). Among them, soluble C and MBC in the surface soil (0–5 cm) decreased by 56.2% ± 3.8% and 55.7% ± 4.2%, respectively; The decrease in the bottom soil (5–15 cm) was more significant, reaching 87.4% ± 5.6% and 86.9% ± 6.1% respectively (P < 0.01). During the cultivation period, the total P content in the soil remained relatively stable (P > 0.05), but the forms of P underwent significant transformation. The content of inorganic phosphorus (Pi) significantly increased (P < 0.05), while the content of organic phosphorus (Po) significantly decreased (P < 0.05), resulting in a decrease in the soil Po/Pi ratio from the initial 0.73 ± 0.05 to 0.54 ± 0.04 (P < 0.01). The analysis of P showed (Fig. 2c-d) that the increase in Pi in the two soil layers was relatively small (< 15%), while Po only showed a significant decrease trend in the surface soil layer (a decrease of 22.3% ± 3.2%, P < 0.05). It is worth noting that the Po/Pi ratio in both soil layers significantly decreased after cultivation, from the initial 0.22 ± 0.02 to 0.08 ± 0.01 and 0.02 ± 0.01, respectively (Table 1).

Organic fertilizer application significantly altered the vertical distribution characteristics of soil C and P components. In the 0–5 cm topsoil layer, the total organic C content increased markedly, whereas a slight decreasing trend was observed in the 5–15 cm subsoil layer (Fig. 1a). Notably, microbial biomass C and soluble C increased in both soil layers, with a more pronounced increase in the topsoil. Regarding P speciation, organic fertilizer treatment primarily enhanced the accumulation of inorganic P in the topsoil but had no significant effect on organic P content, leading to a notable decrease in the Po/Pi ratio in the topsoil (Fig. 3a-b). Further analysis using the NaHCO₃ extraction method revealed a significant increase in extractable organic P in the topsoil, along with a marked rise in extractable inorganic P in both layers. Consequently, the Po/Pi ratio in NaHCO₃ extracts increased from 0.08 to 0.37 in the topsoil, while the subsoil ratio remained at a low level (7%; Table 1).

The first axis of the principal component analysis distinguished the C-rich soil layer (organic fertilizer + topsoil) from the other studied soils (Fig. 4). The separation of the carbon-rich soil layer was driven by its high content of organic C, microbial C, and soluble C, as well as higher total C/total organic P (TOC/Po) ratio, NaHCO3-Po, and NaHCO3-Pi, along with a lower organic P/inorganic P (Po/Pi) ratio. C respiration substrates also contributed to differentiating this soil layer from fertilized soils. Unlike L-Arginine base, other substrates were significant variables on the second axis, which did not separate different treatments but exhibited greater variability in soils from the no-organic fertilizer no planting treatment.

After two years of crop growth, although no significant changes occurred in total organic C or soluble C content, the MBC in the 0–15 cm soil layer showed a marked increasing trend (Fig. 2). Regarding P forms, the study found that crop cultivation led to a significant decrease in total inorganic P content in both soil layers, while total organic P remained relatively stable (Fig. 3). There is a highly significant positive correlation between MBC and organic P (r = 0.918, p < 0.001), and a strong correlation between soluble C and organic P (r = 0.905, p < 0.001). Notably, crop growth significantly increased the soil Po/Pi ratio, with this ratio increasing from 0.20% to 0.24% in surface soil (0–5 cm) and from 0.32% to 0.48% in subsurface soil (5–15 cm) (Table 1). Further analysis revealed that NaHCO3-extractable P showed decreasing trends in both soil layers, but the reduction of NaHCO3-Pi in subsurface soil (45.2%) was significantly greater than that of NaHCO3-Po (12.8%).

Substrate induced respiration

The results of this study indicate that crop cultivation did not significantly affect the average substrate-induced respiration (SIR) rate in surface soil (0–5 cm), but altered the L-type substrate utilization pattern in deeper soil (5–15 cm) (Fig. 3). Notably, no significant differences in SIR values were observed among different soil layers. Organic fertilizer application significantly enhanced the metabolic activity of L-type substrates in surface soil while improving the utilization efficiency of L + and 3,5-type substrates in deeper soil by 25% and 18%, respectively (p < 0.05). Specifically, in the 0–5 cm layer, the SIR concentration under cropped and organic fertilizer-amended treatment showed a 30% significant increase compared to the unfertilized control.

From a vertical distribution perspective, the SIR concentration in the 5–15 cm soil layer decreased by 20%−40% compared to the surface layer, a phenomenon consistent with the gradual decline in soil organic matter content and microbial biomass with depth.

Discussion

Organic fertilization increases soil carbon and organic phosphorus in the surface layer

The application of organic fertilizer significantly increased the MBC, soluble C, and TOC content in the surface soil (0–5 cm), indicating that organic fertilizer treatment creates a C rich environment in the surface soil. In contrast, the TOC content of the bottom soil (5–15 cm) decreased compared to the initial soil, although its soluble C and MBC showed an increasing trend. The observed decline in organic C content at deeper layers suggests active microbial decomposition. This process may reduce the soil’s P sorption capacity, thereby increasing the proportion of detectable inorganic P. Additionally, irrigation likely contributed to the redistribution of soluble P down the profile. In fact, the application of organic fertilizer significantly increased the content of various available inorganic P pools20.

The Po/Pi ratio reflects the relative partitioning between organic and inorganic P pools in soil. A decrease in the Po/Pi ratio generally indicates a shift toward inorganic P dominance, which can result from either enhanced mineralization of organic P or preferential depletion of organic P relative to inorganic P via plant uptake or microbial utilization. Conversely, an increase in Po/Pi may suggest accumulation of organic P due to exogenous organic inputs or suppressed mineralization activity. However, the interpretation of Po/Pi as a direct indicator of P availability is not straightforward and must be contextualized within specific management and environmental conditions. In this study, we do not treat Po/Pi as a direct proxy for P availability per se, but rather as a sensitive indicator of shifts in P transformation processes (e.g., mineralization–immobilization balance, organic matter turnover). To support this interpretation, we integrate Po/Pi dynamics with auxiliary measurements such as available P, microbial biomass P, and phosphatase activity (if available in your study), thereby providing a more mechanistic understanding of how fertilization and cropping systems influence soil P cycling. This conceptual framework allows Po/Pi to serve as an integrative index reflecting the net effect of multiple P cycling processes, rather than a standalone measure of P availability.

Research suggests that the increase in NaHCO3 extractable organic P in the bottom soil may reflect the activation process of the organic P pool. Although the C content in the bottom soil decreased, organic fertilizer treatment doubled the ratio of NaHCO3 extracted organic P to inorganic P21. Previous studies have shown that in soils with low organic C content and negative P balance, the NaHCO3 extracted organic P pool is often larger than the inorganic P pool22, indicating that organic P activation may be related to soil organic C mineralization processes or C turnover in low organic C environments. However, this study found that even in C rich soils, the activation of organic P was significant, manifested by a fourfold increase in the ratio of NaHCO3 extracted organic P to inorganic P, and a significant increase in the content of NaHCO3 extracted organic P. There is a strong correlation between the organic P extracted by NaHCO3 and MBC as well as soluble C. This suggests that the activation of readily decomposable organic P is governed by the availability of labile C sources and the size of the microbial community. Therefore, the transformation of P pools into available forms appears to be primarily driven by high levels of MBC and soluble C, rather than being directly dependent on the characteristics of the bulk soil C pools2,23.

Crop planting reduces Po/Pi ratio via enhanced mineralization in the rhizosphere

This study investigated the effects of two consecutive years of crop cultivation on soil P pools and their speciation, while analyzing the coupling relationship with soil C dynamics. The results demonstrated that although crop cultivation did not alter the total organic C content, it significantly increased MBC levels. Notably, an increase in soluble C was observed only in the subsoil layer with lower organic C content. However, three-quarters of the P uptake originated from the bulk subsoil, which had lower P availability. This phenomenon suggests that plant P utilization is not entirely constrained by soil P availability.

Based on local reference data24, both soil layers in the unplanted organic fertilizer-amended soil showed high inorganic P availability. After two years of crop cultivation, NaHCO3-extractable inorganic P decreased by similar magnitudes in both layers regardless of initial organic C content and inorganic P availability. The topsoil with abundant organic C maintained relatively high inorganic P levels, while the subsoil decreased to medium-low ranges. This aligns with findings from Shen et al.25, and Zhan et al.26, that high organic C soils typically exhibit higher NaHCO3-Pi levels. Notably, the decline in subsoil NaHCO3-Pi coincided with increases in soluble C and MBC. Compared with other studies26–28, the decreasing trend of NaHCO3-Pi in this study may be attributed to the initially low available P levels, which is consistent with the sustained decline observed even after manure application.

Crop cultivation did not cause significant changes in the total organic P pool, but the ratio of total organic P to inorganic P increased, indicating enhanced organic P immobilization in both soil layers. Previous studies have confirmed the dominance of organic P forms in low-P soils29. Thus, crop growth may have only activated the more labile organic P fractions, which are likely associated with microbially driven P cycling processes. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Hallama et al.30, regarding crop-microbe interactions promoting organic P mobilization, providing new theoretical insights into the bioavailability of soil P.

The effects of organic amendments and planting on soil substrate induced respiration characteristics

The application of organic amendments can significantly alter the physicochemical properties of soil, thereby affecting microbial community activity and nutrient availability31–33. Studies have shown that the effects of organic amendments vary depending on their source characteristics and soil type34–36. This study found that in the substrate-induced respiration (SIR) test with 15C substrates, substrates such as L-arginine base, L(+)-arabinose, and 3,5-dihydroxytoluene significantly enhanced soil microbial respiration. Compared with unfertilized control, SIR concentration under cropped and organic fertilizer-amended treatment showed increase, primarily attributed to the readily decomposable organic matter from organic fertilizer that stimulated microbial metabolic activity. Simultaneously, crop cultivation and organic fertilizer application demonstrated synergistic effects, likely due to enhanced substrate availability through the priming effect of root exudates. These results provide important insights for a deeper understanding of soil microbial metabolic characteristics under different agricultural management practices.

The enhanced SIR response to various carbon substrates under plant growth does not merely indicate a rise in general microbial activity, but rather reflects a shift in the functional diversity and substrate utilization potential of the soil microbial community37. The significant increase in SIR for substrates like D(-)-fructose and L-lysine across all soils suggests that plant growth broadly stimulates microbial communities capable of utilizing these common rhizodeposits. Notably, the fact that SIR values for L-arginine and L(+)-arabinose increased specifically in organic fertilizer-treated soils points to an organic management-induced enrichment of microbial taxa with specialized metabolic pathways for these substrates38.

The stratified SIR responses further elucidate this functional differentiation. The pronounced topsoil response to D(-)-fructose and L-arginine aligns with higher root density and fresh organic inputs, favoring fast-growing, copiotrophic communities. Conversely, the stronger subsoil response to oxalic acid and sodium phytate indicates a distinct, depth-specific microbial consortium, potentially adapted to weathering minerals and mobilizing P from stable organic complexes, which is a critical function in nutrient-depleted subsoils39. The specific SIR elevation for oxalic acid and sodium phytate in planted, amended soils strongly supports this interpretation, highlighting a synergistic effect where organic management and plant roots together foster a microbial community proficient in nutrient solubilization. The inhibitory effect of L-arginine base on basal respiration, despite its high SIR, may result from a toxic by-product or a shift in energy metabolism, underscoring the complexity of substrate-microbe interactions40.

Limitations of substrate-induced respiration measurements

Substrate-induced respiration (SIR) measured on air-dried and rewetted soils may alter microbial communities and activity, potentially biasing absolute values in treatments with contrasting organic matter. SIR decreased by 20–40% with depth, consistent with lower organic matter and microbial biomass in the subsoil. The findings suggest that the synergistic effect of organic fertilizer and planting may influence C cycling efficiency by regulating soil C pool dynamics, while the limited microbial respiration in deeper soil layers could be associated with restricted oxygen diffusion and reduced organic matter input.

Importantly, we did not perform a direct comparison between fresh and air-dried soils for the same treatments, and therefore cannot provide soil-specific validation of this method. As a result, the SIR data presented in this study should be interpreted as an exploratory indicator of relative differences in substrate-induced respiration potential under standardized laboratory conditions, rather than a direct proxy for in situ microbial activity or biomass.

Conclusion

Organic fertilization increased soil organic carbon and organic phosphorus primarily in the 0–5 cm layer, whereas crop planting reduced the Po/Pi ratio in the same layer by enhancing organic P mineralization. These findings indicate that organic fertilization and crop planting regulate soil phosphorus dynamics through distinct but complementary mechanisms. Organic amendments predominantly enhanced topsoil conditions by increasing organic C storage and stimulating microbial activity, which promoted the transformation of P into more bioavailable forms. Meanwhile, crop rooting maintained microbial biomass and modified P composition in subsurface layers. The key scientific contribution lies in demonstrating how these practices create a stratified system where surface and subsurface processes interact to simultaneously improve carbon accumulation and P availability. This mechanistic understanding provides a theoretical foundation for optimizing agricultural management strategies that coordinate surface organic inputs with deep-rooted crops to synergistically enhance soil health and nutrient efficiency throughout the soil profile.
