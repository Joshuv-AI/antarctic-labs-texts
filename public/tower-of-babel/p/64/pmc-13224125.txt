Lipopeptide-mediated inhibition of Aflatoxin biosynthesis in Aspergillus flavus revealed by DESI-MSI and transcriptomics
Authors: Lingxia Peng, Guohong Zeng, Ashun Yin, Zhiwei Wang, Ziying Wang, Gurusamy Abirami, Xiangjie Pan, Shuifeng Zhang, Zongsuo Liang, Xiaodan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAflatoxin contamination caused by Aspergillus flavus represents a critical threat to global food safety and agricultural sustainability, yet the mechanisms underlying its effective biological control remain poorly understood, particularly at the spatial and metabolic levels. Here, we systematically investigated the biocontrol potential of Bacillus velezensis B2 and elucidated the mechanisms of its bioactive metabolites using an integrated multi-omics strategy. Chemical profiling and purification identified surfactin-iturin complexes (SLCs) as the major antifungal components. Functional assays demonstrated that SLCs completely inhibited spore germination at 160 μg/mL (MIC = 0.125 mg/mL) and exhibited approximately 10-fold higher antifungal potency than the crude extract. SLC treatment also induced severe hyphal deformities, including swelling and membrane disruption. Spatial metabolomics using DESI-MSI revealed a dynamic metabolic shift from m/z 1020 to m/z 1034 during coculture, with surfactin-type metabolites dominating the interspecies interaction interface. Transcriptomic analysis identified 3560 differentially expressed genes (1559 upregulated and 2001 downregulated), and demonstrated that SLCs globally suppressed the aflatoxin biosynthetic pathway by downregulating key genes, including aflK, aflQ, aflP, aflO, aflM, aflE, aflH, and aflT. Consistently, no detectable AFB1 was observed in treated samples (LOD = 0.05 μg/kg). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SLCs inhibit aflatoxin production through combined structural damage and metabolic reprogramming, and highlight a spatially resolved multi-omics framework for deciphering microbial interactions and developing effective biocontrol strategies in food safety.Keywords: Bacillus velezensis B2, Aspergillus flavus, DESI-MSI, Transcriptomics, Isolation and purification, SLCs

Introduction

Aflatoxins (AFTs) are highly toxic and carcinogenic secondary metabolites primarily produced by Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus, characterized by a dihydrofuranocoumarin core structure [Shabeer S et al., 2022]. Recognized as some of the most potent natural carcinogens, AFTs pose severe threats to global food safety, public health, and agricultural sustainability. Among them, aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is particularly concerning due to its extreme toxicity and carcinogenicity, causing damage to multiple organs—including the liver, kidneys, and brain—and contributing to systemic disorders such as immunosuppression and growth retardation [Cheng L et al., 2019]. The substantial economic losses associated with A. flavus contamination further highlight the urgent need for effective and sustainable control strategies.

Compared with conventional chemical approaches, which often suffer from environmental risks and inconsistent efficacy, biological control using antagonistic microorganisms and their metabolites has emerged as a promising and eco-friendly alternative. Our previous high-throughput sequencing and antagonistic assays revealed a strong negative correlation between Bacillus species isolated from Coix seeds and the accumulation of multiple mycotoxins, including aflatoxins, zearalenone, and deoxynivalenol [Peng LX et al., 2025]. Among these, B. velezensis B2 exhibited pronounced inhibitory activity against aflatoxin production in A. flavus [Nartey LK et al., 2021]. This strain is known to produce antifungal lipopeptides (LPs), particularly surfactin and iturin; however, the mechanisms by which these metabolites interfere with fungal development and toxin biosynthesis remain insufficiently resolved.

To address this gap, we systematically investigated the antifungal mechanism of B. velezensis B2 through a multi-level analytical framework integrating bioactivity-guided fractionation, spatial metabolomics, transcriptomics, and functional validation. First, crude extracts from the fermentation broth were fractionated using silica gel column chromatography, gel filtration, and preparative HPLC, leading to the identification of surfactin–iturin complexes (SLCs) as the principal bioactive components [Li B et al., 2016]. Functional characterization demonstrated that SLC treatment induced pronounced morphological abnormalities in A. flavus, including hyphal swelling and membrane disruption, confirming its potent antifungal activity. To further elucidate the metabolic basis of this interaction, desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) was employed to visualize the spatial distribution of metabolites in both monoculture and co-culture systems. Owing to its label-free nature, high spatial resolution, and sensitivity, DESI-MSI has been widely applied across biomedical, plant, and microbiological research [Robinson EW and Williams ER., 2005; Pirro V et al., 2017; de Abreu et al., 2013; Fernandes AM et al., 2016; Song et al., 2009;Rodrigues et al., 2018]. In this study, DESI-MSI enabled in situ mapping of metabolic dynamics during microbial interactions, revealing a spatially organized redistribution of lipopeptides associated with fungal inhibition. In parallel, transcriptomic analysis revealed that SLC treatment significantly downregulated key genes involved in the aflatoxin biosynthetic pathway, including aflK, aflQ, and aflP, indicating a coordinated repression of toxin biosynthesis at the transcriptional level [Shabeer S et al., 2022; Wogan GN., 1966; Yu J et al., 2013; Nan P et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2014].

Collectively, this study establishes an integrated spatial—molecular framework for understanding lipopeptide-mediated inhibition of aflatoxin biosynthesis, revealing a previously underexplored link between membrane disruption and transcriptional suppression. These findings not only advance fundamental knowledge of microbe–fungus interactions but also provide a mechanistic foundation for the development of sustainable biocontrol strategies—particularly for mitigating A. flavus contamination in Coix and other vulnerable crops.

Materials and methods

Microbial strains and culture conditions

B. velezensis B2: The strain was isolated from the rhizosphere soil of Camellia sinensis in the Meijiawu Tea Garden, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, and was purified and identified by the research group of Professor Zeng Guohong at the Microbiology Laboratory of Zhejiang Sci-Tech University [Wu Q et al., 2022]. The strain was stored in a 25% glycerol solution at −80 °C.

A. flavus: The strain was obtained from Professor Hu Xiufang of the Microbiology Laboratory at Zhejiang Sci-Tech University and stored in a 50% glycerol solution at −80 °C.

Preparation of spore suspensions

Preparation of A. flavus Spore Suspension: A 7-day-old A. flavus culture plate was used to prepare the spore suspension. Under sterile conditions in a laminar flow hood, a spore elution solution (prepared by adding 10 μL of Triton X-100 to 100 mL of ultrapure water and sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min) was added to the plate. Spores were gently dislodged using a sterile triangular glass rod, collected into a centrifuge tube, and filtered through quartz cotton to remove residual mycelia and culture medium, the spore concentration was quantified using a hemocytometer under a microscope and adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL unless otherwise specified for subsequent experiments.

Monoculture and coculture conditions

Monoculture of B. velezensis B2: The strain was inoculated into sterile Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 12–15 h. Following incubation, 10 μL of the culture was spotted onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates and incubated at 28 °C for 3, 7, and 10 days. The plates were then stored at 4 °C until further analysis.

Coculture of B. velezensis B2 and A. flavus: A spore suspension of A. flavus (1 × 106 CFU/mL), prepared from a 7-day-old culture, was used for coculture experiments. 10 μL of the spore suspension were inoculated onto the side opposite the B. velezensis B2 colony on a PDA plate. The coculture plates were incubated at 28 °C for 3, 7, and 10 days and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Extraction and UPLC-MS analysis of metabolites

Extraction from B. velezensis B2: The culture supernatant was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to remove bacterial cells. The supernatant was then extracted with n-butanol (1:1, v/v), and the organic phase was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. The resulting crude extract was lyophilized and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Extraction from A. flavus: The culture supernatant was acidified to pH 2 with concentrated hydrochloric acid and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The precipitate was extracted three times with methanol, and the pooled solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator following the method described [Song Y et al., 2009]. The crude extract was lyophilized and stored at −80 °C.

UPLC-MS analysis: The dried extracts were redissolved in methanol and analyzed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The injection volume was 2 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0-27 min, 5-95% B; 27-27.1 min, 95-5% B; and 27.1-30 min, 5% B. Mass spectrometry was performed using electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative ion mode. The source temperature was set at 120 °C, and the desolvation temperature was 250 °C. Mass spectra were acquired over a scan range of m/z 50-1500. Data acquisition and processing were carried out using MassLynx software (version 4.1).

DESI-MS and DESI-MSI analysis

Sample Preparation: The PDA plates containing monocultures or cocultures were dried into thin films. Glass slides with bacterial colonies were fixed onto a custom 2D moving platform using double-sided tape.

Instrument Parameters: The capillary voltage was set at 0.45 kV, and the temperature was maintained at 250 °C. The mobile phase consisted of methanol and water (9:1 v/v) with a flow rate of 2 μL/min. Nitrogen gas was used for nebulization at 0.9 psi. The sprayer was positioned at a 68°, 1-2 mm from the slide, and 5 mm from the mass spectrometer inlet. All analyses were performed in negative ion mode with a mass range of m/z 100-1400.

Data Acquisition: DESI imaging was performed at a scan speed of 80 μm/s with a spatial resolution of 240 × 240 μm. Data were processed using HDImaging software.

Antifungal activity assays

Inhibition Zone Assay: The extracted metabolites were dissolved in DMSO to a concentration of 50 mg/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of A. flavus spore suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was spread onto PDA plates, and wells (6 mm diameter) were punched into the agar. 50 μL of the metabolite solution were added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 28 °C for 4 days. The diameter of the inhibition zones was measured.

Minimal Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of SLCs: Different concentrations of SLCs (0.125-15 mg/mL) were added to wells in PDA plates inoculated with A. flavus. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 5 days, and the inhibition zones were measured [Nanguy SP et al., 2010].

The separation and purification of SLCs

The strain stored in a 50% glycerol solution at −80 °C was inoculated into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of LB medium (composed of 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl, and distilled water adjusted to pH 7.0-7.4). The flask was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm overnight. Under sterile conditions, the culture was inoculated at a 1:100 (v/v) ratio into a new flask and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 4 days, resulting in a total of 40 L of culture, which yielded 30 g of crude product. The seed culture medium and production medium consisted of the same components, and all media were sterilized at 121 °C by steam autoclaving for 20 min. Following the scale-up process, the 40 L culture was centrifuged to separate the supernatant, and crude lipopeptides were extracted using the acid precipitation method. The crude extract was separated on a silica gel column, with a gradient of dichloromethane: methanol (15:1, 10:1, 7:1, 5:1, 1:1), and the final elution was performed using pure methanol. Different fractions were collected and tested for antifungal activity against A. flavus. The active fractions were further separated on a Sephadex LH-20 gel column using 50% methanol-water. The separated components were then tested for antimicrobial activity. The active components were further purified using preparative liquid chromatography, which separated the crude extract and yielded a pair of isomers identified by mass spectrometry. One of the isomers was confirmed to be a surfactin-iturin complex (SLCs).

Effect of SLCs on A. flavus

Spore Germination: SLCs was added to PDB medium at concentrations of 5-160 μg/mL, and 1 mL of A. flavus spore suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was inoculated. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 75 rpm for 10 h. Spore germination rates were calculated under an optical microscope [Yan F et al., 2020].

Mycelial Growth: SLCs was incorporated into PDA medium at concentrations of 0-250 μg/mL. A 6 mm well was punched into the agar, and 50 μL of A. flavus spore suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were added. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 5 days, and mycelial growth was measured.

Microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

For scanning electron microscopy observation, A. flavus spores and mycelia were first treated with different concentrations of SLCs (10, 40, and 80 μg/mL) for 24 h at 28 °C. Untreated samples served as the control. After treatment, the samples were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min and washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). The samples were then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at 4 °C for 4 h, followed by three washes with the same buffer (10 min each). Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%, v/v) for 15 min per concentration, with two final 15 min washes in 100% ethanol. The dehydrated samples were then freeze-dried overnight, mounted onto aluminum stubs with double-sided carbon tape, and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold (approximately 10-15 nm) under argon atmosphere. Observations were performed using a scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 5-10 kV, and images were captured at magnifications of 5000× to document morphological changes in conidia and hyphae [Ning H., 2022].

Transcriptomics analysis

RNA Extraction and cDNA Library Construction: Total RNA was extracted from A. flavus using the Trizol method. RNA quality was assessed using a Nanodrop2000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced using Illumina platforms [Martin M., 2011; Grabherr MG et al., 2011].

RT-qPCR: Differentially expressed genes were validated using RT-qPCR with the actin gene as a reference. Primers were designed as described by Fang [Shen J et al., 2022; Cheng J et al., 2013]. The primers used are listed in Table S1. The PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix kit (TRAN) was employed, and the reaction system consisted of 10 μL.

Aflatoxin extraction

Spores of A. flavus (untreated control) and those treated with SLCs were cultured for 40 h, then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to remove hyphal debris. The resulting supernatants were thoroughly mixed with C18 adsorbent by vortexing. After a second round of centrifugation to remove the C18 matrix, the supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters to ensure sterility before proceeding with further analyses.

Determination of aflatoxin B1

AFB1 quantification was carried out using LC-MS/MS, following the validated method established by Fang et al. [Fang JH et al., 2025]. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile), delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. A 3 μL sample was injected for analysis. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode using selected reaction monitoring (SRM) of the precursor ion m/z 313.1 [M+H]+, with a cone voltage of 40 V and collision energy of 21 eV to ensure optimal fragmentation.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate with three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Fisher's LSD post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons, and Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used for data visualization.

Results and discussion

UPLC-MS spectrometry analysis

To identify the potential antifungal metabolites produced by B. velezensis B2 and to understand the biochemical basis for its antagonistic activity, we first characterized its secondary metabolite profile using UPLC-MS. The LC-MS analysis in negative ion mode is presented in Fig. S1. The retention times of 15.88, 16.44, 16.54, 26.44, 27.33, and 27.99 min correspond to molecular masses of 1087.56, 1101.57, 1101.57, 1006.66, 1020.68, and 1034.69, respectively (Fig. 1). These peaks represent ion adducts of C14 Iturin A, C15 Iturin A, C15 Iturin A, C13 Surfactin, C14 Surfactin, and C15 Surfactin, as listed in Table 1. These results indicate that strain B2 preferentially synthesizes cyclic lipopeptides with varying fatty acid chain lengths, which is a typical feature of Bacillus-derived lipopeptide biosynthesis. The coexistence of Iturin and Surfactin families suggests a synergistic antifungal strategy, as Iturin is primarily responsible for strong antifungal activity, while Surfactin enhances membrane permeability and facilitates compound diffusion. These findings are consistent with those reported by Shengjie Li [Li S et al., 2022] and Xiangyang Liu [Liu X et al., 2010]. Therefore, this initial metabolic profiling not only confirmed the identity of the major bioactive compounds but also provided a strong rationale for further purifying the surfactin-iturin complex to evaluate its antifungal efficacy against A. flavus.

Isolation, purification, and antifungal activity of SLCs against A. flavus

Having identified the presence of both iturin and surfactin in the crude extract, we next performed purification to concentrate the active components and determine their true antifungal potential without interference from inert impurities. The crude lipopeptide extract was purified through sequential chromatographic techniques including silica gel column chromatography, gel filtration chromatography, and preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Comparative antimicrobial assays (Fig. 1C) revealed that the purified surfactin-iturin complex (SLCs) exhibited significantly enhanced antifungal activity compared to the crude extract. SLCs exhibited a concentration-dependent antifungal effect against A. flavus, with efficacy increasing at higher concentrations (Fig. 1B, D, and 1E). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was identified as 0.125 mg/mL, resulting in an inhibition zone diameter of 12.21 ± 0.35 mm (mean ± SD, n = 3). Remarkably, at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, SLCs produced significantly larger inhibition zones compared to those generated by 15 mg/mL of the crude lipopeptide extract, underscoring their enhanced antifungal potency. Spore germination assays demonstrated complete inhibition of A. flavus spore germination at 160 μg/mL SLCs after 10 h of incubation (Fig. 1A), compared to an 84% germination rate in the control group. The inhibition of spore germination by SLCs displayed a clear dose-dependent pattern, with higher concentrations leading to progressively greater suppression. These results suggest that SLCs effectively inhibit A. flavus spore germination at optimal concentrations, with complete inhibition observed beyond a critical threshold. The enhanced antifungal activity observed after purification suggests that the bioactive components are enriched and that potential antagonistic or inactive impurities present in the crude extract are removed. The strong dose-dependent inhibition further indicates that SLCs directly interact with fungal cells rather than acting indirectly. Notably, the significantly higher efficacy of purified SLCs compared to crude extracts implies a synergistic interaction between surfactin and iturin components.This synergy likely explains the sharp reduction in MIC and complete inhibition of spore germination at relatively low concentrations. This purification and bioassay step was crucial, as it demonstrated that the synergistic effect between surfactin and iturin is the primary driver of the potent antifungal activity, far exceeding the crude extract's efficacy.

Inhibitory effect of SLCs on mycelial growth

Since spore germination is only the initial stage of fungal establishment, we further investigated the effect of SLCs on mycelial expansion, which is directly linked to fungal colonization and aflatoxin contamination severity. After incubation at 28 °C for 5 days, the mycelial diameter of A. flavus treated with SLCs was significantly reduced compared to the control group (Fig. 2). These crude extract demonstrated a pronounced inhibitory effect on A. flavus mycelial growth. Compared to the control, the treated mycelia appeared thinner with irregular morphological edges. A significant dose-dependent reduction in mycelial diameter was observed as the concentration of SLCs increased from 0 to 200 μg/mL. At 250 μg/mL, the mycelial growth was reduced to a diameter barely visible to the naked eye. Notably, the mycelial diameter of A. flavus treated with 250 μg/mL of the elution solution fell between those treated with 25 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL of the purified crude extract. This indicates that the further separation and purification of the elution solution yielded SLCs with significantly enhanced antifungal activity, exhibiting an inhibitory effect nearly 10-fold greater than that of the crude extract. This effect is consistent with previous studies showing that lipopeptides disrupt fungal cell membranes and inhibit hyphal elongation by altering membrane permeability and intracellular homeostasis [Liu J et al., 2014]. This mycelial growth inhibition assay confirmed that SLCs are not merely spore-static but actively suppress vegetative growth, a key trait for an effective biocontrol agent.

Microscope and SEM evaluation

To directly visualize the structural damage implied by the growth inhibition assays and to gain insights into the mechanism of action, we examined conidial and mycelial morphology using scanning electron microscopy. The morphological changes of A. flavus conidia and mycelia following SLCs exposure were observed by scanning electron microscopy at various concentrations (Fig. 3). The results unequivocally demonstrate the antifungal efficacy of these SLCs against A. flavus. In the control group (Fig. 3A), untreated A. flavus conidia displayed prominent surface protrusions and a robust, plump morphology. Some conidia exhibited surface depressions, likely attributable to nutrient deprivation-induced cell death. The mycelia in the control group exhibited vigorous growth, with a smooth and intact surface morphology.

Effects of SLC Treatment on the Morphology of A. flavus Spores and Mycelia. (A–D) Morphological changes in A. flavus spores following treatment with control (CK), and SLCs at 10, 40, and 80 μg/mL, respectively. (E–H) Morphological alterations in A. flavus mycelia under the same treatments: control (CK), and SLCs at 10, 40, and 80 μg/mL, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at 5K× magnification revealed observable morphological disruptions in A. flavus conidia and hyphae following treatment with SLCs at concentrations of 10, 40, and 80 μg/mL. Following 24 h of exposure to 10 μg/mL SLCs, some conidia exhibited surface depressions and localized deformation, accompanied by irregular surface features (Fig. 3B). The conidiophores at the hyphal tips showed noticeable structural deformation, including fragmentation and detachment of conidia. Furthermore, the conidial surfaces exhibited collapse-like depressions, while the hyphae appeared shrunken and collapsed, evident through pronounced shrinkage, collapse, and twisting. Partial hyphal fragmentation was observed, suggesting possible structural damage to the cell envelope. A trend of increased damage was observed with increasing concentrations, with increasing SLCs concentrations (40 and 80 μg/mL) leading to increased frequency of deformed or collapsed conidial and intensified hyphal shrinkage, collapse, and distortion (Fig. 3C and D). At higher concentrations, the hyphal surfaces exhibited more severe surface deformation, suggesting potential damage to cellular structures.

A. flavus mycelia treated with different concentrations of SLCs (Fig. 3E–H). In the untreated sample, mycelia displayed a smooth and uniform surface, characteristic of normal growth, with no significant structural abnormalities (Fig. 3E). In contrast, treatment with 10 μg/mL SLCs induced minor surface irregularities, indicative of early-stage structural damage (Fig. 3F). At 40 μg/mL, mycelia exhibited pronounced shrinkage, deformation, and a roughened surface texture, accompanied by clear structural integrity loss (Fig. 3G). At 80 μg/mL, mycelia displayed severe twisting, deep wrinkling, and extensive shrinkage, with the surface texture showing substantial damage, confirming the effective disruption of the fungal cell wall structure by high concentrations of SLCs (Fig. 3H). These progressive morphological changes underscore the dose-dependent inhibitory effect of SLCs on A. flavus mycelial integrity, accompanied by significant leakage of intracellular contents. The observed collapse, wrinkling, and surface deformation at higher SLCs concentrations are characteristic of membrane-directed damage, providing direct visual evidence that SLCs compromise the structural integrity of the fungal cell envelope.

Monoculture mass spectrometry imaging of B. velezensis B2

To determine whether B. velezensis B2 produces its antifungal metabolites constitutively or only under specific developmental cues-a key factor for its reliability as a biocontrol agent—we performed DESI-MSI on monocultures over a 10-day time course. To explore the antagonistic interaction between B. velezensis B2 and A. flavus, we performed monoculture and co-culture experiments followed by morphological analysis. Direct DESI analysis of B2 on glass slides after 3 days revealed prominent ion peaks at m/z 1034, 1020, 1006, and 992 (Fig. 4A–D). By day 7, additional peaks at m/z 741, 721, 707, 693, and 255 appeared (Fig. 4B–E). On day 10, the primary peaks remained, while the intensities of m/z 741, 721, 707, 693, and 255 decreased (Fig. 4C–F). To assess additional secondary metabolites, colony surfaces were imprinted with double-sided tape. DESI analysis of the imprints showed m/z 707 and 721 at lower intensities after 3, 7, and 10 days, whereas m/z 1006, 1020, and 1034 remained consistently strong (Figs. S2 and S3). Notably, m/z 255 was minimally detected on glass slides by day 3 and absent from tape imprints, suggesting it is not produced in newly divided cells.

DESI-MS Imaging and Scanning of B. velezensis B2 Colonies Over Time. (A–C) DESI-MS imaging of B. velezensis B2 colonies on glass slides after incubation at 37 °C on potato agar for (A) 3 days, (B) 7 days, and (C) 10 days. (D–F) DESI-MS scans of B. velezensis B2 colonies grown on potato dextrose agar plates at 37 °C for (D) 3 days, (E) 7 days, and (F) 10 days, respectively.

Across all sampling methods, m/z 1006, 1020, and 1034 exhibited consistently high intensities and uniform spatial distribution, indicating they are primary metabolites present in both young and old cells. In contrast, m/z 255, 693, 707, 721, and 741 were predominantly detected on glass slides, with minimal presence in tape imprints. This discrepancy may reflect accumulation of these compounds on older cell surfaces, which are displaced upward during continued division, limiting their transfer to the tape. Morphological observations supported these findings: newly divided cells appeared milky-white, while older cells pushed toward the colony center became darker and drier. Spatial distribution maps showed that on day 3, compounds were localized at the colony center, shifting to the periphery by days 7 and 10. This pattern suggests that m/z 1006, 1020, and 1034 are produced by actively growing young cells, with signal attenuation as older cells overlay them.

In addition to the identified ions, DESI-MS revealed numerous other secondary metabolites from B2, but their low abundance or ionization efficiency requires further characterization. The constitutive production and uniform distribution of the m/z 1006, 1020, and 1034 ions across all time points and spatial regions indicate that these surfactins are core, constitutively synthesized metabolites, which is a desirable trait for a reliable biocontrol strain. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that B. velezensis B2 regulates metabolite production in a growth-stage-dependent manner, with constitutively produced ions (m/z 1006, 1020, 1034) playing essential physiological roles and temporally varying ions (m/z 255, 693, 707) reflecting metabolic differentiation between young and aging cells.

Mass spectrometry imaging analysis of cocultured B. velezensis B2 and A. flavus

Monoculture conditions do not fully represent the competitive environment in which a biocontrol agent must function. Therefore, we co-cultured B2 with A. flavus to investigate whether the presence of the fungal pathogen triggers an adaptive metabolic response from the bacterium. In the co-culture experiment, 10 μL aliquots of B. velezensis B2 and A. flavus were inoculated on opposite sides of PDA plates and incubated at 28 °C for 3, 7, and 10 days. After 3 days of co-cultivation, bacterial-fungal co-metabolites were detected, with prominent signals corresponding to bacterial-derived metabolites at m/z 1006, 1020, and 1034 (Fig. 5A–D). Notably, m/z 1020 emerged as the primary antifungal compound during the first 7 days (Fig. 5B–E), whereas m/z 1034 became the dominant inhibitory metabolite by day 10 (Fig. 5C–F). A trace-level signal at m/z 255.0844 was also observed but remained negligible throughout the 10-day period under both mono-culture and co-culture conditions, suggesting that its production may be induced specifically by prolonged fungal interaction.

(A–C) DESI-MS imaging of two colonies on glass slides after incubation at 37 °C on potato agar for (A) 3 days, (B) 7 days, and (C) 10 days. (D–F) DESI-MS scans of two colonies after co-cultivation on potato agar plates at 37 °C for (D) 3 days, (E) 7 days, and (F) 10 days.

Consistent with monoculture observations, B. velezensis B2 constitutively produced surfactin-type metabolites (m/z 1006, 1020, 1034) regardless of cultivation conditions. These compounds were detectable as early as day 3 and remained stable over 10 days. Spatial distribution analysis showed uniform localization of these surfactins across both the actively expanding colony edges and the fungal interaction zones, supporting their function as constitutive defense molecules. Importantly, during co-culture with A. flavus, a dynamic shift from m/z 1020 to m/z 1034 dominance occurred, indicating an adaptive metabolic response under competitive stress. This shift may reflect functional specialization of different surfactin homologs, where specific variants are preferentially produced in response to fungal challenge. This co-culture experiment was essential, as it revealed the metabolic plasticity of B2—its ability to switch between different surfactin variants in response to fungal competition—a finding that could not have been obtained from monoculture alone.

These findings highlight the critical role of secondary metabolites in microbial competition and demonstrate that surfactins are not only core metabolites but also key biochemical determinants of the competitive fitness of B. velezensis B2. The observed metabolic plasticity offers a mechanistic insight into the potential of B2 as a reliable biocontrol agent, owing to its consistent and elevated production of antifungal surfactins under diverse environmental conditions. Future research using advanced extraction methods and chromatographic techniques will be required to comprehensively characterize these compounds and elucidate their biosynthetic pathways.

SLCs affected the transcriptome of A. flavus

Differential gene expression analysis and functional annotation

To gain genome-scale insights into the molecular mechanisms by which SLCs suppress A. flavus growth and aflatoxin production, we performed transcriptome sequencing on SLCs-treated samples. Transcriptome profiling of A. flavus treated with 10 μg/mL SLCs revealed significant alterations in gene expression patterns. Compared to untreated controls, 4328 genes showed differential expression, with stringent thresholds (P < 0.05, |log2Ratio|≥2) identifying 3560 statistically significant DEGs, of which 1559 were upregulated and 2001 downregulated (Fig. 6A and B). GO functional classification distributed these DEGs across three major categories: Biological Processes (2486 genes), Cellular Components (465 genes), and Molecular Functions (1377 genes). Within Biological Processes, DEGs were primarily associated with metabolic regulation, including both primary and secondary pathways (e.g., small molecule metabolism and aflatoxin biosynthesis), as well as cellular processes such as component organization and differentiation. Cellular Component analysis showed enrichment in genes related to membrane systems, cell wall organization, and cyclin-dependent kinase complexes. Molecular Function analysis identified DEGs involved in catalytic activity (e.g., oxidoreductases), transport mechanisms, and transcriptional regulation.

Transcriptomic Analysis of Differentially Expressed Genes in A. flavus. (A) Gene Ontology (GO) annotation of differentially expressed genes. (B) Volcano plot showing the distribution of differentially expressed genes. (C) Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation of differentially expressed genes in A. flavus.

KEGG pathway analysis of the 1554 functionally annotated genes in the treatment group demonstrated significant enrichment in primary metabolism (736 genes), secondary metabolite biosynthesis (321 genes), tyrosine metabolism (51 genes), and fatty acid metabolism (46 genes) (Fig. 6C). Further enrichment analysis showed significant upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, folate biosynthesis, purine metabolism, and amino sugar metabolism (P < 0.05), suggesting activation of cellular repair mechanisms upon SLCs treatment. In parallel, transcriptome analysis revealed significant downregulation of key aflatoxin biosynthetic genes, including aflK, aflQ, aflP, aflO, aflM, aflE, aflH, and aflT (Table S2).

These findings provide molecular evidence for a dual antifungal action of SLCs: broad inhibition of fungal growth through disruption of essential metabolic pathways, and targeted suppression of mycotoxin production via downregulation of aflatoxin biosynthesis genes. The coordinated transcriptional reprogramming across multiple functional categories supports the broad-spectrum antifungal efficacy of SLCs against A. flavus. The observed trade-off between growth and secondary metabolism, where upregulation of primary pathways may compensate for stress while aflatoxin synthesis is suppressed, has been widely documented in fungi under stress conditions [Caceres et al., 2020]. This transcriptomic analysis was critical to move beyond observable phenotypes and uncover the underlying regulatory network, demonstrating that SLCs not only inhibit growth but also actively reprogram fungal metabolism at the transcriptional level. Thus, SLCs not only inhibit fungal growth but also reprogram metabolic priorities in A. flavus.

RT-qPCR verification of differentially expressed genes

To validate the reliability of the RNA-seq findings and confirm the expression changes of key aflatoxin-related genes, we performed independent RT-qPCR analysis. Based on the RNA-seq enrichment results for aflatoxin biosynthesis, quantitative RT-qPCR was performed on eight aflatoxin biosynthesis-related genes in the 10 μg SLCs treatment group. The results showed that almost all tested genes were downregulated, with significant changes observed for all eight genes (P < 0.05, Fig. 7). Among them, AflV, AflW, AflY, HypB, and HypC are known regulatory factors in aflatoxin biosynthesis [Amaike and Keller, 2011]. Following SLCs treatment, transcriptome data indicated that the relative expression of these five genes decreased significantly (P < 0.05), suggesting that SLCs inhibits aflatoxin production by suppressing these regulatory factors.

RT-qPCR Validation of Key Differentially Expressed Genes. Expression levels of selected genes were validated by RT-qPCR. Vertical bars represent the standard error of the mean, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

The AflC gene is involved in the synthesis of the polyketide backbone and plays a critical role in the early steps of aflatoxin biosynthesis. Aflatoxin precursors have been shown to involve up to 14 biosynthetic genes [Caceres et al., 2020]. In our experiment, the expression of aflK, aflQ, aflP, aflO, aflM, aflE, aflT, and aflH was significantly downregulated (P < 0.05). These RT-qPCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data, further validating the inhibitory effect of SLCs on aflatoxin biosynthesis genes. The agreement between the two methods confirms the reliability of the sequencing results.

The downregulation of both regulatory and structural genes in the aflatoxin biosynthetic pathway indicates that SLCs acts at multiple levels of gene regulation. This multi-layer inhibition is particularly effective for suppressing toxin production, as it prevents both pathway activation and enzymatic progression [Amaike and Keller, 2011]. This orthogonal validation strengthened our confidence in the transcriptomic data and confirmed that the observed suppression of aflatoxin biosynthesis is both statistically and biologically significant.

The effect of SLCs on the AFB1 production of A. flavus

Given the transcriptional downregulation of aflatoxin biosynthetic genes, it was imperative to directly measure AFB1 accumulation to determine whether the observed changes in gene expression translate into a true reduction in toxin production at the metabolic level. In the blank control group, AFB1 accumulation was consistently high throughout the experiment (Fig. 8). By contrast, no detectable AFB1 was observed in any of the SLCs-treated groups (LOD = 0.05 μg/kg). These results demonstrate that SLCs not only suppress the growth and proliferation of A. flavus but also effectively block aflatoxin biosynthesis at the metabolic level. The complete absence of detectable AFB1 is consistent with the transcriptional downregulation of aflatoxin biosynthetic genes observed in RNA-seq and qPCR analyses, indicating that SLCs acts at both the transcriptional and metabolic levels. Collectively, these findings support the potential of SLCs as a biocontrol agent against A. flavus contamination in agricultural products, offering a novel and feasible strategy for mycotoxin control. The dual inhibition of gene expression and metabolic flux significantly strengthens the reliability of SLCs for this application.

Aflatoxin biosynthesis pathway

To interpret the transcriptomic data within a biochemical context and identify the specific enzymatic steps targeted by SLCs, we mapped the affected genes onto the aflatoxin biosynthetic pathway. The suppression of key enzymatic steps in the aflatoxin pathway suggests that SLCs interfere with critical metabolic nodes, likely through redox imbalance or cofactor depletion. Given that many of these enzymes are NADPH-dependent, disruption of cellular redox homeostasis may represent a central mechanism underlying this pathway inhibition (Fig. 9).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that B. velezensis B2 effectively inhibits A. flavus growth and aflatoxin production through the production of surfactin-iturin complexes (SLCs). Using DESI-MSI, we revealed the spatiotemporal distribution of these secondary metabolites, uncovering dynamic chemical defense patterns in response to fungal interaction. Transcriptomic analysis further showed that SLC treatment downregulated key aflatoxin biosynthetic genes, resulting in no detectable AFB1. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic evidence for a dual-action biocontrol strategy—combining structural disruption with transcriptional suppression of toxin biosynthesis—and establish a foundation for developing SLC-based interventions to mitigate mycotoxin contamination in crops.
