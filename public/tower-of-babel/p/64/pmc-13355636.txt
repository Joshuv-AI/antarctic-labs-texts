Immunoglobulin Heavy Constant Mu as a Shared Gene in Periodontitis and Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Bioinformatic Analysis
Authors: Hong Yang, Tao Cheng, Bo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsRheumatoid arthritis (RA) and periodontitis (PD) are two inflammatory diseases sharing immunopathogenic features and a common inflammatory environment. This exploratory bioinformatic study aimed to generate hypotheses regarding shared genes between PD and RA.MethodsGene expression omnibus (GEO) datasets for PD and RA were utilized. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to identify modules associated with PD and RA. Differential gene expression analysis was performed to find DEGs, followed by functional enrichment analysis using ‘clusterProfiler’. Machine learning models were applied to screen target genes. Immunoglobulin Heavy Constant Mu (IGHM) expression in clinical blood samples was validated using RT-qPCR and ELISA.ResultsBy intersecting WGCNA module genes with upregulated DEGs, we identified 17 candidate genes for PD and 37 for RA. Three machine learning models (RF, SVM, and GLM) were further applied to screen these candidates. The intersection of the PD and RA candidate genes identified IGHM as a potential shared gene. Notably, both IGHM mRNA and protein exhibited high expression levels in clinical blood samples from patients diagnosed with PD and RA. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that IGHM had potential diagnostic value for PD and RA. GSEA revealed that IGHM was associated with B cell receptor, Th cell differentiation, and NF-kappa B signalling pathways in both diseases. Expression levels of two TFs IRF4 and BHLHE41 were significantly positively correlated with IGHM in both PD and RA. Immune infiltration analysis showed that the adaptive immunity level was significantly higher, innate immunity level lower, and T cell response level lower in high IGHM group compared to low IGHM expression group in both PD and RA.ConclusionIGHM may serve as a potential shared gene of PD and RA and may have potential as a general surrogate plasma/B-cell marker reflecting immune responses in both diseases.Keywords: Periodontitis, Rheumatoid arthritis, IGHM, Immunity

Introduction

Periodontitis (PD) is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects the supporting structures of teeth, and further develops into a systemic disease featuring hyper-inflammation, immune system disruption, microbiota dysbiosis, etc.1 The disease is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of bacterial infection, host immune response, genetic factors, and environmental influences.2 The management of periodontitis typically involves a combination of professional dental cleaning, oral hygiene instruction, and in severe cases, surgical intervention, with the goal of controlling infection and reducing inflammation.3,4 Recent studies have further highlighted the importance of optimized supportive periodontal care protocols and the potential value of adjunctive agents in improving treatment outcomes while reducing adverse effects.5,6 Despite these advances in clinical management, the disease’s progression is fundamentally driven by the host’s immune response. Both adaptive immunity and innate immunity are pivotal in the progression of PD.7 Importantly, the existence of periodontitis can potentially influence the course of other immune system dysregulation diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA).8

RA is characterized by chronic inflammation, and it’s a systemic autoimmune disease.9 Both PD and RA involve chronic inflammatory processes, and autoimmunity also functions in PD.10 PD and RA share common risk factors such as smoking and periodontal bacteria.11 The severity of PD was recognized as the third strongest predictor for RA.12 Patients with RA have been observed to exhibit a higher prevalence of PD,13 and PD treatment has demonstrated a positive impact on reducing the activity of RA.14 Mutuality of these two diseases has been reported in many studies.15,16

The rationale of the present study is that, despite growing evidence linking PD and RA, the specific genes shared between these two diseases remain incompletely understood. Therefore, the aim of this exploratory research is to identify potential shared genes between PD and RA. This endeavour may provide a basis for advancing our understanding of the interplay between oral health and systemic inflammation, potentially contributing to the development of novel candidate targets that may improve the management of these conditions.

Materials and methods

Research object

Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) datasets with accession numbers of GSE16134, GSE10334, and GSE223924 were downloaded for PD analysis. The GSE16134 dataset included 241 PD samples and 69 normal samples. The GSE10334 dataset comprised 183 PD samples and 64 normal samples, while the GSE223924 dataset contained 10 PD samples and 10 normal samples. Normal samples were used as Control group.

Additionally, RA datasets numbered GSE12021, GSE55457, and GSE55235 were downloaded. The GSE12021 dataset consisted of 12 RA samples and 9 normal samples. The GSE55457 dataset includes 13 RA samples and 10 normal samples, and the GSE55235 dataset comprised 10 RA samples and 10 normal samples. Normal samples were used as Control group.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

In this study, all analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1.

The coexpression network analysis was performed utilizing the ‘WGCNA’ package (version 1.72-117) in R. Initially, variance analysis was conducted to filter the top quartile of genes, which were then subjected to WGCNA analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients were determined for all pairs of genes to establish a network. A suitable soft threshold power (β) was chosen to guarantee that the network conformed to the scale-free topology criterion. The gene network was constructed in one step. The adjacency matrix was converted into the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM), which was followed by the creation of a hierarchical clustering dendrogram for the genes. The association significance between gene expression and clinical traits was evaluated. Finally, module-trait association analysis was conducted to identify the gene modules of interest.

Differential gene expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

The ‘limma’ R package18 (version 3.52.4) was employed to carry out an analysis aimed at identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes that exhibited significant differences in expression levels were selected using specific thresholds: a log-transformed fold change with an absolute value greater than 2 (|Log2FC| > 2) and an adjusted P-value that was less than .05 (P.adjust < .05).

After intersecting genes from WGCNA module and DEGs, functional enrichment analysis was performed on the overlapping genes. The R package ‘clusterProfiler’19 (version 4.7.1.2) was utilized for Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, including Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). P-value < .05 was used to filter for significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways

Screening target gene by machine learning models

Machine learning models were used to identify potential candidate genes associated with PD or RA. The R language’s ‘caret’ package (version 6.0-49)20 was utilized to construct three machine learning models: a Random Forest model (RF), a Support Vector Machine model (SVM), and a Generalized Linear Model (GLM). Candidate genes were used as explanatory variables, with the presence or absence of a disease serving as the response variable. All gene expression profiles were standardized (centred and scaled) prior to model input. To optimize model performance, hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search within the ‘caret’ package, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the criterion for selecting the optimal model. Subsequently, the three models were analysed using the interpretation function from the ‘DALEX’ package, and cumulative residual distribution plots were generated to identify the best-performing model. The model with the largest area under the REC curve (Area Over the REC Curve, ARC) – that is, the model whose residuals were most concentrated near zero – was selected as the optimal model. Ultimately, the importance of these genes in predicting the response variable (normal or disease) was analysed.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and disease oncology (GO)

Samples were classified into two groups, the one with target gene’s expression levels higher than the median was high expression group, while the other one was low expression group. GSEA was then performed between these groups, and significantly enriched pathways were identified with a standard of P-value < .05.

Subsequently, differential gene expression analysis between the two groups was carried out using the R package ‘limma’. Based on these DEGs, the ‘DOSE’ package21 (version 3.26.1) in R was utilized for DO analysis.

Transcription factor (TF) binding sites prediction

A total of 1639 TFs were utilized in this research listed in Table S1.22 Based on these TFs, differential expression analysis was performed by criteria of |Log2FC|>1 and P.adjust < .05, obtaining differentially expressed TFs. We accessed the genomic data from the UCSC Genome Browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) to obtain the 3000 base pair (bp) sequences upstream of the transcription start sites of the genes of interest. Following this, we retrieved motif files for various TFs from the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/), which is a repository of DNA binding motifs for eukaryotic TFs. Subsequently, employing the computational tool FIMO (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/fimo), we conducted an analysis to identify the presence of these TF binding motifs within the upstream regulatory regions of the gene promoters.

Analysis of immune landscape

The software CIBERSORT23 was utilized to calculate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types within the samples.

Clinical samples

In this study, a total of 15 clinically diagnosed patients with PD, 15 patients with RA, and 15 healthy controls without any systemic or inflammatory diseases were enrolled. Blood samples were collected from these participants at the Department of Rheumatology, Physical Examination Centre, and Department of Chest Medicine, Zibo First Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the First Hospital of Zibo (YXLL20230710). The clinical characteristics of the enrolled subjects are presented in Table 1.

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA from blood samples was extracted using RNAprep Pure High-Efficiency Blood Total RNA Extraction Kit (DP443, Tiangen Biotech, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Premix Kit (AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology, China). RT-qPCR was conducted using the SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CW3360M, CWBIO, China) on an SLAN 96S real-time PCR instrument (Hongshi, Shanghai, China). The RT-qPCR amplification protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. β-actin served as an internal reference, and the 2−ΔCt method was employed to calculate relative mRNA expression levels. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were collected in tubes and subsequently centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes to isolate the serum. The concentrations of immunoglobulin M (IgM) in the serum were specifically quantified using the Human IgM ELISA Kit (EK176, Multi Sciences, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density (OD) was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Statistical analyses

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was selected to evaluate the disparity in gene expression levels and the infiltration of immune cells across distinct groups. For the assessment of the relationship between variables, the Pearson correlation method was applied using the ‘cor’ function. Statistical significance was determined at the threshold of P < .05.

All experimental data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.0). Differences between groups were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, and a P-value of less than .05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Identifying potential PD-related genes

To identify potential PD-associated genes, WGCNA analysis was conducted using the dataset GSE16134, opting for a soft threshold power β of 12 (Figure 1A) to construct gene networks, which resulted in the identification of 10 gene modules (Figure 1B). The cohort was stratified into PD and normal samples, with sample type serving as the trait data for WGCNA. The correlation between each gene module and the two sample groups was calculated (Figure 1C-D), and the gene module with the smallest P-value associated with PD was selected, namely the blue module, which comprised 759 genes.

Next, a differential gene expression analysis was performed on PD versus normal samples using GSE16134. A total of 20 DEGs were identified in PD samples compared to normal samples, including 18 upregulated genes and 2 downregulated genes (Figure 1E). The intersection of the 759 module genes and the 18 upregulated DEGs (up_DEGs) yielded 17 overlapping genes, which were designated as candidate genes for PD (Figure 1F).

Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 17 candidate genes. Two KEGG pathways were significantly enriched (P-value < .05), including intestinal immune network for IgA production and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Figure 1G). 42 BP terms such as immunoglobulin mediated immune response and B cell mediated immunity; 11 CC terms including immunoglobulin complex, blood microparticle, etc.; and 7 MF terms such as antigen binding were significantly enriched. Top 7 terms of each GO type are shown in Figure 1H and detailed functional enrichment analysis results are presented in Table S2.

Identifying potential RA-related genes

To identify potential RA-associated genes. WGCNA analysis was conducted utilizing the dataset GSE12021, with a soft threshold power β set to 7 (Figure 2A), to construct gene networks, yielding a total of 8 gene modules (Figure 2B). Samples within the cohort were categorized into RA and normal groups, with the sample type serving as the trait data. Correlations between each gene module and the two sample types were calculated (Figure 2C-D), and the gene module with the most significant association with RA, identified as the blue module, was selected, encompassing 632 genes.

After that, a differential gene expression analysis was performed between RA and normal samples based on the GSE12021 dataset. RA samples exhibited 56 DEGs compared to normal samples, comprising 46 upregulated genes and 10 downregulated genes (Figure 2E). By intersecting the 632 genes from the module and the 46 upregulated DEGs (up_DEGs), 37 overlapping genes were obtained, which were identified as candidate genes for RA (Figure 2F).

Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted on the 37 candidate genes. A total of 17 KEGG pathways were found to be significantly enriched (P-value < .05) consisting of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Th17 cell differentiation, etc., along with 211 BP terms such as B cell receptor signalling pathway and antigen receptor-mediated signalling pathway, 15 CC terms including immunoglobulin complex, blood microparticle, etc., and 34 MF terms comprising antigen binding, CXCR chemokine receptor binding, etc. The top 10 significant KEGG pathways and the top 8 significant GO terms are illustrated in Figure 2G-H. Comprehensive results are detailed in Table S3.

IGHM was identified as a shared gene of PD and RA

Based on above candidate genes of PD and RA (Figures 1F and 2F), three machine learning models-RF, SVM, and GLM-were deployed to evaluate their significance in disease prediction. In the context of PD, residual distributions (Figure 3A) and box plots (Figure 3B) for the three models were plotted, indicating that the SVM model displayed optimal predictive performance. Employing the 17 candidate genes as variables, their importance in predicting the response variable (presence or absence of disease) was assessed, revealing that all 17 genes possessed considerable predictive value (Figure 3C-D). These genes may serve as potential candidate biomarkers for PD.

Similarly, in RA, the same three machine learning models were used to assess the significance of 37 candidate genes in disease prediction. The ‘DALEX’ package was employed to plot residual distributions (Figure 3E) and box plots (Figure 3F) for the models in RA, with the SVM model once more showing the best predictive performance. The predictive importance of the 37 genes in relation to the response variable was evaluated, identifying CXCL9, AIM2, IGHM, CXCL10, and CXCL13 as having significant predictive value (Figure 3G-H). In PD, as training sample size increased, validation accuracy reached above 0.95 and plateaued. The training accuracy consistently ranged from 0.9 to 0.975, with a gap of less than 0.05 from validation accuracy that did not widen with increasing samples. These results indicate stable model performance without significant overfitting (Figure S1A). The same trend was observed in the RA dataset (Figure S1B). These genes may serve as potential candidate biomarkers for RA. The intersection of the 17 PD-associated candidate genes and the 5 RA-associated candidate genes revealed IGHM as a common candidate gene shared between the two diseases (Figure 3I).

Subsequently, the expression of IGHM was validated in PD and RA versus normal samples. In PD, IGHM expressed significantly higher in PD samples compared to normal samples in GSE16134, GSE10334, and GSE223924 datasets (Figure 4A-C). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, which evaluated the ability of IGHM to distinguish PD patients from healthy controls, yielded area under the curve values of 0.886, 0.862, and 0.96 (Figure 4D). In RA, expression levels of IGHM were also significantly higher in RA than normal samples in GSE12021, GSE55457, and GSE55235 datasets (Figure 4E-G). ROC analysis, assessing the ability of IGHM to distinguish RA patients from healthy controls, produced AUC values of 0.954, 0.938, and 1 (Figure 4H). Moreover, we collected clinical blood samples from patients with PD, AR, and healthy subjects, and analysed IGHM expression using RT-qPCR and ELISA. The results demonstrated that both mRNA and protein expression levels of IGHM were significantly elevated in the PD and AR samples compared to those from healthy controls (Figure 4I-J).

Potential functional pathways of IGHM in PD and RA

Building upon the identification of IGHM as a shared gene in both PD and RA, GSEA was conducted on PD and RA samples from the GSE16134 and GSE12021 cohorts to explore potential functional pathways. Samples were stratified into high and low expression groups based on the median expression level of IGHM (IGHMhigh and IGHMlow). Enrichment analysis was then performed between these groups.

In PD, the GSEA revealed that 63 pathways were significantly enriched in IGHMhigh group compared to IGHMlow group (P.adjust < .05). Notably, the B cell receptor signalling pathway, Chemokine signalling pathway, N-Glycan biosynthesis, NF-kappa B signalling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation were among the pathways significantly enriched (Figure 5A). A differential expression analysis with criteria of |Log2FC| > 1 and P-value < .05 was followed by DO analysis, which identified 271 significantly enriched diseases such as primary immunodeficiency disease. The top 20 of these enriched diseases are illustrated in Figure 5B.

GSEA and DO analysis. A, C, GSEA analysis of PD and RA. B, D, DO analysis of PD and RA.

In RA, 43 pathways were significantly enriched in IGHMhigh group compared to IGHMlow group (P.adjust < .05). The pathways enriched by GSEA included the IL-17 signalling pathway, NF-kappa B signalling pathway, Rheumatoid arthritis, T cell receptor signalling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation (Figure 5C). A similar differential expression analysis followed by DO enrichment analysis identified 131 significantly enriched diseases such as primary immunodeficiency disease, with the top 20 diseases visualized in Figure 5D. The detailed results of these enrichment analyses are presented in Table S4.

Upstream regulation of IGHM in PD and RA

To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms that govern the expression of IGHM, we proceeded to investigate its regulatory mechanisms by examining the relationship between differentially expressed TFs and IGHM expression.

In PD and normal samples, we identified 11 differentially expressed TFs based on the criteria of |Log2FC| > 1 and P.adjust < .05 (Figure 6A). Utilizing the GSE16134 dataset, we calculated the correlation between the mRNA expression levels of these 11 TFs and the IGHM gene. Applying the criteria (P < .05 and |correlation| > .5), we found that 10 TFs exhibited significant correlation with IGHM expression levels. Expression levels of six TFs including IRF4, XBP1, ZNF275, MEF2C, BHLHE41, ZNF215 were significantly positively correlated with IGHM, and four TFs including RORA, FOXP2, HLF, FOXN1 were significantly negatively correlated with IGHM (Figure 6B). A network graph depicting the interaction between these TFs and IGHM was constructed using Cytoscape and is presented in Figure 6C.

Association of IGHM with the immune microenvironment in PD and RA

Understanding the immunological microenvironment is crucial for deciphering the complex interactions between immune cells and disease pathology. Given the pivotal role of immune dysregulation in both PD and RA, we aimed to investigate the immunological landscape associated with the IGHM gene expression.

Employing the CIBERSORT algorithm, we calculated the relative abundance of 22 immune cell infiltrates in PD samples from the GSE16134 cohort. The differences in immune cell infiltration between IGHMhigh and IGHMlow groups were analysed. The results indicated significant differences in naive B cells, memory B cells, plasma cells, and 14 other immune cell types between IGHMhigh and IGHMlow groups (Figure 8A). Further analysis of the Pearson correlation between IGHM and the significantly different immune cell types revealed a significant negative correlation with memory B cells, resting dendritic cells, activated dendritic cells, macrophages M1, macrophages M2, follicular helper T cells, T cells gamma delta, activated mast cells, naive CD4 T cells, activated NK cells, monocytes and CD8 T cells, and a significant positive correlation with naive B cells, plasma cells (Figure 8B). Additional findings highlighted significant higher adaptive immunity level and lower innate immunity level in IGHMhigh than IGHMlow group (Figure 8C). The level of T cell responses was significantly lower in IGHMhigh compared to IGHMlow group (Figure 8D).

Due to the limited number of RA samples, we pooled RA samples from the GSE12021, GSE55457, and GSE55235 cohorts. Then we calculated the relative abundance of the 22 immune cell infiltrates in the merged cohort of RA samples. The analysis showed significantly higher level of T cells gamma delta, Macrophages M1 in IGHMhigh compared to IGHMlow group, and significantly lower level of resting mast cells in IGHMhigh compared to IGHMlow group (Figure 9A). Correspondingly, further analysis of the Pearson correlation between IGHM and the significantly different immune cell types demonstrated a significant negative correlation with resting mast cells and a significant positive correlation with T cells gamma delta and Macrophages M1 (Figure 9B). RA samples revealed same results with PD samples that the adaptive immunity level was significantly higher and innate immunity level was significantly lower in IGHMhigh than IGHMlow group (Figure 9C). Also, the level of T cell responses was significantly lower in IGHMhigh compared to IGHMlow group (Figure 9D).

Discussion

Notings on the association between oral health and inflammatory joint diseases date back millennia, with the ancient philosopher Hippocrates proposing that extracting teeth might alleviate arthritis symptoms.24 Nowadays, the close interconnection between PD and RA is still of great interest. Specifically, through WGCNA analysis combined with differential expression screening and three machine learning models (RF, SVM, GLM), we identified IGHM as a common candidate gene shared between PD and RA. Validation in multiple independent datasets and clinical samples confirmed that IGHM expression was significantly upregulated in both diseases. ROC analysis yielded AUC values >0.85, suggesting that IGHM may have potential diagnostic value for distinguishing PD or RA patients from healthy controls. These findings position IGHM as a potential shared molecular feature of PD and RA.

IGHM, also known as the immunoglobulin heavy constant mu (IgM), is a crucial component of the human immune system. It is one part of the immunoglobulin M class of antibodies, which are pentameric in structure and play a significant role in the primary immune response.25 IgM antibodies, generated by plasma cells, are the first to be produced in response to an antigen and are particularly effective at agglutination (clumping together) and activating the complement system, which enhances the ability to clear pathogens.26 In this study, plasma cells were significantly positively correlated to IGHM expression level in PD. The humoral immune response, particularly involving IgG and IgA, is recognized for its protective role in the development of PD. However, a deeper analysis of the affected tissues revealed an increased presence of plasma cells that expressed IgM and a decreased presence of those expressing IgA. This pattern of immune response more closely resembles what is typically observed in systemic immune reactions.27 In a study of four distinct stages of PD, IgM has been identified as a detector for severe PD.28 IGHM expression level was detected to be valuable in predicting RA.29 Peripheral blood plasma B cells derived from patients with RA exhibited elevated levels of IGHM expression.30 Our result displayed higher infiltration of plasma cells in IGHMhigh compared to IGHMlow group in RA, suggesting that IGHM expression may be closely associated with plasma cell infiltration in both PD and RA.

Beyond plasma cells, we further explored the signalling pathways associated with IGHM. By GSEA analysis, some signalling pathways such as B cell receptor, Th cell differentiation, and NF-kappa B signalling pathways were found to function associated with IGHM in PD and RA. B cells play a critical role in both diseases. In PD, B cell activating factor functions may influence macrophage polarization.31 In RA, pathogenic B cells and M1 macrophages are abnormally increased, and B cell plays a vital role in disease pathogenesis.32 In RA, B cell receptor signal is needed for B cell activation,32 and is involved in autoreactive B cell development.33 Notably, IgM is one of the earliest isotypes expressed on the BCR and is coexpressed with IgD on naïve B cells.34 Furthermore, mature naïve autoreactive B cells have been suggested to be a potential source of RA pathogenesis.35 Consistently, in the present study, we observed that naïve B cell abundance was higher in the IGHM-high group than in the IGHM-low group in both RA and PD. Collectively, these findings suggest that IGHM, as the gene encoding the IgM heavy chain, is associated with BCR signalling, and may be linked to naïve B cell infiltration in both diseases. Notably, BCR signalling is known to activate the downstream NF-κB pathway, which plays a central role in inflammation.36 NF-κB is essential in chronic inflammatory diseases including PD.37 It has been proven to be highly activated in chronic PD patients than healthy controls based on immunohistochemical analysis.38 The NF-κB signalling pathway is also extensively implicated as a crucial factor in the pathogenesis of RA.39 Suppressing the NF-κB signalling pathway leads to apoptosis, inhibition of proliferation and angiogenesis in human synovial fibroblast-like cells in RA.40 Inoue et al41 found that stimulation of chicken DT40 B cells with an antiIgM monoclonal antibody significantly increased nuclear NF-κB activity. This finding indicates that IgM can initiate downstream signalling cascades upon antigen recognition, thereby activating the NF-κB pathway, suggesting a potential functional link between IGHM and the NF-κB pathway. However, this hypothesis requires further validation through subsequent functional experiments.

Expression levels of IRF4 were significantly positively correlated with IGHM in both PD and RA. Interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) is a shared susceptibility factor in PD and RA.42 IRF4 involves protein synthesis in plasma cells and development of B cells and plasma cells.43 It has been found to be upregulated in PD and COVID19.44 IRF4 was deduced to be a potential pharmacodynamic biomarker for the clinical assessment of IFNβ therapy in RA patients.45 Consistent with these findings, our immune analysis revealed that the IGHMhigh group exhibited significantly higher adaptive immunity levels and lower innate immunity levels in both PD and RA compared to the IGHMlow group. This pattern may suggest similarities between the immune response to periodontal pathogens in PD and the autoimmune response in RA.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the absence of PD-RA comorbid cohorts in public transcriptomic databases, we were unable to directly validate the expression characteristics of IGHM in patients with both diseases. Future studies should collect clinical samples and transcriptomic data from PD-RA comorbid patients to validate the expression characteristics of IGHM in the comorbid state. Second, the present study is based on computational predictions using public data. We acknowledge that inferring causality between IGHM expression and immune cell abundance is not possible based on our current data, as immune analysis only reveals correlation. Meanwhile, all transcription factor and functional pathway analyses are exploratory analyses. Future in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, such as ChIP-seq, dual-luciferase reporter assays, gene knockdown/overexpression cell models, and animal models, are needed to determine whether IGHM directly influences immune infiltration and to validate regulation of IGHM by upstream transcription factors and the effects of IGHM on downstream signalling pathways. Third, our clinical validation samples exhibited demographic imbalances in age and sex distribution. Additionally, the sample size was small, and potential confounding factors (eg, age and sex) were not statistically adjusted, limiting the support of blood validation for tissue findings. Furthermore, although periodontitis can lead to prosthetic damage and increased failure rates, this study did not include clinical samples from periodontitis patients with prosthetic impairment for validation; therefore, the association between IGHM expression and this clinical outcome remains to be confirmed. Moreover, there is a biological mismatch between the discovery and validation stages. The GEO datasets were derived from disease-relevant tissues, while validation was performed in peripheral blood. Consequently, blood-based findings may not fully reflect tissue-specific mechanisms. Future studies should expand sample sizes, perform clinical feature stratification (including age and sex subgroup analyses), incorporate longitudinal follow-up data, and include larger, well-matched cohorts with tissue-specific validation to address these limitations.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this exploratory study is the first to systematically integrate WGCNA, machine learning, and immune infiltration analysis to identify IGHM as a shared gene in PD and RA with diagnostic potential. Some signalling pathways such as B cell receptor, Th cell differentiation, and NF-kappa B signalling pathways, were found to be associated with IGHM in PD and RA. Additionally, IGHM may play a role in adaptive immunity in the two diseases, likely reflecting B-cell/plasma-cell-related immune activity. As an immunoglobulin gene, elevated IGHM expression in inflammatory tissues is expected. Although IGHM may not provide specific information beyond general plasma-/B-cell markers, its expression is closely linked to plasma cell infiltration and naïve B cell infiltration, suggesting that IGHM may serve as a general surrogate plasma/B-cell marker reflecting immune responses.
