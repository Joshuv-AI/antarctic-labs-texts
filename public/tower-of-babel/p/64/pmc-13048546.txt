The broad societal value of pembrolizumab for women’s cancer in Canada
Authors: Elizabeth Brook, Karl Patterson, Nishini Wijenayake, Matthew Madin-Warburton, Kathryn Mishkin, Marie-Claude Meilleur, Annabelle Davies, Kate Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe impact of women’s cancers is multifaceted, with broad societal and economic consequences. Health technology assessments often rely solely on costs and outcomes directly relevant to the healthcare system. Our study incorporates the additional and novel value elements across three perspectives for pembrolizumab in four women’s cancers in a Canadian setting.Patients and methodsWe analyzed the net monetary benefit (NMB) of pembrolizumab-based treatments of early-stage triple-negative breast cancer, metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, microsatellite instability-high endometrial cancer, and cervical cancer from three perspectives: traditional payer perspective (TPP), traditional societal perspective (TSP), and broad societal perspective (BSP). Indications and comparators were modeled independently, combined, and weighted by prevalence.ResultsPembrolizumab-based therapy generated an NMB over four times greater with the BSP (Canadian dollars, CAD$925,078), compared with TPP (CAD$226,090) and TSP (CAD$222,556). The largest driver of results was the inclusion of insurance value. Results excluding insurance value still generated an NMB of CAD$484,384, more than twice the TPP.ConclusionsBroadening the perspective to include additional value elements considerably increased the overall value of treatment of women’s cancer compared with the TPP, indicating that the perspective used by health technology bodies may not fully capture the societal value of therapeutics.

Introduction

Globally, more than 2 million women per year are diagnosed with breast or gynecological cancers, with incidence rates continuing to rise [1]. Female breast cancer (BC) accounted for 11.6% of all cancers diagnosed in 2022 and was the second most common cancer worldwide [2,3]. BC is categorized into different subtypes, and of interest to this research is triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), specifically early-stage triple-negative breast cancer (eTNBC) and metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC). Gynecologic cancer consists of five main types of cancers that originate from a woman’s reproductive organs, including cervical cancer (CC) and endometrial cancer (EC). Globally, in 2022 CC and EC were the fourth and sixth most common cancers in women, respectively [4,5]. This study is conducted from a Canadian perspective, where BC is the most common cancer in women, accounting for 25% of new cases and 14% of all cancer deaths in 2024 [6]. In 2024, EC accounted for 7% of new cancer diagnoses in Canadian women, CC made up 1.3% of new cases and 1.1% of all cancer deaths [7].

The economic impact of women’s cancer is substantial, particularly the impact on productivity in terms of employment. Canadian employment statistics show that the labor force participation rate for women aged > 15 years-old has consistently increased since 1990. With a 61.6% participation rate for women in 2023, women represented 47.5% of employed Canadians in 2023 [8]. Reflecting global trends, both the employment rate and the incidence of BC diagnosis in all women aged 20–54 has increased in Canada in recent years [9,10]. Specifically for Canadian women over the age of 55, the employment rate was 30.3% in June 2024, which was a large increase compared with 16.1% in June 1990 [11]. This age group also coincides with the same age group for which both BC and EC risk increases substantially [12,13].

The rising incidence and burden of women’s cancers on both the patient and wider society highlight the vast psychological, financial, and societal impact that women’s cancer diagnosis has; as well as a need for access to innovative therapeutic interventions, which will have far-reaching societal implications beyond simply improving individual patient health. These include extending life to allow more time to receive other novel therapies (real option value [ROV]), providing a value of hope (VoH) of long-term survival, and mediating the risks associated with diagnosis for the healthy population (insurance value). To incentivize the development of treatments that provide societal benefits, it is crucial for cost-effectiveness analyses to capture the full value of these interventions to society.

Cost-effectiveness analyses, undertaken by health technology assessment (HTA) bodies to determine the value of new treatments, are typically conducted from a traditional payer perspective (TPP). This primarily considers traditional payer costs and outcomes, which includes viewpoints of the patient directly related to the immediate issue, hospital or clinic, and healthcare system (e.g., direct medical costs) [14]. However, the impact of cancer is extensive and often goes beyond both just the patient and immediate healthcare system interaction [15]. Studies have shown that cancer diagnosis affects not only the female patient directly, but also their families, with additional expenses incurred related to both medical and non-medical costs [16]. As women hold multiple important social roles, research has demonstrated that when one woman is affected by cancer, their community and wider society are burdened [17]. If evaluations focus solely on the cost and benefits to payers, there may be an under estimation of the true impact of treatment as well as a disincentive for companies to create products that generate substantial societal benefits not directly accruable to payers. This could ultimately result in reduced societal welfare.

Previous research by the Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) produced a Special Task Force Report [3] identifying additional and novel elements of value to be considered in cost-effectiveness analyses that broaden the perspective beyond the TPP [18]. The report defines the broad societal perspective (BSP) as 12 “elements of value” illustrated in a “value flower,” consisting of core elements, which form the basis of value assessments in cost-effectiveness analyses and are most frequently used, common but inconsistently used elements, and novel elements.

Research by Shafrin et al. conducted a similar study in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), investigating how the BSP impacted cost-effectiveness by using the elements identified in the ISPOR report [19]. They found that broadening the perspective of analysis led to greater net benefit to society. More recently, the Generalized Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (GCEA) framework has been proposed as an extension of the value flower and it considers 15 elements of value categorized by uncertainty, dynamics, beneficiary, and additional value components [20].

This case study aims to quantify how the BSP influences cost-effectiveness of new interventions in four women’s cancers in which pembrolizumab has received recommendations for use in Canada: eTNBC, mTNBC, metastatic microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) EC, and CC. These four indications were chosen primarily due to the policy progress in these disease areas and the reimbursement status of pembrolizumab, either as a monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy as treatment. By doing so, this study seeks to provide further evidence to inform healthcare decision-making and optimize resource allocation, by providing a foundation for exploring further research into incorporating broad societal elements of value into HTA decision-making processes.

Methods

This study was based on previously collected, anonymized clinical trial data provided by the study sponsor who received ethical approval to conduct the individual studies; as such the authors were not required to obtain additional ethical approval nor was consent from participants required. Each trial was approved by the appropriate ethics body at each participating center.

Case study

To quantify the impact of broadening the perspective on cost-effectiveness analyses, pembrolizumab-based therapies for eTNBC, mTNBC, EC, and CC were investigated as a case study. These diseases were chosen based on the availability of pembrolizumab-based therapies and the policy progress made in these areas. Pembrolizumab is a programmed cell death receptor 1 inhibitor for advanced malignancies, with regulatory approval from the US, Canadian, and European regulatory authorities for all four women’s cancers of interest in this analysis.

Four constituent cost-effectiveness models, which had been previously reviewed by multiple HTA bodies including Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) and Institut national d’excellence en santé et services sociaux (INESSS) in Canada, were adapted to include the additional elements of value. They were also updated to include the most recently published Canadian inputs, wherever available, to inform the Canadian payer perspective. All costs in the four economic models were inflated to 2023 Canadian dollars (CAD$); and a base case discount rate of 1.5% per annum was applied for both quality adjusted life years (QALYs) and costs, in line with CDA-AMC guidelines [21]. A Microsoft Excel® based tool was developed to extract and combine results from these models. Further details are provided in Supplementary Appendix S1. The aim of this analysis was to expand on these existing models and provide foundational research for the impact of broadening the analysis perspective on results.

A three-state partitioned survival model was used for mTNBC, with data from KEYNOTE-355, and EC, with data from KEYNOTE-158 [22–25]. The CC model used a three-state semi-Markov state transition model with data from KEYNOTE-826 [26,27]. The eTNBC model used a four-state Markov model structure with data from KEYNOTE-522 [28,29]. The health states for the eTNBC model comprised event-free (EF), locoregional recurrence (LR), distant metastasis (DM), and death. For the three remaining indications, the health states consisted of progression-free (PF), progressed disease (PD), and death. Detailed information on each of the model structures is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1.

The cost-effectiveness models were adapted, without altering their original transition probabilities or parameters, by adding the new value elements to capture their effects on both costs and outcomes. These new costs and outcomes were imported into the analytic tool, which serves as the central controller. The analytic tool reruns the models with the added societal and broader societal value elements, extracts the results by indication, and aggregates them with prevalence-based weighting.

Three perspectives were explored: TPP, which aligns with the perspectives adopted for HTA approval by including direct net costs and QALYs gained; the traditional societal perspective (TSP) which adds productivity costs; and BSP. A targeted literature review was completed to identify the methodology and inputs required to quantify the TSP and BSP. In the first instance, inputs specific to the four indications of interest were sought. If disease-specific inputs were not available, general cancer-specific inputs were preferred over non-cancer inputs.

The final list of novel elements of value within the BSP consisted of productivity, caregiver burden, insurance value, VoH, ROV, severity of disease, out of pocket (OOP) costs, and fertility treatment costs. There were elements of value identified in both the ISPOR report and GCEA framework which were excluded in the analysis due to paucity of data for parameterization, these include scientific spillovers, reduction in uncertainty, and equity.

Traditional payer perspective

The TPP consists of direct medical costs to the payer, including the provincial bodies in Canada, and benefits for the patient covered by that payer. These include costs related to treatment (list prices), administration, treatment-related resource use, and adverse events. Treatment benefits captured by this perspective included survival improvements, decreases in morbidity, changes in adverse event rates, and the preservation of quality of life (QoL). These benefits were synthesized into a QALY measure. The existing cost-effectiveness models were built with the TPP as the base case.

Traditional societal perspective

In addition to the elements included as part of the TPP, the TSP incorporates the cost associated with productivity losses as three of the constituent models (eTNBC, mTNBC, and EC) already had the option to include these costs in analysis. Productivity losses were estimated using the human capital approach which places a monetary value on loss of health as the lost value of economic productivity due to ill health, disability, or premature mortality. Productivity was further categorized into work impairment costs and home impairment costs.

Work impairment costs (labor force participation) were considered in working age patients and were therefore capped to zero at average age of retirement, which was 64.2 years-old in Canada [30]. Work impairment costs per week were estimated by combining the number of hours in a standard work week (33.3 hours) [31], hourly wage (CAD$31.19), and the proportion of hours lost per week due to illness for patients in each health state [31,32]. Costs were applied based on the distribution of patients by health state in each weekly cycle, until the cohort reached retirement age.

For home impairment, the value of “home production” was sourced from literature as the average hourly earnings of female personal care and service occupation workers, adjusted for fringe benefits, and multiplied by 8 hours per day. The number of days lost due to illness was multiplied by the number of “days lost per woman” [33,34].

The costs were categorized by the number of years since diagnosis (<2, 2–4, 5–10, ≥11 years) and age group (18–44 years, 45–64 years, ≥65 years), following the same methodology and categories used in the source study [34]. The cost per week for each age group and years since diagnosis (Year 0) were calculated and applied to the proportion of patients in the respective health state. The cost per health state was assumed to be identical due to the paucity of data by health state.

Broad societal elements of value and associated definition.

Caregiver burden

Caregiver costs

Caregiver costs were estimated using the opportunity cost of caregiver time and the value of work hours lost due to providing care [41]. Data sourced from literature reported that 56% of caregivers of women with advanced BC were in full- or part-time employment, and also reported the hours of work lost by caregivers in the preceding 4 weeks of the palliative care period, and the preceding 2 weeks of the terminal care period [42]. For each cost-effectiveness model, the caregiver work loss was estimated per cycle by health state. The least-progressed health states (PF in mTNBC, EC, and CC; EF and LR in eTNBC) were informed by the mean caregiver work loss during palliative care. The most progressed health states (PD in mTNBC, EC, and CC; DM in eTNBC) were informed by the mean caregiver work loss during the terminal care stage. The number of hours lost was combined by the value of production per hour and the proportion of carers in full- or part-time work, and then applied to the proportion of patients in each health state.

Caregiver QoL

Caregiver QoL was considered as disutility per cycle by health state in each cost-effectiveness model. A previous study explored the correlation between caregiver and BC patient QoL, finding a weak but statistically significant correlation at the 5% significance level between the CarerQoL visual analog scale score and patients’ EQ-5D-5L questionnaire utility score (0.301, p = 0.002) [43]. Equivalent to caregiver costs, patient disutility was also estimated by health state by calculating the difference between general population utility and health state utility. A caregiver weight (0.301; assuming the correlation coefficient is a reasonable estimation of a beta coefficient) was applied to patient disutility to estimate the caregiver disutility by health state, which was then adjusted for the proportion of patients with a caregiver by health state.

The proportion of work hours lost per week for caregivers, derived from the caregiver costing calculation, was used to inform the proportion of caregivers in the base case. Using this method, the proportions were 0% for EF, 5% for LR and PF, and 17% for DM and PD. Additionally, research has shown that there is at least one caregiver for every cancer survivor [44]. A scenario was conducted assuming that 100% of patients had a caregiver in the most progressed health states (DM for eTNBC, PD for mTNBC, EC and CC).

Insurance value

Due to the lack of women’s cancer-specific values, the impact of insurance value was estimated by exploring two sources, from two different indications. The first derived the value of new interventions to patients with multiple sclerosis and to individuals who face the risk of developing multiple sclerosis in the future [45]. This study estimated the ratio between the value of treatment to healthy people versus sick people and found that the insurance value ratio was 0.81 (8.9 billion vs 11.1 billion United States dollars), meaning that the value of treatment to the healthy was 81% relative to the value to the sick. The second study conducted in lung cancer estimated the value that healthy individuals placed on generous insurance coverage for treating serious disease, and the surplus value of generous insurance coverage above and beyond the traditional value [46]. In this study, insurance value was calculated as a ratio of generous insurance to the healthy versus the surplus value (the value to the healthy deducted by the value to the sick), resulting in an insurance value ratio of 9.78.

For this study, the insurance value was used to inflate the TPP QALYs, to represent the potential value for healthy people rather than just those with the disease. The more conversative ratio of 0.81 was used in the base case analysis [45], and the higher estimate of 9.78 was explored in a scenario [46].

Value of hope

VoH is the difference between the patient certainty equivalent in terms of QALYs and the incremental QALY gain between the intervention and the comparator. The certainty equivalent (the QALYs a patient would need to obtain to be indifferent between the intervention and comparator) was estimated by indication. To estimate the certainty equivalent, a constant relative risk aversion utility function for QALYs (x) is assumed; ux=xη. A risk premium parameter > 1 implies a patient is risk-loving. A higher risk premium is associated with a lower certainty equivalent. The resulting VoH was added to the TPP QALYs.

As no women’s cancer-specific studies to inform VoH were identified, previous literature, which used survey data to estimate a risk premium of 1.39 for NSCLC and 1.27 for melanoma, were used as the inputs for this case study [47]. In the base case, the risk premium for NSCLC was selected as it was the more conservative estimate, and the risk premium for melanoma was explored in a scenario.

Real option value (ROV)

For this case study, ROV of an intervention is expressed as the survival benefits of the innovation multiplied by the expected future improvements in medical technology [48]. As no women’s cancer-specific studies were identified to inform ROV, data for the ROV percentage was sourced from the literature in three indications: renal cell carcinoma, non-squamous NSCLC and squamous NSCLC [49]. In this study, by using conventional analyses researchers found that the intervention increased life expectancy by 6.3, 7.5, and 4.5 months in RCC, squamous NSCLC and non-squamous NSCLC, respectively. However, the incorporation of future additional innovations into analysis led to an additional 1.2, 0.4, and 0.5 months of life expectancy gain in the three indications respectively. These resulting values of ROV were 5%, 10%, and 18% for squamous NSCLC, non-squamous NSCLC, and renal cell carcinoma, respectively. The most conservative estimate of 5% ROV was selected for the base case analysis and was multiplied by the number of estimated patient QALYs. Patient QALYs consisted of the TPP QALYs and value of hope QALYs. This was carried out for each indication and the resultant value was the number of QALYs associated with ROV.

Severity of disease

A severity modifier was calculated according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Methods Manual (2022) [50]. The quality-adjusted life expectancy (QALE) without the disease was estimated using the Canadian life tables [51] and utility values from health-related quality of life norms (the Health Survey England 2017–2018 EQ-5D-5L questionnaire mapped to EQ-5D-3L questionnaire using the Hernandez Alava et al. algorithm, in the absence of available Canadian inputs) [52]. Patient characteristics used in the analysis were consistent with those informing the original cost-effectiveness analysis. The QALE with disease is equal to the comparator QALYs derived in the TPP.

The selected severity modifier was the greater weight produced by either the absolute or proportional QALY shortfall calculation. Out of pocket (OOP) expenses

In this analysis, OOP expenses were considered as part of the BSP for this reason and may be categorized as non-medical expenses incurred by patient directly.

OOP expenses incurred in the first year after diagnosis were sourced from an existing Canadian study and were included as a one-off cost in the first model cycle [40]. Due to the unavailability of indication- and treatment-specific inputs, the same OOP cost of CAD$1,002 was applied for all four indications and treatments.

Fertility costs

Approximately 50% of women aged ≤ 40 years in developed countries currently diagnosed with BC have not yet completed their families [53]. The most commonly used ovarian preservation options for patients with BC in Canada were ovarian suppression, embryo freezing, egg freezing, and ovarian tissue freezing [53]. The costs for each of these treatments were considered, and where a range of costs were reported the midpoint was used. Fertility costs were only considered in patient populations of reproductive age, therefore a maximum age of 42 years-old for initiating fertility treatment was implemented; based on the maximum age for government funded fertility treatment from two of the largest provinces in Canada, Ontario and Quebec [54,55].

The proportion of patients undergoing fertility treatment was sourced from literature. Survey data from the US showed that 48% of female cancer patients had taken steps to lower the chance of infertility related to cancer treatment either before or during treatment after diagnosis [56]. This study also surveyed patients undergoing different types of fertility treatments. These statistics were used to inform the proportions of patients undergoing ovarian suppression (64.10%) treatment, egg (33.33%), and embryo (20.51%) freezing (Table 3). No studies reporting the proportion of patients undergoing ovarian tissue freezing were found; therefore, this was assumed to be the same as egg freezing (20.51%).

Fertility treatment costs were included as a separate cost, as part of the BSP, to provide a comprehensive view of their broader societal impact. While these are medical costs, they do not fall under the category of traditional medical costs, such as those outlined in Section 2.2 which were included in the TPP. Instead, fertility costs represent a unique aspect of healthcare expenses with substantial implications for societal well-being resulting from women’s cancer. Arguably, changes in fertility (linked to chemotherapy treatment) would also impact a patients QoL, but this impact has conservatively not been captured here, as the health-related QoL inputs were informed by the clinical trials, which used EQ-5D, which is not expected to capture the potential impact of treatments on utility.

Net monetary benefit calculation

The quantitative outcome of this case study is an overall NMB, which characterizes the value of a treatment in monetary terms. The NMB uses the willingness to pay (WTP) threshold, set at CAD$100,000 per QALY gained, and multiplies it by the incremental treatment benefit (Equation 1). A positive incremental NMB indicates that the intervention is cost-effective when compared with the comparator at the given WTP threshold [57].

NMB was aggregated across all four indications to present an overall population NMB. To aggregate results across the different women’s cancers, incremental costs and QALYs were weighted by indication prevalence using Canadian epidemiology data: eTNBC, 68.13%; mTNBC, 8.20%; EC, 6.25%; CC, 17.34% (Supplementary Appendix Table S6).

A standard of care (SoC) comparator was used to formulate the overall NMB. SoC comprised a weighted average of the comparators by indication based on market share. For the eTNBC and CC models the comparator of chemotherapy mix was aligned to the relevant trials. For mTNBC and EC, market share data were sourced from CDA-AMC reimbursement recommendations (Supplementary Appendix Table S5) [58,59].

The base case analysis presents probabilistic results; this aligns with methodology used in HTA guidance for health economic modeling, including CDA-AMC [60]. Probabilistic analysis involved randomly sampling all parameters that were subject to uncertainty in the model from their assigned probability, sampling was conducted simultaneously to ensure consistency between sampled values across the four models.

Sensitivity analyses

Scenario analysis to explore both structural and methodological uncertainty was undertaken. Eleven different scenarios were run in the model to compare the impact of adding societal elements to the TPP. These scenarios included testing alternative data inputs or assumptions for various elements of value, such as insurance value and VoH.

Results

Overall population

The base case analysis for the TPP perspective yielded an NMB of CAD$226,090 compared with CAD$222,556 for TSP and CAD$925,078 for BSP. The BSP generated a NMB over four times greater than the TPP at WTP threshold of CAD$100,000. Although, the QALY gain from the TSP is the same as TPP, the TSP yields a lower NMB than the TPP because of the increased costs resulting from the inclusion of productivity loss for long-term survivors receiving pembrolizumab. Discussion

Women’s cancers impact not only the patients – their families, caregivers, and wider communities are also affected and suffer as a consequence [61]. High morbidity and high mortality associated with illness continue to persist in the four indications of interest despite advancements in prevention and treatment.

The results from our case study showed that broadening the analysis perspective has important implications on the overall value of treatment, with a similar impact in all four areas of cancer. Specifically, the inclusion of broader societal elements of value resulted in a substantially higher NMB of CAD$925,078, which is CAD$698,988 more than the TPP (CAD$226,090). This research highlights that the TPP commonly used for HTAs likely does not capture the full impact and value of new therapies, especially within a single payer system. This research is also the first to consider fertility costs within the BSP. The influence of these novel elements on broader value should also be considered within the context of other recent and emerging therapeutic options for women’s cancers.

In practice, the CDA-AMC requires the TPP to be presented as the base case analysis in all submissions; however, as of March 2024 CDA-AMC announced that any submissions as part of the complex review process will be required to include the societal perspective [62]. Example elements include time off for treatment, productivity loss from being unable to work, and costs to other government areas.

L’Institut national d’excellence en santé et en services sociaux (INESSS), another Canadian HTA body, evaluates the TSP by including productivity losses [63]. These advancements demonstrate that HTA bodies are moving toward a broader perspective for analysis and emphasize the need for methodology which accurately captures these additional benefits. This research is particularly pertinent to these integrated markets and provides a good foundation for how to incorporate these additional elements of value to capture the wider costs and outcomes associated with new innovations, though also highlights a paucity of data and need for additional research. Although HTA agencies and departments of health are budget constrained, this research provides evidence to support additional arguments that novel treatments could provide value beyond that traditionally measured with the cost-effectiveness framework.

A key challenge of this study was the paucity of disease-specific inputs and resulting generalizability across populations. A targeted literature review was conducted before this study to inform the inputs for each element of value identified from the ISPOR Special Task Force Report. The paucity of data to inform quantitative inputs was not surprising given that, to date, there has been relatively little research into modeling the broader societal value of therapeutics. For most elements of value, the inputs were assumed identical across indications due to a lack of disease-specific inputs identified. These elements of value include caregiver cost burden and caregiver QoL, insurance value, VoH, ROV, OOP expenses, and fertility costs. Relying on literature from other disease areas and applying it to women’s cancer may introduce bias and reduce the interpretability of results, as these inputs may not accurately reflect the disease’s direct impact on patients. Although this is acknowledged as a limitation of this research, notable effort was made to ensure that inputs were a relevant proxy to the indications for interest. For example, the majority of the input sources were from oncological studies to ensure similarity. Despite the paucity of data, this study demonstrates clear benefits of including societal elements. If HTA agencies were to consider these elements in their decision making, it may create demand for better evidence and prompt future research to fill current gaps.

The model does have limitations regarding the implementation of the novel values: Insurance value was the greatest driver of NMB and was influenced heavily by the source used to inform this input. The study used to inform the base case insurance value estimated that the ratio between the value of treatments to healthy people and the value of treatments to sick people was 81%. This ratio was then used to adjust the total QALYs gained to represent the potential value for healthy people rather than just those with the disease. In other terms, the QALYs associated with insurance value in this analysis account for the QALY gain related to treatment for both sick patients and healthy population; whereas TPP QALYs only consider QALY gain for sick patients. The alternative input explored in the scenario analysis was estimated by measuring the value that healthy individuals placed on generous insurance coverage for treating serious disease. Researchers quantified the value that novel lung cancer treatment can provide to these healthy individuals beyond its value in expectation. This highlights that the quantitative estimation of insurance value inputs is highly uncertain and currently published methodology potentially lacks face validity when applied directly to economic models. In particular, in the study informing the higher insurance value input, the researchers comment that incorporating their estimated insurance value may require an adjustment to payer valuations of QALY gains [46]. This emphasizes the significance of insurance value in determining the NMB of treatments from a societal perspective. Additionally, applying both insurance value and the disease severity adjustment could lead to double counting, as insurance value effectively accounts for patients reducing their risk of losing health due to diseases, which would encompass their severity. Research has highlighted that cancer diagnosis has both positive and negative consequences for caregivers’ QoL [64,65]. Positive effects reported by caregivers include a sense of purpose and intimacy from caregiving; whereas, negative effects include a lack of social life outside of caregiving and increased physical and emotional exhaustion [65]. In this case study, the potential positive impacts on caregiver QoL, and possibility of bereavement-related disutility, were not accounted for [64]. In alignment with HTA methodology, this study presents the probabilistic NMB result to capture the parametric uncertainty surrounding model inputs. This enables the cost of fertility treatment to be captured for sampled patients who are below the maximum age to undergo fertility treatments. If only the mean patient age were considered, the cost of fertility treatments would not be captured. This emphasizes the importance of incorporating the parametric uncertainty associated with input parameters.

Additional future research could analyze the inclusion and impact of elements of value from the ISPOR value flower that are not included in this case study. These elements include reduction in uncertainty, scientific spillovers, and equity. While equity concerns are gradually being incorporated into more CEAs [68], the challenges faced are still substantial due to the complexity and data intensive nature of implementation. One method of incorporation includes the introduction of preference weights, which are already included in disease severity. Another incorporation method includes distributional CEAs which quantifies the distribution of health effects and costs between groups in a population, referred to as inequalities, and of any ensuing trade-offs between health maximization and equity [69]. Despite these methodologies, this research was limited by the lack of Canadian data to parameterize equity efficiently into analysis. The remaining two elements were not included in this research due to a lack of quantitative information identified during the literature review phase. Future research to quantify the impact of these elements of value in Canada may enhance what currently encapsulates value from the BSP.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to consider the broader societal value of treatment across multiple types of women’s cancer. Future research could aim to incorporate the elements of value which were omitted from this case study to further broaden the perspective. These include equity, reduction in uncertainty, and scientific spillovers. If indication-specific inputs for elements such as insurance value become available, these would further enhance the accuracy of the analysis.

The overall objective of this study was to quantify the broad societal value of treating four types of women’s cancer. Despite the sparse existing literature, this case study used innovative techniques to build, evaluate, and provide evidence for the three perspectives of cost-effectiveness analyses. Our results demonstrated that broadening the analysis perspective substantially increased the NMB, and thus overall value, of treating women’s cancers. As more HTA bodies begin to explore the incorporation of broader perspectives for analysis, this research provides an exploratory foundation for further research to support the integration of broad societal elements of value into decision making as part of HTA.
