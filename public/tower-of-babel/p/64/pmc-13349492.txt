Myocardial Infarction Following Systemic Air Embolism During Computed Tomography-Guided Lung Biopsy
Authors: Amane Otoi, Toru Miyoshi, Haruhiko Higashi, Shunsuke Tamaki, Kazuhisa Nishimura, Katsuji Inoue, Shuntaro Ikeda, Osamu Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

A man in his 70s underwent computed tomography (CT)-guided lung biopsy for suspected lung cancer in the right lateral decubitus position. After the first biopsy, CT revealed air in the left ventricle and aorta. To prevent cerebral embolism, he remained in the same position with oxygen until the air disappeared (Figure). However, 5 hours after the biopsy, he developed acute abdominal pain. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a low-density area in the posterolateral wall of the left ventricle in the left circumflex (LCx) territory. ECG showed no ST-segment changes, but his serum troponin I was elevated to 88,292 pg/mL, with peak creatine kinase of 1,995 U/L and CK-MB 137 U/L. Cardiac CT demonstrated reduced iodine enhancement without coronary stenosis.

Elevation of cardiac biomarkers with imaging evidence of myocardial injury is consistent with acute myocardial infarction. Because no obstructive coronary disease was identified, myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) was considered. Intracardiac air detected after the biopsy suggested transient coronary air embolism causing myocardial ischemia.1 Air in the left ventricle may have migrated into the coronary circulation.

Air embolism is a rare complication of CT-guided lung biopsy (0.07%).2 Air may enter the pulmonary vein through the biopsy needle or via a bronchovascular fistula.2 The patient presented with abdominal pain rather than chest pain, possibly reflecting inferolateral myocardial ischemia.
