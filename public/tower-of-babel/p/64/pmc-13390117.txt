Can machine learning uncover viable non-platinum catalysts for hydrogen evolution? An unsupervised clustering approach to transition metal–ligand complexes
Authors: Achouak Benarbia, Ali Alshami, Ayyaz Mustafa, Olusegun Stanley Tomomewo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen is central to the global clean energy transition, yet the widespread use of green hydrogen is constrained by its dependence on costly, supply-limited platinum-based catalysts in Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolyzers. While alternatives have been explored, identifying efficient and stable non-platinum catalysts remains a major challenge due to the vast chemical space and the expense of experimental screening. This study pioneers a novel unsupervised machine learning framework to systematically map this vast chemical space and identify promising catalyst candidates without reliance on pre-existing experimental data. Using the tmQM dataset comprising over 108 000 transition metal–ligand complexes; we selected five key descriptors (HOMO–LUMO gap, dipole moment, molecular size, metal node degree, and total charge) to represent catalytic potential and stability. Three unsupervised clustering algorithms (K-Means, DBSCAN, and Gaussian Mixture Models) were applied to identify chemically and electronically distinct groups. Unlike supervised models that predict properties for known structures, our unsupervised approach uncovers previously unidentified classes of non-platinum complexes that possess features ideal for catalytic activity. This work establishes a new, efficient workflow for catalyst science, shifting the focus from slow, one-by-one candidate evaluation to a rapid, holistic mapping of chemical space to pinpoint the most promising regions for focused experimental investigation. Importantly, all shortlisted complexes are structurally documented in crystallographic databases, which enables direct experimental validation and minimizes trial-and-error in material design.

Introduction

The widespread deployment of green hydrogen, a cornerstone of the global clean energy transition, is critically dependent on the efficiency and economic viability of water electrolysis technologies.1 Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolyzers offer high performance and dynamic response, but their scalability is fundamentally constrained by a reliance on scarce and costly platinum-group metal (PGM) catalysts for the Hydrogen Evolution Reaction (HER).2 This dependence on platinum not only inflates the capital cost of electrolyzers but also introduces significant supply chain volatility, representing a primary barrier to producing green hydrogen at a cost competitive with fossil-fuel-derived alternatives. The production cost of green hydrogen is the primary barrier to its wide-scale deployment. As of 2023, the Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCoH) for green hydrogen ranges between $3 to $6 per kilogram, depending on local electricity prices, electrolyzer technology, and utilization factors.3

To address this challenge, extensive research has focused on developing HER electrocatalysts from earth-abundant, non-PGM materials.4 Significant progress has been made with several classes of solid-state materials, including transition metal sulfides, phosphides, and carbides, which have demonstrated promising activity.5–7 However, as summarized in Table 1, these materials often exhibit performance trade-offs, such as high overpotentials or insufficient long-term stability under the harsh acidic and oxidative conditions of a PEM electrolyzer.

This persistent performance gap motivates the exploration of a highly promising yet comparatively underexplored frontier: the domain of discrete, molecular metal–ligand complexes. Recent studies have highlighted their growing potential, with a particular focus on ligand design and metal–ligand interactions.9 Ligands are no longer viewed as passive components; they actively participate in catalytic cycles through proton shuttling, electron donation, and intermediate stabilization.10 For instance, systems such as N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs), thiosemicarbazones, and bis(thiosemicarbazonato) zinc complexes have demonstrated enhanced HER performance by tuning electronic structures and coordination geometries.11,12 Other work has established that geometric control and ligand electronics in salicylaldehyde-based ligands coordinated to molybdenum can enable HER in acidic environments,13 while corrole-chelated nickel complexes exhibit favorable onset potentials.14 Secondary coordination effects are also critical, with studies on ligand-protected copper clusters and sulfonic-acid-functionalized phosphide complexes showing that hydrogen bonding can promote activity and stabilize intermediates.15 Beyond ligand effects, tuning the metal center itself has led to promising non-precious HER catalysts, with earth-abundant metals like Ni, Co, and Cu offering high performance at a reduced cost, particularly in bimetallic or ternary frameworks.16–18 These systems offer unparalleled tunability, providing a powerful platform for optimizing catalytic activity at a molecular level.

The primary obstacle to leveraging this tunability has been methodological: the chemical space of potential metal–ligand complexes is astronomically vast. The combinatorial possibilities of different metal centers, ligand scaffolds, and coordination geometries make traditional trial-and-error experimental synthesis or even high-throughput computational screening via methods like Density Functional Theory (DFT) prohibitively slow and resource-intensive. While machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative tool for accelerating materials discovery,19 its application in catalysis has predominantly relied on supervised models.20,21 These models require large, pre-existing, labeled datasets of known catalyst performance to be trained. Such a dataset for HER activity across the diverse space of metal–ligand complexes does not exist, rendering supervised ML an effective tool for optimizing known systems but fundamentally limiting its capacity for true, de novo discovery in uncharted chemical domains.

Here, we introduce a novel discovery framework that circumvents these limitations by employing unsupervised machine learning to perform a systematic, data-driven exploration of the vast chemical space of transition metal–ligand complexes. Using a dataset of over 108 000 experimentally realized structures from the tmQM database, we apply clustering algorithms to map this space based on intrinsic electronic and structural descriptors that are proxies for HER catalytic activity and stability. This approach does not require any prior experimental performance labels, enabling it to identify chemically distinct classes and pinpoint promising, unexplored regions of chemical space.22,23 This work reframes the catalyst discovery process from a slow, serial evaluation of individual candidates to a rapid, holistic “map-and-focus” strategy, thereby accelerating the identification of high-potential targets for focused experimental investigation.

By demonstrating a scalable workflow that bridges high-throughput computational screening with experimental viability, as all identified candidates are based on known, crystallographically characterized structures, this work establishes an efficient new paradigm for catalyst discovery to navigate the immense chemical space of molecular catalysts, accelerating the search for affordable, earth-abundant HER catalysts. The value of this framework lies in its ability to pinpoint high-potential regions for focused experimental investigation, thereby contributing directly to the technological and economic feasibility of a global green hydrogen economy by overcoming a critical materials discovery bottleneck.

Methodology

This study adopted a data-driven framework integrating quantum chemistry, feature engineering, and unsupervised machine learning to identify and prioritize high-performing metal–ligand complexes (TMCs) for hydrogen evolution in PEM electrolyzers. The objective was to classify complexes based on intrinsic electronic and structural descriptors, then define precise thresholds to extract a subset of top candidates with optimal catalytic potential.

The approach unfolds in five main phases:

• Dataset acquisition and preprocessing

This study adopted the tmQM dataset developed by Balcells and Skjelstad in 2020, updated in 2024,24 which provides a comprehensive and systematically curated collection of over 108 000 mononuclear transition metal complexes. Derived from the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD), the tmQM dataset offers a uniquely relevant resource for HER catalyst discovery because it captures a broad spectrum of experimentally known metal–ligand systems with diverse ligand fields, oxidation states, and coordination geometries.

What distinguishes tmQM from other datasets is its scale, chemical diversity, and the quality and depth of its quantum mechanical characterization. Each structure was initially optimized using the semi-empirical GFN2-xTB method and subsequently refined via single-point density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the TPSSh-D3BJ/def2-SVP level of theory. This dual-level protocol ensures computational efficiency and high-fidelity property estimation, making the dataset particularly suitable for machine learning applications. An additional preprocessing step involved calculating molecular size, which was absent from the original dataset. Preprocessing was achieved using custom Python scripts that extracted atomic identities and their stoichiometric counts to determine the total number of atoms per complex, representing a coarse yet meaningful proxy for steric volume and molecular accessibility. Each TMC entry was linked to a corresponding CSD record, which included detailed experimental synthesis procedures and crystallographic data. This feature greatly enhances the practical value of the dataset by allowing future experimental researchers to directly consult original synthesis routes.

• Descriptor selection and engineering

Molecular descriptor selection was guided by a domain-specific understanding of HER catalysis and a critical review of recent literature. Five descriptors were retained for the ML pipeline and final candidate selection: dipole moment, HOMO–LUMO gap, natural charge, molecular size, and metal node degree. Each reflects a mechanistic or structural factor influencing either catalytic activity or molecular stability.

Dipole moment: acts as a proxy for electronic asymmetry, which is known to influence the local electric field at the catalyst–electrolyte interface and facilitate the orientation of polar water molecules. High dipole moments indicate a polar charge distribution, which can enhance interactions with protons and the surrounding solvent, promoting HER kinetics. Er et al. (2018) and Qiu et al. (2015) established that polar complexes often exhibit stronger adsorption and charge-transfer capabilities.25,26

HOMO–LUMO gap: the HOMO–LUMO gap is a well-established proxy for reactivity and electrostatic stability. Smaller gaps suggest higher electron mobility and a greater readiness to engage in redox transformations, both desirable in HER catalysts.27 The HL_Gap values are reported in atomic unit (a.u.) where 1 hartree (a.u.) corresponds to approximately 27.2114 eV.

Total charge (q): total charge ensures electrochemical and synthetic feasibility; neutral complexes are more stable under PEM conditions and are experimentally accessible.28

Molecular size: this parameter relates to steric hindrance and the spatial reach of the complex. Larger molecules tend to offer greater stability and a more accessible surface area, which may benefit catalyst longevity in acidic PEM conditions.

Metal node degree (MND): quantifies the number of ligand connections to the metal center. Higher values suggest structurally complex coordination environments, which are often correlated with thermodynamic and geometric stability.29

To validate the robustness of our descriptor selection, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the full set of quantum chemical descriptors available in the rich tmQM dataset. While the manual selection was primarily driven by physical relevance to HER activity and stability, the PCA results confirm that this set aligns with the dataset's intrinsic variance. Specifically, the first five principal components capture 90.6% of the total variance in electronic and structural behavior. The component loadings demonstrate that our selected descriptors are the primary drivers of this variance, confirming that they provide an efficient and statistically representative basis for the study.

• Clustering framework

Three unsupervised clustering algorithms (K-Means, DBSCAN, and Gaussian Mixture Models) were applied to reveal latent groupings among transition metal complexes. Clustering performance was objectively evaluated using Silhouette, Calinski–Harabasz, and Davies–Bouldin indices, with 2D visualizations aiding in interpretability. Clusters were subsequently analyzed to assess the distribution of catalytic descriptors, providing a basis for prioritizing application-relevant candidates. Detailed description of these methods is available in the SI.

• Thresholding and candidate down-selection

Descriptor thresholds were extracted from the most promising clusters using the best-performing clustering model. These thresholds were cross-validated against trends reported in HER catalyst literature. A refined subset of candidates was generated by applying these constraints to the full dataset. This reduced list emphasized complexes that balance catalytic activity, thermodynamic stability, and synthetic feasibility.

K-Means clustering results

We applied the K-means clustering algorithm to the dataset using the aforementioned descriptors after standardization to uncover structural and electronic patterns among transition metal–ligand complexes (TMCs). The optimal number of clusters was selected based on the elbow method, which evaluates the within-cluster sum of squared errors (inertia) as a function of the number of clusters k. The inertia curve exhibits a distinct inflection at k = 6, beyond which additional clusters contribute marginal improvements in intra-cluster compactness (Fig. 4), supporting the use of six clusters as a balance between the underfitting and over-segmentation of the chemical space.

Elbow plot used to identify the optimal number of clusters based on inertia.

We visualized the spatial distribution of clusters generated by K-means using 2D projections of the five selected molecular descriptors. Fig. 5A–C represent the most chemically and catalytically relevant descriptor relationships: HL gap vs. dipole moment (Fig. 5A), MND vs. molecular size (Fig. 5B), and HL gap vs. metal charge (q) (Fig. 5C). Two additional projections were selected based on observed cluster separation and domain relevance to complement this core set: dipole moment vs. molecular size, which highlights steric–electronic interplay in catalyst design, and HL gap vs. MND, which illustrates the co-variation of reactivity and coordination. Other combinations were explored and are available in the SI; however, these five views offered the most chemically interpretable and non-redundant insights into cluster composition.

(A–E) Pairwise descriptor plots for K-means clustering output.

One cluster occupied the region of low HL gap and high dipole moment, which represents a chemically significant zone of reactive and polar complexes. The quality of the K-means clustering model was assessed using three standard internal validation metrics. With K = 6, the Silhouette score was 0.713, indicating a strong degree of internal cohesion and inter-cluster separation. The DBI was calculated as 0.491, falling within the “very good” range, which reflects low intra-cluster variance relative to inter-cluster distances. The CHI yielded a value of 55.52, confirming strong between-cluster dispersion relative to within-cluster compactness.

DBSCAN clustering results

We employed the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm to explore the density-based structure of the dataset. DBSCAN identifies clusters as high-density regions separated by low-density boundaries, offering a robust approach for detecting arbitrarily shaped clusters and outliers without requiring the number of clusters to be specified a priori.

The DBSCAN algorithm was optimized using a grid-search cross-validation approach to tune the two key hyperparameters: epsilon (ε), representing the maximum radius of neighborhood around a point, and min_samples, defining the minimum number of data points required within that radius to qualify as a dense region. The tested range for epsilon was 0.1 to 2.0 (interval = 0.1), and min_samples ranged from 2 to 20 (interval = 2). The best-performing parameter combination was determined by running the algorithm for various combinations to generate the final DBSCAN results, discussed below.

Most of the data points formed a dense region extending across a wide range of dipole moments, mostly below 20 D, and the HL gap values were primarily below 0.2 eV (Fig. 6A, HL gap vs. dipole moment). A limited number of clusters are visually separable, with the most populated one concentrated in the region of low HL gap and low to moderate dipole moment. Several points scattered above 25 D are visible, but these are isolated and sparsely distributed, indicating low-density regions where DBSCAN does not form dense clusters. Fig. 6B (molecular size vs. MND) reveals horizontally aligned bands reflecting discrete MND values. The most concentrated clusters lie within molecular sizes between 30 and 130 atoms, and MND between 4 and 10. The colors reveal that DBSCAN identified a few dominant clusters that span across these ranges. Points beyond 200 atoms or an MND greater than 15 are sparse and exhibit less consistent cluster labeling, suggesting that DBSCAN treats these as noise or fails to form reliable dense groupings in these areas. Fig. 6C (HL gap vs. q) indicates three dominant horizontal bands corresponding to discrete formal charge values: +1, 0, and −1. Most of the clustered points lie within the q = 0 band, particularly where the HL gap ranges from 0.05 to 0.20 eV. Other charge states, such as −2, appear less populated and are not consistently colored, implying that these were either excluded from dense clusters or assigned to smaller groups. Fig. 6D (dipole moment vs. molecular size) illustrates a broad, dense region covering dipole moments up to ∼20 D and molecular sizes up to 150 atoms, where DBSCAN forms the most consistent clusters. Above these ranges, points are visibly more scattered with a lower frequency of cluster labeling, suggesting they fall outside of the density thresholds required for core cluster membership. Fig. 6E (HL gap vs. MND) illustrates that the data points again formed structured horizontal layers based on discrete MND values. Most clustered regions are concentrated between the HL gap = 0.05–0.20 eV and MND = 8–11, where density is highest. Cluster differentiation diminishes beyond these bounds, with increasingly sparse points exhibiting inconsistent coloring, indicative of low-density areas not captured as clusters.

(A–E) Pairwise descriptor plots for DBSCAN clustering output.

Overall, these visualizations reveal that DBSCAN forms well-defined clusters only in high-density regions of the descriptor space, particularly where values for HL gap, dipole moment, MND, and molecular size overlap within dominant statistical modes. Outliers and less frequent configurations are not captured, revealing the algorithm's sensitivity to local density thresholds and its strength in identifying compact, consistent subgroups.

The quality of the DBSCAN clustering model was assessed using three internal validation metrics. The Silhouette score was 0.523, indicating reasonable clustering performance with moderate cohesion within clusters and some degree of overlap between neighboring regions. The DBI was 1.067, which falls within the range of “decent clustering” as defined by standard interpretative guidelines (see Appendix II, SI), reflecting acceptable inter-cluster separation with potential room for improvement. The CHI was 22.90, suggesting a lower degree of between-cluster variance relative to within-cluster compactness when compared to the other algorithms applied in this study. These results indicate that while DBSCAN succeeds in identifying well-defined, dense regions in the data, its performance is somewhat limited by the presence of sparsely distributed points and the absence of globally compact cluster structures.

Gaussian mixture model (GMM) results

The Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) clustering algorithm was applied using the same five molecular descriptors to further explore the latent structure of the dataset. GMM assigns probabilities to each data point belonging to different clusters, allowing it to model overlapping and non-spherical clusters. The optimal number of components was set to 6 based on performance evaluation.

Overall, GMM effectively partitions the dense descriptor space, particularly in regions where descriptor distributions overlap. The probabilistic cluster boundaries allow for finer differentiation, especially in high-density areas where sharp separations may not be chemically justified.

The clustering performance of the GMM revealed an SI score of 0.669, indicating reasonable cluster cohesion and separation, with GMM effectively capturing structure within overlapping regions of the data. The DBI was 0.848, falling in the range of acceptable clustering quality, suggesting moderate intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster distinction. The CHI yielded a value of 37.19, pointing to a fair balance between within-cluster consistency and between-cluster dispersion. These scores confirm that GMM provides a statistically meaningful yet more flexible clustering configuration, particularly suited for datasets where chemical descriptors exhibit continuous or overlapping distributions.

This study presents a structured chemical landscape of transition metal–ligand complexes (TMCs) for HER, derived from clustering based on key electronic and structural descriptors. These clusters offer a data-guided roadmap for selecting catalysts tailored to specific HER objectives. For example, low HL gap clusters indicate high electronic reactivity, while those with elevated dipole moments suggest favorable proton affinity. No single cluster simultaneously optimizes all five descriptors, highlighting the need to balance trade-offs between reactivity, stability, and molecular accessibility. Researchers can focus on subspaces where multiple performance factors converge, streamlining candidate selection, by visualizing and analyzing cluster profiles. This framework supports informed prioritization and accelerates experimental validation by narrowing the focus to high-potential regions in chemical space. We refined this further by defining quantitative thresholds based on clustering trends and literature values, enabling the extraction of multi-objective candidates dispersed across different clusters.

Descriptor thresholding and candidate narrowing

To ensure the discovery process is driven by the intrinsic density of the chemical space rather than arbitrary selection, descriptor thresholds were statistically derived from the most promising clusters identified by the optimal machine learning model. This unsupervised clustering step is a critical prerequisite; it characterizes the latent manifold of the 108 000 transition metal complexes, revealing the multidimensional ‘chemical regimes’ where reactivity and stability properties are naturally compatible. These cluster-informed thresholds were cross-validated against established HER literature trends.

Below, each threshold is discussed in terms of its catalytic significance, supported by peer-reviewed studies:

• HOMO–LUMO gap (HL gap)

An HL gap between 0–2.7 eV balances electronic stability and redox activity, enabling effective charge transfer during HER without compromising molecular integrity.35–37

• Dipole moment (dipole moment)

Values between 3.5 and 6.0 D enhance solvation and electrostatic interaction with protons, improving interfacial kinetics and facilitating charge separation.38

• Metal node degree (MND)

A coordination number of 4 to 6 allows for catalytic flexibility and intermediate stabilization without excessive steric hindrance.39,40

• Molecular size

Complexes containing 10–30 atoms strike a balance between surface accessibility, mass transport, and electronic complexity.41

• Metal charge

An overall formal charge in the range of 0 to +2 facilitates favorable electron–proton interactions while maintaining solution stability and avoiding excessive coulombic repulsion.42 We restricted our final selection to neutral (charge = 0) complexes, which also represent most of the viable candidates within the optimal cluster identified by K-means, to ensure compatibility with experimental conditions in acidic media and to avoid solubility or stability issues associated with charged species.

These thresholding summarized in Table 4 form the basis of a structural and electronic filtering scheme designed to systematically reduce the full 108 000-complex dataset to a high-priority subset with the greatest potential for experimental viability and catalytic performance under acidic conditions. To quantitatively validate the predictive power of these unsupervised machine learning thresholds, we benchmarked the optimal ML cluster against the seminal DuBois-type nickel catalyst [Ni(PCy2 NtBu2)2]2+, these systems are recognized as some of the most active synthetic 3d transition-metal HER electrocatalysts in the literature, with turnover frequencies (TOF) exceeding 10−5.43,44

To ensure strict methodological consistency with the tmQM dataset, we extracted the experimental crystallographic coordinates of the mononuclear DuBois cation and calculated its baseline electronic properties using pure Density Functional Theory at the identical TPSSh-D3BJ/def2-SVP level of theory. The calculated electronic baseline demonstrates a striking quantitative alignment with our model's autonomously selected parameter space: the benchmark catalyst exhibits a narrow HOMO–LUMO gap of 2.07 eV and a high total dipole moment of 3.39 D, both of which fall precisely within or border the optimal ML thresholds (0–2.7 eV and 3.5–6.0 D, respectively). This alignment is chemically significant; the high dipole moments prioritized by our cluster directly mirror the electronic asymmetry necessary for pendant amine proton relays to function effectively during proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET).

Furthermore, structural comparisons confirm that our algorithm successfully isolated critical inner-sphere prerequisites, identifying the necessary square-planar NiP4 coordination geometry (MND = 4) characteristic of the DuBois systems. While the benchmark DuBois catalyst is highly bulky (>50 atoms) and exists as a +2 cation, our model deliberately constrained the selection to neutral, lower-molecular-weight complexes (10–30 atoms). This constraint was enforced to optimize for synthetic feasibility, membrane compatibility, and long-term stability within the highly acidic and confined environments of modern PEM electrolyzers. By demonstrating that our unsupervised framework independently isolates both the structural motifs and the precise electronic prerequisites of the most successful catalytic architectures, we provide confidence that the new candidates identified from the tmQM dataset are located within a high-probability “catalytic hit” space.

This filtering step retained 110 transition metal–ligand complexes that simultaneously satisfied all target ranges. The list represents a significant reduction from the full dataset and highlights the selectivity of the proposed multi-criteria screening approach. These complexes are expected to offer a balance between catalytic reactivity (low HL gap), polarization and charge separation (dipole moment), structural integrity (MND and size), and synthetic feasibility (neutral charge). This refined set significantly reduces experimental search space and focuses attention on a chemically meaningful subdomain for HER catalyst discovery.

The bar chart highlights a clear predominance of nickel (Ni), which appears in 17 complexes, followed closely by rhenium (Re) and platinum (Pt), each present in 14 entries. tungsten (W) and manganese (Mn) also feature prominently, suggesting that these metals frequently form complexes that meet the multi-criteria HER performance thresholds set in this study.

Frequency of transition metals in final TMCs.

Nickel (Ni) emerged as the most frequently occurring transition metal among the 110 HER candidates shortlisted through descriptor-based filtering. This prevalence reflects its well-documented catalytic potential and its compatibility with the electronic and structural criteria prioritized in this study. Nickel is a mid-row transition metal known for its favorable electronic configuration, which enables it to readily participate in redox processes essential for the hydrogen evolution reaction. Nickel also exhibits moderate binding strength to hydrogen intermediates, making it well-suited to facilitate the Volmer and Heyrovsky steps in HER mechanisms.26 Beyond its catalytic properties, nickel and its alloys are also recognized for their mechanical durability and wear resistance, particularly in harsh operational environments which reinforces their suitability for integration in electrolyzer systems.45

While the predominance of nickel-based complexes in our final shortlist aligns with its well-documented role in bio-inspired catalysis, the primary novelty and chemical insight of this work lie in the model's ability to autonomously map the ‘catalytic champion’ space without any prior training on experimental performance data. The significance of this unsupervised ‘map-and-focus’ framework is demonstrated not merely by identifying the optimal metal center, but by effectively filtering out thousands of ineffective coordination environments to pinpoint specific, underexplored ligand–metal pairings. For instance, our model highlights unique, chemically plausible formulations that feature halide, nitrogen, or sulfur-based ligands capable of modulating the electronic asymmetry and intermediate stabilization required for HER. By independently converging on the same structural and electronic prerequisites (such as high dipole moments and moderate coordination numbers) necessary for efficient proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET), this framework moves beyond traditional computational screening. It provides a targeted, data-guided library of highly specific structural configurations, significantly narrowing the experimental search space for the next generation of non-platinum PEM electrolyzer catalysts.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study successfully demonstrates a novel, unsupervised machine learning framework designed to overcome a critical bottleneck in the search for affordable electrocatalysts for the Hydrogen Evolution Reaction (HER). By systematically mapping the vast, unlabeled chemical space of over 108 000 transition metal–ligand complexes, our work establishes a new paradigm for materials discovery. The primary contribution is not merely the identification of individual catalyst candidates, but the validation of a “map-and-focus” strategy that accelerates the discovery process without reliance on pre-existing experimental performance data. This methodological advance is particularly significant as it circumvents the limitations of traditional trial-and-error screening and data-hungry supervised learning models, enabling true de novo discovery in underexplored domains of chemistry. The successful identification of a refined list of promising, experimentally realized candidates, with a notable prevalence of nickel-based complexes, serves as a powerful validation of this data-driven approach, confirming its ability to pinpoint high-potential regions in chemical space for targeted investigation.

Looking forward, the recommendations for future work extend across both experimental and computational domains:

• Experimental validation: the immediate and most critical next step is the experimental synthesis, characterization (including X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) and X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)) and electrochemical testing of the top-ranked candidate complexes, particularly the shortlisted nickel-based systems. Validating their HER activity, overpotential, and long-term stability under acidic conditions relevant to PEM electrolyzers will be essential to confirm the predictive power of the computational framework.

• Expansion to other catalytic systems: the unsupervised discovery framework developed here is highly extensible. It should be applied to other critical energy-related reactions that face similar materials discovery challenges, such as the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), CO2 reduction, or nitrogen fixation. Applying this methodology to different chemical databases could rapidly identify promising molecular catalysts across a wide range of applications.

• Enhancement of the descriptor set: future iterations of this work should expand the descriptor set to build more sophisticated and holistic screening models. This could include integrating computationally derived metrics that are more directly linked to catalytic activity, such as the calculated hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔG_H*), as well as practical, real-world constraints like estimated material cost, synthetic complexity, and toxicity metrics.

• Integration with supervised learning: while this study focused on unsupervised discovery, the experimentally validated candidates can serve as the seed data for a subsequent, more targeted optimization phase. Once performance data is collected, supervised machine learning models can be trained to predict specific metrics like Tafel slope or turnover frequency, creating a powerful workflow that transitions seamlessly from broad discovery to fine-tuned optimization.
