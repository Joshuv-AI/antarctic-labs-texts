Prenatal maternal psychological distress is associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes in toddlers with congenital heart disease
Authors: Yao Wu, Nickie Andescavage, Susan Lauren Weiner, Melissa Liggett, Julius S. Ngwa, Anushree Kapse, Kushal Kapse, Caitlin Elizabeth McDermott, Mary T. Donofrio, Katherine L. Wisner, David Wessel, Adré J. du Plessis, Catherine Limperopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Pregnant women carrying fetuses with congenital heart disease (CHD) report high psychological distress. This study examines associations between prenatal maternal distress and 18-month neurodevelopmental outcomes in toddlers with CHD.METHODS:We recruited women with pregnancies complicated by fetal CHD. Maternal anxiety, stress, and depression were measured using the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, Perceived Stress Scale, and Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. Neurodevelopment at 18 months was assessed using Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development III and Infant-Toddler Social and Emotional Assessment. Postnatal parenting stress was measured using Parenting Stress Index-Short Form.RESULTS:We studied 123 pregnant women (21–39 gestational weeks). Half screened positive for elevated anxiety, 60% for stress, and 34% for depression. Elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress was associated with lower motor scores and increased externalizing behaviors in CHD toddlers, with stronger associations in single-ventricle than two-ventricle CHD. Elevated prenatal maternal distress was associated with increased postnatal parenting stress at 18-month testing, including higher Total Stress, Parental Distress, and Difficult Child scores. Postnatal parenting stress mediated associations between prenatal psychological distress and toddler externalizing behaviors.CONCLUSION:Elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress is associated with impaired motor and social-emotional development in toddlers with CHD and persistent postnatal parenting stress.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, affecting ~1% of live births.1 Advances in surgical and perioperative care have dramatically improved survival rates, yet developmental disabilities and socio-behavioral problems in CHD survivors in the years following successful surgical repair continue to rise.2–6 Children with CHD show increased risk for impairments in motor, cognitive, learning, as well as emotional and behavioral regulation domains.2–6 These challenges often appear in infancy and persist into adolescence and adulthood, affecting educational attainment, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life.2,7

A prenatal diagnosis of CHD invariably increases parental stress.8–10 Elevated maternal depression, anxiety, and stress levels during pregnancy are associated with disturbances in early brain structure and function in offspring,10–13 which in turn correlates with long-term neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disabilities in children and adults.14–21 In pregnancies with healthy fetuses, adverse child neurodevelopmental outcomes, including cognitive, language, memory, learning, social-emotional problems, and neuropsychiatric dysfunction, have been increasingly reported after prenatal exposure to maternal distress.14,18,22–30 However, the impact of elevated prenatal maternal distress on the neurodevelopmental outcomes of toddlers with CHD remains unknown. Moreover, understanding how prenatal maternal distress relates to postnatal parenting stress is critical, as parenting stress reflects the long-term psychosocial impact of CHD and may represent an important pathway through which maternal distress affects child behavioral outcomes.

Importantly, CHD comprises a heterogeneous group of cardiac malformations that differ in physiology, surgical course, and neurodevelopmental risk. Single-ventricle (SV) CHD comprises a group of complex congenital heart defects in which only one functional ventricular chamber supports systemic circulation that typically require staged palliative surgeries and often lead to chronic hypoxemia.31–33 Children with SV CHD demonstrate lower intelligence, memory, learning, visual-motor, and cognitive performance, as well as greater behavioral difficulties compared with those with two-ventricle (2V) CHD,34–36 and their parents often experience higher psychological distress and caregiving burden.10,37 These differences underscore the need to investigate whether the impact of prenatal maternal distress on child neurodevelopment varies by CHD ventricle physiology.

Sociodemographic factors, including maternal education and race, may influence levels of maternal psychological distress.38,39 Families from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds may face reduced access to healthcare and greater caregiving challenges after delivery.40,41 Examining these factors may help contextualize variability in maternal distress among families affected by CHD.

Identifying early modifiable risk factors for impaired neurodevelopment in high-risk infants with CHD is critical for developing targeted interventions aimed at optimizing maternal-infant relational health and infant neurodevelopmental outcomes. The primary objective of this study was to examine the association between prenatal maternal psychological distress (including stress, anxiety, and depression) and neurodevelopmental outcomes (cognitive, language, motor, and social-emotional) in toddlers with CHD at around 18 months of age. The secondary objective was to examine the association between prenatal maternal psychological distress and postnatal parenting stress (parental distress, parent-child dysfunctional interaction, difficult child, and total stress) at 18-month testing. Subanalyses were performed to explore whether these associations varied by ventricle physiology (SV vs 2V CHD) and whether prenatal maternal distress differed by sociodemographic characteristics (maternal education and race). We hypothesized that elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress would be associated with poorer neurodevelopment and greater parenting stress, that these associations might differ between SV and 2V CHD.

METHODS

Study design

Study participants were consecutively recruited into a longitudinal prospective cohort study between January 2016 and April 2024. Pregnant women who underwent a fetal echocardiogram at Children’s National Hospital with a confirmed critical CHD (requiring neonatal cardiac surgery) were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included: 1) fetuses with extracardiac anomalies noted on antenatal ultrasound or chromosomal abnormalities; 2) pregnant women with genetic disorders, substance use disorder, or multiple pregnancy. Participants were scheduled to complete up to two maternal stress measures between 21 and 39 weeks of gestational age. The 18-month assessments were conducted at Children’s National Hospital, Washington, DC, and families received a $75 compensation for participating in the follow-up. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review board at Children’s National Hospital (Protocol 3898). This cohort is part of the study titled “Antecedents of Impaired Brain Development in Fetuses with Heart Disease”. A subset of participants (N = 48) was included in a prior publication10 examining associations between prenatal maternal psychological distress and in utero fetal brain structure in CHD, and the current study reports follow-up neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Demographics

The CHD classification as SV or 2V was determined based on the fetal diagnosis. Maternal education, employment, race, and age were self-reported, and clinical data, including perioperative cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest, seizures, stroke, sepsis, and length of hospitalization, were obtained from medical records.

Maternal stress, depression, and anxiety

Prenatal psychological distress was measured using psychometrically sound questionnaires, i.e., Perceived Stress Scale (PSS),42 Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)43 and Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S, state anxiety; STAI-T, trait anxiety).44 The PSS assesses the degree to which people perceive their lives as stressful over the preceding month. Higher PSS scores indicate higher perceived stress and a PSS score ≥15 (range: 0–40) indicates stress is higher than average.45 The EPDS (10-item; range: 0–30) measures the severity of perinatal depression symptoms in the past 7 days. Higher scores indicate more depressive symptoms, and a score ≥10 indicates the presence of depression symptoms during pregnancy.46 The STAI-S measures the current feeling of anxiety and STAI-T measures the long-term experience of anxiety. A higher STAI-S or STAI-T score (range: 20–80) suggests increased anxiety, and scores ≥40 are considered to have symptoms of anxiety.47 In this work, we then defined positive prenatal psychological distress for each participant if there was an elevated score for stress, anxiety, or depression at any prenatal visit.

Toddler’s neurodevelopmental testing.

Standardized neurodevelopmental testing for toddlers was performed by a trained examiner blinded to CHD diagnosis and other clinical findings. Neurodevelopmental assessments were conducted using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (BSID-III, 3rd edition)48 and the Infant-Toddler Social and Emotional Assessment (ITSEA).49 The BSID-III evaluates cognitive, language, and motor functioning in children aged 1–42 months.48,50 The BSID-III was performed by a licensed psychologist. The normalized mean ± SD of each composite score is 100 ± 15. A score <85 (1 SD) indicates a developmental delay.51 The ITSEA is a parent-completed measure that evaluates child social-emotional behavior at 12–36 months.52,53 The ITSEA assesses four domains: externalizing (aggression/defiance, activity/impulsivity, and peer aggression), internalizing (general anxiety, depression/withdrawal, separation distress, and inhibition to novelty), dysregulation (negative emotionality, eating, sleep, and sensory sensitivity), and competence (attention, mastery, motivation, compliance, imitation/play, empathy, and prosocial peer relations).49,54 Externalizing, internalizing, dysregulation scores of 65 or higher and competence domain scores of 35 or lower indicate elevated behavior problems.54

Parenting stress at 18-month testing.

Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF)55 was used to evaluate the degree of stress in the parent-child dyad. The PSI-SF is a self-reported measure of parenting stress, which includes three subscales: parental distress, parent-child dysfunctional interaction, and difficult child. The PSI-SF also gives a defensive responding scale (7 items from the parental distress scale) that assesses parental bias in reporting by quantifying the desire of parents to present a favorable impression of themselves and minimize problems in the parent-child relationship, and a total stress scale that is a sum of parental distress, parent-child dysfunctional interaction, and difficult child.55,56

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB 2022b and SAS OnDemand for Academics. Study sample characteristics were presented as mean (SD) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Continuous maternal psychological distress scores and 18-month neurodevelopmental and parenting stress scores were used in the analyses below. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) allowing for multiple measurements per subject were used to estimate the effect of maternal psychological distress on the 18-month neurodevelopmental outcome and parenting stress in CHD, adjusting for maternal education, toddler’s sex, ventricle physiology (SV vs. 2V), perioperative events (i.e., cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest, seizures, stroke, sepsis, yes or no), and the length of hospitalization. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by randomly selecting all prenatal distress measures from a single time point per participant and examining their associations with 18-month neurodevelopmental outcomes and parenting stress in CHD using generalized linear regression models, adjusting for the same covariates as in the GEE analyses. These covariates were selected based on prior studies suggesting associations with neurodevelopmental outcomes in toddlers with CHD.57–61 An interaction term (prenatal distress score × ventricle physiology) was further added to estimate whether associations between prenatal maternal distress and infant neurodevelopmental outcomes differ between SV vs 2V CHD, with two-ventricle coded as 0 and single-ventricle coded as 1 in the model. GEE was also used to evaluate maternal psychological distress scores over time, in SV vs. 2V CHD, and in different education and race categories. ANOVA testing was performed to examine the toddlers’ 18-month neurodevelopmental outcomes in SV vs. 2V CHD. P-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for the number of exposures (STAI-S, STAI-T, PSS, EPDS) with a p < 0.0125 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study population

We enrolled 123 pregnant women (75 with male and 48 female fetuses) who completed prenatal maternal mental health measures between 21 and 39 gestational weeks, of which 71 completed the measures twice (mean time at visit_1: 29.59 gestational weeks; visit_2: 35.06 gestational weeks). The gestational age at birth for the cohort was 38.57 ± 1.08 weeks [range: 34–41.7; 94% born at term] and did not significantly differ between SV and 2V groups (mean: 38.50 vs. 38.65 weeks, p = 0.48). The majority of our participants (n = 98; 80%) attended college and (n = 85; 69%) reported employment in professional positions. Thirty-six offspring with CHD died (5 prenatally, 8 postnatally before neonatal cardiac surgery, 23 after neonatal cardiac surgery, prior to neurodevelopmental testing). Four subjects were excluded due to abnormal genetic diagnoses. Of the remaining 83 subjects eligible for 18-month follow-up, 17 were lost to follow-up and 2 withdrew from the study. Sixty-four toddlers (77% retention rate) completed neurodevelopmental testing. The BSID-III was administered at a mean age of 20.65 [range: 14–43 months], and the ITSEA at a mean age of 20.03 [range: 14–35] months. The demographic characteristics of our study cohort are shown in Table 1.

CHD diagnostic groups

The three most common CHD diagnostic groups in our cohort were hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) (n = 28, 23%), transposition of the great arteries (TGA) (n = 28, 23%), and tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) (n = 18, 15%). Fifty (41%) fetuses had SV CHD.

Maternal stress, depression, and anxiety

The mean ± SD scores for prenatal maternal state anxiety, trait anxiety, perceived stress, and depression were 37.40 ± 12.27, 34.54 ± 9.78, 15.36 ± 7.27, 7.25 ± 5.07, respectively. Fifty percent of the pregnant women reported elevated prenatal anxiety (48% for state anxiety, 32% trait anxiety), 60% with elevated stress, and 34% screened positive for depression. Forty-two percent tested positive for both stress and anxiety; 31% for stress and depression; 25% for anxiety and depression; and 24% for anxiety, stress, and depression. Sixty-nine percent of the pregnant women were positive for psychological distress. In women carrying fetuses with SV vs 2V CHD, prenatal maternal depression score trended higher in women carrying fetuses with SV compared to those carrying fetuses with 2V CHD (mean: 8.34 vs 6.60, p = 0.07) (Supplementary Table S1). The stress, anxiety, and depression scores did not significantly change as a function of advancing gestational age (all p > 0.05).

Maternal distress scores across different education levels and race categories are shown in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively. Maternal state anxiety scores were higher in mothers with graduate professional training compared to mothers with college graduation (40.36 vs 34.10, p = 0.01). Maternal trait anxiety and stress were higher in mothers with partial high school/high school graduate compared to mothers with college graduation (trait anxiety: 40.57 vs 32.15, p = 0.001; stress: 19.11 vs 13.53, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S2). Maternal psychological distress scores did not significantly differ across maternal race categories (“Asian or Pacific Islander”, “Black, not of Hispanic Origin”, “Hispanic”, and “White, not of Hispanic Origin”) (Supplementary Table S3). Although Black mothers compared to White mothers had higher stress scores (17.61 vs 14.19, p = 0.04), this difference was not statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S3).

Toddler’s neurodevelopmental results

The mean ± SD cognitive, motor and language composite scores for toddlers with CHD on the BSID-III were 99.23 ± 11.07, 94.92 ± 13.83, and 90.79 ± 14.13, respectively, of which 34% experienced language delays, 8% cognitive delays and 8% motor delays (score < 85). For the ITSEA, mean ± SD externalizing, internalizing, dysregulation, and competence scores were 50.95 ± 9.36, 49.07 ± 11.50, 46.25 ± 11.35 and 46.48 ± 9.38, respectively. The most common problems were internalizing behavioral problems (16%), followed by dysregulation (11%), externalizing (9%), and competence (9%) behaviors. Neurodevelopmental scores did not vary significantly with age at testing (Cognitive: β = 0.0008, p = 0.99; Language: β = −0.19, p = 0.56; Motor: β = 0.15, p = 0.65; Externalizing: β = −0.26, p = 0.38; Internalizing: β = −0.29, p = 0.43; Dysregulation: β = −0.14, p = 0.68; Competence: β = −0.15, p = 0.60). Compared to those with 2V CHD, toddlers with SV had significantly lower cognitive (92.0 vs 102.16, p = 0.002) and motor (85.21 vs 98.59, p = 0.002) scores (Supplementary Table S1).

Parenting stress at 18-month follow-up

Mean ± SD total stress, parental distress, parental-child dysfunctional interaction, difficult child and defensive responding scores on the PSI-SF were 59.10 ± 17.90, 22.23 ± 9.28, 15.94 ± 4.73, 21.33 ± 6.99, and 12.77 ± 5.84, respectively. Parenting stress scores did not significantly differ in toddlers with SV versus 2V CHD (Supplementary Table S1).

Association between prenatal maternal psychological distress and toddler’s neurodevelopmental outcomes

Elevated prenatal maternal depression was associated with lower motor scores (β = −0.64, p = 0.003); elevated prenatal maternal anxiety (state and trait), stress, and depression were associated with increased externalizing scores in toddlers with CHD (STAI-S: β = 0.21, p = 0.006; STAI-T: β = 0.27, p = 0.0007; PSS: β = 0.44, p < 0.0001; EPDS: β = 0.50, p = 0.003) (Table 2), controlling for maternal education, sex, ventricle physiology (SV vs 2 V), perioperative events, and length of hospitalization. Sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S4), in which prenatal maternal distress measures from a single time point per study participant were randomly selected, produced consistent findings with the main analyses in Table 2. Elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress continued to be associated with lower motor scores and higher externalizing scores at 18 months (Supplementary Table S4).

We also examined whether the associations between prenatal maternal distress and toddler neurodevelopmental outcome differed in SV and 2V CHD. Table 3 shows the β coefficients and p-values for the interaction between prenatal maternal psychological distress and ventricle physiology. Significant differences were observed between SV and 2V CHD in several associations: the association between prenatal maternal depressive symptoms and motor outcomes was stronger in SV vs 2V infants (EPDS: β = −0.59, p = 0.008), indicating a more pronounced adverse effect in SV vs 2V infants; for ITSEA outcomes, the effect of prenatal perceived stress on externalizing behaviors was higher in SV infants (PSS: β = 0.47, p = 0.001). Maternal depressive symptoms also showed a stronger association with dysregulation (EPDS: β = 0.77, p = 0.009), and the associations of maternal trait anxiety and stress with competence were stronger in SV vs 2V infants (STAI-T: β = −0.32, p = 0.005; PSS: β = −0.48, p = 0.0004); however, the associations between prenatal maternal distress and dysregulation or competence scores were not significant in the main analyses, which included all CHD subjects without the interaction term (Table 2).

Association between prenatal maternal psychological distress and parenting stress at 18-month testing

Elevated prenatal maternal (state and trait) anxiety, stress, and depression were associated with persistent parental distress at 18-month testing (STAI-S: β = 0.32, p = 0.0008; STAI-T: β = 0.50, p < 0.0001; PSS: β = 0.64, p < 0.0001; EPDS: β = 1.03; p < 0.0001) (Table 4). In addition, elevated prenatal maternal trait anxiety and depression were associated with increased reports of a ‘difficult child’ at 18-month testing (STAI-T: β = 0.20, p = 0.004; EPDS: β = 0.40, p = 0.006). Finally, maternal trait anxiety, stress, and depression were associated with significantly higher overall (total) stress scores at 18-month testing (STAI-T: β = 0.78, p < 0.0001; PSS: β = 0.85, p = 0.001; EPDS: β = 1.46; p < 0.0001) (Table 4). All these associations remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders, including maternal education, toddler sex, ventricle physiology, perioperative events, and the length of hospitalization.

Sensitivity analyses using prenatal maternal distress measures from a single time point per participant produced findings consistent with the main analyses in Table 4. Elevated prenatal distress remained associated with higher Parental Distress and Total Stress scores at 18 months, although associations with the Difficult Child subscale were attenuated and no longer significant after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S5).

Post hoc mediation analyses

Given the strong associations observed between prenatal maternal distress and postnatal parenting stress at 18 months, we performed post hoc causal mediation analyses to evaluate whether associations between prenatal maternal distress and infant neurodevelopmental outcomes were mediated by ongoing parenting stress, or whether associations persisted independent of parenting stress. Specifically, because prenatal maternal depression (EPDS) was significantly associated with infant motor scores, and prenatal maternal distress measures (STAI-S, STAI-T, PSS, EPDS) were significantly associated with infant externalizing scores (Table 2), we tested whether these associations were mediated by PSI-SF total stress measured at 18 months.

Table 5 shows that the association between prenatal maternal depression and infant motor outcomes was not significantly mediated by parenting stress at 18 months (indirect effect: β = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.42 to 0.18), and the direct effect was also not significant (direct effect: β = −0.58, 95% CI: −1.32 to 0.16). For infant externalizing outcomes, parenting stress at 18 months significantly mediated the associations with prenatal maternal trait anxiety, perceived stress, and depression (STAI-T: β = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.03–0.39; PSS: β = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05–0.45; EPDS: β = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.05–0.54). The corresponding direct effects were not statistically significant (STAI-T: β = 0.06, 95% CI: −0.23 to 0.30; PSS: β = 0.18, 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.48; EPDS: β = 0.21, 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.76), suggesting that postnatal parenting stress accounted for a substantial portion of the association between prenatal maternal distress and infant externalizing behaviors.

All mediation models were adjusted for maternal education, toddler sex, ventricle physiology (SV vs 2V), perioperative events, and length of hospitalization. Bootstrapping with 1000 samples was used to generate bias-corrected confidence intervals, and mediation was considered significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report that elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress in the setting of a fetal CHD diagnosis is associated with greater motor and social-emotional impairments in toddlers. Despite similar anxiety and stress scores for mothers carrying SV and 2V CHD, we observed different associations between prenatal maternal distress and motor and externalizing scores in SV CHD compared with 2V CHD. Moreover, we demonstrated that elevated prenatal maternal stress, anxiety, and depression were associated with persistently elevated parenting stress measures at 18-month testing. Elevated prenatal maternal anxiety and depression were also associated with increased PSI-SF Difficult Child subscale at 18-month testing. In addition, parenting stress at 18 months mediated the relationship between prenatal maternal distress and toddler externalizing behaviors.

Our findings highlight the high prevalence of psychological distress among mothers carrying fetuses with CHD requiring neonatal surgery, with 60% reporting heightened stress, 50% elevated anxiety, and 34% depressive symptoms, even though our cohort was largely composed of mothers with high educational attainment and professional employment. These rates are notably higher than those reported in low-risk pregnancies, where up to 27% of pregnant women in the upper/middle socioeconomic status experience elevated psychological distress.13,62,63 Consistent with prior studies, a prenatal diagnosis of CHD increases maternal stress, with rates nearly double those observed in mothers of low-risk pregnancies.9,64 Importantly, the effects of prenatal maternal psychological distress persist postpartum, as reflected by its significant associations with parenting stress scores at 18 months.

It is important to acknowledge the role of social determinants of health on maternal mental health during pregnancy, including socioeconomic status, race, and education levels. Our data suggest that prenatal stress and trait anxiety are more prevalent among pregnant women with partial or complete high school educations compared to those with college graduation. These data highlight the importance of addressing maternal mental health problems in pregnancy within a broader social context to ensure that all mothers and children have access to care and improved maternal-infant outcomes.

Our findings underscore the impact of elevated prenatal maternal psychological distress on neurodevelopment in toddlers with CHD, with a particular vulnerability observed in motor and social-emotional outcomes. Domains such as cognitive, language, learning and memory, behavioral problems, and neuropsychiatric dysfunction are affected by elevated maternal distress during pregnancy.14,18,22–30 Motor and social-emotional impairments have been previously reported in children with CHD.65–67 Our findings suggest connections between prenatal maternal mental health and motor and social-emotional development in CHD.

The underlying mechanisms of neurodevelopmental impairments associated with elevated maternal psychological distress are complex, but are thought to be driven by altered brain development and may have their origins early in the fetal period.68,69 The associations between prenatal maternal psychological distress and brain structural, functional, and metabolic changes have been widely reported in the infant, child, and adult offspring.19,20,70–83 More recently, emerging data suggest that elevated prenatal psychological distress impacts fetal brain development even in pregnancies with healthy fetuses. This includes impaired fetal hippocampal, cerebellar, and white matter volumes, increased cerebral cortical gyrification and sulcal depth, altered brain metabolism, and disrupted functional connectivity.11–14,84–87 Selective vulnerability of the hippocampal and cerebellum in utero fetuses with CHD have been reported in the setting of elevated maternal stress,10 which may underlie the long-term neurobehavioral complications of CHD.

In our cohort, toddlers with SV CHD were found to be at heightened risk for cognitive and motor problems compared to toddlers with 2V CHD. This aligns with previous research indicating that children with SV CHD exhibit lower intelligence quotient, learning, memory, and visual-motor performance compared to those with 2V CHD after cardiac surgery.34 Additionally, fetal total brain and cerebrum volumes were reported to be lower in SV CHD compared to 2V CHD excluding transposition of the great arteries,10 which could contribute to the neurodevelopmental differences observed between SV and 2V CHD. Importantly, our findings also suggest that the influence of prenatal maternal distress on toddler motor and social-emotional development may be more prominent in SV CHD compared to 2V CHD. Specifically, despite similar anxiety and stress scores for mothers carrying SV and 2V CHD, the adverse associations between prenatal maternal distress and motor and externalizing outcomes were more pronounced in SV versus 2V CHD, suggesting that SV infants may be particularly vulnerable to prenatal maternal distress for certain motor and socio-emotional outcomes. Additionally, our previous study indicated that prenatal depression is higher among women carrying fetuses with SV CHD compared to those with 2V CHD.10 Given that elevated prenatal maternal depression was associated with decreased motor and increased externalizing scores, as suggested by our current study, increased maternal depression in SV CHD may further contribute to the impaired developmental outcomes. These combined physiological and maternal psychological factors may underlie the more severe neurodevelopmental impairments observed in SV CHD compared to 2V CHD.

We also report associations between prenatal maternal distress and parenting stress scores at 18-month testing in CHD. Relatedly, higher parenting stress has been reported in parents of infants with CHD compared to parents of healthy peers.8,88 In addition, we found that elevated prenatal maternal trait anxiety and depression was associated with higher scores on the PSI-SF Difficult Child scale at 18 months. Previous studies reported that parents expressed difficulty with setting limits or discipline of the child with heart disease.89,90 Our findings indicate that elevated maternal psychological distress during pregnancy may contribute, at least in part, to persisting parental stress and challenges experienced in parenting surviving toddlers with CHD.

Importantly, the association between prenatal maternal depression and infant motor outcomes was not significantly mediated by parenting stress at 18 months, suggesting that prenatal maternal depression may influence motor development in CHD toddlers through other mechanisms not captured by postnatal parenting stress. In contrast, parenting stress mediated the relationship between prenatal maternal distress and toddler externalizing behaviors, indicating that some behavioral difficulties may be influenced by postnatal parenting stress. These findings may help guide clinical practice: associations not mediated by parenting stress highlight the need for prenatal maternal mental health screening, whereas outcomes mediated through postnatal parenting stress suggest that postnatal interventions targeting parental stress may help mitigate adverse child behavioral outcomes.

This study advances our understanding of the neurodevelopmental consequences of elevated prenatal psychological distress in pregnancies complicated with CHD. Our data highlight the need for universal maternal health screening and support across the prenatal-neonatal continuum of care and beyond hospital discharge following cardiac surgery. Maternal psychological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy, and mindfulness-based interventions, effectively reduce stress, anxiety, and depression.91–94 Additionally, mother-baby interventions like baby massage, skin-to-skin care, and family nurture interventions have been shown to enhance social-relatedness, attention, cognitive, and language development in high-risk preterm infants at 18 months.95–97 Our findings emphasize the importance of targeted maternal and mother-baby parenting interventions to improve developmental outcomes in children with critical CHD.

Potential limitations of our study include: 1) maternal distress assessment at specific time points may not fully capture the entire mental health status across pregnancy; 2) our participants may not represent other regions, as the metropolitan Washington DC area features a highly educated, low-unemployment population with greater healthcare access; 3) no information on the symptom course of the mothers in the 18-month follow-up period culminating in the child assessment is available; 4) relatively small sample size at the 18-month assessment; however, to our knowledge, this is the first study to include both prenatal maternal psychological distress data and toddler behavioral outcomes in this population. The long-term neuropsychological sequelae for mothers and children with CHD at school-age remain unknown and require ongoing follow-up.

CONCLUSION

Elevated maternal psychological distress during pregnancy is associated with impaired motor and social-emotional outcomes in toddlers with CHD as well as enduring parenting stress. These adverse effects are more pronounced in toddlers with SV vs 2V CHD, suggesting further vulnerability for this high-risk group. Postnatal parenting stress partially mediates the association between prenatal maternal distress and toddler externalizing behaviors. Identifying early modifiable risk factors for altered neurodevelopment in high-risk toddlers with CHD is critical for developing targeted interventions, aiming to reduce maternal psychological distress and optimize long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in this high-risk population.
