Exploring the Effects of Dietary Intake on Gut Microbiome Diversity and Composition of Older Adults with Type 1 Diabetes: A Pilot Study
Authors: Aastha S Dubal, Angelica Cristello Sarteau, Maria Andrea Azcarate-Peril, Zorka Djukic, Gabriella Ercolino, Angela Fruik, Julia Ho, Zoe Lee, Jeffrey Roach, Michael Valancius, Laura A Young, Ian M Carroll, Anna R Kahkoska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGut microbiome diversity and composition have been associated with type 1 diabetes (T1D) onset and progression. These features have not been explored among the growing number of older adults with T1D, including how they may differ by diet.ObjectivesIn the present observational study, we investigated differences in gut microbiome diversity and composition across quantiles of high and low macronutrient, fiber, and simple sugar intake.MethodsWe collected observational data on routine diabetes management, diet, and other health indicators from older adults with T1D over 7–9 d, including 2 24-h dietary recall interviews, diabetes technology, and medical record data. Stool samples were collected for gut microbiome characterization using high-throughput sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene. Gut microbiome characteristics were assessed by Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC2), Shannon index, and Bray–Curtis distances.ResultsAmong 17 older adults [mean (SD) age 73.5 (6.2) y; diabetes duration: 34.0 (16.0) y, and glycated hemoglobin: 7.0 (0.9) %], increased fat intake was associated with enriched genus Lactococcus, increased fiber intake was associated with enriched genus Hemophilus and unclassified Clostridium within family CAG:508, and increased fructose intake was associated with enrichment of unclassified genus SFMI01 within family Christensenellaceae. Greater carbohydrate intake was associated with depletion of the family Enterobacteriaceae. Microbial diversity differed significantly with greater fiber and fructose intake.ConclusionsWe found statistically significant evidence suggesting that the gut microbiome of older adults with T1D may be modulated by macronutrient, fiber, and fructose intake. Similar trends between fiber intake differences and significantly different microbial community structure have been found in younger populations. Together with the literature, our other findings suggest that macronutrient, fiber, and fructose intakes may impact the gut microbiome differently in older adults with T1D.

Introduction

It is estimated that 100 trillion bacteria encompassing 500–1000 species reside symbiotically within the human intestinal tract and are known together as the gut microbiome [1,2]. They coexist with the host to form a superorganism and can change based on host phenotypic variations, such as sexual dimorphisms, disease status, or age and environmental factors, including medication use, physical activity, or diet [1,2].

Diet is a widely accepted environmental factor that affects gut microbiome composition [2]. Different dietary substrates (i.e., fiber) serve as energy sources for different gut microbes and create byproducts such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that can interact with the host’s physiology [2]. Presence or absence of certain nutrients can affect the abundance and diversity of gut microbiome inhabitants and by extension, circulating metabolites [2].

Gut microbiome–host interactions are involved in the susceptibility and development of several autoimmune conditions, including type 1 diabetes (T1D) [3,4]. Marked by an inability to produce insulin because of an autoimmune attack on pancreatic β-cells, individuals with T1D often experience hyperglycemia and ketosis without exogenous insulin [5]. At all ages, diet is a vital aspect of blood sugar optimization and avoiding repeated exposure to hyper- and hypoglycemia, with the ultimate goal of reducing risk of short-term (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis) and long-term consequences (i.e., macro- and microvascular complications).

Preclinical and clinical evidence support distinct changes in gut microbiome characteristics of children and adults with T1D compared with those without [[6], [7], [8]]. Through production of SCFAs, gut microbial communities may affect T1D onset, progression, and complications [3,4]. Individuals with T1D have been previously shown to have diminished microbial richness (α-diversity), altered microbial community structure (β-diversity), and differential abundance of SCFA-producing taxa in response to dietary intakes, indicating high gut microbiome variability; however, they have yet to be investigated in older adults with T1D [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Current literature in younger adults and children with T1D suggests that increases in carbohydrate and protein intake affect the relative abundance of the Bacteroides genus in the gut microbiome [12,13]. One study found a positive association between fiber intake and enrichment of the Bifidobacterium genus, and another found negative associations between fructose intake and the abundance of Bacteroides, Alistipes, and Akkermansia genera [10,12]. Three studies reported that fiber intake impacts α- and β-diversity, but the directions of associations vary [[9], [10], [11]]. None of the studies reported differences in α- and β-diversity as a function of fructose intake. No differences have been reported in gut microbiome characteristics across varying percentages of total caloric intake from protein, carbohydrates, and fats [14].

These gut microbiome characteristics remain unexplored in adults aged ≥65 y with T1D, a growing population with unique clinical considerations [[15], [16], [17]]. Moreover, the impact of other health behaviors (i.e., diet) has not been characterized and dietary recommendations for this population are sparse [18]. Our objective was to conduct an exploratory analysis in individuals aged ≥65 y with predominantly longstanding T1D to assess the magnitude of cross-sectional associations between dietary intake and gut microbiome. Here, we investigated how diversity, composition, and relative abundance of taxa varied by dietary fiber, simple sugar, and macronutrient intakes, which we conceptualized as actionable targets for future interventions to potentially modulate the gut microbiome through diet in older populations with T1D. As a part of this exploratory analysis, we aim to estimate effect sizes that could be used to inform the power calculation for future studies.

Research design and methods

To select the dietary factors to examine in this study, we synthesized existing research from human studies investigating dietary factors that impact gut microbiome features in individuals with T1D. A search of the PubMed database was conducted to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles published between 1 January, 2000 and 20 January, 2025 that provided empirical data on α-diversity, β-diversity, and/or relative microbial abundance changes in response to dietary factors in individuals with T1D.

Participants

Participants for this study were recruited from the diabetes eating and longevity investigation (DELI) Snapshot study, a small, clinical nutrition pilot that collected observational data to characterize patterns of nutrient intake, physical activity, sleep/wake habits, and diabetes management among older adults with T1D. Multiple sources of clinical and behavioral data were collected from 25 participants over a 10-d period from 27 March, 2024 to 20 August, 2024. Data were analyzed from 1 September, 2024 to 7 February, 2025.

The DELI Snapshot study recruited adults aged ≥65 y with a clinical diagnosis of T1D or latent autoimmune diabetes of adulthood treated as T1D. All participants were using insulin therapy (rapid or long acting) and were receiving care in the University of North Carolina Healthcare (UNCH) system, which was defined as having had an outpatient visit with primary care (including internal medicine, family medicine, or geriatric medicine) or with endocrinology within the past 1 y. As a pilot study with a resource limited, English-speaking team and study materials in English, we required that individuals be fluent in English.

Individuals with visual, hearing, or other impairments that would preclude them from completing study activities were excluded. Individuals with a medical history of gastrointestinal or digestive accessory organ surgery and individuals with antibiotic use within 2 wk of study participation were excluded because they are known manipulators of the gut microbiome [19]. Participants were screened for current oral probiotic or prebiotic use. One participant reported consumption of a 2-oz juice-based drink containing inulin from blue agave fiber and Bacillus Subtilis De111 (https://goodbelly.com/collections/wellness-shots).

Of the 25 participants in the DELI Snapshot study, 20 were eligible and consented to provide a whole stool sample (N = 20). One participant did not provide a stool sample because of experiencing discomfort while using the specimen collection kit (N = 19). One participant was excluded from analyses because of recent antibiotic use, and another because of having pancreatogenic diabetes instead of autoimmune T1D (N = 17).

Initial eligibility screening was performed via the UNCH electronic health record (EHR) and the Carolina data warehouse for health. We also conducted a manual chart review of provider schedules for the UNCH at Eastowne endocrinology clinic in EPIC to identify eligible participants with upcoming clinic visits. Recruitment methods included MyChart messaging, email, telephone outreach, and in-person outreach in the UNCH at Eastowne endocrinology clinic.

A purposive sampling strategy was designed to include a wide variety of social, demographic, and health-related factors that may influence how diet and diabetes management are approached by participants. Thus, during the screening call, participants were also asked a set of purposive sampling questions over the phone (Appendix B). Sampling characteristics of interest included age, race, ethnicity, sex, rurality, healthcare utilization, independent diabetes management compared with caregiver/assisted living, and diabetes technology use.

Following completion of the study, participants were given a $120 incentive. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC) (protocol number 22-2704) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Dietary and clinical data collection

Participants completed 2 unannounced T1D-adapted 24-h dietary recall phone interviews (45–60 min each), 1 on a weekend and 1 on a weekday, to collect patterns of dietary intake and insulin dosing from the previous 24 h. The UNC Connected Health for Applications and Interventions Core conducted these interviews using the multiple pass method [20]. Data were collected and nutrient intakes were estimated using Nutrition Data System for Research software version 2023 (12/18/2024) developed by the Nutrition Coordinating Center, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN.

ASD and GE conducted a manual chart review of each participant’s medical history from the year prior to enrollment from UNC EHR, including BMI (in kg/m2), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and comorbidities.

Stool sample collection

Individuals collected their first whole bowel movement after their first T1D-adapted 24-h dietary recall using the Fisherbrand Commode Specimen Collection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Paper instructions with guiding images were provided to all participants in addition to a questionnaire to self-report the date, time, color, and texture of the bowel movement (Appendix C). The samples were shipped to UNC on ice using FedEx Priority Shipping or dropped off in person with study staff 24–48 h post collection. On receipt, the samples were homogenized, aliquoted into 5 1-mL portions, and stored at −80°C.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Genomic microbial DNA from human fecal samples was isolated by the UNC Microbiome Core. Samples were transferred to a 2 mL tube containing 200 mg 106/500 μm glass beads (Sigma) and 1 mL of Qiagen InhibitEX buffer. The suspension was agitated for 10 min on a digital vortex mixer at 1000 g and incubated at 95°C for 5 min, followed by agitation for 15 s on a Digital Vortex Mixer. After a 1-min centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube containing 22.5 μL of Qiagen proteinase K and 0.3 mL of Qiagen AL buffer was added, agitated on a digital vortex mixer for 15 s, and incubated at 70°C for 10 min. After a brief centrifugation, 0.3 mL of ethanol was added to the lysate and vortexed. DNA was purified using a standard on-column purification method with Qiagen buffers AW1 and AW2 as washing agents and eluted in DNase-free water [[21], [22], [23]].

Universal primers targeting the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were used to amplify 12.5 ng of total DNA. Primer sequences contained overhang adapters appended to the 5’ end of each primer for compatibility with the Illumina sequencing platform. The primers used were F515/R806. Master mixes contained 12.5 ng of total DNA, 0.5 μM of each primer, and 2× KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems). The thermal profile for the amplification of each sample had an initial denaturing step at 95°C for 3 min, followed by cycling of denaturing at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and a 30-s extension at 72°C (25 cycles), a 5-min extension at 72°C and a final hold at 4°C. Each 16S amplicon was purified using the AMPure XP reagent (Beckman Coulter). In the next step, each sample was amplified using a limited-cycle PCR program, adding Illumina sequencing adapters and dual-index barcodes (index 1(i7) and index 2(i5)) (Illumina) to the amplicon target. The thermal profile for the amplification of each sample had an initial denaturing step at 95°C for 3 min, followed by a denaturing cycle of 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s and a 30-s extension at 72°C (8 cycles), a 5-min extension at 72°C and a final hold at 4°C. The final libraries were again purified using the AMPure XP reagent (Beckman Coulter), quantified, and normalized before pooling. The DNA library pool was then denatured with NaOH, diluted with hybridization buffer, and heat denatured before loading on the MiSeq reagent cartridge (Illumina) and on the MiSeq instrument (Illumina) at the High-Throughput Sequencing Facility in the Carolina Center for Genome Sciences at the UNC School of Medicine. Automated cluster generation and paired-end sequencing with dual reads were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions [[24], [25], [26]].

For validation of the DNA isolation process, a known bacterial community, ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (catalog number D6300), and a blank composed of only DNA isolation reagents were included in the DNA extraction process and again in the library preparation. In addition to the isolation controls, the library preparation included a library blank composed of library preparation reagents alone.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequencing output from the Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform was converted to fastq format and demultiplexed using Illumina BclFastq 2.20.0. Sequence counts per sample ranged from 48,137 to 640,241, with a mean of 217,065 counts/sample. The resulting paired-end reads were processed with QIIME 2 (2024.5). Index and linker primer sequences were trimmed using the QIIME 2 invocation of Cutadapt. The resulting paired-end reads were processed with the QIIME 2 wrapper for DADA2, including merging paired ends, quality filtering, error correction, and chimera detection. Amplicon sequencing variants from DADA2 were assigned taxonomic identifiers using the Greengenes 2 2022.10 database. Quantification results were normalized and transformed to relative abundance using Total Sum Scaling normalization [[27], [28], [29]].

Statistical analysis

Usual daily dietary intakes of fiber (total, soluble, insoluble, and pectin), protein (total, animal, and plant), fat [total, omega-3 (n–3) fatty acids, omega-6 (n–6) fatty acids], carbohydrates, and fructose were estimated by averaging the daily intakes participants reported on weekday and weekend days. Fructose was selected as a key dietary exposure because associations between fructose intake and microbiome composition have been observed in individuals with T1D, unlike other simple sugars [12]. Subgroups were determined as quantiles (median split of nutrient intake estimates). All data cleaning and manipulation efforts were supervised and verified by a member of the study team with expertise in analyzing dietary intake data (ACS).

One individual’s intakes of protein (total and animal) and carbohydrates were statistical outliers, defined as >3 1 SD from the mean [30]. Although such values are not unreasonable for an individual to consume within 1 d, they were unrepresentative of the target population subgroup represented by our small sample and were excluded from analyses [30,31]. Intakes of all other nutrients analyzed were reasonable and representative of our sample [30].

Microbial diversity analysis and visualization were performed using QIIME 2 (2024.5), R (4.3.3), and MicrobiomeAnalyst (https://www.microbiomeanalyst.ca/). Species accumulation and rarefaction curves were estimated using QIIME 2 at a rarefaction depth of 5000 sequences/subsample. α-Diversity at the amplicon sequencing variant (ASV) level was measured by Shannon Diversity Index, which quantifies the number of distinct ASVs. Unpaired Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed. P values were corrected using the Benjamin–Hochberg method. β-Diversity, a measure of gut microbiome community uniqueness, was quantified at the ASV level by Bray–Curtis distance (dissimilarity). Multivariable statistical analyses were performed using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). Results were visualized through principal coordinate analysis. Analysis of the differential abundance of microbial taxa at the genus level was performed through the R package Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2). An absolute LogFoldChange cutoff value of 1.0 was used. P values were corrected using the Holm–Bonferroni method.

An α = 0.1 significance threshold was applied for all corrected P values, given the exploratory nature of the study and small sample size.

Data and resource availability

The datasets generated and analyzed during this study are available from the corresponding author on request to protect participant confidentiality.

Discussion

In a sample of 17 older adults with T1D, we found statistically significant differences in gut microbiome diversity and composition according to dietary intake. Our findings empirically support a biological hypothesis regarding the impact of diet on gut microbiome characteristics in older adults with T1D. To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize differences in α-diversity, β-diversity, and differential abundance of taxa in older adults with T1D as they relate to macronutrient, fiber, and fructose intakes. The significance of our pilot study lies in the potential for nutrient consumption to modulate the gut microbiome in older adults with T1D differently than in younger populations, offering novel insights for future dietary interventions to optimize health and longevity in this group [15,32,33]. We also determine sample sizes to power future studies and demonstrate the feasibility of whole stool sample self-collection by older adults with T1D.

Comparison to older adults without T1D

It is known that in older adults, the gut microbiome is impacted by years of environmental exposures, including diet, resulting in reduction of influential commensal taxa [34]. In our sample of older adults with T1D, enrichment of unclassified member SFMI01 of family Christensenellaceae was associated with higher fructose intake. A review from Ghosh et al. [34] on the gut microbiome and aging in adults without T1D highlighted that Christensenellaceae enrichment was associated with disease-free aging in 7 out of 23 studies included in their review. No other gut microbiome characteristics from our study align with extant evidence in older adults, suggesting that dietary intakes of macronutrients, fiber, and fructose in people with T1D may be associated with gut microbiome changes aside from those that occur with aging. Future studies should investigate the role of fructose in the aging gut microbiome, specifically Christensenellaceae abundance, in older individuals with and without T1D to inform nutrition recommendations for this group.

Comparison to younger individuals with T1D

Our study may support findings from younger populations with T1D in the literature. It is known that fiber greatly impacts the gut microbiome, and it has been the focus of several studies investigating the gut microbiomes of people with T1D [9,10]. Harbison et al. [9] reported significant differences in β-diversity across fiber intake subgroups in children with T1D, whereas Ho et al. [10] observed a similar effect in their randomized control trial on prebiotic supplementation in pediatric patients with T1D. The results from our pilot support this finding; individuals with T1D who consume more fiber may have distinct gut microbiomes from those consuming less total fiber, regardless of age.

Our pilot study contributes novel findings as well. For example, in populations aged <65 y with T1D, there is conflicting evidence regarding the relationship between carbohydrate intake and Bacteroides abundance, a producer of the SCFA butyrate [12,13]. Igudesman et al. observed an inverse relationship whereas Mejia-Leon et al. observed a direct relationship [12,13]. In our study, no significant differences in Bacteroides abundance were observed between carbohydrate intake quantiles. However, the distinct taxa Enterobacteriaceae, which maintain an anaerobic gut environment, synthesize vitamins K and B12, and protect against pathogenic colonization, were enriched with high carbohydrate consumption [35]. Our study findings could suggest that carbohydrate intake has more profound effects on Enterobacteriaceae abundance than Bacteroides abundance as patients with T1D age and future studies are needed to further probe this relationship.

No prior studies in patients with T1D have found a relationship between fat intake and differential abundance of microbial taxa, whereas our study did, pointing to fat intake as a potential dietary exposure for future studies to further investigate. Our data suggest that fat intake may have a greater impact on Lactococcus abundance in the gut microbiome of individuals with T1D as they age. Lactococcus, used to ferment dairy, promotes microbial diversity and SCFA production, both of which are characteristics of a flourishing gut microbiome [36,37].

We also found novel associations between total fiber intake and gut microbiome characteristics that are not found in existing literature. In our sample of older adults with T1D, we observed an increase in α-diversity to be associated with higher fiber intake whereas Harbison et al. [9] demonstrated that there was no difference in α-diversity across total fiber intake groups in children with T1D. Ho et al. [10] observed a slight decrease in Shannon Diversity Index in children receiving prebiotic supplementation (initial: 5.30 ± 0.08, final: 5.13 ± 0.08; and P = 0.035). Our results suggest that for older adults with T1D, increased fiber intake may impact α-diversity inversely than in younger populations and may contribute to a more taxonomically diverse gut microbiome as they age.

Current evidence of the effects of fiber intake on the differential abundance of microbial taxa in the gut microbiome of individuals with T1D varies. Ho et al. [10] reported Bifidobacterium longum enrichment alongside long-term fiber supplementation (logLDA >4); however, Igudesman et al. [12] observed no such effect. Our results suggest that fiber intake may play an increasingly important role in gut microbiome diversity and composition as individuals with T1D age, particularly for Hemophilus and CAG:508 abundance. Members of Hemophilus are potential pathogens and can upregulate inflammation, whereas members of order TANB77 such as CAG:508 are known biomarkers for increased efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade cancer immunotherapies [38,39]. Taken together, our results suggest that in older adults with T1D, increased fiber intake may promote growth of inflammation-related gut microbes, highlighting this nutrient as an important exposure of interest for future studies.

No prior studies link fructose intake to changes in α- or β-diversity in individuals with T1D, but our study found significant associations, pointing to fructose as a unique modulator of the gut microbiome in older adults with T1D. We also observed enrichment of SFMI01, an unclassified member of the family Christensenellaceae, in individuals consuming higher fructose. Christensenellaceae have been found to be depleted in individuals with metabolic syndrome [40,41]. Specifically in individuals with obesity and T1D, Igudesman et al. [12] observed an inverse relationship between a 10 g/d increase in fructose intake and Akkermansia, Bacteroides, and Alistipes abundance, but not Christensenellaceae. Although our results depart from preclinical evidence that greater high-fructose corn syrup intake is inversely related to Christensenellaceae abundance in the gut microbiome, they could indicate fructose having an increasingly important role in gut microbiome characteristics as individuals with T1D age, further indicating fructose as an important target for future studies in this population [42].

Limitations

The small sample size (N = 17) limits statistical power and generalizability to larger, more diverse populations of older adults with T1D and hinders stratification by sex, another potential modulator of the gut microbiome. Additionally, the subgroup analyses were based on quantiles rather than clinical thresholds or recommended intakes and are therefore sample dependent. Larger studies that follow our sample size calculations based on our effect size estimates are needed to further explore and replicate these findings in a more diverse population. Finally, our results may be confounded by early-life exposures that were not probed in this study (i.e., long-term prior antibiotic use, history of infections, breastfeeding or formula-fed at birth, and other microbiome disruptions). PICRUSt2 functional gene analysis and metabolomics investigation of circulating SCFA levels are needed to begin understanding the molecular mechanisms driving these findings and their potential implication in T1D progression and management outcomes in older adults.

Despite these limitations, our data suggest that macronutrients, fiber, and fructose intake may modulate the gut microbiome, potentially influencing diversity and SCFA production, which could promote health in older adults with T1D.
