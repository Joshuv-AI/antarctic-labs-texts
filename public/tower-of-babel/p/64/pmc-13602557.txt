Chamber-specific cardiac proteome remodeling in a Göttingen minipig model of obesity and diabetes
Authors: Samantha Gallero, Christian T Voldstedlund, Simone Krog, Trine Pagh Ludvigsen, Lisbeth Høier Olsen, Thomas E Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity and Type 2 Diabetes are major contributors to cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction, yet the molecular mechanisms driving early myocardial alterations remain incompletely understood. Evidence from rodent models and end-stage human disease suggests that cytoskeletal remodeling and oxidative stress may contribute to early increases in cardiomyocyte stiffness and hypertrophy. Whether these processes are involved at earlier disease stages in translationally relevant large-animal models remains unclear.MethodsHeart tissue from male Göttingen minipigs subjected to a 13-month intervention with a standard control diet, high-fat-fructose-cholesterol diet, or high-fat-fructose-cholesterol with streptozotocin-induced diabetes was analyzed. Untargeted proteomics was performed on left atrium and left ventricle tissue, followed by pathway enrichment analyses to identify diet- and chamber-specific proteomic alterations.ResultsGlobal proteomic analyses revealed that anatomical region represented the dominant source of variance, with ~ 200–300 proteins significantly regulated across dietary interventions. Pathway enrichment analyses highlighted alterations in protein and macronutrient metabolism, mitochondrial function, and extracellular matrix organization. Correlation and Hallmark analyses further linked ventricular remodeling to glucose-associated and mitochondrial pathways, while atrial remodeling was more closely associated with metabolic and nutrient-sensing pathways. Among 75 microtubule and 53 redox-related proteins examined, 16 and 14, respectively, were significantly altered in a chamber- and intervention-dependent manner.ConclusionEarly cardiac hypertrophy associated with obesity and/or diabetes is accompanied by extensive proteomic remodeling, characterized by distinct atrial and ventricular profiles. However, the relatively modest changes in microtubule and redox-related proteins suggest that these are unlikely to be primary drivers of early myocardial remodeling.

Introduction

Obesity and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) are major risk factors for cardiac complications, including hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and increased myocardial stiffness [1, 2]. While previous work has largely examined extracellular matrix remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and myocardial fat infiltration, increasing evidence supports cardiomyocyte-intrinsic alterations, particularly involving cytoskeletal and redox regulatory pathways, as key contributors to early cardiac remodeling.

At the cellular level, disturbances in the microtubule cytoskeleton and redox homeostasis have been linked to increased cardiomyocyte stiffness, impaired calcium (Ca2+) handling, and disrupted metabolic signaling [3–6]. Microtubules are highly dynamic components of the cardiomyocyte cytoskeleton that regulate intracellular transport, organelle positioning, mechanotransduction, and structural integrity. Studies in rodent models suggest that obesity enhances α-tubulin expression and detyrosination, thereby increasing microtubule stability and cellular stiffness [7, 8]. Additionally, obesity increases α-tubulin acetylation, thereby enhancing microtubule stability and disrupting glucose metabolism in mice [9].

Redox balance within cells is maintained by tightly regulated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which arise from specific intracellular sources, such as enzymes and organelles, and are countered by the antioxidant system [10]. In cardiac muscle, microtubule stiffening has been reported to hyperactivate stretch-sensitive NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2), a key source of cytosolic ROS, particularly hydrogen peroxide, leading to altered intracellular Ca2+ handling [11–14]. Another member of the same enzyme family, NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), has also been linked to the development of cardiomyopathy [15]. In addition to these enzymatic ROS sources, mitochondria represent a major site of ROS generation, particularly under conditions associated with obesity and diabetes-related heart failure [4]. In cardiomyocytes, NOX4 is localized to mitochondria [16], and activation of NOX2 has been reported to exacerbate mitochondrial ROS production [17]. Notably, both mitochondria and NOX2 appear to be positioned and/or regulated by the microtubule network [4, 13], while oxidative stress can directly modify microtubule dynamics and function [18]. Together, these observations support the concept that microtubule remodeling and redox dysregulation are mechanistically interconnected processes that may contribute to the early stages of cardiometabolic cardiac remodeling.

Despite growing evidence linking obesity and diabetes to cardiac dysfunction, important gaps remain in understanding how early cardiometabolic stress reshapes the cardiac proteome across anatomically and functionally distinct cardiac chambers. Most available evidence derives from rodent models and late-stage human disease cohorts, with limited insight into early molecular remodeling in large-animal models. Moreover, previous studies have predominantly focused on the whole heart or on the left ventricle, while chamber-specific responses, particularly within the atria, remain insufficiently characterized.

To address these knowledge gaps, heart tissue from a Göttingen minipig model of diet- and diabetes-induced cardiometabolic stress was analyzed. This model shares key structural and physiological characteristics with the human heart and allows direct comparison between atrial and ventricular chambers. Recent large-scale proteomic comparisons have demonstrated that human and pig hearts exhibit substantial similarity, further supporting the translational value of this model [19]. Heart tissue samples were obtained from a previously established Göttingen minipig cohort that was fed for 13 months with a Standard diet (SD), a High-Fat, High-Fructose, High-Cholesterol (FFC) diet, or an FFC diet combined with Streptozotocin (STZ) to induce a diabetic phenotype. The obese and obese-diabetic animals developed larger hearts and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy [20, 21], consistent with early-stage cardiometabolic remodeling. Using untargeted proteomics, we evaluated whether protein-level alterations in microtubule-associated and redox-related pathways could already be detected at this early stage, and whether these changes were chamber-specific within the heart. By comparing atrial and ventricular proteomic remodeling, this study aimed to characterize chamber-specific molecular adaptations associated with cytoskeletal dynamics and oxidative signaling during the early stages of obesity- and diabetes-associated cardiac remodeling.

Results

Cardiac hypertrophy develops in both obese and diabetic minipigs, accompanied by compensatory hyperinsulinemia in the obese and hyperglycemia in the diabetic group

Male castrated Göttingen minipigs were assigned to one of three dietary interventions for 13 months: SD, FFC diet, or FFC diet in combination with STZ treatment (Fig. 1A). Following the intervention, animals in both the FFC and FFC + STZ groups exhibited significant increases in body mass compared to the SD group (Fig. 1B). Postmortem assessment of heart weight revealed significant cardiac hypertrophy in both the FFC and FFC + STZ groups relative to SD-fed controls, with no significant difference between the FFC and FFC + STZ groups (Fig. 1C). To evaluate systemic metabolic alterations, fasting plasma insulin and glucose concentrations were measured. The FFC group showed significantly higher plasma insulin levels compared to both SD and FFC + STZ groups (Fig. 1D), indicative of compensatory hyperinsulinemia. In contrast, the FFC + STZ group exhibited markedly elevated fasting plasma glucose levels compared to the SD and FFC groups, consistent with the impaired insulin production characteristic of Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) and late-stage T2DM (Fig. 1F). Plasma glucose levels in the SD and FFC groups were not significantly different.

Global proteomic profiling reveals regional separation between the left ventricle and left atrium.

Global proteomic profiling of left ventricular and left atrial tissue samples identified a total of 5,673 features, of which 5,244 passed quality filtering and were included in downstream analyses (Fig. 2A). The overall distribution of protein intensities demonstrated a broad dynamic range with consistent signal across samples, supporting robust quantitative profiling across cardiac regions (Fig. 2A, C). Principal component analysis revealed clear separation primarily driven by anatomical region, with principal component 1 (38% variance explained) separating left ventricle from left atrium samples (Fig. 2B). Hierarchical clustering similarly grouped samples primarily according to cardiac region rather than treatment condition (Fig. 2C). In contrast, treatment-dependent separation was comparatively modest, suggesting that chamber-specific proteomic identity represented the dominant source of variance across samples.

Paired comparisons between the left ventricle and left atrium samples revealed extensive yet consistent regional proteomic differences across all treatment groups (SD, FFC, and FFC + STZ) (Fig. 2D–F). Proteins consistently more abundant in the left ventricle compared to the left atrium included the contractile protein Myosin heavy chain-β (MYH7), consistent with the distinct contractile properties and mechanical demands of ventricular myocardium. In contrast, proteins such as Atrial natriuretic peptide (NPPA), B-type natriuretic peptide (NPPB), and Myosin light chain 7 (MYL7) displayed consistently lower abundance in the left ventricle compared to the left atrium, in accordance with specialized properties of the atrium. Together, the preservation of these regional proteomic signatures across all experimental groups highlights the stability of chamber-specific molecular specialization despite the induction of metabolic disease. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that the ventricular proteomic profile was enriched for pathways related to mitochondrial function, oxidative metabolism, and energy production, including the respirasome, oxidoreductase complexes, and generation of precursor metabolites (Fig. 2G–I). In contrast, atrium-enriched proteins were linked to extracellular region components, regulatory processes, and response-to-stimulus pathways, further highlighting functional specialization between cardiac regions.

ANOVA identifies a restricted subset of proteins with coordinated regulation

To systematically quantify the relative contributions of anatomical region, treatment, and their interaction to proteomic variation, a mixed-design ANOVA was performed for each protein quantified across all samples (i.e., without missing values). Given the paired structure of the dataset and the presence of both within- and between-subject factors, Generalized Eta Squared (GES) was used to estimate effects for each feature. Across the proteome, the chamber effect (left ventricle vs. left atrium) accounted for the largest proportion of explained variance (Fig. 3A), consistent with the dominant separation observed in principal component and clustering analyses (Fig. 2B–C). In contrast, treatment effects contributed more modestly, while treatment x chamber interaction effects were restricted to a smaller subset of proteins (Fig. 3A–C).

To prioritize biologically meaningful changes, proteins were filtered based on both nominal statistical significance (p < 0.05) and a substantial GES threshold (> 0.3). Although a considerable number of proteins met these criteria for individual main effects, substantially fewer proteins simultaneously exhibited significant treatment and treatment × chamber interaction effects (Fig. 3D). This subset is particularly informative because it captures proteins whose responses to treatment are context-dependent, i.e., different depending on anatomical region. Such proteins are more likely to reflect mechanistically relevant, tissue-specific adaptations rather than global or nonspecific shifts, making them stronger candidates for biological interpretation and downstream validation.

Overlap analysis identified a restricted subset of proteins (n = 38) satisfying these criteria (Fig. 3D–I). Despite the large size of the proteome, the limited overlap suggests that coordinated regulation across both systemic (treatment) and local (tissue-specific) axes is relatively uncommon. Hierarchical clustering of these proteins revealed distinct abundance patterns across treatment groups and cardiac regions, supporting the existence of compartment-specific proteomic remodeling in response to metabolic perturbation (Fig. 3E). Functionally, this subset included proteins involved in mitochondrial metabolism (e.g., Isocitrate dehydrogenase 3 subunit gamma (IDH3G), Succinate-CoA ligase ADP-forming beta subunit (SUCLA2)), redox regulation (e.g., Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)), and structural or regulatory processes (e.g., Structural maintenance of chromosomes 3 (SMC3), Neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (NCAM1)) (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that a focused group of proteins integrates systemic perturbations with regional cardiac identity, suggesting that key metabolic and regulatory pathways are selectively and differentially modulated across cardiac compartments.

Obese and diabetic hearts display differential enrichment of lipid, extracellular matrix, and mitochondrial pathways

To define chamber-specific biological pathways associated with early metabolic disease-induced cardiac remodeling, GSEA was performed on the left atrial and left ventricular proteomic profiles across experimental groups. Overall, the enrichment patterns revealed distinct remodeling trajectories between cardiac regions, with the left atrium exhibiting predominantly metabolic adaptations, whereas the left ventricle displayed stronger extracellular matrix and structural remodeling signatures, particularly following diabetes induction (Fig. 4A–D).

Each dot represents a gene ontology (GO) term, plotted by its normalized enrichment score (NES), with positive NES values (left) indicating activated pathways and negative NES values (right) indicating suppressed pathways. In the left atrium of FFC-fed animals (Fig. 4A), enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms were primarily associated with lipid handling and oxidative metabolism, including cholesterol efflux, regulation of lipid transport, lipid oxidation, mitochondrial pathways, and energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds. Together, these changes suggest altered metabolic and mitochondrial remodeling in response to caloric overload. In contrast, negatively enriched pathways included chromosome organization, chromatin organization, protein-DNA complex organization, DNA binding, and mRNA processing, indicating suppression of transcriptional and RNA-associated regulatory processes.

The left atrium of FFC + STZ animals retained enrichment of pathways related to lipid handling, including cholesterol efflux, lipoprotein particles, and protein-lipid complexes (Fig. 4B). However, the overall enrichment profile shifted toward catalytic activity, protein folding, and extracellular region-associated proteins, suggesting progression toward a broader adaptive and secretory remodeling phenotype. In contrast, suppressed pathways included transmembrane transporter activity and eicosanoid metabolic processes, consistent with altered transmembrane transport and lipid signaling pathways.

In the left ventricle of FFC-fed animals, enriched pathways were dominated by extracellular matrix-associated and structural processes, including extracellular matrix organization, external encapsulating structure, and Ca2+ binding (Fig. 4C). In parallel, suppressed pathways included RNA processing, mRNA metabolic processes, and mRNA processing, suggesting altered transcriptional regulation and RNA turnover in response to caloric overload.

The left ventricle of FFC + STZ animals displayed the most extensive remodeling profile (Fig. 4D), characterized by enrichment of pathways related to extracellular matrix remodeling and structural organization, including extracellular matrix, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, collagen trimer, glycosaminoglycan binding, peptidase regulator activity, and calcium ion binding. Additional enrichment of pathways related to skeletal system development and response to stimulus further supported activation of broad structural remodeling programs. In contrast, negatively enriched pathways were predominantly mitochondrial, including mitochondrial envelope, mitochondrial membrane, and cardiac ventricle development, suggesting suppression of mitochondrial and energy metabolism-related processes following diabetes induction. Collectively, these findings support the existence of distinct chamber-specific remodeling responses to caloric overload and diabetes, with the atrium displaying predominantly metabolic adaptations and the ventricle exhibiting stronger extracellular matrix and structural remodeling signatures.

Proteomic features associated with physiological variables and Hallmark pathways

Correlation analysis between proteomic features and physiological variables

To explore functional associations, correlation analyses were performed separately between ventricular and atrial protein abundance and physiological variables (body weight, heart weight, plasma glucose, and plasma insulin). Filtering for correlations exhibiting a Kendall’s rank correlation coefficient above 0.65 or below − 0.65 yielded a limited number of strong associations in both ventricular and atrial comparisons (Fig. 5A–E, H–L). In the ventricle, most proteins meeting this threshold were associated with plasma glucose concentration (Fig. 5A, D), forming an interconnected network centered around glucose (Fig. 5A). Notably, several of these proteins (e.g., Complement component 8 gamma chain (C8G), Nebulette (NEBL), Inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain 1 (ITIH1), and Chaperonin containing TCP1 subunit 7 (CCT7)) displayed extensive co-correlation, potentially indicating coordinated regulation within glucose-associated pathways. In contrast, correlations with body weight and heart weight were fewer and more sparsely connected, indicating a more limited and less coordinated proteomic association with these variables. While a similar number of glucose-associated correlations was observed in the atrium, the dominant associations in this compartment instead related to heart weight and body weight (Fig. 5H–L), suggesting a shift toward structural or morphological coupling rather than metabolic integration. Together, these findings indicate that ventricular proteomic variation is more tightly aligned with glycaemic control, particularly plasma glucose concentration, whereas atrial proteomic variation appears more closely linked to body- and cardiac size-related parameters.

Hallmark pathway associations of correlated proteomic features

To determine whether proteomic features exhibiting strong associations with physiological variables were linked to established biological pathways, features with significant Kendall rank correlations above 0.65 or below − 0.65 were mapped to the 50 human gene sets included in the MSigDB Hallmark collection. This collection summarizes well-defined biological states and processes by reducing redundancy among overlapping gene sets, thereby providing a more focused biological framework for pathway interpretation.

In the left ventricle, correlated proteomic features were primarily associated with oxidative phosphorylation and complement pathways (Fig. 5F). Notably, features annotated to oxidative phosphorylation were exclusively negatively associated with physiological variables, whereas complement-associated features were exclusively positively associated. Among individual proteins, ITIH1 displayed the highest number of Hallmark annotations, with three associated pathways, while Isocitrate dehydrogenase (NAD+) 3 beta subunit (IDH3B) and CD55/ Decay-Accelerating Factor (DAF) each mapped to two Hallmark gene sets (Fig. 5G).

In the left atrium, the Hallmark terms containing the greatest number of correlated proteomic features were fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling (Fig. 5M). At the individual protein level, Superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and Sorbitol dehydrogenase (SORD) showed the broadest Hallmark representation, with six and four associated Hallmark annotations, respectively (Fig. 5N). Together, these analyses suggest that ventricular proteomic associations were dominated by mitochondrial and immune-related pathways, whereas atrial associations involved a broader metabolic signature, including fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and nutrient-sensitive signalling, potentially reflecting the regional shifts occurring in the cardiac proteome across the applied treatments.

Chamber-specific regulation of metabolic, immune, and structural proteins across obese and diabetic groups

To compare the most significantly altered cardiac proteins across dietary and diabetic conditions, hierarchical clustering of the most significantly regulated proteins across all experimental groups was performed (Fig. 6A). This analysis revealed distinct, chamber-specific abundance patterns between the left atrium and the left ventricle.

Among the upregulated proteins, most were upregulated in the left ventricle, particularly in animals from the FFC + STZ group, suggesting more severe metabolic impairment under diabetic conditions. Notable targets included apolipoproteins (Apolipoprotein A-IV; APOA4 and Apolipoprotein E; APOE) and secreted metabolic regulators (Signal Peptide Peptidase-Like 2C; SPP2C), which were consistently elevated across chambers and groups. Extracellular matrix remodeling proteins, such as Elastin (ELN), Charged multivesicular body protein 6 (CHMP6), and Collagen type XIV alpha 1 chain (COL14A1), as well as lipid-binding proteins like Fatty Acid–binding protein 4 (FABP4), also showed increased abundance, particularly in ventricular tissue. In addition, several proteins linked to translation and metabolism, including Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2B subunit epsilon; EIF2B5 and IDH3B were elevated, suggesting elevated anabolic activity.

In contrast, downregulated proteins were predominantly detected in the left atrium, with comparable levels in both the FFC and FFC + STZ groups. These included lipid-handling proteins (e.g., Perilipin-5; PLIN5, ATP-binding cassette transporter A1; ABCA1), immune-related factors (e.g., Lysozyme C-3; LYSC3), mitochondrial-associated proteins (e.g., Glutamate-rich mitochondrial matrix protein; MGRRF, A0A287ADE7), and extracellular matrix regulators (e.g., Olfactomedin-like protein 2 A; OLFML2A). In addition, uncharacterized proteins such as A0A287AP95 and A0A8W4FIK4, both predicted to contain immunoglobulin-like domains, were also consistently reduced. Together, these changes suggest that obesogenic diet-induced metabolic stress, with or without superimposed diabetes, converges on shared molecular pathways, potentially impairing lipid metabolism, immune defense, and tissue structural integrity. Notably, the contrasting regulation patterns observed between the atrium and ventricle highlight chamber-specific vulnerabilities and adaptabilities in the context of early cardiometabolic disease.

Redox-related proteins show limited but distinct, chamber-specific regulation across obese and diabetic groups

To assess whether early cardiac remodeling in obesity and diabetes was associated with oxidative stress, the abundance of 53 curated redox-related proteins was analyzed across cardiac chambers and experimental groups (Fig. 6B). Hierarchical clustering of log2 fold changes revealed that only a limited number of proteins were differentially regulated, with changes showing strong chamber- and condition-specificity.

In the FFC versus SD comparison, 4 proteins showed significant upregulation, predominantly in the left atrium, including the antioxidant enzymes Peroxiredoxin-3 (PRDX3) and Thioredoxin reductase 2, mitochondrial (TXNRD2), as well as the autophagy adapter Sequestosome-1 (SQSTM1, also referred to as p62). In the FFC + STZ versus SD comparison, 7 proteins were significantly altered. These included upregulation of SQSTM1, Thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), a matricellular protein linked to inflammation and tissue remodeling, and ATP synthase inhibitory factor 1 (ATP5IF1), which modulates mitochondrial ATP production and protects against ATP hydrolysis under stress conditions. Conversely, Parkin (PRKN), TP53-induced glycolysis and apoptosis regulator (TIGAR), GPX4, and Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 3 (PDK3) were significantly downregulated, suggesting disruptions of redox balance, mitochondrial quality control, and metabolic regulation.

Overall, the relatively limited number of regulated redox-associated proteins supports the notion that overt oxidative stress-related remodeling is not a dominant feature at this stage of disease progression, despite evidence of chamber-specific molecular adaptations.

Microtubule protein regulation is chamber-specific and limited in both obesity and diabetes, with broader changes in the diabetic group

To determine whether early cardiometabolic stress was associated with alterations in cytoskeletal organization, the abundance of 75 proteins across cardiac chambers and treatment groups was analyzed (Fig. 6C). Consistent with the analysis presented earlier, the heatmap showed restricted, chamber-dependent changes.

In the FFC versus SD comparison, 4 microtubule-related proteins showed significant alterations. Microtubule-associated proteins 1 A/1B light chain 3 A (MAP1LC3A, also referred to as LC3A), a marker of autophagy, and Translationally controlled tumor protein (TPT1), involved in microtubule stabilization and stress responses, were upregulated. In contrast, Tubulin alpha-8 chain (TUBA8), a structural component of microtubules, and Histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6), a regulator of tubulin acetylation and cytoskeletal plasticity, were downregulated.

In the FFC + STZ versus SD comparison, a total of 12 microtubule-related proteins were significantly regulated, representing a broader response than observed in the FFC group and predominantly affecting the left ventricle. Within the left atrium, several proteins, including Microtubule-associated protein 1B (MAP1B), SLAIN motif-containing protein 2 (SLAIN2), Chaperonin-containing TCP1 subunit 4 (CCT4), Tubulin beta-4B chain (TUBB4B), and Chaperonin-containing CCT2, were upregulated, pointing to an atrial-specific enhancement of microtubule stabilization and folding mechanisms. Meanwhile, in the left ventricle, upregulated proteins included Alpha-tubulin N-acetyltransferase 1 (ATAT1), which promotes microtubule stability via acetylation, along with MAP1LC3A, Septin-8 (SEPTIN8), and Microtubule-associated protein RP/EB family member 1 (MAPRE1), all of which are involved in cytoskeletal organization and dynamic remodeling. In contrast, only Ferritin heavy chain-like protein (FES), TPT1, and Caveolin-3 (CAV3) were downregulated, suggesting selective cytoskeletal repression under diabetic stress. Altogether, these findings reveal a more pronounced and chamber-specific cytoskeletal response to combined caloric overload and diabetes, particularly in the ventricular compartment.

To complement the proteomics findings, we also measured post-translational modifications (PTMs) of α-tubulin by Western blotting, focusing on acetylation (Lys40) and detyrosination, two well-established markers of microtubule stability. Western blot analysis did not reveal significant changes in either modification across most groups (Fig. 6D–F). In the left ventricle, acetylated-α-tubulin levels were slightly but significantly reduced in the FFC group compared to SD, whereas FFC + STZ animals showed values comparable to controls (Fig. 6D and E, top panel). Detyrosinated-α-tubulin levels remained largely unchanged across all groups in both cardiac chambers (Fig. 6D and E, bottom panel). Thus, only a few and subtle differences were observed for microtubule PTMs.

Materials and methods

This study is part of a larger, long-term intervention including 84 castrated male Göttingen minipigs (Ellegaard Göttingen minipigs A/S Dalmose, Denmark) to investigate diet-induced metabolic complications related to obesity and diabetes, focusing on the heart and other key organs. Therefore, data for some of the experimental groups have already been reported in other publications [20, 21, 45–49].

Animals, study design, and experimental diets

Following a two-week acclimatization period on a standard minipig chow diet, 29 castrated male Göttingen minipigs from the larger 84-animal cohort, aged 6 to 7 months, were weight-stratified and assigned into three experimental groups: (1) a lean control group fed a SD (n = 7), (2) an obese group FFC diet (n = 14), and (3) a T1DM group induced by STZ, also fed the FFC diet (n = 8) (Fig. 1A). Animals in the SD group were fed the standard diet throughout the study, receiving 2% of the average cohort body weight per day until reaching 35 kg, after which the daily feed ration was fixed at 500 g. Animals in the FFC and FFC + STZ groups were initially fed 2% and 2.5% of the respective average cohort body weight per day, with a maximum daily feeding amount of 1000 g during the first 11 months of the study. In the final two months, the feeding amount for the FFC group was reduced to 500 g/day due to severe obesity and emerging lameness. The FFC group underwent an initial loading phase, during which animals were fed a high-fat, high-fructose, and 2% cholesterol diet (5B4L, TestDiet®, Missouri, USA) for the first five months (Table 1). To prevent excessive increases in plasma cholesterol levels, this was followed by a similar diet containing 1% cholesterol (9G4U, TestDiet®) for the remaining eight months of the study (Table 1). In contrast, animals in the FFC + STZ group were fed the 1% cholesterol diet (9G4U, TestDiet®) throughout the entire study period to maintain plasma cholesterol levels comparable to those of the FFC group. All animals were fed individually once per day for 13 months. Throughout the study, animals were housed at the Experimental Animal Unit of the University of Copenhagen under controlled environmental conditions, with a room temperature maintained at 22–24 °C, relative humidity of 50–70%, and a natural light/dark cycle. Animals were group-housed, except during specific procedures requiring individual housing, and had free access to water and bedding material.

Diabetes induction protocol

Six weeks after initiation of the dietary intervention, a Type 1-like diabetes was induced in the FFC + STZ group by intravenous administration of STZ (60 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, Denmark) for three consecutive days, following a protocol modified from Gerrity et al. [50]. To maintain a stable hyperglycemic state (~ 15 mmol/L fasting blood glucose), animals received daily subcutaneous injections of long-acting insulin (Insulin glargine, Lantus®, Sanofi-Aventis) adjusted as needed. Blood glucose was monitored daily during the first week after STZ administration and subsequently 1–2 times per week throughout the study. To maintain fasting glucose levels near 15 mmol/L, animals received daily subcutaneous insulin treatment, with dosage adjustments according to a previously described protocol [46].

Plasma parameters

Plasma insulin concentrations were determined by Luminescence Oxygen Channeling Immunoassay, based on a previously described method [51], with a slight modification in which the originally described biotinylated antibody X14-6F34 was replaced by the biotinylated mAb OXI005. Plasma glucose levels were measured using the BIOSEN S_Line auto analyzer (EKF Diagnostics, Cardiff, United Kingdom), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Euthanasia procedures and sample collection

At the end of the dietary intervention, the minipigs were fasted overnight, and body weight was measured prior to euthanasia. Animals were anesthetized by intravenous or intramuscular injection of a Zoletil®50 mixture, containing zolazepam and tiletamine, combined with ketamine, xylazine, and butorphanol, as previously described [52]. Once a surgical level of anesthesia was achieved, euthanasia was performed by exsanguination through transection of the axillary arteries. Following euthanasia, the heart was excised via thoracotomy, weighed, and sectioned. Samples from the left atrium, left ventricle, and other cardiac chambers not relevant to this study were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

SDS-PAGE and western blot analyses

Tissue samples from left ventricle and left atrium were homogenized for 1 min at 30 Hz using a Tissue Lyser in ice-cold homogenization buffer (10% glycerol, 1% Tergitol, 20 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, 10 mM NaF, 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 1 mM EGTA (pH 8.0), 2 mM Na₃VO₄, 10 µg/ml leupeptin, 10 µg/ml aprotinin, and 3 mM benzamidine). Lysates protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon, Millipore) and blocked at room temperature for 1 h in 3% BSA in TBS-Tween 20. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (Table 2), followed by a 1 h room-temperature incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Immobilon Forte Western HRP, Millipore) and visualized with the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was used to assess total protein loading and transfer efficiency. Band intensities were quantified using ImageLab 6.1 software, and standard curves were used to ensure detection remained within the linear range for each target protein.

LC-MS/MS sample preparation

Tissue samples were lysed in cold 100 µL Easypep lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher), 1 µL Pierce Universal Nuclease 100 kU (Thermo Fisher) using the Beatbox tissue disruptor (Preomics) with 20 cycles of 30 s BeatBox medium setting followed by 3 min incubation on ice. Samples were incubated for 10 min at 95 °C followed by 10 min of BeatBox in medium setting. After, protein concentration was calculated using the BCA assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were reduced with 5 mM of Tris (2-carboxyethyl)phosphine for 15 min at 55 °C, alkylated with 20 mM chloroacetamidefor 30 min at room temperature, and digested by adding Trypsin/LysC at 1:50 enzyme/protein ratio at 37 °C for 16 h. Peptides were desalted using EasyPep peptide clean-up plates (Thermo Fisher) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Eluates were dried by vacuum centrifugation, resuspended in 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and quantified with Tecan Lunatic. After the quantification, samples were diluted to 0.1 µg/µl in a TwincTec plate for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis.

LC-MS/MS method

Peptides were loaded on a 110 cm µPAC NEO HPLC column (Thermo Scientific) in 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and 0.1% formic acid in water using a NEO Vanquish HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) UHPLC. Peptides were separated applying a non-linear gradient of 5–50% of 0.1% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile over 100 min at a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 50 °C. Upon elution, peptides were injected via an EASY-Spray source and 20 μm fused silica emitter (Evosep) into a Tribrid Ascend mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with a Field Asymmetric Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) device (Thermo Scientific). FAIMS compensation voltage was set at − 45 V. Spray Voltage was set to 2800 (V). Data was acquired in data-independent mode. The MS full scan was acquired with an Orbitrap MS resolution of 120.000 in the range of 390–1010 m/z. Automatic Gain Control (AGC) target was set to 100%, and the maximum injection time was 251 ms. Data-Independent Acquisition scans with 8 Th width and 1 Th overlap spanning a mass range of 400–1000 m/z were acquired at 15,000 MS resolution, AGC target 1000%, and maximum injection time of 27 ms. Higher-energy collisional dissociation was set to 30%.

Bioinformatics

Data were log2-transformed, and missing values were not imputed. A minimum threshold of n ≥ 2 per group was applied for unpaired comparisons using two-sided Welch’s t-tests and multiple hypothesis testing was corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Likewise, paired mean differences were only calculated for features quantified across at least 2 pairs. While the low sample size limits statistical power, this approach was deemed appropriate given the exploratory nature of the study. Consequently, analyses requiring complete datasets (e.g., principal component analysis) were restricted to proteins with no missing values. For analysis of main effects using repeated-measures two-way ANOVA, only features with complete data across all samples were included GSEA and ORA were performed using Sus scrofa as reference organism, with all quantified proteins used as background. GO enrichment analyses were conducted across three categories: cellular component (GOCC), biological process (GOBP), and molecular function (GOMF). GSEA was performed as previously described in detail [53], while ORA was assessed using a hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Redundant GO terms were collapsed using a similarity threshold of 0.7. Correlation analyses were performed using Kendall’s rank correlation coefficients, and the Human Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [54] was used to map feature to hallmark associations.

Statistical analysis

All proteomic data analyses were conducted in RStudio (v4.3.3), while statistical analyses for non-proteomic data were performed using GraphPad Prism® 10 for Windows (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Graphical illustrations were created using BioRender.

Discussion

Our phenotypic characterization confirms that prolonged FFC feeding was sufficient to induce obesity and hyperinsulinemia in male Göttingen minipigs without causing hyperglycemia within the study timeframe, whereas the addition of STZ successfully established a diabetic phenotype marked by hypoinsulinemia and persistent hyperglycemia. Despite these divergent systemic metabolic profiles, both interventions elicited comparable cardiac hypertrophy, as further supported by histological evidence of cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area [20], underscoring a convergent impact of obesity and diabetes on early structural remodeling of the heart. At the molecular level, the interventions induced pronounced alterations in proteins linked to macronutrient regulation, mitochondrial function, and the extracellular matrix, whereas only a limited number of microtubule- and redox-associated proteins were significantly affected. Taken together, these findings suggest that distinct cardiometabolic insults can trigger overlapping cardiac maladaptation, to which microtubular and redox disturbances may contribute. However, these processes are unlikely to represent major early drivers of obesity- and diabetes-associated cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling.

Our proteomic analysis identified hundreds of proteins that were significantly altered across different experimental conditions and anatomical chambers. The overall response was highly intervention-dependent, with distinct patterns emerging across both diet interventions and cardiac chambers. For example, SORD was altered exclusively in response to the FFC diet, whereas APOA4 responded only to the FFC + STZ intervention. In contrast, IDH3B was modulated under both dietary conditions, suggesting shared stress-related regulatory mechanisms. Chamber differences were also evident. Some proteins responded specifically in the left atrium (e.g., Serum amyloid P-Component; APCS), others only in the left ventricle (e.g., C-C Motif chemokine ligand 21; CCL21), and some showed consistent changes in both chambers (e.g. LYSC3). APCS enrichment in the left atrium may reflect chamber-specific differences in immune and fibrotic remodeling, as serum amyloid P/PTX2 has established immunomodulatory and anti-fibrotic functions [22]. Conversely, the stronger association of CCL21 with the left ventricular remodeling in the present study is consistent with previous reports linking CCL21/CCR7 signaling to pressure-overload–induced left ventricular remodeling and heart failure progression [23], although whether this reflects fundamentally distinct chamber-specific inflammatory programs remains unclear. These findings nonetheless emphasize that the atrial and ventricular compartments of the heart exhibit distinct molecular responses to metabolic stressors such as obesity and diabetes. In the context of cardiovascular disease, this is particularly relevant. In hypertension, the left atrium often undergoes remodeling earlier than the left ventricle, due to its increased sensitivity to mechanical stretch and neurohumoral activation (e.g., sympathetic stimulation and Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System signaling), along with early changes in Ca2+ handling and electrophysiological properties [24]. A previous study of experimental congestive heart failure in dogs also showed far more extensive and dynamic gene-expression remodeling in the left atrium than in the left ventricle, particularly in pathways related to fibrosis, inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix remodeling, highlighting profound chamber-specific cardiac adaptation [25]. This underscores the importance of analyzing cardiac chambers separately, as studies in rodent models that rely on whole-heart or left-ventricle-only analyses may overlook critical chamber-specific adaptations.

Among the significantly altered proteins, several have been previously linked to cardiomyopathy and cardiac metabolic dysfunction. Four of these will be discussed in more detail. Firstly, the APOA4 was significantly increased in the FFC + STZ group, with consistent upregulation observed in both the atrium and ventricle, suggesting a response associated with the diabetic-induced metabolic state. Although direct associations between APOA4 and cardiomyopathy remain limited, recent studies have implicated APOA4 in cardiac amyloidosis [26, 27], and certain APOA4 variants have been linked to modulation of cardiovascular risk [28, 29]. Beyond its role in lipid transport, APOA4 has been shown to suppress hepatic glucose production, enhance glucose uptake in white and brown adipose tissue, promote fatty acid oxidation, and potentiate glucose-stimulated insulin secretion [30]. These pleiotropic metabolic functions make APOA4 a protein of interest for further investigation in the context of Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a condition closely associated with metabolic dysregulation in humans [31]. SORD was specifically upregulated in the atrium in response to the FFC diet alone, indicating chamber- and condition-specific regulation. SORD is a key enzyme in the polyol pathway, catalyzing the conversion of sorbitol to fructose, and has been implicated in several cardiomyopathic conditions. In the context of diabetes, elevated blood glucose levels may increase flux through the polyol pathway, leading to sorbitol accumulation in cardiac tissue, which may contribute to diabetic cardiac dysfunction [32]. Higher atrial SORD expression may reflect the greater metabolic and stress-responsive remodeling capacity of the atrial myocardium compared with the ventricles, including enhanced activation of oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways during cardiac stress. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of SORD has been shown to reduce ischemic damage in isolated perfused heart models [33], supporting its potential as a therapeutic target in ischemia-related cardiac diseases.

The chemokine CCL21 was significantly upregulated in the ventricle in response to the FFC diet, suggesting chamber-specific chemokine activation during early metabolic stress. Elevated circulating CCL21 levels have been reported in chronic heart failure and are independently associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [34]. In a murine model of myocardial infarction, CCL21 blockade via neutralizing antibodies reduced infarct size, attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration, and improved cardiac function [35]. Additionally, in patients with aortic stenosis, increased myocardial expression of CCL21 and its receptor, C-C Motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7), correlated with myocardial fibrosis and poorer clinical outcome [36]. Together, these findings support a pathogenic role for CCL21 in promoting cardiac inflammation, adverse remodeling, and heart failure progression, and suggest its potential as a biomarker or therapeutic target in cardiometabolic disease. Finally, the lipid droplet-associated protein PLIN5 was significantly downregulated in our study in three of the four comparisons, specifically in both atrial and ventricular chambers of the heart in response to the FFC diet. PLIN5 is proposed as a potential therapeutic target that protects cardiomyocytes from lipotoxicity through both lipid droplet–dependent and independent mechanisms [37]. Consistent with this, experimental PLIN5 deficiency has been shown to exacerbate cardiac hypertrophy [38], underscoring its importance in maintaining cardiac lipid homeostasis. Identification of proteomic changes, as highlighted here, may not only provide mechanistic insight but also guide the discovery of novel therapeutic targets for HFpEF and other cardiomyopathies.

Interconnected alterations in the microtubule cytoskeleton and redox regulatory pathways have previously been associated with various cardiomyopathies, particularly in advanced disease models and patients with conditions such as HFpEF. Therefore, we specifically analyzed proteins related to these pathways. Overall, our proteomic analysis revealed modest yet significant changes, largely reflecting treatment- and chamber-specific responses. Several of the left atrium-specific proteins identified here (MAP1B, SLAIN2, Tubulin beta-4B chain (TUBB4B), CCT2 and CCT4) converge on microtubule dynamics and cytoskeletal proteostasis, suggesting that atrial-specific remodeling may involve altered microtubule turnover, trafficking or structural adaptation rather than isolated regulation of unrelated proteins. This supports that cardiomyocyte microtubules are extensively remodeled in heart disease and regulate mechanics, intracellular transport and excitation–contraction coupling, while TRiC/CCT chaperonins are required for actin/tubulin folding and cardiac myofibrillar integrity [39]. Another notable example was TIGAR, the expression of which was significantly reduced in obese-diabetic ventricle, with a similar, though less pronounced, reduction observed in the obese non-diabetic comparison. TIGAR is a p53-inducible enzyme with fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (Fru-2,6-BPase) activity [40]. By degrading Fru-2,6-bisphosphate, an allosteric activator of Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK1), TIGAR decreases glycolytic flux and redirects glucose metabolism toward the pentose phosphate pathway, enhancing ribose-5-phosphate production for nucleotide synthesis and generating NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense [40]. Rodent studies indicate that reduced TIGAR activity can protect the heart against ischemia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis [41, 42], pressure-overload heart failure [43], and Angiotensin II-induced cardiac hypertrophy [44], suggesting a potentially adaptive role of TIGAR downregulation under metabolic and hemodynamic stress. Conversely, ATAT1 was significantly upregulated in the ventricle of the FFC + STZ group. ATAT1 catalyzes the acetylation of α-tubulin on Lysine 40, a post-translational modification previously reported to increase in diet-induced obesity in mice, where it has been linked to disrupted glucose metabolism [9]. Interestingly, the enzyme responsible for removing this modification, HDAC6, was modestly but significantly downregulated in the ventricle of FFC group compared to SD group. Despite these proteomic changes in the enzymes regulating tubulin acetylation, direct measurements of α-tubulin acetylation levels did not reveal significant upregulation, suggesting that changes in these post-translational mechanisms do not occur at this early disease stage. Nevertheless, the proteomic shifts could prime the heart for later changes in α-tubulin modification. Alternatively, the early cytoskeletal adaptations to caloric overload and diabetic stress may involve subtle or compartment-specific mechanisms that are not detectable by global western blot quantification.

In conclusion, obesity and diabetes induced widespread proteomic changes in male Göttingen minipigs, which may contribute to the early development of cardiac remodeling and dysfunction. Microtubule and redox-related proteins were generally not enriched by the interventions, suggesting that these are not major drivers of early pathology. Notably, the atrial and ventricular compartments displayed distinct molecular responses, highlighting the value of chamber-specific profiling for uncovering chamber-dependent mechanisms of cardiometabolic stress.
