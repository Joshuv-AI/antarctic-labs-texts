Transdisciplinary research on water security in transboundary river basins
Authors: Aliya Assubayeva, Stefanos Xenarios, Alicia Correa, Jorge Forero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWicked problems, such as water security, require a shift to transdisciplinary research by bringing disciplinary perspectives, conducting participatory research, and developing case-specific solutions. Translational research (TR), one of the transdisciplinary approaches, was applied in our study to translate the broad theoretical framework and research implications of water security into practical considerations, priorities, and actionable measures to better define the research-policy-society interactions that emerged in the Aral Sea basin in Central Asia and the Mira-Mataje basin in Latin America. The implementation of TR revealed notable differences in the water security priorities of the two case studies; however, there are similarities regarding increasingly competing water needs, the urgency of rehabilitating and expanding aging and limited water infrastructure, low preparedness for climate-related hazards, and weak water governance mechanisms. The application of TR in transboundary basins reveals the need for knowledge synthesizers and knowledge brokers to bridge the gap between research, policy, and society.

Introduction

The divergence between scientifically based assumptions and contextual narratives in water science is discussed in the literature and the policy discourse by reflecting on the difficulty of communicating research findings into policy implementation.1,2,3,4 “Wicked problems,” such as water security, are characterized by limited data, uncertainties, conflicting values and stakes, and the need for urgent decision-making, and require a shift from multidisciplinary and interdisciplinary studies to transdisciplinary research.5,6,7 Pohl defines transdisciplinary research as the exploration of socially relevant issues through the integration of disciplinary perspectives and the inclusion of participatory research and case-specific knowledge, aiming to develop knowledge that is oriented toward the common good, encompassing descriptive, normative, and practical aspects (p. 625).8 Transdisciplinary research is still evolving, and methodologies vary depending on the research objectives, analytical approaches, the integration of academic and non-academic actors, the level of stakeholder engagement in the co-production of knowledge, and the potential impact aimed at addressing the complex problems.5,6,7,8,9,10,11

Depending on the nature of the scientific discovery and the discipline, it can become challenging to translate research findings into practice, adoption, and implementation.1,2,3,4,5 The translational research (TR) approach, also known as knowledge-to-action or research-to-practice, has recently been employed as a more integrated and multi-perspective approach to applying scientific knowledge to real-world challenges by bridging the gap between theory and practice, thereby developing evidence-based solutions. TR connects scientific discoveries with policy formulation and practical implementation, engaging diverse stakeholders in the co-production of knowledge.12,13,14 The TR approach has been widely applied in medical studies to emphasize the need for a shift from basic research to applied research through implementation, diffusion, and dissemination to practice-based research aiming to improve policy and have an impact.15,16,17 Recent studies have also introduced TR in the water sector to facilitate the integration of scientific insights, policy considerations, and practical applications to tackle evolving water-related issues, recognizing the inadequacy of addressing these challenges solely through one disciplinary lens, scientific inquiry, or policy implementation.12,13,14,17 The TR approach is one of the methodologies in transdisciplinary research, also including quantitative storytelling, participatory action research, citizen science5,6; however, it distinguishes itself through a structured, staged methodology that bridges academic knowledge, policy priorities, and community perspectives.3,12,13 Unlike quantitative storytelling, which emphasizes narrative framing and stakeholder deliberation, TR emphasizes iterative validation across academic, policy, and societal levels, making it particularly suitable for contexts with fragmented governance and limited stakeholder engagement.13,14,15

In our study, we adapted the TR methodological approach to define and frame water security in transboundary river systems of Central Asia and Latin America. Water security has been widely discussed among scholarly and policy discourses as an overarching framework to address several challenges, including climate change uncertainties and stressors, population growth and competing water needs, and degradation of environmental goods and services.18,19,20,21,22 Various disciplines have extensively analyzed the concept of water security; however, a universally accepted definition remains elusive. Gray & Sadoff20 define water security as “the availability of adequate quantity and quality of water for livelihoods, health, ecosystems, and production” (p.548). Some interpretations highlight the intricate and multi-faceted nature of water security.23,24,25 Several frameworks and indices were suggested to measure water security by focusing on different regions such as Europe and Central Asia,26 Asia and the Pacific,18,27 or market-oriented economies,28 by introducing various diagnostic and assessment tools and indicators.25,29 However, context-specific conceptualization and operationalization of water security on transboundary scales are understudied,30,31 especially in the Global South.19,21,23,32,33

Case studies in our research are from the Global South, namely the Central Asian case, represented by the Aral Sea basin (Aral basin) enclosing two major transboundary rivers - Amu Darya and Syr Darya, formed in the Pamir and Tien Shan mountains – their tributaries shared by Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, and Afghanistan (Figure 1). During the Soviet Union, water resources were managed as a single system with interconnected water infrastructure, dominantly for cotton irrigation in arid and semi-arid areas, causing the environmental disaster of the Aral Sea.22,34,35 After independence, water disputes over water allocation in the region have increased due to conflicting national water needs among riparian countries.36,37Figure 1Transboundary river basins of the study(A) The Aral basin is located in Central Asia.(B) The Mira-Mataje basin is in Latin America. The Latin American case examines the Mira-Mataje transboundary river basin, shared by Colombia and Ecuador, where the Mataje River serves as the border between the two countries (Figure 1). The Mira and Mataje Rivers originate from the Andean mountains and flow westward to the Pacific Ocean. This case study has a significant cultural heritage attributed to indigenous and Afro-descendant communities that have inhabited the region for centuries.38,39 However, these communities have been historically overlooked despite their long presence and traditional knowledge and worldviews for the protection of ecosystems and biodiversity.39,40

This study introduces the TR approach to identify the dimensions of water security in the transboundary river systems of Central Asia and Latin America and their practical applications, specifically addressing the societal challenges in the two case studies. The TR approach is applied in three stages (Table 1): a systematic review of scientific publications (first stage), translation into practice and reaching consensus on the water security priorities (second stage), and translation to society and local communities (third stage). In the first phase, the review is structured on the AWDO framework27 and Xenarios et al.,22 where water security consists of interdependent five dimensions: household and urban water security, economic water security, environmental water security, water-related hazards, and water governance. The second phase of the TR involves communicating scientific findings to stakeholders and water professionals in the two case studies to reach some agreement on the water security priorities in the two regions using multi-rounds of Delphi survey. The concluding stage of the TR involves research field trips to Kazakhstan and Ecuador, providing insights into the cultural perspectives on water held within local communities. Central Asia and Latin American cases differ in geography (such as land cover in Figure 1), history, and socio-economic development. There are, nonetheless, notable shared characteristics pertaining to ineffective management procedures and inadequate oversight of water resources. Also, in both cases, hazard extremes are intensified due to environmental changes as evidenced by El Niño and La Niña events in Latin America39,41,42,43 and repeated heatwaves and droughts in Central Asia.44,45,46 The need for framing water security to address socio-technical and environmental challenges in both cases has been underscored in the literature without, however, sufficiently explaining how the research findings translate into policy direction in the two regions.19,21,23,32,33 From a research and policy perspective, the study contributes to the urgent need to refine water security objectives and address relevant contestations through inclusive and participatory approaches, incorporating the knowledge and values of local communities. By introducing the TR approach, we bridge knowledge from water research, perceptions of policymakers and water professionals, and local communities’ perspectives to strengthen water security via targeted policy priorities in transboundary basins. Empirical research, data triangulation, and data integration from multiple sources (academic literature, surveys, and interviews) facilitated the application of TR to a comprehensive understanding of water security objectives and underpinned the validity of the findings. The research findings emphasize the importance of translating scientific outcomes into practical knowledge and policy actions by developing the capacity of knowledge brokers and synthesizers. The applied methodological approach aims to enhance the understanding of water security objectives in the transboundary systems of Central Asia, Latin America, and other regions facing similar challenges.

Results

As indicated in Table 2, in both regions, the main challenges for the household and urban water security dimension are traced to the deteriorating and limited water supply and hygiene facilities, insufficient wastewater treatment, and limited progress on Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG 6). Indicatively, the degradation of supply and sanitation facilities and their consequent impact on the achievement of SDG6 is evident in both cases, although remarkably different infrastructural conditions characterize each region. In the case of Central Asia, all countries have inherited large-scale water and wastewater systems dating back to Soviet times, which are deteriorating but, in many instances, are still operated to this day.34,35,47 In the Latin American case, a lack of basic water supply and sanitation facilities is discernible, particularly in peri-urban and rural areas, which are also characterized by pervasive poverty.48,49,50

The common challenges in the economic dimension mostly revolve around deteriorating irrigation systems, inappropriate agricultural practices, and the withdrawal and discharge of extractive industries into water. In both cases, the population’s livelihoods heavily depend on water resources for agriculture, but outdated or missing irrigation systems threaten the population as competition for transboundary water resources increases. In the Aral basin, the coordination, funding, and administration of interconnected water-energy infrastructure for transboundary rivers are mainly the subject of ongoing negotiations among the riparian countries.47,51,52 In the case of the Mataje River, which serves as both a border between Colombia and Ecuador and a hotspot for illegal activities, violence is occurring, leading to forced displacement, migration, and pollution from mining activities.38,39,53,54

The literature highlights a closely intertwined relationship between environmental and hazard-related factors in both regions, primarily centered on insufficient predictive capacities, lack of early warning systems, and limited exchange of hydro-meteorological data.45,46,55,56 Emphasis is also given to global environmental changes and the weak preparedness of both regions to address climate-induced hazards. In Central Asia, heatwaves and droughts in 2000–2001 and 2021 have caused severe losses in agricultural production and livestock.45,46,57 Also, the consecutive droughts have further reduced the water discharges of the main tributaries in the Aral Sea. To mitigate the environmental impact of the Aral Sea crisis, the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea was established by five Central Asian republics in collaboration with international development partners.58 In the Mira-Mataje basin, the environmental challenges were mainly related to soil erosion from palm oil monoculture, pollution from intensive shrimp farming, and health impacts from the overuse of pesticides in farming.38,40,59,60 Also, the increased frequency and severity of ENSO events in the region have led to heightened occurrences of intense rainfall and drought.41,42,55,56 These phenomena have further compounded environmental challenges and significantly impacted the livelihoods of communities reliant on agricultural activities.50,60,61 There has been limited capacity from state agencies to support environmental conservation in vulnerable areas, and to fill this gap, various associations and funds, such as FONAG in Ecuador, have taken action to protect water sources and prevent land degradation through land acquisition and conservation efforts, particularly in the paramos regions.62,63,64,65

Water governance has received the least attention in the academic literature in both cases, mentioning limited stakeholder consultation, weak implementation of river basin management approaches, challenging transboundary cooperation, and poor planning capacity for long-term water strategy, monitoring, and enforcement.37,61,66,67 In both cases, centralized water management persists despite the efforts of state and non-state actors to empower local and regional institutions and engage stakeholders in water management initiatives. Indicatively, in Central Asia, there have been continuous efforts to enhance the roles of river basin organizations by also encouraging the stakeholders’ engagement through river basin councils, albeit with little success so far.35,37 In the case of Latin America and, particularly, Ecuador, decentralization has encountered challenges in the form of centralized reforms and the restriction of polycentric governance.61,63,68 Also, in the Latin America case study, a culturally diverse population comprising indigenous and Afro-descendant communities exists. Indigenous water rights in the Andean region have been historically marginalized, leading to legal pluralism.40,59 Since 2008, the Ecuadorian Constitution has recognized access to water as a human right,68 but this is at early stages of implementation.

Translational research stage 2: Translation to practice

The second phase of the TR aimed to bridge academic knowledge with policy realities from practitioners’ perspectives through the two-round Delphi survey. We distributed 417 survey invitations for the Aral basin case and 117 email invitations for the Mira-Mataje basin case, of which 115 and 27, respectively, completed the surveys. Most respondents had undergraduate and postgraduate degrees in both cases, while two-thirds had more than six years of experience in relevant case studies. More than half of the respondents were employed in universities and research institutes, and there were also representatives from government agencies, NGOs, international organizations, and the private sector. Both surveys presented an almost equal distribution in age structure (18–65+) and a slightly skewed distribution on the gender aspect in favor of male participants. In the Mira-Mataje basin, most participants were from Ecuador and Colombia, while in the Aral basin, more than half of the respondents were from Central Asia. Detailed socio-demographic profiles of the experts for the Aral Sea basin case are described in Assubayeva et al.36 and attached to Table S2 for the Mira-Mataje case.

Table 3 summarizes the results of the Delphi survey on water security priorities in the case studies. As shown in the first Delphi round for the Aral basin, the water experts prioritize economic activities over household and urban facilities, while the water-related hazards are ranked further later in discussion, with the last position taken by environmental aspects. The prioritization differs in the case of Mira-Mataje. Water governance is regarded as the top priority by water professionals, followed by environmental considerations and household and urban infrastructure. Economic activities are deemed less important, with water-related hazards being the least prioritized. In addition, we inquired about the key challenges associated with each aspect of water security as previously outlined in the scholarly literature. At the policy level, deteriorating irrigation systems and water supply facilities, limited drought forecasting and preparedness, and water allocation disputes on transboundary rivers were deemed the key water security challenges in the Aral basin. Limited consultation and engagement with stakeholders (including communities), deforestation and biodiversity loss, inadequate wastewater treatment, inappropriate agricultural practices, and limited early warning systems were identified as key concerns in the Mira-Mataje basin. The consensus among professionals in the water sector remained robust at 82% and 87% for the Aral and Mira-Mataje basins, respectively.

Additionally, Table 3 outlines the recommendations proposed by experts in the field of water management aimed at enhancing water security. Most policy recommendations for the Aral basin refer to “hard solutions,” such as building and managing irrigation systems, constructing and rehabilitating water supply facilities, technical measures for drought mitigation, and engineering solutions for river basin management. There were a few recommendations on the institutional and capacity-building aspects, mostly targeting the need to improve the performance of regional organizations focused on transboundary cooperation. In the case of the Mira-Mataje basin, the “soft” solutions appear to predominate, such as involving local communities in decision-making, promoting integrated water resources management, and investing in human resource capacity and training. However, suggestions for more technical recommendations about investing in water infrastructure (drinking water supply, sanitation, wastewater, and irrigation systems) while improving water resources monitoring, measurement, and data availability are also listed. In both cases, there is a strong consensus on the recommendations among water professionals, standing at 83% and 87%, respectively.

Translational research stage 3: Translation to communities

Following the previous two stages of TR, we conducted field trips to interview water users and river basin council members in Kazakhstan and community leaders in Ecuador. Different viewpoints have also been outlined concerning the two regions’ water security needs and priorities. Specifically, the concern in Kazakhstan predominantly revolves around water availability and quantity, whereas in Ecuador, the primary focus is on water quality and the role of water in community life. Nevertheless, in both regions, the interviewees stated a rise in tensions over water allocation, driven by competing and conflicting water demands, a lack of meaningful stakeholder involvement, and insufficient water governance capacity to address growing water security challenges. Later in discussion, we capture some of the interviewees’ quotes from both cases, denoting the varied perceptions of water security and the need for a broader understanding of communities’ perspectives on water management and planning processes.

Defining water security

In the Kazakhstani context, most of the interview participants highlighted water quantity and water allocation concerns due to dependence on transboundary rivers, rising water demands, and the impact of climate change on surface water runoff, as selected interview participants mentioned:“Water security is about the availability of water and meeting the needs of industry, agriculture, and population at a sufficient level for all these systems to function.” (INT09)“Due to growing water deficit, efficient allocation and management of water resources are critically important” (INT17).“Water resources availability is important not only for water security but also for food security, energy security, ecosystem security, and peace” (INT10).

In the Ecuadorian case, Afro-Ecuadorian and Awa community leaders mentioned cultural aspects and the role of water in their lives. As Inés, who resides in downstream areas, expressed it:“For us, in the afro-descendant communities [ …], water is certainly an important component, but one among several others in a fundamental ecosystem: the river. It is the river to the one we give major importance, for both existence, and survival […] “Our teacher, Juan García, used to say that without a river, there is no water; without a mountain, there is no river, and there can’t be a mountain without the natural resources that sustain the lives of men and women” (Inés).We found a similar way of expressing this in the words of Jairo, the Awa leader also living downstream:“Water for us is sacred. The vitality is there, and harmony is there. There, we heal ourselves. There is mutual respect between us and water. If we do not respect water, they become sick when we take our children close to the river (Jairo).”

Stakeholder engagement practices

Limited stakeholder involvement was mentioned in both cases at the previous stages of TR. Stakeholder engagement practices vary in study areas. In Kazakhstan, water users and stakeholders potentially can be part of river basin councils, which were established to discuss water issues among water users in relevant river basins. As was mentioned by interview participants:“It is a positive sign that the government supports at least two meetings of the river basin councils, which is also written in the Water Code” (INT04, INT08, INT09).“There are visible and actionable results for the river basin council. For example, we discussed Lake Balkhash’s pollution in one of the basin council meetings. We also had representatives of an industrial company, which later developed a plan to reduce its pollution. Another example was when we prevented geological companies from doing copper exploration in the delta of the lake” (INT01).

However, the Balkhas-Alakol river basin council is more active and engaged than others because of its active members (INT02, INT13). The river basin councils have an advisory and consultative role, and their recommendations are not legally binding.69 Initially, the introduction of river basin councils in Kazakhstan and other Central Asian countries was supported by projects and funding from international donors and development partners,37,69 which raises questions about their ownership and sustainability. According to the interviewees, river basin councils in Kazakhstan and other Central Asian countries are still in their infancy and should be further developed as a platform for dialogue among water users, shared responsibility, and actionable results.

In the Ecuadorian context, stakeholder engagement can be considered in examples of community-based water boards (juntas de agua). Despite the importance of these institutions, they often lack legal status, which can only be obtained through an administrative process, a step that can be an obstacle to their proper functioning. As quoted:“Water juntas were established a long time ago. However, they do not always exhibit state recognition. This is a problem because when the maintenance of irrigation systems is required, the first thing that state institutions require is junta legal recognition (Marcelo).”

Not in the community base. This information is often not accessible to people living near the riverside (Jairo).”

Water governance capacity

Improved water governance to address contextual water security issues was emphasized in both cases. Interview participants in Kazakhstan highlighted the need for a substantial shift from administrative management to a hydrographic (river basin) approach to better address regional problems, especially in regions with high reliance on transboundary rivers. As mentioned, human interventions such as canal diversions and new dams have affected water discharges, further aggravated by consecutive droughts in recent years. As was mentioned by interview participants:“ … river system can be compared with the blood vessels where everything is interconnected …” (INT11)

“ … river system can be compared with the blood vessels where everything is interconnected …” (INT11)

The groundwork involvement of local actors and stakeholders was earmarked to support the management on the basin level substantially. As quoted:“ … it is useless to solve water pollution and allocation problems downstream without addressing issues upstream … river basin management is about the consolidated and coordinated response with involvement of stakeholders …” (INT03).

“ … it is useless to solve water pollution and allocation problems downstream without addressing issues upstream … river basin management is about the consolidated and coordinated response with involvement of stakeholders …” (INT03).

In the case of the Ecuadorian interviewees, the weak capacity of the water governing bodies to address water quality problems was emphasized. As noted, the provincial and municipal governments are responsible for water infrastructure operation, maintenance, and construction, but without sufficient resources or expertise to address these critical issues. Indicatively, in many instances, the absence or poor operation of wastewater treatment facilities results in untreated wastewater being discharged into rivers.

The interviewees also underscored that communities in the Mira-Mataje basin historically exhibited diversity and inhabited remote, isolated, and rainforest areas. These communities primarily comprised Afro-Ecuadorian, Afro-Colombian, and indigenous groups that lacked support from local and regional authorities. The water quality and allocation aspects have been recently administered on a regional level by depriving local communities of engagement in relevant initiatives. As quoted:“When water distribution was made, they decided to give 50% to Santa Ana hacienda and 50% to the entire community of Concepción. Therefore, the interest of the hacienda owners was put before that of the medium and small agricultural producers (Marcelo).”

This recognition and respect for the collective heritage of Afro-Ecuadorian and indigenous communities is also seen by Jairo as a key strategy for the conservation of the basin:“We fear authorities will not identify how to administer these territories. This will allow the expansion of palm crop mining. They are coming only with an economic goal [ …]. We say that ancestral territories must be respected. The most important part of the Mira-Mataje basin is within the territories of indigenous and African descendants. These areas should be considered sacred (Jairo).”

Interviews in both cases revealed that water governance could help address water challenges by bringing water users together, raising awareness of water issues in the basin, and discussing potential solutions at the local level. In the cases of Kazakhstan70 and Ecuador,71 river basin/watershed councils have a consultative role. However, the current form of river basin councils has a limited impact, and progress varies depending on the members of these councils. Some projects in the Ecuadorian region are short-term, but these communities need long-term support for capacity building, empowerment, and adaptation to climate change. According to interview participants, improving the participation of underrepresented groups and stakeholders in the discussion, co-designing and co-production of water projects and policies could lead to the development of participatory governance mechanisms and address contextual water issues by incorporating local knowledge and expertise.

Discussion

Water security, such as other wicked problems, requires a shift from multi- and interdisciplinary to transdisciplinary research for better integration of research, policy, and society in addressing pressing challenges.22,30,31,36 Various frameworks attempt to disentangle the multidimensionality and ambiguity of the water security concept, but the complexity becomes more profound in transboundary systems where shared basins may present competing regional, national, and local interests. This study applied transdisciplinary methodology, namely the TR approach, to explore water security challenges and priorities in two transboundary basins in the Global South. The introduction of TR in our study was aimed at translating the broad theoretical framework and research implications of water security into practical considerations, priorities, and actionable measures to better define the research-policy-society interactions that emerged in the Aral basin in Central Asia and the Mira-Mataje basin in Latin America (Table 1). The implementation of TR revealed notable differences in the water security priorities of the two case studies related to hydro-climatic conditions, socio-economic developments, and cultural and historical contexts. However, there are similarities regarding increasingly competing water needs among different users, the urgency of rehabilitating and expanding aging and limited water infrastructure, low preparedness for climate-related hazards, and weak water governance mechanisms to address pressing community priorities and growing water challenges at the local level.

The first stage of TR – synthesis of scholarly literature on water security in both regions revealed that climate change and environmental pressures will further deteriorate water security, albeit for different reasons in each region.22,33,44,45,55,56 In the case of Central Asia, shifts in precipitation and discharge patterns and an increase in median temperatures by the end of the 21st century cause hydro-climate risks in the region.44,45,72 Hydro-meteorological extremes are already evident in the region, such as major incidents of mudflows and avalanches in Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan, with human losses and severe impacts on road networks and infrastructure, and extreme precipitation combined with hydrological factors caused flooding, as occurred in Kazakhstan in 2024. In the Latin America case, more frequent ENSO events and extreme climatic shocks heighten communities’ vulnerability and socio-economic losses due to their heavy dependence on resource-based livelihoods.41,42,43 These communities, especially women, children, and the elderly, are also sensitive and immobile because they are culturally tied to their locations and face socio-economic disadvantages.68,73,74

At the second TR stage, the Delphi surveys revealed that economic, household, and urban aspects of water security, mainly perceived through technical infrastructure improvements, were prioritized in the Central Asian case. Water governance and environmental aspects in Latin America were prioritized, while technological interventions were considered solutions to overcome the main hindrances. The discrepancy in prioritization may be attributed to the historical path dependency of the two regions. Central Asia had a voluminous expansion of irrigation infrastructure during the Soviet era, particularly after the 1960s.34,35 Since then, irrigation infrastructure and water supply facilities, wastewater treatment plants, dams, and reservoirs have required infrastructure rehabilitation expenses. Despite the irreversible socio-ecological impacts of the Aral Sea desiccation, water-engineering solutions and technocentric mindsets prevail to increase water supply rather than managing water demands and improving water governance.75,76 In Latin America, socio-political reforms and environmental challenges shape water security priorities, where marginalized communities face consequences.40,61,77 The study basin hosts culturally diverse Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations, where poverty rates remain high.77,78 Colombia mandates prior consultation with indigenous communities, contrasting with Ecuador’s regulations. In the case of Central Asia, water infrastructure rehabilitation is required, and in the case of Latin America, the construction of water infrastructure is needed to prevent growing water insecurities.

Our study’s final stage of TR revealed local perceptions of water security definitions, local stakeholder engagement practices, and local water governance realities. If water availability was a main concern in the Kazakhstani case, in the Ecuadorian case, the concerns and anxieties of the local community leaders revolve around the water pollution challenges and the indispensable role of water in their lives. Integrated Water Resources Management elements, such as river basin councils, river basin organizations, and river basin planning, exist in both case studies,70,71 mainly promoted and funded by international organizations, donors, and development partners.37,39,61,65 Interviews in the Kazakhstani case were conducted on one of the proactive river basin councils mainly due to engaged council members; however, river basin councils in Kazakhstan and Central Asia need further development to be a platform for bringing water users, preventing water disputes at local levels, solving water challenges, and building resilience at the basin level. Due to the limited capacity of national governments to manage and conserve water resources and protect water sources, Ecuador has several non-governmental organizations and water funds, mainly funded by international organizations.61,62,64,68 For example, FONAG is a well-known water fund that aims to conserve water sources and important paramo ecosystems, buys land to protect upland areas, and carries out conservation and restoration work,64,65 although protecting water sources and providing public goods are considered to be the role of the government. As also noticed in the previous two TR stages, the different viewpoints on quantity and technocentric solutions versus quality and governance priorities in Central Asia and Latin America could be attributed to cultural path dependencies, aggravated climate-induced hazards, and loosely regulated human interventions on freshwater systems.

A plethora of technical interventions, such as desalinization, water-transfer projects, dams, and reservoirs, have been applied predominantly to accommodate water quantity and quality challenges (e.g., scarcity, overflow, pollution), albeit with limited success in comprehending the root causes of the problems.79,80,81,82 The transboundary system adds another layer of complexity.19,21,38,66,79 Since there are more than 300 internationally shared river basins and more than 300 transboundary aquifers that span political boundaries, different interests among riparian countries, different water security perceptions, and different water problem realities on regional, national, and local scales complicate achieving a common mission and vision on water security and sustainable water management at different scales. Hence, breaking the isolation of science, policy, and practice would require bridging science-policy-community gaps and strengthening these interactions by translating research findings into policy strategies, bringing local realities and targeted interventions, and considering cultural path dependencies. These can be implemented through social learning,83 particularly improved communication and data sharing, better interbasin cooperation, and better intersectoral coordination. Better collaboration between water users, stakeholders, policymakers, and researchers might be improved by investing in capacity and the role of knowledge brokers and knowledge synthesizers to achieve a common vision on water security and water resilience at the basin, country, and local levels and to promote awareness and understanding of intergenerational equity84 in freshwater availability and quality.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that aims to apply TR to interregional water security, focusing on socio-environmental challenges in transboundary river basins. The findings of this study are based on empirical and applied research, which systematically integrates academic synthesis, policy-level consensus, and community engagement. The study provides a context-sensitive understanding of water security that reflects local realities, governance structures, and socio-environmental challenges. This stakeholder-driven framing offers methodological insights for operationalizing water security in complex, multi-scalar settings. However, the TR approach is only one type of transdisciplinary approach – co-producing knowledge, such as citizen science, participatory action research, and others. The application of transdisciplinary research underscores the need for iterative validation across knowledge domains and stakeholder levels and offers a pragmatic pathway for integrating scientific insights with policy priorities and community perspectives. Implementing transdisciplinary research requires multidisciplinary collaboration, access to information and people, new models and modes of communication, and pragmatic research.14,85,86

Limitations of the study and future directions

This study is not without its limitations. Due to resource constraints and security risks, we only interviewed communities in one country in each of these transboundary basins, Kazakhstan and Ecuador, respectively. While the study captured valuable insights from Afro-descendant and Awa indigenous communities, we recognize that water security perceptions and governance realities may differ significantly across different stakeholders. Water security priorities and realities also differ significantly between upstream and downstream riparian countries, which shape national water policies, seasonal water release negotiations, and regional cooperation mechanisms.1 Additionally, the Delphi survey’s identification of water security priorities could be criticized for the potential professional biases of panel members. However, we invited diverse stakeholders to both rounds of surveys, including government agencies, NGOs, academia, and international organizations working in these regions, to ensure a representative sample. Future studies may conduct country-specific Delphi panels to explore how professional identity influences perceptions of water security and conduct community interviews across multiple riparian states to ensure balanced representation and to capture the full spectrum of upstream-downstream dynamics. Future studies may expand the synthesis of literature, including gray literature and country reports, if they aim to compile country-specific information.

It should be noted that the study area of the Latin American case study was also smaller than the Aral basin, and consequently, the data collection (literature, survey participants, interviews) was smaller. However, the study’s intention was not to compare territorially equivalent areas from a hydrological perspective. The focus was on integrating, through the TR approach, the different knowledge bases (academic, practical, local) in the study areas, which are inherently subjective and often contradictory. Indicatively, reaching out to and incorporating the perspectives of water users and the voices of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian communities living in remote areas may be equally important for elaborating hydrological planning and developing more inclusive water policies.

From a methodological perspective, we acknowledge that the TR approach has built on its foundational use in biomedical and public health research, where it is typically structured in stages that move from basic research to clinical application and community-level impact. Reversing the TR stages by starting with community engagement could foreground local priorities and cultural values earlier in the process, potentially reshaping the synthesis and policy translation phases, so future studies may explore alternative sequencing to enhance co-design and inclusivity. We acknowledge that water security remains a wicked concept30,31 that is described differently in different disciplines and contextual settings, especially when applied to different regions and scales.19,29,87 The innovation of this study does not lie in redefining the concept of water security, which has already evolved in the literature to encompass dimensions beyond quantity and availability. Rather, the contribution of this research is in the application of the TR approach to explore how water security is perceived, framed, and prioritized by diverse stakeholders—including professionals, community leaders, and water users—in two transboundary basins of the Global South.

Method details

The TR approach aims to accelerate the application of scientific advances,16,88 to target policy interventions,13,89 and to break the isolation of science, policy/practice, and community.3,17 TR has its roots in medicine and biomedicine to bridge basic and clinical research via translating scientific knowledge into practice, also called 'from bench to bedside'.88 There has been criticism regarding the perceived lack of knowledge transfer and innovation, particularly in biomedical research, despite the increase in research funding.17 Similar concerns are also noted in other academic disciplines. Indicatively, in agriculture, there exist unresolved inquiries, such as the impact of specific crop varieties on overall crop yield in authentic field conditions, as opposed to solely within controlled laboratory environments or theoretical simulations85 or the extent to which certain agricultural practices and technologies are climate resilient.86 The TR approach has several stages, consisting of translating and synthesizing basic scientific discoveries into practice via clinical research, testing, disseminating, communicating, and implementing this knowledge into community practices and realities.15,16,89 Translating basic or pure research into practice has always been challenging, but the gap between scientific advances and the reality of practice has been increasing over the years.3,16,17,85,88

We adapted the TR methodology in two transboundary regions in the Global South to explore how water security is interpreted and what water challenges and priorities are in these contexts. Table 1 presents the three TR stages and their application to the two regions and the methodological approaches introduced. In the initial stage of the TR process, we conducted a systematic literature review focusing on the academic literature pertaining to various facets of water security in Central Asian and Latin American cases. We applied the keyword search of water security dimensions and attributes from Xenarios et al.22 Namely, the first layer of the search was at the regional level (water security in Central Asia, water security in Latin America), the second layer - was at the country level (water security in Uzbekistan, water security in Colombia, and for other riparian countries), and the third layer - attributes of water security (household and urban water security dimension - drinking water, sanitation, wastewater, SDG 6; economic water security - irrigation, hydropower, industry, WEF; environmental water security - rivers, lakes, mountains, ecosystems; water-related hazards - droughts, floods, landslides, avalanches; and water governance - administration, legislation, water policy, transboundary cooperation). We conducted a content analysis of key water security issues and challenges, including peer-reviewed publications from the ScienceDirect, JSTOR, and Web of Science databases. Water challenges were classified into three categories based on their coverage: low, medium, and high. Specifically, ‘low coverage’ indicates limited or insufficient data and discussion; ‘medium coverage’ reflects moderate attention; and ‘high coverage’ denotes comprehensive treatment of the topic. Due to the different sample sizes of the collected literature across our cases, we classified water challenge as having a high coverage if it was discussed in at least one third of the collected studies, medium coverage if it was discussed between 10% and 32% of the literature and low coverage if it was mentioned in fewer than 10% of the studies.

In the second stage, we applied the Delphi technique to present key findings from the previous stage on academic literature synthesis and identify agreement and disagreement on water security issues at the policy level in the two regions. The Delphi survey usually consists of several rounds and aims to reach consensus among panel members on critical security challenges with limited information or high uncertainties . In our study, the Delphi survey was conducted in two rounds, separately for each case, initially identifying experts' opinions on water challenges and potential solutions (first round) and then assessing levels of agreement on water security priorities (second round). The surveys had a similar structure but also some variations when applied to the two case studies. Initially, in the Delphi survey for the Aral basin, we did not consider water governance as a separate dimension of water security.22,36 Since the Delphi survey on the Mira-Mataje basin was conducted later, we included water governance aspects, such as the causes of inappropriate water policies and management, as governance has been raised as a profound challenge in the Aral basin case and in the literature. Another difference was that in the case of the Aral basin, we additionally asked about national water security priorities for all Central Asian countries due to the emerging competing interests and water scarcity concerns in the region (read more in Assubayeva et al.36). In both cases, we used a purposive and stratified sampling to select survey participants based on their education, experience, and expertise from various professional backgrounds and included different stakeholders, such as policymakers, NGOs, practitioners, and academics from all riparian countries. The Delphi study in Central Asia was conducted in Russian and English using Qualtrics software from June to October 2020. The Latin American case study was conducted in Spanish and English using LimeSurvey software from September to November 2023. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (IREC) to conduct surveys and interviews. All Delphi participants confirmed their voluntary participation before starting the survey, and written and/or oral consent was obtained from all stakeholders interviewed. In both cases, we analyzed the surveys using descriptive statistics for agreement rates to identify trends in water security prioritizations.

The final stage focused on the local perspective, building on the findings of the previous rounds by conducting semi-structured interviews with water users, stakeholders, and communities in both regions. This TR stage aimed to reveal local perspectives of water security, stakeholder engagement practices, and water governance realities in the relevant basins. We used purposive sampling to identify interviewees using criteria of knowledge, experience, physical and social accessibility, willingness to participate, and communication/language capabilities. For the Central Asian case, we focused on the Balkash-Alakol river basin council in Kazakhstan since this basin comprises one of the most advanced applications of the river basin management approach in Central Asia.37,69 From August to October 2021, seventeen face-to-face interviews were carried out with various water user representatives, including those from industrial, municipal, agricultural, river basin organizations, local communities, and non-governmental organizations in both Almaty city and Almaty region (described in Table S1). The data was analyzed using thematic analysis, and interview transcription and coding were conducted using the NVivo qualitative software. The interviewees preferred anonymous quotations.

In the Mira-Mataje basin, indigenous and Afro-descendant territories constitute half of the basin. Specifically, Awá and Afro-descendant territories cover most of the basin—22% and 25%, respectively.38 Although it would have been valuable to gather information from other indigenous and mestizo communities, we had to limit the number of interviews due to time constraints and security concerns. Therefore, in Ecuador, we interviewed leaders from the Afro-descendant and Awa indigenous communities, with whom we have previously engaged and built trust and who have extensive knowledge of water management challenges in the region. It is worth mentioning that Afro-descendant communities from the upper and lower basin exhibit significant cultural differences, shaped by both historical and ecological factors. For this reason, we decided to interview leaders of each community separately. In November 2023, interviews were conducted with one Awa and two Afro-descendant local community leaders residing in the remote areas of San Lorenzo and La Conception/Ibarra. The following individuals have been granted permission to be directly quoted. Specifically, downstream, we interviewed Ines Morales Lastra, a prominent female figure from the Afro-Ecuadorian community in the northern region of Esmeraldas. In the midstream, Jairo Tankinkú, an influential leader of the Awá indigenous community and president of the Confederation of Indigenous Nationalities of the Coast of Ecuador, was interviewed. Finally, in the upper basin, we spoke with José Marcelo Acosta, a community leader from Concepción, a rural town in the highland area. We employed thematic analysis using MAXQDA software for transcribing interviews and coding.
