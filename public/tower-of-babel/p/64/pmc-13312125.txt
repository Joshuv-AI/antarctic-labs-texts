Considerations in reviewing network meta-analyses of heterogeneous clinical trial designs: a methodological review in Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis
Authors: Christopher Ma, Eric B Collins, Si-Tien Wang, Yixi Teng, Warren Stevens, Remo Panaccione, Dolly Sharma, Namita Joshi, Pierre A Morisset, Raja Atreya, Gil Y Melmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNetwork meta-analysis (NMA) is a common approach to synthesizing efficacy and safety data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Within the last 5 years, there has been an increase in the number of published NMAs in the clinical literature. One potential risk of this trend is the acceptance of NMA findings with translation into therapeutic decisions for daily clinical practice without first critically assessing the validity of the method’s core assumptions, including trial exchangeability and homogeneity.MethodsWe use the recent surge in NMAs of advanced therapies for Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) as a case study to illustrate the risks of overly permissive interpretation practices toward NMA methods and findings. We first highlight the potential violations of core NMA assumptions posed by known sources of heterogeneity across trials in CD and UC, and then outline several methodological strategies, ranging from data handling techniques to meta-regression, that can help to address or mitigate these violations.ResultsWe put forward a checklist for users of NMAs to aid the interpretation of their results in the context of both methods and study inclusion/exclusion.ConclusionsWhile NMAs offer important insights into the relative treatment effects of different therapies and useful guidance to healthcare decision-makers, it is important that readers and users of NMA publications carefully review and critically evaluate the methods and results to ensure potential biases introduced by cross-trial heterogeneity are sufficiently mitigated and transparently documented, and that the results of any given NMA are interpreted within the context of the wider literature.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, network meta-analysis, clinical trial design, heterogeneity

Introduction

Network meta-analysis (NMA) can be a potentially powerful analytic approach for synthesizing data across randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Serving as a step beyond traditional meta-analysis, which historically has been used to pool the proportion of participants in RCTs achieving selected outcomes, NMA is useful in producing estimates of comparative efficacy and safety between interventions for which there are otherwise no direct head-to-head RCTs.1 Given its ability to simultaneously integrate results from multiple trials and produce both direct and indirect treatment comparisons, NMA can provide valuable information for healthcare decision-making. This has become increasingly important in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), where the therapeutic armamentarium has expanded significantly over the past decade, although there remain limited head-to-head trials.2 Critically appraising the relative efficacy and safety of available therapies helps to inform patients and providers in selecting the most appropriate treatment option(s) in different clinical scenarios and plays a growing role in drug approval and reimbursement. For example, NMA serves as a key component of health technology assessment submissions to bodies such as the United Kingdom’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence and Canada’s Drug Agency.

Multiple methods for deriving both direct and indirect estimates in NMA have been developed, using either Bayesian or frequentist statistical frameworks.3 Additionally, well-documented and open-source statistical software packages for conducting traditional pairwise meta-analysis and NMA are widely accessible. Under these conditions, NMA publications have proliferated in the clinical literature. A search of PubMed from January 1, 2010 to January 1, 2025 for “NMA” in titles and abstracts produced over 11 000 results, 70% of which were published in the last 5 years. Limiting the search to Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC) reduced the results to 137, nearly 65% of which were published in the last 5 years (Table S1). Often, these NMAs vary in search strategy, study selection, data inclusion, and analysis plan, leading to differences in their conclusions and interpretability.

The increased dependence of healthcare decision-making on NMA-generated relative treatment effects highlights the importance of clarifying the statistical assumptions made (or violated) by NMA as well as how the choice of RCTs synthesized in NMA may impact findings. This is of particular concern in disease areas such as IBD, where interventions are often studied using heterogeneous trial designs.4,5 Here, we review the tenets of conducting valid direct and indirect comparisons in NMA, the statistical assumptions which may be impacted by RCT designs, and utilize RCTs of biologics and small molecules, collectively referred to as advanced therapies (ATs), in moderate-to-severe CD and UC as a case study to critically appraise the validity of NMA conclusions.

A brief overview of NMA and its core assumptions

A summary of the key steps and core assumptions of a robust NMA is presented in Table 1. Extending the traditional meta-analysis to include both direct and indirect7 evidence, individual NMAs remain limited by the quality of the included RCTs and are subject to the assumption of RCT similarity (and related methodological and statistical assumptions of exchangeability and homogeneity, respectively).8 Therefore, a critical assessment of the risk-of-bias of synthesized trial results using a standardized assessment tool is an important first step in evaluating the robustness of NMA findings. One such tool is version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2), which is structured into a fixed set of domains of bias, focusing on those that are currently understood to affect the results of randomized trials, that is, the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcomes data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.9 The overall risk-of-bias judgment is either “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.”10 A summary of the overall judgements of all RCTs included in a given NMA should be reported.

Key steps and core assumptions of a robust NMA.3,6

Another key step for a robust NMA is evaluating how clinically and methodologically similar the included trials are, as a proxy to assessing the implicit NMA assumption of exchangeability. Exchangeability has been previously described as “[.] no important differences between the trials making different comparisons, other than the treatments being compared.”11 In other words, the trials ultimately included in an NMA must be comparable with each other in order for indirect comparisons to be considered valid. These indirect estimates of efficacy or safety have not been derived from randomization, but rather from the mathematical relationship between interventions and a common comparison node (or nodes) within a network. Therefore, all trials included within a network must be, on average, sufficiently similar such that all important factors that may influence the outcomes of interest are comparable across included studies.

What constitutes “important differences” can seem ambiguous. This core assumption may be better understood as a combination of three overlapping constituent assumptions:12 (1) an assumption of homogeneity, such that populations and trial designs are sufficiently similar across synthesized RCTs; (2) an assumption of network connectivity, such that true common comparators, such as placebo, exist across synthesized RCTs; and (3) an assumption of consistency (also referred to in the literature as transitivity), such that indirect estimates produced by the NMA align with direct estimates observed across synthesized RCTs (generally, in head-to-head trials) in the NMA. If an NMA violates these assumptions, the relative effects of comparators as estimated in the NMA may be obfuscated and/or biased and thus should be interpreted with caution.13 A more practical approach to considering whether these assumptions are valid in NMA is to conduct a simple thought experiment, considering: (1) would all competing interventions included in the NMA be jointly randomizable if you were to do a single trial with each intervention as a study arm?; and (2) would any patient from an included RCT have also been eligible to be randomized into any other trial included in the NMA?

RCTs of Advanced Therapies in CD and UC: complicating factors for NMA

Advanced therapies (ATs) have become an important component in the treatment of moderate-to-severe CD and UC. While increasing number of ATs are gaining approval for the treatment of CD and UC, there remains a paucity of comprehensive head-to-head trials from which a complete picture of comparative efficacy can be directly observed.14 While the NMA methodology can help fill this data gap, it also has important limitations. Indeed, the RCT designs utilized for the study of ATs in CD and UC are in and of themselves complicated, have evolved over time, and may not always be directly suitable for inclusion in any given NMA.4, 14,15 A comprehensive review of how trial designs in IBD has evolved has been performed elsewhere,14 but here, we highlight differences in RCTs that are specifically relevant for their use in NMA.

Induction to maintenance: re-randomized or treat-through

Historically, regulatory requirements have dictated that RCTs of ATs in moderate-to-severe CD and UC include both an initial induction phase—typically 4-12 weeks in length and during which a patient’s short-term response to treatment is assessed—and a follow-up maintenance phase—typically a year in length and during which a patient’s longer-term response to treatment is evaluated.14,15

Induction phase designs are generally more similar across RCTs. Patients are randomized to receive either the AT under investigation or the comparator (primarily placebo, but rarely, another conventional treatment or AT) for the entire induction phase. However, induction phase trials have varied in length, ranging from 4 to 12 weeks. Increasingly, induction trials have become longer in duration and many trials now include extended induction periods for initial partial or non–responders, recognizing that the full therapeutic benefit of an intervention may not be realized in the first three months of treatment. Therefore, comparisons of outcomes from ultra-short duration studies may be underestimated.

How patients progress from the induction to maintenance phase of an RCT is notably different across IBD studies. The two primary designs utilized are typically referred to as “responder re-randomization” or “treat-through.” A visual illustration of these designs is presented in Figure 1, and the design employed by the phase 3 programs of approved ATs in UC and CD are summarized in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

A responder re-randomization maintenance phase RCT design is one in which only patients who responded to active treatment during the induction phase move on to receive maintenance treatment. These active treatment induction responders are then re-randomized to either continue receiving the maintenance dose(s) of the intervention to which they responded or be withdrawn from active treatment to receive placebo during the maintenance phase. The criteria by which induction response is assessed may differ across RCTs, although generally, clinical responders are permitted to continue into maintenance. This results in heterogeneity in the maintenance trial population, whereby clinical responders who have variable endoscopic or biomarker responses may be included. Furthermore, the proportion and characteristics of these induction responders may influence their response rates in maintenance and absolute rates of efficacy at the end of maintenance are typically enhanced amongst the initial induction responder population compared to the entire study cohort exposed to treatment.

A treat-through maintenance phase RCT design is one in which all patients, regardless of response status during the induction phase, continue their randomized treatment allocation through to the end of maintenance or until study participation ends (ie, patients who received an active treatment in induction receive the maintenance phase dosing of the same active treatment; patients who received placebo in induction continue to receive placebo in maintenance). However, recognizing that patients may be reluctant to continue long-term on an ineffective treatment or placebo, non–responders are generally allowed to withdraw to receive rescue therapy open-label and are thus, not considered to have achieved maintenance outcomes.

Implications of re-randomization to NMA: is placebo, placebo?

In a network of re-randomized maintenance trials, the core assumptions of a robust NMA described in Table 1 are likely violated, including network connectivity via a true common comparator across the included RCTs. Placebo is often utilized as the common comparator to construct a connected network in CD16,17 and UC18,19 NMAs. However, the placebo arms of re-randomized maintenance trials are inherently heterogeneous. Rather than “true” placebo, as in a treat-through design, re-randomized maintenance placebo arms consist of patients who were successfully induced and then withdrawn from differing ATs with differing pharmacodynamics (PD), pharmacokinetics, and mechanisms of action (MoA). Possible differences in induction phase lengths and response criteria also contribute to heterogeneous treatment withdrawal (ie, placebo) efficacy in the re-randomized maintenance phase.

Such heterogeneity in treatment withdrawal efficacy were observed across re-randomized maintenance trials in both CD20 and UC.21 Similarly, some MoAs were observed in re-randomized trials to be associated with lower22–24 or higher25–28 than the observed average treatment withdrawal efficacy. For example, monoclonal antibodies with long half-lives or very durable PD effects may result in higher rates of randomized withdrawal to placebo response, even at the end of maintenance period. Naessens et al. referred to this as “differential carryover effects from induction [that] can bias the [NMA] results”.29 In short, the placebo arm of one re-randomized study is not necessarily equivalent to that of another and such violations may particularly bias NMA against the relative efficacy of those ATs associated with high efficacy treatment withdrawal arms.

Implications of combining differing trial designs in NMA: furthering heterogeneity

Although treat-through maintenance RCTs have fewer challenges with heterogeneity of placebo response rates based on induction treatment exposure, treat-through designs are historically less commonly utilized in RCTs of ATs in moderate-to-severe CD and UC. Furthermore, most ATs have only been studied in 1 design or the other. Thus, NMAs seeking to provide comprehensive estimates of comparative efficacy often include pooled data from both treat-through and re-randomized trial designs.30,31

An unadjusted mixing of RCT designs likely violates the core NMA assumption of homogeneity. Historically, it has been observed that there are systematic differences between placebo groups in treat-through and re-randomized maintenance RCTs. Placebo efficacy is generally observed to be lower in treat-through trials than in re-randomized trials,27, 32 even in trials of ATs with similar MoA. For example, Barberio et al. found through a meta-analysis of remission maintenance with biologics and small molecules in IBD that pooled remission maintenance rates in IBD were 20.3% and 14.6% for participants receiving placebo in re-randomized and treat-through trials, respectively.33 Lower rates are observed in treat-through trials because their intent-to-treat (ITT) populations include both observed induction non–responders, as well as participants who dropped out of the trial and were imputed as non–responders. This impacts both efficacy and safety assessments, recognizing that pooling proportions of adverse events may be invalid when there are differences in exposure duration between AT and comparator groups in treat-through studies.

For the active treatments, pooling both treat-through and responder re-randomization trials may result in biases in both directions. For example, if placebo is used as the common comparator in the NMA, relative efficacy of ATs studied in treat-through trials with lower placebo efficacy may be overestimated. Participants in treat-through trials may also have higher efficacy rates if allowed greater time to respond to treatment. In contrast, re-randomized patients may inherently be more likely to achieve maintenance efficacy outcomes as they are known to have successfully responded to the AT under evaluation during induction. While the overall direction of bias depends on the trial context, in the meta-analysis conducted by Barberio et al., the corresponding remission maintenance rates for active treatment (ie, biologics and small molecules in IBD) were 41.9% and 30.9% in re-randomized and treat-through trials, respectively.33 Although the study did not observe a significant impact of trial design on the therapeutic gain of active treatment over placebo (risk ratio of maintaining remission ∼2.0 for both designs), the differences in absolute rates will likely influence the findings of a NMA that pools maintenance trials together, irrespective of design, to generate treatment effects relative to an “average” placebo arm.

Implications of patient population heterogeneity to NMA: differing populations, differing impacts

A final aspect of AT RCTs in moderate-to-severe CD and UC to consider in the context of NMA is potential heterogeneities in patient populations.1 Patient populations which differ in treatment effect modifiers (TEMs), such as disease duration, disease activity, disease extent, prior treatment exposure (both type and count) and/or response (primary vs. secondary non–response), and concomitant therapies (particularly corticosteroids), are inherently anticipated to respond differently to treatment. The impact and magnitude of treatment effects based on differences in these effect modifiers varies in the literature. Prior treatment exposure/response is a particularly important TEM in IBD; a common population stratification across CD and UC AT trials is by whether patients are prior AT-naïve, -exposed, or -failed. Separate NMAs have been conducted by these subgroups,17, 19 but even so, heterogeneity exists in their definitions.

In older trials, when the only ATs approved for IBD were biologics that inhibit tumor necrosis factor (TNF) alpha, patients were generally stratified into TNFi-naïve and -exposed/failed groups. Prior treatment exposure to other MoAs/more than one AT was either not permitted, possible, or expected. Over time, as the drugs studied in these older RCTs got approved and become part of the IBD treatment landscape, ATs for IBD expanded to include biologics and other ATs with other MoAs, such as anti–interleukin therapies (ustekinumab, risankizumab, mirikizumab, and guselkumab), anti–integrin therapy (vedolizumab), and small molecules targeting Janus kinase (JAK inhibitors; upadacitinib, tofacitinib, and filgotinib) and sphingosine 1 phosphate receptor (S1P modulators; ozanimod, etrasimod).34,35 As clinical access to other ATs has expanded, stratification by prior treatment exposure/response is becoming increasingly complex and heterogenous across trials.36 For example, in the recent registrational QUASAR trial for guselkumab in UC, efficacy outcomes were reported in multiple subsets of AT-failed patients including 1 biologic/JAKi; ≥2 biologic/JAKi; any TNFi; 1 TNFi; ≥2 TNFi; TNFi only; 1 TNFi only; any vedolizumab, vedolizumab only, and tofacitinib.37 A summary of the baseline prior treatment characteristics of the phase 3 study populations of approved ATs in UC and CD are presented in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

The degree of prior treatment failure is a general proxy for disease severity; patients who failed more prior therapies, especially those with differing MoAs, are generally anticipated to have more refractory disease and to be less likely to respond to the next line of AT. In the QUASAR study, clinical response rates to guselkumab were lowest for patients who had failed other MoAs (anti–integrin, JAKi), as well as for those who had failed ≥2 prior therapies.37 Imbalances in other TEMs, such as disease duration in CD, should be considered as potential sources of heterogeneity in population disease severity as well.38,39 As such, imbalances in prior treatment and other TEMs across RCTs may introduce bias for ATs studied in less severe patient populations and against ATs studied in more severe patient populations.

Conducting and interpreting NMA in the context of known heterogeneity in CD and UC RCTs

While the heterogeneous nature of CD and UC AT RCTs presents a challenge to the core assumptions of NMA, several NMA methodological approaches are available to potentially address or mitigate this challenge. We outline these methods in the sections below, then conclude the review with recommendations on how best to read and thoughtfully evaluate IBD NMA publications and results in the context of heterogeneity.

Methods to address heterogeneity: common NMA techniques

A key step prior to undertaking NMA is the conducting of a feasibility assessment. Such an assessment aims to qualitatively identify potential clinical heterogeneity between RCTs to be synthesized in the NMA, posit if identified heterogeneity presents risks to NMA validity, and inform techniques which may be employed to mitigate these risks.40 A feasibility assessment generally includes, review of study designs, study risk of bias, outcome definitions, population inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the presence of known TEMs.

Several NMA techniques can be leveraged to address heterogeneity in pooled analyses. A standard technique is to perform subgroup analysis by key TEMs.13 In CD and UC NMAs, this is most commonly done by separately analyzing participants by prior treatment status.17, 19 Other NMAs have conducted subgroup analyses by maintenance re-randomized or treat-through trial design.41,42 While subgroup analysis generates estimates for a specific population, it is limited in its ability to address heterogeneity if outcomes of interest are not reported stratified by the specific subgroup across relevant RCTs, and over-stratification reduces sample size and statistical power in any given analysis.

Another commonly utilized NMA technique in CD and UC to explore heterogeneity is meta-regression.13 Meta-regression introduces a covariate into the NMA in order to adjust the NMA’s calculated relative effects for different levels of the considered covariate. Meta-regression is frequently utilized as a means of baseline risk adjustment. Under the assumption that the difference in baseline risk (ie, placebo efficacy across trials is a proxy for differences in patient populations across trials, utilizing trial-specific placebo efficacy as a covariate can potentially adjust for both known and unknown differences across RCTs included in NMA).13 It is important to note that meta-regression is subject to “ecological fallacy”—meaning that the adjustment is based on average values from entire study population, rather than data from individual patients. As a result, the findings may not always reflect what is happening at the individual patient level.

Methods to address heterogeneity: data handling approaches

Other approaches to mitigate heterogeneity driven by RCT design in CD and UC NMAs have focused on direct adjustments to NMA data inputs through data handling and imputations. One data handling approach to addressing differences in re-randomized and treat-through study designs is to utilize the treat-through maintenance outcome subset amongst induction responders, essentially using induction response in treat-through trials as a proxy for re-randomization into the maintenance phase.19, 43,44 Generally, this results in higher estimates of treatment efficacy than were reported in the treat-through approach and approximates the response rate in patients receiving active treatment in NMA with induction responders in maintenance phase analysis. However, this approach also has limitations. Heterogeneity remains in placebo populations due to potential treatment carryover effects in re-randomized trials. Combining this imputation with a meta-regression controlling for baseline risks (ie, placebo outcomes) across the included trials may help to further mitigate this heterogeneity beyond using either technique in isolation. This approach is also inherently limited by the reporting of relevant maintenance outcomes subset by induction responders for treat-through trials.

Approaches to converting re-randomized to treat-through trials have also been proposed, such as approximating treat-through outcomes in active treatment arms by combining the observed number of patients achieving a re-randomized maintenance outcome and the observed number of non–responders patients achieving a maintenance outcome, adjusted with a dilution factor to account for re-randomization.45–48 Such an approach is dependent on applicable non–responder efficacy reporting from re-randomized RCTs. This data may be difficult to source from publicly available manuscripts as treatment non–responder populations are generally outside the primary study population. Furthermore, placebo populations remain inherently heterogeneous, owing not only to differences in treatment carryover effects but also because placebo induction non–responders are generally moved to active treatment in re-randomized trial designs. Again, combining this imputation with a meta-regression controlling for baseline risks (ie, placebo outcomes) across the included trials may help to mitigate this heterogeneity more than if these approaches were used in isolation. Biases stemming from the breaking of randomization may be a concern as well.49 For these reasons, among others, review groups in Health Technology Assessment (HTA) submissions have generally preferred the assumptions of a treat-through to re-randomized data handling approach over a re-randomized to treat-through approach.43, 50

To specifically address the inherent heterogeneities in placebo arms of re-randomized trials, a “treatment sequence” NMA methodology has also been proposed in which all placebo arm efficacy values are imputed based on the maintenance placebo efficacy of an anchor trial and adjusted for the induction response rate of any given trial undergoing imputation.51 Such an approach, however, appears to arbitrarily relate maintenance placebo efficacy across re-randomized trials. As there are known intractable differences between placebo arms of re-randomization trials, creation of such a relation seems counterproductive to the aim of improving trial comparability. Review groups in HTA submissions have been skeptical of this approach.52

Reviewing NMA publications: interpreting results through a critical lens

Given potential threats to the validity of NMA in IBD and the variety of methods that have been employed to mitigate these threats, this review posits that NMAs in the IBD disease space should be evaluated with a discerning eye and the veracity of any conclusions should be critically reviewed by readers. Inspired by similar “users’ guides” in other disease spaces, we propose the following questions for readers to ask themselves when interpreting NMA results in IBD and evaluating the validity of said results.12, 53,54

Do the results presented for a given AT in NMA reflect the results observed in RCTs of the same AT? Such a check can serve as a qualitative assessment of the consistency/transitivity assumption as well as a general assessment of the NMA’s validity. If, for example, an AT is consistently found in RCTs to be superior to placebo, but this is not found in the NMA of interest, this may suggest further investigation into potential differences between the placebo arms incorporated into the NMA is warranted. As the knowledge base of real-world evidence for the effectiveness of ATs in CD and UC grows, such evidence can also serve as points of comparison to published NMA results.55–59

Do the rankings of assessed ATs align or differ from rankings derived using the same metric as presented in other NMAs in the disease space? Differences in treatment ranking based on the same metric for the same AT in the same treatment phase and population across NMAs are suggestive of differences in RCT inclusion and/or NMA methodology. Special attention should be given to potential differences in the methodological approach used to generate the rankings, as to ensure that differences in rank order are not due to differences in the ranking metric used in any given NMA. For example, a common metric used in NMA is the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).60,61 SUCRA is derived from effect estimates in NMA, with each treatment ranked based on probability of being first, second, third, and so on, for achieving the specified outcome. The cumulative probability for each treatment of ranking at or above each rank is then calculated; the SUCRA represents the area under the cumulative ranking curve and scaled from 0% to 100%, where 100% represents a treatment that is always ranked “first” and 0% represents a treatment that is always ranked “last.” While a common interpretation is that the treatment with the highest SUCRA is the most effective, this is an oversimplification. SUCRA has important limitations as it does not capture certainty of the evidence, does not reflect the magnitudes of differences in treatment effect (including whether treatment effects are clinically meaningful), account for potential tradeoffs for different therapeutic options, nor captures the precision of estimates, which may often overlap in IBD. Therefore, readers should not take SUCRA rankings simply to mean that there is a “single best treatment,” and rankings must be interpreted in the context of the therapeutic choices. Furthermore, readers should ensure that any comparison in rankings between NMAs is based on comparable ranking methods. If the ordering of the same ranking metric differs across NMAs, potential differences in RCT inclusion and methods, and how they are likely to impact results, should be further investigated between NMAs. Detailed discussions on NMA ranking metrics, the methodologies to derive them, and their various interpretations have been robustly discussed elsewhere in the literature.60–65

Does the common comparator used in the NMA have the potential to bias for or against certain ATs? As discussed in the prior sections of this review, heterogeneities in the common comparator (generally placebo in the context of CD and UC) may lead the NMA to inflate or deflate the relative efficacy of treatments. If trials designs were homogenous, differences in placebo efficacy may represent real and relevant differences in baseline risk between populations. However, in the context of re-randomization, differences in placebo efficacy in the maintenance phase may stem from differences in carryover effects across MoAs. These differences may then be further exacerbated by the inclusion of treat-through trials in addition to re-randomized trials. If the NMA does not take steps to address such differences, the results of the NMA should be interpreted with caution.

Is the NMA transparent with specific limitations to the data synthesized and methodology utilized? Finally, readers of CD and UC NMA publications should review the discussion presented for any given NMA in detail to determine if the authors correctly and transparently identified the known limitations and complexities of NMA when synthesizing heterogeneous trial designs. A well-conducted NMA should make explicit potential biases introduced from heterogeneous data, what qualitative and quantitative methods were used to identify heterogeneity and inconsistency/violation of transitivity, what means were taken to address bias driven by heterogeneities, and how results should be interpreted in the context of such potential biases. Applicable qualitative methods generally constitute a review of clinical trials in context of the prior discussed feasibility assessment. Quantitative methods may include a node-splitting approach or an unrelated mean relative effects model to statistically assess potential inconsistency/violation of transitivity between direct and indirect evidence, while a Wald test P-value or an I2 statistic could be reported to quantify potential heterogeneity in baseline characteristics, baseline risk, and/or treatment effect across trials. In addition to discussion of heterogeneous trials and applicable statistical evidence to assess heterogeneity, other limitations in the analysis originating from the synthesized trials should be noted. For example, if early phase and/or small sample size trials are synthesized, there should be clear discussions of impacts on the NMA, such as a potential lack of results generalizability to the wider patient population. This is particularly relevant if the number of small sample size trials is notable and/or large sample size trials are absent, resulting in increased uncertainty of effect estimates (namely in wider confidence or credible intervals) and rankings of treatments studied in such trials. Likewise, inclusion of trials in the NMA with a high risk of bias per standardized assessment, such as the Cochrane Risk of Bias version two (RoB2) tool,9 should be explicitly discussed. If methodological rationales as well as potential biases and limitations are not made forthright, the data handling and methods of the NMA should be scrutinized to ensure appropriate approaches have indeed been utilized and that known biases highlighted by this review are not amplified in any way. Standardized frameworks to assess the quality of NMA evidence, such as GRADE,66 CINeMA,67 and the PRISMA NMA extension6 offer guidelines for applying said scrutiny and determining the certainty of evidence produced by NMA.

Conclusions

NMAs are useful for generating estimates of comparative efficacy when limited direct head-to-head RCTs are available. This makes NMA a powerful tool in disease spaces like CD and UC, where the number of potential AT options has greatly expanded but the number of head-to-head comparisons remains limited. Indeed, NMAs in such disease spaces can serve as important points of reference and guidance for healthcare decision-makers. However, the assumptions of NMA can be complicated by study designs used in CD and UC RCTs. Readers of NMA publications in CD and UC should carefully review and critically evaluate the methods and results presented within these publications to ensure potential biases introduced by heterogeneous trial designs are sufficiently mitigated and transparently documented. Furthermore, results of any given NMA should be holistically interpreted in the context of real-world evidence and wider clinical literature.
