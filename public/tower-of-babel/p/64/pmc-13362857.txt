Association between the triglyceride-glucose-waist-to-height ratio index and mortality in patients with hypothyroidism: A retrospective cohort study based on NHANES
Authors: Xin Pan, Weiyi Xu, Zuyao Lu, Xuchen Deng, Dongdong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) index and mortality risk among individuals with hypothyroidism. Building on prior evidence linking insulin resistance and central obesity to adverse health outcomes, this research specifically sought to evaluate whether the combined metric of TyG-WHtR (integrating triglyceride-glucose index and waist-to-height ratio) holds enhanced predictive utility for mortality in a nationally representative sample of United States adults with hypothyroidism. Using data from the 2007 to 2012 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, we included 8437 adults. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between TyG-WHtR and hypothyroidism prevalence. Multivariable Cox regression models, restricted cubic splines, and subgroup analyses with Bonferroni correction (significance threshold α′ = 0.0071) were employed to evaluate mortality risks. Predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analyses. Higher TyG-WHtR quartiles were independently associated with an increased prevalence of hypothyroidism (Model 3, Q4 vs Q1: odds ratio [OR] = 1.62, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.20–2.18; P trend < .001). Among patients with hypothyroidism, the highest TyG-WHtR tertile (T3) was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.07–5.41) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.02–1.29), whereas the moderate tertile (T2) showed no significant association (all-cause: HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 0.82–3.08; CVD: HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.60–1.49). Restricted cubic splines confirmed a positive linear association between TyG-WHtR and mortality outcomes (both P overall < .05). After Bonferroni correction, a significant interaction was maintained only for body mass index in relation to all-cause mortality (P interaction < .007). The TyG-WHtR index demonstrated stable predictive power, with a 3-year area under the curve (AUC), 5-year AUC and 10-year AUC of 0.736, 0.725 and 0.714 for all-cause mortality and 0.675 (3-year), 0.652 (5-year), and 0.609 (10-year) for CVD mortality, respectively. This study provides new evidence for risk stratification using the TyG-WHtR index in the hypothyroid population. An elevated TyG-WHtR index is not only associated with a higher prevalence of hypothyroidism, but also serves as a stable and independent predictor of all-cause and CVD mortality.

1. Introduction

A preliminary version of this article has been published as a preprint.[1]

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a well-established and cost-effective surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR),[2] a core pathophysiological driver of cardiometabolic disorders. Accumulating evidence confirms that the TyG index independently predicts the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD), all-cause mortality, and CVD-related mortality.[3–5] To enhance the predictive accuracy, recent epidemiological studies have developed composite indices that integrate the TyG index with anthropometric measures of adiposity. For instance, indices such as TyG-body mass index (BMI) and TyG-waist circumference (WC) demonstrate superior discrimination of IR and future cardiovascular risk compared with the TyG index alone.[6,7]

Among various obesity metrics, the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) offers distinct advantages over BMI or WC alone. WHtR better reflects central adiposity and has proven more strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk profiles and visceral fat accumulation.[8,9] Consequently, the integration of TyG with WHtR, forming the TyG-WHtR index, represents a promising approach for capturing both metabolic dysregulation and adverse body fat distribution.

This combined metric is particularly relevant in populations with endocrine dysfunction. There is a bidirectional pathophysiological link between hypothyroidism and IR. A bidirectional pathophysiological link exists between hypothyroidism and IR. Thyroid hormone deficiency impairs glucose metabolism and promotes dyslipidemia, thereby promoting IR. Conversely, obesity and hyperinsulinemia may exacerbate thyroid dysfunction, contributing to the pathogenesis of hypothyroidism and Hashimoto thyroiditis.[10] While preliminary studies have reported a positive correlation between the TyG index and prevalent hypothyroidism,[11] the prognostic utility of the TyG-WHtR index, specifically its ability to stratify long-term mortality risk among patients with established hypothyroidism, remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, this retrospective cohort study utilized nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2007–2012). We aimed to rigorously evaluate the association between TyG-WHtR index and the risk of all-cause and CVD-specific mortality in adults with hypothyroidism. By elucidating this relationship, our findings seek to establish TyG-WHtR as a valuable and stable prognostic marker, thereby informing targeted risk stratification and personalized clinical management strategies for this vulnerable population.[12,13]

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source and study population

The NHANES is a series of cross-sectional surveys conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, which is a large-scale, population-based survey of the noninstitutionalized United States population. The NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board and written consent was obtained from all participants. This study employed a retrospective cohort design using data from the 2007 to 2012 NHANES 2007 to 2012. Detailed information regarding the survey, sampling procedures, and laboratory tests are available on the official website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes). All participants were prospectively followed-up until December 2019.

There were 30,442 individuals in the NHANES 2007 to 2012. We excluded participants who met the following criteria: were younger than 18 years of age (n = 11,823); were pregnant (n = 182); reported using medications that could affect serum thyroid function (including amiodarone, thyroid hormone replacement, and/or antithyroidal drugs) (n = 492); had missing or incomplete information on thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and free thyroxine (FT4) concentrations (n = 8587); serum TSH concentrations below the lower limit of the normal range (n = 397); and incomplete TyG-WHtR information (n = 524). Ultimately, the final analysis included 8437 unique participants; a flowchart illustrating the participant selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Flowchart of the participant selection process from the NHANES 2007 to 2012 database. NHANES = National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey.

2.2. Definition of serum thyroid function

Serum thyroid hormone levels were measured using the following assay methods: FT4 concentration was measured using a 2-step enzyme immunoassay and TSH concentration was measured using a third-generation 2-site immunoenzymatic assay. Thyroid peroxidase antibody and thyroglobulin antibody titers were measured using a Beckman access immunoassay system. The normal ranges for TSH and FT4 were defined as 0.39 to 4.6 mIU/L and 9 to 25 pmol/L, respectively, according to the reference established by previous NHANES studies.[14,15] A threshold of 4.5 mIU/L was established for elevated TSH levels, in accordance with the findings reported by Hollowell et al and recommendations from clinical practice guidelines.[15,16] Participants with serum TSH ≥ 4.5 mUI/L, and FT4 concentrations within the normal range (9–25 pmol/L), were considered to have subclinical hypothyroidism. Participants with serum TSH ≥ 4.5 mUI/L, and FT4 level < 9 pmol/L, were considered to have overt hypothyroidism.[17]

2.3. All-cause and cardiovascular mortality

The mortality of participants aged ≥ 18 years in the NHANES program was passively followed-up using National Death Index records until December 31, 2019. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as death from heart disease or cerebrovascular disease using the underlying causes of death according to the International Classification of Diseases, including death from acute rheumatic fever and chronic rheumatic heart disease (I00–I09), hypertensive heart disease (I11), hypertensive heart and renal disease (I13), ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), other heart diseases (I26–I51), and cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69). Follow-up time was defined as the time between the baseline examination date and the date of death or the last date of follow-up (December 31, 2019), whichever came first.

2.4. Covariate assessment

The covariates included age, sex (male or female), race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, or other), BMI (< 25 kg/m2: underweight and healthy weight, ≥ 25 kg/m2, < 30kg/m2: overweight, ≥ 30 kg/m2: obese), and educational status (< high school, high school and General Educational Development, > high school). Data on smoking status (current smoker, former smoker, or never smoker) were collected using standardized questionnaires during the interviews. Drinking status was defined based on consuming more than 12 drinks per year, with those answering “yes” considered drinkers.

2.5. Statistical Analysis

The complex weighting of NHANES data was performed by the “survey” package in R. We merged the data based on the sequence number for each participant according to the recommendations of the official NHANES website. The relevant information can be found at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/tutorials/nhanes-cms/determine/merge.html. In the final cohort, we performed a duplicate value query on the sequence number using R language, and no duplicate participants were identified in our analysis. The general population was divided into quartiles based on the TyG-WHtR index. Continuous variables were described as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables were expressed as frequency (proportion). Linear trends were assessed by calculating the P trend using an unadjusted linear regression. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to investigate the association between the TyG-WHtR index and hypothyroidism, with the lowest group (Q1) serving as the reference. Model 1 was not adjusted for covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender. Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, household income, educational status, smoking status, drinking status, and urinary iodine level.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to estimate HRs for mortality in patients with hypothyroidism, with adjustments for potential confounders. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for race, BMI, household income, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Weighted logistic regression and weighted Cox regression models were applied to control for confounding. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model with 4 knots (placed at the 5%, 35%, 65%, and 95% of the TyG-WHtR index) was used to examine potential nonlinear relationships. Knot placement followed Harrell guidelines to balance the model flexibility and overfitting. The proportional hazards assumption for Cox regression was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with all P values > .05, indicating that the assumption was met.To assess the predictive accuracy of the TyG-WHtR index and BMI for mortality, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to compare the discriminative powers of these indices. Furthermore, considering the longitudinal nature of our data, with a median follow-up of 10.2 years, a time-dependent ROC analysis was conducted to evaluate the dynamic predictive performance at 3, 5, and 10 years. Subgroup ROC analyses were performed across the different BMI categories to verify the robustness of the TyG-WHtR index.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to sex, age (< 65 or ≥ 65 years), BMI (< 25, 25–30, or ≥ 30 kg/m2), race (White, Black, Mexican American, or Other), diabetes, hypertension, and smoking status. To control for the risk of false positives across the 7 subgroup analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold for interaction P values of α′ = .05/7 ≈ 0.0071. After correction, only BMI remained significantly associated with the TyG-WHtR in relation to all-cause mortality.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value < .05.

3.2. Association strength between TyG-WHtR quartiles and hypothyroidism

Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that TyG-WHtR index was independently associated with an increased risk of hypothyroidism. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the ORs for hypothyroidism were 1.12 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.82–1.53) for Q2, 1.38 (95% CI: 1.02–1.87) for Q3, and 1.62 (95% CI: 1.20–2.18) for Q4 (P trend < 0.001). These results confirm a robust dose-response relationship between TyG-WHtR and the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction (Table 2) .

3.3. Hypothyroidism and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

Over a median follow-up of 10.2 (interquartile range, 7.8 to 12.6) years, 1234 individuals died, including 366 cardiovascular deaths. When comparing individuals with normal thyroid function, those with subclinical hypothyroidism had a higher hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause mortality (HR = 1.34, 95% CI = 1.19~1.79, P = .01), and those with overt hypothyroidism had an elevated HR for all-cause mortality (HR = 1.53,95% CI = 1.06~2.20, P = .023) after adjusting for age, sex, race, BMI, household income, education status, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Furthermore, in the fully adjusted model, a significant association was observed between subclinical hypothyroidism (HR = 1.67, 95% CI = 1.09~2.58, P = .02) and overt hypothyroidism (HR = 1.88, 95% CI = 1.02~3.46, P = .042) and the risk of CVD mortality (Table 3).

4. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that elevated TyG-WHtR levels, correlated with a higher prevalence of both subclinical and overt hypothyroidism. These results were consistent with those reported in the literature. Specifically, Choi et al identified a significant positive association between the TyG index and TSH levels, consistent with prior research conducted on Korean cohorts after controlling for confounding variables.[13,18]

The interrelationship between hypothyroidism and insulin signaling is well documented, with perturbations in 1 system frequently leading to disruptions in the other. In individuals with hypothyroidism, IR is linked to a reduction in resting energy expenditure and thermogenesis.[19,20] A population-based, cross-sectional study of 2808 Chinese adults further illustrated this association and adjusting for confounding variables, the study found that obese females exhibited a higher prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism than their nonobese counterparts (22.1% vs 13.4%; OR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.20–2.80; P = .005).[21] Additionally, an experimental investigation utilizing rat thyroid cell cultures revealed that IR can impair iodide uptake by inhibiting the sodium/iodide symporter and reducing thyroglobulin secretion in response to TSH, potentially through the action of inflammatory cytokines.[22]

However, the association between hypothyroidism and mortality remains unclear. However, a substantial body of research has demonstrated that hypothyroidism is associated with an increased risk of both all-cause mortality and CVD mortality compared with euthyroid individuals in the general population. A meta-analysis of 55 cohort studies, involving a total of 1898,314 participants, indicated that individuals with hypothyroidism exhibited a significantly higher risk of CVD mortality (HR: 1.96; 95% CI: 1.38–2.80) and all-cause mortality (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.13–1.39) relative to those with euthyroidism. Subclinical hypothyroidism is also associated with an elevated risk of CVD mortality. These findings align with those of the previous studies conducted by Park et al[23] and Leung et al.[24]

A cohort study utilizing registry data, encompassing 235,168 individuals with a median follow-up duration of 7.2 years, identified an elevated mortality rate among untreated hypothyroid patients (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.26–1.69; P < .001) compared to euthyroid controls.[25] This finding remained statistically significant upon stratification of the cohort into individuals, marked hypothyroidism (TSH > 4.0 mIU/L and ≤ 10 mIU/L; P < .001), and marked hypothyroidism (TSH > 10 mIU/L; P = .002). Furthermore, our study established a statistically significant association between subclinical hypothyroidism, overt hypothyroidism, and an increased risk of CVD as well as all-cause mortality in a fully adjusted model. The TyG index has been associated with mortality in various populations. Shen et al demonstrated that a baseline TyG index exceeding threshold values (TyG index > 9.05, all-cause mortality and > 8.84 for CVD mortality) was positively correlated with mortality (HR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.02–1.99 for all-cause mortality and HR = 1.77, 95% CI = 1.08–2.91 for CVD mortality), consistent with the findings reported by Zhang et al.[26]

In addition, 2 recent studies established a significant association between the TyG index and all-cause and CVD mortality in the general population, particularly among individuals aged < 65 years, irrespective of their diabetes status.[27,28] A systematic review and meta-analysis further demonstrated that the WHtR is significantly more effective than WC in predicting CVD.[29] Moreover, 2 investigations utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study database indicated that the combined use of the TyG index and WHtR offers a superior assessment of CVD risk compared with using either the TyG index or WHtR independently.[30,31] Chen et al identified a U-shaped relationship between TyG-WHtR and coronary heart disease.[32] However, to date, no study has explored the association between TyG-WHtR index and mortality across diverse populations. Our study demonstrated that the highest tertiles of the TyG-WHtR index were significantly positively associated with all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with hypothyroidism, after adjusting for variables such as age, sex, race, BMI, household income, educational status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

RCS analysis demonstrated a positive linear relationship between TyG-WHtR index and all-cause and CVD mortality in individuals with hypothyroidism. Subgroup analyses further revealed that TyG-WHtR index was associated with increased mortality risk among hypothyroid individuals aged ≥ 65 years and those with a BMI of ≥ 25 kg/m2. Consequently, the TyG-WHtR index positively correlated with a higher prevalence of both subclinical and overt hypothyroidism. Additionally, the TyG-WHtR index may offer enhanced predictive value for all-cause and CVD mortality in the context of hypothyroidism. Nonetheless, because this study represents an inaugural investigation of the predictive capacity of the TyG-WHtR index for mortality, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

This study had several limitations. First, beause this was a retrospective cohort study, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between TyG-WHtR and hypothyroidism. Similarly, although the survival status and causes of death were ascertained through the National Death Index, the NHANES data, originating from a cross-sectional survey, did not permit causal inferences. Second, the study participants were exclusively from the United States, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other countries. Third, the study could not account for changes in thyroid function and TyG-WHtR index over the follow-up period.

5. Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence for the clinical utility of the TyG-WHtR index in risk stratification of patients with hypothyroidism. Our findings demonstrate that an elevated TyG-WHtR index is not only strongly associated with a higher prevalence of subclinical and overt hypothyroidism but also serves as an independent predictor of long-term all-cause and CVD mortality.
