GBA3 as a regulator of sphingolipid metabolism in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xuehong Wang, Lanyu Li, Tian Sun, Zhidong Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognosis and clinical treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), one of the most prevalent malignant tumors, remains significantly challenging, underscoring the urgent need for novel biomarkers and therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes. Our study investigated the clinical significance and biological functions of glucosylceramidase beta 3 (GBA3) in HCC progression.MethodsGBA3 expression was analyzed in 39 paired HCC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, validated in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, and further confirmed via immunohistochemistry (IHC) in 90 additional paired specimens. The connection between GBA3 expression and clinical outcomes was analyzed through use of TCGA data and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The functional roles of GBA3 were examined through knockdown experiments in HCC cells, with proliferation, migration, and invasion being evaluated. Mechanistic studies were conducted to clarify the link between GBA3 downregulation and ceramide metabolism via ceramide synthase 3 (CerS3).ResultsThe expression of GBA3 messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein were significantly downregulated in HCC tissues as compared to non-tumor tissues. Low GBA3 expression correlated with advanced HCC and shorter patient survival. Functional assays demonstrated that GBA3 knockdown enhanced HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, GBA3 downregulation reduced CerS3 expression, disrupting ceramide metabolism and promoting HCC progression.ConclusionsGBA3 downregulation in HCC is related to the aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, indicating its potential as a sphingolipid metabolism-related prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified glucosylceramidase beta 3 (GBA3) as the key hydrolase of glucosylceramide (GlcCer), which is significantly downregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and promotes progression by modulating ceramide metabolism. GBA3 expression correlates with patient prognosis, suggesting its potential as an independent prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in HCC.

What is known and what is new?

• GlcCer, a key sphingolipid metabolite, has been implicated in cancer progression. Although GBA1-mediated GlcCer hydrolysis is well-characterized, the role of GBA3, which could also hydrolyze GlcCer to ceramides, remains unexplored in HCC.

• We found that the downregulation of GBA3 promotes HCC progression by decreasing ceramide levels, and its expression level could predict patient survival, highlighting its dual role as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings establish GBA3 as a novel metabolic regulator in HCC pathogenesis, expanding our understanding of sphingolipid dysregulation in HCC. The GBA3-ceramide axis represents a previously underappreciated pathway that may complement known mechanisms of tumorigenesis.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has an alarmingly high incidence and mortality rate in the world (1). Although systemic therapies and novel agents for HCC have recently been developed, the 5-year survival rate is still unsatisfactory due its insidious onset, high malignancy, and considerable heterogeneity (2). Consequently, elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of HCC and developing innovative therapeutic strategies remain critical priorities and ongoing challenges in current HCC research.

Studies have demonstrated that metabolic reprogramming, as one of the hallmark characteristics of malignant tumors (3), plays a critical regulatory role in the emergence and development of HCC (4). It has been well-established that metabolic reprogramming in HCC manifests as a series of complex metabolic alterations, encompassing glucose metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism (5). Notably, as an essential component of lipid metabolism, aberrant sphingolipid metabolism has been closely associated with the aggressive biological behaviors of HCC (6).

Sphingolipid metabolism constitutes a complex network centered with ceramide (Cer) being its central molecule. Studies from our and other research teams have demonstrated that Cer induces apoptosis, thereby critically regulating tumor cell survival and death (7,8). Other research has also suggested that Cer can inhibit tumor invasion and metastasis (7). Cer biosynthesis occurs through the following pathways: de novo synthesis, salvage pathway, and sphingomyelin (SM) hydrolysis. Notably, the salvage pathway generates Cer via the deglycosylation of glucosylceramide (GlcCer), a catabolic process primarily occurring in lysosomes. Intriguingly, glucosylceramidase beta 3 (GBA3) has been identified as capable of cleaving hydrolyzes GlcCer in cytosol (9). In 2020, it was first suggested that decreased GBA3 expression might correlate with poor prognosis in patients with HCC (10); however, the precise mechanism by which GBA3 affects HCC pathogenesis remains largely elusive.

GBA3, a glycoside hydrolase (GH) family 1 enzyme, can hydrolyze a variety of substrates containing a β-D-glucose or similar sugar moiety attached to a hydrophobic group (11). A study had found that GBA3 plays a role in the metabolism in mammalian cells (12). GBA3 is significant factor in early-stage gastric cancer and contributes to drug resistance in colon cancer (13,14). Nevertheless, the precise function and underlying mechanisms of GBA3 in HCC have not yet been elucidated (15).

In our study, HCC tissue showed a significant reduction in GBA3 expression in messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels as compared to adjacent non-tumor tissue, which was consistent with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Further statistical analysis revealed an inverse correlation between tumor diameter and GBA3 mRNA expression. Analysis of TCGA data suggested that GBA3 may play a key role in advanced HCC. Moreover, functional studies demonstrated that the low expression of GBA3 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells. These results indicate that GBA3 might function as a suppressor of metastasis in HCC. In terms of mechanism, we found that low GBA3 expression could lead to decreased Cer levels by regulating ceramide synthase 3 (CerS3) expression, which is a critical alteration given the role of Cer in HCC pathogenesis and progression. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://jgo.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jgo-2025-567/rc).

Methods

Patient and tissue samples

Thirty-nine paired samples, including HCC tumor and adjacent peritumoral tissues, along with relevant clinicopathological data, were obtained from patients with HCC who underwent surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University in Guilin, Guangxi, China, between May 2017 and March 2019. Notably, to ensure accurate comparison of GBA3 expression between HCC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, we strictly selected specimens from pathologically confirmed HCC patients while excluding those with metastatic HCC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and other non-HCC liver malignancies in our analysis. Immediately after collection, the fresh tumor specimens were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 ℃ for subsequent analysis. Additionally, an HCC tissue microarray (TMA) was acquired from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Ethical approval for our study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (No. 2017QTZCLL-1). All samples used for research purposes were provided under prior written informed consent by the participating patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Chemicals and reagents

TRIzol reagent was obtained from Life Technologies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and TB Green Premix was purchased from Takara Bio (Kusatsu, Japan). The Fast-Quant RT Kit (with gDNase) was obtained from Tiangen (Beijing, China). The antibodies against GBA3 and CerS3 were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). The antibody against Cer was purchased from Thermo Fish Scientific. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and β-actin antibodies were obtained from ZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China). 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DBA) and hematoxylin were purchased from ZSGB-BIO. Crystal violet and paraformaldehyde were acquired from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

RNA extraction from tissues and cells was performed with TRIzol reagent. Complement DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed by reverse-transcribing 1 µg of total RNA with the Fast-Quant RT Kit. The following primer sequences were used for amplification: GBA3 forward, 5'-AAGACCCAGCTAGACCCCGA-3'; GBA3 reverse, 5'-AAAGCAGACGCCTCTCCTGT-3'; GADPH forward, 5'-CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT-3', and GADPH reverse, 5'-GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT-3'. Each 25 µL of quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) reaction comprised 2.0 µL of cDNA, 1 µM of primer, and 12.5 µL of TB Green Premix. Reactions were run in duplicate and repeated three times for reproducibility. Gene expression levels were quantified with the comparative CT (2−ΔΔCT) method, normalized against glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and presented as fold changes as compared to the control group.

PCR array was purchased from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany) and conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

TCGA data processing and survival analysis

TCGA RNA-sequencing data from the liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) dataset and the relevant clinical information were downloaded with the Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net) developed by University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC). All of results were analyzed via the Wilcoxon test. Overall survival analysis was conducted with the “survival” R package (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The median GBA3 expression levels were used as a cutoff for the log-rank test.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC, tissues embedded in paraffin were initially heated at 60 ℃ for 1 hour, followed by dewaxing with dimethylbenzene and gradual rehydration with decreasing concentrations of alcohol. The sample was boiled in citrate antigen recovery buffer (pH =8.0) in a pressure cooker for 2.5 minutes and was then allowed to cool to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 10 minutes, which was followed by rinsing the sections with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% Tween-20. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a rabbit polyclonal anti-GBA3 antibody, diluted 1:1,000 in the working solution. Following PBS-Tween washing, a secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO) was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Signal detection involved 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and was followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Negative controls were prepared by replacing the primary antibody with nonspecific rabbit serum under the same conditions. Two blinded pathologists without knowledge of the patients’ clinical or follow-up data independently assessed GBA3 protein expression levels with a semiquantitative IHC scoring system. The scoring criteria were as follows: 0, no positive cells; 1, <25% positive cells; 2, 25–50% positive cells; 3, 51–75% positive cells; and 4, >75% positive cells.

Cells and treatment

The HepG2 and Huh7 human liver cancer cell lines were obtained from the Cell Storage Center of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and the SK-Hep-1 line was sourced from American Type Culture Collection (Manassa, VI, USA). MHCC-97H and MHCC-97L were obtained from Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University, which were established at this hospital. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fish Scientific) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Transfection

Huh7 cells were transiently transfected with 200 pmol of GBA3-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA) or control siRNA (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sk-Hep1 cells were transiently transfected with 200 pmol of pcDNA3.1GBA3 or a negative control (GenePharma) as described above. The following sequences were used: GBA3 siRNA sense, GGACGACAGGUUUCAUCAATT; and anti-sense, UUGAUGAAACCUGUCGUCCTT.

Western blotting

Post-transfection, cells were lysed using lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride protease inhibitor (100 mM). The lysates underwent centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 ℃. The supernatant was collected, and its protein concentration was measured with a bicinchoninic acid assay protein assay kit (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein was combined with 4× SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and heated for 5 minutes. Equal protein quantities were resolved with SDS-PAGE, with protein separation monitored with a prestained molecular weight marker (Thermo Fisher). The proteins were electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were incubated in Tris-buffered saline with Tween20 (TBST) buffer (1 M of Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 5 M of NaCl, and 0.1% Tween-20) with 5% nonfat milk for 1 hour at room temperature under gentle agitation. Membranes were washed three times with TBST, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃, and then treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse) for 1 hour at 37 ℃. Protein signals were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Beyotime).

Cell viability assay

Post-transfection, Huh7 and SK-Hep-1 cells (5×103–7×103/well) were plated in 96-well plates and cultured for 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours. Cytotoxicity was then evaluated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, China). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader after 1-hour incubation with CCK-8 reagent at 37 ℃. In addition, we also used the cloning assay to detect the ability of proliferation. Huh7 and SK-Hep1 cells (800–1,000/well) were plated in six-well plates after transfection and cultured for 2 weeks. Then, after fixation with paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes and staining with crystal violet for 1 hour, the samples were air-dried and photographed.

Migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion capacities were evaluated with Transwell inserts (8-µm pore size; Coring Inc., Corning, NY, USA). In summary, 1×104–2×104 cells in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, with the lower compartment filled with complete medium [DMEM with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS)] serving as a chemoattractant. After a 48-hour incubation at 37 ℃, cotton swabs were used to gently remove non-migrated cells from the upper membrane surface. Cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 12–16 hours, and then imaged with an inverted light microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded into 24-well plates for 24 hours, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes, and stained with ceramide antibody, appropriate secondary antibodies, and DAPI was used for staining nucleus . Images were analyzed with a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Intergroup comparisons were evaluated with either the Student t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (for multiple groups), with a threshold of P<0.05 being considered statistically significant.

Results

Decreased GBA3 expression in HCC predicted adverse prognosis

To assess GBA3 expression in patients with HCC, 39 pairs of tumor tissues and their corresponding peritumoral samples were assessed via real-time PCR. After normalization of GBA3 expression according to GAPDH, 34 of the 39 HCC tissues (87.2%) showed a ≥ two-fold decrease in GBA3 mRNA expression compared with that in the corresponding peritumoral tissues (P<0.001; Figure 1A). Further analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between GBA3 expression levels and HCC tumor size. Notably, GBA3 expression was significantly downregulated in tumors >5 cm compared to small HCC (≤5 cm diameter; P=0.004). However, comparative analysis showed no statistically significant difference in GBA3 expression between large- (>5–10 cm) and giant- (≥10 cm) HCC subgroups (P=0.29) (Figure 1B).

Analysis of TCGA data validated these findings, revealing that GBA3 mRNA expression in 371 tumor samples was decreased significantly compared to that in 50 normal samples (Figure 1C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of TCGA cohort indicated that reduced GBA3 expression correlated with poorer clinical outcomes (Figure 1D). Furthermore, advanced-stage (stage 3–4) and higher tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification (T3) tumors displayed significantly lower GBA3 expression than did early-stage (stage 1–2) and well-differentiated tumors, respectively (Figure 1E-1H).

To confirm these observations at the protein level, we performed IHC analysis on an independent cohort of 90 HCC specimens. IHC revealed significant cytoplasmic GBA3 downregulation in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor counterparts (P<0.001; Figure 2A,2B). The diagnostic performance of GBA3 protein expression yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.62 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.534–0.698; Figure 2C]. However, we did not observe a significant correlation between low GBA3 expression and clinical characteristics in this cohort, which may be attributed to the advanced disease stage (predominantly late-stage HCC) of the included cases (Table 1). Although the AUC value was relatively modest, the differential expression of GBA3 in HCC remained statistically significant. We propose that the predictive performance could be enhanced through two potential strategies: (I) increasing the sample size to improve statistical power (16); and (II) constructing a combined biomarker panel incorporating established indicators such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and PIVKA-II (protein induced by vitamin K absence/antagonist-II) (17).

Collectively, these results demonstrated consistent downregulation of GBA3 in HCC at both the transcriptional and translational levels. The inverse correlations between GBA3 expression and tumor size, advanced stage, higher TNM classification, and poor survival outcomes suggested that GBA3 may act as a tumor suppressor in HCC progression.

GBA3 expression stratified HCC prognosis in a gender-specific but not race-specific manner

To further investigate the potential modifying factors in the association between GBA3 expression and HCC prognosis, we conducted stratified Kaplan-Meier survival analyses. Our findings revealed that the prognostic impact of GBA3 downregulation was significantly influenced by demographic factors, with distinct patterns being observed across racial and gender subgroups.

Notably, race-stratified analysis demonstrated that low GBA3 expression was not significantly associated with survival outcomes in either Asian (P=0.055) or Whiteman (P=0.43) patient cohorts (Figure S1A,S1B). However, gender-specific analysis indicated a significant prognostic association in male patients with low GBA3 expression (P=0.03), while female patients showed no such correlation (Figure S1C,S1D). These results suggested that the tumor-suppressive function of GBA3 may be sex-dependent in HCC progression.

Low expression of GBA3 promoted the progression of HCC

To clarify the functional role of GBA3 in HCC, we performed comprehensive biological assays with HCC cell lines. Our initial characterization revealed a distinct GBA3 expression pattern across multiple HCC cell lines, with Huh7 cells exhibiting the highest mRNA levels and SK-Hep1 cells showing the lowest expression (Figure 3A). Functional studies demonstrated that GBA3 knockdown in Huh7 cells (achieved using GBA3-specific siRNA; Figure 3B,3C) significantly enhanced cellular viability (P<0.001), migration (P<0.001), and invasion (P<0.001) compared to control cells (Figure 3D-3F). Conversely, GBA3 overexpression in SK-Hep1 cells (via pcDNA3.1-GBA3 transfection; Figure S2A) markedly suppressed proliferation (P<0.001), migration (P<0.001), and invasion (P<0.001) (Figure S2B-S2D). These results indicated that GBA3 might function as a metastasis suppressor in HCC.

GBA3 in regulating HCC progression through CerS3-mediated Cer metabolism

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which GBA3 downregulation promotes HCC progression, we used a sphingolipid metabolism pathway array screen (40 genes). The results demonstrated that GBA3 knockdown significantly reduced CerS3 expression by 14.3-fold (P<0.001; Figure 4A and Table 2), which was further validated at the protein level by Western blotting (Figure 4B). Notably, as CerS3 is a key synthase for ceramide production, its expression showed positive correlation with GBA3 levels (downregulated with GBA3 knockdown but upregulated with GBA3 overexpression). Subsequent immunofluorescence quantification revealed that Cer content markedly lowered upon GBA3 suppression, whereas GBA3 overexpression elevated Cer levels (Figure 4C,4D). These findings suggest that GBA3 may regulate HCC progression through the CerS3-Cer metabolic axis.

Discussion

GBA3 belongs to the Klotho protein family, which includes α-Klotho, β-Klotho, KLPH, and GBA3. Although these proteins share structural homology (showing 41%, 36%, and 41% amino acid identity with α-Klotho for β-Klotho, KLPH, and GBA3, respectively), they exhibit distinct enzymatic properties. Notably, while both α-Klotho and β-Klotho contain two GH motifs, KLPH and GBA3 possess only one GH motif. Crucially, GBA3 is uniquely characterized by its demonstrated GH activity within this family (18), particularly its ability to hydrolyze GlcCer in the cytosol. Klotho family members (α-Klotho and β-Klotho) have been established as suppressors in cancer and the aging processes (19); however, the precise role of GBA3 in tumorigenesis remains largely unexplored. So, this study will preliminarily investigate the mechanistic role of GBA3 in HCC from a novel perspective.

In our study, we found that GBA3 mRNA expression was decreased in HCC tissues as compared with that in the corresponding peritumoral tissues; furthermore, the protein level of GBA3 was also decreased in HCC as compared with that in the corresponding peritumoral tissue. The change in GBA3 expression at both the transcriptional and translational levels suggested that GBA3 expression might be lost during the course of malignant transformation in HCC and that GBA3 might act as an anti-oncogene. Another finding of this study was that decreased GBA3 expression was related to larger tumor size and advanced HCC. Data from our cohort showed that GBA3 was decreased in large HCC tumor (size ≥5 cm). Interestingly, while our findings align with a previous report demonstrating GBA3 downregulation in HCC through TCGA analysis and IHC validation (10), our study further clarified the role of GBA3 in HCC beyond these initial observations.

Our analysis revealed significant prognostic implications of GBA3 expression in patients with HCC. The Kaplan-Meier plotter survival analysis showed that low GBA3 expression predicted poor survival in patients with HCC. Furthermore, GBA3 mRNA expression in stage 1+2 tumors did not affect the prognosis significantly, but patients with HCC with low GBA3 expression in stage 3+4 tumors had a poor prognosis, revealing the prognostic diagnostic value of GBA3 in advanced HCC; GBA3 might also play an important role in accelerating the development of HCC. These results confirmed the above-mentioned regarding decreased GBA3 expression being related to larger tumor size and advanced HCC. In addition, the association with the prognosis of low GBA3 mRNA expression in T1 and T3 stage cancer also constitutes evidence for this phenomenon: the higher the stage is, the worse the prognosis. Moreover, we found that the capacity of migration in HCC was markedly enhanced after GBA3 was knocked down, while the opposite occurred when GBA3 was overexpressed.

Kaplan-Meier plotter survival analysis also showed that the poor prognosis of HCC was associated with low GBA3 expression in males but not in females, indicating that GBA3 might interact with certain sex-biased genes to affect the survival of patients with HCC. Moreover, in the cohort of Asian and Whiteman populations, respectively, a low expression of GBA3 was not correlated with poor prognosis. It is widely known that chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is the primary cause of HCC in Asia, especially in China (20). Therefore, the effect of GBA3 in the Asian and Whiteman cohorts seems to indicate that GBA3 might not be a factor in HBV-related HCC or influence its prognosis.

Our cell assays further proved that the low expression of GBA3 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC. Our investigations on mechanism revealed that GBA3 knockdown significantly downregulated CerS3 expression, leading to a marked reduction in cellular Cer levels. Conversely, GBA3 overexpression upregulated CerS3 expression, resulting in substantially increased Cer content. These reciprocal alterations demonstrate a direct regulatory relationship between GBA3 and the CerS3-Cer metabolic axis in HCC. A large body of evidence supports the assertion that GBA3 is involved in GlcCer metabolism, and GBA3 is the only member of the Klotho family that can catabolize GlcCer (9,18,21). However, the function of GBA3 remains unclear, particularly within cancer. Cer is a central molecule in the sphingolipid pathway, can induce apoptosis (22,23), and can be glycosylated to generate GlcCer; moreover, alterations in the level of GlcCer has been found in cells and tissues in patients with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, skin disorders, or cancer. The upregulation of GlcCer promotes cell survival and proliferation (24). Hence, since GBA3 is the enzyme that catabolizes GlcCer, the decrease in GBA3 expression may lead to GlcCer accumulation and subsequent reduction of Cer levels, thus affecting the growth of HCC. Taken together, our results indicate that GBA3’s role in HCC relates primarily to the interconversion between GlcCer and Cer. This may clarify the mechanism underlying the effect of Klotho family members on HCC.

Conclusions

The mRNA or protein expression of GBA3 was reduced in HCC tissues, and GBA3 expression was correlated with the prognosis of patients with HCC. GBA3 expression was decreased, and the prognosis was worse in patients with advanced HCC. Low GBA3 expression in promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC, indicating that GBA3 might be a prognostic indicator of HCC, especially for advanced HCC. Our findings suggest that GBA3 likely influences HCC progression through its regulatory effects on the CerS3-Cer axis, suggesting a novel mechanism by which GBA3, a key enzyme in the ceramide (Cer) salvage pathway, regulates de novo Cer synthesis, thereby expanding the theoretical framework of sphingolipid metabolism.
