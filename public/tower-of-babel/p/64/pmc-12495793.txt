Isolation and characterization of probiotic yeasts from traditional fermented horsemeat products in Xinjiang: functional evaluation and potential application
Authors: Xue Sun, Yuxian Wang, Dilmurat Zilale, Tong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausages in Xinjiang are rich in microbial resources, mainly including lactic acid bacteria and yeasts. Although the probiotic effects of lactic acid bacteria have been widely studied, there is relatively little research on the probiotic properties of yeasts, which limits their application in functional food development. This study isolated 44 yeast strains from traditional fermented horsemeat products in Xinjiang, and 16 strains exhibiting strong tolerance to temperature, acidic pH, simulated gastrointestinal fluids, and high bile salt solution were screened. Molecular identification revealed that these strains belonged to Candida zeylanoides (C-5, C-8, C-13, C-15, C-21, C-22, C-24, R-8), Candida parapsilosis (R-10, R-12, R-14, R-19, R-20), Candida metapsilosis (R-18), and Rhodotorula alborubescens (R-6, R-11). Several strains demonstrated significantly higher cell surface hydrophobicity, auto-aggregation, and co-aggregation with pathogenic bacteria than the control strain Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745, indicating strong intestinal adhesion and antagonistic potential. All strains have high antioxidant activity (> 60%) and certain cholesterol-lowering ability. Furthermore, extracellular metabolites from select strains exhibited antibacterial activity and potential glucose-lowering effects. Specifically, C. zeylanoides C-8 inhibited α-glucosidase by 54.14%, and C-8 inhibited angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) by 37.05%. Hemolysis assays confirmed that all strains were non-hemolytic, suggesting good biosafety. These findings highlight the multi-functional probiotic potential of yeast strains from traditional fermented horsemeat products and provide a scientific basis for their development as novel functional food ingredients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04322-8.Keywords: Yeasts, Fermented horsemeat products, Tolerance, Probiotic potential, Biosafety

Introduction

Xinjiang, one of the largest provincial-level administrative regions in China, is home to numerous nomadic people whose fermented food products have gained widespread recognition [1]. Among these, fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausage made by the Kazakh people using traditional methods are particularly popular across the region due to their unique flavor and rich nutritional value [2]. Notably, critical stages of the production process are conducted in open environments, facilitating spontaneous interactions between raw materials and naturally occurring microorganisms. This interaction plays a crucial role in developing the unique taste and sensory characteristics of these traditional fermented meat products [3]. Such traditional practices not only reflect ethnic ingenuity but also serve as natural carriers for the exploration of indigenous microbial resources.

Among these microorganisms, probiotics have attracted increasing attention for their potential health benefits to the host. The term “probiotic” originates from Greek, meaning a substance that is good for life [4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) define probiotics as live microorganisms that are beneficial to the health of the host when ingested in sufficient quantities [5]. While commonly studied probiotics include bacterial genera such as Lactobacillus, Bacillus, Bifidobacterium [4], research on probiotic yeasts remains relatively limited. In 1920, Henri Boulard isolated a strain of Saccharomyces boulardii (S. boulardii) that proved effective in the treatment of cholera. Following clinical studies confirming its relative safety, the strain was introduced to the market as a probiotic preparation in the 1950s [6]. To data, S. boulardii remains the only yeast widely recognized as a probiotic. Subsequent research has revealed that S. boulardii possesses a broad spectrum of functions, including anti-inflammatory activity, inhibition of pathogenic bacteria, immunomodulation effects, modulation of the gut microbiota, improvement of digestion function, and the treatment of gastrointestinal disorders [7–10].

In recent years, a variety of yeast strains with probiotic potential have been isolated from fermented foods including cocoa, kefir and Guajillo pepper through in vitro characterization methods [11, 12]. The functional properties of these yeasts have subsequently been explored. Pichia kudriavzevii isolated from kefir exhibited a symbiotic relationship with lactic acid bacteria, demonstrated antimicrobial and antioxidant activities, and inhibited biofilm formation by Salmonella [13]. Simões et al. isolated six yeast strains with probiotic properties from fermented table olives, two of which showed inhibitory effects on Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella enteritidis, and Listeria monocytogenes [14]. Psomas et al. screened three yeast strains with cholesterol-lowering ability from infant feces and cheese, which could assimilate cholesterol and facilitate its excretion [15]. Lata et al. isolated four yeast strains from pickled vegetables, among which Pichia kudriavzevii Y33 showed the highest cholesterol assimilation ability in bile salt-containing media, achieving an assimilation rate of 88.58% [16]. Reyes-Becerril et al. found that β-D-glucan extracted from Debaryomyces hansenii BCS004 exhibited strong antioxidant activity [17]. Similarly, Karim et al. found that Kluyveromyces marxianus possessed potent antioxidant capacity [18], which were further enhanced when used in combined with polyphenol-rich grape seed extract [19]. Moreover, Albuquerque et al. found that S. boulardii THT 500101 improved glucose and lipid homeostasis in diabetic mice [20], indicating promising new applications for this yeast strain in the treatment of diabetes.

Despite these advances, systematic exploration and functional validation of probiotic yeast resources from traditional fermented meat products in Northwest China remain largely unexplored. Xinjiang has rich traditional fermented foods, which are widely appreciated for their appealing taste, long shelf life, and high nutritional value. During the fermentation process, due to differences in production methods and microorganisms in the surrounding environment, unique local microbial strains are well preserved in fermented foods. Therefore, this study aimed to isolate yeast strains from traditional fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausage in Xinjiang and to characterize their potential probiotic properties. The findings will provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel probiotic yeast products.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and isolation of yeasts

The samples for this experiment were four traditional fermented horsemeat and four horsemeat sausages, collected from different areas of Ili, Xinjiang. After collection, all samples were immediately stored at −80 ℃ and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions. Under aseptic conditions, 1 g of each horsemeat and horsemeat sausages samples were weighed and added to 99 mL of 0.9% sterile saline solution. The suspension was mixed thoroughly, after which 1 mL was transferred into a test tube containing 9 mL of saline. Serial tenfold dilutions were prepared up to 10−6. Subsequently, 200 µL of each diluted sample was plated onto Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YPD) medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) to inhibit bacterial growth. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Yeast colonies exhibiting distinct morphologies and sizes were selected and repeatedly streaked onto fresh YPD plates until purified single colonies with consistent morphology were obtained. The YPD medium was composed of 2% (w/v) peptone, 1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) dextrose, and 2% (w/v) agar.

Tolerance assessment of isolated yeasts

Temperature tolerance

To assess the tolerance of the isolated strains to 37 °C, the method described by Adisa et al. [21] was followed with modification. Fresh cultures were inoculated into liquid YPD medium at a concentration of 4% (v/v) and incubated at 180 rpm for 24 h in a shaker at both 30 °C and 37 °C. Uninoculated YPD broth served as the blank control. The growth of the strains was evaluated by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) using a spectrophotometer.

Acid tolerance

The acid tolerance of the isolated strains was evaluated following the method of Muche et al. [22] with slight modification. The activated strains were adjusted to an optical density of OD₆₀₀ = 1.0 ± 0.05 and inoculated into YPD Liquid medium adjusted to pH 2.0. The cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Optical density at 600 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer, with uninoculated YPD medium serving as the blank control. Strains exhibiting stronger growth under acidic conditions were selected for further testing. These selected strains were re-cultured under the same acidic conditions, serially diluted, and plated. After incubation at 30 °C for 48 h, the number of colonies was counted. Acid-tolerant strains were then selected for subsequent experiments.

Simulated gastrointestinal tolerance

The tolerance of the isolated strains to simulated gastrointestinal conditions was evaluated according to the method of Lara-Hidalgo et al. [12] with slight modification. Simulated gastric fluid was prepared by adding pepsin (3 g/L) into a salt solution containing NaCl (2.05 g/L), KCl (0.37 g/L), CaCl2 (0.11 g/L), and KH2PO4 (0.6 g/L). The pH of the solution was adjusted to 2.0. Simulated intestine fluid was prepared by adding trypsin (3 g/L) and pancreatin (1 g/L) into Na2HPO4 (26.9 g/L) buffer solution containing NaCl (8.5 g/L), bile salt (3 g/L), and the pH was adjusted to 8.0. Activated strains were cultured and harvested by centrifugation. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were washed twice with 0.9% (v/v) sterile saline. Simulated gastric fluid was then added to the washed pellets, and the mixture was incubated in a shaker at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 2 h to simulate gastric peristalsis. Following gastric incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellets were resuspended in simulated intestinal fluid. Incubation was continued at 37 °C for an additional 3 h. Samples collected before and after exposure to the simulated gastrointestinal conditions were serially diluted and plated to determine colony-forming units (CFUs). The survival rate was calculated based on the viable cell counts: Survival rate (%)=(Nt/N0)×100, where Nt and N0 represents the number of colonies of the strain at 5 h and 0 h, respectively.

Bile tolerance

A bile tolerance test of all yeast isolates was performed following the method of Greppi et al. [23] with slight modification. The activated strains were inoculated into sterilized YPD Liquid medium at a volume ratio of 4% and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 24 h. The resulting bacterial suspension was centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and the cell pellets were washed twice with 0.9% sterile saline. The bacterial pellets were then resuspended in Liquid media containing bile salt concentrations of 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.3%, respectively. Samples were taken at 0 h and after 4 h of incubation, serially diluted, and plated. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h, after which CFUs were counted.

Molecular biology identification of isolated yeasts

The activated strain was inoculated and incubated overnight. A 1.5 mL aliquot of the bacterial suspension was transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of sterile PBS. After centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was again discarded. Then, 100 µL of TE buffer was added to the pellet. The tube was placed in a boiling water bath for 10 min, then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for 20 min, and subsequently returned to the boiling water bath for another 10 min. Following this, the sample was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 3 min, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube for subsequent use.

The D1/D2 domain of the 26S rRNA gene from the yeast strains was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the forward primer NL1 (5’-GCATATCAATAAGCGGAGGAAAAG-3’) and the reverse primer NL4 (5’-GGTCCCGTGTTTCAAGACGG-3’) [24]. The resulting PCR products were purified and sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for sequencing. The obtained sequences were submitted to the GenBank database, and sequence similarity was assessed using the BLAST tool available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the neighbor-joining method with the SeqMan module (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA) and MEGA version 11.0 software [25].

Cholesterol-lowering ability

The cholesterol-lowering ability of isolated yeasts was measured as previously described [30]. The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the activated yeast culture was adjusted to approximately 0.8. Inhibition of pathogenic bacteria

The inhibitory activity of the probiotic strains against two Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus ATCC 25923 and L. monocytogenes CMCC(B) 54002) and two Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. enteritidis CMCC(B) 50335) was evaluated following the method of Kathade et al. [31] with slight modification. The probiotic strains and pathogenic bacteria were cultured separately in YPD and LB broth, respectively. After cultivation, the probiotic strain cultures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for further use. Pathogenic bacteria were cultured until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.4 ± 0.05, then mixed into LB medium maintained at approximately 55 °C at an inoculation rate of 1% (v/v). The mixture was poured into Petri dishes containing Oxford cups. After the agar solidified, the Oxford cups were removed, and 200 µL of the fermentation supernatant was added into each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured using a vernier caliper.

Safety assessment of isolated yeasts

Hemolysis assays

The activated strains were streaked onto blood agar plates and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Hemolytic activity was evaluated by observing the appearance of hemolytic zones around the colonies. A greenish discoloration of the agar surrounding the colonies was classified as α-hemolysis. The presence of a clear, transparent zone indicated β-hemolysis, and the absence of any visible hemolytic reaction was considered γ-hemolysis [32]. S. aureus was used as the positive control in this assay.

Antibiotic sensitivity assay

The sensitivity of the strains to five commonly used antibiotics (erythromycin, gentamicin, amikacin, cefradine, ciprofloxacin) was assessed using commercial antibiotic test strips. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5 ± 0.05, and 100 µL of the suspension was evenly spread onto YPD agar plates. After the surface of the medium had dried, antibiotic strips were carefully placed on the agar surface and gently pressed with sterile tweezers to ensure full contact with the medium. The plates were then incubated at 30 °C for 24 ~ 48 h. Following incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured using a vernier caliper. Each assay was performed in triplicate, and the mean inhibition zone diameter was calculated [33].

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were repeated three times and the data results were expressed in the form of mean ± standard deviation and statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA with SPSS 22.0 software. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening, isolation, and identification of probiotic yeasts

Due to variations in the dominant yeast populations among different fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausage samples, a mixed-sample approach followed by gradient dilution plating was employed to isolate diverse yeast strains. Individual colonies exhibiting robust growth on selective media were subjected to multiple rounds of streak purification on YPD agar plates until morphologically consistent single colonies were obtained. A total of 44 yeast strains were successfully isolated from fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausage samples, with representative colony morphologies shown in Fig. 1. These strains displayed similar morphological characteristics, with most colonies being moist and viscous, thick, smooth surface, regular edges, primarily white in color, and a few colonies appearing red. Microscopic observation revealed that yeast cells were generally circular or ellipsoidal in shape, with some strains displaying pseudohyphal structures.

Colony morphology and microscopic characteristics of yeast strains isolated from fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausage

To identify yeast strains suitable for in vivo applications, we evaluated the growth capabilities of these isolates under various environmental stresses. Considering that the optimal growth temperature for yeasts ranges from 20 to 30 °C, while the normal human body temperature is approximately 37 °C, probiotic candidates must demonstrate the ability to survive at elevated temperatures to exert beneficial effects in vivo. Therefore, all isolated strains were cultured at both 30 and 37 °C to assess their growth patterns. As shown in Table 1, all 44 yeast isolates survived and maintained certain reproductive capacity at 37 °C. While most strains exhibited higher optical density values at 30 °C compared to 37 °C, several strains (R-8, R-9, R-19) showed enhanced growth at the elevated temperature, suggesting their potential suitability for intestinal delivery applications. Furthermore, probiotics require sufficient acid tolerance to establish colonization and exert their beneficial effects in the gastrointestinal tract. When grown in Liquid media at pH 2.0, significant variations in acid tolerance were observed among different strains (Fig. 2). Ultimately, 21 strains demonstrating robust growth under acidic conditions (higher OD values) were selected for further viability assessment (Table S1). Nearly most strains maintained survival rates exceeding 95% after 4 h of incubation in YPD Liquid medium at pH 2.0. Strain C-14 exhibited the highest survival rate at 107.65%, followed by C-22 at 104.55%. Even under strongly acidic conditions at pH 1.5, the strains maintained survival rates above 80%, indicating their strong acid tolerance.

Tolerance of different yeast strains in a simulated gastrointestinal environment

In addition to acid tolerance, probiotic candidates must also demonstrate resistance to bile salts, which are present in the intestinal environment. Table 3 illustrates strain survival under varying bile salt concentrations. At 0.1% bile salt concentration, survival rates ranged from 95.30 to 105.56%, with strain R-8 exhibiting the highest survival rate, followed by C-24 at 104.65%. At 0.2% concentration, survival rates ranged from 90.35 to 103.55%, with strain R-19 showing the highest survival rate. At 0.3% concentration, survival rates ranged from 93.85 to 107.03%. Generally, strain viability exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing bile salt concentration. Despite the inhibitory effects of bile salts on growth, 16 strains maintained survival rates above 95% even at 0.3% bile salt concentration. Ultimately, these 16 yeast strains simultaneously demonstrating temperature, acid, and bile salt tolerance were selected as potential probiotics. Phylogenetic analysis based on 26 S rRNA sequencing was conducted to identify these strains (Figure S1). The results revealed that the 16 yeast isolates belonged to four distinct species: C-5, C-8, C-13, C-15, C-21, C-22, C-24, and R-8 showed close phylogenetic relationships to Candida zeylanoides CBS619, with 100% sequence identity; R-10, R-12, R-14, R-19, and R-20 also exhibited 100% sequence identity to Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019; R-18 exhibited 98% sequence identity to Candida metapsilosis ATCC 96144; while R-6 and R-11 demonstrated close phylogenetic relationships (100% sequence identity) to Rhodotorula alborubescens CBS 482. The accession number of each isolate obtained from GenBank were presented in Table S2.

Evaluation of probiotic potential of yeast strains

Hydrophobicity and auto-aggregation ability

Intestinal colonization is a key prerequisite for the functionality of probiotics, and their adhesion ability to intestinal epithelial cells serve as an important indicator of colonization, which can effectively prevent strains from being cleared due to intestinal peristalsis and gain competitive advantages in the microbiota [36]. Studies have shown that the hydrophobicity of strains can be assessed by their affinity for hydrocarbon compounds, with stronger hydrophobicity correlating with enhanced adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells [37, 38]. In this study, the cell surface hydrophobicity of 16 yeast strains was evaluated using three organic solvents (chloroform, ethyl acetate, and xylene). As shown in Fig. 3A, all strains exhibited varying degrees of hydrophobicity toward the tested solvents. Strain R-8 showed the highest affinity for chloroform, with a hydrophobicity of 87.55%, while strain R-6 demonstrated the greatest hydrophobicity toward ethyl acetate at 91.53%. Hydrophobicity toward xylene ranged from 14.67 to 50.44%, with the reference strain S. boulardii displaying the lowest hydrophobicity. Notably, several strains exhibited significantly higher hydrophobicity than the control strain (p < 0.05), suggesting superior surface adhesion potential. In addition, auto-aggregation enhances microbial adhesion to the gastrointestinal tract, therefore, probiotic strains should be endowed with auto-aggregation properties [39]. As illustrated in Fig. 3B, the auto-aggregation capacity of strains increased with time. Yeast strains showed different auto-aggregation capacity at 2 h of incubation, which can be related to the strain specific properties. The control strain S. boulardii exhibited the highest auto-aggregation rate of 86.41% at 2 h. After 4-h incubation, all yeast strains demonstrated auto-aggregation rates exceeding 80%, with strain R-6 showing the highest rate at 91.61%. Previous studies have also reported the distinct auto-aggregation characteristics of yeast strains at different incubation times [39, 40].

Co-aggregation ability with pathogens

The co-aggregation ability is important for probiotics in preventing the colonization of pathogens in the intestine and could be part of the anti-infection mechanism of probiotic yeast [41]. Figure 4 presents the co-aggregation results of yeast strains with four common foodborne pathogens (E. coli, S. aureus, S. enteritidis, L. monocytogenes) after 2 h and 24 h incubation at 37 °C. The co-aggregation ability of 16 strains with E. coli and L. monocytogenes demonstrated a clear time dependence, with the co-aggregation rate of the strains generally higher after 24 h of incubation compared to 2 h. There were differences in the co-aggregation efficiency between different yeast strains and various pathogens, indicating that the surface characteristics of the strains are key factors affecting the co-aggregation process. During short-term incubation (2 h), most strains demonstrated higher co-aggregation rates with S. aureus and S. enterica, while after prolonged cultivation (24 h), the co-aggregation abilities with E. coli and L. monocytogenes became more pronounced. Additionally, the results showed that the strains had higher co-aggregation rates against pathogenic bacteria than the control strains, indicating the good co-aggregation ability of isolated yeast strains.

Antioxidant ability

DPPH is a stable free radical existing in monomeric form both in solid and solution states, with a characteristic maximum absorption at 517 nm. It is an ideal indicator for evaluating antioxidant capacity, with a higher DPPH scavenging rate indicating stronger antioxidant activity [42]. As shown in Fig. 5A, the supernatants of all yeast strains tested in the study exhibited significant antioxidant activity, with DPPH radical scavenging rates exceeding 60%. This value suggests that these strains may contain rich antioxidant substances, such as polyphenols, flavonoids, or other metabolic products. Notably, the R. alborubescens R-6 strain demonstrated exceptional antioxidant activity, with a DPPH radical scavenging rate of 88.51%. This was closely followed by the C. zeylanoides R-8 strain, which achieved a scavenging rate of 87.60%. No statistically significant difference was observed between these two strains and the control strain, but their high scavenging rates still indicate strong antioxidant potential.

(A) DPPH and (B) hydroxyl free radical scavenging rate of different yeast isolates

In addition to DPPH scavenging, the hydroxyl radical scavenging ability of the yeast strains was also investigated, as hydroxyl radicals are a type of reactive oxygen species and can cause extensive cellular damage, leading to diseases such as hypertension and cancer [43]. Therefore, effectively scavenging excessive free radicals can enhance immune function, making it an important indicator for evaluating the antioxidant capacity of strains. As shown in Fig. 5B, different strains exhibited significantly varying hydroxyl radical scavenging abilities, with scavenging rates ranging from 31.37 to 87.61%. Six yeast strains demonstrated excellent scavenging rates above 60%, including C. parapsilosis C-5 strain (85.78 ± 0.07%), C-8 strain (74.28 ± 0.14%), C-24 strain (62.92 ± 0.03%), and R-8 strain (73.58 ± 0.24%), and statistical analysis (p < 0.05) confirmed that it was significantly higher than the control strain, suggesting that it may contain special antioxidant active substances or unique metabolic mechanisms.

Cholesterol-lowering ability

This study further investigated the cholesterol-lowering ability of the selected yeast strains. Figure 6 presents the results of the Cholesterol-lowering ability of 16 yeast strains. The results indicate that all 16 yeast strains were effective in reducing cholesterol levels in cholesterol-enriched YPD Liquid medium, demonstrating a widespread Cholesterol-lowering potential. There were noticeable differences in the Cholesterol removal abilities of the strains, with degradation rates ranging from 21.69 to 58.53%. This range reflects significant variations in the metabolic characteristics and enzyme activities among the strains. Notably, five yeast strains exhibited excellent degradation rates exceeding 50%, suggesting that these strains may possess unique cholesterol metabolic mechanisms or produce highly efficient cholesterol-degrading enzymes. Among all the tested strains, C. parapsilosis R-20 showed the strongest Cholesterol-lowering activity, with a degradation rate of 58.53%. Statistical analysis (p < 0.05) confirmed that this was significantly higher than the control strain, indicating that C. parapsilosis R-20 has a significant advantage and application potential in cholesterol-lowering function.

Antimicrobial activity

This study evaluated the antimicrobial activity of the supernatants from the selected yeast strains, with a particular focus on their inhibitory effects against common Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and L. monocytogenes) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and S. enterica). As shown in Table 4, the results indicated that most of the yeast strains exhibited inhibitory effects against one or two of the four pathogens under in vitro conditions, demonstrating a certain level of antimicrobial potential. Notably, under the conditions of this study, none of the tested yeast supernatants showed inhibitory activity against L. monocytogenes, which may reflect the strong resistance of this pathogen to antimicrobial substances produced by yeast or its unique cell wall structure. Regarding the antimicrobial spectrum, different strains showed clear specificity. C. parapsilosis R-19 exhibited the strongest inhibition against E. coli, with an inhibition zone diameter of 17.33 ± 0.69 mm. The C. parapsilosis C-21 strain showed the best inhibition against S. aureus, with an inhibition zone diameter of 13.01 ± 0.56 mm. Meanwhile, C. parapsilosis R-10 demonstrated the strongest inhibition against S. enterica, with an inhibition zone diameter of 15.65 ± 1.24 mm. However, the control strain (CNCM I-745) showed no inhibitory effects against any of the tested pathogens, highlighting the antimicrobial advantages of the selected yeast strains. Some strains, such as C-13 and C-21, exhibited a broad antimicrobial spectrum, inhibiting both S. aureus and S. enterica. Strains C-8 and R-18 showed dual inhibitory activity against E. coli and S. enterica. This broad-spectrum antimicrobial characteristic offers a particular advantage in applications related to food preservation and biological safety.

Safety evaluation

Hemolytic activity assays serve as an important safety parameter for the preliminary screening of probiotic candidate strains. Hemolysis type is determined by observing whether the strain causes the lysis of red blood cells on blood agar plates, forming a transparent zone. In this study, three types of hemolysis were carefully differentiated: α-hemolysis, characterized by partial destruction of red blood cells and the formation of a green transparent zone, indicating potential pathogenicity; β-hemolysis, characterized by complete destruction of red blood cells and the formation of a clear transparent zone, representing high pathogenicity; and γ-hemolysis, which shows no destruction of red blood cells, indicating the absence of hemolytic activity [44]. As shown in Figure S2, none of the 16 tested strains exhibited α-hemolysis or β-hemolysis on Columbia blood agar plates. All strains grew well without forming any transparent zones around them, confirming their non-hemolytic properties (γ-hemolysis) and proving that all tested strains lack hemolytic activity. These results establish the basic safety profile of the strains and are important for assessing their safety as potential probiotics or functional food additives.

Antibiotic susceptibility patterns are crucial for assessing the safety of these strains as potential probiotics or functional food additives. They help identify potential resistance risks and provide guidance for clinical applications. This study further evaluated the antibiotic susceptibility of the selected yeast strains. As shown in Table 5, the results indicate that all tested strains exhibited resistance to erythromycin, cephalexin, and ciprofloxacin. This common resistance pattern may reflect unique structural characteristics of yeast cells or natural resistance mechanisms, which are related to differences in the structure of bacterial cells. However, significant differences were observed among the strains in their sensitivity to gentamicin and amikacin. Strains such as C-5, C-8, C-13, C-24, and R-8 showed sensitivity or moderate sensitivity to gentamicin, while strains R-6, R-10, R-11, R-12, R-14, R-18, R-19, and R-20 exhibited resistance. Similarly, differences in amikacin sensitivity were observed. Some strains, such as C-5, C-8, C-15, C-24, and R-8, were sensitive, and C-13, C-21, and C-22 showed moderate sensitivity, while the remaining strains displayed resistance. As a reference strain, S. boulardii (CNCM I-745) showed resistance to all the antibiotics tested in this study.

Glucose-lowering ability

This study further evaluated the inhibitory abilities of the selected yeast strains against three important metabolic enzymes: α-glucosidase, α-amylase, and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE). These enzymes are closely related to carbohydrate metabolism and blood pressure regulation, and inhibiting their activity is significant for managing metabolic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension [45–47]. Figure 7A shows the inhibition of α-glucosidase by the supernatants of 16 yeast strains. Significant differences in inhibitory activity were observed among the strains, with inhibition rates ranging from 22.43 to 54.14%. The C. parapsilosis C-8 strain exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity (54.14%), followed by R. alborubescens R-11 (52.44%) and C. zeylanoides C-13 (51.95%). Notably, 11 strains showed inhibition rates between 40% and 50%, while the control strain, S. boulardii, displayed the lowest inhibition rate of 22.43%. Figure 7B describes the inhibition of ACE by the yeast strains. The study found that the inhibition rates of ACE by yeast supernatants were all below 50%, with the highest inhibition rate observed in C. zeylanoides C-8 (37.05%) and the lowest in R. alborubescens R-11 (12.46%). Overall, these multifunctional properties give these strains a unique advantage in the development of functional foods and dietary supplements with multiple health benefits.

Discussion

Many traditional foods are rich in diverse microbial communities formed during natural fermentation, with yeast and lactic acid bacteria playing a key role in shaping their unique flavor and nutritional profiles [13]. While extensive research has focused on the benefits of lactic acid bacteria for gut health, the functional potential of yeasts as probiotics remains underexplored compared to their bacterial counterparts. However, existing studies have indicated that yeasts play an important role in maintaining gut microbiota balance, particularly through mechanisms such as inhibiting pathogenic microorganism growth, enhancing mucosal immunity, and producing beneficial metabolites [6]. Recent studies have identified yeast strains isolated from traditional fermented foods, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Kluyveromyces spp., which exhibit good tolerance to gastric acid and bile salts, as well as the ability to adhere to intestinal epithelial cells and exhibit antimicrobial activity [48, 49]. These yeasts not only may have probiotic functions themselves but can also synergize with lactic acid bacteria to enhance the survival and activity of the latter, thereby exerting a complementary probiotic effect [49]. Past research has primarily focused on isolating lactic acid bacteria from yogurt [4], whereas recent attention has shifted to the role and function of yeasts in the traditional fermented foods [50]. Through isolation and screening, we have identified several yeast strains with promising probiotic potential, warranting further investigation into their applications in maintaining gut health, modulating immune responses, and enhancing the biological fortification of foods.

This study isolated and identified 16 yeast strains with probiotic potential from multiple sources, of which 14 belonged to the Candida genus. Although Candida genus contains some pathogenic bacteria, studies have found that multiple strains such as C. boidinii, C. diddensiae, and C. molendinolei, exhibit probiotic and safety characteristics [51]. The screened strains grew well at 37 °C, with a survival rate of 99–101% in simulated gastric fluid, demonstrating their basic ability to traverse the gastrointestinal tract. Especially, R. alborubescens R-11 was able to maintain a survival rate of over 95% under pH 2.0 condition, demonstrating significant higher acid tolerance compared to Lactobacillus spp. (42.92 ~ 75.35%) and Meyerozyma genus (> 65%) isolated from other fermented foods [52, 53]. This superior acid resistance may be attributed to its unique cell wall structure. Gastrointestinal tolerance is fundamental for probiotics to exert their effects, and only enough viable microorganisms that reach the intestine can achieve probiotic function. Additionally, hydrophobicity and auto-aggregation are essential characteristics for probiotic microorganisms, as these features contribute to enhanced microbial adhesion to the gastrointestinal tract [39]. This study found that strain R-8 exhibited hydrophobicity of up to 87.55% against chloroform, while R-6 showed 91.53% hydrophobicity against ethyl acetate, significantly higher than the hydrophobicity observed in Pichia kudriavzevii, Picha sp., and S. cerevisiae isolated from fermented dairy products (23 ~ 50.4%) [54]. Additionally, all the selected strains exhibited auto-aggregation rates above 80% after 4 h, significantly higher than the yeast strains isolated from kefir products (30.59 ~ 43.33%). The strong hydrophobicity facilitates the adhesion between the microbe and epithelial cells, while the higher aggregation ability provides increased cell intensity involving the adhesion mechanism [54].

Co-aggregation ability contributes to creating a competitive microenvironment, inhibiting the colonization and growth of pathogenic bacteria [55]. The strains exhibited increasing auto-aggregation rates over time and demonstrated moderate co-aggregation abilities with four pathogenic bacteria, especially Salmonella, likely due to the presence of Type I hyphae in these bacteria, which can specifically bind to mannose on the yeast cell wall [12]. The hemolytic activity and antibiotic resistance of probiotics are considered safety concerns, as hemolysis can lead to cell lysis, and antibiotic resistance gene have the potential to be horizontally transmitted to the pathogens, both of which pose a threat to host health [40, 56]. The results indicated that all tested strains showed no hemolytic activity and exhibited resistance to most of the tested antibiotics. Only moderate sensitivity or sensitivity was observed against gentamicin and amikacin, allowing probiotic yeasts to potentially maintain activity during antibiotic treatments.

Furthermore, the selected strains demonstrated significant functional diversity. In terms of antioxidant activity, the supernatants of the strains exhibited DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging rates exceeding 65%, which was significantly higher than the ~ 30% scavenging rate reported for kefir yeasts [39], and also surpasses the 61.6% reported for Saccharomyces cerevisiae CCMA 0731 by Menezes et al. [11]. The main antioxidant components were superoxide dismutase, catalase, and carotenoids produced by the yeast strains [57, 58]. Cholesterol-lowering ability is another important functional characteristic. Studies have shown that a 1% reduction in Cholesterol levels can reduce the risk of cardiovascular diseases by 3% [12]. The tested strains may achieve cholesterol-lowering effects through mechanisms such as cholesterol adsorption, bile salt hydrolysis, or metabolic degradation [59, 54]. In enzyme inhibition activity, R. alborubescens R-11 demonstrated a 54.14% inhibition rate against α-glucosidase, higher than the 75% reported for Lipomyces starkeyi VIT-MN03 [29]. This enzyme is closely related to postprandial blood glucose control, and inhibiting their activity is an effective way to alleviate hyperglycemia in patients with type 2 diabetes [45, 46]. Additionally, all strains showed some ACE inhibition activity, suggesting an auxiliary role in blood pressure regulation. ACE is an important enzyme for blood pressure regulation, as it converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II, leading to vasoconstriction and an increase in blood pressure [60].

Conclusion

In this study, yeast strains were successfully isolated from traditional fermented horsemeat and horsemeat sausages in Xinjiang and comprehensively evaluated for their probiotic potential. Through phenotypic screening, molecular identification, and functional characterization, 16 yeast strains were identified with promising probiotic attributes, particularly in their tolerance to gastrointestinal conditions, absence of hemolytic activity, and broad functional capacities. Among these, R. alborubescens R-11 exhibited superior overall performance, showing robust acid and bile salt resistance, high antioxidant and cholesterol-lowering activities, significant inhibition of α-glucosidase, and moderate ACE inhibition. The strain also demonstrated strong co-aggregation ability with pathogenic bacteria, which may contribute to its pathogen exclusion effects in the gastrointestinal tract. Importantly, the antibiotic resistance profile and safety evaluation support its potential application as a probiotic. Our findings highlight the rich microbial diversity present in traditional fermented foods and underscore the importance of strain-level evaluation when identifying probiotic candidates. The multifunctional characteristics exhibited by R. alborubescens R-11 suggest its potential role in gut health regulation, metabolic syndrome management, and functional food development. Nevertheless, further in vivo studies and clinical trials are necessary to confirm its safety and efficacy in human applications.
