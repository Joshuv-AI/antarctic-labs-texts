Rotator Cuff Repair Study Designs Correlate With Revision Shoulder Surgery Rates: A Systematic Review
Authors: Anthony Smyth, Ilona Schwarz, Jack Hop, Kelly Leach, Rachel Frank, Jonathan Bravman, Eric McCarty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify arthroscopic rotator cuff repair study groups and evaluate if study design or other study characteristics correlate with a repeat ipsilateral shoulder surgery.MethodsA systematic search of the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted on March 20, 2021, and April 2, 2021. The following search terms were used by 2 different researchers: ((Rotator cuff repair[Title/Abstract]) AND (Revision[Title/Abstract]) NOT (Systematic Review[Title/Abstract]) NOT (arthroplasty[Title/Abstract]). All English-language studies published between 2002 and 2021 were manually reviewed for revision rate as a primary outcome of primary rotator cuff repair. Revision rate is defined as the percentage of primary rotator cuff repairs that underwent revision.ResultsSixteen studies with 25 total treatment groups were included. Five Level IV studies and 11 Level III studies encompassed a total of 95,578 patients. Of these treatment groups, the revision rate was compared by the study style (prospective vs retrospective), sample size, time required to follow up, time to follow up, average age, and postoperative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score. No significant difference was found between revision rates of retrospective and prospective studies. A significant correlation was found between time required to follow up and revision rate (.42, P = .0415).ConclusionsIn this study, we found that prospective and retrospective studies report similar revision rates after arthroscopic rotator cuff repairs. There was an association between longer follow-up and higher revision rate.Clinical RelevanceProspective and retrospective studies yielding similar results is an important finding when showing the validity of retrospective studies. Understanding the positive correlation between a longer time for follow-up and a higher revision rate along with how age, postoperative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores, and average time to follow up correlate with revision rates is a useful consideration when designing studies and evaluating data.

Rotator cuff tears are the most common cause of shoulder disability.1 Despite extensive research and surgical advances, the standards of care for rotator cuff tears are not fully understood.2 Over the past 2 decades, novel surgical techniques and a deeper etiologic understanding have elucidated more effective treatment algorithms that have improved postoperative outcomes.3 Previous work has shown that despite assumptions that prospective studies of shoulder redislocation rates produce more accurate data, retrospective studies show no significant difference in outcomes when compared to prospective studies.4 Despite the assumption that prospective studies are superior, the difference in outcomes between retrospective and prospectives papers that study arthroscopic rotator cuff repairs (RCRs) is not well understood. The purpose of this review was to identify arthroscopic RCR study groups and evaluate if study design or other study characteristics correlate with a repeat ipsilateral shoulder surgery. We hypothesized that the rate of all-cause revision surgery after an RCR would be similar whether a study has a prospective or retrospective design.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted by 2 independent reviewers (A.S., I.S.) via the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines following the appropriate checklist and template. Each reviewer searched and documented results from 3 databases—PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library—using the following search terms with no addition exclusionary criteria: ((Rotator cuff repair[Title/Abstract]) AND (Revision[Title/Abstract]) NOT (Systematic Review[Title/Abstract]) NOT (arthroplasty[Title/Abstract]). The searches were conducted on March 20, 2021, and April 2, 2021. All English-language studies published from 2002 until the search date were identified and subsequently filtered. After removal of duplicates, manual scans then were performed, and studies were removed that did not have rotator cuff revisions as a primary result, or the study population did not focus on primary arthroscopic RCR (Fig 1). Rotator cuff repair revisions included all-cause repeat ipsilateral shoulder surgery for chart review style studies or Current Procedural Terminology code 29827 for database studies. Data extraction was performed independently by a resident (A.S.) and reviewed by another resident (I.S.). No funding was required to obtain, collect, or analyze any data.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram for selection of studies.

Reporting Outcomes

The papers were scanned, and outcome data for each of the patient sample groups was recorded. For each patient population, data that were extracted included revision rate, study design (retrospective vs prospective), number of patients (n), time required to follow up, average time to follow up, average age, and postoperative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score.

Statistical Analysis

When analyzing the difference between retrospective and prospective studies, a t test was performed to evaluate for statistical significance. For the remaining variables, the revision rate was plotted on the y-axis with the associated variable on the x-axis. R coefficients and P values were subsequently calculated for each graph. All statistical analysis was performed by a statistician (K.L.).

Results

We identified 127 studies for potential inclusion (Fig 1). A total of 16 met the inclusion criteria (Table 1) (7 were Level III, 5 were Level IV, and 4 did not report a level of evidence) with a total of 95,578 patients in 25 treatment groups. Of these studies, 4 (25%) had prospective study designs and 12 (75%) had retrospective study designs. All 95,578 patients were treated for primary arthroscopic RCR for varying degrees of injuries, with a variety of operation techniques and numerous different risk factors. Except for database-style studies, these studies wholly did not identify specific inclusion or exclusion criteria for type of surgery for revision; therefore, all-cause repeat ipsilateral shoulder surgery was subject to review and inclusion. The various types of surgeries included repeat RCR, lysis of adhesions/debridement, biceps tenodesis, and arthroplasty (Table 2). Database-style studies only included revision surgeries identified by Current Procedural Terminology code 29827 (arthroscopy, shoulder, surgical; with RCR). The correlation of number of patients, time required to follow up, average time to follow up, average age, and postoperative ASES score to the revision rate is shown in Fig 2, Fig 3, Fig 4, Fig 5, Fig 6 (Table 3).

Prospective Versus Retrospective

Prospective studies had an average revision rate of 3.88%, whereas retrospective studies had an average revision rate of 6.4%, which was not a statistically significant difference (t = –1.21, P = .2396). None of the other study measures were significantly different between prospective and retrospective studies. There exists a large difference between the mean sample sizes between prospective and retrospective studies (181.5 vs 7,904.3, respectively; t = –1.55, P = .1484). Discussion

In this study, we found no significant difference in outcomes reported between retrospective and prospective studies investigating arthroscopic RCRs. If it were true that retrospective studies have a higher revision rate, it is likely because more time is permitted between operation and evaluation, whereas in prospective studies, the researchers usually set a standard amount of time (e.g., 2 years). The results of this systematic review complement what was previously discovered with shoulder redislocation rates in Gohal et al.4 and expand the understanding of how study designs correlate with outcomes in RCRs.

Often, for a study to be considered high quality, a minimum amount of time is required from operation to follow-up. The correlation described in these graphs shows that time required to follow up has a statistically significant correlation with revision rate, and average time to follow up is relatively incomparable with revision rate. The primary finding of required time to follow up is logically consistent with the idea that as more time passes, more primary RCRs will fail. This study contributes to the overall strength of literature that shows the importance of follow-up after 2 years.21 Evaluators should take this into perspective when evaluating studies with different required times to follow up.

Although not statistically significant, the correlation between study size and revision rate appeared to show that smaller study groups undergo fewer revisions. This could be because prospective studies usually have smaller study groups; smaller study groups are usually the result of focused studies at academic centers, where there may be more efforts at reducing revision rates, and the smaller studies are not the result of large retrospective database searches.

The most interesting finding of this study is the correlation between a higher postoperative ASES shoulder score and a higher revision rate. Although not statistically significant, which may be due to the small sample of studies, it appeared as though higher ASES scores show increased functionality and decreased pain following RCR. A potential reason for this correlation is that increased functionality and decreased pain could be associated with riskier or more aggressive athletic behavior following an operation, which may naturally create the circumstances for more symptomatic retears and revisions. In the future, a higher-powered study might be able to elucidate this correlation between ASES scores and revisions.

This study identified a couple of clinically relevant ideas. The first is the further expansion in the literature that longer follow-up will result in high revision rates. This could relate to standardizing more long-term follow-ups with postoperative patients to identify those who could potentially benefit from surgical reintervention. Second, if ASES score proves to be statistically significant in its correlation with needing a revision, then administering this test in the postoperative period could help surgeons when counseling patients on risks and postoperative restrictions.

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered when evaluating the results of this study. With only 4 prospective studies included in the analysis, this study is inadequately powered to be able to definitively say that a correlation does not exist between study design and revision rate. Given the parameters are limited to the search criteria provided, it would not be possible to expand the number of studies in this study. Additionally, given the variety of populations and lack of consistent reporting across the studies, differentiating groups between age, comorbidity, and primary surgical technique was not possible.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that prospective and retrospective studies report similar revision rates after arthroscopic RCRs. There was an association between longer follow-up and higher revision rate.
