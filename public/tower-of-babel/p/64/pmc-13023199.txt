From diverse CT scans to generalization: towards robust abdominal organ segmentation
Authors: Nicolás Álvarez Llopis, Felipe Ocampo Osorio, Jesús Alejandro Álzate-Grisales, Alejandro Mora Rubio, Francisco García García, Reinel Tabares-Soto, María de la Iglesia Vaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal organ segmentation is a rapidly advancing area in medical imaging, boasting numerous remarkable applications in clinical and research settings. Despite these advancements, most existing segmentation models are developed using single-source data. This homogeneity raises concerns regarding the applicability of these models to more diverse and complex clinical scenarios. This study aimed to develop a generalizable model for the semantic segmentation of abdominal organs using three widely recognized public datasets: BTCV, AMOS, and TotalSegmentator. Extensive cleaning and preprocessing were undertaken to address the challenges posed by data heterogeneity. The merging process resulted in a diverse and comprehensive dataset of 680 CT scans encapsulating varied image conditions and anatomical representations. The comparative analysis utilized two architectural frameworks: nnUNet, representing Convolutional Neural Networks, and Swin-UNETR, embodying Vision Transformers. Results demonstrate the superiority of the nnUNet model across all experiments, demonstrating superior robustness and adaptability under diverse conditions and unseen cases. However, further research could contribute to achieving a more balanced performance across patient groups. With an average Dice Similarity Coefficient of 92.3%, the developed nnUNet model establishes itself as a highly effective and competitive approach in abdominal organ segmentation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02206-5.Keywords: Organ segmentation, Computed tomography, Generalizability, CNN, ViT, nnUNet, Swin-UNETR

Introduction

Abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans are fundamental to contemporary medical imaging, providing insights into the complex structures of the human abdomen. These scans are utilized for various diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, including tumor detection, identifying inflammatory diseases, pinpointing vascular irregularities, and planning for radiotherapy. Additionally, they allow for assessing postoperative outcomes, offering clinicians detailed visual data on patient recovery and progress. Despite their widespread use, the interpretation of abdominal CT scans remains a manual process. Manual contour delineation of target organs is both labor-intensive and time-consuming, and it is susceptible to inaccuracies due to inter- and intra-observer variability [1], [2]. These inconsistencies can adversely influence treatment decisions and impact patient care.

In response to these challenges, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have been transformative, introducing more efficient and reliable methods that show significant promise in automating tasks traditionally performed by human experts. However, most existing implementations are developed with single-center, single-phase, single vendor, and single-disease data sources, raising concerns about their ability to generalize to more diverse cases [3]. The uncertainty in model generalizability can be attributed to several contributing factors, often referred to as ‘domain gaps’ or ‘domain shifts’ [4] [5]. First, variations in imaging protocols across different medical centers introduce inconsistencies. Second, the broad range of diseases in training cohorts complicates the models’ generalization ability. Third, inconsistent image characteristics, such as contrast and resolution, add variability layers. Finally, inconsistent annotation practices among different oncologists or radiologists further compromise the integrity of ground truth data.

Recognizing these limitations, this project is dedicated to developing a robust model that leverages the strengths of multiple diverse data sources to improve the precision and utility of automated abdominal organ segmentation. The objective is to create a model that can accurately segment scans from diverse demographics and with varying image properties. Such heterogeneous data sources are intended to ensure an adequate handling of variations inherent in global healthcare settings, thereby broadening the practical utility of AI in medical diagnostics.

Related work

Advancements in artificial intelligence have significantly enhanced medical diagnostics by utilizing both structured and unstructured data, particularly benefiting detailed abdominal assessments using computed tomography (CT) scans [6]. Recent research in abdominal organ segmentation has employed CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), exploring architectures ranging from classic Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to advanced models like the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [7]. Notable CNN-based models include SegResNet [8], introduced by Myronenko in 2018, which uses a 3D CNN with an encoder-decoder architecture and a variational autoencoder (VAE) branch for regularization [9]. This network incorporates ResNet blocks and Group Normalization [10–12] to reconstruct dense segmentation masks. DiNTS [13], presented by He et al. in 2021, is a Neural Architecture Search (NAS) method tailored for 3D medical image segmentation [14]. It addresses challenges like flexible network topologies and GPU memory constraints, minimizing the “discretization gap” through a topology loss [15], and demonstrates strong transferability across tasks such as brain, liver, and lung segmentation. STU-Net [16], introduced by Huang et al. in 2023, refines the nnUNet framework by optimizing convolutional blocks and scaling strategies. Pre-trained on the TotalSegmentator dataset [17] with 104 structures, it shows strong transfer capabilities. MedNeXt [18], developed by Roy et al. in 2023, combines Transformer-inspired designs with ConvNeXt approaches [19], addressing challenges in data-scarce medical environments [20–23].

Recent studies highlight the continued relevance of U-Net architectures and the growing importance of techniques like data augmentation, domain adaptation, and semi-supervised learning to address annotation scarcity and improve model generalizability in medical image segmentation [24]. These strategies are becoming central to the development of robust deep learning systems that can operate effectively under common data constraints in clinical practice. Building on these insights, Upadhyay et al. (2023) proposed architectural and training modifications to the U-Net framework with the introduction of Mod-UNet and its semi-supervised extension, Semi-Mod-UNet [25]. FKD-Med, by Sun et al. (2024), is an innovative framework that combines Federated Learning (FL) and Knowledge Distillation (KD) to address data scarcity and communication inefficiencies in medical image segmentation across institutions using U-Net. By enabling privacy-preserving data sharing and drastically reducing model size (while even improving accuracy) [26].

Vision Transformers (ViTs) offer advantages in capturing long-range dependencies and managing clinical data variability. nnFormer [27], introduced by Zhou et al. in 2021 (published in 2023), is a 3D Transformer architecture integrating convolution and self-attention mechanisms. It features local and global self-attention and a novel “skip attention,” replacing standard skip connections. Experiments on datasets like MSD (brain tumor task), BTCV, and ACDC [28] show nnFormer’s superior performance compared to previous Transformer-based models and nnUNet. CoTr [29], presented by Xie et al. in 2021, is a hybrid framework combining CNNs and Transformers. It employs a Deformable Transformer (DeTrans) to handle long-range dependencies through a deformable self-attention mechanism, reducing complexity by focusing on key positions and processing multi-scale, high-resolution feature maps essential for precise segmentation. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) has gained attention for its versatility and accuracy without specific training requirements. MA-SAM [30], introduced by Chen et al. in 2023, adapts SAM for 3D medical image segmentation across modalities like CT, MRI, and surgical videos. It incorporates 3D adapters into SAM’s transformer blocks and uses a parameter-efficient strategy, FacT [31], for fine-tuning. Evaluated on datasets like BTCV, AMOS22, and EndoVis 2018 [32], MA-SAM outperforms advanced 3D methods, including nnUNet, in Dice scores for organ segmentation. DA-TransUNet, developed by Sun et al. (2024), enhances medical image segmentation by integrating dual attention mechanisms (spatial and channel) into a Transformer-based U-Net architecture. Its design optimizes computational efficiency and feature extraction, achieving superior performance across multiple clinical datasets [33].

An innovative hybrid architecture combining CNNs and State Space Models (SSMs) emerged with U-Mamba [34], presented by Ma, Li, and Wang in early 2024. U-Mamba integrates CNNs with SSMs [35], combining local feature extraction with long-range dependency modeling and leveraging the efficiency of the Mamba block [6]. It includes a self-configuration mechanism for different datasets and was evaluated on 3D organ segmentation in CT and MRI (FLARE [36] and AMOS22), instrument segmentation in endoscopy (EndoVis 2017 [37]), and cell segmentation (NeurIPS 2022 [38]). Models such as MaS-TransUNet (Upadhyay et al., 2024) combine the strengths of CNNs and Transformers within a U-shaped encoder-decoder structure and integrate multiple attention mechanisms, including self-, edge-, channel-, and feedback-attention [39]. MIPC-Net, introduced by Pan et al. (2024), improves medical image segmentation by combining position and channel attention to better capture boundaries and abnormal regions. Its architecture also strengthens information recovery between encoder and decoder. Tested on multiple datasets, it outperforms existing methods, particularly in boundary accuracy [40]. The results surpassed CNN and Transformer-based models, demonstrating superiority in modeling long-range dependencies and computational efficiency. Despite these advancements, limitations persist due to models being trained and evaluated on single datasets, restricting generalization in varied clinical contexts. Models like SegResNet and CoTr, evaluated only on specific datasets, lack cross-testing on multiple datasets, making it difficult to validate robustness. Confusion between generalization and transferability has led to inaccurate claims in studies like DiNTS, where only transferability is demonstrated. Although MA-SAM addresses generalization more effectively, it still shows decreased performance with unseen data, indicating the need for further adjustments to ensure reliability in diverse scenarios.

The literature underscores the importance of enhancing model generalization through unified datasets that encompass anatomical diversity and minimize biases for precise predictions in varied clinical environments. While ViT-based architectures offer advantages over CNNs in capturing complex patterns more effectively in abdominal organ segmentation, there remains a need for models that can reliably generalize to unseen data. Our work aims to address these gaps by developing a robust model trained on multiple diverse datasets, enhancing generalizability in abdominal organ segmentation.

Materials and methods

Dataset

Three comprehensive datasets were utilized: BTCV, AMOS, and TotalSegmentator. BTCV contains 50 abdominal CT scans with annotations verified by radiologists [17]. AMOS includes 600 scans (500 CT and 100 MRI) covering 15 organ categories, with ground-truth segmentations generated through a semi-automatic process and validated by radiologists [21]. TotalSegmentator provides 1228 CT images annotated for 117 anatomical structures, refined via an iterative process with nnUNet [17]. Table 1 provides a comparison of their key attributes:

To ensure quality and relevance in the segmentation of abdominal organs, data cleaning was performed in the three sets used. From the TotalSegmentator set, which initially contained 1228 CT scans, only those corresponding to the abdominal region were retained, and those with insufficient segmentation were eliminated, resulting in 378 for the study. In the AMOS set, of the 600 scans, 272 high-quality scans were obtained. For the BTCV set, 30 relevant scans were included. A selective slice filtering process was applied on all datasets to focus on the abdominal region and exclude irrelevant sections. The liver mask was used to exclude the cervical and thoracic regions, but pelvic and femoral areas were retained to provide contextual anatomical information and avoid bias. After cleaning and preprocessing, the datasets were merged to train a model with better generalization ability. They were unified by selecting 12 common classes present in all of them: spleen, right kidney, left kidney, gallbladder, esophagus, liver, stomach, aorta, inferior vena cava (IVC), pancreas, right adrenal gland and left adrenal gland. Resulting in a final dataset composed of 680 CT scans from various sources and conditions.

To mitigate this, only scans containing all 12 relevant segmentation masks were included, ensuring a balanced class distribution and promoting fair analysis.

Segmentation models

In this study, two medical image segmentation models were used: nnUNet and Swin-UNETR, each adapted to the specific needs of abdominal organ segmentation. nnUNet [17] is a self-adjusting framework designed to optimize CNN configurations specifically for medical datasets. nnUNet adapts to the characteristics of each dataset by automatically adjusting architecture, preprocessing, and training parameters. It configures its pipeline by combining general settings, rule-based adjustments based on key features (such as image size and voxel spacing), and empirical post-processing decisions. This framework employs an ensemble approach that combines predictions from 2D and 3D U-Nets, omitting the low-resolution cascade configuration due to the sufficient patch coverage at full resolution.

Swin-UNETR [44] uses the Swin Transformer architecture, limiting self-attention calculations to non-overlapping local windows while allowing cross-window connections within a U-shaped network. Its encoder employs a hierarchical Swin transformer mechanism in 4 stages, converting images into 3D token sequences and using a shifted window scheme to compute self-attention efficiently. The decoder integrates skip connections from the encoder at various resolutions, refining and recovering spatial details through residual blocks and deconvolutional layers. This design enables the model to capture multiple scales and details of anatomical structures in complex images, ensuring precise and detailed segmentation.

Sliding window inference

During the validation phase, a sliding window inference technique was applied to overcome input size limitations and improve segmentation accuracy (Fig. 8). This method divides the full image into overlapping patches that match the model’s input size, processing them independently and then assembling them to form the final segmentation. Maintaining a 50% overlap between patches ensures full coverage and reduces edge errors, as each pixel is evaluated in different contexts, helping to refine and improve uniformity in the segmentation.

Model configuration

To train the models in this study, specific configurations of optimization, loss functions, and transfer learning strategies were applied. The nnUNet model uses the SGD optimizer with Nesterov momentum [50], accelerating convergence with a high momentum value of 0.99, while Swin-UNETR utilizes AdamW [51], which individually adjusts the learning rate and directly applies weight decay. Both models applied a weight decay of 3e-5 to improve regularization and stability during training.

The chosen loss function, Dice Cross-Entropy, combines Dice loss to maximize overlap between predictions and ground truth with Cross-Entropy loss to enhance voxel-level classification accuracy. Swin-UNETR is the only model that incorporates transfer learning, utilizing pre-trained weights on a set of 5050 3D images from various clinical sources [52], which strengthens its performance in multi-organ segmentation tasks.

Experimental setup

The experiments for this study were conducted in collaboration with the Joint Research Unit in Biomedical Imaging FISABIO-CIPF, using a high-performance computing environment. The core infrastructure included 40 CPUs and 4 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs, each with 32 GB of VRAM and 375 GB of RAM, providing the necessary power and memory to efficiently process extensive datasets for GPU-intensive model training.

A batch size of 2 was used for nnUNet’s 3D model, while Swin-UNETR required a batch size of 1 due to its higher computational demands. For nnUNet’s 2D approach, a larger batch size of 195 was feasible due to the lower computational load. Only nnUNet used a Polynomial Learning Rate scheduler, starting with a learning rate of 0.01 and gradually reducing it for precise adjustments during training [21].

Evaluation metrics

To effectively evaluate the performance of the multi-organ semantic segmentation models, this study employs the Dice score (DSC), which is a statistical tool used to measure, in this context, the similarity between the organ segmentation predicted by the model and the ground truth segmentation. The Dice index is calculated using the following formula:

where the cardinality of the intersection of the number of voxels that are classified as part of the object of interest in both the ground truth segmentation and the segmented image; and and the cardinalities of each segmentation.

Two variations of this metric are used to furnish a comprehensive evaluation of the models:

Mean Dice Score: Provides a global assessment of the models’ performance, calculating the average Dice coefficient across all classes. It reflects the overall accuracy of the models in organ segmentation.

Data post- processing

To improve model performance, post-processing inspired by [53] is applied, addressing segmentation challenges using anatomical knowledge. In simple terms, these post-processing steps are designed to “clean up” the raw segmentation outputs by correcting common errors based on known anatomical properties. This process focuses on three key aspects:

First, the post-processing removes components smaller than the primary one in each class, assuming that the organ of interest is represented by the largest contiguous region. This step filters out small, isolated regions that are likely noise or segmentation errors, thereby improving the overall accuracy and the Dice score.

Additionally, it addresses confusion between symmetric organs, such as kidneys and adrenal glands, by grouping them into a single class and assigning them a position (left or right) based on their location in the image to enhance specificity. In other words, when the model mistakenly overlaps or mixes up similar organs, this method uses anatomical location to clearly label each one, reducing ambiguity.

Finally, size criteria are applied based on the expected volume of each organ to eliminate false positives, adjusting the rate to optimal values (0.9, 0.75, 0.25, and 0.1) according to the training set. This means that if a segmented region is much smaller than what is typically expected for that organ, it is likely an error and is removed from the final prediction.

In order to find the optimal post-processing scheme, various compositions of the aforementioned strategies are defined, namely:

To fine-tune the post-processing, each organ’s predictions are optimized using five-fold cross-validation, testing different “rate” values to achieve the best performance for each case.

Results

Model performance

The performance of the 2D, 3D, and ensemble models was evaluated in the comparative analysis of the nnUNet framework configurations. Evaluation is influenced by organ size variations: larger organs like the spleen, liver, and right kidney achieved higher Dice scores (97.4%, 98.2%, and 96.5%), reflecting the model’s ability to segment large, consistent structures. Smaller organs like the gallbladder, esophagus, and adrenal glands received lower scores (86.8%, 89.0%, 84.9%, and 85.2%), presenting challenges due to less visual information and greater variability. Nevertheless, the model demonstrated a notable capacity to adapt to complex patterns, achieving a mean Dice score (mDice) of 92.3%, indicating its robust performance and clinical potential (Table 3).

Test set results

To better evaluate the segmentation capability of the models on unseen data, tests were performed on the BTCV and AMOS datasets. Since the reference segmentations for BTCV were not available on the official Synapse Challenge site, segmentations from the Zenodo repository (https://zenodo.org/records/1169361) were used. These segmentations are consistent with the official images but lack quality due to the absence of organs like the right kidney, aorta, inferior vena cava, and both adrenal glands. Therefore, the Dice scores for BTCV were calculated excluding these classes.

In contrast, Swin-UNETR obtained lower scores, with differences of 0.85% and 2.94% for BTCV and AMOS, respectively, compared to nnUNet.

External data evaluation

To further assess the generalization ability of the models, they were evaluated with external datasets, including FLARE 2022 and KiTS19. The KiTS19 dataset includes lesion segmentation, but for this study, lesions were excluded, focusing on organ segmentation under various conditions, such as ethnicity and the use of contrast agents. The FLARE 2022 publication [54] describes three external validation cohorts (North American, European, and Asian patients); however, it is unclear which ethnicity the available dataset belongs to. A similar uncertainty exists with KiTS19 [22], which does not provide ethnicity information, although it mentions that the research was supported by a North American entity. Additionally, the datasets vary in contrast enhancement, leading to differences in tissue intensity values.

The results in Table 7 show that both models have excellent generalization capabilities in relation to their validation and test performance. Overall, nnUNet outperformed Swin-UNETR in all previous experiments. The FLARE 2022 dataset results reflect a similar performance to AMOS and better than BTCV. However, in the KiTS19 test set, Swin-UNETR unexpectedly outperformed nnUNet, which is likely due to a specific case rather than an indication of superior performance.

Statistical analysis

The graphs for each analysis are available in Appendix A0.2, including histograms, quantile-quantile (q-q) plots, and Shapiro-Wilk tests to verify the normality of the distributions. Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used as non-parametric alternatives to Student’s t-test and ANOVA to study the significant differences between the data groups. Dunn’s tests were applied after the Kruskal-Wallis test to identify specific differences between the groups.

Correlation analysis of performance across multiple datasets

A Spearman correlation coefficient analysis was conducted to assess the interrelationship of segmentation efficacy among the three studied datasets. The organ classes were ranked based on their mean Dice scores for each dataset, as shown in Table A.1, and the Spearman rank correlation coefficients for the three datasets can be found in Table A.2.

The analysis showed a positive correlation between the datasets in a pairwise approach, with an almost exact order in the rankings between the models, except for the aorta surpassing the left kidney in the Swin-UNETR model. The results revealed rank correlations of approximately 90.55% and 91.48%, indicating a consistent pattern of segmentation performance across diverse anatomical structures. This suggests that high-performing anatomical structures in one dataset tend to perform similarly in the others, and this consistent performance could be attributed to the intrinsic characteristics of the organs, such as their anatomical variability discussed in Section 12, where larger volumes are easier to segment than smaller ones.

A discrepancy in sex distribution was identified in the dataset, with 59% of subjects being male and 41% female. Figure 11 illustrates that the male group has a higher distribution of Dice scores, suggesting a potential bias in the representation of sexes that could affect segmentation performance. The Mann-Whitney U test revealed p-values of approximately 0.012 and 0.003 for nnUNet and Swin-UNETR, indicating statistically significant differences in segmentation performance. The results were more accurate in the male cohort, confirming a performance bias related to the sex of the patients and highlighting a limitation in the models’ capabilities.

Age groups were identified (10–39 years, 40–59 years, 60–79 years, and 80–99 years) to represent the young, middle-aged, older, and senior populations, taking into account the physiological changes in abdominal organs discussed in Section 3.1.4. Figure 12 shows that the 10–39 age group has the lowest median Dice score, suggesting the lowest average performance, with some negative outliers. The 40–59 age group shows improved performance, while the 60–79 age group surpasses the 40–59 group in median performance, maintaining a consistent trend into maturity. The 80–99 age group shows a slight decrease in median score compared to the older adults group but still performs better than the younger and middle-aged groups.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that effective generalization in medical image segmentation can be achieved by combining multiple datasets, thereby overcoming the limitation of training models with data from a single source. By integrating the BTCV, AMOS, and TotalSegmentator datasets, a model was developed that generalizes better across various conditions and anatomical variations, achieving superior performance compared to state-of-the-art models. Additionally, its ability to maintain stable results across data from different backgrounds and ethnicities highlights its potential for applications in diverse clinical settings.

Thorough image preprocessing has been fundamental in the methodology, identifying and correcting several human errors during this process, such as missing segmented classes in the masks and incorrect segmentations. Unifying multiple datasets underscores the need to normalize the properties that characterize the heterogeneity of these datasets, primarily the intensity and spacing of the scans.

One of the most striking findings of this study is the clear superiority of nnUNet over Swin-UNETR, which challenges the hypothesis that ViT-based architectures generally demonstrate superior performance in segmenting abdominal organs compared to CNNs. Although ViTs have shown considerable potential in various computer vision applications, the results obtained indicate that CNNs retain significant advantages in the specific context of abdominal organ segmentation. It is crucial to consider that the particular conditions of this study may have favored the performance of nnUNet. Factors such as the nature of medical image segmentation tasks and the scale of the problem likely influenced the observed superiority of CNNs. While Swin-UNETR did not outperform nnUNet in this study, its Vision Transformer-based architecture shows great potential for medical segmentation. Its main limitations include high computational demands and the need for large volumes of data, which hinder its use in clinical settings with limited resources and directly affect its ability to capture fine anatomical details. Nonetheless, hybrid convolutional approaches, domain-specific pretraining, optimization techniques, and certain architectural adjustments could improve its performance and broaden its applicability in diverse clinical scenarios.

When comparing our results with state-of-the-art models (Table 10), the nnUNet demonstrated superior performance, achieving higher Dice scores of 85.97% on BTCV, 90.32% on AMOS, and 94.25% on TotalSegmentator. These scores surpass existing models, highlighting nnUNet’s strong generalization ability across different datasets, even though it was trained on combined datasets rather than individually. It’s important to note some limitations in this comparison due to omitted classes in the AMOS benchmark and unspecified evaluated classes in the TotalSegmentator benchmark, which may affect direct comparability. While Swin-UNETR did not outperform all competitors, it surpassed other transformer-based models, including its original version. This suggests that training on combined datasets improved its generalization capabilities, indicating potential for further enhancement. In addition to evaluating segmentation performance with the Dice Similarity Coefficient, we incorporated the Hausdorff Distance metric to assess the boundary precision of the models (Table 5). The HD analysis reveals that nnUNet consistently produces lower boundary errors compared to Swin-UNETR across all datasets (e.g., overall HD of 5.85 mm vs. 9.27 mm on BTCV, 3.58 mm vs. 5.07 mm on AMOS, and 2.06 mm vs. 3.89 mm on TotalSegmentator). Notably, the results also indicate that larger organs, such as the liver and spleen, tend to achieve lower HD values due to their more consistent and distinguishable boundaries, while smaller organs, including the gallbladder, esophagus, and adrenal glands, exhibit comparatively higher HD scores, being the Swin-UNETR values even higher. This suggests that segmentation of smaller structures, which are more susceptible to boundary variations and partial volume effects, remains a challenging task.

The presence of performance biases identified in this study (particularly those related to sex, patient age, and scanner model) raises highly relevant clinical, ethical, and economic considerations. Clinically, reduced segmentation accuracy in underrepresented groups can lead to diagnostic errors, suboptimal treatments, or inadequate surgical planning, directly impacting the quality of care. This is especially critical in contexts like oncology or radiotherapy, where precision is vital. Ethically, such biases may reinforce healthcare disparities, violating principles of fairness and distributive justice. Models that underperform for women, younger patients, or those scanned with specific equipment risk institutionalizing clinical decisions that are unbalanced and unjust—particularly for already vulnerable populations. Economically, these biases could increase healthcare costs due to repeat imaging, incorrect treatments, or potential litigation, and may undermine trust in AI-based tools. Therefore, future research should actively address these biases through data augmentation for underrepresented groups, equitable training objectives, and post-hoc calibration before deployment in clinical practice.

Our results indicate that segmentation performance for smaller organs is notably lower than for larger structures. This dropoff can be attributed to several factors: smaller organs often have subtle and less well-defined boundaries, are more susceptible to partial volume effects, and may exhibit higher inter-observer variability during manual annotation. To address these challenges, future work could explore multi-scale training strategies that more explicitly focus on the fine details of small structures. Additionally, incorporating specialized loss functions that assign higher weights to the segmentation of smaller organs may help mitigate these discrepancies. Alternative approaches, such as using high-resolution patches or dedicated refinement stages in the network architecture, could further enhance the delineation of these challenging regions.

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of dataset diversity and comprehensive preprocessing in developing robust segmentation models. The success of nnUNet underscores the continued relevance and effectiveness of CNN-based architectures for medical image segmentation tasks, especially when trained on diverse and well-preprocessed datasets.

Conclusions

This project addressed the unification of datasets from different sources and with variable properties to achieve comprehensive generalization in abdominal organ segmentation. The BTCV, AMOS, and TotalSegmentator datasets underwent a cleaning process to remove redundant cases and unify them into a single clean dataset with broad anatomical representation. The nnUNet and Swin-UNETR models were used to compare different architectures, with the CNN-based nnUNet achieving superior performance. Additionally, nnUNet demonstrated a strong generalization ability, standing out both in its individual performance across each of the three datasets and on external datasets with diverse origins and imaging conditions.

The diverse conditions of the datasets required a procedure to facilitate their unification. This process involved eliminating numerous scans. The CT angiographies from TotalSegmentator had to be discarded due to increased intensities in organs of interest, which would complicate cohesion with non-contrast scans. Moreover, many cases in the AMOS and TotalSegmentator datasets lacked vital organs in their segmentations, resulting in their direct removal due to the lack of information to complete the missing segmentation. It was necessary to crop the images of interest to limit the scans to the abdominal region, especially for those that included sections of the human body unrelated to this study. This process significantly reduced the number of useful scans, resulting in a homogeneous dataset of 680 scans and 12 classes characterized by their quality, cohesion, and diversity.

The experimental results demonstrate that the nnUNet model can process data from various sources and properties. The five-fold cross-validation achieved a Dice Similarity Coefficient of 92.3%, with individual scores of 85.97% for BTCV, 90.32% for AMOS, and 94.25% for TotalSegmentator. Although the performance was solid at the aggregate level, there were drops in BTCV and AMOS due to their lower representation. The test set results showed scores of 86.17% for BTCV and 90.93% for AMOS, highlighting the model’s robustness in unseen cases. The developed nnUNet surpasses both the original model and recent ViT-based implementations, although the comparison conditions were not identical. The model was also tested with external data, such as FLARE 2022 and KiTS19, obtaining results of 90.43% and 82.07%, respectively, demonstrating its capability to handle variable data sources.

Additionally, the observed lower performance for smaller organs underscores the need for further refinement of segmentation algorithms tailored to these structures. In future studies, we propose the exploration of targeted solutions, such as enhanced multi-scale training, weighted loss functions, and specialized network modules, to improve segmentation accuracy for small anatomical structures. These refinements have the potential to reduce boundary errors and improve overall clinical utility, thereby addressing a key limitation identified in our current work.

Data analysis identified sources of bias related to sex, age, and the CT scanner model used. To avoid reducing the dataset, an approach was taken to manage and mitigate these imbalances. Despite mitigation techniques, biases persisted, with the model performing better in male groups, older patients, and scanners from GE Medical Systems and Siemens. Although the differences are statistically significant, performance in underrepresented cohorts remains adequate, though further efforts are required to balance performance across the various groups. The presence of performance biases in the model raises important clinical, ethical, and economic implications. Clinically, it can impact the quality of diagnosis and treatment in underrepresented groups, especially in critical areas such as oncology. Ethically, it reinforces inequalities in healthcare access and quality, violating principles of fairness. Economically, bias-related errors may lead to higher healthcare costs, legal risks, and distrust in AI technologies. These findings highlight the need to actively mitigate bias through better data collection practices, equitable training, and model calibration.

This study has shown that ViTs do not always outperform CNNs. The nnUNet model significantly outperformed the Swin-UNETR model in terms of performance on both internal and external data and in mitigating bias in the affected groups. This difference is also evident in the influence of post-processing, which was considerably lower with nnUNet than with Swin-UNETR, where the post-processing influence of the latter was ten times greater, indicating a wide margin for improvement. While it could be argued that the architectures selected as representatives of each group might have influenced these results, the superiority of CNNs in abdominal organ segmentation compared to the state of the art has been clearly demonstrated.
