Injury incidence and patterns in men’s UEFA EURO 2024 and CONMEBOL Copa America 2024: descriptive findings from two simultaneously staged tournaments
Authors: Evert Verhagen, Katrine Okholm Kryger, Zoran Bahtijarevic, Osvaldo Pangrazio, Francisco Forriol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjury surveillance is fundamental for understanding and managing risks in elite football. The simultaneous organisation of Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) EURO 2024 and Confederación Sudamericana de Fútbol (CONMEBOL) Copa America 2024 created a unique chance to use a standardised method across two major men’s tournaments, providing descriptive insights into injury rates and patterns in various competitive settings.ObjectiveTo analyse the incidence, severity and burden of time-loss injuries across both tournaments.MethodsA prospective injury surveillance study was conducted using standardised methodologies from UEFA’s Elite Club Injury Study and International Olympic Committee reporting guidelines. Injury incidence rates (IRs) were calculated per 1000 hours of exposure, and injury burden was measured in tournament days lost per 1000 hours.ResultsA total of 85 injuries were recorded, resulting in an overall IR of 4.0 per 1000 hours (95% CI: 3.1 to 4.8). Match injury rates (16.3 per 1000 hours; 95% CI: 11.9 to 20.7) were significantly higher than training rates (1.8 per 1000 hours; 95% CI: 1.2 to 2.4). Injury rates were comparable between UEFA EURO (3.9 per 1000 hours; 95% CI: 2.9 to 4.9) and CONMEBOL Copa America (4.2 per 1000 hours; 95% CI: 2.6 to 5.7). The injury burden was higher in UEFA EURO (30.0 vs 18.3 days lost per 1000 hours). Muscle injuries (58%) and lower extremity injuries (86%) were the most common.ConclusionsInjury patterns were broadly similar across tournaments, with lower extremity injuries, particularly muscle injuries, being the most common. The study highlights the presence and impact of pre-existing injuries on player availability and underscores the practical value of harmonised injury surveillance, even in small sample contexts, to enhance international data comparability and support future multi-tournament analyses.

Introduction

Injuries are a reality in professional football, impacting player health and team performance. Effectively managing injury risks requires a foundation in injury prevention, starting with reliable surveillance.1 By monitoring injuries over time, researchers and practitioners can identify patterns, understand risk factors and refine strategies to keep players fit and performing at their best.2

The Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) has led football injury surveillance research for over two decades. After launching the men’s UEFA Elite Club Injury Study in 2001,3 the organisation has systematically tracked injury trends in elite men’s and—more recently also—women’s European football.4 This includes UEFA’s men’s European Championships which have featured injury monitoring since UEFA EURO 2004, creating a resourceful dataset for long-term comparisons and strategies for injury prevention.5 In 2024, South America’s Confederación Sudamericana de Fútbol (CONMEBOL) Copa America joined this initiative, implementing its injury monitoring programme. This geographical expansion enables a broader examination of injury trends across football cultures and playing conditions.

Previous research suggests that injury risk and performance in elite football can be affected by factors like match location, climate, travel demands and tactical strategies.68 Despite the awareness of these contextual factors, few studies have employed standardised methods to compare injury patterns across different continents or confederations. The simultaneous staging of UEFA EURO 2024 and CONMEBOL Copa America 2024 offered a unique opportunity to analyse injury patterns across two international men’s tournaments. Both events used the same standardised methodologies, allowing for direct comparisons of injury rates, types and circumstances between European and American men’s teams.

This study presents findings from injury data collected during UEFA EURO 2024 and CONMEBOL Copa America 2024. It examines overall injury rates and compares injury trends between the two tournaments. By identifying key similarities and differences, we aim to provide meaningful insights that enhance injury prevention efforts at the highest levels of international men’s football.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a prospective injury surveillance design during the 2024 UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America tournaments. We invited all 40 teams that qualified for UEFA EURO 2024 (n=24) or CONMEBOL Copa America 2024 (n=16), of which 39 agreed to participate in the study (table 1). We tracked the 1012 players throughout their participation in the tournaments.

Data collection commenced on the first day of training camps and concluded on the day of each team’s final match in the tournament. Consequently, teams’ participation varied depending on the duration of their preparation phase and progression through the competition. All participating teams sent us complete data.

Data collection

Data collection during UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America followed the previously described methodology implemented via the UEFA Elite Club Injury Study and during past UEFA EUROs.3 5 Data collection also adhered to the football-specific extension of the IOC (International Olympic Committee) consensus on recording and reporting epidemiological data on injury and illness in sports.9

Team doctors were responsible for data collection. Before the tournament, each doctor was provided with a study manual and data collection forms and briefed about the study procedure in meetings with UEFA and CONMEBOL representatives. The manual contained information about the study purpose, design, relevant definitions and examples of completing the data collection forms.

Exposure

Each team doctor received attendance record forms and was responsible for completing them. These records tracked player attendance at on-pitch training sessions and match participation. Exposure times were recorded in minutes to base the injury incidence on player exposure time.

Injury

An injury was defined as any physical damage sustained during football activities, including scheduled matches or training sessions, that prevented the player from fully participating in10 either training or matches.11 Pre-existing injuries were defined as injuries present on the player’s arrival at the national team base camp. Team doctors reported these on day one of the preparation phase and were not included in incidence rate (IRs) or burden calculations. A player was considered injured until the team doctor permitted full participation in team training or match play. The national team doctors monitored all injuries until the final rehabilitation date, which included return-to-play dates following the UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America tournaments when players had returned to their clubs.10

The team doctor documented all injuries sustained during the tournament on an injury card (online supplemental Appendix 1). Basic information was recorded, including the activity during which the injury occurred (training or match play), the injury type, injury location and injury diagnosis, whether it was a reinjury, the injury circumstances (contact or non-contact) and the mechanism of injury (overuse or trauma). The minute of injury and the referee’s sanction, if any, were also noted for match injuries.

If a player sustained a lower extremity muscle injury, a specific ‘lower extremity muscle injury card’ would be completed instead of the general injury card (online supplemental Appendix 2). This card was identical to the general injury card, except that it included specific additional fields related to the classification of the injury and its treatment.

A specific ‘head injury card’ was to be completed if a player sustained a head injury (online supplemental Appendix 3). This included, but was not limited to, concussions. The head injury card was identical to the general injury card, except that it had additional specific fields relating to the location of impact, injury diagnosis and injury mechanisms.

Data verification

All records were returned weekly. Player attendance records were reviewed alongside injury reports to confirm exposure and identify any discrepancies in injury documentation. Publicly available match reports were also used as a secondary verification source. If records were not returned in time, incomplete or unclear, the main author contacted the team doctor responsible directly for follow-up. On receipt, reported injuries were coded following the Orchard Sports Injury and Illness Classification System (V.15).12

Analyses

Analyses followed previous comparable descriptive injury studies.35 1315 Injury IRs were calculated for all players and separately for UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America based on the number of new injuries reported per 1000 hours of tournament exposure. Thus, the exposure time of each player until the onset of injury was considered. The corresponding 95% CIs were obtained using a Poisson model.

Following football-specific recommendations, we categorised injury severity to the number of days absent from training and match play: slight (0 days), minimal (1–3 days), mild (4–7 days), moderate (8–28 days) and severe (>28 days).9 Per these recommendations, we also calculated the median days of time-loss and 25th (Q1) and 75th (Q3) percentiles for aggregated time-loss data across players.

Finally, we calculated the injury burden in two ways; as the number of tournament days lost per 1000 hours of tournament exposure (ie, excluding time-loss beyond the tournament) and as the total number of days injured per 1000 hours of tournament exposure (ie, including time-loss beyond the tournament).10 16 17 Using bootstrapping with 10 000 draws sampled at the player level, we calculated 95% CIs around these injury burden measures.15

Differences in injury IRs and injury burdens were considered statistically significant when the 95% CIs did not overlap, corresponding to a p<0.05.

Results

Exposure

UEFA EURO had 622 players contributing 14 359 hours of exposure, with 12 075 hours (84%) during training and 2284 hours (16%) during matches, across 471 training sessions and 80 matches, including 29 friendly matches. Similarly, CONMEBOL Copa America involved 390 players providing 6968 hours of exposure, with 6003 hours (86%) during training and 965 hours (14%) during matches, over 202 training sessions and 29 matches, all of which were competitive. This resulted in 673 training sessions and 110 matches, for a total of 18 078 hours of training and 3249 match hours, respectively (table 1).

Pre-existing injuries

At the start of the tournament, 17 players with pre-existing injuries were selected. Of these, three did not return to play during the tournament, so we excluded them from further analyses (table 2).

Injury rates

Across both tournaments, 85 new injuries were recorded: 32 during training and 53 during matches. This corresponds to an overall injury rate of 4.0 injuries per 1000 hours (95% CI: 3.1 to 4.8). As expected, the injury rate for training was significantly lower than that for match play: 1.8 injuries per 1000 hours (95% CI: 1.2 to 2.4) vs 16.3 injuries per 1000 hours (95% CI: 11.9 to 20.7) (table 2).

Overall injury rates were similar across both tournaments, with 3.9 injuries per 1000 hours (95% CI: 2.9 to 4.9) for UEFA EURO and 4.2 per 1000 hours (95% CI: 2.6 to 5.7) for CONMEBOL Copa America. Match injury rates appeared slightly higher in CONMEBOL Copa America (18.7 vs 15.3 per 1000 hours), although the wide and overlapping CIs suggest no meaningful difference.

Muscle injuries

Muscle injuries were the most dominant injury type (n=50, 58%). There was a slight difference between UEFA EURO, where 35 muscle injuries comprised 63% of all injuries, and CONMEBOL Copa America with 15 muscle injuries that comprised 50% of all injuries (figure 4). However, again, a valid comparison cannot be made due to the low number of injuries. The majority of muscle injuries were to the hamstring (n=16, 32%), followed by the gastrocnemius (n=10, 20%) and the quadriceps (n=8, 16%).

Head injuries

There is a lot of discussion about head injuries in football and their subsequent impact, despite the number of head injuries being low in football. During the CONMEBOL Copa America, only one head injury was recorded. This concerned a concussion during a match after contact with another player, accompanied by a loss of consciousness.

During UEFA EURO, four head injuries were recorded, resulting in two fractures, one concussion and two lacerations. Both fractures, one of which also led to a concussion, were not caused by a foul but a result of an offensive header duel in which the player collided with an opponent’s shoulder. In both cases, the player was immediately substituted. The other head injuries occurred during sprinting in which contact with, respectively, an elbow and shoulder was made. In the latter case, it was due to an opponent’s foul, and the player was immediately substituted.

Discussion

This study comprehensively analyses injury patterns, rates and severities during the UEFA EURO 2024 and CONMEBOL Copa America 2024 tournaments, highlighting similarities and differences between these international men’s football competitions. Despite differing contexts, the injury rates in training and matches were similar, with match injuries consistently exceeding training injuries in both tournaments. Lower extremity injuries, particularly muscle injuries, dominated the injury profiles, underscoring persistent challenges in injury prevention for elite men’s football.

While the limited number of injuries restricts the ability to make definitive conclusions or identify statistically significant differences between tournaments, this study highlights the practicality and benefits of implementing standardised injury tracking across several major international events. Even without large effect sizes, data collected from multiple confederations can form the basis for combined analyses, facilitate early detection of trends and prepare the way for broader epidemiological comparisons in upcoming tournaments. The consistent methodology between UEFA and CONMEBOL provides a foundation for developing a more globally unified injury surveillance system.

Injury rates and patterns

The overall injury rates during UEFA EURO (4.0 per 1000 hours) and CONMEBOL Copa America (4.2 per 1000 hours) did not differ. They were lower than prior findings from comparable elite-level men’s football tournaments.5 15 Most recently, Serner et al reported 5.6 injuries per 1000 hours of exposure for the men’s 2022 FIFA World Cup in Qatar.15 Further, in the past, Waldén et al reported 10.1 injuries per 1000 hours of exposure during the 2004 UEFA Euro Championships in Portugal.5 Even though injury monitoring during a tournament only presents a snapshot injury spectrum heavily dependent on the tournament context,18 19 the lower injury rates found in our study are considered a positive outcome.

Match injury rates were higher in both competitions (17.1 and 18.7 per 1000 hours, respectively), reflecting match play’s increased intensity and physical demands. The high prevalence of lower extremity injuries, particularly muscle injuries, mirrors trends observed in previous studies and reaffirms the need for targeted prevention strategies, especially during match play.3 15

The comparative analysis of UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America reveals consistent injury patterns, despite potential differences in tournament contexts such as climate, player preparation and travel demand.6 Both tournaments reported nearly identical injury rates for training and matches. This consistency may reflect a convergence in elite-level medical and performance practices across confederations, with teams increasingly adhering to shared standards in injury prevention, load monitoring and recovery protocols. While we cannot draw definitive conclusions on tournament-specific injury risks, small contextual differences observed may warrant tailored prevention approaches in future research with larger datasets.18 19

Injury burden

The injury burden, measured as tournament days lost per 1000 hours, was substantial in both tournaments, though higher in UEFA EURO (30.0 tournament days lost per 1000 hours of exposure) than in CONMEBOL Copa America (18.3 tournament days lost per 1000 hours of exposure). Although there were minor differences in injury types between tournaments, this difference could be ascribed to the difference in duration of both tournaments. While the UEFA EURO lasted longer, the potential tournament time loss after injury was also higher.

When considering time-loss beyond the tournament period, the overall injury burden rose markedly, with UEFA EURO showing a higher burden (167.0 injury days per 1000 hours) than CONMEBOL Copa America (98.0 injury days per 1000 hours). While this approach aligns with IOC recommendations to record the date an athlete would have been ready for full training or competition,10 it carries important limitations. In the absence of direct monitoring during the off-season, this metric assumes that recovery continued until the first documented club appearance. This likely overestimates true time loss for some players who may have been fit earlier. Consequently, although the inclusion of post-tournament days lost increases comparability with studies following IOC guidance, residual bias remains and direct comparisons with other tournaments, particularly those held mid-season, should still be interpreted with caution.

Head injuries

UEFA EURO recorded four head injuries compared with one in CONMEBOL Copa America. Of these five head injuries, two were concussions. The head injury incidence observed in this study is broadly comparable to that reported for the men’s FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 (0.14 injuries per 1000 hours).15 Nonetheless, a direct comparison should be interpreted cautiously, given the small absolute numbers and differing tournament contexts.

Despite the low number of head injuries observed, their potential for long-term neurological consequences, as well as the growing attention from governing bodies, media and player welfare groups, justifies their continued prioritisation in surveillance and injury management frameworks.20

Pre-existing injuries

A finding in this study was the number of players selected for UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America despite having pre-existing injuries. A total of 14 players (12 in UEFA EURO and 2 in CONMEBOL Copa America) were included in their squads while already injured, with three never returning to play during the tournament. This highlights the complexity of player selection decisions, which may involve a mix of medical assessments, competitive strategy and team dynamics. While the inclusion of players recovering from injury is not unusual, it may influence squad depth and readiness in short-format tournaments.6 21 Furthermore, pressuring players to recover in time for major tournaments might increase the risk of reinjury or prolonged post-tournament recovery, affecting both club and national team performances in the following season.2224

Practical implications

Our findings highlight the necessity for improved injury prevention and management strategies at international tournaments. By pinpointing critical areas of concern, stakeholders—including team medical staff, coaches, tournament organisers and governing bodies—can adopt more effective measures to safeguard players and enhance performance.

Stronger pretournament injury management

The selection of injured players poses risks for individual players. With 17 players selected despite having pre-existing injuries and 3 never returning to play, stricter guidelines might be needed before final squad selection. Collaborative decision-making between club and national team medical staff can help ensure players are not rushed into competition prematurely, reducing reinjury risks and minimising long-term consequences.25

Tournament-specific injury prevention strategies

Although we did not directly measure contextual factors, previous research suggests that environmental conditions, match location, travel fatigue and playing style can influence injury risk in elite football.6 20 Such contextual elements should be considered when designing future multicentre surveillance efforts or tailoring prevention strategies to specific tournaments or regions. Addressing contextual elements can help stakeholders take a proactive approach to injury prevention, ensuring that players perform at their peak and maintain long-term health and career longevity. The continued harmonisation of injury surveillance across international tournaments will further support the development of evidence-based, globally applicable injury prevention strategies.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into injury patterns at UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America, several limitations must be acknowledged.

Reliance on team medical staff for injury reporting

As in most injury surveillance studies, data collection relied on team medical personnel, which introduces the potential for reporting bias and variability in injury classification. Differences in medical expertise, resources and recording practices among teams may have affected the accuracy and consistency of reported injuries. Despite employing standardised methodologies, variations in the diagnosis and classification of injuries could have influenced the findings.

Potential underreporting of minor injuries

Given the high-stakes nature of international tournaments, minor injuries may have been underreported if they did not lead to missed playing time. Players and medical staff might have prioritised short-term recovery strategies to keep athletes available, resulting in potential gaps in injury data, especially for minor muscle strains and overuse injuries. Future studies could combine player self-reporting with medical assessments to enhance injury tracking.

Differences in tournament structures and sample sizes

While the overall injury rates between UEFA EURO and CONMEBOL Copa America were similar, tournament format, duration and squad depth differences may have influenced injury trends. UEFA EURO featured more teams, a longer duration and more matches than CONMEBOL Copa America, potentially leading to higher injury burden. The smaller sample size of CONMEBOL Copa America also limits direct statistical comparisons between the two tournaments.

Environmental and contextual factors not fully accounted for

Although we identified possible contextual differences between tournaments, such as geography and travel schedules, these were not formally measured and thus not included in the analysis. Future research should consider integrating Global Positioning System (GPS) workload data, environmental conditions and tactical metrics to better contextualise injury mechanisms.

Limited follow-up on long-term injury consequences

This study focused on injuries sustained during the tournaments and did not track long-term recovery outcomes once players returned to their clubs. While we documented players’ return to play after the tournament, further research is needed to examine the post-tournament impact of injuries on club performance, reinjury risk and overall player health.

Conclusions

The parallel injury surveillance studies conducted during UEFA EURO 2024 and CONMEBOL Copa America 2024 highlight shared injury patterns and risks across elite men’s international tournaments. While injury rates were consistent, the persistent burden of match-related injuries and lower extremity muscle strains requires ongoing attention. By leveraging these findings, football stakeholders can implement tailored interventions to enhance player safety and optimise performance in future tournaments.
