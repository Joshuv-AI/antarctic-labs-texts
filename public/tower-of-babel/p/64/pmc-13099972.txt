Quantitative protein analysis and glycan profiling of umbilical cord plasma and corresponding maternal plasma samples
Authors: Eliška Benešová, Marco René Bladergroen, Simone Nicolardi, Veronika Vidová, Petr Janků, Jana Klánová, Zdeněk Spáčil, Manfred Wuhrer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein glycosylation is an essential post-translational modification boosting proteomic diversity. The major groups of human plasma glycoproteins are immunoglobulins (Igs) and acute-phase proteins (APPs). Changes in plasma Ig and APP levels and their glycosylation have been previously associated with inflammatory diseases, several types of cancer, pregnancy, or aging. In this study, we present a novel approach to investigate the neonatal and maternal plasma at the end of pregnancy using a combination of targeted quantitative proteomics and total plasma N-glycome profiling. We present a new SRM-based multiplex assay to quantify 19 APPs and 7 Igs. We applied this assay to 98 samples of umbilical cord plasma and corresponding maternal plasma samples and observed significantly lower levels of all analyzed proteins in neonates, apart from the glycoprotein alpha-2-macroglobulin. Unlike previous methods, our SRM-MS assay allows us to quantify dozens of proteins simultaneously and to distinguish protein variants. We compared maternal and fetal total plasma-released N-glycomes in the same set of samples. We quantified 66 individual glycans, which were used to calculate 73 glycosylation traits. In neonates, the major N-glycans were di-antennary complex-type with core fucosylation, and the production of higher, branched, sialylated N-glycans was limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42080-3.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biomarkers

Introduction

Glycosylation is the most prevalent and intricate post-translational modification observed in human proteins. Approximately 70% of all proteins in the human body undergo glycosylation.1 Glycoproteins are usually found in cell membranes, extracellular environments, or intracellular organelles.2 Glycosylation affects protein physical properties and plays a role in many key biological processes such as protein stability and folding, cell–cell interactions, immune responses, or receptor binding and activation.3,4 Differential glycosylation has been previously associated with autoimmune diseases, inflammatory diseases, pregnancy, aging, Alzheimer’s disease, and several types of cancer.1,3,5,6

Significant groups of plasma glycoproteins are immunoglobulins (Igs) and acute phase proteins (APPs).4,7 Igs are glycoproteins composed of two light and two heavy chains.8 Differences in amino acid sequences in the constant region of the heavy chain divide Igs into five classes – IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, and IgD. Igs are critical in the immune system and are naturally produced in response to invading pathogens.9 APPs are plasma proteins whose concentration changes by at least 25% during an inflammatory response.10 They usually reach the maximum level within 24 to 48 h after initiating the inflammatory response.11 APPs are important mediators synthesized by hepatocytes in response to IL-6 and other inflammatory cytokines during acute and chronic inflammation.12,13 Precise quantification of APPs is essential to differentiate between harmful and beneficial immune responses.14 Typically, serum levels of APPs are quantified using immunoanalytical methods. However, these methods based on antigen–antibody interaction must be rigorously evaluated for potential cross-reactivity to ensure immunoassay specificity and accurate quantification.15,16 On the other hand, mass spectrometry protein quantification provides high quantitative accuracy, reproducibility, selectivity, sensitivity, and multiplexing capacity.17,18.

Changes in levels of APPs and Igs during pregnancy were extensively studied, particularly for alpha-1-antitrypsin (A1AT), alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (A1AG), haptoglobin (HP), C-reactive protein (CRP), and immunoglobulins IgA, IgG, and IgM. A1AT levels are elevated in pregnancy.19,20 Increased levels of A1AT were associated with its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory function during pregnancy.21 Conversely, low A1AT levels during pregnancy were associated with premature labor activation.22

A decrease in A1AG level was observed during all trimesters of pregnancy.23 Moreover, it has been known for decades that A1AG glycosylation is changing with pregnancy as well.24–26 Accordingly, Reiding et al. detected changes in total plasma protein N-glycosylation throughout the pregnancy.27 They performed MALDI-TOF–MS analysis of total serum protein N-glycosylation and observed a decrease in bisection, an increase in glycan branching, and an increase of α2,3-linked sialylation.27 Ruhaak et al. focused on the overall longitudinal N-glycosylation profiles in the blood plasma of 32 healthy pregnant women during and after their pregnancy and on the protein-specific glycosylation profiles of IgG, IgA, and A1AT.28 Altered glycosylation patterns were observed during pregnancy (average results from the first, second, and third trimester) compared to 6 months after childbirth. Levels of oligosialylated structures increase during pregnancy in contrast to decreased levels of monosialylated di-antennary structures. Significantly different protein-specific N-glycosylation was observed, specifically an increase of tri-antennary glycans in A1AT during pregnancy and a decrease in di-galactosylated di-antennary glycans in IgG after delivery.28

Neonates have a naïve and immature immune system, which is partially compensated by intrapartum transfer of IgG across the placenta.9 Transplacental IgG transport is enabled by neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), expressed mainly in the syncytiotrophoblasts in the placenta.29 Neonatal IgG levels typically correlate with maternal levels. However, the amount of transmitted IgG depends on the amount of FcRn receptors, and thus, the IgG binding to the receptor may be saturated.30 Other classes of Igs and APPs are not transported across the placenta.30,31 However, the fetal liver can produce APPs early in the second trimester.32.

Previous studies focused on comparing total N-glycome and IgG glycosylation profiles between maternal and umbilical cord blood. Higher levels of IgG galactosylation were observed in umbilical cord blood compared to maternal plasma.33,34 Jansen et al. confirmed this difference in IgG glycosylation and focused on the total plasma N-glycome of paired maternal plasma (MP) and umbilical cord plasma (UCP), which revealed more pronounced differences.35 Two tri-antennary tri-sialylated glycans showed 4 to fivefold lower levels in UCP glycome than MP glycome. These glycans are generally found on acute-phase proteins, especially A1AT and A1AG.4

In this study, we used proteomic and glycomic profiling to get a better understanding of the biological and physiological processes at the end of pregnancy and in neonates. We developed and validated a multiplex assay based on ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) with selected reaction monitoring (SRM) for quantification of 19 APPs and 7 Igs in plasma samples. We used this assay to compare APP levels between 98 samples of UCP and corresponding samples of MP. For the same sample set, we compared maternal and fetal total plasma released N-glycomes (TPNG) (Fig. 1).

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Formic acid (FA) for mass spectrometry (cat. #94,318), ammonium bicarbonate BioUltra ≥ 99.5% (cat. #09,830), iodoacetamide (IAA) ≥ 99.0% (cat. #I6125), 1- hydroxybenzotriazole monohydrate (HOBt) ≥ 97.0% (cat. #54,802), and superDHB ≥ 99.0% (cat. #50,862) were all purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). 1,4-Dithiothreitol (DTT) ≥ 99.0% (cat. #6908) was obtained from Carl Roth (Karlsruhe, Germany). Nonidet P-40 (NP-40, cat. #M158-100ML) was purchased from VWR (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). 50% Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, cat. #71,686) and 85% phosphoric acid (cat. #79,606) were obtained from Fluka (Buchs, Switzerland). 1-Ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)-propyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, cat. #24,810) was obtained from Fluorochem (Hadfield, UK). Recombinant peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase-F, cat. #11,365,177,001) was obtained from Roche Diagnostics (Mannheim, Germany), and mass spectrometry grade trypsin Gold (cat. #V5280) was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI). LC–MS grade acetonitrile (ACN, cat. #00,136,878) was purchased from Biosolve (Valkenswaard, The Netherlands). Analytical grade ethanol (cat. #1.00983) and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, cat. #1,081,780,050) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Deionized water was produced using a Millipore Simplicity 185 ultrapure water system (Merck Millipore Corp. Billerica, MA). Stable isotopically labeled (SIL) crude peptide standards (SpikeTides_L) with C-terminal arginine (R*, 13C6H14O215N4) or lysine (K*, 13C6H14O215N2) were custom-ordered from JPT Technologies (Berlin, Germany). Sequences of SIL peptides, molecular weights, and quantifier transitions are listed in Supplementary Table S-1.

Plasma samples

Study samples and data were obtained from the Central European Longitudinal Studies of Parents and Children: The Next Generation (CELSPAC: TNG). The CELSPAC: TNG study is designed as a new prospective birth cohort which will follow up 3000 children from their prenatal period to adolescence with the aim of assessing environmental factors potentially affecting children’s health. The CELSPAC: TNG study is based on previous efforts of Masaryk University, especially the Czech part of the WHO-initiated European Longitudinal Study on Pregnancy and Childhood (ELSPAC).36

The CELSPAC: TNG study was approved by the Multicentre and Local Ethical Committee of University Hospital Brno, the Czech Republic (No. 20140409-01, date 2014/04/09) and performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Participating mothers signed the informed consent form on behalf of themselves and their newborn child. All data and samples are available for secondary use in further research and development purposes.

In total, 98 samples of UCP and 98 corresponding samples of MP were used in this study. The cohort study included only singleton pregnancies. All samples were collected in EDTA tubes. MP samples were collected between the 38th and 41st week of pregnancy, and UCP samples were collected immediately after delivery. All plasma samples were immediately placed at −80 °C and stored until sample processing and analysis. The newborn study subjects were female (n = 46) and male (n = 52) neonates. The gestation age [week + days] ranged from 38 + 2 to 41 + 3. The average birth weight was 3510 g, ranging from 2470 to 4560 g. Ten neonates were delivered by Cesarean section. Characteristics of mothers and neonates, including their gestational age, delivery mode, birth weight, birth length, and Apgar score, are shown in Supplementary Table S-2.

In-solution tryptic proteolysis

In-solution tryptic proteolysis was performed as described previously37 with minor modifications. The plasma sample was diluted 50-fold using 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (ABB), and 30 µl of the diluted plasma sample was transferred into a 96-well plate. The sample was reduced (3 µl of 200 mM DTT, 95 °C, 10 min), then cooled down to ambient temperature, and cysteine residues were alkylated (3 µl of 400 mM IAA, ambient temperature, 30 min in the dark). Trypsin was added to the sample in a 1:40 ratio (w/w of the enzyme to total protein content) for enzymatic proteolysis (37 °C, 16 h). The proteolysis was quenched by adding 200 µl of 2% FA in water (pH < 3). Working solutions containing the mixture of SIL peptide internal standards were added (2.5 µl), and the final concentration of each SIL peptide in the sample was 150 nM. Tryptic peptides were desalted using a reverse-phase solid phase extraction (SPE) cartridge in a 96-well plate format (Oasis PRIME HLB 30 mg, Waters, Milford, MA). The sample was loaded onto the cartridge, washed with 300 µl water with 2% FA, pH < 3, and eluted two times with 200 µl of 50% ACN in water with 2% FA, pH < 3. The extract was dried in a vacuum concentrator centrifuge (Savant SPD121 P SpeedVac, Thermo Fisher). The sample was reconstituted in 25 µl of 5% ACN in water with 0.1% FA before UHPLC-SRM analysis.

Tryptic peptide analysis by liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry

Tryptic peptide analysis was performed as described previously14,37 with a few modifications. Samples were analyzed using the UHPLC system (Infinity™ 1260 Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and a reversed-phase analytical column (C18 Peptide CSH; 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm i.d. × 100 mm; cat. #186,006,937; Waters; Milford, MA). The sample injection volume was 3 µl. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.3 ml/min with mobile phases A (0.1% FA in water) and B (0.1% FA in 95% ACN) in the linear gradient elution mode (0–25 min) with a wash step (25.30–30 min) and re-equilibration step (31–35 min). The gradient program was as follows: 0.0 min 5% B; 25 min 30% B; 25.3 min 95% B; 30 min 95% B; 31 min 5% B; 35 min 5% B. Column temperature was 40 °C and autosampler temperature was 5 °C. SRM protein assays were performed using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent 6495B, Agilent Technologies, USA) with a standard-flow Jet Stream electrospray ionization (ESI) source. ESI parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 3.5 kV, nozzle voltage 800 V, gas flow rate 20 l/min at 220 °C, sheath gas pressure 25 PSI and flow rate 10 l/min at 400 °C. Positively charged ions were detected in dynamic SRM mode with 2 min retention time window centered on the peptide experimental retention time. All tryptic peptides and SIL peptide internal standards were identified using 3 SRM transitions (qualifiers) and quantified using a single quantifier SRM transition. In total, 334 transitions were monitored (Supplementary Table S-3) with a total cycle time of 500 ms. At least 20 datapoints per peak were used for quantification.

Matrix-matched peptide calibration curves to assess quantitative performance

Protein assays were validated using matrix-matched calibration curves for each SIL peptide standard to determine the linearity range, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ). A pool of plasma samples was processed as described in previous section In-solution tryptic proteolysis. A mixture of SIL peptides at different concentrations was added to a sample prior to SPE desalting, and UHPLC-SRM analysis was performed. The dilution series of SIL peptides consisted of 7–11 calibration levels and covered the concentration range expected in individual plasma samples. Each calibration level was measured at 3–6 technical replicates.

Enzymatic N-glycan release

Samples were processed according to standard protocols.27,38,39 Briefly, 6 µl of plasma was added to a well containing 12 µl of 2% SDS and incubated for 10 min at 60 °C. After cooling down to room temperature, 12.6 µl of release mixture (6 µl of 4% NP-40, 6 µl of 100 mM phosphoric acid in 5 × PBS, and 0.6 µl of PNGase-F) was added, and plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C.

Automated preparation of released N-glycans

Linkage-specific sialic acid derivatization, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) SPE purification, and MALDI-target spotting were performed on an automated system as described previously.38

For the derivatization of sialic acids, 2 µl of PNGase-F released plasma was mixed with 40 µl of esterification reagent (250 mM EDC and 250 mM HOBt in ethanol) followed by incubation for 60 min at 37 °C. Next, 40 µl of ACN was added, and plates were incubated for 10 min to facilitate precipitation.38

Subsequently, samples were purified using in-house assembled cotton HILIC microtips. The microtips were washed three times with 40 µl of MilliQ water and then three times with 40 µl of 85% ACN. Samples were loaded by slowly pipetting 20 times up and down and washed three times with 40 µl of 85% ACN with 1% TFA and three times with 40 µl of 85% ACN. Samples were eluted with 20 µl of MilliQ water by pipetting five times up and down. Next, 7 µl of the matrix (2.5 mg/ml superDHB in 50% ACN with 0.1 mM NaOH) and 7 µl of the purified sample were premixed, and 2 µl of the mixture was spotted onto an anchorchip-800 MALDI target plate. Spots were allowed to dry by air and measured using MALDI-FTICR-MS.38 The method was optimized to promote uniform sodium adduct formation, via addition of sodium to the MALDI matrix.

TPNG analysis by MALDI-FTICR-MS

MALDI-FTICR-MS spectra were recorded as previously described.39,40 Briefly, the measurements were performed on a Bruker 15 T solariX XR FTICR mass spectrometer equipped with a CombiSource and a ParaCell (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The system was operated via ftmsControl (version 2.1.0). Spectra were recorded from m/z 1011.86 to 5000.00, and each spectrum consisted of ten scans with 200 laser shots at each raster. A Bruker Smartbeam-II Laser System was used for irradiation at a frequency of 500 Hz using the “medium” predefined shot pattern. Bruker Daltonics DataAnalysis software (version 5.0) was used for data visualization and further processing.39,40

Data processing and statistical analysis

In proteomic LC–MS data processing, Skyline software (Version 4.2.0.19009 MacCoss Lab, Uni of Washington, WA) and commercial software (MassHunter Quantitative Analysis software, Agilent Technologies, CA) were used to integrate SRM signals to report peak areas of tryptic peptides. All data was processed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office Professional Plus, 2013).

Glycomic MALDI-FTICR-MS spectra were manually checked and converted into text format (“.xy”) files using Bruker Daltonics DataAnalysis (Version 5.0). The calibration of exported files and targeted extraction of the detected glycan compositions was performed by software MassyTools (Version 1.0.2 build 200129a).41 The extraction window was dependent on the analyte’s m/z according to the formula 0.00003 * "m/z"—0.0269. The formula was obtained from the linear trendline derived from manual peak width measurements (full width at half maximum).39 Glycan compositions were built using the standard MassyTools building blocks: hexose (H), N-acetylhexosamine (N), fucose (F), ethyl esterified N-acetylneuraminic acid (E), and lactonized N-acetylneuraminic acid (L). The lists of dummy analytes, internal calibrants, and extracted glycan compositions are shown in Supplementary Tables S-4, and S-5.

Four parameters calculated by MassyTools were used for sample curation: “total intensity”, “fraction of spectrum in analytes”, “fraction of analyte with signal-to-noise above nine”, and “fraction of analyte area minus background area with signal-to-noise above nine”. Spectra were excluded if at least one of the parameters was below the average minus three times the standard deviation. In this way, eight pairs of UCP and MP were excluded.

Analyte curation was performed using three quality criteria: signal-to-noise of the analyte had to be above 9, the quality score for the comparison of the theoretical and observed isotopic envelope of the analyte had to be lower than 0.2, and the PPM error of the analyte had to be lower than 10. Analyte curation was performed separately for UCP and MP, and glycoforms were included when present in at least 50% of one group. These criteria excluded all dummy analytes. In total, 66 glycoforms were included in subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table S-5). In this study, we did not perform MS/MS analysis but relied on previous structural analysis of released N-glycans for glycoform assignment.39,42–44 Calculation of glycosylation traits was performed in R-studio using an in-house written script. The description of glycosylation traits and calculations are shown in Supplementary Table S-6 and S-7, respectively.

GraphPad PRISM software (Version 9.5.1 (733), Boston, MA) and Rstudio (Version 1.3.1073)45 were used for statistical analysis and visualization of proteomics and glycomics data. Protein levels, individual glycans, and glycosylation traits were statistically tested pairwise using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Bonferroni multiple testing correction was performed, and the significance threshold was corrected separately for proteomics and glycomics data. For 56 peptides, the significance threshold of α = 0.00089 was used. For 66 individual glycans together with 73 glycosylation traits, the significance threshold of α = 0.00036 was used.

Results and discussion

Comparison of Ig levels in UCP and MP

This study aimed to develop and validate an SRM-based multiplex assay for relative quantification of 19 APPs and 7 Igs. Analyzed proteins with their abbreviations and UniProt entry names are listed in Table 1. Total IgA (IgA1 + 2) was analyzed using two peptides common for both IgA subclasses and total IgG (IgG1 + 2 + 3 + 4) using one peptide common for all four IgG subclasses. We used this newly developed assay to compare selected glycoprotein levels in 98 UCP samples and corresponding MP samples revealing profound differences in protein concentrations between MP and UCP.

The neonatal immune system is functionally immature and mainly relies on protection from maternal Igs, specifically on IgG, which is transferred to the fetus via the placenta from the 13th week of gestation, and on IgA received from the breast milk after birth.46–49 Levels of maternal IgG decrease during pregnancy to 7.80 g/l (34–38 weeks), probably because of the active transfer of IgG to the fetus.19,35 According to previously published studies, neonatal IgG levels should exceed maternal IgG levels by 20–30% at full term.30,50 However, the amount of transmitted IgG depends on the amount of FcRn receptors, and the FcRn receptors may be saturated.30 Moreover, the neonatal and maternal IgG levels are typically correlated; thus, the neonatal IgG concentration is affected by maternal IgG concentration.30,51 Previous studies suggested that IgG levels in UCP may be lower than in MP if the total maternal IgG level reaches 15 g/l.52–55 In our study, we observed higher levels in MP for IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, and total IgG1 + 2 + 3 + 4 detected by common peptide (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8).

Comparison of protein levels in MP and UCP for high-abundance proteins (A) and low-abundance proteins (B).

Even though IgG subclasses have more than 90% of identical amino acid sequences, each subclass plays a diverse role in the immune system.56 IgG1 is primarily induced in response to soluble protein antigens and membrane proteins, typically accompanied with lower levels of IgG3 and IgG4.56 IgG2 is induced in response to bacterial capsular polysaccharide antigens.8,56,57 IgG3 antibodies are generally associated with the response to protein antigens,8 and IgG4 is often induced in response to allergens.56 Several previously published articles and reviews reported that the transplacental transfer of IgG subclasses occurs preferentially for IgG1, followed by IgG4, IgG3, and IgG2.30,55,58,59 However, other published studies showed different transfer hierarchy.60–62 Varied results have been attributed to differences in study populations, e.g., gestation age, birth weight, ethnicity, parity, or maternal antigen exposure.58,63 In our study, we observed that the percentage composition of IgG subclasses from total IgG is identical for most mother-neonate pairs (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S-9). Other classes of Igs are not transferred via the placenta. IgA is obtained via breastfeeding after birth. In our study, the median concentration of IgA1 in UCP was 0.5 nM, and levels of IgA2 were under LOQ. Likewise, levels of IgM in UCP were under LOQ (Supplementary Figure S-2 and Supplementary Table S-8).

Comparison of APP levels in UCP and MP

Mild inflammatory changes may occur during pregnancy, including increased levels of APPs. On the contrary, the synthesis of APPs in neonates may be limited. In our study, levels of proteins haptoglobin (HP), ceruloplasmin (CP), apolipoprotein B-100 (ApoB), apolipoprotein D (ApoD), and apolipoprotein F (ApoF) in UCP were under LOQ. On the other hand, the synthesis of glycoprotein alpha-2-macroglobulin (A2M) was significantly higher in UCP than in MP, with median concentration of 281 nM and 241 nM, respectively (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8). A2M levels are higher in newborns compared to mothers, and elevated levels of A2M increase the capacity of cord plasma to inhibit thrombin and provide protection from thrombosis.64 In contrast, levels of antithrombin-III (ATIII), an essential thrombin inhibitor in adult plasma, are decreased in UCP compared to MP.64 Accordingly, we found that levels of ATIII in UCP (4 nM) were lower than in MP (11 nM) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8). Elevated levels of A2M appear to compensate for decreased ATIII levels, and A2M plays an essential role in thrombosis protection in neonates.64–66

Levels of A1AT during pregnancy were extensively studied previously and reported to be elevated compared to the average adult range.19,20,67 Increased levels of A1AT were associated with its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory function during pregnancy.21 On the other hand, the reference range of A1AT levels in neonates has not been determined yet, and there are discrepancies in the literature when comparing adult and neonatal levels.68,69 In our study, levels of A1AT were lower in UCP compared to MP (145 nM and 258 nM, respectively) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8). However, it is essential to establish the reference plasma range for neonates and children because decreased plasma levels of A1AT may be caused by a rare inherited disorder called A1AT deficiency. A1AT deficiency is the most common genetic cause of liver disease in children.70 The disorder is caused by mutation in the SERPINA1 gene. Mutation leads to misfolding of A1AT protein and its aggregation in endoplasmic reticulum of hepatocytes resulting in proteotoxic stress and liver damage.71 Moreover, A1AT deficiency is a predisposition to severe lung disease, such as emphysema or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.72 Lung damage is caused by decreased systemic levels of A1AT and insufficient inhibition of neutrophil elastase.70,71 Symptoms of liver and lung damage may appear in childhood; thus, the early diagnosis of A1AT deficiency is essential.70 Currently, the disorder remains heavily underdiagnosed with an average diagnostic delay of 5–10 years, which could negatively affect the patient’s prognosis.73

Levels of A1AG are decreased during all trimesters of pregnancy.23 In line with our study, neonatal levels were reported to be lower than adult levels.74,75 However, our protein assay could distinguish two variants of A1AG: A1AG1 and A1AG2. We observed lower levels of both A1AG1 and A1AG2 in UCP (11 nM and 6 nM, respectively) compared to MP (42 nM and 33 nM, respectively) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8). Anell-Olofsson et al. observed significantly increased levels of A1AG in neonates born vaginally compared to neonates delivered by Caesarean Sect. 74 In our study, we did not observe statistically different results according to the mode of delivery. However, the group of neonates delivered by Caesarean section was small (n = 10).

Even though the physiological role of A1AG remains unclear, it is known that A1AG is involved in the plasma binding and transport of drugs, such as amide local anesthetics.76,77 Lower levels of A1AG lead to reduced anesthetics binding, resulting in higher plasma concentration of local anesthetics, which could increase the risk of local anesthetics systemic toxicity.74 Thus, it is essential to precisely determine the reference ranges of A1AG concentration in various patient categories, including newborns and children.

According to previous studies, hemopexin (HPX) levels in newborns reach approximately 20% of typical adult values (between 0.4 and 1.5 g/l) and start rising immediately after birth.78 In our study, we observed a more considerable difference in HPX levels in UCP (15 nM) compared to MP (144 nM). However, a previous comparison was performed with average adult value and did not consider the possibility of altered concentration during pregnancy. Similarly, for vitronectin (VTN), the study by Hogasen et al. reported the median value of VTN in cord blood at 86% of the adult value (0.24–0.53 g/l).79,80 In our study, we observed significantly lower levels in UCP (9 nM) compared to MP (76 nM) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S-2, Supplementary Table S-8). VTN is involved in the blood coagulation, cell attachment, neural differentiation, neurogenesis, or regulation of axon size.81

Serotransferrin (TF) is a metal binding protein. It has a high affinity to bind ferric iron (Fe3+) and regulates levels of iron in biological fluids.4,82 Levels of TF in cord serum were previously described as decreased compared to the average adult range.83 In our study, the median concentration of TF in UCP was 167 nM, and in MP 472 nM (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8).

TF, fibrinogen (FG), and CP levels were previously reported to be elevated during pregnancy compared to the average adult level.4,67,84,85 FG is a hexamer composed of two sets of three different polypeptide chains – α, β, and γ chains linked together by 29 disulfide bonds.86 In contrast to previous studies, we could distinguish between fibrinogen alpha chain (FGA), fibrinogen beta chain (FGB), and fibrinogen gamma chain (FGG). All different polypeptide chains were significantly lower in UCP than in MP. The median concentration of FGA in UCP was 131 nM and in MP 524 nM. For FGB, the median concentration in UCP was 69 nM, and in MP 310 nM. Finally, the median concentration of FGG in UCP was 88 nM and in MP 495 nM (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8). FG is essential in blood clotting, hemostasis, and wound healing.87 In neonates and small infants, the hemostatic system is immature, and FG levels are decreased.88 This leads to an increased risk of bleeding and blood transfusion requirements during pediatric surgeries.89 Moreover, FG quantitative results vary depending on the assay used for quantification.88 Thus, precise age-related reference values for FG are necessary.

Alpha-2-HS-glycoprotein (AHSG) is also called fetuin-A, alpha-2-Z-globulin, or ba-alpha-2-glycoprotein. AHSG could form soluble complexes with calcium and phosphate, thereby inhibiting calcification outside of bone and preventing tissue calcification.90,91 According to a study by Hausler et al., the highest levels of AHSG are in preterm neonates (23rd to 30th week of gestation).90 After that, the values decrease and remain stable till adolescence. In our study, median levels of AHSG in UCP were significantly lower than in MP (110 nM and 149 nM, respectively) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8).

Beta-2-glycoprotein 1 (ApoH) is the major antigen of antiphospholipid antibodies and plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of antiphospholipid syndrome, an autoimmune disease that may lead to thrombosis and pregnancy-related morbidity.92,93 Levels of ApoH increase with age and are reduced during pregnancy.4,94 Previously, significantly reduced levels were shown in cord plasma compared to adult plasma.95 Our study confirmed this result, and we observed a median concentration of 22 nM in UCP and 34 nM in MP (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S-2, and Supplementary Table S-8).

To conclude, differences in plasma protein levels between adults and neonates or changes in mothers during pregnancy are already known for most of the plasma proteins. However, previous studies typically reported only single protein quantification using immunoanalytical methods, such as immunoturbidimetric assays,74 immunoblotting,66 immunonephelometric assays,19,78 or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).53,69,90 These methods are based on antigen–antibody interaction and must be carefully evaluated for potential cross-reactivity which could lead to distorted or overestimated quantitative results.15,16,96 Moreover, immunoanalytical methods have limited multiplexing capacity.97 On the other hand, MS-based protein quantification provides high sensitivity, reproducibility, quantitative accuracy, and multiplexing capacity.17,18,98 Even though studies focusing on protein profiling in plasma,99–102 serum,103,104 or dried blood spots14,105 were published, we are the first study using targeted SRM-MS methodology for simultaneous quantifications of dozens of proteins for comparison between maternal and umbilical cord plasma. Moreover, our method allows us to distinguish protein variants, such as A1AG1 and A1AG2 or FGA, FGB, and FGG.

Comparison of N-glycan profiles and glycosylation traits in UCP and MP

Even though the analysis of total plasma released N-glycans does not provide information about site- and protein-specificity, it has several advantages over glycoproteomic methods. First of all, analysis of released glycans provides the most in-depth structural characterization of glycan species.43 Glycomic methods are robust, repeatable, sensitive, and high-throughput which makes glycomics optimal for large-scale clinical studies.39 Moreover, glycomic sample processing may be automated and enables linkage-specific sialic acid derivatization to distinguish α2,3-linked and α2,6-linked sialic acid residues. Finally, analysis of released N-glycans using MALDI-MS enables relative quantification.

On the other hand, glycopeptide analysis preserves information about site- and protein-specificity, site occupancy and microheterogeneity. However, the complexity of analytes mixture is highly increased, and thus glycoproteomic approach requires high sensitivity and resolution analytical techniques and often also additional steps in sample preparation, for example affinity enrichment of the glycoprotein of interest to decrease the complexity of the sample.6,43 Moreover, glycopeptides exhibit lower ionization efficiency compared to non-glycopeptides in ESI–MS which also makes their analysis challenging.106 High-throughput glycopeptide profiling methods are often limited to enriched glycoproteins, for example IgG.

To conclude, the analysis of released glycans can provide a broad picture of overall plasma glycosylation, which can be later used for design of site-specific glycopeptide assays. Combining both glycomic and glycoproteomic approaches can provide the most complete view of glycosylation.

In this study, 66 individual glycans were quantified from the TPNG of UCP and MP. The individual glycans were used for the calculation of 73 glycosylation traits. To investigate further the difference in the glycomic profiles between UCP and MP, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using data on both individual glycans and glycosylation traits. In the PCA score plot, groups of UCP and MP were clearly distinguished (Supplementary Figure S-3). The PCA loadings plot was used to decipher which individual glycans and glycosylation traits caused the separation in the PCA.

We observed a higher relative intensity of smaller, di-antennary, fucosylated glycans in UCP samples compared to MP samples. The most significant fold change was observed for glycans H5N4F1, H4N4F1, H4N5F1, H5N5F1, H5N4, and H5N4F1E1 (Fig. 4A, Table 2, and Supplementary Table S-5) as is reflected in the corresponding glycosylation traits. General traits CA1 (complex mono-antennary glycans) and CA2 (complex di-antennary glycans) were higher in UCP (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S-6). In UCP, we observed higher relative intensity for fucosylation-associated traits such as CF (complex fucosylated glycans), A2F (fucosylated di-antennary glycans), A3F (fucosylated tri-antennary glycans), and A4F (fucosylated tetra-antennary glycans, Fig. 5, Supplementary Figure S-4 and Supplementary Table S-6). However, the relative abundance of fucosylation of sialylated di-antennary glycans (A2SF) was smaller in UCP. Interestingly, this trend was observed only for α2,6-linkage (trait A2EF), not for α2,3-linkage (trait A2LF, Supplementary Figure S-4 and Supplementary Table S-6).

It has been previously reported that di-antennary complex-type glycans with core fucosylation, such as H4N4F1, H5N4F1, or H5N4F1S1, for the majority originate from the Fc portion of IgG. IgGs carry approximately 30 different N-glycans, of which 90% are core-fucosylated, about 10% bear a bisecting GlcNAc, and 10–15% have 1–2 terminal sialic acids.107 The sialylation on IgG is almost exclusively α2,6-linked.108 The IgG glycosylation profile is modulated during life.109 Glycans attached to the IgG Fc portion heavily influence IgGs´ dynamics and modulate biological activity because they can influence interaction with complement and cellular receptors.110 The core fucosylation is a critical determinant of antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity.111 IgG represents the only class of glycoproteins actively transported across the placenta to the fetus utilizing the FcRn receptor found on the placenta.35,112 It has been suggested that newborn IgG is exclusively maternal-derived, and the newborn starts to produce its own IgG in the first weeks after birth.110,113 In our study, N-glycans typical for IgG were the major N-glycans in the TPNG profile of newborns. However, we analyzed N-glycans released from the complex plasma samples, so these N-glycans could also originate from proteins other than IgG. A previous study by Jansen et al. focused on comparing IgG-released N-glycan and TPNG profiles in UCP and MP.35 In TPNG profiles, they observed increased relative intensities of di-antennary and fucosylated glycans, which is in line with our study. With regard to the IgG-related glycans H5N4, H5N4F1, and H4N5F1, those were increased in UCP in IgG-released glycans as well as in TPNG, both in the previous study35 and in our current study. There are, however, also some discordant results: Jansen et al. observed decreased relative intensities in glycans H3N5F1, H3N4F1, and H4N4F1E1 in the IgG-released N-glycan profile. In the TPNG profile, they observed significant differences only for glycan H3N5F1.35 We observed significantly higher relative intensities in UCP for all these glycans (H3N5F1, H3N4F1, and H4N4F1E1) in TPNG. Of note, the relative intensities of these glycans were under 1%. Nevertheless, these IgG-specific results compared to the TPNG results could suggest that the di-antennary, fucosylated glycans, which are the significant glycans in TPNG of UCP, are not attached only to IgG but are also released from other plasma proteins. Site-specific analysis of other plasma proteins, not only IgG, would be necessary to confirm this hypothesis.

On the other hand, branched, heavily sialylated glycans were increased in MP compared to UCP. The biggest fold change was observed for glycans H6N5L3, H7N6E1L3, H6N5E2L1, H6N5E1L2, H7N6E2L2, and H6N5F1E3 (Fig. 4B, Table 2, and Supplementary Table S-5). The glycosylation traits analysis confirmed this observation. General glycosylation traits CA3 (complex tri-antennary glycans) and CA4 (complex tetra-antennary glycans) were higher in MP compared to UCP (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S-6). In MP, we observed higher relative intensities for all complex sialylated glycans (CS) and separated types of sialic acid linkages. Both complex α2,6-sialylated glycans (CE) and complex α2,3-sialylated glycans (CL) had higher relative intensities in MP (Fig. 5). Sialylation of di-antennary (A2S), tri-antennary (A3S), and tetra-antennary glycans (A4S) were also higher in MP compared to UCP. Interestingly, this trend was not observed for α2,6-sialylation of tri-antennary glycans (A3E) and α2,6-sialylation of tetra-antennary glycans (A4E, Supplementary Figure S-5 and Supplementary Table S-6). For all bisection traits, higher relative intensities were observed in MP compared to UCP (Supplementary Figure S-6, Supplementary Table S-6).

Tri- and tetra-antennary sialylated glycans are generally attached to acute-phase proteins.114 Previous studies showed increased di-antennary and tri-antennary sialylated glycans during pregnancy.28,115 Jansen et al. analyzed released N-glycome of plasma samples collected in pregnancy’s first, second, and third trimesters and 6, 12, and 26 + weeks post-pregnancy.115 During the pregnancy, they observed an increase in relative abundance for tri-antennary glycans H6N5E1L2, H6N5E2L1, H6N5F1E2L1, and H6N5F2E2L1. After delivery, levels of these glycans slowly decreased. The difference between the third trimester and six weeks post-pregnancy was insignificant for any of these glycans. However, later postpartum (26 + weeks), they observed a significant decrease for glycans H6N5E1L2, H6N5E2L1, H6N5F1E2L1, and H6N5F2E2L1. They suggested that this trend could be caused by the changes in the acute-phase protein levels during pregnancy and after delivery. For example, A1AT, HP, FG, TF, or CP levels increase during pregnancy.4,19,67,84,85 On the other hand, IgG, A1AG, or ApoH levels are reduced.4,19,23,35,94 Furthermore, levels of acute-phase proteins are significantly lower in newborns compared to mothers, as described in section Comparison of APP levels in UCP and MP and shown in Fig. 2. Differences in quantitative proteomics between mothers and neonates probably also contribute to the difference between their N-glycan profiles.

In the group of neonates, the differences in N-glycans profiles may also be gender-based. However, in our study, we did not observed and grouping in PCA analysis based on gender of neonates (Supplementary Figure S-7).

Correlations between glycan and protein levels for MP

We next explored the correlations between glycans specific protein levels. The glycans H3N4F1 and H4N4F1 as well as the bisected versions thereof, H3N5F1 and H4N5F1, all positively correlated with IgG levels (Supplementary Figure S-8), in line with the fact that these glycans in plasma are largely IgG-derived.4 Similarly, the glycans H5N5F1E1 and H5N5F1E2 correlated with IgM and IgA levels, respectively, indicating that these glycans in the plasma glycome are largely derived from these immunoglobulins.4

Conclusions

This study aimed to investigate the neonatal and maternal internal environment at the end of pregnancy using proteomic and glycomic profiling. We developed and validated an SRM-based multiplex assay to quantify 26 glycoproteins in plasma and applied it to 98 paired UCP samples and corresponding MP samples. We showed different proteomic profiles between MP and UCP samples. Most of the analyzed glycoproteins showed a significant decrease in UCP compared to MP, apart from glycoprotein A2M. Even though differences in protein levels between adults and neonates or changes in mothers during pregnancy are already known for most of the plasma proteins, our study is the first using targeted SRM-MS methodology for simultaneous quantifications of dozens of proteins for comparison between maternal and umbilical cord plasma. Moreover, our method allows us to distinguish protein variants, such as A1AG1 and A1AG2 or FGA, FGB, and FGG.

In the same set of samples, we analyzed TPNG profiles. Our results suggest limited production of higher, branched, sialylated N-glycans in newborns. In UCP samples, the major detected N-glycans were IgG-derived. During the first weeks and months after delivery, when the immune system is developing, the production of plasma proteins is increasing, and the changes in the total N-glycome will probably accompany it. However, this assumption should be further explored and confirmed in follow-up studies.
