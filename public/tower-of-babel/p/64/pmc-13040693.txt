Life-course influence of birthweight and subsequent pathways on healthy aging: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Lijie Kong, Chun Dou, Chaojie Ye, Dong Liu, Mingling Chen, Jie Zheng, Min Xu, Yu Xu, Mian Li, Zhiyun Zhao, Jieli Lu, Yuhong Chen, Guang Ning, Weiqing Wang, Yufang Bi, Ruixin Liu, Tiange Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBirthweight readily measurable marker of fetal growth that may influence health across the lifespan. We aimed to investigate the potential causal association between birthweight and healthy aging and to identify the mediating roles of subsequent socioeconomic, behavioral, functional, and disease-related factors to inform life-course strategies to promote healthy aging and reduce health inequities.MethodsWe performed two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses in European-ancestry participants to estimate the effect of birthweight (n = 298,142–423,683) on two robust, composite healthy aging phenotypes (genetically independent phenotype of aging (aging-GIP) and multivariate aging-related genetic factor (mvAge)) and six individual aging phenotypes, including healthspan, resilience, parental lifespan, self-rated health, phenotypic age deceleration, and 90th percentile self-longevity (n = 34,710–1,958,774), and screened for 100 candidate mediators (n = 14,267–1,812,017) using a two-step mediation analysis.ResultsGenetically determined each 1-SD higher birthweight was associated with higher aging-GIP (β [95% CI] in different models ranging from 0.131 [0.066–0.196] to 0.162 [0.089–0.235] SDs) and mvAge (0.036 [0.010–0.063] to 0.045 [0.024–0.067]), independent of later-life obesity indicators; also with more interpretable benefits, including 12%–16% higher odds of longer healthspan, a 0.079–0.089 SD improvement in resilience, and a 1.22–1.74 year increase in parental lifespan. Of 100 candidates, 26 and 25 mediated the effect of birthweight on aging-GIP and mvAge, respectively, including socioeconomic indicators (education, household income, occupational attainment; individual mediation proportion: 12.72%–27.79%); behaviors (e.g., cheese intake, age at first sex; 10.38%–29.56%); physical functions (e.g., blood pressure, grip strength; 7.57%–42.65%); and cardiometabolic diseases (e.g., type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases; 25.02%–70.11%).ConclusionsHigher birthweight within the normal range directly promotes healthy aging, mediated by multifaceted modifiable factors. Our findings advocate adopting a life-course approach to foster healthy aging, starting with optimal birthweight and extending to interventions that enhance socioeconomic status, promote healthy behaviors, strengthen physical functions, and prevent cardiometabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04737-8.

Background

Promoting healthy aging necessitates prevention strategies from a lifecycle lens [1], as the widening gap between lifespan and healthspan creates both socioeconomic strains and a mounting burden of age-related diseases [1–4]. Birthweight, a readily measurable marker of fetal growth, reflects multifaceted public health factors such as maternal nutrition and prenatal healthcare, of great public health significance [5–7]. Compelling observational and genetic evidence has associated lower birthweight with accelerated aging and higher risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and mortality [8–12]. However, meticulous evidence on the life-course impact of birthweight on healthy aging and how birthweight influences subsequent socioeconomic, behavioral, and functional factors and diseases across the life-course is limited.

Healthy aging is inherently multidimensional and cannot be adequately captured by a single clinical endpoint [13–16]. To address this complexity, recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have developed composite healthy aging metrics, including the genetically independent phenotype of aging (aging-GIP) and the multivariate aging-related genetic factor (mvAge) [13, 15]. By integrating common genetic markers across different aging domains, these phenotypes capture the overarching genetic susceptibility to multidimensional aging, reflecting both longevity and quality of life [13, 15], and facilitate research linking birthweight and healthy aging.

Several uncertainties remain to be elucidated. First, obesity is intricately intertwined with birthweight and aging trajectories [17, 18], but whether birthweight exerts an impact on healthy aging independent of later-life obesity is unknown. Second, although emerging evidence links birthweight with later-life socioeconomic status and behaviors [9, 19], the multifaceted mediating pathways in the effect of birthweight on healthy aging are unexplored, which is imperative for informing life-course health management. Thus far, conventional cohort studies face challenges in mapping the holistic effect chain of birthweight on healthy aging outcomes due to the need for relatively long follow-up periods, sufficient sample size, and comprehensive phenotypic data for extensive evaluation, which limits the depth and breadth of evidence available. Alternatively, the Mendelian randomization (MR) approach offers a solution by using genetic variants that are naturally randomly assigned at conception as instrumental variables (IVs) to establish causality, which can reduce susceptibility to confounding and reverse causality [20], especially when the exposure of interest (e.g., birthweight) is not practically or ethically amenable to randomization.

In this two-step MR study, we systematically evaluated the life-course influence of birthweight on healthy aging. Specifically, we (i) examined the total effects of genetically predicted birthweight on two robust, composite healthy aging phenotypes (aging-GIP and mvAge) and six individual aging-related phenotypes; (ii) assessed whether these associations were independent of later-life obesity; (iii) elucidated potential mediating pathways by screening 100 candidate mediators spanning socioeconomic, behavioral, physical, and disease domains; (iv) explored the origins of these associations in an exploratory analysis utilizing fetal- and maternal-specific birthweight genetic instruments; and (v) evaluated the robustness of findings through replication in independent GWAS datasets.

Methods

Study design

This study consisted of two analytic phases (Fig. 1, Additional file 1: Figs. S1–3). In Phase 1, we assessed the associations between genetically predicted birthweight and healthy aging phenotypes using univariable MR (UVMR) and clarified whether the effects were independent of childhood body mass index (BMI), adulthood BMI, or adulthood waist circumference (WC) using multivariable MR (MVMR). In Phase 2, we screened for 100 candidate mediators in the associations of birthweight with aging-GIP and mvAge, and quantified individual mediating proportions using the two-step MR. Our reporting strictly adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines (Additional file 2: Table S1) [21]. This study was conducted using publicly accessible, summary-level GWAS data from European-descent individuals. Data sources and genetic instruments

Genetic instruments were selected for birthweight (n = 298,142–423,683); covariates (childhood BMI, adulthood BMI, and adulthood WC, n = 39,620–681,275); and 100 candidate mediators including three socioeconomic indicators, 25 behavioral factors, 33 physical function-related traits, and 39 common diseases (n = 14,267–1,812,017). The outcome included two robust, composite metrics of healthy aging (aging-GIP, n = 154,478; mvAge, n = 1,958,774) and six individual aging phenotypes (healthspan, resilience, self-rated health, parental lifespan, PhenoAge deceleration, and 90th percentile self-longevity phenotype; n = 34,710–1,012,240). The aging-GIP was established on the basis of GWASs of typical aging phenotypes (i.e., the first five individual aging phenotypes) and could capture the shared genetic architecture across core aspects of aging, consolidate diverse genetic signals, and enhance the identification of biological mechanisms underlying aging processes [13]. The mvAge was constructed by integrating GWASs of multidimensional aging phenotypes (the last five individual aging phenotypes) using genomic structural equation modeling to derive a common genetic factor and could capture broader biological pathways underlying healthy aging [15]. Statistical analysis

UVMR and MVMR analyses

We performed UVMR to assess the total effects of genetically predicted birthweight on healthy aging phenotypes. MVMR was further applied to estimate the direct effect of birthweight after adjusting for childhood BMI, adulthood BMI, or adulthood WC, determining whether birthweight impacts healthy aging independently of later-life obesity. We employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method for maximum efficiency [76], as the primary analysis in UVMR and MVMR. The IVW method produces the most precise, unbiased, and efficient causal estimates, given that the IVs meet the MR assumptions [77]. The random-effect IVW method adjusts for heterogeneity of IVs, and the multivariable inverse-variance weighted (MV-IVW) method accounts for correlations between multiple exposures [78, 79]. The causal estimates were provided as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for binary outcomes and β coefficients with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes.

Exploratory and replication analyses

To investigate the origins of the association between birthweight and healthy aging, we conducted exploratory UVMR analyses using fetal-only, fetal-effect (adjusted for maternal genotype), maternal-only, and maternal-effect (adjusted for fetal genotype) genetic instruments for birthweight (Additional file 3: Method S1).

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we then performed a replication analysis, repeating the primary and exploratory analyses using an independent set of genetic instruments from a large birthweight GWAS of 423,683 European-ancestry individuals (Additional file 3: Method S1) [23, 24].

Mediation MR analysis

To identify potential mediating pathways through which birthweight influences healthy aging, we employed two-step MR analyses focusing on aging-GIP and mvAge. These analyses were hypothesis-driven and aimed to conduct exploratory research. Each mediator was analyzed separately in a one-by-one framework. The first step estimated the causal effect (β1) of birthweight on each mediator using UVMR. The second step assessed the causal effect (β2) of each mediator on each healthy aging outcome with adjustment for birthweight using MVMR, contingent on the mediator being causally linked to the outcome in UVMR. When evidence indicated that birthweight influenced the mediator, which in turn affected the outcome, we calculated the mediation proportion by dividing the mediation effect (β1 × β2) by the total effect of birthweight on the outcome. The 95% CIs for the mediation proportions were determined using the Delta method [80], with negative mediation proportions set at a minimum threshold of 0%. The mediation proportions cannot be summed, because they are not mutually exclusive and may be influenced by correlations among mediators.

MR sensitivity analyses

For UVMR analyses, we performed the weighted median, simple mode, weighted mode, MR-Egger, and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) methods as sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the IVW estimates under different assumptions (Additional file 3:Method S3) [76, 79, 81, 82]. Where there was sample overlap between exposure and outcome datasets, we assessed the type 1 error rates based on the calculator at https://sb452.shinyapps.io/overlap/ [83], and conducted the MRlap analysis to validate the causal estimates, as the MRlap is robust to biases introduced by sample overlap, winner’s curse, and weak instruments [84]. For MVMR analyses, we employed the multivariable MR-Egger (MVMR-Egger), MVMR-median, and multivariable MR-Lasso (MVMR-Lasso) methods to validate the robustness of the MV-IVW results (Additional file 3: Method S3) [85]. We calculated the F-statistics to evaluate the validity of the IVs, Cochran’s Q statistic to assess the heterogeneity, and the intercept of MR Egger to test for the pleiotropy of the IVW estimates. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for false discovery rate (FDR) correction to account for multiple testing.

All MR analyses were conducted with the R packages TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MRPRESSO, MVMR, and MRlap in R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided. A P value < 0.05 indicates statistical significance. The IVW estimates with P < 0.05 and FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 and supported by at least one sensitivity analysis were considered causal evidence, whereas the IVW estimates with P < 0.05 but FDR-adjusted P value ≥ 0.05 or not supported by sensitivity analysis were considered suggestive causal evidence.

Results

Total effect of birthweight on healthy aging phenotypes

In UVMR, genetically determined each 1-standard deviation (SD) higher birthweight was associated with higher aging-GIP (IVW-estimated β, 0.086 SDs; 95% CI, 0.027 to 0.145) and mvAge (0.034; 0.013 to 0.054) after FDR corrections for multiple comparisons (Fig. 2a, Additional file 2: Table S4). Consistently, positive associations were observed between each 1-SD higher birthweight and better resilience (β, 0.055 SDs; 95% CI, 0.012 to 0.098), higher odds of healthspan (odds ratio (OR), 1.12; 95% CI, 1.04 to 1.21), and longer parental lifespan (β, 0.846 years; 95% CI, 0.281 to 1.421). These associations, except for parental lifespan, were supported by two UVMR sensitivity analyses (Additional file 2: Table S4). In exploratory analyses, genetically determined fetal-effect (or fetal-only) birthweight was positively associated with aging-GIP, mvAge, and healthspan, whereas maternal-effect (or maternal-only) birthweight showed no significant associations with healthy aging phenotypes after FDR correction (Additional file 2: Table S4). There was potential heterogeneity between IVs, but the instrumental validity test suggested sufficient instrument strength (F-statistics ≥ 24), and no horizontal pleiotropy was detected (all Pintercept ≥ 0.05; Additional file 2: Table S5). Replication analysis confirmed the primary findings, showing that genetically determined birthweight was positively associated with aging‑GIP and mvAge, as well as healthspan, resilience, and parental lifespan (Additional file 2: Table S8, Table S9). Similarly, as for exploratory analyses, associations with better healthy aging outcomes were observed for fetal-effect birthweight but not for maternal-effect birthweight, suggesting the association might be predominantly driven by fetal genetic effects. These results were robustly supported by at least one sensitivity analysis, with no evidence of weak instruments (F-statistics ≥ 41) or horizontal pleiotropy (all Pintercept ≥ 0.05).

Independent effect of birthweight on healthy aging phenotypes adjusting for later-life obesity indicators

To assess whether genetically predicted birthweight influences healthy aging independently of later-life obesity, we performed MVMR analyses adjusting in turn for childhood BMI, adulthood BMI, and adulthood WC. The positive associations of genetically predicted birthweight with aging-GIP and mvAge, as well as healthspan, resilience, and parental lifespan, persisted with adjustment for childhood BMI, adulthood BMI, and adulthood WC, respectively (Fig. 2b). These MV-IVW estimates were well-validated by at least one MVMR sensitivity analysis (Additional file 2: Table S10), and replication analysis yielded highly consistent results (Additional file 2: Table S11), further corroborating the effect of genetically predicted birthweight on healthy aging independent of later-life obesity.

Two-step MR identifying qualified mediators between birthweight and healthy aging phenotypes

In total, 26 and 25 candidates met all screening criteria and were qualified as mediators between birthweight and aging-GIP and mvAge, respectively (Fig. 3). From birthweight to mediators, strong UVMR evidence supported associations between genetically predicted birthweight and these mediators (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 and confirmed by at least one UVMR sensitivity analysis; Fig. 4, Additional file 2: Tables S12–S13). Mediating effects of mediators in the associations between birthweight and healthy aging phenotypes

Among physical functions and diseases, type 2 diabetes showed the highest individual mediation proportion (70.11%) for the effect of birthweight on aging-GIP, followed by myocardial infarction ((MI), 57.78%); coronary artery disease ((CAD), 47.48%); hypertension (45.30%); coronary heart disease ((CHD), 44.77%); systolic blood pressure ((SBP), 42.65%); grip strength (36.72%); coronary atherosclerosis (29.02%); angina pectoris (25.02%); 2 h-glucose post-challenge ((2hGlu), 24.94%); triglycerides (24.18%); apolipoprotein B ((ApoB), 23.41%); and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ((nonHDL-C), 20.35%), etc. (Fig. 4a). Behavioral mediators between birthweight and aging-GIP ranked by individual mediation proportion included cheese intake (29.56%), age at first sex (25.36%), age at first live birth (23.69%), and leisure screen time (17.35%). Socioeconomic mediators, including educational attainment, household income, and occupational attainment, each mediated 20.90% to 27.79% of the total effect.

For mvAge, the mediator profiles and rankings were largely similar to those observed for aging‑GIP (Fig. 4b). Type 2 diabetes remained the strongest mediator (mediation proportion: 55.17%), followed by CVDs (28.41% to 46.03%) and physical functions (7.57% to 27.12%). Behavioral and socioeconomic factors retained similar rankings, though with slightly attenuated proportions.

Discussion

This study elucidated the effects of birthweight on healthy aging phenotypes and the mediating pathways involved. We found that genetically determined higher birthweight within the normal range was associated with a higher likelihood of healthy aging, as represented by aging-GIP and mvAge, as well as healthspan, resilience, and parental lifespan, and was consistently independent of childhood BMI, adulthood BMI, and adulthood WC. Out of 100 candidates, 26 and 25 mediators were identified to explain the effect of birthweight on aging-GIP and mvAge, respectively, encompassing socioeconomic indicators (i.e., educational attainment, household income, and occupational attainment); behaviors (i.e., cheese intake, age at first sex, age at first live birth, and leisure screen time); physical functions (e.g., glycemic traits, SBP, grip strength, lipids and lipoproteins, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)); and cardiometabolic diseases (e.g., type 2 diabetes and CVDs), implying underlying mediating pathways and preventive targets. Our findings support the role of birthweight in shaping healthy aging, and enlighten socioeconomic, behavioral, and therapeutic interventive targets for bridging aging disparities attributable to lower birthweight.

Previous cohort studies on birthweight and aging have primarily focused on mortality rather than healthy aging phenotypes, reporting null [11, 86], inverse linear [12], or U-shaped relationships [87]. Differences in study population characteristics, sample size, and potential confounders might partly explain these conflicting observations. In this study, we found that independent of later-life obesity indicators, genetically determined each 1-SD (≈484 g) increase in birthweight was significantly associated with healthy aging, reflected by composite phenotypes including higher aging-GIP and mvAge. These associations were further corroborated by individual, interpretable phenotypes, including 12%–16% higher likelihood of longer healthspan remaining free from seven major age-related diseases or death, a 0.079–0.089 SD improvement in resilience (equivalent to approximately 0.3 fewer health deficits), and an increase of 1.22–1.74 years in parental lifespan. These findings largely isolate the potential impact of later-life obesity, indicating that healthy aging is rooted in healthy birth [8, 88]. Our exploratory analyses also suggest that fetal genetic effects, rather than maternal genotype (as a proxy for the intrauterine environment), may primarily drive the genetically determined birthweight’s influence on healthy aging. Two previous mother–offspring pairs (and father-offspring pairs) MR studies also reported null associations of maternal single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) influencing offspring birthweight with offspring cardiometabolic outcomes, implying a limited role of maternal intrauterine influences as captured by maternal genetics [22, 89]. As maternal genetic instruments may imperfectly capture intrauterine exposures (e.g., non-genetic maternal factors like smoking and diet) [22], our results do not exclude the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) processes driven by environmental factors beyond those explained by maternal genetics.

Given that birthweight is determined early in life, we further explored modifiable mediators in subsequent life trajectories to elucidate underlying mechanisms and identify specific preventive targets among individuals with a suboptimal birthweight. Of the initial 100 candidates, we revealed that several socioeconomic indicators such as educational attainment, household income, and occupational attainment, as well as behaviors including dietary intakes of cheese, age at first sex, age at first live birth, and leisure screen time, each mediated a substantial proportion (about 10% to 30%) of the total effect of birthweight on healthy aging. Importantly, these socioeconomic and behavioral mediators should be interpreted within a life-course framework rather than as direct biological consequences of birthweight. Although such traits are strongly shaped by family and social environments, accumulating evidence indicates that fetal growth restriction, proxied by lower birthweight, can exert lasting influences on brain structure and function, neurodevelopment, physical capacity, and overall health capital [90–92]. These early-life biological constraints may subsequently shape educational trajectories, occupational attainment, and health-related behaviors through health-related selection and capacity-dependent pathways. Although socioeconomic and behavioral factors seem to play a less direct role than physical functional factors in the pathway from birthweight to healthy aging, their influence might be more widespread and should not be overlooked [93]. Previous evidence has documented that most of these identified socioeconomic (e.g., education, income) and behavioral mediators (e.g., dietary habits) are important determinants of multiple health and longevity outcomes [18, 50, 94]. Notably, although cheese intake was identified as a potential mediator, it likely reflects broader dietary patterns and should not be interpreted as a direct dietary recommendation. Taken together, our findings highlight the public health implications of birthweight on healthy aging through later-life socioeconomic and behavioral factors, offering interventive opportunities to reshape aging trajectories for individuals with lower birthweight.

In addition, we revealed that of multiple physical functional factors and diseases mediating the effect of birthweight on healthy aging, cardiometabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and different types of CVDs manifested more pronounced mediation proportions (approximately 25% to 70%). These findings were supported by compelling evidence that restricted fetal growth might lead to alternations in insulin signaling pathways, vascular adaptations, and epigenetic modifications, resulting in disrupted glucose metabolism and CVDs in later life [6, 7, 95]. Additional physical function-related mediators including grip strength, lipids and lipoproteins, and liver and kidney indicators exerted an individual mediation proportion of less than 37%, most of which are well-documented contributors to aging [18, 50, 94]. These mediators help guide tailored prevention strategies for individuals with suboptimal birthweight later in life, focusing on managing cardiometabolic risk factors and enhancing physical functions (e.g., grip strength) to promote healthy aging. Notably, given that various mediators may influence healthy aging through overlapping or correlated biological pathways, we assessed each mediator separately in practical scenarios. While this approach improves interpretability and methodological stability, the resulting mediation proportions cannot be summed and should be interpreted with caution.

This study highlighted the importance of birthweight and its subsequent life-course pathways in shaping healthy aging, with imperative public health and clinical implications. We outlined mediators that span the entire lifespan, encompassing early-life factors like age at first sex, midlife physical functions, and late-life cardiometabolic diseases, alongside lifelong behaviors and socioeconomic factors. The temporal breadth and diversity of mediators reflect the complexity of how birthweight influences healthy longevity throughout the human lifespan and underscore the multifaceted nature of this relationship, aligning with the World Health Organization life-course framework [1, 96]. This conveys an encouraging public health implication: low birthweight, as a marker of suboptimal early-life development, does not inevitably lead to an adverse aging trajectory. Instead, modifying key mediators in later life can help mitigate early-life disadvantages, offering opportunities to promote healthy aging equity.

The main strengths of this study included the rigorously designed MR analytical framework, large sample size (over one million), a comprehensive and representative array of aging phenotypes, meticulous and extensive considerations of candidate mediators, and the robust associations inferred from the main analysis supported by various sensitivity analyses and replication analyses. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, common genetic variants explain a smaller proportion of inter-individual variation in mortality compared to the exposome [97]. Although multiple sensitivity analyses and complementary tests yielded consistent results, indicating no evidence of weak-instrument bias and suggesting that horizontal pleiotropy and sample overlap were unlikely to substantially influence the causal estimates, the MR assumptions cannot be fully verified [20]. Accordingly, the causal associations should be interpreted with caution, and our MR estimates should be viewed as providing a stable, genetically informed layer of evidence that complements observational evidence. Second, although the composite healthy aging phenotypes (aging-GIP and mvAge) capture the broad genetic liability across various aging-related traits that include binary and continuous units, their abstract nature makes the interpretation of MR estimates challenging. Nonetheless, we also presented corroborative evidence from individual aging phenotypes (e.g., healthspan and parental lifespan) that more directly map to clinical endpoints, which may facilitate the clinical interpretation of MR estimates that reflect lifelong estimates [20]. Third, the obesity indicator as a covariate in the MVMR analyses was treated as a linear continuous exposure. Therefore, the resulting estimates reflect average linear effects of genetic liability to obesity. Although observational evidence reports non-linear associations between BMI and mortality [98], the potential non-linear effect of the obesity indicator was not taken into account in our analyses. Fourth, the potential exposure-mediator or mediator-mediator interactions cannot be modeled in the present two-step MR setting. Nevertheless, the MR approaches use genetic variants fixed at conception and naturally randomly assigned to individuals as proxies for the exposure and the mediator [20], which largely alleviates the potential bias from these interactions, and we assess the mediation proportion of each mediator individually in practical scenarios. Fifth, although we included common and important socioeconomic indicators, behaviors, physical functions, and diseases to facilitate clinical practice, this study cannot fully capture all mediation pathways, such as non-heritable factors (e.g., pension resources allocation influenced by national policies). Sixth, to limit potential influence from population stratification, our analyses were restricted to individuals of European ancestry, and the generalization of our findings to other ethnic groups warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides genetic evidence that birthweight is associated with healthy aging phenotypes, and that part of this association appears to operate through modifiable socioeconomic, behavioral, functional, and cardiometabolic pathways. Our findings support the importance of adopting a life-course approach to foster healthy aging by optimizing birthweight and targeting subsequent modifiable mediators, including improving socioeconomic status, promoting healthy behaviors, enhancing physical functions, and preventing cardiometabolic diseases.
