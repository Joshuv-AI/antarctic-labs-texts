Larval ecology of Aedes spp. mosquitoes and arbovirus transmission risk in four mid-sized cities of southern Cameroon
Authors: Bertille Carine Djapoum Theno, Gisele Aurelie Foko Dadji, Francis Zeukeng, Borel Djiappi-Tchamen, Nelly Armanda Kala-Chouakeu, Roland Bamou, Marie Paul Audrey Mayi, Abdou Talipouo, Parfait Awono-Ambene, Joseph Lebel Tamesse, Rachid Soulimani, Liwang Cui, Abraham Fomena, Christophe Antonio-Nkondjio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVector-based surveillance is crucial for monitoring arboviral diseases such as dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Zika. In Cameroon, data on the distribution and bionomics of Aedes species in medium-sized cities within the equatorial forest region are limited. This study assessed the distribution, larval habitats, and bionomics of immature Aedes spp. stages across four mid-sized cities of southern Cameroon. Larval surveys were conducted from March 2022 to March 2023 in Bertoua, Kribi, Sangmelima, and Abong-Mbang during rainy and dry seasons. Water-holding containers were inspected using the dipping method, and collected larvae and pupae were reared to adults for morphological identification. Entomological indices were used to estimate arboviral transmission risk, and species co-occurrence in larval habitats was recorded. A generalized linear model was used to compare abundance and distribution among sites, seasons, and breeding site. From 758 larval habitats inspected (214 in Bertoua, 213 in Sangmelima, 184 in Kribi, 147 in Abong-Mbang), 3820 mosquitoes belonging to four genera and 11 species were emerged. Aedes spp. mosquitoes were the most abundant (n = 2903), with Aedes albopictus and Aedes aegypti as dominant species that often co-occurred. Seasonal variation in the abundance of all mosquito genera was significant, with peak Aedes spp. abundance in the rainy season (n = 2359; 81.3%). Tires were the most common larval habitats, mostly located within 10 m of human dwellings. Kribi and Bertoua exhibited the highest container, house, and Breteau indices, suggesting high potential risk. The study supports the need for regular vector-based surveillance to control arboviral disease in Cameroon. Such studies could also provide critical information for strengthening integrated vector management.Keywords: Arboviral diseases, Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus, Southern Cameroon

Introduction

The burden of arboviral diseases, including dengue, chikungunya, and yellow fever, is fast growing in Africa (Braack et al., 2018; Agha and Tchouassi, 2022). Over the past decade, frequent outbreaks of these diseases have been reported in sub-Saharan Africa (Ajogbasile et al., 2020; Alayu et al., 2020). In 2023, there were around 171,991 reported cases of dengue in the region, resulting in 753 deaths (WHO, 2023). Although no important outbreak of dengue has been reported so far in Cameroon, dengue or chikungunya cases have frequently been detected in febrile patients visiting healthcare facilities across the country (Galani et al., 2020; Simo Tchetgna et al., 2021; Djeunang Dongho et al., 2022; Nana-Ndjangwo et al., 2022; Noche et al., 2023). Also, several neighboring countries, including the Republic of the Congo and Chad, have recently registered severe dengue epidemics (Minsanté Chad, 2023; WHO, 2023). In addition, a resurgence of yellow fever cases has been reported in Cameroon since 2021, including 46 cases in 26 health districts in 2021, 42 cases in 12 health districts in 2022, and 63 cases nationwide in 2023 (Nalova Akua, 2024). This alarming situation requires immediate action to strengthen the fight against these diseases. Although there is an effective vaccine for yellow fever, vector control still plays a significant role in the prevention of Aedes-borne diseases (Wilson et al., 2020). Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are the major arbovirus vectors in Cameroon (Tedjou et al., 2019). Aedes aegypti has been reported in the country since the early 1950s (Rageau and Adam, 1952), whereas Ae. albopictus invaded the country in the 2000s (Fontenille and Toto, 2001) and is now the predominant species in most environmental settings (Tedjou et al., 2019; Djiappi-Tchamen et al., 2021).

The global expansion of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus is underpinned by a complex interplay of climatic and anthropogenic determinants. At the macroecological level, ambient temperature and relative humidity are primary drivers, directly influencing larval development rates, adult longevity, and the frequency of hematophagous activity (Kraemer et al., 2015; Mantilla-Granados et al., 2025). At the micro-habitat level, anthropogenic factors such as rapid, unplanned urbanization and suboptimal waste management provide an abundance of artificial breeding sites. While Ae. aegypti remains highly anthropophilic, predominantly colonizing domestic water-storage jars and flower pots, Ae. albopictus exhibits significant ecological plasticity. This species successfully exploits both natural niches, such as tree holes and bamboo stumps, and man-made containers like discarded tires, which offer dark, humid microclimates mimicking forest environments (Djiappi-Tchamen et al., 2021; Herath et al., 2025). Despite these known global drivers, the specific ecological factors shaping the distribution and co-occurrence of these species in the unique rainforest-urban interface of South Cameroon remain insufficiently characterized.

Although several studies have assessed the distribution of Aedes species in Cameroon over the past decade (Simard et al., 2005; Kamgang et al., 2010; Tedjou et al., 2019), few have been conducted in the rainforest-urban southern region, where signs of increasing human activity and habitat modification are evident. In this area, urban expansion is often associated with deforestation linked to settlement growth, agricultural practices, and population increase (Bruggeman, 2016; Romain et al., 2017). However, the influence of these ecological and environmental changes on the distribution of Aedes mosquitoes remains poorly understood. This study provides the first comprehensive seasonal assessment of Aedes species distribution and breeding site characteristics in this rapidly urbanizing forest ecosystem. By integrating entomological surveys across different seasons and habitat types, our work generates baseline ecological data for a previously understudied region. The southern Cameroon forest region borders Gabon, the Republic of the Congo, and the Central African Republic, where frequent outbreaks of chikungunya and dengue have been reported over the last decade (Gabor, 2016; Mbanzulu, 2020; Poungou, 2024). Consequently, this region constitutes a permanent risk area for arboviral invasion or resurgence. The present study assessed the bionomics and distribution of Aedes species in four medium-sized urban cities in the south Cameroon forest region and explored seasonal, ecological and environmental factors shaping the distribution of Aedes mosquitoes in this environment.

Materials and methods

Study areas

The study was conducted during both dry and rainy seasons from March 2022 to March 2023. This 12-month transect aligns with Cameroon’s bimodal rainfall pattern, enabling detection of temporal variation of species abundance. Sampling occurred in Kribi, Sangmélima, Bertoua, and Abong-Mbang, sites strategically selected to represent an urbanization gradient, diverse ecological zones, and Aedes spp. invasion risks across Cameroon’s southern forest regions (Fig. 1). Sampling was conducted over a continuous 12-month period from March 2022 to March 2023 to capture the bimodal rainfall regime typical of the South Cameroon Plateau and coastal forest zones. This time-frame ensures the inclusion of both the primary and secondary rainy and dry seasons, providing the necessary resolution to detect seasonal shifts in larval density and habitat occupancy. The study sites namely Kribi, Sangmélima, Bertoua, and Abong-Mbang were strategically selected to represent a climatological and urbanization gradient. This allows for the assessment of Aedes spp. invasion risks and population dynamics across varying ecological contexts, from coastal humid forests to semi-deciduous inland zones. The city of Kribi (2°56′14.10″N, 9°54′27.54″E) is located along the Atlantic Ocean coast. This city hosts a major deep-sea port that has been operational since 2018. This port handles significant volumes of international trade, which may increase the risk of Aedes spp. introduction and spread through shipments, cargo, and associated breeding sites such as containers and tires. Abong-Mbang (2°58′52.28″N, 13°11′9.43″E) and Sangmelima (2°55′59″N, 11°58′59″E) are located inland in the heart of the equatorial forest region, while Bertoua (4°34′30″N, 13°41′04″E) is located further east, closer to the Central African Republic in a forest savannah transition area. Further characteristics of study sites are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All sites have an equatorial climate characterized by four seasons, two rainy and two dry splited as follows: a long rainy season from September to November, a long dry season from December to February, a short rainy season from March to June and a short dry season from July to August. Moderate seasonal variations are often observed from one site to another.

Study design and mosquito survey

Twelve surveys per year were carried out in each study site, corresponding to three surveys per season across the four annual seasons to account for patchy larval distribution, short gonotrophic cycles (∼3–5 days), and fluctuating adult activity peaks driven by microclimate variations (WHO, 2016). All localities were sampled with the same effort and survey schedule. Larval habitat inspections took place over a period of 7–10 days in each sampling site. In each locality, approximately 100 randomly selected households were systematically inspected across a representative cross-section of both downtown and urban areas. Inspections were primarily focused on breeding sites in the peridomestic surroundings of these households, such as tanks, abandoned plastic containers, and used tires, the latter being particularly frequent around garages. Larval sampling was conducted using the WHO dipping method (Service, 1976). Any water-holding container around houses was inspected as a potential Aedes spp. breeding site if accessible with owner consent; dry, inaccessible, or non-residential sites were excluded. Additionally, we surveyed breeding sites in selected public areas, specifically markets where used tires were abundant. All immature stages (larvae and pupae) found in the inspected containers were collected.

During inspections, each potential larval habitat was geo-referenced with a global positioning system (GPS) and the following parameters were registered in a validated questionnaire: larval habitats type, presence/absence of immatures stages, type of larval instars (L1-L2 or L3-L4), presence of pupae, distance of the larval habitats in human vicinity (< 10 m, 10–100 m, and > 100 m from the nearest house). After collection, immature stages from the same larval habitats were stored in individual clean, properly labelled bottles and transported to the insectary of OCEAC (Organization for the Coordination of the Fight Against Endemic Diseases in Central Africa) for rearing under controlled conditions (28 ± 2 °C, 75 ± 5% relative humidity and 12:12 h light:dark photoperiod). After emergence, adult mosquitoes were provided with a 10% sucrose solution and were identified under a stereomicroscope using taxonomic keys (Edwards, 1941; Jupp, 1996).

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel 2013 was used to enter the data, standardize variable formats, and filter the dataset by removing duplicated entries and observations with missing key variables. Subsequent statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.0.4). Species richness (total number of species) and mosquito abundance (total number of specimens collected) were determined per study site season and larval habitats. Shannon diversity index (H =−∑i=1Spiln(pi)), Simpson’s dominance index (D =∑i=1Spi2) and Evenness (E = Hln⁡(S)) were also estimated, where S is the species richness, pi is the relative abundance of the ith species niN. Counting and species-level identification were performed on the emerged adults. Analyses included the two major vector species, Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. Abundance, spatial distribution, and larval habitat range for each species were analyzed separately to account for potential differences in abundance and ecological preferences. To assess factors influencing Aedes spp. abundance, Poisson/negative binomial generalized linear models (GLMs) were performed, as they are specifically designed to handle non-normal distribution and count data. The dependent variable was the abundance of Aedes spp. immature stages collected per site (larval counts). The independent variables included breeding site type (e.g. tires, containers, natural sites), study locality (Bertoua, Abong-Mbang, Sangmelima, Kribi) and season (dry vs rainy). Models with a single independent variable or combinations of these, as well as with and without interactions between variables, were compared using AIC (Akaike information criterion) in order to report the most informative GLM. After measuring the correlation between the predictor variables using variance inflation factors (VIFs), we applied a forward stepwise model selection, and the model with the lower AIC value was selected. We used the “vif ()” function from the “car” library to calculate the VIF for each predictor variable in the model; while a value of “1” = no correlation, “between 1 and 5” = moderate correlation, “> 5” = high correlation. Statistical comparisons of the prevalence of Aedes spp. larvae regarding season, study locality, and breeding site were performed using a generalized linear model (GLM) with a binomial distribution and logit link function.

To estimate the risk of arbovirus transmission in each study locality, Stegomyia indices were estimated: House Index (HI), Container Index (CI), and Breteau Index (BI). HI represents the percentage of houses infested with Aedes spp. larvae; CI represents the percentage of containers infested with immature stages of Aedes spp.; and BI represents the number of positive containers per 100 inspected houses. When BI, HI, and CI exceeded 50, 35%, and 20%, respectively, the risk of Aedes spp. transmission of yellow fever was considered to be high. When BI, HI, and CI were less than 5%, 4%, and 3%, respectively, the area was considered unlikely for yellow fever transmission to occur. Likewise, for dengue transmission risk, an HI below 0.1% signals a low risk, values from 0.1% to 5% indicate medium risk, and values above 5% point to high risk for dengue transmission (WHO, 1971; PAHO, 1994). The level of significance for statistical analysis was 0.05.

Results

Seasonal distribution of Aedes spp. in study localities

The highest density of Aedes mosquitoes species, including Ae. albopictus, Ae. aegypti and Ae. palpalis, was recorded during the rainy season (n = 2359; 81.3%) compared to the dry season (n = 544; 18.7%). Aedes spp. abundance between the two seasons varied significantly (P < 0.0001). Aedes albopictus was the most abundant species in each season (Table 1). The highest number of Aedes spp. mosquitoes collected (n = 812; 28.0%) during both seasons was recorded at Sangmelima, closely followed by Kribi (n = 775; 26.7%), Bertoua (n = 802; 27.6%) and Abong-Mbang (n = 514; 17.7%). Out of the three Aedes species collected, all three were found in Abong-Mbang, and only two were found in the remaining sites (Table 1).

Distribution of Aedes spp. larval habitats across study localities and seasons

A total of 758 larval habitats capable of harboring at least one immature stage of Aedes spp. were inspected across study localities, including 214 (28.23%) in Bertoua, 213 (28.10%) in Sangmelima, 184 (24.27%) in Kribi, and 147 (19.40%) in Abong-Mbang. The number of larval habitats inspected per locality varied significantly (χ2 = 15.77, P = 0.0013). During entomological surveys, Aedes populations were identified in 223 (29.42%) larval habitats, including used tires, tanks, abandoned plastic, metal containers, and breeze blocks (Fig. 2). Among these, used tires were the most abundant (n = 98, 43.95%), followed by plastic (n = 64, 28.70%) and metal containers (n = 32, 14.35%). No significant difference was recorded for Ae. aegypti (Kruskal-Wallis χ2 = 10.20, df = 5, P = 0.0697) and Ae. albopictus (Kruskal-Wallis χ2 = 8.485, df = 5, P = 0.1315) (Fig. 3). There were significantly more larval habitats during the rainy season (n = 439, 57.92%) than during the dry season (n = 319, 42.08%) (P < 0.0001). Aedes albopictus was mostly found in used tires in Sangmelima (n = 23, 19.66%), while Ae. aegypti reached a peak distribution in the same habitat type in Bertoua (n = 20, 15.38%) during the rainy season (Table 2). Larval abundance differed significantly among localities (LR χ2 = 10.12, df = 3, P = 0.018) and between seasons (LR χ2 = 98.05, df = 1, P < 0.001). A significant interaction between locality and season was also observed (LR χ2 = 24.51, df = 3, P < 0.001).

Species diversity and abundance per collection site

From the 223 positive larval habitats inspected, 3820 mosquitoes belonging to four genera, Aedes, Culex, Anopheles and Lutzia (13 species) were registered (Table 1). Species belonging to the genus Aedes were the most abundant. Three species of this genus were collected, including 2081 (71.7%) Ae. albopictus, 790 (27.2%) Ae. aegypti, 4 (0.1%) Ae. palpalis, and 28 morphologically unidentified Aedes spp.

In total, 1140, 1121, 957, and 602 mosquitoes emerged as adults from larvae collected in Bertoua, Sangmelima, Kribi, and Abong-Mbang, respectively. Out of the 2903 Aedes spp. mosquitoes registered, 812 (28.0%) were collected in Sangmelima, 802 (27.6%) in Bertoua, 775 (26.7%) in Kribi and 514 (17.7%) in Abong-Mbang. A significant difference in the abundance of Aedes spp. was observed across study localities (χ2 = 83.42, df = 3, P < 0.0001).

GLM analysis indicated significant variation in the abundance and distribution among localities, seasons, and breeding sites for both Ae. aegypti (Supplementary Table S2) and Ae. albopictus (Supplementary Table S3). For Ae. aegypti, significant effects were observed for locality (LR χ2 = 107.46, df = 3, P < 0.0001), season (LR χ2 = 138.93, df = 1, P < 0.0001), and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 424.66, df = 5, P < 0.0001), with significant two-way interactions between locality and season (LR χ2 = 48.83, df = 3, P = 0.0001), site and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 79.15, df = 5, P = 0.0001), and season and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 25.65, df = 2, P = 0.0001). Similarly, larval abundance of Ae. albopictus showed significant variation among localities (LR χ2 = 132.35, df = 3, P < 0.0001), between seasons (LR χ2 = 515.61, df = 1, P < 0.0001), and among breeding site types (LR χ2 = 872.69, df = 5, P < 0.0001). Significant two-way interactions were found between locality and season (LR χ2 = 194.63, df = 3, P < 0.0001), locality and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 822.75, df = 8, P < 0.0001), and season and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 484.22, df = 3, P < 0.0001), with a significant three-way interaction among locality, season, and breeding site type (LR χ2 = 13.60, df = 3, P = 0.0035).

The biodiversity indices revealed varied ecological profiles across the study sites (Table 3). The highest values of the Shannon diversity index were recorded at Sangmelima (H = 1.78) and Bertoua (H = 1.77) compared to Kribi (H = 1.65) and Abong-Mbang (H = 1.45). On the other hand, Kribi showed the highest species richness (S = 11) while Sangmelima showed the lowest richness (S = 7). Regarding the evenness index, Sangmelima and Bertoua exhibited more balanced and stable mosquito communities, while Abong-Mbang and Kribi showed signs of uneven species distribution (Table 3).

Co-occurrence of Aedes spp. and other mosquito species in larval habitats

Out of 223 positive larval habitats, larvae of the invasive species Ae. albopictus were found alone in 100 (44.84%), while larvae of Ae. aegypti were recorded alone in 25 (11.21%). Co-occurrence of mosquito species was common, with 42 (42.88%) of used tires, 28 (43.75%) of plastic containers and 19 (59.38%) of metal containers harboring multiple species. The association of Ae. albopictus and Ae. aegypti was particularly frequent in plastic containers 17 (26.56%). The most widespread triple association (Ae. albopictus + Ae. aegypti + Culex sp.) appeared in 3 (3.06%) of used tires (Supplementary Table S4). Association involving Ae. palpalis co-occurring with the predatory larvae of Lutzia spp. were observed exclusively in metallic containers 1 (3.12%). Notably, habitat-specific complex sympatries included Ae. albopictus, Cx. nebulosus and Cx. poisilipes exclusively in plastic containers (n = 2, 3.12%) and metal containers (n = 1, 3.12%). Aedes spp. mosquitoes also shared similar habitats with other genera, such as Anopheles and Lutzia.

Distance between larval habitats of Aedes spp. and human habitations

During entomological surveys, the highest proportion (71.3%) of Aedes spp. larval habitats were found very close (0–10 m) to human dwellings (Fig. 4), and very few larval habitats (4.9%) were far (10–100 m) from human habitations.

Stegomyia indices of arbovirus transmission risk

To better understand the epidemiological implications of Aedes spp. infestation in the study areas, the Stegomyia indices were analyzed across localities and seasons, providing insight into potential risks of arboviral transmission (Table 4). The highest infestation levels during the rainy season were recorded at Bertoua, with a Container Index (CI = 57.69%), a House Index (HI = 76%), and a Breteau Index (BI = 150), all well above the WHO epidemic risk thresholds (HI > 35%, BI > 50 and CI > 20%). Similarly, a high potential transmission risk during the rainy period was registered at Kribi (HI = 62%, CI = 50.44% and BI = 114), while moderate but still significant infestation levels were found at Sangmelima (HI = 66%, CI = 33.33%, and BI = 78). In contrast, comparatively lower indices were registered at Abong-Mbang (HI = 28%, CI = 24.68%, and BI = 38), suggesting a relatively reduced, though non-negligible, risk of outbreak (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study investigated the distribution, larval habitats, and bionomics of immature stages of Aedes spp. mosquitoes across four mid-sized cities of southern Cameroon. Aedes albopictus and Ae. aegypti emerged as the most abundant species in the four sites, whereas Ae. palpalis was found only in Abong-Mbang. The lower diversity of species in this environment is similar to previous studies in Yaoundé and Douala (Kamgang et al., 2010; Tedjou et al., 2020; Djiappi-Tchamen et al., 2021; Yougang et al., 2022). Aedes albopictus emerged as the predominant species, outpacing Ae. aegypti densities in most cities. The significant predominance of Ae. albopictus over Ae. aegypti observed in our study may be attributed to its superior competitive ability. This species is considered a habitat generalist, with larvae that can exploit a wide range of breeding sites. Its high adaptability to diverse environmental conditions may therefore provide a competitive advantage over other species, as reported in previous studies (Tedjou et al., 2019; Juliano et al., 2004). Densities of Ae. aegypti were found to be particularly high in the cities of Bertoua and Kribi during the rainy season, suggesting the presence of convenient environmental conditions for this species in these settings (Kamgang et al., 2013, 2017). Studies have indicated that increased urbanization and rainfall effectively boost the success of Ae. aegypti by expanding their larval habitat and human host density (Montgomery et al., 2025).

Immature stages of Aedes spp. were found to colonize a variety of habitats, including discarded tires, plastic containers, and metallic containers. Used tires were the primary larval habitat in the different cities harboring the highest densities of Aedes spp. larvae, particularly during the rainy season. These results aligned with previous studies in Cameroon that identified used tires as a factor sustaining the distribution of Aedes spp. mosquitoes in the country (Kamgang et al., 2013; Tedjou et al., 2019; Hondt et al., 2020; Djiappi-Tchamen et al., 2021, 2022). Similar observations were made in Mozambique and Ethiopia (Abílio et al., 2018; Ferede et al., 2018). Mosquitoes prefer tires because of their ability to retain water for extended periods and offer protection from predators, tires are thus suitable oviposition sites (Camara et al., 2016). The dark color and shaded interior of used tires closely mimic the conditions found in the natural tree-hole habitats preferred by Aedes spp. mosquitoes (Yee, 2008). This similarity in color likely contributes to the tendency of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus to successfully colonize and breed in discarded tires (Camara et al., 2016). Used tires were more abundant in Sangmelima and Bertoua and contributed to higher Aedes spp. abundance in these cities; however, Kribi also exhibited high Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus abundance despite having fewer positive tires, indicating that other breeding sites such as plastic containers also played an important role in supporting mosquito populations. Plastic containers used for water storage were also found to be common larval habitats for mosquitoes, particularly in Kribi and Bertoua.

Larvae of Ae. albopictus were less frequent in metallic tanks. Females of Aedes spp. usually choose oviposition sites based on a combination of physical attributes (e.g. depth, color, clearance, surface, location, height, shade, sun exposure, and proximity to food sources) and ecological factors (e.g. competition and predation) (Diallo et al., 2012; Leisnham and Juliano, 2012; Abreu et al., 2015). It is likely that metallic containers could be less suitable than plastic containers since they are more conducive to heat. Metal containers absorb and retain more heat than plastic ones, causing the water inside to become hotter. When the water temperature exceeds 35 °C, it harms larval survival and prevents larvae from pupating. Because plastic containers do not heat up as much, they offer a more stable, suitable environment for larval development, resulting in shorter growth periods and faster pupation (Kumar et al., 2016; Doeurk et al., 2025).

Higher densities of Aedes spp. mosquitoes were observed in Sangmelima and Bertoua compared to Kribi and Abong-Mbang. These differences may coincide with higher human population density in Bertoua mainly, and deforestation in Sangmelima. This is consistent with previous studies showing that higher population density is often associated with unplanned urbanization practices which increase Aedes spp. abundance, while also deforestation or land-use change can also enhance the suitability of their habitats (Kamgang et al., 2017; Tedjou et al., 2019; Kolimenakis et al., 2021; Saager et al., 2023). Studies conducted so far in Cameroon indicated that the rapid urbanization of Cameroon cities is associated with poor hygiene and sanitation, poor management of wastes, dumping of spared tires in garages, or absence of a water supply system, which all create favorable conditions for the establishment of mosquito in urban settings (Antonio-Nkondjio et al., 2011). The high nuisance of mosquitoes as a consequence of unplanned urbanization has been reported in several cities across Africa (Robert et al., 2003; Zahouli et al., 2017; Nchoutpouen et al., 2019; Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2021; Badolo et al., 2022). It is also possible that the high density of Aedes spp. in the city of Sangmelima could have resulted from the massive deforestation in this area over the last decades. Such modification of the natural environment could likely favor the expansion of a species such as Ae. albopictus, which invaded Cameroon recently and is well adapted to well-urbanized settings.

Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus were found to breed in similar habitats and to share larval sites in some instances. Co-occurrence of these species was observed in 26% of the sampled larval habitats, suggesting that while they may interact when sharing containers, the intensity of competition is likely limited and context-dependent. Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting overlapping habitat preferences and potential competition for resources between the two species (Yang et al., 2021; Lizuain et al., 2022). Larvae of Aedes spp. were also found to share their habitats with other mosquitoes, including Culex spp., Lutzia spp., and Anopheles spp. This situation could point to high resource competition between species in the natural environment. Due to their predator status, mosquitoes of some genera, such as Lutzia, could play the role of regulator in these habitats, yet this points to the complex dynamics of mosquitoes in the natural environment.

Larval habitats of Aedes spp. were mainly found close to human habitations. This close association between mosquito larval habitats and human living spaces could expose the population to mosquito infective bites and risk of disease transmission. During the rainy season, the Stegomyia index recorded in Bertoua, Kribi, and Sangmelima greatly exceeded the epidemic risk thresholds defined by the WHO, indicating intense Aedes spp. infestation and a high potential for arboviral transmission. The particularly elevated index values observed in Bertoua and Kribi reflect a high density of productive larval habitats, likely resulting in increased adult mosquito abundance and enhanced human-vector contact. The high Container Index (CI) in Bertoua further suggests widespread availability of water-holding containers, highlighting structural deficiencies in water supply systems, household storage practices, and solid waste management. Although Abong-Mbang exhibited comparatively lower indices, these values remain above WHO alert thresholds and therefore indicate a persistent, albeit reduced, epidemic risk. These findings underscore the urgent need for proactive, seasonally targeted vector control implemented before and during the rainy season, prioritizing the elimination or protection of highly productive peri-domestic breeding sites such as unmanaged plastic containers and also discarded tires. Such control efforts must be operationalized through coordinated community engagement, improved municipal solid waste management, and continuous entomological surveillance to enable early detection of increasing vector densities. Very high infestation levels were reported in urban and peri-urban studies in Yaounde, with Breteau Index (BI) and Container Index (CI) exceeding WHO thresholds, reflecting the influence of dense human population and unplanned urbanization on Aedes spp. proliferation (Tedjou et al., 2020; Djiappi-Tchamen et al., 2021). These findings underscore how local environmental and demographic factors modulate Aedes spp. abundance and the potential for arboviral outbreaks, emphasizing the need for intensified vector surveillance and control, particularly during the rainy season in densely populated urban centers. This points to an increasing risk of arbovirus transmission if no control measure is initiated, as reported for major cities of Cameroon (Monamele and Demanou, 2018; Yousseu et al., 2018).

The present study highlights the wide distribution of Aedes spp. mosquitoes in the forest region and the possible arbovirus disease transmission risk associated with this distribution. The study findings could serve as an urgent call for action for public health authorities and communities to take immediate action against these diseases. While this study provides a comprehensive assessment of Aedes spp. bionomics in the rainforest-urban interface of South Cameroon, some limitations have been identified. Mosquito identification was strictly morphological; although performed by experts, molecular validation would have more precisely identified the 28 unidentified Aedes spp. and potential cryptic species. Additionally, our larval-only focus and use of the dipping method may not fully reflect adult biting densities.

Conclusion

Arboviral diseases, such as dengue, are increasingly important in Africa, highlighting the need for effective interventions. Our study shows that Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus are widely distributed across various container types in urban and forested areas of Cameroon, indicating potential risk for sporadic arboviral transmission. These findings emphasize the importance of targeted community-level interventions, including habitat management and reduction of artificial breeding sites. Furthermore, the observed abundance of these primary arboviral vectors underscores the need to strengthen continuous entomological monitoring to support early detection and control of arboviral vectors and mitigate disease risk.
