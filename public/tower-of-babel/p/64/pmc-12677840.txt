Design and Evaluation of Sticky End‐Type Bivalent DNA Aptamers Containing M08s‐1 as Anticoagulant Agents
Authors: Maasa Yokomori, Mizuki Murasawa, Muneyuki Matsuo, Masanobu Nagano, Keitaro Yoshimoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAptamer is a promising therapeutic modality, offering strong target affinity and target versatility. In the previous studies, DNA aptamers with potent thrombin inhibitory activity, M08s‐1 and its bivalent derivatives, have been developed as novel anticoagulant agents. Among them, the heterobivalent aptamer Pse08‐29 exhibits the most potent ex vivo anticoagulant activity, surpassing the approved drug argatroban. However, its long oligonucleotide chains composed of 106 nucleotides may hinder its drug development due to limited chemical synthetic efficiency. Here, a segmented form of Pse08‐29 composed of two DNA strands, assembled via a sticky end‐type strategy, is developed. The cleavage positions and optimal linker lengths are studied based on the anticoagulant activity and structural stability, revealing that a few cleavage sites within the duplex linker region are acceptable without losing the activity. Surprisingly, one of such aptamers, Stick08‐29(19‐B), shows the significantly enhanced serum stability compared to Pse08‐29, despite the two nick‐like sites existing. Finally, selected bivalent aptamers, consisting of two split DNA strands (<65 mer), exhibit the high anticoagulant activity as well as Pse08‐29. This highlights not only efficient nucleobase reduction with preserved function but also a new design strategy for bivalent aptamers.Keywords: antithrombin aptamer, anticoagulant drugs, bivalent, sticky end

Introduction

In recent years, nucleic acid‐based therapeutics have emerged as a promising third modality in drug development, complementing traditional small molecules and antibody‐based therapies.[ 1 , 2 ] Therapeutic platforms such as mRNA vaccines, small interfering RNAs, and aptamers exhibit high target specificity and tunable chemical properties, enabling their application in personalized medicine and the treatment of refractory diseases.[ 1 , 2 ] The COVID‐19 pandemic has notably accelerated the clinical development and commercialization of nucleic acid therapeutics, particularly mRNA‐based vaccines.[ 1 ] While clinical research on aptamers remains relatively limited, the FDA approval of the retinal disease drug Izervay (avacincaptad pegol) in 2023 represents a significant and further milestone, as it was the second aptamer drug approved nearly two decades after Macugen.[ 3 ] This highlights the renewed potential of aptamer‐based drugs for broader clinical applications. Aptamers, single‐stranded nucleic acids capable of folding into defined 3D structures,[ 4 ] offer binding affinities comparable to monoclonal antibodies.[ 5 ] Moreover, they possess several advantages over antibody‐based drugs, including low immunogenicity, cost‐effective chemical synthesis, enhanced storage stability, and batch‐to‐batch consistency.[ 6 ] These favorable attributes have led to the exploration of aptamers in various therapeutic targets.

Anticoagulation therapy has emerged as one of the most promising avenues for aptamer‐based interventions, especially due to high anticoagulant activity and neutralizing capability.[ 5 , 7 ] Thrombin, a serine protease central to the coagulation cascade, catalyzes the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin and activates multiple procoagulant factors.[ 8 ] Its activity is regulated by two distinct surface regions, exosites I and II, which mediate substrate recognition and allosteric modulation.[ 8 ] Given its pivotal role in thrombogenesis, thrombin represents an attractive target for aptamer‐based inhibition.[ 9 ] In recent years, our research group has developed a novel antithrombin DNA aptamer, M08s‐1,[ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 – 14 ] using exponential enrichment (SELEX) with microbead‐assisted capillary electrophoresis.[ 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 – 18 ] M08s‐1, which binds to exosite I of thrombin, exhibited higher anticoagulant activity than NU172,[ 19 ] an antithrombin aptamer previously reported in clinical trials. Furthermore, bivalent aptamers derived from M08s‐1 demonstrated ≈100‐fold enhanced binding affinity to both human and mouse thrombin compared to its monomeric form.[ 11 ] Intravenous injections of these aptamers into mice resulted in significantly stronger anticoagulant activity compared to NU172 and the clinically used direct thrombin inhibitor, argatroban,[ 20 ] which is a low‐molecular‐weight anticoagulant used in patients with heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia[ 21 ] who cannot use heparin as a primary anticoagulant. Notably, the heterobivalent aptamer Pse08–29,[ 11 ] constructed by rigidly linking exosite I‐bindable M08s‐1 and exosite II‐bindable TBA29[ 22 , 23 ]via a double‐stranded DNA linker, showed the most potent anticoagulant activity under ex vivo conditions. Sequence‐specific hybridization with a complementary oligonucleotide enabled the suppression of the aptamer's activity, indicating its potential utility in mitigating bleeding‐related complications.[ 11 ] This hybridization mechanism of double‐stranded DNA may help reduce bleeding risks during anticoagulant therapy.[ 24 ]

While the bivalent aptamers including Pse08‐29 exhibited enhanced anticoagulant activity, increasing the oligonucleotide chain length presents various challenges in pharmaceutical development and application such as increased manufacturing costs, reduced synthesis efficiency, and difficulties in maintaining structural integrity.[ 25 , 26 – 27 ] Although the coupling yield of phosphoramidite chemistry is generally very high (≥99%), the overall yield decreases as the sequence length increases—for instance, ≈60% for a 106 mer long DNA aptamer, Pse08‐29 (0.995106 × 100).[ 25 ] In addition, the stringent conditions imposed by solid‐phase synthesis can lead to depurination as well as single‐nucleotide deletions or insertions.[ 27 , 28 ] Also, long‐chain aptamers often exhibit complex folding and structural formation, and it can be particularly challenging to maintain their proper folded conformation.[ 29 ] This may lead to reduced specificity and functionality of the aptamer, potentially diminishing the expected therapeutic efficacy.[ 29 ]

In this study, we propose “sticky end‐type” aptamers as an approach to address the challenges associated with long‐chain aptamers. For constructing sticky end‐type aptamers, the bivalent DNA aptamer was split into two fragments with single‐stranded overhangs. These overhangs, called sticky end, allow the fragment to “stick” selectively to another DNA fragment with a complementary sticky end. Sticky ends are widely employed in the construction of DNA origami as a technique to facilitate effective assembly between units, enabling the formation of nanometer‐scale structures.[ 30 , 31 – 32 ] The aim of this study was to obtain a “sticky end‐type” aptamer with anticoagulant activity and serum stability comparable to or greater than those of existing aptamers. The cleavage positions and optimal linker lengths of Pse08‐29 were studied based on the anticoagulant activity and structural stability. The two aptamers used, M08s‐1 and TBA29, have been reported to form unique secondary structures, including G‐quadruplex motifs and duplex regions. These structural features play a crucial role in target binding, and the selection of cleavage positions may affect the structural integrity, making their evaluation important. Furthermore, the linker length influences the stability of the complex and should be optimized from a structural perspective. We successfully developed a sticky end‐type bivalent aptamer with high anticoagulant activity and the significantly enhanced serum stability compared to Pse08‐29.

Results and Discussion

To guide the design of sticky end‐type bivalent aptamers, we focused on the structural integrity of the G‐quadruplex of M08s‐1 and TBA29, which is essential for thrombin binding at exosite I and II, respectively. In particular, the hybrid‐type G‐quadruplex structure has been reported to contribute significantly to aptamer functionality.[ 13 ] In the previous study, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was used to evaluate the conformational states of the aptamers, revealing that truncation destabilized the intramolecular G‐quadruplex and promoted intermolecular aggregation. We hypothesized that the position of the cleavage site could influence the stability of this structure and thus affect the biological activity of the aptamer. Based on this rationale, we performed CD spectroscopic analysis to evaluate how truncation at different positions impacts the G‐quadruplex conformation of M08s‐1 and TBA29. For each sequence, nucleotides were truncated from the terminal end toward the G‐quadruplex region, and the resulting structural stability was analyzed (Figure 1 ). The full‐length M08s‐1 sequence (truncation position 0) and the sequence truncated at position 1 adopted a hybrid‐type G‐quadruplex (G4) structure, consistent with the previously reported findings.[ 13 ] The structural stability of the sequence truncated at position 2 remains unclear. In contrast, the sequences truncated at positions 3 and 4 displayed a parallel‐type G4 structure, characterized by a positive CD peak around 265 nm and a negative peak near 240 nm. Based on these findings, position 2 was selected as the cleavage site threshold for maintaining structural integrity. The previous study suggested that both intramolecular and intermolecular G‐quadruplex structures are populated in solutions.[ 13 ] The CD spectral analyses indicate that truncation up to positions 3 and 4 may destabilize the intramolecular G4 structure, while promoting the formation of an intermolecular G4 conformation. Therefore, it was concluded that the inhibitory activity is likely to decrease. Similarly, evaluation of the stability of the G‐quartet structure in TBA29 confirmed that truncation up to position 3 retained a G‐quartet conformation comparable to that of the full‐length sequence.

Next, we evaluated the potency of the anticoagulant activity of the sticky end‐type aptamers. We investigated the effect of the cleavage sites and linker lengths on aptamer performance. For aptamers with varying linker lengths (13–20 linkers), five different sequence sets were designed with the cleavage site shifted in two‐nucleotide increments (Figure 2 and S1, Supporting Information). The sequences were screened to exclude known immunostimulatory motifs such as CpG dinucleotides (GACGTT and GTCGTT), which are recognized by Toll‐like receptor 9 and known to activate innate immune responses even in double‐stranded contexts.[ 33 , 34 – 35 ] Using these aptamers prepared by hybridizing two sequences, we conducted activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) assays to assess their effects on coagulation activity in both mouse and human plasma. As a control, we used the previously reported aptamer Pse08‐29. Figure 3A shows the relative aPTT of aptamers in human plasma, and Figure 3B shows the relative aPTT in mouse plasma. The relative aPTT was calculated by subtracting the aPTT of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) spiked in plasma from the aPTT of a tested sample spiked in human plasma, representing the extent of aPTT prolongation induced by the aptamer. In both human and mouse plasma, multiple aptamers exhibited greater activity than the control aptamer Pse08‐29.

Focusing on the cleavage sites, it was suggested that B, C, and D, in which the cleavage occurs within the linker region, were favorable with respect to anticoagulant performance. In contrast, cleavage site A, where the cleavage site extends into the functional aptamer sequence, may exhibit reduced activity due to disruption of the active structure. These insights are further supported by our previous structural and functional analysis, including CD spectroscopy and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements.[ 13 ] The G‐quadruplex structure of M08s‐1 is essential for interaction with thrombin's exosite I, and even minor truncations near the G‐quadruplex core—such as deletion of the duplex domain or purine junction—can shift the structural equilibrium toward intermolecular aggregation and impair biological activity. SPR sensor analysis showed that such truncations significantly reduced the equilibrium dissociation constant, reinforcing the link between structural integrity and functional performance. Therefore, maintaining the hybrid‐type G‐quadruplex conformation is critical for aptamer functionality, and in sticky end‐type aptamers, the cleavage site must be carefully chosen to preserve this structural feature. Additionally, cleavage site E with the short complementary region may hinder complex formation during annealing, potentially affecting the aptamer's coagulation activity. Regarding linker length, aptamers with enhanced activity were observed across nearly all linker lengths (Figure S2, Supporting Information). In mouse plasma, aptamers with longer linker lengths tended to exhibit greater anticoagulant activity. Aptamers, i.e., Stick08‐29(13‐E), Stick08‐29(14‐C), Stick08‐29(15‐B), Stick08‐29(17‐D), Stick08‐29(18‐D), Stick08‐29(19‐B), Stick08‐29(19‐C), and Stick08‐29(20‐C), showed significantly higher anticoagulant activity (p < 0.05) compared to Pse08‐29 in both human and mouse plasma.

To confirm complex formation of the sticky end‐type bivalent aptamers via hybridization, we performed an evaluation using Native PAGE (Figure 4 ). For each aptamer, the presence of a single band in electrophoresis indicates successful complex formation, whereas the appearance of two bands suggests that the complex was not formed. Based on the observed band patterns, it was confirmed that longer linker lengths and sticky end regions tend to facilitate complex formation. There are also aptamers such as Stick08‐29(13‐E) and Stick08‐29(18‐D) which exhibited high anticoagulant activity despite the absence of complex formation. This result is particularly intriguing from the perspective of interexosite communication in thrombin. In our previous studies, we demonstrated that aptamer pairs of M08 and TBA29, when coincubated with thrombin, can form ternary complexes and exhibit a positive allosteric effect, in which the binding of one aptamer enhances the association rate of the other.[ 10 , 14 ] Such allosteric effects have also been reported for existing thrombin aptamers.[ 23 , 36 ] From the perspective that clinical trials and regulatory submissions may be simplified, however, sticky end‐type bivalent aptamers could represent a more favorable option for pharmaceutical development compared to the coadministration of individual aptamers.[ 37 ] The complex stabilities of the eight aptamers that exhibited high aPTT values in both human and mouse plasma were also evaluated using NUPACK software,[ 38 ] which provided computational predictions of structure formation probabilities and thermodynamic stability parameters. The NUPACK results showed a similar trend to those observed in the Native PAGE analyses (Figure S3, Supporting Information). According to simulation by NUPACK, the candidate bivalent aptamers were ranked in the order of complex formation ratio as follows: Stick08‐29(19‐B), Stick08‐29(15‐B), Stick08‐29(20‐C), Stick08‐29(19‐C), Stick08‐29(17‐D), Stick08‐29(18‐D), Stick08‐29(14‐C), and Stick08‐29(13‐E).

Evaluation of complex stability of sticky end‐type aptamers by 12% Native PAGE. The red triangles represent aptamers with higher anticoagulant activity than Pse08‐29 in both human and mouse plasma. Blue asterisks represent aptamers with higher anticoagulant activity and complex stability.

Finally, aptamer stability was assessed under simulated physiological conditions. To evaluate the degradation resistance of the aptamers, Stick08‐29(19‐B) and Stick08‐29(20‐C) were selected as promising aptamer candidates based on the results of aPPT assays (Figure 3) and NUPACK data (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Among the eight candidate bivalent aptamers that exhibited high aPTT activity, Stick08‐29(19‐B) was the most promising candidate in terms of both anticoagulant activity and complex stability. As the second‐tier candidates, Stick08‐29(15‐B), Stick08‐29(20‐C), and Stick08‐29(19‐C) were identified based on complex ratio in Figure S3, Supporting Information. Among these three, Stick08‐29(20‐C) exhibited the highest aPTT value and was therefore selected as the second candidate for the experiments.

Stick08‐29(19‐B) and Stick08‐29(20‐C) were incubated at 37 °C in the presence of 50% (v/v) human or mouse serum. Following incubation, the samples were analyzed by urea‐denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Urea PAGE) (Figure 5 ). In Urea PAGE, the mobility of nucleic acids depends solely on their length; thus, nucleic acids cleaved by enzymatic degradation exhibit increased mobility. For Stick08‐29(19‐B), no degradation was observed even after 4 h in either human or mouse serum. In contrast, for Stick08‐29(20‐C), the band at the arrow position had almost completely disappeared after 4 h in both human and mouse serum. Therefore, Stick08‐29(19‐B) was confirmed to have higher serum stability than Stick08‐29(20‐C) in both sera.

Assessment of aptamer degradation in human and mouse serum using Urea PAGE. The aptamers were incubated in 50% (v/v) human or mouse serum at 37 °C for 4 h at a final concentration of 2 μM.

Notably, the enhanced serum stability of Stick08‐29(19‐B), which exhibits a half‐life of ≈4 h compared to ≈30 min for the parent aptamer Pse08‐29,[ 11 ] was achieved with no chemical modifications. This finding highlights the potential of structural design strategies to improve nuclease resistance. One plausible explanation for the high serum stability of Stick08‐29(19‐B) is that the nick site positioned adjacent to the G‐quadruplex domain may sterically hinder enzymatic cleavage.[ 39 ] When bound to thrombin, further stabilization of the local structure of G‐quadruplex may occur, shielding the nick site from nucleolytic attack. These observations suggest that nick designs utilizing structural protection mechanisms may offer a generalizable approach to enhancing aptamer stability without requiring chemical modification. However, it is noteworthy that 20 C exhibited reduced nuclease resistance despite having a nick site located closer to the G‐quadruplex domain compared to Pse08‐29. This result indicates that not only the position of the nick site but also the surrounding sequence context may influence nuclease susceptibility.[ 40 , 41 ] Therefore, both the nick site location and the neighboring sequence environment should be carefully considered in the design of nuclease‐resistant constructs.

Conclusion

In this study, we devised a sticky end‐type bivalent DNA aptamer targeting thrombin. To obtain shorter oligonucleotides as final products from the nucleotide synthesizer, the cleavage positions and linker lengths of anticoagulant bivalent aptamer Pse08‐29 were optimized, resulting in the formation of sticky end‐type aptamers via hybridization. Through aPTT assays, several unique aptamers were found to exhibit anticoagulant activity superior to that of Pse08‐29[ 11 ] which exhibited superior anticoagulant activity compared to both NU172 and argatroban. Since the constructed bivalent aptamers consist of two split short DNA strands (<65 mer), they have advantages in terms of large‐scale synthesis and manufacturing compared to longer aptamer Pse08–29 (106 mer).[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 – 29 ] These findings suggest that the sticky end‐type aptamer‐based therapeutics have the potential to serve as alternatives to conventional anticoagulant drugs. Stick08‐29(19‐B) was identified as the most promising anticoagulant aptamer in this study, owing to its potent activity, complex stability, and serum stability. The presence of a nick near the aptamer domain may facilitate sequence‐specific neutralization by complementary oligonucleotides, and this possibility will be explored in future studies. The promising aptamer is expected to contribute to the development of novel therapeutic approaches and medical technologies.

Experimental Section

Materials

PBS was prepared by dissolving Dulbecco's PBS powder, comprising 4.0 g NaCl, 0.1 g KCl, 0.6 g Na2HPO4, and 0.1 g KH2PO4 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), in 500 mL of ultrapure water. The pH was subsequently adjusted to 7.4. A 0.5 M EDTA solution was prepared by dissolving 93 g of disodium EDTA dihydrate (EDTA·2Na·2H2O; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Japan) and ≈10 g of sodium hydroxide (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Japan) in ultrapure water. The pH was adjusted to 8.0, and the final volume was brought to 500 mL. All oligonucleotides listed in Table S1, Supporting Information, were obtained from Eurofins Genomics K.K. (Japan) with OPC purification grade. Each oligonucleotide was dissolved in PBS to a final concentration of 150 µM. Annealing was performed by heating the solutions to 95 °C for 3 min using a dry bath incubator (Major Science Co., Ltd., Taiwan), followed by controlled cooling at a rate of 0.1 °C s− 1.

CD Spectroscopy

Aptamers were diluted to a final concentration of 2 μM in PBS. Annealing was performed by heating the samples at 95 °C for 2 min using a thermal cycler (Takara Bio Inc., Japan), followed by gradual cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C s− 1. CD spectra were recorded at room temperature using a JASCO J‐1500 spectropolarimeter. Measurements were performed in a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. The spectra were acquired in CD mode with HT detection, over a wavelength range of 320–220 nm, with a resolution of 0.5 nm and a bandwidth of 1.0 nm. The sensitivity was set to 100 mdeg, and the response time was 1 s. Spectra were collected at a scan speed of 200 nm min−1, and each measurement was averaged over three accumulations.

In Vitro Measurement of aPTT in Plasma

aPTT was measured in vitro using human or mouse plasma. A volume of 5 μL of 30 μM aptamer solution was added to 145 μL of normal human plasma (Cosmo Bio Co. Ltd., Japan) or normal mouse plasma (Cosmo Bio Co. Ltd., Japan), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of aPTT reagent (Sysmex Co., Ltd., Japan) was added to 50 μL of the plasma–aptamer mixture, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for an additional 2 min. Finally, 50 μL of 0.025 M calcium chloride solution (Sysmex Co., Ltd., Japan) was added, and the change in light scattering intensity at 660 nm was immediately monitored over time using a fully automated blood coagulation instrument (Sysmex Co., Ltd., Japan) to determine the aPTT.

Evaluation of Sticky End‐Type Aptamer Complex Formation by Native PAGE

Aptamer solutions at a concentration of 4 μM were mixed with 2× DNA loading buffer at a 1:1 ratio and subjected to electrophoresis on a 12% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel (Native PAGE). Following electrophoresis, the bands were visualized using a UV transilluminator (FUSION SOLO S, Vilber Lourmat, France).

Evaluation of Aptamer Stability in Serum

To assess the serum stability of the aptamer, 40 μL of aptamer solution was mixed with 40 μL of either normal human serum (Cosmo Bio Co. Ltd., Japan) or normal mouse serum (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), followed by incubation at 37 °C using an incubator (Ikuta Industry Corp., Japan). After incubation, 20 μL of 100 mM EDTA solution was added to each sample, which was then stored at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, 100 μL of 2× DNA loading buffer for Urea PAGE [containing 8 M urea (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), 2 mM disodium EDTA dihydrate (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Japan), 2 mM Tris, and 75 μM bromophenol blue (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Japan)] was added. The samples were heated at 95 °C for 5 min using a dry bath incubator (Major Science Co., Ltd., Taiwan) and then subjected to electrophoresis on a 12% denaturing polyacrylamide gel containing urea. Band visualization was performed using a UV transilluminator (FUSION SOLO S, Vilber Lourmat, France).
