Results of Achilles Insertional Repair Techniques: Minimally Invasive Compared to Open Repair
Authors: Jorge I. Acevedo, Sofia Rivera, Kelsey A. Crawford, Lauren Barnes, David E. Jaffe, John Chao, Dominic S. Carreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) is a common yet challenging condition that often resists conservative treatment. Traditional open surgical repair carries risk of complications including delayed wound healing and infection. This study compares the outcomes of a minimally invasive surgical (MIS) technique using arthroscopic-assisted double-row repair vs open repair.Methods:Fifty-six patients with IAT underwent surgical intervention between June 2021 and September 2024 (32 MIS, 24 open). Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were assessed pre- and post-operatively with a minimum 1-year follow-up. Six patients were excluded from analysis for comparability between groups. Statistical analysis used 2-tailed t tests with a Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167). The proportion of each cohort reaching the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was compared via χ2 analysis, and complications were correlated to body mass index (BMI) and age using logistic regression analyses. Sensitivity analyses using multiple linear regressions, adjusted for follow-up time, were performed on the full cohort (α = 0.05).Results:The MIS group had significantly worse preoperative scores compared with the open group. But at a mean follow-up of 2.2 years for the MIS group and 2.0 years for the open group, the MIS group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System physical function and visual analog scale scores (P < .001 for both) and a significantly greater proportion of patients at MCID (P < .05 for both). Foot and Ankle Ability Measure activities of daily living subscale showed similar findings without significance detected. All PROs retained significance on sensitivity analysis. Complication rates were 0% in the MIS group and 5.0% in the open group with no correlation to BMI or age.Conclusion:Despite limitations necessitating further studies, our results begin to suggest superior functional outcomes following MIS repair for IAT compared to traditional open repair, supporting MIS as a potentially safer and more effective alternative for appropriate patients.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.

Introduction

Insertional Achilles Tendinopathy (IAT) is a degenerative condition that causes pain and swelling at the attachment of the Achilles to the calcaneus. IAT results from a combination of biomechanical stress and anatomic variations such as increased calcaneal pitch angle and/or calcaneus length. It is frequently associated with Haglund deformity (HD), a posterior-superior calcaneal prominence that may compress the Achilles tendon and retrocalcaneal bursa contributing to IAT symptoms. 1 HD and retrocalcaneal bursitis (RB) form what is commonly referred to as Haglund syndrome (HS).2-4 Systemic comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and steroid use (including oral contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy) can also contribute to the degenerative changes associated with IAT. 5

IAT affects up to 33% of individuals with Achilles-related pain and often presents with tenderness within the distal 2 cm of the Achilles tendon, swelling at the back of the heel, pain that is aggravated by activity, and stiffness associated with prolonged rest.1,2 Initial treatment involves conservative modalities but approximately 40% of patients ultimately require surgical intervention. 6

Traditional open techniques have proven highly effective since first formally described by McGarvey et al, 7 who introduced the central tendon-splitting approach for debridement, Haglund resection, and tendon reattachment using suture anchors. Rigby et al 8 provided one of the earliest clinical validations for suture bridge techniques in IAT, which served to restore the anatomic footprint of the Achilles insertion while providing enough biomechanical stability to allow for a faster rehabilitation timeline. Subsequent studies have consistently demonstrated favorable outcomes with this approach, with most patients experiencing durable pain relief and a return to regular shoe wear and activities. 9 Further, studies focused on the biomechanical properties of the Achilles tendon collectively support the necessity of repair when the tendon is significantly detached to ensure mechanical integrity and optimize clinical outcomes.10,11

Open repair techniques, although effective, involve a central tendon-splitting incision and open debridement, which carry risks of delayed wound healing, nerve injury, infection, painful scarring, and prolonged rehabilitation.2,12,13 These limitations have spurred interest in minimally invasive surgical (MIS) approaches, which aim to reduce soft tissue trauma, accelerate recovery, and minimize complications. 3 Endoscopic calcaneoplasty and percutaneous repairs have demonstrated favorable outcomes, including shorter recovery periods, fewer complications, and high patient satisfaction.3,14-16

This study aims to compare the patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and complication rates between an MIS technique, combining endoscopic Haglund resection with percutaneous double-row Achilles repair, and traditional open repair in the surgical management of IAT.

Methods

A prospective cohort study was conducted at 3 institutions between June 2021 and September 2024. Informed consent was obtained before patients were entered into the data registry. The storage of data was granted according to institutional requirements. Institutional review board approval (Piedmont Healthcare Institutional Review Board, Atlanta, GA) was granted to review the deidentified data registry. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were diagnosed with IAT based on clinical examination, confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging, and their symptoms had not improved with at least 6 months of conservative therapy. One-year minimum follow-up was required. Exclusion criteria included prior Achilles surgery or significant medical comorbidities that contraindicated surgery.

Patients were assigned to treatment groups based on the performing surgeon and institution, with 32 patients undergoing minimally invasive repair and 24 patients undergoing traditional open double-row repair. The surgeons in this study did not elect to perform arthroscopic or open surgery based on patient demographics; each surgeon performs this surgery either open or arthroscopically. The MIS technique combined endoscopic resection of HD with percutaneous double-row Achilles tendon repair and a rip-stop suture, whereas the open group underwent standard double-row repair.

Functional outcomes were assessed using Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System physical function (PROMIS PF), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure activities of daily living subscale (FAAM ADL), and the visual analog scale (VAS) pre- and post-operatively. PROMIS PF was developed for a US population mean score of 50, with higher scores indicating better physical function. FAAM ADL is a 0%-100% score, with higher scores indicating improved function in activities of daily living. The VAS scale is a continuous scale (0-10), with lower scores indicating decreased pain. No a priori primary outcome was designated.

Six total patients (2 MIS and 4 open) with a follow-up of exactly 365 days were excluded from primary data analysis to ensure comparability of follow-up time between groups. Functional recovery is time-dependent, and these patients had substantially shorter follow-up than the rest of the cohort, thereby affecting group means. The decision to exclude these patients was based on distributional inspection of follow-up times and applied uniformly across the 2 groups.

Demographic analysis was conducted using independent 2-tailed t tests for age and body mass index (BMI), χ2 tests for gender and tobacco use, and a Fisher exact test for diabetes. Exploratory statistical analysis was performed using 2-tailed paired t tests to compare pre- and postoperative scores within groups, and independent 2-tailed t tests to compare scores between groups and the change in scores pre- to post-operatively between groups. To adjust significance level for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni adjustment was conducted (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167). Hedges g was chosen as a bias-corrected effect size measure optimized for unequal and/or small sample sizes. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) scores were calculated using a distribution-based method (0.5 × SD of ∆ in PROs). 17 The proportion of each cohort that met the MCID were calculated and compared via χ2 analyses in order to bridge statistical significance with clinical relevance (α = 0.05). Complications including wound dehiscence, infection, and reoperation, were recorded and correlated to BMI and age using logistic regression analyses.

Given the exclusion of 6 patients, we ran sensitivity analyses in the form of multiple linear regressions for each PRO using the full cohort, adjusted for follow-up time, in order to confirm robustness of our primary analyses (α = 0.05).

MIS Surgical Technique and Postoperative Protocol

Patients were positioned prone under spinal or general anesthesia with a regional block. Four arthroscopic portals were drawn at the 2-, 5-, 7-, and 10-o’clock positions when viewing the tendon posteriorly (Figure 1). A posterior scope and a burr were employed through the distal portals to visualize the resection of the HD and calcaneal spur, as well as the debridement of soft tissue and any tendon calcifications as needed. Intraoperative fluoroscopy was used to verify appropriate bone resection and removal of all tendon calcification (Figure 2). Using fluoroscopic guidance, knotless suture anchors were then placed in a double-row configuration to reattach the Achilles tendon to the calcaneus (Figure 3). This technique includes the addition of a knotless rip-stop suture to the proximal anchors as shown in Figure 4.

Patients were placed in a short-leg splint following surgical intervention. At their 2-week postoperative appointment, sutures were removed and patients were advised to remain non-weightbearing in a tall controlled ankle motion boot with 2 Achilles wedges. Patients were advised to transition to weightbearing as tolerated at week 3 and began physical therapy at week 4 postoperative. Patients were advised to transition to stiff-soled shoes with ½-in. heel lifts by week 6 to 8, and to stiff-soled shoes with no lift by week 12.

Open Surgical Technique and Postoperative Protocol

The open repair technique involved a longitudinal midline incision, release of the entire Achilles insertion, open resection of HD, tendon debridement, and double-row repair using suture anchors (Figure 5).

(A) Open procedure release of the Achilles tendon off of the calcaneal insertion and (B) resection of the calcaneus and retrocalcaneal bursa.

At their 2-week postoperative appointment, sutures were removed and patients were advised to remain non-weightbearing in a tall controlled ankle motion boot with 3 Achilles wedges. Patients were advised to transition to weightbearing as tolerated and began physical therapy at week 3 postoperative. Patients were advised to wean out of the wedges as tolerated, to transition to stiff-soled shoes with ½-in. heel lifts by week 6 to 8, and to stiff-soled shoes with no lift by week 12.

Results

A total of 56 patients were included in the study, with 32 patients undergoing MIS repair and 24 patients undergoing open repair. Fifty patients (30 MIS, 20 open) were included in data analysis. The two groups were comparable in terms of age and tobacco use; however, the MIS cohort had a significantly higher average BMI compared with the open cohort (35.2 ± 6.9 vs 29.8 ± 4.0, P < .01). The MIS cohort also had significantly more female patients than the open group (76.7% vs 45%, P < .05) (Table 1). The mean follow-up duration was 2.2 years (range = 1.1-3.5 years) for the MIS group and 2.0 years (range = 1.2-3.6 years) for the open repair group. Although all of the MIS patients provided complete follow-up data, 2 of the open patients did not provide PROMIS scores, and 3 did not provide VAS scores at follow-up. These patients were excluded from the relevant analyses.

Patients in both the MIS and open group demonstrated statistically significant improvement in PROMIS PF scores. MIS patients increased from a mean of 28.1 preoperatively to 53.4 at final follow-up (P < .001), and open patients increased from a mean of 39.2 to 47.7 (P < .001). Although there was a significant difference between preoperative PROMIS PF for the MIS and open cohorts (P < .001), the MIS group showed significantly greater improvement in PROMIS PF scores compared to the open group (∆ = 25.4 ± 16.2 vs ∆ = 8.5 ± 8.9, respectively), P < .001, g = 1.19 (95% CI 0.57, 1.82), with a significantly greater proportion of MIS patients achieving the PROMIS PF MCID at follow-up than open patients, χ2(1) = 4.02, P < .05, OR = 4.50 (95% CI 0.96, 21.1) (Table 2, Figure 6). Both groups also demonstrated statistically significant improvement in VAS scores. The MIS group improved from a mean VAS score of 7.9 to 1.3 (P < .001), and the open group improved from a mean of 5.6 to 2.1 (P < .001). There was also a significant difference between preoperative VAS for the MIS and the open cohorts (P < .001), and the MIS group showed significantly greater improvement in VAS scores compared to the open group (∆ = 6.7 ± 2.4 vs ∆ = 3.4 ± 2.5, respectively), P < .001, g = 1.33 (95% CI 0.69, 1.98), with a significantly greater proportion of MIS patients achieving the VAS MCID at follow-up than open patients, χ2(1) = 6.63, P < .05, OR = 12.1 (95% CI 1.27, 115) (Table 2, Figure 7). Additionally, both groups showed significant improvements in FAAM ADL scores. The MIS group improved from a mean FAAM ADL score of 34.8 to 83.7 (P < .001), and the open group improved from a mean of 54.7 to 83.5 (P < .001). As with the other PROs, there was a significant difference between preoperative FAAM ADL scores for the MIS and open cohorts (P < .01). The MIS group showed greater improvement in FAAM ADL scores than the open group (∆ = 49.0 ± 28.0 vs ∆ = 28.7 ± 30.0, respectively), and a greater percentage of patients at the FAAM ADL MCID on follow-up. With the numbers available, no significant difference could be detected: P = .019, g = 0.69 (95% CI 0.12, 1.27), and χ2(1) = 2.08, P = .149, OR = 2.79 (95% CI 0.67, 11.6), respectively (Table 2, Figure 8).

There were no complications in the MIS group, whereas the open group had a complication rate of 5.0% (1/20): 1 case of surgical wound dehiscence that was resolved with local wound care and oral antibiotics. With the numbers available, no significant difference could be detected in wound complications between groups: χ2(1) = 1.53, P = .216, Cramér V = 0.17. Further, no significant association could be detected between wound complications and BMI or age across the entire cohort: OR = 1.10 (95% CI 0.83-1.44), P = .53, and OR = 0.89 (95% CI 0.68-1.16), P = .34, respectively.

Discussion

Open surgical repair, when performed with adequate exposure and stable fixation, has shown to be a reliable and effective treatment option for IAT, even in the case of complete tendon detachment. 11 Rigby et al 8 reported that 97.6% of patients returned to regular shoe wear and activities of daily living, whereas Nunley et al 10 reported 96% long-term satisfaction, no loss of plantarflexion strength, and durable pain relief. Despite these findings, there is a concern for risks of delayed wound healing, nerve injury, infection, painful scarring, and prolonged rehabilitation.

Several studies have shown similarly favorable outcomes in treating HD and associated RB with minimally invasive endoscopic calcaneoplasty. Notably, however, these studies have consistently demonstrated reduced wound complications, faster recovery, and higher patient satisfaction when compared to open techniques.2,3,14-16,18 Miller et al 19 provided a detailed description of a technique that mirrors the approach used in our MIS cohort. They employed 4 endoscopic portals for complete retrocalcaneal debridement followed by a percutaneous double-row Achilles reattachment. In their case series of >75 patients, there were no instances of wound complications, and patients returned to protected weightbearing by postoperative day 10. The major difference in our MIS technique is the added strength of the rip-stop sutures.

Ferranti et al 18 similarly reported excellent outcomes using percutaneous calcaneoplasty in patients with HD and mild to moderate IAT. They found significant improvement in both VAS and Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment–Achilles (VISA-A) scores and reported no wound complications, with 84.5% of patients expressing complete satisfaction with their outcomes. Although their study did not include double-row reattachment, it further supports the trend toward lower complication rates and improved early recovery associated with minimally invasive procedures.

Lee and Lee 14 demonstrated that endoscopic calcaneoplasty provided significant pain relief and functional recovery in patients with IAT, with no reported wound complications. Their protocol allowed early weightbearing and return to ambulation within 2 weeks, underscoring the value of preserving soft tissue integrity through smaller portals. Similarly, Hall et al 2 presented a comprehensive review of endoscopic and percutaneous strategies for HS and IAT, highlighting reduced wound complication rates, faster recovery, and better cosmetic outcomes compared with open techniques. Notably, they emphasized the utility of these techniques in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes or smoking, where traditional open approaches carry elevated risks for wound breakdown. Additionally, when soft tissue trauma is minimized, the unique mechanical properties of the Achilles tendon may be optimized, including its role in elastic energy storage and efficient force transfer during gait. 20 In other words, minimally invasive approaches may preserve native tendon structure, which may contribute to improved tendon healing and load transmission.

The present study builds on the literature, introducing a minimally invasive technique combining endoscopic resection of HD and percutaneous double-row Achilles tendon repair with a rip-stop suture. Vora et al 21 demonstrated that a rip-stop, double-row repair construct had significantly greater load to failure and less gapping under cyclic loading compared with standard or modified double-row repair. These biomechanical advantages justify our incorporation of a rip-stop element in the MIS technique and likely contributed to the favorable outcomes and absence of tendon failure in the group that underwent minimally invasive repair.

Although all patients improved significantly in PROs on postoperative follow-up, the MIS group improved significantly more than the open group for PROMIS PF and VAS specifically. Our sensitivity analyses, done to evaluate the potential impact of excluding 6 patients to ensure comparability between groups, made clear that the exclusion did not bias the results and confirmed the robustness of our findings. A limitation of these results is that patients in the MIS cohort had significantly lower preoperative PROs compared with the MIS cohort, which may weaken the conclusions drawn by the highly significant change scores for the MIS cohort. This limitation is likely due to chance, as MIS vs open surgical selection was not completed on an individualized patient basis. Despite this, the conclusion that the MIS patients achieved superior pain and function outcomes is further supported by the MCID analysis. The calculated MCID (0.5 × SD ∆ in PRO) value was cohort specific, which provides a critical advantage for comparative analysis. The MCID indicates a clinically relevant improvement in symptoms relative to baseline, and a significantly higher proportion of the MIS cohort achieved the PROMIS PF and VAS MCID compared with the open cohort. Our findings align with and build on previous clinical and biomechanical literature advocating for minimally invasive approaches for IAT management.

Limitations of this study include its nonrandomized design, lack of a priori primary outcome designation, and a relatively small sample size lacking a priori sample size calculation. Patients were assigned to the treatment group based on the treating physician, and therefore, differences in surgical technique may affect outcomes. A limitation of the data is the statistically significant difference in preoperative PROs between cohorts. Additionally, using a distribution-based method for the MCID analysis only defines “the minimum value below which a change in pain score is hopefully not due to measurement error.” 22 The election to exclude 6 patients from the analysis because of short follow-up time may also cause concern for selection bias. However, given that the choice of surgery was surgeon-specific, rather than based on patient demographic or progression of IAT, and that our secondary analyses confirmed our findings, we believe our results build on the current literature despite these limitations. Future randomized controlled studies with larger cohorts and longer-term follow-up are necessary to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Minimally invasive Achilles repair, combining percutaneous suture techniques and endoscopic calcaneoplasty, may be a safe and effective alternative to open surgery for patients with IAT. Despite limitations, our results suggest that the MIS technique can offer superior functional recovery and decreased pain as compared to the open technique. These findings offer support for the possible adoption of MIS procedures for the surgical management of IAT.
