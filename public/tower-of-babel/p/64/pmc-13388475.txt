Model‐Informed Evaluation of Hydroxyurea Exposure During Lactation
Authors: Anhar Hosawi, Kei Irie, Min Dong, Julie Ware, Russell Ware, Tomoyuki Mizuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydroxyurea is a cornerstone therapy for sickle cell anemia; however, evidence guiding its use during lactation remains limited. This study aimed to develop a population pharmacokinetic (PK) model to characterize hydroxyurea disposition in maternal plasma and breast milk, and to quantify infant exposure under clinically relevant breastfeeding scenarios. Population PK modeling was conducted using data from the HELPS study (NCT02990598) with nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling. Maternal plasma PK parameters were first estimated using observed plasma concentration data, followed by estimation of parameters characterizing drug transfer into breast milk. Model‐based stochastic simulations were performed across infant ages 0–9 months to evaluate exposure, expressed as the World Health Organization (WHO)‐recommended relative infant dose (RID), with 10% as the consensus safety threshold. The model diagnostics demonstrated that both plasma and breast milk PK were adequately described by the model. Simulation analyses demonstrated that the estimated RID remained below 10% across all evaluated infant age groups. This integrated plasma‐breast milk population PK model provides a quantitative framework to assess lactational drug exposure. The findings suggest that infant exposure to hydroxyurea through breastfeeding is unlikely to exceed the 10% safety threshold across all age groups (0–9 months), supporting continued maternal therapy during lactation with appropriate clinical oversight.

Sickle cell anemia (SCA) is a life‐threatening inherited blood disorder affecting approximately 100,000 individuals in the United States and millions worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 It is associated with severe clinical complications, including multi‐organ failure, vaso‐occlusive pain crisis, stroke, and premature mortality. 1 , 2 , 4 Advances in medical care have substantially improved survival, increasing life expectancy from approximately 10 years in the 1960s to over 45 years in the 2000s. 1 , 5 , 6 With improved survival, SCA has evolved into a chronic condition of adulthood, and parenthood has become an attainable life goal for many patients. 1 Consequently, reproductive health has emerged as an essential component of comprehensive SCA care. 1

Breastfeeding is the normative standard for infant feeding and provides a balanced source of nutrients, as well as immunologic and bioactive compounds that support growth, immune protection, and neurodevelopment. 7 , 8 , 9 In contrast, infant formula does not contain many of these human milk‐derived bioactive factors and, compared with breastfeeding, has been associated with higher infectious morbidity and adverse long‐term health outcomes across some studies. 10 The World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend exclusive breastfeeding for approximately the first 6 months of life, 11 , 12 followed by continued breastfeeding with appropriate complementary foods for up to 2 years of age or longer, as mutually desired. 7 , 8 , 9 , 11 , 12 In regions with a high prevalence of SCA, including Sub‐Saharan Africa, where approximately three‐quarters of affected births occur, breastfeeding represents an accessible nutritional strategy, particularly in settings affected by food insecurity. Food insecurity remains a major public health concern globally. 1 , 12

Hydroxyurea is the primary disease‐modifying therapy for SCA and is approved by both the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA). 1 , 2 , 11 , 13 Extensive clinical evidence over the past 25 years has established its safety and efficacy, leading to recommendations for initiation as early as 9 months of age. 4 , 11 , 14 By increasing fetal hemoglobin (HbF) production and reducing inflammation, hydroxyurea reduces vaso‐occlusive events, hospitalizations, and mortality. 1 , 13 , 15 Despite these benefits, concerns regarding potential fetal harm based on animal studies have limited its use during pregnancy. 16 It is often discontinued during lactation because of theoretical safety concerns and limited data on infant exposure through breast milk. 1 , 11 , 13 However, withholding hydroxyurea may increase morbidity risk in lactating women with SCA. 11

The HELPS study (NCT02990598) reported that estimated infant exposure remained below the WHO‐recommended 10% relative infant dose (RID) threshold for safety under standard assumptions of milk intake. 11 However, the extent to which variability in breastfeeding patterns, milk intake, and dosing timing may influence infant exposure has not been systematically evaluated. To address this gap, we developed a population PK model incorporating an integrated breastmilk compartment to characterize the hydroxyurea concentration–time profiles in maternal plasma and breast milk. This model enables quantitative assessment of how variations in breastfeeding frequency and the timing of drug administration influence infant exposure. By extending beyond fixed assumptions, this model‐informed framework supports evidence‐based decision‐making regarding continuation of hydroxyurea therapy during lactation.

METHODS

Patients and sampling

Data from the prospective Hydroxyurea Exposure in Lactation Study (HELPS; NCT02990598) were used for model development. 11 The HELPS study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board. A total of 101 plasma and 93 breast milk hydroxyurea concentrations were obtained from 16 lactating women who received a single oral dose of 1000 mg hydroxyurea (approximately 15–20 mg/kg). Baseline plasma and breast milk samples were collected prior to dosing, followed by serial post‐dose sampling. Seven subjects provided plasma samples at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 hours after dosing. Milk samples from these subjects were collected at the same timepoints, with an additional sample at 12 hours. Five subjects provided both plasma and milk samples at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 25 hours after dosing. Two subjects provided plasma and milk samples at 3, 6, 9, and 25 hours after dosing. For the remaining two subjects, only milk samples were collected. Sampling times were 3, 6, 8, and 24 hours for one subject, and 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 24 hours for the other. Hydroxyurea concentrations in plasma and breast milk were measured using validated high‐performance liquid chromatography methods as previously described. 11

Population PK modeling

Population PK analysis was conducted using nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling with NONMEM version 7.5.0 (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA). First‐order conditional estimation with interaction (FOCE‐I) was employed to estimate structural PK parameters, inter‐individual variability (IIV), and residual variability (RV).

Plasma concentrations were modeled first with compartmental models to characterize systemic disposition. Lag‐time and transit‐compartment models were evaluated to describe potential delay in hydroxyurea absorption. A breast milk compartment was subsequently integrated into the plasma model to characterize drug transfer into milk, estimating milk volume and inter‐compartmental clearance (Figure 1 ).

Covariate analysis

Body weight was evaluated as a covariate using allometric scaling, with theoretical exponents of 0.75 for apparent clearance (CL) and intercompartmental clearance (Q), and 1 for volumes of distribution of the central compartment (V) and peripheral compartment (V2), standardized to 70 kg. A reduction in the objective function value (OFV) of 3.84 (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant to include body weight in the final model.

Model evaluation

Model selection was based on convergence, parameter plausibility, and goodness‐of‐fit diagnostics. Parameter precision and stability were assessed using nonparametric bootstrap analysis (1000 replicates). The final model was considered stable if bootstrap median parameter estimates were within 10% of the final estimates and 95% confidence intervals excluded zero. Predictive performance was evaluated using visual predictive checks (1000 simulations).

Model‐informed simulation analysis

Monte Carlo simulations were performed using NONMEM to evaluate lactation scenarios across infant age groups (0–9 months old). A virtual population of 1000 lactating women was generated using demographic distributions from NHANES to simulate hydroxyurea concentrations in breast milk at a standard dose of 20 mg/kg/day. 18 , 19 The dose was assumed to be administered at time zero, and hydroxyurea milk concentrations were predicted over the 24 hours following dose administration. An age range of 18–45 years was selected to represent women of childbearing age with SCA. The body weight range was chosen to be consistent with the HELPS study population, as the participants were breastfeeding mothers and therefore expected to have body weights representative of this group. 11

Model‐informed simulations

Simulated milk intake increased from birth, reaching a peak of approximately 152.6 mL/kg/day at 19.7 days of age, followed by a gradual decline with increasing infant age. Predicted infant hydroxyurea exposure mirrored this trajectory. Estimated infant exposure increased during the early postnatal period in parallel with rising milk intake, peaked (median: 0.76 mg/kg/day) near the time of maximal milk consumption, and subsequently declined as milk intake and feeding frequency decreased (Figure 5 and Table 2 ).

DISCUSSION

This study presents the first population PK model incorporating a dedicated breast milk compartment to characterize hydroxyurea PK during lactation. The model accounted for inter‐individual variabilities in hydroxyurea PK parameters among lactating women. Body weight was identified as a significant covariate influencing clearance and volume of distribution, consistent with previously published population PK models. 4 , 26 , 27

The estimated apparent CL (8.57 L/h/70 kg) is consistent with the previously reported oral clearance of 73.16 mL/min/m2 by Rodriguez et al., corresponding to approximately 7.6–7.9 L/h for a typical adult body surface area (1.73–1.8 m2). 28 We therefore interpret our CL estimate as physiologically plausible and within the range of published values, while acknowledging that inclusion of renal function markers could further refine the inter‐individual variability in the clearance estimate.

Hydroxyurea has a reported volume of distribution that approximates total body water. 29 A population PK study in patients with SCA reported a volume of distribution of 45.3 L/70 kg. 26 In addition, a population PK study in cancer patients described the volume of distribution as V = 0.186 × (body weight) + 25.4 L, corresponding to approximately 38.4 L/70 kg. 30 The volume of distribution estimated in our model (39 L/70 kg) is consistent with previously reported values.

Our model‐estimated Ka (2.5 h−1) falls within the reported range of approximately 2–3 h−1, indicating rapid oral absorption. 26 , 30

Our model indicated rapid equilibrium between the plasma and milk compartments (CL plasma‐milk = 2.26 L/h), consistent with what was reported in the HELPS study. 11

The model was utilized to estimate infant exposure to hydroxyurea through simulations of clinically relevant lactation scenarios. In our simulations, daily milk intake and feeding frequency were informed by the weight‐normalized human milk intake (WHMI) curve reported by Yeung et al. 20 , 31 This model predicts peak milk intake of approximately 152.6 mL/kg/day at around 20 days of age, followed by a gradual decline, reflecting early postnatal feeding physiology. Epidemiological data further suggest that milk volume increases during early infancy, followed by a plateau through approximately 6 months and subsequent reductions as complementary feeding is introduced. 32 The simulated milk intake trajectories fell within these reported epidemiologic patterns.

Simulation results showed that the RID of infant's hydroxyurea dose (median range: 0.27–0.76 mg/kg/day) remained below the generally accepted safety threshold of 10% even under worst‐case conditions. These findings are consistent with a recent physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling study that evaluated hydroxyurea disposition in lactating women and breastfed infants using a mechanistic maternal–infant framework, with predicted RID values remaining below the 10% threshold. 33 The concordance between our PopPK simulations and the PBPK‐based predictions supports that hydroxyurea exposure through breastfeeding is low and may be continued during lactation under appropriate clinical supervision and strengthens the evidence base for informed dosing and breastfeeding recommendations in women with SCA receiving hydroxyurea therapy.

Currently, the FDA label for hydroxyurea advises discontinuing breastfeeding. The results of our study suggest that infant exposure through breast milk is below the recommended 10% RID threshold across all infants aged 0–9 months. These findings provide quantitative, model‐informed evidence that may support a reevaluation of current labeling. In particular, integrating such data with emerging clinical evidence could inform a more nuanced, risk‐based approach to lactation recommendations, rather than defaulting to discontinuation of breastfeeding. From a clinical implementation perspective, one of the major remaining uncertainties is the lack of clinical PK data in breastfed infants, particularly those younger than 6 months of age. Additional data characterizing systemic exposure in breastfed infants would help reduce this uncertainty and support future clinical decision‐making and regulatory discussions. Pediatric physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling may complement these efforts by translating the observed breast milk exposure data into age‐specific predictions of infant systemic exposure and helping inform the design and interpretation of future clinical studies.

A key limitation of this study is the absence of renal function data. Because hydroxyurea is primarily eliminated renally and patients with SCA may experience renal impairment, assumptions of normal renal function may have influenced clearance estimates in some individuals. Future studies incorporating renal biomarkers are warranted to refine exposure predictions and further characterize variability in lactating populations.

CONCLUSION

We developed a population PK model describing hydroxyurea disposition in plasma and breast milk during lactation. Model‐informed simulations indicate that infant exposure remains below the 10% relative infant dose safety threshold across all evaluated infant age groups. This framework supports quantitative risk assessment and informed clinical decision‐making regarding hydroxyurea therapy during lactation.
