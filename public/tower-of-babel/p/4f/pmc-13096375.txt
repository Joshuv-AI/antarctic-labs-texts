Breakup of strong cratonic lithosphere causes extensive magmatism at continental margins
Authors: Shi Wang, Wei Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extensive magmatism and Large Igneous Provinces (LIPs) may form at continental margins during continental breakup. While high mantle temperatures and geochemical fertility (such as plume activity) are typically considered the causes of magmatic generation, the role of lithospheric strength remains underexplored. Here, we employ two-dimensional thermomechanical models to reveal that rifting within strong cratonic lithosphere can trigger a pronounced syn-breakup magmatic surge, which may be responsible for the formation of volcanic margins and even LIPs characterized by syn-breakup magmatism. This surge results from enhanced asthenospheric upwelling driven by the collapse of elevated rift flanks, a process that occurs only when lithospheric strength is sufficiently high. Multidisciplinary evidence from the Labrador Sea-Baffin Bay rift system supports this mechanism, linking its excessive magmatism to high lithospheric strength. This framework demonstrates that such excessive magmatism does not require an anomalously hot mantle or special mantle fertility, offering a new perspective on the genesis of volcanism and LIPs along continental margins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42222-7.

Introduction

Continental rifting and breakup exhibit pronounced variability in associated magmatism, ranging from magma-poor margins to highly magmatic volcanic margins. Volcanic margins are characterized by extrusive rocks that form seaward-dipping reflectors (SDRs) and by high-velocity lower crustal bodies (HVLCs), which are interpreted as intrusions or underplating1; e.g., the central Atlantic U.S. margins2. In contrast, non-volcanic margins show little or no volcanism, featuring exhumed mantle and hyper-extended crust, e.g., the Iberia-Newfoundland conjugate margins3,4. Many volcanic margins are spatially and temporally associated with Large Igneous Provinces (LIPs)—short-lived events that emplace large volumes of magma (> 105 km3) within a few million years5. The connection between onshore LIPs and offshore marginal volcanism supports a genetic link between LIP emplacement and continental breakup.

Rift-related magmatism results from decompressional melting of the sub-lithospheric mantle, which is, to first order, controlled by the temperature of the mantle6. This has led to the conventional view that excessive magmatism associated with volcanic margins represents the influence of hot mantle plumes7,8. Large-scale magmatism, and possibly associated mantle plumes, play an important role in causing or facilitating continental breakup9. However, the timing, magnitude, and geodynamic role of plume involvement appear to vary substantially across tectonic settings and breakup stages10,11. The plume-centered paradigm has been increasingly challenged by (1) volcanic margins and LIPs located far away from any known plumes (e.g., the central Atlantic U.S. margins and the related Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP)12,13; (2) normal igneous crustal thickness following breakup implying normal mantle temperature14; and (3) abrupt transitions between magma-rich and magma-poor domains15,16. The extreme lateral extent of some LIPs, such as CAMP, which spans over 10,000 km13, is also difficult to reconcile with a plume head, which has a typical diameter of 2,000–2,500 km8. Therefore, non-plume mechanisms have been proposed to explain excessive magmatism during rifting, including: (1) fertile and/or wet mantle composition17,18 caused by subduction metasomatism and water fluxes into the mantle19,20; (2) active upwelling21; (3) continental insulation raising mantle temperature22; (4) small-scale convection12,23–25; and (5) depth-dependent extension14.

Besides the above causes of volcanic margins, it is well established that the rheological structure of the lithosphere controls the deformation style and rift architecture during continental breakup26–28. Lithospheric strength also governs vertical motions, such as rift shoulder uplift during extension29. However, the effects of lithospheric strength on magma generation remain largely underexplored. Here, we use thermomechanical numerical models to investigate how lithospheric strength modulates magmatism during continental breakup. Our models reveal a previously unrecognized syn-breakup surge in magma production driven by the collapse of rift shoulders, which arises only in cases with a strong, cratonic lithosphere with sufficiently large effective elastic thickness (Te). Our results provide a novel mechanism to explain voluminous breakup-related magmatism. Geological analogues of this mechanism are likely rare because it involves rifting through strong lithosphere and syn-breakup magmatism. The Labrador Sea–Baffin Bay rift system provides a compelling example.

Model setting

The model domain is 940 km wide and 384 km deep, discretized into 240 × 96 elements, which gives ~ 4 km resolution (Supporting Information Fig. S1). To simulate lithospheric extension, we impose symmetric horizontal outflow velocities of 10 mm/yr (half-rate) on both lateral boundaries, balanced by inflow at the bottom boundary to conserve the mass. Thickened crust by 4 km is used as a weak seed to initiate the rift process in the middle of the model domain (Supporting Information Fig. S1). The initial thermal structure is a steady-state conductive geotherm in the lithosphere with radioactive heat generation in the crust considered, and adiabatic in the underlying asthenosphere. Melt generation is parameterized following Katz et al. (2003)30, which explicitly relates melt fraction to temperature and pressure. Melt extraction is assumed to be instantaneous and complete, with emplacement at the surface forming igneous crust without lateral transport. Although this simplified treatment limits the model’s ability to reproduce the exact surface distribution of magmatism, it captures the primary characteristics of mantle melt production.

Two rifting modes with distinct magmatic generation

Two endmember models are employed to illustrate the distinct rifting and magmatism modes governed by lithospheric strength: (a) a rift with steady melt production for a typical orogenic continental lithosphere (Case AW1), and (b) a volcanic rift with a syn-breakup surge in magma production for a strong cratonic lithosphere (Case BS3). After the model simulation begins, extension initiates as distributed deformation within the ductile lower crust, delaying crustal thinning (Fig. 1a). The mantle lithosphere breaks at ~ 4 Myr via ductile shear zones, while the crust remains thick (Fig. 1b). Final crustal breakup occurs at 15 Myr, producing a wide, asymmetric margin (Fig. 1c). The presence of a ductile lower crust suppresses surface topography throughout the rifting process; therefore, no rift shoulders are formed during the extension. Magmatism increases gradually; igneous crustal thickness reaches 5 km at mantle breakup and stabilizes at 6–7 km as it reaches a steady-state oceanic spreading stage, consistent with the global average oceanic crustal thickness (Fig. 2a).

Case BS3 simulates the rifting of a strong cratonic lithosphere, which is 210 km thick with a strong, coupled lower crust and strong mantle lithosphere (fm = 10). As the model evolves, extension localizes rapidly along shear zones cutting through the whole lithosphere, producing a narrow graben flanked by high rift shoulders (Fig. 1d). Rapid, coupled thinning of the crust and mantle leads to mantle lithosphere breakup at 7.88 Myr, closely followed by crustal breakup at 8.75 Myr, forming a narrow margin (Fig. 1e and f). Rapid breakup is accompanied by strong asthenospheric upwelling, peaking at 600 mm/yr—60 times the extension rate (Fig. 1e). A syn-breakup surge in magma productivity occurred, caused by the combined effect of shallow melting permitted by quick lithospheric thinning and strong mantle upwelling. This sharp, transient increase in magma productivity, with igneous crustal thickness reaching 30 km, quickly wanes to steady-state normal oceanic crust thickness in about 0.5 Myr (Fig. 2d).

The magmatic surge is primarily caused by the high lithospheric strength. Notably, this magmatic surge and mantle upwelling are accompanied by a sudden subsidence of rift shoulders. As the strong lithosphere breaks up, elevated rift flanks—previously supported by large flexural rigidity—lose support and subside at rates up to 5 mm/yr (Fig. 1e and Supporting Information Fig. S2). This vertical motion drives focused asthenospheric counterflow into the decompression melting zone, intensifying melt production. Sensitivity tests including hillslope erosion indicate that moderate erosion substantially attenuates the rift shoulder relief but does not strongly affect melt production (Supporting Information Fig. S3), suggesting that this mechanism is robust to moderate erosion, although fully coupled landscape–tectonic feedbacks remain beyond the scope of this study. Thus, the high lithospheric strength not only controls deformation style, but also amplifies syn-breakup melt production through enhanced asthenospheric flow.

Key parameters controlling magmatic surges

Further parameter tests confirm that a strong lithosphere favors the magmatic surge. We vary three parameters controlling lithospheric strength, namely the lithospheric thickness, the lower crust rheologies and the viscosity pre-factor of lithospheric mantle \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{m}$$\end{document} (See Supporting Information Table S1 for detailed parameters for all cases). The results show that a stronger crust and a thicker, stronger lithospheric mantle—all contributing to higher overall lithospheric strength—lead to greater volcanic volumes of magmatic surges (Figs. 2 and 3a). A stronger lithospheric mantle has a thicker brittle-plastic portion (Fig. 2e and h), which allows shear bands to penetrate into deeper depths (Fig. 1), therefore developing higher rift shoulders before their syn-breakup collapse.

Effective elastic thickness Te represents the integrated lithospheric strength, which governs the flexural behavior of the plate and the development of uplifted rift flanks29. We tested the relation between magmatic volume and Te in the model and compared it with observations (Fig. 3). The total volume of the magmatic surge increases with Te for cases with different lithosphere rheological structures (Fig. 3b). When lithospheric thickness and crustal rheology are fixed, a threshold for Te must be exceeded for a magmatic surge to occur, and this threshold increases with lithospheric thickness (Fig. 3b and c). By comparison with global observations of continental lithospheric thickness and Te32, we found that for lithosphere thicker than 150 km, the required Te to produce a magmatic surge falls within the observed range, and the likelihood of a surge increases with increasing thickness. In contrast, for lithosphere thinner than 150 km, the required Te generally exceeds observed values, suggesting that magmatic surges are unlikely (Fig. 3c). In cratonic regions, lithospheric thickness commonly reaches 200 km34, and Te typically exceeds 100 km35, which is favorable for magmatic surges, with larger volumes predicted at higher Te.

Labrador sea-baffin bay rift system

While rifting is often facilitated along pre-existing weak sutures and orogens37,38, it can also occur within strong cratons. A key example is the Labrador Sea-Baffin Bay rift system (Fig. 4). It separates the North Atlantic and Rae cratons and was associated with the Davis Strait igneous province, offering an ideal natural test case for our model. This margin system records the breakup of Greenland from northeastern Canada. A striking spatial transition from non-volcanic to volcanic margin is observed here. The northern Labrador Sea, Davis Strait and southern Baffin Bay margins are identified as volcanic margins with extensive magmatism16,39–42, constituting the Davis Strait Igneous Province43, including offshore flows and SDRs and onshore flows. To the south, the southern Labrador Sea margins abruptly change to magma-poor, with characteristic exhumed mantle and hyper-extended continental crust16,44 (Fig. 4). Considering a rift length of ∼1,700 km, the magmatic volume per rift length is 300–350 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{m}^{3}/km$$\end{document}. This spatial magmatic pattern mirrors a corresponding transition in lithospheric structure. The northern volcanic margins cut through Archean cratons and Paleoproterozoic orogens, where seismic tomography models reveal high S-wave velocity craton keels with a lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary (LAB) depth of 200–250 km. In contrast, the southern magma-poor margins lie within younger mobile belts, characterized by lower S-wave velocities45,50–52 (Fig. 4). LAB depth shallows to 150–200 km from north to south across the Grenville front53,54, and is accompanied by a reduction in elastic thickness too. We note the different studies and methods yield very different absolute values of Te in this region (e.g., ranging from 50 to 60 km to > 110 km for West Greenland), but a southward decreasing trend is generally consistent between different studies55–57. The pre-breakup lithospheric structure is not directly recorded in present-day geophysical observations. In particular, this region experienced Mesozoic magmatic events associated with initial stretching and thinning, which may have locally modified lithospheric thickness and strength. However, the northern margin (> 64°N), where syn-breakup magmatism is most pronounced and the core of the low seismic velocity mantle domain is observed (Fig. 4), appears to have been less affected by these earlier events58.

We propose that the abundant magmatism in the northern Labrador Sea and Baffin Bay margins is produced by the rifting of strong cratonic lithosphere. Considering a 210 km thick lithosphere with Te of 130 km as in case BS5, 270 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{m}^{3}/km$$\end{document} magma is produced during breakup without thermal anomaly (Fig. 3b), which is similar to the estimation of 390–525 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{m}^{3}/km$$\end{document} for Labrador Sea-Baffin Bay rift system. The glaciation in Greenland started from Pliocene induces local curvature of the plate, which decreases the present-day observed Te59, implying the Te at the time of breakup could have been higher. A minor temperature rise of 50 °C of the asthenosphere can nearly double the total magmatic volume of case BS5 to 500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{m}^{3}/km$$\end{document} (see Supporting Information Fig. S4). The main discrepancy lies in the duration of magmatism: the modeled magmatic surge is short-lived (< 1 Myr), whereas the primary magmatic phase in the Labrador Sea–Baffin Bay region spans ~ 4 Myr. The asynchronous nature of rifting, with breakup propagating from the Labrador Sea to Baffin Bay over ~ 6 Myr16,60, could prolong magmatism associated with syn-breakup rift-shoulder collapse. Future 3-D time-dependent rift modeling is needed to explore this effect.

Sedimentary and volcanic stratigraphy from the Nuussuaq Basin in West Greenland records a latest Cretaceous to lower Paleocene syn-rift uplift event followed by rapid subsidence at rates exceeding 3 mm/yr, with volcanism initiated shortly thereafter61 (Fig. 4). This sequence of uplift-subsidence-volcanism agrees with our model of the abrupt surge of magmatism caused by sudden subsidence of the uplifted rift shoulder in strong lithosphere. Baffin Island also experienced syn-rift uplift, constrained by thermochronology47,62, though no subsidence is resolved in the breakup stage. In contrast, the southern Labrador and Newfoundland marginal basins exhibit little or no stratigraphic evidence for late syn-rift stage uplift63,64 (Fig. 4). This absence of vertical motion and magmatic activity aligns with rifting in weaker lithosphere, where rift shoulders are suppressed due to crustal flow and/or limited elastic support. These spatial variations in lithospheric strength also control rift style, producing a north-south gradient in margin architecture—the strong lithosphere in northern Labrador Sea favored a narrow margin with abundant magmatism, whereas the weaker lithosphere to the south led to a wide, magma-poor margin with wide, extended continental crust65.

This excessive magmatism has been vastly proposed as the result of the proto-Iceland plume influence65,67,68. Reconstructions place the proto-Iceland plume between ~ 100 and 80 Ma close to the center of volcanism in this region with large uncertainty, leading to possible plume influence by either reactivation of ponded heat material11 or long-distance transport of hot material beneath lithosphere68,69. But several key observations remain insufficiently explained by a simple plume model: (1) Rapid subsidence precedes magmatism, contrary to models where subsidence follows volcanic loading8,70 or where underplating is expected to cause uplift instead of subsidence71; (2) Seafloor spreading initiation propagated from magma-poor to magma-rich margins16, opposite to predictions from plume-centered models72; (3) The mantle source of Davis Strait picrites is dominantly depleted, with limited excess temperatures73,74, challenging a plume origin; (4) The sharp transition between magma-rich and magma-poor segments is difficult to reconcile with the gradual variation of plume influence15. Here we offer an alternative mechanism, though we do not preclude a role for plume influence.

Implications for breakup-related LIPs

Other than the Davis Strait magmatic province, post-Pangaea-breakup LIPs include the CAMP which also exhibits similarly low per-rift-length melt volume (400 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{m}^{3}/km$$\end{document}) (Fig. 3d and e) and possible syn-breakup emplacement—though the accurate timing is hampered because the earliest oceanic crust was created during a magnetic quiet zone37,75. Together with its extremely large spatial extent, short duration (< 1 Myr)13, and relatively low mantle temperatures compared with other LIPs76, it also raises the possibility of a lithosphere-driven origin. CAMP formed during rifting between the Appalachian terrane and the West African Craton. Although the present-day lithosphere beneath the Appalachian margin is thin (∼100 km)77, this is likely a post-rift modification due to prolonged lithospheric delamination78,79. The pre-rift structure remains poorly constrained, making CAMP a plausible but uncertain candidate for lithosphere-driven magmatism. Applying this mechanism to pre-Pangaea-breakup LIPs is further challenging, as their extents, melt volumes, and tectonic contexts are poorly constrained. LIP activities in supercontinent cycles often peak during the dispersal phase and are commonly linked to elevated mantle temperatures from plume impingement or continental insulation80,81. However, both the magnitude and geodynamic origin of such anomalies remain debated82. Our model suggests that if breakup occurs within strong cratonic lithosphere, the required thermal anomaly to generate LIP-scale magmatism may be significantly reduced.

In summary, we discovered a new mechanism of magmatic surge induced by the breakup of strong lithospheres, responsible for excessive magmatism of volcanic margins and LIPs. This process is characterized by (1) strong lithosphere, (2) high rift shoulder, (3) short and syn-breakup magmatic timing, (4) quick breakup-related subsidence, all of which fit the Labrador Sea-Baffin Bay rift system. This mechanism can explain volcanic margins and LIPs with relatively small volumes, with no or minor temperature anomaly required. Further studies incorporating melt-induced lithospheric weakening, fully coupled landscape–tectonic feedbacks, and 3-D time-dependent asynchronous rift modeling are needed to better constrain the applicability and significance of this mechanism. We highlight the importance of lithospheric mantle strength in magmatism evolution during the continental breakup. More studies on uplift/subsidence history, pre-rift lithosphere structure and timing of breakup and magmatism can help better identify this new mechanism.

We note a direct application of this parameterization to mid-ocean ridges yields excessive magma production at normal mantle temperature. This is because Katz et al. (2003) does not take composition-dependent solidus into account90. Katz et al. (2003) fit experiments on lherzolite peridotites with very different fertilities, but mid-ocean ridge basalts reveal a depleted mantle source. Therefore we modified the solidus to fit melt fraction predicted by Krein et al.90. This is achieved by raising the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{1}$$\end{document} parameter in Katz et al. (2003) by 50 °C, while keeping all other parameters unchanged. This is equivalent to raising the solidus up by 50 °C, which accounts for the depleted mantle composition. This alteration is validated as we reproduce the spreading rate vs. oceanic crust trend91,92, and fit global average at normal mantle potential temperature of 1350 °C (Supporting Information Fig. S5). Therefore, we choose 1350 ◦C as the reference mantle temperature. All melt is instantaneously extracted to the surface after generation to calculate the igneous crust thickness. To quantify the magnitude of the magmatic surge, an arbitrary threshold is used. When the igneous crust thickness is thicker than the threshold, it is considered as the surge stage and all melt is integrated into the total volume of the surge. This threshold is set as 10 km in this study, which is well above normal oceanic thickness.

For each model, we estimate the effective elastic thickness (Te) to quantify the integrated lithospheric strength. Te governs the flexural response of the plate and thus influences uplifted rift flank development.
