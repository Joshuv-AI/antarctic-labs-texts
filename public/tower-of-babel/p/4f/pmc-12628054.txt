Sulfated chitosan enhances BMP-2-mediated spinal fusion via skeletal stem cell rejuvenation in aging
Authors: Xiaogang Wang, Dongao Huang, Luli Ji, Yuanman Yu, Fuwei Zhu, Jing Wang, Changsheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spinal degenerative diseases in elderly patients often require spinal fusion, but outcomes are limited by an aging microenvironment and stem cell dysfunction or depletion. In this study, we developed a bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2)/sulfated chitosan (SCS)/calcium phosphate cement (CPC) composite scaffold to enhance spinal fusion in aged mice. BMP-2/SCS significantly improved spinal fusion success rates (83.3 %) compared to BMP-2 alone (16.7 %) and high-dose BMP-2 (50 %). The BMP-2/SCS group promoted robust new bone formation and H-type vessel development, facilitating vascular-bone coupling in the fusion region. Mechanistically, SCS suppressed BMP-2-induced osteoclast overactivation and reduced MMP-9 secretion, leading to vertebral skeletal stem cell (vSSC) rejuvenation. Rejuvenated vSSCs primarily differentiated into the osteogenic bone/cartilage lineage while stromal lineage differentiation was suppressed. In contrast, co-administration of BMP-2 and alendronate sodium abolished BMP-2-mediated increases in vSSC numbers, vSSC rejuvenation, and bone integration, highlighting the indispensable role of osteoclast activity in BMP-2-induced bone regeneration. Collectively, this study demonstrates that BMP-2/SCS scaffolds effectively reverse age-related deficiencies by creating a rejuvenated bone microenvironment, promoting vascular-bone coupling, and enhancing osteogenesis, offering a promising strategy to improve spinal fusion outcomes in elderly patients.

Introduction

Spinal fusion surgery is a crucial procedure for treating degenerative disc disease, spinal deformities, fractures, and other spine-related conditions [1]. As the aging population grows, the number of spinal fusion cases has significantly increased in recent years [2]. However, the rate of non-union after surgery remains high, with reports indicating up to 48 % in some cases [3,4], particularly in multi-level spinal fusion procedures. Multi-level fusion involves the fusion of multiple vertebral segments, which presents greater challenges for bone healing and increases the risk of non-union. This non-union issue not only causes persistent pain for patients but also raises significant public health concerns due to the long-term complications and associated healthcare costs [[5], [6], [7]].

To address the challenge of non-fusion and promote tissue regeneration, various strategies have been explored, including the use of autologous iliac crest bone grafts (ICBG) and the supplementation of bone-inducing growth factors [8]. While ICBG remains the gold standard for enhancing spinal fusion, it is associated with several complications, such as damage to blood vessels and nerves [9], joint disruption [10], fractures [11], herniation of abdominal contents [12], and delayed iliac abscesses [13]. Furthermore, multi-level spinal fusion procedures often require larger volumes of bone graft than what can be harvested from the iliac crest, further complicating the treatment and increasing the risk of complications.

Due to these challenges, biological augmentation using bone-inducing growth factors has attracted considerable interest. Bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) has emerged as a leading growth factor for promoting spinal fusion due to its strong osteoinductive properties [14]. In clinical trials, BMP-2 has shown promising results, including a 100 % lumbar fusion rate without the need for additional internal fixation [15,16]. However, its clinical application is limited by its short half-life and rapid clearance from the bloodstream (6.7 min) [17], requiring high doses to maintain effectiveness. These supraphysiological doses, while enhancing osteogenesis, are associated with complications such as soft tissue inflammation [18], ectopic ossification [19], and bone cyst formation [20]. These issues are particularly concerning for aging patients, where low doses may not provide sufficient bone healing, while higher doses may increase the risk of adverse effects. Therefore, strategies that enhance the efficacy of BMP-2 while minimizing potential risks are essential for improving spinal fusion outcomes. As age advances, the local spinal microenvironment commonly exhibits osteoporosis and elevated inflammatory levels [21]. In addition, aging can cause reduced stem cell viability [22], fewer stem cells [23], and altered differentiation tendencies [24]. Consequently, the therapeutic impact of conventional fusion materials or growth factors like BMP-2 may be diminished [25], making spinal fusion repair more challenging.

Extensive research has demonstrated that heparin in the extracellular matrix can specifically bind to the heparin-binding domain of BMP-2, enhancing its osteogenic activity [26]. However, heparin presents several drawbacks, including structural variability and anticoagulant properties, which can lead to bone resorption and an increased risk of osteoporosis with prolonged use [27]. To address these issues, our previous studies developed sulfated chitosan (SCS), a heparin-like polysaccharide that retains a strong affinity for recombinant human BMP-2 but lacks the anticoagulant effects of heparin [28]. SCS has been shown to promote the expression of BMP receptors and enhance BMP-2-mediated osteogenesis [28]. Additionally, SCS reduces inflammation in vivo, as demonstrated in studies of vascular regeneration in the hindlimbs [29]. These promising findings suggest that SCS could serve as an effective carrier to amplify the osteogenic effects of BMP-2. Therefore, we propose that adding SCS could enhance the osteogenic potential of BMP-2, particularly in patients with osteoporosis, and improve spinal fusion outcomes in the elderly, where bone regeneration is typically compromised.

In this study, we used calcium phosphate cement (CPC) as a scaffold to co-load BMP-2 and SCS. The physical adsorption of BMP-2 and SCS onto the CPC scaffold significantly enhanced spinal fusion in aged mice compared to BMP-2/CPC alone. The BMP-2/SCS combination promoted the enrichment and rejuvenation of skeletal stem cells (vSSCs) in the fusion region, revitalizing the bone microenvironment and accelerating fusion repair. Mechanistic analysis revealed that osteoclast-derived MMP-9, regulated by the SCS/BMP-2 combination, played a key role in rejuvenating vSSCs and enhancing osteogenesis. Additionally, co-administration of BMP-2 with bisphosphonates (BP) impaired BMP-2's osteogenic effects, suggesting that BMP-2's ability to rejuvenate vSSCs and promote bone regeneration depends on osteoclast activity. These findings highlight the potential of the BMP-2/SCS combination to improve spinal fusion outcomes, particularly in aging populations where bone regeneration is typically impaired.

Results

Aging microenvironment reduces spinal fusion efficiency of BMP-2/CPC scaffold

To evaluate the efficacy of BMP-2/CPC scaffolds in promoting spinal fusion under aging conditions, we selected a BMP-2 dosage of 10 μg based on previous studies demonstrating its optimal osteoinductive potential in young models while minimizing adverse effects, such as ectopic ossification and inflammation. BMP-2 (10 μg) and CPC (20 mg) were unilaterally implanted into the L4/L5 intertransverse process regions of young (6–8 weeks) and aged (18 months) mice, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. After 8 weeks, micro-CT analysis revealed a significant decline in spinal fusion success rates in aged mice (16.7 %) compared to young mice (83.3 %) (Fig. 1b). Representative micro-CT images showed clear bone bridging with organized trabecular structures surrounding the fusion site in young mice, whereas aged mice displayed sparse and disorganized trabecular bone formation (Fig. 1c). Synergistic effects of SCS and BMP-2 on spinal fusion in aged mice

To overcome the limited spinal fusion efficiency of BMP-2/CPC scaffolds in aged mice, we designed five experimental groups: (1) Control group (CPC alone), (2) BMP-2 group (CPC with 10 μg BMP-2), (3) hBMP-2 group (CPC with 25 μg BMP-2), (4) BMP-2/SCS group (CPC with 10 μg BMP-2 and 20 μg SCS), and (5) hBMP-2/BP group (CPC with 25 μg BMP-2 and alendronate sodium, based on reports [30,31] that bisphosphonates suppress osteoclast overactivity and improve bone healing in osteoporosis). Here, “BMP-2” and “hBMP-2” denote the low-dose (10 μg) and high-dose (25 μg) BMP-2 groups, respectively.

The appearance of CPC-based scaffolds was evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM images revealed clear morphological changes in the CPC scaffolds after the incorporation of BMP-2, BMP-2/SCS, and hBMP-2/BP, including the formation of crystal-like structures (Fig. S1a). These alterations confirmed the successful integration of the added components. BMP-2 dynamic release curves showed that incorporating SCS into CPC/BMP-2 scaffolds reduced the initial BMP-2 burst from approximately 70 %–25 % (from 6.7 to 2.2 μg/mL) on day 1 and moderated the cumulative release to approximately 70 % by day 14 (Fig. S1b and c), indicating a more sustained release profile. The adsorption efficiency of BMP-2 on CPC particles was approximately 21.4 μg/mg at an initial concentration of 1 mg/mL and around 8.3 μg/mg at 0.5 mg/mL (Fig. S1d). These results indicate that the BMP-2 adsorption efficiency on CPC is considerably higher than that observed in the experimental groups described above.

After 8 weeks of repair, micro-CT analysis revealed significant differences in spinal fusion success rates among the groups. In the control group, no spinal fusion was observed. The BMP-2 group achieved a partial fusion rate of 16.7 %, with limited new bone formation. The hBMP-2 group displayed an improved fusion rate of 50 %, with more robust trabecular bone formation compared to the BMP-2 group. Notably, the BMP-2/SCS group achieved the highest spinal fusion success rate at 83.3 %, demonstrating superior bone formation and integration. In contrast, the hBMP-2/BP group showed poor outcomes, with only 16.7 % partial fusion and the remaining samples displaying no fusion (Fig. 2a). Although BP was added to counteract the excessive osteoclast activation commonly associated with high-dose BMP-2, its addition completely negated the positive effects of BMP-2 on spinal fusion. Detailed micro-CT images (Fig. 2b) further supported these findings. The control group showed no evidence of new bone formation or trabecular integration in the L4/L5 region. In the BMP-2 group, sparse and fragmented trabeculae were observed. The hBMP-2 group exhibited an increase in trabecular number and bone integration compared to the BMP-2 group. By contrast, the hBMP-2/BP group displayed poor trabecular formation and weak bone bridging. Interestingly, the BMP-2/SCS group exhibited abundant, well-organized trabecular bone, as highlighted in the magnified regions, indicating excellent bone integration.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 2c–f) confirmed these results. BMP-2 promoted new bone formation, and increasing its dose in the hBMP-2 group further enhanced BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N. However, combining hBMP-2 with BP (hBMP-2/BP group) resulted in parameters similar to the control group, with no significant improvement. Importantly, the BMP-2/SCS group demonstrated a significant increase in new bone volume and trabecular architecture, comparable to the hBMP-2 group, without the need for a higher BMP-2 dose.

To further evaluate the effects of BMP-2/SCS on spinal fusion in aged mice, histological analysis, immunofluorescence staining, and ELISA assays were conducted to assess bone morphology, mineralization rates, and osteogenic activity. H&E staining results (Fig. 3a) revealed distinct differences among the experimental groups. The control group displayed extensive fibrous connective tissue with no detectable trabecular structures, indicating an early stage of bone formation and the absence of spinal fusion. The BMP-2 group showed some newly formed trabecular bone, but the presence of abundant fibrous connective tissue highlighted incomplete fusion. The hBMP-2 group exhibited more robust trabecular bone formation, with indistinct boundaries between old and newly formed bone, clear marrow cavities, and minimal fibrous tissue, demonstrating better fusion outcomes. However, the hBMP-2/BP group showed limited new bone formation, primarily at the edges of the old bone, along with abundant fibrous connective tissue, resulting in poor fusion. In the Masson's Trichrome staining (Fig. 3a), the control group exhibited extensive fibrous connective tissue with nearly no trabecular bone formation, consistent with the H&E findings. The BMP-2 group showed moderate connective tissue infiltration along with a limited amount of newly formed trabecular bone, while the hBMP-2 group demonstrated a marked increase in developing collagen-woven bone structures with reduced fibrous tissue. In contrast, the hBMP-2/BP group exhibited minimal trabecular bone formation and abundant fibrous connective tissue, closely resembling the control group. Notably, the BMP-2/SCS group displayed the most pronounced collagen-bone architecture, with both Masson's Trichrome and H&E staining revealing continuous, integrated collagen-woven bone formation.

Sequential fluorescence labeling (Fig. 3b–d, e) corroborated the histological findings. BMP-2 increased mineralization rates, which were further enhanced in the hBMP-2 group. However, the hBMP-2/BP group exhibited significantly reduced mineralization rates, comparable to the control group, indicating impaired bone formation due to excessive osteoclast inhibition. In contrast, the BMP-2/SCS group achieved mineralization rates similar to the hBMP-2 group, despite utilizing a lower BMP-2 dose, suggesting a synergistic effect of SCS in promoting mineral deposition. Immunofluorescence staining for osteocalcin-positive osteoblasts (Fig. 3f) showed significantly higher osteoblast counts in the BMP-2/SCS and hBMP-2 groups compared to other groups. This trend was consistent with the mineralization results. Additionally, ELISA assays (Fig. 3f and g) revealed elevated levels of osteocalcin and PINP in the BMP-2/SCS and hBMP-2 groups, confirming enhanced osteogenic activity. Notably, the hBMP-2/BP group displayed levels comparable to the control group, further highlighting the adverse effects of bisphosphonates on BMP-2-mediated bone formation.

These results demonstrate that the BMP-2/SCS combination significantly promotes bone formation, accelerates mineralization, and enhances osteogenic activity, effectively improving spinal fusion in aged mice.

The effect of SCS/BMP-2 on vascularization in spinal fusion of aged mice

Vascularization is a key determinant of successful bone tissue engineering. This study investigated the effects of BMP-2/SCS on vascularization in the spinal fusion regions of aged mice using CT angiography and immunofluorescence staining. The 8-week CT angiography results (Fig. 4a and b) revealed sparse and disorganized blood vessels in the control group, indicating minimal vascularization. In the BMP-2 group, vascular density significantly increased compared to the control group. The hBMP-2 group demonstrated further improvement with denser vascular networks, while the hBMP-2/BP group showed a marked reduction in vascularization. This observation suggests that excessive osteoclast inhibition impairs angiogenesis, consistent with reports that osteoclast lineage cells secrete factors like PDGF-BB [32] and ANG [33] to support H-type vessel formation and protect against vascular senescence. Among all groups, the BMP-2/SCS group exhibited the most robust vascularization, characterized by well-developed and organized blood vessels in the fusion region. H-type vessels, characterized by the co-expression of Emcn and CD31, are functional blood vessels critical for bone repair as they deliver oxygen, nutrients, and recruit Osterix + osteoprogenitor cells to enhance bone formation. Aging significantly reduces H-type vessel formation, posing challenges to bone regeneration. To better understand their dynamics, we analyzed H-type vessels at 3, 7, and 14 days post-surgery (Fig. 4e–j). The spatiotemporal analysis of H-type vessel formation revealed distinct trends across the experimental groups. In the control group, H-type vessels were minimal at all time points (3, 7, and 14 days), reflecting poor vascularization and limited healing capacity. The BMP-2 group showed no significant H-type vessel formation at day 3, a peak at day 7, and a sharp decline by day 14 (from 35/mm2 to 12/mm2), indicating transient vascularization. The hBMP-2 group exhibited an accelerated response with notable H-type vessel formation as early as day 3 (19.6/mm2), which peaked at day 7 (66.5/mm2) but declined sharply by day 14, showing a temporary angiogenic effect of high-dose BMP-2. In contrast, the hBMP-2/BP group displayed minimal H-type vessels throughout all time points, suggesting that excessive osteoclast inhibition significantly impaired vascularization. The BMP-2/SCS group showed a unique pattern, with early H-type vessel formation at day 3 (19/mm2), comparable to the hBMP-2 group. By day 7, H-type vessel numbers peaked at 62.8/mm2, slightly below the hBMP-2 group (66.5/mm2) but significantly higher than the BMP-2 group (36.1/mm2). Importantly, at day 14, the BMP-2/SCS group maintained the highest H-type vessel count (29.6/mm2), far surpassing both BMP-2 and hBMP-2 groups, which experienced significant declines. These findings demonstrate that BMP-2/SCS not only accelerates early H-type vessel formation but also sustains it over time, providing a stable vascular network to support bone regeneration and spinal fusion in aged mice. Osterix immunofluorescence staining (Figure k,l) supported these conclusions, showing higher numbers of Osterix+ osteoprogenitor cells in the BMP-2/SCS group at day 7, correlating with elevated H-type vessel formation.

In summary, BMP-2/SCS significantly promotes vascularization and H-type vessel formation during the spinal fusion process in aged mice. This sustained vascularization supports bone regeneration, leading to improved bone formation, vascular integration, and spinal fusion outcomes observed in the BMP-2/SCS group (Fig. 2b).

The effect of SCS/BMP-2 on osteoclastogenesis in spinal fusion of aged mice

Osteoclasts are essential for bone repair, contributing to vascularization, osteoblast differentiation, and calcium deposition. Emerging evidence highlights their positive role in bone regeneration. To investigate the regulation of osteoclast activity by BMP-2/SCS during spinal fusion, we performed tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP, osteoclast marker) staining, cytokine measurement, and in vitro analyses.

At 8 weeks post-repair, TRAP staining results (Fig. 5a and b) showed minimal TRAP+ osteoclasts in the control group, indicating low levels of bone remodeling and regeneration. In the BMP-2 group, significant TRAP+ osteoclasts were observed, predominantly around the scaffold, suggesting active resorption of calcium-phosphate material and facilitation of local bone remodeling. The hBMP-2 group displayed an even greater number of TRAP+ osteoclasts (23/mm2 vs. 17/mm2 in the BMP-2 group), which may reflect overly active osteoclast activity, potentially accelerating new bone degradation and impairing bone integration. In contrast, the BMP-2/BP group exhibited almost no TRAP+ osteoclasts, consistent with the known effects of BP, which binds tightly to bone or calcium-phosphate material surfaces and induces osteoclast apoptosis [34]. Quantitative analysis confirmed that osteoclast numbers in the BMP-2/BP group were comparable to the control group, suggesting minimal bone remodeling activity. The BMP-2/SCS group, however, demonstrated a moderate number of TRAP+ osteoclasts, lower than the BMP-2 group but significantly higher than the control group. Given the improved bone integration observed in the BMP-2/SCS group, this balanced osteoclast activity appears to support effective bone remodeling. ELISA results (Fig. 5c) corroborated these findings, with C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I), a marker of bone resorption, reflecting osteoclast activity. Based on the observed lower number of osteoclasts in the BMP-2/SCS group compared to the BMP-2 group, we hypothesized that SCS may attenuate BMP-2-induced osteoclast overactivation. Supporting this, in vitro experiments demonstrated that extracts from BMP-2 scaffolds significantly promoted osteoclast activation (Fig. 5d and e) and bone resorption activity (Fig. 5f and g). In contrast, extracts from BMP-2/SCS scaffolds markedly inhibited osteoclast differentiation and resorption activity. Further analysis of osteoclast-related gene expression revealed that BMP-2 scaffold extracts upregulated key markers of osteoclast differentiation, including Nfatc1, c-Src, Trap, and Ctsk, whereas BMP-2/SCS scaffold extracts significantly downregulated these genes.

These findings suggest that BMP-2/SCS scaffolds support balanced osteoclast activity by promoting local bone remodeling while reducing excessive osteoclast activation observed with BMP-2 and hBMP-2 scaffolds. This balance may contribute to improved bone integration and remodeling during spinal fusion.

SCS/BMP2 regulates the differentiation tendency of skeletal stem cells in the spinal fusion region

Skeletal stem cells (vSSCs) are critical for bone repair and regeneration, exhibiting remarkable self-renewal and multipotent differentiation abilities [22,35,36]. Recent studies have highlighted the existence of specific vSSC populations in vertebrae [37], which play a pivotal role in vertebral bone development and physiological repair processes. Aging, however, is characterized by stem cell depletion and functional decline [38,39], leading vSSCs to preferentially differentiate into stromal lineage cells while suppressing bone/cartilage lineage differentiation [22], thus impairing bone regeneration. The study published in Nature provided groundbreaking evidence that the combination of BMP-2 and CSF-1 inhibitors, which target colony-stimulating factor-1 to suppress osteoclast activity, can effectively rejuvenate skeletal stem cells (vSSCs), restoring their youthful differentiation potential [22]. By reshaping the osteoclast-mediated microenvironment, this approach facilitates vSSC recruitment and promotes bone regeneration. Drawing on our findings that BMP-2/SCS similarly modulates osteoclast activity (Fig. 5), we propose that BMP-2/SCS may replicate these effects, enhancing vSSC recruitment and rejuvenation in the spinal fusion region to drive bone repair and regeneration.

To evaluate the impact of BMP-2/SCS on CD51+Tie2-6C3-Thy1−CD105- vSSC recruitment and differentiation during spinal fusion, we conducted flow cytometry at 3 and 7 days post-surgery (gating strategy shown in Fig. S2). At 7 days, BMP-2/SCS significantly increased vSSC proportions compared to the control group (Fig. 6f). While BMP-2 showed a moderate increase and hBMP-2 further enhanced vSSC recruitment, the hBMP-2/BP group exhibited a notable reduction, comparable to the control group. These findings demonstrate that BMP-2/SCS more effectively promotes vSSC recruitment than BMP-2 alone in the spinal fusion region.

Further analysis of vSSC differentiation revealed that BMP-2 significantly promoted vSSC differentiation into the CD51+Tie2-6C3+Thy1+ bone/cartilage lineage at day 3 (Fig. 6b–e). Interestingly, no significant difference was observed between the BMP-2 and hBMP-2 groups, suggesting that increasing BMP-2 dosage does not further enhance this lineage differentiation during the early repair stage. In contrast, the hBMP-2/BP group exhibited minimal bone/cartilage differentiation, comparable to the control group, indicating that BP strongly inhibits this process. Notably, the BMP-2/SCS group displayed a slightly reduced bone/cartilage differentiation tendency compared to the BMP-2 and hBMP-2 groups but maintained a superior effect compared to the control group. By day 7 (Fig. 6c–e), these trends persisted, with the hBMP-2 group demonstrating the most robust bone/cartilage lineage differentiation.

Stromal lineage differentiation, a hallmark of vSSC aging, was further analyzed at days 3 and 7. Both BMP-2 and hBMP-2 groups exhibited significantly reduced stromal lineage (CD51+Tie2-6C3+Thy1- and CD51+Tie2-6C3-Thy1+) differentiation at day 7, highlighting their ability to counteract aging-associated vSSC tendencies. Conversely, the hBMP-2/BP group showed consistently high stromal lineage differentiation, similar to the control group, at both time points, further confirming the suppressive effect of BP on vSSC function. Remarkably, the BMP-2/SCS group displayed the lowest stromal lineage differentiation at both days 3 and 7, indicating its superior ability to suppress vSSC aging and promote a favorable differentiation profile.

To validate the effects of the BMP-2/SCS scaffold on vSSC osteogenic differentiation tendencies in vitro, we isolated vSSCs (CD45−TER119−CD51+Tie2-6C3-Thy1−CD105-) and cultured them on scaffolds for osteogenic induction (Fig. S3a). ALP and alizarin red S analysis showed that BMP-2 significantly promoted vSSC osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S3b–d). The BMP-2/SCS scaffold slightly enhanced ALP activity compared to BMP-2 alone but showed no differences in alizarin red S staining. Gene expression analysis (Fig. S3e) confirmed that BMP-2 upregulated Sp7, Bglap, and Ibsp expression, with no further enhancement by the BMP-2/SCS scaffold. These findings are consistent with flow cytometry results, indicating that the BMP-2/SCS scaffold does not further promote vSSC osteogenic differentiation tendencies compared to BMP-2 alone. To investigate whether SCS can modulate its action to optimize spinal fusion when loaded with varying concentrations of BMP-2, we further observed that SCS did not enhance the osteogenic potential of hBMP-2 on vSSCs (Fig. S3f).

These findings highlight that the BMP-2/SCS scaffold not only enhances the recruitment of vSSCs but also demonstrates a notably stronger effect in suppressing vSSC differentiation into the stromal lineage, a hallmark of vSSC aging. This dual action—facilitating vSSC recruitment and inhibiting stromal differentiation—may rejuvenate vSSCs by preserving their youthful properties, ultimately enhancing their regenerative potential in the context of spinal fusion and tissue engineering.

SCS/BMP2 rejuvenates the aging microenvironment in spinal fusion of aged mice

In addition to stem cell exhaustion and dysfunction, aging is also characterized by the accumulation of senescent cells that secrete a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), including factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which drive chronic inflammation and impair tissue repair. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle of senescence and diminished bone regeneration. Given BMP2/SCS's demonstrated ability to rejuvenate vSSCs, we hypothesize that it can disrupt this cycle by reducing senescent cell burden and SASP activity, thereby improving the aging microenvironment and enhancing spinal fusion outcomes.

Flow cytometry revealed that BMP2 scaffolds reduced the proportion of total senescent cells (p16+) in the spinal fusion region from 21.4 % to 17.5 %, while BMP2/SCS further lowered this to 12.7 % (Fig. 7b and c). These findings were corroborated by immunofluorescence staining of p16 and SA-β-gal, which also indicated a significant reduction in senescence markers in the BMP2/SCS group (Fig. 7h–k). Further analysis of senescent immune cells, particularly myeloid cells (p16+CD45+), showed a decrease with BMP2 treatment from 18.6 % to 12.1 %, and BMP2/SCS further reduced this proportion to 9.92 %, although the difference was not statistically significant. For senescent macrophages (p16+F4/80+), BMP2 alone had no significant impact, but BMP2/SCS significantly reduced their proportion from 49.8 % to 39.9 %. These findings highlight BMP2/SCS's enhanced ability to mitigate senescent immune cell accumulation in the aging spinal fusion microenvironment.

SASP profiling revealed that BMP-2 scaffolds significantly increased the secretion of IL-1β (Fig. 7l) and IL-6 (Fig. 7m), indicating a pro-inflammatory shift in the repair microenvironment. This is consistent with the elevated stromal lineage differentiation observed in the BMP-2 group (Fig. 6b–d), as stromal lineage cells are known to contribute to SASP-related inflammation [22]. While BMP-2's ability to promote bone regeneration is well-documented, its pro-inflammatory effects raise concerns about the risk of inducing inflammaging in aging individuals. In contrast, BMP-2/SCS scaffolds significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels (Fig. 7n) compared to BMP-2 alone, with levels even lower than those in the control group. Additionally, BMP-2/SCS significantly increased anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 (Fig. 7q) and IL-4 (Fig. 7o), suggesting that SCS not only suppresses vSSC differentiation into the stromal lineage but also promotes a more anti-inflammatory and regenerative microenvironment. Additionally, the study measured TGF-β1 levels, a key factor involved in recruiting stem cells to osteoclast-mediated bone resorption sites for in situ osteogenesis and a marker of enhanced osteogenic activity [40]. BMP-2/SCS significantly elevated TGF-β1 secretion (Fig. 7p), aligning with the group's superior osteogenic performance (Fig. 3) and enhanced spinal fusion efficiency (Fig. 2a).

In summary, BMP-2/SCS enhances the aging spinal fusion microenvironment by recruiting and rejuvenating vSSCs, reducing senescent immune cells (myeloid cells and macrophages), and suppressing key senescence markers such as p16 and SA-β-gal. It also decreases SASP secretion while promoting anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic factors, thereby restoring regenerative capacity and improving spinal fusion outcomes in aging individuals.

SCS/BMP-2 rejuvenates vSSC differentiation via osteoclast-derived MMP-9

As previously mentioned, the Nature study confirmed that BMP-2 combined with a CSF-1 inhibitor could induce rejuvenated vSSCs in aged bone repair sites. However, this phenomenon was absent when high doses of CSF-1 inhibitors excessively suppressed osteoclast activity or when CSF-1 inhibitors were omitted [22]. In this study, the BMP-2/SCS group successfully replicated these findings, while the hBMP-2/BP group almost completely nullified BMP-2's effects. Furthermore, we did not observe any appreciable effect of BMP-2 and SCS on vSSC viability (Fig. S5a and b). Taken together, we hypothesize that cytokines secreted by osteoclasts might play a pivotal role in regulating vSSC rejuvenation.

We observed a rejuvenated population of vSSCs at the spinal fusion site, which was most notably characterized by a significantly reduced stromal lineage profile at day 7 (Fig. 6c and d). We therefore analyzed osteoclast-secreted functional proteins at day 7, including MMP-9, MMP-14, IGF-1, and CTSK. The results (Fig. 8b) revealed significant differences in MMP-9 and MMP-14 levels between the BMP-2/SCS and BMP-2 groups, suggesting their potential roles in reversing vSSC aging. After 7 days of repair, stromal lineage differentiation was assessed to evaluate vSSC aging status (Fig. 8c and d). BMP-2/SCS significantly reduced the proportion of stromal lineage vSSCs compared to the control group. However, the BMP-2/SCS/MMP-14 Ab group showed no significant differences from the BMP-2/SCS group, indicating that MMP-14 is not a critical factor for vSSC rejuvenation. Interestingly, the BMP-2/SCS/MMP-9 Ab group exhibited a marked increase in stromal lineage vSSCs, effectively eliminating the rejuvenating effects of BMP-2/SCS. Immunofluorescence results further confirmed a strong co-localization of MMP-9 with TRAP+ osteoclasts (Fig. S5c). Moreover, the MMP-9 fluorescence intensity followed the same trend as the ELISA results (Fig. 8b), with the BMP-2 group exhibiting the highest levels, and the BMP-2/SCS group showing lower levels that remain significantly above the control. Additionally, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that MMP-9 secretion correlates with osteoclast maturation rather than actual bone resorption (Fig. S5d), and that osteoclast-derived MMP-9 has minimal impact on the osteogenic differentiation of vSSCs (Fig. S5e). A concentration gradient study further revealed that MMP-9 concentrations below 100 ng/mL exert anti-senescence effects on vSSCs, with optimal inhibition observed at 50 ng/mL, whereas concentrations above 100 ng/mL did not produce significant effects (Fig. S5f). Thus, during aged spinal fusion, the BMP-2/SCS group may achieve an optimal osteoclast-derived MMP-9 concentration that confers superior anti-senescence effects on vSSCs. In conclusion, our findings indicate that BMP-2/SCS promotes vSSC rejuvenation at spinal fusion sites primarily by modulating osteoclast-derived MMP-9 secretion.

Discussion

Bone repair during aging, particularly in spinal fusion, is hindered by cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, and diminished stem cell functionality. Stem cells, pivotal for maintaining and regenerating bone tissue, exhibit reduced proliferation and differentiation potential in aged individuals. This study primarily focuses on vSSCs, whose stromal lineage bias and SASP-driven inflammatory responses significantly impair bone healing. The findings reveal that BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds significantly enhance spinal fusion repair in aged mice by rejuvenating vSSCs, suppressing stromal lineage differentiation, and reducing senescence markers. Elevated levels of osteoclast-secreted MMP-9 in the BMP-2/SCS group emerged as a critical factor, facilitating extracellular matrix remodeling and creating a favorable microenvironment for vSSC rejuvenation. These effects culminated in enhanced osteogenic differentiation, reduced SASP activity, and improved spinal fusion outcomes (Fig. 9).Fig. 9The aging vSSCs state is accompanied by stromal cells that exacerbate inflammation through increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further accelerating cellular senescence. Intervention with BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds shifts this trajectory by enhancing osteoclast-mediated MMP-9 secretion, which facilitates the transition of aging vSSCs into a more youthful state. This rejuvenation generates a greater abundance of skeletal lineage cells, promoting osteogenic differentiation and driving successful spinal fusion.Fig. 9

The aging vSSCs state is accompanied by stromal cells that exacerbate inflammation through increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further accelerating cellular senescence. Intervention with BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds shifts this trajectory by enhancing osteoclast-mediated MMP-9 secretion, which facilitates the transition of aging vSSCs into a more youthful state. This rejuvenation generates a greater abundance of skeletal lineage cells, promoting osteogenic differentiation and driving successful spinal fusion.

The sustained release profile of BMP-2 from CPC/SCS scaffolds holds meaningful implications for clinical translation. By reducing the initial burst release from ∼70 % to ∼25 % and prolonging release over 14 days, this system helps preserve BMP-2 bioactivity and may lower risks of ectopic ossification and inflammation commonly seen with high local doses. Additionally, the high BMP-2 adsorption efficiency on CPC supports flexible dose modulation, further enhancing the translational promise of this delivery platform in bone repair and spinal fusion.

Inspired by the foundational work reported in Nature, which demonstrated that the combination of BMP-2 and low-dose CSF-1 inhibitors could rejuvenate vSSCs in aged bone repair settings [22]. By targeting osteoclast-mediated microenvironmental remodeling, this approach leveraged the critical role of osteoclast activity in regulating vSSC dynamics. In this study, we successfully validated and replicated this phenomenon using BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds, further underscoring the indispensable role of osteoclast activity in vSSC rejuvenation. More notably, our work introduces a novel mechanism wherein BMP-2/SCS scaffolds mediate osteoclast function to promote the secretion of MMP-9, a key matrix metalloproteinase involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and signaling transduction. This finding, observed for the first time in our study, reveals the specificity of MMP-9 in reversing vSSC aging and fostering a shift toward skeletal lineage differentiation. Unlike the hBMP-2/BP group, where excessive inhibition of osteoclast activity negated BMP-2's regenerative effects (Fig. 2), BMP-2/SCS scaffolds balanced osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling with anti-inflammatory regulation, optimizing the osteoclast-vSSC axis for therapeutic gain.

Early studies have demonstrated that osteoclast activity is crucial for bone regeneration and integration. For instance, in CPC-mediated rat calvarial defect repair, higher osteoclast activity was associated with enhanced bone integration, attributed to effective bone remodeling [41], aligning with the superior spinal fusion outcomes observed in the hBMP-2 group (Fig. 2a and b). Furthermore, preosteoclasts are known to secrete factors such as PDGF-BB and VEGF, which facilitate bone vascularization and accelerate fracture healing in aging bones [42,43]. Beyond their role in bone repair, osteoclasts contribute significantly to maintaining hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) niches [[44], [45], [46]] by modulating bone marrow cavity space and promoting the renewal of niche-supportive osteoblasts, emphasizing their importance in stem cell regulation.

Given that this study focuses on vSSCs, the findings reveal that BMP-2/SCS scaffolds effectively recruit and rejuvenate vSSCs. In contrast, the addition of BP, which inhibits osteoclast activity (Fig. 5a and b), severely diminished BMP-2-induced osteogenesis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), angiogenesis (Fig. 4), and vSSC recruitment and rejuvenation (Fig. 6). This highlights the indispensable role of osteoclasts in modulating vSSC dynamics. Long-term BP use has been linked to adverse effects such as osteonecrosis [47] and atypical femoral fractures [48], likely due to impaired bone remodeling [49]. The results of this study provide a potential explanation for these side effects, suggesting that prolonged BP treatment promotes vSSC differentiation into stromal lineages while suppressing osteogenic differentiation, accelerating skeletal aging and contributing to these complications.

Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), a zinc-dependent member of the MMP family, is integral to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and cellular signaling [50]. Emerging evidence suggests that MMPs possesses potential anti-senescence properties [51,52] by influencing the local cellular microenvironment. This protease degrades ECM components, releasing bioactive molecules that could enhance the recruitment, activation, and function of stem cells [53,54], including vSSCs [55]. In this study, we observed elevated MMP-9 levels in the BMP-2/SCS group, which we hypothesize contribute to vSSC rejuvenation. By reshaping the ECM, MMP-9 likely creates a dynamic niche that supports vSSC function, reduces pro-senescence signaling, and facilitates differentiation into osteogenic lineages. Although the precise molecular mechanisms remain speculative, these findings underscore the importance of osteoclast activity and MMP-9 secretion in maintaining a favorable ECM environment for vSSCs, highlighting the need for further research into the interplay between MMP-9-mediated ECM remodeling and stem cell dynamics.

Moreover, our results revealed that BMP-2/SCS effectively mitigated the aging-associated pro-inflammatory milieu by reducing SASP factor secretion (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6) while enhancing anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, IL-4) (Fig. 7l–q). These outcomes not only corroborate previous findings on BMP-2's dual osteogenic and pro-inflammatory roles [56] but also demonstrate the unique anti-inflammatory properties of SCS in tempering BMP-2's potential drawbacks. By creating a regenerative microenvironment conducive to bone healing, BMP-2/SCS scaffolds achieved superior spinal fusion outcomes in aged models.

While both CPC and SCS have been previously employed as carriers for BMP-2 in bone regeneration, prior studies have largely focused on their osteoconductive properties and the capacity of SCS to enhance BMP-2-induced bone formation in ectopic [57] or young animal models [58]. However, the regenerative environment in aging bone is fundamentally different, characterized by stem cell depletion, impaired osteogenesis, and reduced responsiveness to growth factors. In this study, we demonstrate that SCS, when co-delivered with BMP-2, not only enhances osteoinductive signaling but also rejuvenates senescent skeletal stem cells and restores the osteogenic niche in aged bone. This synergy enables effective spinal fusion even in the compromised microenvironment of aged vertebrae, where conventional BMP-2 therapy alone often fails. These findings establish a new mechanistic paradigm in which SCS functions as a microenvironmental modulator rather than merely a passive carrier, offering a strategic solution to the long-standing challenge of bone regeneration in elderly patients.

While this study establishes BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds as a promising approach to rejuvenating vSSCs and improving spinal fusion outcomes, there are several limitations. First, the precise molecular pathways by which MMP-9 contributes to vSSC rejuvenation and the regulation of the aging microenvironment remain speculative. Although our findings strongly suggest a role for osteoclast-secreted MMP-9, further studies are required to delineate the specific downstream signaling events and to explore its interaction with other microenvironmental factors. Second, while our results are robust in the mouse spinal fusion model, translating these findings to humans involves considerable challenges. Differences in the bone microenvironment, vSSC populations, and immune responses across species necessitate further validation in larger animal models and clinical trials. Future research should also evaluate the long-term effects of BMP-2/SCS scaffolds on bone quality, systemic aging markers, and potential off-target effects.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the efficacy of BMP-2/SCS-loaded CPC scaffolds in addressing age-related deficiencies in spinal fusion by rejuvenating vSSCs, improving osteogenic differentiation, and mitigating the inflammatory and senescent microenvironment. By leveraging osteoclast-mediated MMP-9 secretion, BMP-2/SCS effectively reduces stromal lineage bias and promotes a dynamic extracellular matrix conducive to skeletal regeneration. These findings highlight the potential of BMP-2/SCS scaffolds as a novel therapeutic strategy for enhancing spinal fusion outcomes in aging populations. These findings not only expand the understanding of vSSC dynamics and bone microenvironment modulation but also offer a promising, innovative solution for advancing regenerative strategies in age-associated bone repair.

Experimental section

Preparation of CPC-based scaffolds

SCS was synthesized as previously described [59]. CPC particles and BMP-2 were kindly provided by Shanghai Rebone. Alendronate sodium was purchased from Merck.

The CPC particles, with a maximum size of 300 μm, were designed to enable consistent adsorption of additives like BMP-2, SCS, or BP, ensuring uniform preparation and optimal scaffold functionality. To prepare BMP-2-loaded CPC scaffolds with or without SCS, 2 mg of SCS and 1 mg of BMP-2 were co-dissolved in 0.2 mL of PBS. Subsequently, 2 g of CPC particles were added to the solution, allowing the mixture to achieve saturation adsorption of BMP-2 and SCS onto the CPC particles. The saturated particles were then frozen and lyophilized for 1 day to obtain the CPC/BMP-2/SCS material. BMP-2/CPC scaffolds were prepared using the same procedure without the addition of SCS. Using this method, the following sterile CPC-based formulations were prepared: CPC particles without additives (Control), BMP-2/CPC containing 10 μg BMP-2 per 20 mg CPC (BMP2), hBMP-2/CPC containing 25 μg BMP-2 per 20 mg CPC (hBMP2), BMP-2/SCS/CPC containing 10 μg BMP-2 and 20 μg SCS per 20 mg CPC (BMP2/SCS), and hBMP-2/BP/CPC containing 25 μg BMP-2 and 20 μg BP per 20 mg CPC (hBMP2/BP). To explore potential mechanisms, this study also prepared BMP-2/SCS scaffolds containing 10 μg MMP9 antibody (MCE, USA) or 10 μg MMP14 antibody (Abcam, USA) following a similar procedure. All scaffolds were prepared under aseptic conditions and stored at −20 °C until further use.

Surface morphology characterization

The morphology of CPC particles, predominantly composed of hydroxyapatite structures, was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). The CPC particles were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C until completely dehydrated. To ensure conductivity, the dried particles were coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater. SEM imaging was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV to observe the surface morphology and particle structure. As shown in Fig. S1, the images confirm the hydroxyapatite-dominated structure of the materials across different groups, highlighting variations in surface texture and structural features.

Preparation of CPC-based scaffolds extracts

Scaffold extracts were prepared by soaking CPC particles, including the CPC, BMP-2/CPC, and BMP-2/SCS/CPC groups, in serum-free α-MEM medium at a surface area-to-volume ratio of 3 cm2/mL for 24 h at 37 °C. The extracts were collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to ensure sterility, and stored at 4 °C until further use.

For the BMP-2 release test, the extracts were prepared using a ratio of 20 mg CPC per 1 ml of PBS. The BMP-2 concentration was subsequently evaluated following the manufacturer's protocol.

For BMP-2 absorption efficiency on CPC scaffold, 10 mg of CPC particles were dispersed in 1 mL of BMP-2 solutions at concentrations of 1 mg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively, using ultrasound. Subsequently, the suspensions were incubated in a shaker at 60 rpm and 37 °C for 3 h, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. The BMP-2 concentration in the supernatant was measured using a BMP-2 assay kit, and the amount of adsorbed protein was determined by subtracting the post-adsorption protein concentration from the initial concentration.

Animal surgery

All procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of East China University of Science and Technology (Approval No. ECUST-2024-044). In this study, sterile materials were implanted into both sides of the lumbar vertebrae (L4-L5) of male C57BL/6 mice (Shanghai Jieshijie Laboratory Animal). A total of 144 mice were used, including 140 aged mice (18 months old) and 24 young mice (6–8 weeks old). Mice were anesthetized using a gas anesthesia machine and placed in the prone position. The lumbar vertebrae (L4-L5) were identified using the iliac crest at the hip joint as a landmark. The dorsal surface was shaved with an electric shaver, and the skin was sterilized with povidone-iodine. A midline incision was made to expose the lumbar region, and blunt dissection was performed on both sides of the spinous processes to expose the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae (L4-L5). For each mouse, 20 mg of material was implanted bilaterally into the L4-L5 region. The experimental groups included the control group, BMP-2, hBMP-2, BMP-2/SCS, and hBMP-2/SCS. After implantation, the muscles were sutured, and the wounds were sterilized with povidone-iodine. Postoperative care included close monitoring of the mice for signs of infection or discomfort, and antibiotics and analgesics were administered as needed according to approved protocols.

Micro-CT measurements

At 8 weeks post-implantation, the L4 and L5 lumbar vertebrae, along with the material-implanted areas, were carefully harvested from the mice and fixed overnight in 4 % paraformaldehyde. After drying, the samples were analyzed at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF, Shanghai, China) on beamline BL13HB. Imaging was performed using a monochromatic X-ray beam at an energy of 26 keV, with a sample-to-detector distance of 0.2 m. High-resolution chromatographic images were captured with a 2048 × 2048 sCMOS detector (Flash 4.0, Hamamatsu, Japan) set to a pixel size of 5.5 μm. The three-dimensional architecture of the samples was reconstructed and analyzed using Amira software (2022.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to quantify key trabecular bone parameters, including BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp.

For vascular analysis, a subset of mice underwent vascular perfusion prior to sample collection. The vasculature was flushed with heparinized saline (100 U/mL) and subsequently injected with 5 mL of Microfil (MV-120, Flow Tech Inc.). The samples were stored at 4 °C overnight, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, decalcified, and analyzed at SSRF beamline BL13HB. Imaging was conducted with a monochromatic beam at an energy of 16 keV, while the subsequent 3D reconstruction and quantitative analysis, including vascular parameters such as relative blood vessel volume (BVV/TV), were performed using the same methods as those used for bone tissue analysis.

Gray values for CPC, cortical bone, trabecular bone, microphiled vessels, and decalcified bone were measured using ImageJ software. Specifically, the average gray value for CPC was determined to be approximately 180–200, for cortical bone 140–150, and for trabecular bone 110–130. After decalcification, the gray value for microphiled vessels ranged between 80 and 100, while that for decalcified bone tissue was 60–70. These established ranges were applied in Amira software for precise segmentation of the micro-CT images, enabling accurate differentiation among CPC, bone tissue, microphiled vessels, and decalcified bone. This segmentation strategy ensured reliable separation of these components, thereby guaranteeing the accuracy of the micro-CT quantification analysis.

Sequential fluorescent labeling

To assess dynamic bone formation, calcein labeling was performed by subcutaneously injecting 0.1 % calcein solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 10 mg/kg body weight) dissolved in PBS into the mice at 10 days and 3 days before euthanization. Following sacrifice, undecalcified bone samples were harvested, embedded in resin, sectioned into thin slices using a microtome, and mounted on glass slides.

Calcein double labeling was visualized using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The distance between the two fluorescent labels was measured to calculate the mineral apposition rate (MAR), while the bone formation rate per bone surface (BFR/BS) was determined by combining MAR with the labeled bone surface fraction.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

After μCT analysis, samples were decalcified in 10 % EDTA, dehydrated through graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to observe tissue structure and new bone formation. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, conducted using a commercial kit (Sigma, USA), was employed to evaluate osteoclast activity in the spinal fusion region. Stained sections were analyzed using a Leica microscope (Leica, Germany).

For in vivo immunofluorescence analysis, samples were collected at various time points, including 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, and 8 weeks post-implantation. Retrieved samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 4 h at 4 °C, then rinsed twice with cold PBS to remove residual fixative. The samples were trimmed and immersed overnight in a 20 % sucrose solution containing 2 % polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) at 4 °C. Afterward, the samples were embedded in OCT compound (Leica, Germany) and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm. Immunofluorescence staining was carried out as previously described [59]. Briefly, sections were permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100, blocked with 5 % goat serum at room temperature for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibodies specific for Emcn (Santa Cruz, USA), CD31 (Abcam, USA), Osterix (Abcam, USA), p16 (Abcam, USA), SA-β-Gal (Abcam, USA), and Osteocalcin (OCN, Abcam, USA) at 4 °C for 12 h. Secondary antibodies, matched to the species of the primary antibodies, were sourced from Jackson ImmunoResearch (USA). Lectin staining was performed using reagents from VECTOR Laboratories (USA). Finally, sections were mounted with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was performed to analyze skeletal stem cells (vSSCs), their lineage differentiation, and senescent cell populations within the spinal fusion region. Samples were collected at 3 days and 7 days post-implantation, and single-cell suspensions were prepared from the harvested L4-L5 spinal fusion tissues and material-implanted areas. For vSSC analysis, cells were stained with the following fluorophore-conjugated antibodies: CD200-APC, CD51-PE, Tie2-PerCP-Cy5.5, THY1-FITC, CD105-BV421, and CD45-APC-Cy7, with TER-119-PE-Cy7 used to exclude erythroid lineage cells. Live/dead staining was performed using the Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometry gating was used to isolate vSSC populations and assess their differentiation into stromal and bone/cartilage lineages, which reflect vSSC activity and aging status.

For senescence analysis, performed at 7 days post-implantation, single-cell suspensions were stained with p16-PE for senescence detection and the following myeloid lineage markers: CD45-APC-Cy7, F4/80-APC, CD11b-BV421, and Ly6G-PE-Cy7. Live/dead cell staining was similarly performed, and flow cytometry gating was applied to identify senescent live cells (p16+), senescent myeloid cells (CD45+ p16+), and senescent macrophages (CD45+F4/80+CD11b+Ly6G−p16+). Flow cytometry data were recorded and analyzed to determine vSSC proportions, lineage differentiation profiles, and senescent cell populations across experimental groups. Unless otherwise indicated, all flow cytometry antibodies were purchased from BioLegend.

Flow cytometric sorting of vSSCs

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the spinal fusion region by mechanical dissociation and enzymatic digestion, followed by filtration through a 70 μm filter. The cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies to identify skeletal stem cells (vSSCs): CD45-APC-Cy7 and TER-119-PE-Cy7 for lineage exclusion, CD51-PE for positive selection, and Tie2-PerCP-Cy5.5, 6C3-PE, THY1-FITC, and CD105-BV421 for negative selection. Live/dead cells were distinguished using Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometric sorting was performed using a Sony MA9000 cell sorter, and vSSCs were identified as CD45− TER119− CD51+ Tie2− 6C3−THY1−CD105−. The sorted vSSCs were collected for subsequent in vitro osteogenic differentiation and qRT-PCR analysis.

Cell culture

For osteoclast differentiation, bone marrow monocytes/macrophages (BMMs) were isolated by flushing the tibiae and femora of 6-week-old male C57BL/6 mice with MEM-α medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS and penicillin-streptomycin. The harvested bone marrow cells were cultured overnight, after which non-adherent cells were collected and purified using 50 ng/mL M-CSF (R&D Systems, USA). The purified BMMs were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 7 days in a medium comprising 90 % scaffold extract (BMP-2/CPC or BMP-2/SCS/CPC) and 10 % fresh culture medium, supplemented with M-CSF and RANKL. Osteoclast differentiation was evaluated at the end of the culture period using TRAP staining, performed according to the manufacturer's protocol.

For osteoblast differentiation, vSSCs obtained via FACS were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured in a medium containing 90 % scaffold extract (BMP-2/CPC or BMP-2/SCS/CPC) and 10 % fresh culture medium. After 7 days, ALP activity was measured according to the manufacturer's instructions (Sigma, USA). At 14 days, matrix mineralization was assessed using alizarin red S staining. The staining solution was prepared by dissolving 2 % alizarin red S (Sigma, USA) in purified water, with the pH adjusted to 4.2 using a pH microelectrode (PXF2078, Unisense, Denmark). Quantification of alizarin red S was performed using cetyl-pyridinium chloride extraction, and total protein content was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) for normalization.

RNA analysis by qRT-PCR

To evaluate the effects of BMP-2/CPC and BMP-2/SCS/CPC scaffolds on osteoclast differentiation-related genes and vSSC-mediated osteogenic differentiation-related genes, qRT-PCR was performed. Osteoclasts and osteoblasts were cultured in 6-well plates to ensure sufficient RNA yield, following the same methods described previously. On day 7 of differentiation, cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR was conducted on a CFX96 Touch PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) with an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative Ct method, where 2−ΔΔCt values represent fold changes relative to the control group. Primer sequences for the target genes are provided in Supporting Table 1.

Quantification of growth factors and cytokines

Fresh tissue samples were cryogenically ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen to maintain protein stability. The powdered tissue was dissolved in 1 mL of PBS, thoroughly mixed, and centrifuged at 4 °C to separate the supernatant. The collected supernatant was used for cytokines analysis. ELISA kits were employed to detect CTX-I and PINP (IDS, UK), TGF-β1 (R&D Systems, USA), and TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, and IL-10 (Proteintech, Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology, China). All ELISA experiments were conducted according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM software (version 10.0; GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) on a macOS system. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Biological replicate numbers (N) represent experiments conducted at least three times unless otherwise specified. For all quantitative assessments—including microCT analysis, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, ELISA, qRT-PCR, in vivo and in vitro TRAP staining for osteoclast quantification, and quantitative analysis of osteogenic differentiation—each experimental group included n = 6 independent samples. Comparisons between two groups were made using unpaireds, two-tailed Student's t-tests. For comparisons involving multiple experimental groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was performed as appropriate. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with the following significance levels: ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.005; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.001.
