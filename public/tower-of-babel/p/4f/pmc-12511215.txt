Development and validation of a predictive nomogram for leptomeningeal metastasis risk in NSCLC brain metastases: role of tumor location, driver mutations, and stereotactic radiosurgery
Authors: Shoaib Bashir, Song Jian, Weiping Hong, Hui Wang, Mingyao Lai, Hanbo Lin, Qianwen Liang, Meng Xu, Linbo Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) frequently metastasizes to the leptomeninges, typically following brain parenchymal metastases (BM), with a significant impact on prognosis. However, predictors of leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) development remain poorly characterized. This study aimed to identify independent risk factors for subsequent LM development and establish a predictive nomogram for clinical risk stratification.MethodsThe final analysis included 112 pathologically definite NSCLC patients with BM, treated at Sanjiu Brain Hospital between July 2014 and December 2020, who had not undergone whole brain radiation therapy before LM diagnosis. LM diagnosis was made if the patient had a history of pathologically confirmed lung cancer, new signs and symptoms of the nervous system, and positive CSF cytology or typical MRI findings. The data were retrospectively collected following the initial BM diagnosis until the patient was diagnosed with LM or died of any cause without developing LM. MR images were reviewed independently by two well-experienced radiologists in a double-blind manner. The primary outcome was to identify factors associated with the development of LM following BM diagnosis.ResultsIn the present study, two study cohorts were analyzed: (1) NSCLC-BM patients who subsequently developed LM (n = 56), and (2) NSCLC-BM patients who did not develop LM until death (n = 56). The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 9.9 months (IQR, 4.2–18.2 months) following BM diagnosis. Univariate analysis identified several potential risk factors including EGFR/ALK/ROS1 mutations (OR = 3.868, 95% CI 1.583–10.079, P = 0.003), ventricle- or pia mater-adherent BMs (OR = 10.278, 95% CI 4.203–27.375, P < 0.001), and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) as a protective factor (OR = 0.024, 95% CI 0.001–0.12, P < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed adherent BMs (OR = 9.846, 95% CI 2.981–40.176, P < 0.001) and driver mutations (OR = 5.501, 95% CI 1.444–25.893, P = 0.018) were independent predictors of increased LM risk, while SRS significantly reduced LM risk (OR = 0.029, 95% CI 0.001–0.179, P = 0.002). Fine-Gray competing risks analysis (death without developing LM as competing event) yielded consistent results: adherent BMs (HR = 3.17, 95% CI 1.68–5.97, P < 0.001), mutations (HR = 2.99, 95% CI 1.03–8.70, P = 0.045), and protective effect of SRS (HR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.14–0.46, P < 0.001). A nomogram incorporating these three factors demonstrated excellent predictive performance with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.885 and a C-index of 0.805.ConclusionsPatients with adherent BMs and driver mutations appear to be associated with increased LM risk, while SRS may be associated with reducing this risk. Our novel nomogram incorporating these factors shows promising predictive performance in our cohort, potentially enabling effective risk stratification. These exploratory findings suggest high-risk patients with ventricle- or pia mater-adherent BMs and driver mutations might benefit from consideration of upfront SRS combined with targeted therapy, though prospective validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-025-05220-9.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Brain parenchymal metastasis, Leptomeningeal metastasis, Stereotactic radiosurgery, Nomogram predicting leptomeningeal metastasis risk

Introduction

Leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) represents a devastating complication of advanced systemic malignancies with a uniformly poor prognosis [1–3]. Patient survival typically ranges from weeks to months: 4–6 weeks without treatment, and 3–6 months with treatment [4]. The incidence of LM is approximately 5% among all cancer patients [5]. The most common solid tumors metastasizing to the leptomeninges are lung cancer (predominantly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)), breast cancer, and gastrointestinal malignancies [6]. Among NSCLC patients, 30–64% develop central nervous system (CNS) metastases, of which 4–7% have LM [7]. LM incidence is higher in patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (9.4%) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) (10.3%) mutations [8, 9], which are more prevalent in the Asian population (30–40%) than in Caucasians (10–15%) [5].

Metastatic spread to the leptomeningeal system is postulated to occur via multiple routes, including direct seeding from the brain parenchyma, dura mater (not protected by a blood-brain barrier), hematogenous seeding (mainly through venous plexi), osseous extension, and endoneurial/perineural invasion. Several studies have demonstrated the association between the excision of BM lesions and subsequent LM development, suggesting potential iatrogenic spread [10–13]. Consequently, patients with BM face elevated risk of developing LM. Supporting this relationship, Zhen et al. reported that 52.5% of NSCLC patients with LM had BM comorbidity [14], while another investigation documented this comorbidity in 57.8% of cases [15]. Furthermore, this latter study demonstrated that LM typically occurred as a subsequent event following BM in patients with advanced NSCLC [15]. Similarly, Yin and colleagues identified BM as a potential risk factor for LM development [16].

The harsh leptomeningeal microenvironment, characterized by limited micronutrient availability in cerebrospinal fluid, suggests that malignant cells must acquire additional adaptive capabilities to survive in this nutrient-depleted, hypoxic compartment. These observations collectively support the notion that LM represents a more aggressive biological phenotype with inherently dire outcomes [17], which aligns with the consistently poor prognosis reported across multiple studies [1–3].

Given the poor prognosis associated with LM and evidence that most patients develop LM subsequent to BM in NSCLC, as demonstrated in the aforementioned literature [15], we conducted this retrospective analysis to identify predictors of LM development in NSCLC patients with existing BM. Previous studies have identified surgical resection, tumor distribution, and radiotherapy as potential predictors of LM [18–20]. Recent investigations have further suggested that surgical resection increases the rate of nodular-type LM development; however, this risk can be mitigated through pre-operative irradiation [21–24]. Given that nomograms are widely utilized in clinical practice and represent highly accurate tools for clinical risk stratification [25], we also aimed to develop a predictor-based nomogram. Such a nomogram could potentially assist multidisciplinary teams in optimizing clinical decision-making processes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

Approval from the ethics committee of Guangdong Sanjiu Brain Hospital was obtained for this retrospective analysis of patient clinical data. Due to the retrospective nature of the present study, the requirement to obtain informed consent was waived. This retrospective cohort study comprised two groups of NSCLC patients with BM: [1] those who subsequently developed LM and [2] those who did not develop LM during their clinical course until death.

For the first group, we initially identified 118 leptomeningeal metastatic NSCLC patients with or without brain parenchymal metastasis, who received treatment in our center between July 2014 and December 2020 and had not undergone whole brain radiation therapy (WBRT) before LM diagnosis. Five patients were excluded due to the inability to reliably confirm the precise timing of leptomeningeal metastasis diagnosis, which was essential for establishing the temporal relationship between brain metastases and subsequent LM development. Cases with evidence of LM without BM and synchronous LM and BM (patients who presented with both conditions at initial BM diagnosis, prior to any BM-directed treatment intervention) were excluded. We left with 56 patients who developed LM subsequent to BM diagnosis.

For the second group, 56 patients with NSCLC-BM from the same period of time as the first group, who did not develop LM throughout their entire clinical course until death, were randomly selected from our institutional database by random number generator using R software. This random selection approach ensured unbiased selection and created balanced groups for comparison, enabling identification of predictive factors across the general NSCLC-BM population. All included patients had not undergone whole brain radiation therapy (WBRT) prior to LM diagnosis (for the LM group) or during their clinical course until death (for the non-LM group). The scheme of patient selection is demonstrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart demonstrating the patients’ selection process

Patients included in the present study were required to have complete follow-up records regarding the development of LM or death without developing LM. All the patients should have undergone brain and whole spine magnetic resonance images (MRIs) with gadolinium contrast and CSF cytology (in case of MRI suspicious for LM) to determine the development of LM diagnosis. Contrast-enhanced whole spine and brain MR images were done before performing lumbar puncture (LP) for the CSF cytology to prevent false-positive findings of leptomeningeal enhancement secondary to LP [26]. LM diagnosis was made if the patient had a history of pathologically confirmed lung cancer, new signs and symptoms of the nervous system, and positive CSF cytology or typical MRI findings. The data were retrospectively collected following the initial BM diagnosis until the patient was diagnosed with LM or died without developing LM. The primary outcome of interest was the identification of factors associated with LM development in NSCLC patients with BM. Although our study design pre-defined the presence or absence of LM for group allocation, we analyzed the clinical, radiological, and treatment characteristics at baseline (time of BM diagnosis) to identify factors predictive of subsequent LM development. To diminish missing data, follow-up data were collected retrospectively from the electronic medical database and treating physicians.

Imaging analysis

Two radiologists, both with extensive experience in brain tumors, were responsible for analyzing the imaging data in a double-blind manner. If any discrepancies arose in the interpretation, a third radiologist’s opinion was sought, and the final decision was made based on the majority opinion. Based on the proximity of BM lesions to the pia mater and ventricles, lesions were stratified into adherent or detached lesions. A BM lesion was designated as an adherent lesion when it directly extended into the CSF space (subarachnoid space or ventricles) (Fig. 2A and B) or when there was no brain parenchyma between the BM lesion and the pia mater or ventricles (Fig. 2C and D). A BM lesion separated from the pia mater or ventricles by the brain parenchyma was considered a detached lesion (Fig. 2F and G). For the Patients who underwent surgical resection for BM lesions, the post-surgical contrast-enhanced MRI was analyzed for a CSF space violation; in the case of direct seeding through the ventricular breach, the BM lesion was designated as an adherent lesion (Fig. 2H). The lesion with the closest proximity to the pia mater or ventricles was noted and referred to as the index BM lesion. For each index BM lesion, we measured the shortest distance to both the ventricles and the pia mater. The structure (ventricles or pia mater) with shortest distance was recorded as the neighboring structure for classification purposes. The size of the index BM lesion was measured; size was defined as the largest dimension. T1 contrast images were used to evaluate BM features, including the number of BM lesions, BM distribution, BM lesion size, BM lesions’ proximity to pia mater or ventricles, and features of resected BM.

Data collection

The data of patient characteristics involving the following variables were collected: patient features (e.g., sex, age, smoking habit), tumor features (e.g., primary tumor histology, EGFR/ROS1/ALK mutation status, extracranial metastasis status at initial BM diagnosis, number of BM lesions, BM distribution, proximity of BM lesions to ventricles or pia mater, Index BM neighboring to ventricles or pia mater, index BM lesion size, resected BM features, serum tumor markers including Serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) antigen, cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), cancer antigen 153 (CA-153), neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels at initial BM diagnosis), treatment-related factors (e.g., BM resection surgery, local radiation therapy of Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), target therapy, chemotherapy (including both concurrent and sequential treatment approaches after BM diagnosis)). The extracranial metastatic status at the time of BM diagnosis was determined using clinical reports, oncology codes, and imaging reports. Imaging reports include thorax-abdomen CT and whole-body PET-CT scans.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.1.2 (http://www.R-project.org). Descriptive statistics for quantitative variables were demonstrated as medians (interquartile range, IQR) and categorical variables as numbers (percentages, %). Categorical variables were analyzed via Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous data were subdivided into two groups, and the median value was used as the cut-off value.

We performed comprehensive confounder adjustment using univariable and multivariable logistic regression, as well as Fine-Gray competing risks regression to account for the competing event of death without LM development. The multivariable models included clinically relevant variables identified from univariate analysis.

Logistic regression analysis was used to construct a prediction model. A P-value of a variable had to be < 0.05 in univariable analysis to fit in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. The nomogram was established utilizing the rms package in R. The concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were implemented to determine the predictive model accuracy. Moreover, the risk prediction model for the occurrence of LM in NSCLC-BM patients was constructed using a machine-learning random forest algorithm. In the R library randomForest, the function “rfImpute” was utilized to perform missing value imputations using the proximity matrix from randomForest. The following packages in R were used to reconstruct the LM prediction model, integrate the same variables (index BM proximity, mutation status, and SRS), create prediction plots, and generate multidimensional scaling (MDS) plots: “ggplot2”, “cowplot”, and “randomForest”. The “randomForest”, “stats”, and “dplyr” packages of R were used to train and validate the model. All statistical assessments were two-tailed, and only P < 0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 112 NSCLC-BM patients were retrospectively analyzed in this analysis. Of these NSCLC-BM patients, half (n = 56) were affected by LM, and the other half had no evidence of LM until death. There were 48 (48/112, 42.8%) males and 64 (64/112, 57.2%) females. The patients’ median age was 54 years (range: 27–80 years). The median age of patients in the LM group was 52 (IQR, 45.8–58), and 56 (IQR, 49-65.2) years in the no LM group. Most patients were nonsmokers (88/112, 78.5%). Most patients (101/112, 90.2%) had adenocarcinoma histology, followed by squamous cell carcinoma (11/112, 9.8%). The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 9.9 months (IQR, 4.2–18.2 months) following BM diagnosis. The median follow-up time from initial BM diagnosis was 8.4 months (IQR, 2.2–16.8 months) for the LM group and 11.5 months (IQR, 6.3–19.1 months) for the non-LM group. Predictors of LM

Both logistic regression and competing risks analysis (Gray’s test) were employed to identify potential predictors influencing LM occurrence in NSCLC-BM patients.

Univariate analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that histology (OR 0.084, 95%CI 0.004–0.461, P = 0.020), EGFR/ALK/ROS1 mutation status (OR 3.868, 95%CI 1.583–10.079, P = 0.003), index BM proximity (OR 10.278, 95%CI 4.203–27.375, P < 0.001), SRS (OR 0.024, 95%CI 0.001–0.12, P < 0.001), and target therapy (OR 3.868, 95%CI 1.583–10.079, P = 0.003) were potential predictors of LM development.

Similarly, Gray’s test demonstrated significant association for: index BM proximity (67.9% (95%CI 54.8–81.1%) vs. 30.2% (95%CI 17.1–43.2%), P < 0.001 (Fig. 3A1)), EGFR/ALK/ROS1 mutation status (27.6% (95%CI 10.4–45.0%) vs. 69.0% (95%CI 51.0–86.9%), P = 0.006 (Fig. 3B1)), target therapy (27.6% (95%CI 10.4–45.0%) vs. 69.0% (95%CI 51.0-86.9%) P = 0.006, (Fig. 3C1)), SRS (92.6% (95%CI 61.0-100%) vs. 3.7% (95%CI 0-11.1%) P < 0.001 (Fig. 3D1)), and histology (9.1% (95%CI 0-31.3%) vs. 81.8% (95%CI 55.4–100%), P < 0.001 (Fig. 3E1)).

Both univariate logistic regression and the competing risk analyses suggested actionable mutation, index BM proximity, and target therapy were potential risk factors for LM development. In contrast, squamous cell carcinoma and SRS were potential protective factors. Patients who did not undergo SRS received alternative treatments, including targeted therapy alone, chemotherapy (with or without targeted agents), or observation with palliative care, based on their performance status, molecular profile, and clinical judgment of the treating physician. Multivariate analysis

Due to the high correlation between mutation status and target therapy (both variables demonstrated identical patient distributions and univariate results), target therapy was excluded from the multivariate analysis to avoid multicollinearity issues. Additionally, the LM group had only one patient with squamous cell carcinoma, which cannot serve as a reliable reference. Therefore, the final multivariate analysis included index BM proximity, mutation status, and SRS.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that index BM proximity (OR 9.846, 95%CI 2.98-40.176, P < 0.001), mutation status (OR 5.501, 95%CI 1.444–25.893, P = 0.018), and SRS (OR 0.029, 95%CI 0.001–0.179, P = 0.002) were independent predictors of LM. Similarly, multivariable Fine-Gray competing risks regression analysis demonstrated that index BM proximity (HR 3.17, 95%CI 1.68–5.97, P < 0.001), mutation status (HR 2.99, 95%CI 1.03–8.70, P = 0.045), and SRS (HR 0.25, 95%CI 0.14–0.46, P < 0.001) were independent predictors. Adherent BMs and driver mutation-positive status were risk factors, whereas SRS was a protective factor for LM occurrence. The results of multivariate logistic regression and Fine-Gray competing risks regression analysis are depicted in Table 2.

Subsequent LM risk prediction nomogram

Since both models, constructed using multivariate logistic regression and multivariate Fine-Gray competing risks regression, were statistically significant, the variables of index BM proximity, mutation status, and SRS from the above models were integrated to develop a nomogram for predicting the development of LM in NSCLC-BM patients (Fig. 4). The nomogram provides individualized risk assessment by assigning points to each predictor variable, with the total points corresponding to the probability of LM development.

Performance of prediction models

Conventional nomogram

Our predictor-based novel nomogram for predicting subsequent LM risk demonstrated excellent predictive performance. The model achieved a high AUC of 0.885 (Fig. 5A) and a C-index value of 0.805. Internal validation was performed using a cross-validation method with 10 folds and a bootstrap method with 200 bootstraps, both of which demonstrated robust performance of the nomogram (Fig. 5B and C, respectively). Notably, the factors upon which this novel nomogram was established have previously been reported to be associated with LM development. This consistency with prior research supports the nomogram’s validity.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the LM occurrence predicting model (A). Calibration curves using the cross-validation method and 10 folds (B) and the bootstrap method and 200 bootstraps (C)

Machine learning validation

We further validated our findings using random forest algorithm, choosing the occurrence of subsequent LM as the outcome. Firstly, the prediction model’s predictability was validated using the entire population using the randomForest package in R by growing 500 and 1000 decision trees. Using the entire data set with 500 decision trees, the model’s accuracy was 78.7%, with sensitivity of 83.9% and specificity of 73.2% (Fig. 6A1). With 1000 trees, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were 77.7%, 83.9%, and 71.5%, respectively (Fig. 6A2).

Validation by machine-learning random forest algorithm using entire (A1 and A2), training (B1 and B2), and validation data (C1 and C2), growing 500 and 1000 decision trees. MDS plots for entire (A3), training (B3), and validation data (C3)

For model validation, the cohort was divided into training and testing sets using a stratified 60/40 split. In the training set, accuracy was 84.1%, with sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 84.2%, for the 500 and 1000 trees (Fig. 6B1 and B2). In the testing set, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were 83.7%, 97%, and 61.2%, respectively, when the trees were 500 or 1000 (Fig. 6C1 and C2). MDS plots demonstrated the model’s ability to distinguish between patients who developed LM and those who did not (Fig. 6A3, B3, and C3 for entire dataset, training set, and testing set, respectively).

Discussion

Key findings and clinical implications

The present study compared demographic, tumor, and treatment characteristics of NSCLC-BM patients with and without LM. Patients in both groups had significantly different characteristics, including histology, mutation status, target therapy, index BM proximity to ventricles or pia mater, and SRS local treatment. Patients who developed LM were more likely to have adenocarcinoma histology, adherent BM lesions, lower rates of SRS treatment, and higher rates of driver mutations with corresponding target therapy. Most patients in the LM group had adenocarcinoma histology [7, 15], harbored a driver mutation, and received target therapy [8, 9], aligning with previous studies. These findings suggest that the occurrence of subsequent LM in patients with NSCLC-BM is not a random event; rather, specific patient and tumor characteristics influence LM risk.

Risk factors for LM

To ensure robust identification of LM predictors, we employed both logistic regression and competing risks analytical approaches, accounting for the competing event of death without LM development. Notably, both univariate and multivariate analyses using these different statistical methods yielded consistent results, strengthening the validity of our findings.

In univariate analyses, both approaches identified identical significant predictors: histology, EGFR/ALK/ROS1 mutation status, index BM proximity, SRS, and target therapy. Importantly, multivariate analyses using both logistic regression and Fine-Gray competing risks regression confirmed the same three independent predictors: index BM proximity (OR 9.846 vs. HR 3.17), mutation status (OR 5.501 vs. HR 2.99), and SRS (OR 0.029 vs. HR 0.25), with consistent directional effects across both analytical approaches.

NSCLC-BM patients with ventricular or pia mater adherent BM lesions were significantly more susceptible to developing subsequent LM, which is consistent with a study by Lowe and colleagues in patients with BM from melanoma [27]. Another study reported marginally significant results that BM patients with lesions involving the ventricles or pia mater had increased vulnerability to developing subsequent LM and higher LM hazards [13]. Furthermore, our findings confirm that patients harboring targetable mutations have increased susceptibility to LM, which is consistent with previous reports [8, 9]. Additionally, our findings demonstrated that patients who received SRS had significantly lower risk of developing LM than those who did not receive SRS. This finding is largely consistent with a previous study reporting that post-surgery SRS could significantly decrease LM risk [13]. However, Lowe et al. found no association between SRS and LM; this discrepancy may be attributed to their study population comprising patients with melanoma BM rather than NSCLC [27].

Importantly, we recognize the complex relationship between SRS utilization and other clinical factors in our cohort. SRS omission was more common in patients with driver mutations receiving targeted therapies, as current practice often considers deferring local therapy in favor of systemic approaches with CNS activity for these patients. The observed protective effect of SRS must be interpreted within this clinical context, where treatment decisions are influenced by mutation status and availability of targeted therapies. Despite this potential confounding relationship, SRS remained an independent predictor in our multivariate models that included mutation status, suggesting its protective effect may extend beyond its association with other prognostic factors.

Clinical recommendations

Current ASCO-SNO-ASTRO Guidelines for treating brain metastases recommend that NSCLC patients with asymptomatic brain metastases harboring actionable mutations (specifically EGFR and ALK) can be offered target therapy, with local therapy being deferred until there is evidence of intracranial progression [28]. This practice pattern was reflected in our cohort, where SRS utilization was less frequent among patients with driver mutations. However, our analysis indicates that patients with BM lesions adherent to the ventricles or pia mater face higher risk of developing LM. Given that SRS treatment was associated with reduced LM risk even after accounting for mutation status in our multivariate models, we recommend systematic MR imaging analysis, focusing on identifying BM lesions adherent to ventricles or pia mater, and potentially reconsidering the approach of deferring local therapy in these high-risk patients. For these particular cases, upfront irradiation with SRS in combination with target therapy may be considered, rather than relying solely on targeted therapy despite its CNS activity. However, a prospective study is needed to validate these findings and confirm their clinical implications, particularly to disentangle the complex relationships between mutation status, targeted therapy selection, and SRS utilization.

Predictive model performance

The predictive models developed in this study demonstrated strong performance in identifying patients at risk for LM development. The nomogram incorporating the three key independent predictors showed excellent discrimination ability with high AUC and C-index values. This suggests that these clinical and radiological factors can be effectively combined into a practical tool for risk stratification. The consistency between our conventional statistical approach and machine learning validation further strengthens confidence in these predictors, with the random forest algorithm achieving high accuracy in both training and testing datasets. These complementary analytical approaches provide robust evidence supporting the clinical utility of our risk prediction framework.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its observational retrospective nature may introduce potential selection bias and residual confounding. We acknowledge that baseline imbalances between groups may introduce confounding bias that multivariable adjustment cannot fully eliminate, requiring cautious interpretation of our findings as primarily exploratory in nature. While propensity score matching could have addressed these imbalances, we opted to preserve statistical power given our limited sample size and prioritized developing a clinically applicable prediction model for the entire NSCLC-BM population. Nevertheless, our multivariable approaches included all significantly imbalanced variables as covariates, and the consistency of findings across different analytical methods (logistic regression, competing risks analysis, and machine learning) supports the robustness of the identified predictors. Second, the relatively small cohort size and missing data may limit generalizability. Third, the nomogram lacks external validation, though we partially addressed this limitation through machine learning analysis. Fourth, we analyzed only baseline characteristics at initial BM diagnosis without systematically assessing BM progression or changes in clinical variables at LM diagnosis, potentially missing important insights into LM pathophysiology, as LM may arise from progressive rather than initial BM.

These limitations underscore the need for prospective validation studies with larger, well-matched cohorts and dynamic assessment of BM status throughout the disease course to better elucidate the temporal relationship between BM progression and LM development.

Conclusions

The present study identifies potentially important predictors of LM development in NSCLC patients with BMs. Index BM proximity to ventricle or pia mater and presence of EGFR/ALK/ROS1 mutations appear to be associated with increased risk of subsequent development of LM risk, while SRS may be associated with reduced LM risk. Our novel risk-assessment nomogram, incorporating these three factors, demonstrates encouraging predictive performance in our cohort, though it requires external validation. Despite the exploratory nature of our findings and acknowledged limitations of our retrospective design, these results suggest a hypothesis that patients with high-risk features—particularly those with ventricle- or pia mater-adherent BMs and driver mutations—might benefit from consideration of upfront SRS combined with targeted therapy rather than deferring local treatment. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and determine whether this risk stratification approach could potentially optimize treatment sequencing and improve outcomes in NSCLC patients with brain metastases.
