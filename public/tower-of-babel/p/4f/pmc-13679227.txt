Evaluating different large language model performances in evidence-based dental implantology: a blinded controlled experimental evaluation using expert panel assessment
Authors: Vasilios Alevizakos, Robert Gaudin, Iman Ghanad, Jonathan von See, Theodor von See, Florin Eggmann, Constantin von See
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored for clinical decision-making and evidence-based information retrieval in dental implantology. However, concerns remain regarding the accuracy, reliability, and clinical applicability of different AI approaches, including general-purpose, fine-tuned, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models. This study aimed to compare their ability to provide accurate, evidence-based, and clinically relevant responses to implantology-related questions.MethodsSixteen master’s-level clinical questions were presented to all four LLMs under controlled conditions. Their anonymized responses were independently evaluated by three dental implantology specialists based on three core criteria: specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility, using a 0–10 scale. Descriptive statistics were reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Differences among models were assessed primarily using Kruskal–Wallis tests, and inter-rater reliability was evaluated using Fleiss’ kappa.ResultsLLM4 consistently achieved the highest median scores across all criteria (Specificity: median 9.0, IQR 9.0–10.0; Accuracy: median 9.5, IQR 9.0–10.0; Clinical Utility: median 10.0, IQR 9.0–10.0), significantly outperforming the other models (all p < 0.001). Cohen’s d revealed large to very large effect sizes in comparisons involving LLM4, particularly against LLM2 and LLM3. LLM2 scored lowest and showed the highest variability. Inter-rater reliability was slight across all metrics (κ = 0.078–0.180), indicating moderate evaluator disagreement.ConclusionsAmong the models tested, LLaMA 3.2 + RAG demonstrated superior and more consistent clinical performance in dental implantology. While fine-tuning provided moderate benefits, real-time access to structured guidelines significantly enhanced response quality. Despite low inter-rater agreement, these findings highlight the promise of RAG models in supporting high-complexity, evidence-based clinical domains, while emphasizing the need for standardization in AI evaluation protocols.Keywords: Dental implants, Large language models, Clinical decision support, Artificial intelligence

Background

Dental implantology has become a cornerstone of modern dentistry, providing predictable and long-term solutions for tooth replacement while preserving function and esthetics. As the field continues to evolve, artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being explored as a tool for clinical decision-making, patient education, and research support. Among AI-driven technologies, large language models (LLMs)—such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and fine-tuned or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models—have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in processing and synthesizing complex information [1]. However, their ability to integrate and apply evidence-based knowledge in specialized fields like dental implantology remains a topic of ongoing debate [2–4].

A major challenge in the clinical use of LLMs lies in their knowledge acquisition and retrieval mechanisms [5, 6]. General-purpose chat models, such as ChatGPT, are trained on vast datasets but lack direct access to real-time or updated clinical guidelines [7–9]. This limitation raises concerns about hallucination (the generation of factually incorrect or misleading information), which can be particularly problematic in healthcare applications. While fine-tuned models attempt to address this issue by training on discipline-specific literature, their effectiveness is often constrained by the quality, scope, and periodic updates of the datasets used for training [10, 11]. Meanwhile, RAG models provide a promising alternative by dynamically retrieving external sources during response generation, offering a theoretically more accurate and evidence-based approach [12]. However, the effectiveness of RAG models depends heavily on retrieval accuracy, document relevance, and the model’s ability to integrate multiple sources cohesively, an aspect that has been met with mixed results in prior studies.

The literature presents conflicting viewpoints regarding the most effective AI approach for clinical applications [13]. Some studies suggest that fine-tuned models outperform general-purpose LLMs by reducing factual errors and improving specificity in niche domains [14]. Others argue that RAG models offer superior reliability by maintaining real-time access to structured databases and clinical guidelines [15]. However, concerns remain regarding context fragmentation, where retrieved information may not always be seamlessly synthesized into a coherent and clinically useful response. Additionally, the rapid evolution of implantology guidelines poses a challenge for both fine-tuned and RAG models, as static training datasets may become outdated, and retrieval mechanisms may introduce bias based on the selection of available literature.

Given these uncertainties, this study seeks to assess the real-world performance of general-purpose, fine-tuned, and retrieval-augmented LLMs in applying evidence-based knowledge to dental implantology. By evaluating their responses to expert-level questions, we aim to determine whether fine-tuning or retrieval-augmentation truly enhances clinical accuracy and decision-making utility compared to general AI models. The study further investigates how these models balance specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility, as well as their ability to generate clear, readable, and contextually relevant information for dental professionals.

As AI continues to advance, understanding its strengths, limitations, and potential biases is crucial for integrating it effectively into dental and medical practice. By addressing the controversy surrounding chat-based, fine-tuned, and retrieval-augmented models, this study aims to provide critical insights into the evolving role of AI in evidence-based implantology and healthcare applications.

The questions were designed to reflect the advanced clinical decision-making expected at a master’s course level, with evidence-based treatment guidelines serving as the benchmark for evaluation.

A blinded controlled experimental study design was employed, focusing on three core evaluation criteria: specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility. Specificity measured how directly and comprehensively the responses addressed the clinical questions, while accuracy evaluated alignment with established guidelines. Clinical utility assessed the practical relevance of the responses for decision-making in real-world clinical practice. No advanced prompt engineering techniques were applied. All models received identical prompts under standardized conditions without model-specific optimization, role assignments, few-shot examples, chain-of-thought prompting, or iterative refinement. This approach was selected to maximize direct comparability between models and to minimize prompt-related confounding effects. Their responses were anonymized to eliminate evaluator bias. Three independent dental implantology specialists, blinded to the identity of the models, evaluated the responses on a 0–10 scale for each criterion, producing 48 ratings (3 raters × 16 questions) per model for each criterion. Evaluator ratings were securely and anonymously collected using SurveyMonkey (SurveyMonkey Europe UC, 70 Sir John Rogerson’s Quay, Dublin 2, D02 R296, Ireland), an encrypted online survey platform. All evaluations were performed independently by experienced clinician-scientists with expertise in implant dentistry. No formal calibration sessions or consensus-building exercises were conducted prior to the assessment process. Evaluators applied the predefined criteria of specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility independently.

The reference standard (“ground truth”) was derived from the official treatment guides of the International Team for Implantology (ITI). These evidence-based consensus documents served as the benchmark against which all LLM-generated responses were evaluated. Accuracy ratings reflected the degree of agreement between the generated responses and the corresponding recommendations contained within the ITI treatment guides.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3) and Python (version 3.9.16). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges, were calculated for all evaluation criteria.

Prior to inferential analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Because several groups demonstrated deviations from normality and unequal variances, additional non-parametric analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Descriptive statistics were reported as medians and interquartile ranges because the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were not consistently met. Differences among the four large language models were therefore primarily assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Parametric analyses were not used as the primary basis for inference.

Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa (κ). A κ value greater than 0.75 was interpreted as substantial agreement, values between 0.40 and 0.75 as moderate agreement, and values below 0.40 as slight to poor agreement.

To further account for the hierarchical structure of the data, exploratory mixed-effects models were additionally fitted with model as a fixed effect and evaluator and question included as random effects. These analyses were performed as robustness analyses to assess whether the observed differences between models remained consistent after accounting for clustering within evaluators and questions.

Ethical approval was not required for this study as it did not involve human or animal participants. Evaluators provided informed consent to assess anonymized LLM-generated responses. All data were securely anonymized, and the study adhered to ethical guidelines for research integrity.

Results

Descriptive analysis revealed notable performance differences among the models. LLM4 consistently achieved the highest median scores across specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility, whereas LLM2 showed the lowest median performance and the widest score dispersion (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Assessment of statistical assumptions

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for all evaluation criteria and models. Significant deviations from normality were observed across all model groups for specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility (all p < 0.01).

Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Significant heterogeneity of variances was observed for specificity (F = 6.49, p = 0.0003), accuracy (F = 10.42, p < 0.001), and clinical utility (F = 22.76, p < 0.001).

Given these violations of parametric assumptions, additional non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted as robustness analyses. Significant differences among the four LLMs were confirmed for specificity (H = 76.36, p < 0.001), accuracy (H = 104.83, p < 0.001), and clinical utility (H = 76.82, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Exploratory mixed-effects analyses were performed to account for the nested structure of the data, with evaluator and question included as random effects. The results were consistent with the primary ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis analyses. LLM4 remained significantly superior, whereas LLM2 remained significantly inferior across the evaluated outcomes. No statistically significant differences were observed between LLM1 and LLM3. These findings further support the robustness of the observed performance differences among the evaluated models.

Post-hoc comparisons

To identify the specific pairs of models driving these differences, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) tests were performed. LLM4 significantly outperformed both LLM2 and LLM3 in all criteria (p < 0.01), while LLM1 also significantly outperformed LLM2 in specificity and clinical utility (p < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed between LLM1 and LLM3 in most comparisons.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence of the heterogeneous performance of large language models (LLMs) when applied to expert-level clinical decision-making in dental implantology. Among the four models evaluated, the RAG model (LLM4) demonstrated statistically and practically superior performance across all core criteria: specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility. Its performance was both consistently high and stable, as reflected in its highest median scores and comparatively narrow interquartile ranges across evaluators and items.

LLM4’s architecture, which incorporates dynamic access to domain-specific content during inference, likely accounts for its advantage over other models [16]. This mechanism allows for real-time contextualization of evidence-based guidelines, enabling more accurate, targeted, and clinically useful responses [17]. In contrast, LLM2, which was developed with a focus on enhanced clinical reasoning, underperformed across all criteria. This finding challenges the assumption that clinical intent in model design is sufficient for achieving domain-relevant performance and emphasizes the necessity of both high-quality data access and adaptive reasoning capabilities in clinical LLM applications [18].

Notably, LLM1 also exhibited strong performance, particularly in clinical utility, suggesting that well-trained general-purpose models can still provide substantial value in specialized domains when supported by robust training data. LLM3, while fine-tuned on implantology-specific guidelines, did not match the performance of LLM4, reinforcing the hypothesis that static fine-tuning without retrieval augmentation may limit adaptability in complex clinical contexts.

An unexpected finding of the present study was the comparatively modest performance of the fine-tuned model. Although LLM3 was trained exclusively on evidence-based implantology guidelines, it did not outperform the general-purpose model and remained substantially inferior to the retrieval-augmented approach. Several explanations may account for this observation. Fine-tuning compresses information into model parameters and may therefore reduce the model’s flexibility when confronted with novel clinical scenarios or questions that require contextual reasoning beyond the original training data. Furthermore, fine-tuned models are limited to the information available during training and cannot dynamically access supporting evidence during inference. In contrast, retrieval-augmented systems continuously retrieve relevant source material at the time of response generation, thereby reducing the risk of information loss, knowledge compression, and hallucination. These findings suggest that, for highly specialized evidence-based clinical applications, dynamic knowledge retrieval may offer greater advantages than static fine-tuning alone.

The effect sizes observed in this study reinforce the practical significance of these differences. Comparisons between LLM4 and other models, particularly LLM2 and LLM3, yielded Cohen’s d values well above 1.5, reflecting substantial divergences in output quality that are likely to influence clinical decision-making in real-world settings.

However, inter-rater reliability was notably low across all criteria, with Fleiss’ Kappa values ranging from 0.078 to 0.18, indicating only slight agreement among expert evaluators. This variability highlights an important methodological consideration: even among trained specialists, assessment of AI-generated clinical content can be subjective and influenced by individual thresholds for accuracy, clinical sufficiency, and interpretability [19].

The results of this study hold meaningful implications for the integration of AI into clinical workflows. LLM4’s performance suggests that retrieval-augmented architectures are currently best suited for deployment in high-complexity domains, where up-to-date evidence and precision are essential. Institutions considering AI tools for clinical education or decision support should prioritize models that not only perform well in benchmark tests but also demonstrate access to and contextual understanding of domain-specific guidelines [20].

Moreover, the discrepancy between perceived clinical utility and actual guideline adherence reinforces the importance of human oversight and transparency in AI-generated recommendations. Integration strategies should include clinician training on model strengths and limitations, standardized evaluation checklists, and feedback loops to monitor real-world performance. Ultimately, while LLMs like LLM4 show significant promise, their success in practice will depend on responsible implementation, continuous evaluation, and alignment with human clinical expertise.

The findings of the present study are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating substantial differences in performance among contemporary large language models in dentistry. Chau et al. reported that GPT-4 successfully passed both US and UK dental licensing examinations, outperforming earlier model generations and highlighting the increasing capability of LLMs to process complex dental knowledge [21, 22]. Similarly, a subsequent study in prosthodontic and restorative dentistry demonstrated significantly higher answer accuracy and rationale quality for GPT-4 compared with alternative chatbot architectures. These findings support the notion that model architecture and knowledge integration strategies substantially influence performance in dental applications.

The present study extends previous research by moving beyond examination-style multiple-choice questions and evaluating evidence-based clinical decision-making in implant dentistry. Unlike licensing examinations, implant treatment planning requires not only factual recall but also contextual interpretation and application of evidence-based recommendations [17]. Notably, the superior performance of the retrieval-augmented model observed in the present study suggests that dynamic access to domain-specific knowledge sources may provide important advantages for specialized clinical tasks compared with reliance on static pretrained or fine-tuned knowledge alone [17].

Recent investigations have also demonstrated that strong performance in knowledge-based tasks does not necessarily translate into optimal clinical communication. Rokhshad et al. reported that GPT-4 generated the highest-quality responses to oral oncology-related questions, while important limitations regarding empathy, readability, and citation accuracy remained [23–25]. Although the present study focused on expert assessment of implantology-related content rather than patient communication, these findings collectively suggest that future evaluations of dental LLMs should consider multiple dimensions of performance, including factual accuracy, clinical utility, transparency of reasoning, and communication quality.

The methodologies employed in this study were carefully selected to align with the study’s primary objective of evaluating LLMs’ ability to implement evidence-based knowledge in dental implantology. The use of expert-level questions tailored to a master’s course level ensured that the assessment reflected the complex decision-making scenarios encountered in clinical practice. These questions were derived from evidence-based treatment guidelines, providing a robust foundation for evaluating the accuracy and relevance of LLM-generated responses.

The inclusion of four distinct LLMs—ChatGPT-4, Gemini 1.5, a fine-tuned Mistral 7 model, and a RAG model based on LLaMA 3.2—allowed for a comprehensive analysis of both general-purpose and domain-optimized architectures [2, 26]. This combination offered valuable insights into how fine-tuning and retrieval-augmented techniques can influence the integration of specialized knowledge into LLM outputs [9]. Additionally, the three evaluation criteria (specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility) were well-suited to address the study’s objectives, as they provided a comprehensive view of the models’ ability to align with evidence-based practices and support clinical decision-making [27].

One of the key strengths of this study lies in its use of a blinded, multi-rater evaluation framework. The anonymization of LLM responses ensured unbiased assessments, while the involvement of three independent specialists with expertise in dental implantology enhanced the reliability and validity of the scoring process. The 0–10 scale for each evaluation criterion offered granularity in rating the responses, capturing nuanced differences in model performance [27].

Moreover, the inclusion of readability and quality metrics, such as the Flesch Reading Ease Score, Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level, and the modified DISCERN score, added a critical dimension to the analysis [28]. These metrics allowed for the evaluation of not only the technical accuracy of the responses but also their practical applicability in clinical settings, where clarity and readability are essential.

The use of domain-specific training data for the fine-tuned Mistral 7 model and the retrievable database for the RAG model also represented a methodological strength. These approaches allowed for the integration of evidence-based literature in ways that could address the limitations of general-purpose LLMs, highlighting the potential of domain-specific optimization in healthcare applications.

Despite its strengths, the methodology has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. The small number of questions may limit the generalizability of the findings. While the questions were carefully curated to represent complex scenarios, a larger question pool covering a broader range of topics could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of LLM performance.

Although significant differences were consistently observed across models, several statistical assumptions underlying parametric analyses were not fully met. To address this issue, additional non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis analyses were performed and yielded conclusions consistent with the original ANOVA results. Nevertheless, future studies employing larger datasets and mixed-effects modeling approaches may provide a more comprehensive statistical framework for evaluating LLM performance.

Additionally, the reliance on static text-based questions may not fully capture the dynamic and interactive nature of clinical decision-making, where follow-up queries and clarifications are often necessary. The one-time prompt design, while ensuring uniformity, may have constrained the models’ ability to demonstrate adaptive reasoning.

Another limitation lies in the absence of an in-vivo or real-world testing environment. While this study focused on evaluating the alignment of LLM responses with evidence-based guidelines, the practical application of these responses in clinical settings remains unexplored [29]. Future studies could address this by incorporating simulations or real-world scenarios to evaluate the utility of LLMs in live clinical workflows.

An additional limitation relates to the statistical characteristics of the rating data. Several assumptions underlying parametric analyses were not fully met, including normality and homogeneity of variances. To address this issue, supplementary non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis analyses were performed and yielded conclusions consistent with the original ANOVA results, supporting the robustness of the findings. Nevertheless, future studies involving larger datasets and more advanced analytical approaches, such as mixed-effects models that account for the nested structure of ratings within evaluators and questions, may provide a more comprehensive statistical framework for assessing LLM performance in clinical dentistry.

A further limitation relates to the relatively small number of expert-level implantology questions included in the evaluation. Although each response was independently assessed by three experienced implantologists, the limited question set may have reduced statistical power and may not fully capture the breadth and complexity of clinical implant dentistry. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future investigations incorporating larger and more diverse question sets are needed to confirm the observed performance differences among large language models.

Another limitation is that each question was evaluated using a single response generated by each model. Repeated runs were not performed, and therefore the stability and reproducibility of model outputs could not be assessed. Given the probabilistic nature of large language models, future investigations should incorporate repeated testing to evaluate response consistency and reliability across multiple generations.

The methodologies employed in this study build upon previous research evaluating LLMs in other healthcare domains [30]. Unlike simpler scoring frameworks used in earlier studies, this study adopted a multi-criteria approach, including specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility, which provided a more comprehensive assessment of LLM performance. Furthermore, the incorporation of readability and quality metrics offers a distinct advantage over conventional accuracy-focused evaluations, as it aligns with the practical requirements of clinical communication.

The domain-specific fine-tuning of the Mistral 7 model and the use of a retrievable database for the RAG model reflect advancements over prior studies that relied solely on general-purpose models [31]. These approaches underscore the importance of optimizing LLMs for specific disciplines, highlighting their potential to surpass traditional AI systems that lack such enhancements.

The methodologies used in this study are likely to influence the results in meaningful ways. For example, the multi-rater evaluation process ensures a robust and unbiased comparison of model performance, while the use of evidence-based guidelines as the ground truth strengthens the reliability of the findings [32, 33]. However, certain methodological choices, such as the limited question pool and the static prompt design, might introduce constraints that could affect the generalizability and depth of the results.

The inclusion of fine-tuning and retrieval-augmented techniques is expected to reveal significant differences in model performance. These methodologies provide valuable insights into the relative advantages of these approaches, particularly in addressing the knowledge limitations of general-purpose LLMs.

Future studies could address the limitations identified in this study by expanding the question pool to include a wider range of clinical scenarios and subtopics in dental implantology. Incorporating interactive question designs that simulate real-world decision-making processes could offer a more realistic evaluation of LLM capabilities.

Additionally, integrating other evaluation frameworks, such as patient-centered metrics or case-based simulations, would enhance the practical applicability of the findings. Combining the current scoring approach with qualitative analyses, such as user satisfaction surveys or focus group discussions, could provide deeper insights into the usability and acceptance of LLMs in clinical practice.

Finally, future research should explore the integration of LLMs into live clinical environments, where their responses can be evaluated in real-time scenarios. In addition, present study focused on expert evaluation of clinical content quality and did not assess readability or patient-centered communication characteristics. Future studies should investigate these dimensions to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of large language model performance in dentistry. This would allow for a more thorough assessment of their impact on decision-making and patient care outcomes.

Conclusion

This study assessed the performance of four large language models in addressing complex clinical questions in dental implantology, revealing clear and significant differences in their capabilities. The retrieval-augmented LLM4 consistently outperformed other models in terms of specificity, accuracy, and clinical utility, demonstrating both statistical superiority and practical relevance. Its architecture, which dynamically integrates domain-specific content during inference, appears to offer a decisive advantage in producing evidence-based and contextually aligned responses.

Conversely, the underperformance of models such as LLM2—despite its clinical design focus—highlights the limitations of static or generalized training when applied to specialized, high-stakes domains. The results underscore the importance of both architecture and data access in determining model quality and reliability in clinical applications.

Although evaluator variability remains a challenge, the overall findings support the potential of RAG-based LLMs to serve as effective clinical support tools, particularly in educational and consultative contexts. Future research should explore broader clinical domains, refine evaluation frameworks, and continue to monitor the evolving balance between AI assistance and expert human judgment. With careful integration and ongoing oversight, LLMs like LLM4 may contribute meaningfully to advancing precision care and supporting evidence-based practice in dentistry and beyond.
