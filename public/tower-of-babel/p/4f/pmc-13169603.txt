Modeling and forecasting neonatal mortality in Ethiopia: a comparative study using statistical, machine learning, and deep learning approaches
Authors: Abraham Keffale Mengistu, Muluken Belachew Mengistie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEthiopia faces alarming stagnation in neonatal mortality rates (NMR) at approximately 27 deaths per 1,000 live births, jeopardizing Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 targets. Predictive analytics using advanced computational approaches remains underutilized for guiding interventions in low-resource settings.MethodsUsing national NMR data (1977–2023) from the WHO Global Health Observatory, we compared five forecasting models, ARIMA, Prophet, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM, under unified walk-forward validation. Data were transformed as required by model assumptions. Performance was assessed using RMSE and MAE on the original NMR scale (deaths/1,000 live births).ResultsLSTM achieved the lowest errors (RMSE: 0.72, MAE: 0.58 deaths/1,000 live births), outperforming statistical and machine learning approaches. Forecasts indicate marginal decline to 27.7 (2030) and 27.1 (2034), far exceeding the SDG 3.2 target of ≤ 12. Structural barriers, including rural healthcare access limitations, substandard perinatal care quality, and diagnostic delays likely contribute to this stagnation.ConclusionEthiopia requires a tenfold acceleration in NMR reduction to meet 2030 targets. LSTM-based forecasting could enable proactive resource allocation (e.g., pre-positioning supplies in high-risk districts) but should be interpreted alongside subnational data and uncertainty bounds. This study establishes LSTM as a promising tool for neonatal mortality prediction in data-constrained settings, while acknowledging the need for validation with longer time series and exogenous predictors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01909-z.

Introduction

Neonatal death, or the death of an infant born alive within the first 28 days of life, remains a significant public health burden globally and is a key indicator of maternal and child health [1–3]. Despite global advancement in healthcare infrastructure and targeted interventions, nearly 2.4 million neonatal deaths were recorded globally in the year 2020 alone, accounting for 47% of all under-five mortality, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [4]. This plateau in global neonatal survival stands in stark contrast to the more accelerated declines observed for infant and under-five mortality rates over the past three decades [5, 6]. In low- and middle-income countries, where the majority of these deaths are focused, the neonatal period remains the most dangerous time for child survival [7–9].

In Ethiopia, significant gains have been made in improving maternal and child health outcomes by increasing access to antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, immunization coverage, and health infrastructure strengthening [10, 11]However, Neonatal mortality has not declined at the expected rate [12]. According to WHO and country Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data, the NMR in Ethiopia has been stuck at 27 per 1,000 live births in recent years, with little decline over the last decade [2, 13]. The neonatal mortality rate modeled in this study represents all-cause mortality among infants aged 0–28 days. Understanding the composition of this aggregate measure is essential for interpreting forecast results. In Ethiopia, early neonatal deaths (0–7 days) account for approximately 75% of all neonatal deaths, with leading causes including birth asphyxia (30–35%), complications of prematurity (25–30%), and infections (20–25%) [14, 15]. This cause structure has remained relatively stable over the study period, as evidenced by consistent findings from Demographic and Health Surveys and facility-based studies [2, 13]. We assume this stability in our modeling approach—an assumption justified by the absence of major transformations in perinatal care quality or coverage that would differentially affect specific causes. However, we acknowledge that this assumption may be violated if future interventions target specific causes more effectively than others.

Reducing neonatal death is both a common agenda and a specific target under SDG 3.2 [16], which calls for reducing neonatal mortality to at least as low as 12 per 1,000 live births by 2030 [17, 18]. For Ethiopia, achieving this target remains an uphill task without well-informed action founded on firm evidence. Despite the availability of retrospective data, forecasting future trends in neonatal mortality is not done systematically. Health planning in Ethiopia is largely reactive, with plans often based on outdated figures rather than proactive, data-driven interventions [19–21].The choice of long-memory models in this study is informed by health system mechanisms that create temporal dependencies extending beyond simple autoregressive processes. These include: (1) infrastructure inertia health facilities and transportation networks develop slowly, with effects that accumulate over decades; (2) policy implementation lags national programs often take years to achieve full coverage; (3) intergenerational health determinants maternal nutrition and health status affect neonatal outcomes across birth cohorts; and (4) behavioral change diffusion practices such as exclusive breastfeeding and care-seeking spread gradually through social networks. These mechanisms suggest that neonatal mortality exhibits memory beyond the few years captured by traditional autoregressive models, justifying exploration of LSTM’s longer-memory architecture even with limited annual data.

Traditional statistical forecasting techniques, such as linear regression or ARIMA models, are widely used for mortality prediction [22, 23]. However, they may not be capable of modeling the nonlinearities and complex temporal dependencies in health data. For instance, health research in several sub-Saharan African countries has employed ARIMA models to predict mortality patterns, but these models assume linearity and stationarity, which may not hold for dynamic health systems affected by socio-economic, environmental, and behavioral factors [24, 25]. The underlying causes of Ethiopia’s NMR stagnation include poor quality of perinatal care, limited access to emergency obstetric care, low facility-based delivery rates among rural populations, and delays in recognition and treatment of neonatal infection [26–28].

These chronic challenges underscore the need for precise and timely neonatal mortality prediction to guide public health planning and interventions, yet traditional models may fail to capture their complex, interacting effects over time [29–31].

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), particularly Random Forest, XGBoost, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have shown improved predictive power for health outcomes by modeling nonlinear relationships and handling missing data. However, deep learning methods typically require large datasets for effective training a significant limitation in many LMIC contexts where historical health data may be sparse. This study explores whether LSTM can nonetheless provide value in a data-constrained setting, with the understanding that results should be interpreted cautiously and may not generalize to settings with even shorter time series or different data quality [32, 33]. For example, LSTM models have performed well in COVID-19 trend forecasting [34, 35] and maternal mortality forecasting in heterogeneous settings, often outperforming traditional statistical models [36]. However, their application to neonatal mortality forecasting remains scarce, especially in low-resource settings like Ethiopia. Most existing studies are descriptive rather than predictive, and comparative evaluations of classical, ML, and DL models on the same data are lacking [37, 38]. This methodological gap underscores the need for comprehensive studies exploring whether AI-based models can complement traditional approaches in public health prediction. However, deep learning methods typically require large datasets for effective training a significant limitation in many LMIC contexts where historical health data may be sparse. This study explores whether LSTM can nonetheless provide value in a data-constrained setting, with the understanding that results should be interpreted cautiously and may not generalize to settings with even shorter time series or different data quality [39, 40].

This study addresses three questions: (1) How accurately can statistical, ML, and DL models forecast neonatal mortality in Ethiopia using national-level annual data? (2) Which model class performs best under data constraints? (3) What are the implications of forecast trajectories for progress toward SDG 3.2? By providing systematic comparative evidence, this study aims to support data-driven health planning and method selection for similar LMIC settings. Accurate forecasting of neonatal mortality is not simply a statistical exercise but a vital tool for public health action [41, 42].By predicting trends in mortality and identifying periods of increased risk, health systems can better allocate limited resources, plan targeted interventions, and monitor progress towards national and international health goals [43–45]Against the background of Ethiopia’s persistent challenges in reducing neonatal mortality and the evolving nature of data science, it is both timely and necessary to explore new modeling strategies that can produce more accurate and informative findings to guide policy and practice [46].

Methods

Study design and methodology

In this research, a quantitative, retrospective time series model design was employed to forecast neonatal mortality in Ethiopia based on national-level yearly data. The analytical framework employed a comparative modeling approach, including statistical (ARIMA, Prophet), machine learning (Random Forest, XGBoost), and deep learning (LSTM) models. All of the models were trained on the same dataset with the same preprocessing steps to facilitate fair evaluation and comparison. The simplest performance metric used to compare was RMSE, but additional diagnostics were calculated to discern forecasting robustness and residual behavior.

Data source

Neonatal mortality rates were retrieved from the World Health Organization’s Global Health Observatory (WHO-GHO) database, an open repository of globally comparable health statistics(https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO).

The data collection contains annual records for the NMR of Ethiopia, defined as deaths occurring at the neonatal age (first 28 days) per 1,000 live births. There were other metadata columns, including indicator identifiers, sex and age definition, time and geography codes, and uncertainty bounds. For purposes of this study, the data were narrowed to include records for Ethiopia, both sexes combined, and neonatal age (0–27 days). The “RATE_PER_1000_N” column was selected as the main variable to analyze, which is the point estimate of the NMR. The final dataset was from the earliest year available (1977) up to 2023, and 2024–2034 was assumed as the forecasting horizon. We acknowledge that WHO neonatal mortality estimates are themselves model-based, incorporating data from multiple sources (vital registration systems, Demographic and Health Surveys, census data) with varying quality and coverage. The WHO applies Bayesian hierarchical models to smooth across sources and countries, producing point estimates with accompanying uncertainty intervals that reflect sampling error, model specification uncertainty, and variation across data sources. For Ethiopia, the 95% uncertainty intervals average ± 2.1 deaths per 1,000 live births across the study period.

This input uncertainty propagates through our forecasts; prediction intervals reported in this study reflect only forecast uncertainty conditional on the WHO point estimates and do not account for uncertainty in the input data themselves. To assess the potential impact of input uncertainty on our conclusions, we conducted a sensitivity analysis (reported in Supplementary Material) using the upper and lower bounds of the WHO uncertainty intervals as alternative input series. Forecasts for 2034 ranged from 25.1 to 29.2 deaths per 1,000 live births across these scenarios, underscoring the need to treat point forecasts with caution and consider uncertainty bounds in planning. The propagation of uncertainty follows standard time series forecasting principles: uncertainty in initial conditions compounds over longer forecast horizons, with prediction intervals widening as we project further into the future.

National NMR estimates mask substantial regional heterogeneity. The 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey reported NMR ranging from 22 deaths per 1,000 live births in Addis Ababa to 48 in the Somali region a more than two-fold difference. This variation reflects differences in health infrastructure access, socioeconomic conditions, cultural practices, and environmental factors that our national-level model cannot capture. Additionally, conflict-affected and pastoralist populations are likely under-represented in the surveys underlying WHO estimates. Pastoralist communities in Afar, Somali, and Oromia regions are mobile and often excluded from sampling frames due to logistical challenges; conflict-affected areas may be inaccessible to survey teams during data collection periods, leading to systematic undercounting. This introduces potential coverage bias, with national estimates possibly underestimating true NMR if hard-to-reach populations face higher mortality risks.

Our forecasts should therefore be interpreted as applying to the population represented in WHO’s estimation process, which may not fully capture Ethiopia’s most vulnerable groups. Policy decisions should be informed by subnational data where available, and our national forecasts should be supplemented with regional analyses when planning targeted interventions. The representativeness of national estimates has likely improved over time as survey methodology has advanced, but historical estimates, particularly for the 1970–1990 s, may have greater uncertainty and coverage limitations.

Data preprocessing

To ensure the quality and relevance of the data for time series modeling, various preprocessing steps were undertaken. First, temporal filtering and aggregation were done to retain records that were relevant to the Ethiopian environment. Specifically, only records that referred to neonatal mortality for both sexes and Ethiopia were retained. The dataset was then ordered chronologically by the “DIM_TIME” column to get the time ordering correct, and unnecessary metadata fields such as unique identifiers, publishing state codes, and other useless descriptors were eliminated to make the dataset more manageable and less noisy.

Second, the data set was checked for missing values in the target variable of interest, “RATE_PER_1000_N”, the neonatal mortality rate. Missing value records were addressed with linear interpolation, substituting missing rates with interpolated values derived from surrounding years in an attempt to ensure continuity within the time series. Any completely missing data years, and therefore not available for inclusion within the time index, were excluded for reasons of consistency and avoiding temporal resolution gaps.

To stabilize the variance of the data and to address any heteroscedasticity, a logarithmic transformation was employed. Next, first-order differencing was employed to achieve stationarity, a key requirement for many standard time series models, particularly ARIMA. To respect the distinct requirements of each modeling approach, we applied transformations selectively. For ARIMA and Prophet models that assume or benefit from stationarity, swe trained on log-transformed and first-differenced data following the stationarity tests described above. For Random Forest and XGBoost tree-based models that do not require stationarity and are invariant to monotonic transformations, we trained on original NMR values with lagged features constructed from the untransformed series. For LSTM, which benefits from scaling but not differencing, we applied min-max normalization to the original NMR values to bound inputs between 0 and 1, facilitating gradient-based optimization. All forecasts were back-transformed to the original NMR scale (deaths per 1,000 live births) for evaluation and interpretation, ensuring comparability across models. The validity of these transformations in achieving stationarity was tested by visual inspection of time plots and validated statistically through the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, along with autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation diagnostics.

To train machine learning and deep learning models, the time series was formulated in a supervised learning manner. The time series was converted by applying a sliding window approach to generate lag features.

This rearrangement allowed the application of standard regression algorithms to the task of time series. Data were split into training and test sets (80% for training and 20% for testing) based on chronological order in order to preserve the internal temporal order and prevent data leakage. This study evaluates predictive accuracy only; we do not claim that any model provides causal explanations of neonatal mortality determinants. The inclusion of ML and DL models in a small-sample context tests a specific hypothesis: that these methods can identify nonlinear temporal patterns that improve short-term forecasts, even without the large datasets typically considered necessary for their application. If ML/DL models outperform statistical approaches under these constraints, it would suggest that pattern recognition capabilities may partially compensate for data limitations. If they perform worse, it would reinforce that these methods require larger samples. This comparative framework thus provides evidence on the trade-offs between model complexity and data requirements in LMIC forecasting contexts. The primary value added by ML/DL models in this study is their potential to capture threshold effects, interactions among lagged values, and nonlinear trends that linear models cannot represent, not to identify causal determinants.

Modeling approaches

To comprehensively assess neonatal mortality prediction in Ethiopia, five modeling methodologies were utilized: ARIMA, Facebook Prophet, Random Forest Regressor, XGBoost Regressor, and LSTM neural networks. The models were selected to represent a distinct methodological category, statistical, machine learning, and deep learning, to facilitate rigorous comparison both on accuracy and capture of temporal trend.

Auto Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA)

The ARIMA model, a well-established and commonly used statistical technique for time series forecasting, was used to implement the Box–Jenkins methodology. The model is especially useful in the identification of linear relationships in stationary series. The optimal order of the model was found by merging various methodologies. First, autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) plots were analyzed to determine the appropriate lag structure. Second, various model specifications were evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for choosing the specification that yielded the best fit of fit and simplicity. Log-transformed series were differenced to render the series stationary, and residual diagnostics were executed to verify that the residuals exhibited white noise behavior. Model-produced forecasts were back-transformed to the original scale for easier interpretation.

Facebook prophet

Prophet, developed by Meta (Facebook), is a structural time series model for the handling of seasonality, trend changepoints, and irregular data structures. It is particularly quite robust to missing values, outliers, and shifting trends, because of which it can be applied with health datasets that may have these problems. Prophet was initialized in this study as an additive model with yearly seasonality. The changepoint prior scale, a hyperparameter controlling trend flexibility, was adjusted to maximize fit while minimizing overfitting. The model was estimated on the log-transformed series of the neonatal mortality rate and used to generate forecasts until 2034. The forecast values were exponentially back-transformed to their original scale. Prophet’s flexibility in accommodating different changes in trends made it an insightful baseline against which to compare the normal statistical models.

Random forest regressor

Random Forest is an ensemble learning technique that integrates multiple decision trees’ predictions to perform better generalization and prevent overfitting. In the current application, the Random Forest model was learned from a supervised data set composed of lagged neonatal mortality rate, which was generated using a sliding window method. Major hyperparameters such as the number of trees, max tree depth, and min samples to split a node were optimized using grid search. Five-fold cross-validation was employed to make the model strong and avoid overfitting on the training data. Feature importance statistics were also derived to verify which lag years were contributing the most to the prediction. Random Forest was particularly useful in detecting nonlinear relations and interactions in the mortality data that possibly would not be treated nicely by statistical models themselves.

XGBoost regressor

XGBoost, or eXtreme Gradient Boosting, is a powerful machine learning algorithm based on gradient-boosted decision trees for building predictive models. It possesses high performance, high speed, and high scalability, particularly in structured data sets. For this study, the XGBoost regressor was used on the same lagged-feature data as Random Forest. Hyperparameter tuning was used to tweak the model, varying learning rate, tree depth, and the level of regularization (L2 penalty). Early stopping criteria were also used to avoid overfitting during training. By stage-wise correction of past trees, the XGBoost framework learned complex temporal patterns successfully and yielded an interpretable and efficient solution against traditional and deep learning solutions.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network

One of the recurrent neural networks (RNN) that specifically excels at dealing with sequence data having long-term dependencies and thus is especially suited for time series forecasting tasks is the LSTM model. A two-layer LSTM architecture was used in this study with the TensorFlow model. This relatively simple architecture was chosen deliberately to minimize overfitting given the small sample size. More complex architectures (more layers, more units) were tested but consistently led to validation loss divergence, confirming that simpler models generalize better under data constraints. The effective training sample of 37 sequences (after accounting for the lookback window and validation split) is at the lower bound of what is typically recommended for LSTM, reinforcing our cautious interpretation of results and the need for validation with larger datasets.

We acknowledge that the limited sample size (47 annual observations) is suboptimal for LSTM networks, which typically require larger datasets for robust training. To mitigate overfitting, we employed several strategies: (1) a relatively simple architecture with two layers of 50 units each, (2) dropout regularization (rate = 0.2) after each LSTM layer, (3) early stopping with patience of 10 epochs to halt training when validation loss did not improve, and (4) reduction of the lookback window to 3 years to maximize the number of training sequences (n = 37 after sequence creation). The LSTM’s performance should therefore be interpreted as comparative within this data-constrained context rather than as an absolute measure of its capability. This study tests whether, despite data limitations, LSTM can extract meaningful patterns that simpler models miss. Data were preprocessed using a sliding window to create sequences of lagged observations as input and the current observation as output. Two stacked LSTM layers and a dense output layer constituted the structure. The hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation function was used for LSTM units and linear activation for the output layer in order to allow for continuous prediction. The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer and MSE loss function for minimization. Walk-forward validation was used to simulate real-world forecasting situations such that future data were excluded from training. The number of time steps per individual input sequence (i.e., the number of lags) was determined experimentally to be optimal.

Model evaluation

To assess the forecasting accuracy and generalizability of each model, we employed a suite of statistical performance metrics, including Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and the Coefficient of Determination (R² score). These metrics were selected to capture different aspects of predictive performance, ranging from average deviation to variance explanation and scale-independent percentage error. All evaluations were conducted under a walk-forward validation scheme to ensure realistic forecasting, preserve temporal integrity, and prevent data leakage.To ensure fair comparison across all models, we employed a unified walk-forward validation strategy. For each year from 2000 to 2023, models were trained on all available data from 1977 up to the previous year and used to predict the NMR for the subsequent year. This process yielded 24 annual forecast errors (2000–2023) for each model. Performance metrics (RMSE, MAE, MAPE, R²) were then calculated by averaging across all validation years. This approach preserves temporal order, prevents data leakage, and simulates real-world forecasting conditions where models are used to predict future observations based on past data. All models—ARIMA, Prophet, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM—were evaluated using this identical walk-forward framework.

The RMSE is a commonly used metric in time series forecasting due to its sensitivity to large errors. It measures the square root of the average of squared differences between predicted values ŷt and actual values yt​. RMSE penalizes large deviations more than smaller ones, making it suitable in health forecasting contexts where extreme prediction errors can have serious implications. In addition to these quantitative measures, visual inspection techniques were employed to evaluate the alignment of forecasted values with historical trends. Line plots of actual versus predicted values provided a visual overview of model fit. Residual analysis was also conducted to assess the distribution and autocorrelation of forecasting errors, which can indicate model misspecification or bias. For tree-based models such as Random Forest and XGBoost, feature importance scores were analyzed to identify the most informative lag features. For the LSTM model, learning curves and training-validation loss trajectories were examined to evaluate model convergence and detect overfitting.

Together, these metrics and diagnostic techniques offered a comprehensive evaluation of model accuracy, consistency, and interpretability. They informed the selection of the most suitable forecasting approach for neonatal mortality prediction in Ethiopia and underscored the comparative advantages of advanced machine learning and deep learning techniques over traditional statistical models.

Result

The time series decomposition revealed clear underlying components in the data, including trend, seasonality, and residuals. The trend component indicated a consistent directional movement over time, while the seasonal component captured recurring patterns at regular intervals. The residual component, representing noise or irregular fluctuations, was relatively minimal, suggesting that the data is well-structured and suitable for forecasting (Fig. 1).

We examined the stationarity of the neonatal mortality rate time series; the result confirms that the series is non-stationary. This was also visually supported by the rolling statistics plot, because both the rolling mean and rolling standard deviation had notable trends over time rather than being constant. In addition to this, the ADF test statistic provided a statistic of 2.80 and a p-value of 1.0, which is much greater than the traditional significance level of 0.05. This confirms the presence of a unit root and indicates that the series does not reject the null hypothesis of non-stationarity (Fig. 2). Therefore, differencing is required to transform the series into a stationary form for successful time series modeling and forecasting.

To achieve stationarity, we initially applied first-order differencing. However, the ADF test yielded a test statistic of -2.6925 with a p-value of 0.0753, which exceeds the 0.05 significance level. This result suggests that the differenced series remained non-stationary.

To further stabilize the variance and enhance stationarity, we applied a logarithmic transformation combined with first-order differencing. The resulting series, shown in Fig. 3 (right panel), fluctuates around a constant mean near zero, with no apparent trend or seasonality. The ADF test on this transformed series produced a statistic of -2.8671 and a p-value of 0.0493, which is just below the 0.05 threshold. This confirms that the transformed series is now stationary and suitable for time series modeling. This transformation step was essential for ensuring the reliability and accuracy of subsequent forecasting models, particularly those that assume stationarity.

To further assess stationarity, we plotted the rolling mean and standard deviation of the log-differenced neonatal mortality rate. The rolling statistics were computed using a centered moving window. The plot shows that both the rolling mean and standard deviation remain relatively constant over time, with no significant upward or downward trends. This visual evidence supports the results of the ADF test, confirming that the log-differenced series is stationary (Fig. 4). The stability of these rolling statistics indicates that the series is suitable for time series modeling techniques that assume constant mean and variance.

On the original NMR scale (deaths per 1,000 live births), LSTM achieved the lowest errors among all models (RMSE: 0.72, MAE: 0.58, MAPE: 2.16%), followed closely by XGBoost (RMSE: 0.79, MAE: 0.63) and Random Forest (RMSE: 0.81, MAE: 0.65). Traditional statistical models performed worse, with ARIMA achieving an RMSE of 0.89 and Prophet RMSE of 1.02. All models outperformed the naïve persistence forecast (RMSE: 1.24), with LSTM achieving a 42% reduction in RMSE relative to this baseline. In practical terms, LSTM’s MAE of 0.58 means that, on average, its forecasts deviate from observed NMR by approximately half a death per 1,000 live births, a clinically meaningful level of accuracy given that annual changes in NMR during the study period averaged only 0.3 deaths per 1,000 live births.

The LSTM model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to the other models evaluated. It achieved the lowest error values across all metrics, including RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, and recorded the least negative R² value, indicating a closer fit to the observed data. In contrast, traditional models such as RIMA and Prophet exhibited significantly higher error rates and more negative R² scores. To assess whether LSTM’s performance improvements were statistically significant, we applied the Diebold-Mariano test for equal predictive accuracy. This test compares forecast errors from two models and determines whether observed differences exceed what would be expected by chance under the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy. LSTM significantly outperformed the naïve model (DM statistic = -3.42, p = 0.001), ARIMA (DM = -2.89, p = 0.004), Prophet (DM = -3.15, p = 0.002), and Random Forest (DM = -2.01, p = 0.045). The comparison with XGBoost was not statistically significant at α = 0.05 (DM = -1.87, p = 0.062), indicating that LSTM and XGBoost achieved similar predictive accuracy in this sample. These results suggest that while LSTM consistently achieved the lowest point estimates of error, the improvement over XGBoost may not be robust and should be interpreted cautiously pending replication with larger samples.

All models yielded negative R² values, ranging from − 1.18 (LSTM) to -2.84 (naïve model), indicating that they explain less variance than a simple mean prediction. This finding requires careful interpretation. Ethiopia’s NMR varied within a narrow range (27–32 deaths per 1,000 live births) during the study period, with a standard deviation of only 1.4. When target variance is this low, even modest forecast errors can produce negative R² because the baseline mean prediction becomes difficult to beat. The more informative metrics RMSE and MAE on the original scale show that all models achieve mean absolute errors of 0.6-1.0 deaths per 1,000 live births, suggesting clinically meaningful predictive accuracy despite the negative R². For context, an MAE of 0.6 means that, on average, forecasts deviate from observed values by less than one death per 1,000 live births, a level of precision that can usefully inform planning, even if the model explains little of the total variance. We therefore base our model comparisons on RMSE and MAE, with R² reported only for completeness.

This trajectory remains far above the SDG 3.2 target of ≤ 12 deaths per 1,000 live births, suggesting that without accelerated interventions, Ethiopia will not achieve the 2030 goal (Fig. 5). Although the model indicates a gradual downward trend, the forecast suggests that Ethiopia may not achieve the SDG target of reducing neonatal mortality to below 12 per 1,000 live births by 2030 without significant acceleration in intervention efforts.

Discussion

This study is one of the first to present a rigorous comparison of statistical, ML, and DL approaches to predicting neonatal mortality in an environment of limited resources. The outcomes yield significant methodological insights and implications for public health in Ethiopia and comparable settings. We place the most significant findings below, discuss their significance, and offer wider implications.

The LSTM model achieved the lowest errors among all models compared (RMSE: 0.72, MAE: 0.58 deaths/1,000 live births), outperforming traditional statistical models (ARIMA, Prophet) and tree-based ML models (Random Forest, XGBoost). The negative R² values underscore the limitations of variance-based metrics for near-stationary time series and reinforce our focus on RMSE and MAE as primary performance indicators. A model can be practically useful for forecasting, achieving small absolute errors, while still explaining less variance than the mean if the series itself exhibits little variation. This is precisely the case in Ethiopia, where NMR has remained remarkably stable over decades. Policymakers should interpret these results as indicating that all models can predict next year’s NMR within approximately ± 0.6 deaths per 1,000 live births, providing a useful planning tool, while recognizing that the models cannot explain why the series varies so little, a question requiring causal, not predictive, analysis.While all the models were equally effective in sensing the downtrend NMR of the past in Ethiopia, the ability of the LSTM model to learn complex temporal interactions resulted in significantly more accurate predictions. The projected NMR trajectory registers a minor dip from 27.71 deaths/1,000 live births for 2030 to 27.13 for 2034, far higher than the SDG 3.2 target of ≤ 12 deaths/1,000 live births. The plateau suggests that existing interventions are insufficient to trigger acceleration.

This dominance of LSTM is because of its theoretical prowess in handling sequentially long dependency data, a typical attribute of epidemiological time series in the case of multi-year policy cycles, environmental trends, and healthcare infrastructure growth [47, 48]. ARIMA imposes linear trends, and Prophet is based on externally specified trend changepoints, but LSTM picks up the patterns and memory impacts from observations on its own. For instance, the non-decline of high NMR despite improved coverage in antenatal care may signal lagged system-level effects, a Necessity best captured by LSTM’s recurring architecture. The average performance of ARIMA (RMSE: 0.006; R²: -27.97) serves to underscore the limitation of linear models to handle complex health systems dynamics.

Random Forest and XGBoost offered small improvements over statistical models, but not to the accuracy of LSTM. They excel at handling non-linearity and interactions between features (e.g., lagged NMR effects), but they lack intrinsic ways to model temporal autocorrelation as sequences. That is why their errors are larger (XGBoost RMSE: 0.0015) than those of LSTM. But their interpretability (in terms of feature importance scores) offers actionable knowledge, e.g., what past lags most influence the future NMR, making them valuable for hypothesis generation.

Ethiopia’s plateau at around 27 deaths per 1,000 live births of its endemic NMR is in stark contrast to decreasing under-five mortality rates, demonstrating the heightened vulnerability of the neonatal period.The persistent NMR plateau reflects entrenched structural barriers documented in the literature. Geographic disparities remain fundamental: approximately 80% of Ethiopia’s population resides in rural areas where access to emergency obstetric care is severely limited, and only 20% of health facilities meet WHO standards for perinatal care quality [51, 52]. These systemic failures manifest most acutely in diagnostic delays delayed recognition of neonatal sepsis and birth asphyxia contributes to an estimated 50% of preventable neonatal deaths in Ethiopian facilities [53, 54]. While these statistics derive from cross-sectional health facility assessments and demographic surveys rather than our forecasting models, they provide essential context for interpreting why national NMR has proven resistant to decline despite decades of intervention. The forecasted near-stagnation suggests that incremental improvements to existing programs will be insufficient; achieving SDG 3.2 will require addressing these structural barriers directly. The convergence of our statistical forecasts with qualitative evidence from health system assessments strengthens the case for systemic rather than piecemeal interventions [49, 50]. Our estimates for the future indicate that this plateau will continue without radical action, a sign of entrenched structural barriers to the health system. Geographic differences are the root issue, with 80% of Ethiopia’s population residing in rural areas where there is severely limited availability of emergency obstetric care. Compounding the spatial imbalance are severe quality deficits in perinatal services, with only 20% of facilities delivering necessary care by WHO standards. These failures at the systemic level manifest themselves most acutely in delays in diagnosis, when delayed recognition of neonatal sepsis and birth asphyxia is responsible for nearly half of all preventable deaths [51, 52]. The projections based on LSTM overwhelmingly indicate that incremental increments, such as limited scale-up of existing community health programs, will be insufficient to turn this trend around. Achievement of the SDG target calls for targeted investment in three priority areas: upgrading perinatal care quality with specialized training of midwives in neonatal resuscitation and infection management; intersectoral integration of health systems by linking maternal death surveillance with NMR audits to create accountability feedback loops; and incorporating predictive analytics like LSTM models into national health dashboards to enable real-time risk mapping and pre-emptive resource deployment.

Our estimates suggest that Ethiopia requires a ten-fold quicker pace of NMR decline to meet the 2030 SDG goal, a goal that necessitates urgent, evidence-based policy action. Emergency clinical treatments must be scaled up, particularly Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), which has the potential to reduce NMR by 30% but today reaches fewer than 10% of preterm births due to implementation bottlenecks [53–55]. Complementary financial protection strategies are also needed, including the removal of user fees for emergency neonatal transport to remove cost barriers to time-sensitive care. Above all, our predictive approach enables unprecedented precision in resource targeting: by integrating model findings with health system planning, decision-makers can target ahead of anticipated risk seasons human resources and medical commodities to high-burden regions like Somali and Afar [56–58]. This evidence-based targeting approach transforms static health planning into dynamic response systems, where mortality projections feed readiness programs at the district level before there are seasonal surges in complications among neonates. The intersection of these three levers, evidence-based clinical guidelines, risk protection, and predictive analytics, produces a synergistic system with the potential to snap Ethiopia out of its cycle of stagnation and create replicable models for similar low-resource environments with similar challenges in neonatal survival.

Limitations of the study

This research acknowledges some methodological limitations. First, application of national-level annual data hides subnational fluctuations and intra-annual fluctuations, limiting fine-grained perspectives into regional or seasonal drivers of mortality.The small sample size (47 annual observations) is particularly limiting for deep learning models, which typically require orders of magnitude more data for optimal training. LSTM’s superior performance in this study—achieved through careful regularization may not generalize to other settings with different data volumes or quality. Validation with longer time series and replication in countries with more extensive historical data are needed before LSTM can be recommended as a general-purpose tool for neonatal mortality forecasting. Second, while LSTM was more accurate, its “black-box” nature renders clinical interpretability more difficult, perhaps negatively impacting the trust of health planners. We acknowledge this limitation but note that public health decision-making frequently relies on tools whose internal mechanisms are opaque to end users. For example, weather forecasts guide disaster preparedness without requiring users to understand atmospheric physics, and early warning systems for famine trigger responses based on complex models that few policymakers can interpret. Similarly, LSTM-based mortality forecasts can inform resource allocation and intervention timing even if the underlying pattern recognition remains inaccessible to planners. The key requirement is not interpretability but rather demonstrated predictive validity and clear communication of uncertainty, both of which this study provides. Third, the exclusion of exogenous variables (e.g., climate shocks, conflict, or pandemic-induced disruptions) may render model robustness lower in times of systemic crisis. Finally, extrapolation to other low-resource settings is required to confirm, since contextual determinants like health infrastructure or practices vary geographically.

Future directions

Future research should address several directions to enhance policy relevance. First, validation with longer time series (e.g., monthly or quarterly data where available) would test whether LSTM’s performance improves with higher-frequency observations. Second, the incorporation of exogenous predictors such as health expenditure, skilled birth attendance coverage, and nutritional indicators could transform univariate forecasts into explanatory models capable of simulating policy scenarios. Third, hybrid approaches combining LSTM’s pattern recognition with interpretable components (e.g., attention mechanisms or post-hoc explanation methods) could address the black-box concern. Fourth, subnational forecasting using regional time series would enable geographically targeted recommendations. Finally, prospective validation studies comparing model predictions against actual outcomes as new data become available would provide the strongest test of real-world utility.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LSTM can achieve comparatively accurate forecasts of neonatal mortality even with limited annual data (n = 47), suggesting potential value for resource-constrained settings where longer time series are unavailable. Projections indicate near-stagnation through 2034 (27.13 deaths/1,000 live births), far exceeding the SDG 3.2 target and underscoring the need for accelerated interventions. However, several caveats warrant emphasis regarding how these forecasts should and should not be used:

Forecasting informs but does not directly reduce mortality. Accurate predictions can guide resource allocation, timing of campaigns, and target setting, but they do not themselves save lives. The translation from forecast to impact requires functional health systems, adequate resources, and effective interventions.

Decisions that should not rely on this model include: (a) subnational targeting, which requires regional data we did not model; (b) causal inference about why mortality is changing, which requires determinant-based analysis; (c) predictions under major policy shifts, which would violate the continuity assumption; and (d) precise point estimates for specific years, given the uncertainty bounds around forecasts.

Conditions under which predictions would fail include: (a) structural breaks from major policy reforms, conflict, or pandemics; (b) sustained improvements in data quality that change the relationship between reported and true NMR; (c) shifts in the cause-of-death composition that alter temporal dynamics; and (d) fundamental changes in health system functioning or population health status.

Future research should address three priorities to enhance policy relevance: (1) validation with longer time series and higher-frequency data where available; (2) incorporation of exogenous predictors (e.g., health expenditure, intervention coverage, conflict indicators) to enable scenario analysis and improve robustness to structural change; and (3) development of hybrid approaches that combine LSTM’s pattern recognition with interpretable components to address the black-box concern. For now, LSTM-based forecasts offer a useful complement to existing planning tools providing more accurate short-term projections than traditional methods but should be interpreted alongside contextual knowledge, subnational data, uncertainty bounds, and an explicit understanding of the conditions under which they may fail.
