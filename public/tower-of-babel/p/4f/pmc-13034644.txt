Beyond Standard Culture: Diagnostic Value of Ureaplasma and Chlamydia in Women with UTI Symptoms
Authors: Ali Egemen AVCI, Basri CAKIROGLU, Meftun CULPAN, Turhan CASKURLU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence, diagnostic contribution, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of atypical sexually transmitted pathogens—Ureaplasma urealyticum (U. urealyticum) and Chlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis)—in sexually active women presenting with urinary tract infection symptoms, particularly in cases with pyuria and negative standard cultures.MethodsA total of 185 sexually active women aged ≥18 years presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms and pyuria (≥10 WBC/ high-power field were retrospectively evaluated. Patients with fever or flank pain suggesting pyelonephritis were excluded. Conventional urine culture (significant growth ≥105 colony-forming unit (CFU)/mL), colorimetric culture for U. urealyticum (≥104 CCU/mL considered clinically significant), and multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction for C. trachomatis were performed. Antimicrobial susceptibility of U. urealyticum was assessed using both colorimetric testing and broth microdilution.ResultsConventional urine culture was positive in 62/185 (33.5%) patients, with Escherichia coli detected in 41/185 (22.2%). Among the 123 culture-negative patients, 60 (48.8%) tested positive for atypical pathogens. Overall prevalence rates were 31.4% for U. urealyticum, 17,8% for C. trachomatis, and 1.6% for co-infection, following correction of previous reporting errors. The addition of molecular testing increased the diagnostic yield from 33.5% to 65.9%. U. urealyticum demonstrated the highest susceptibility to minocycline and levofloxacin (86.7%), followed by erythromycin (83.3%) and tetracycline (79.6), whereas marked resistance to clindamycin (77.3%) was noted.ConclusionsAtypical pathogens are prevalent and frequently underdiagnosed causes of lower urinary tract symptoms in women with negative standard urine cultures. Integrating molecular and specialized culture methods substantially enhances diagnostic accuracy and enables targeted therapy. Tetracyclines and macrolides appear to be the most appropriate empirical treatment options in settings with a high prevalence of U. urealyticum.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections in women globally, leading to millions of outpatient visits each year and imposing a substantial healthcare and socioeconomic burden1. The incidence is particularly high among sexually active women, in whom Escherichia coli remains the predominant uropathogen detected through conventional urine culture1, 2.

However, up to 40-50% of symptomatic women may present with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) accompanied by pyuria but negative routine urine cultures despite clear clinical features of UTI, resulting in diagnostic uncertainty, inappropriate empirical antibiotic use, and recurrent or persistent symptoms3, 4. Although widely accepted as the diagnostic gold standard, conventional culture is inherently limited to detecting fast-growing, cell wall-containing microorganisms. Consequently, cell wall-deficient or intracellular pathogens cannot be identified, creating a significant diagnostic blind spot in clinical practice5.

A growing body of evidence suggests that atypical sexually transmitted pathogens—most notably Ureaplasma urealyticum (U. urealyticum) and Chlamydia trachomatis—may contribute to culture-negative LUTS in sexually active women6, 7, 8. These organisms lack a peptidoglycan cell wall and have unique metabolic requirements, necessitating specialized diagnostic techniques. For U. urealyticum, a bacterial load of ≥104 color-changing units (CCU)/mL on colorimetric media is considered clinically significant9. The clinical presentation of infections caused by these atypical pathogens closely mimics acute cystitis, making symptom-based differentiation unreliable10.

Accordingly, current international guidelines [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2021, European CDC (ECDC) 2023] recommend testing sexually active women with persistent urinary symptoms, pyuria, and negative standard cultures for atypical organisms11, 12. Advances in molecular diagnostics, such as nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), combined with improved colorimetric culture methods, have significantly enhanced pathogen detection and enabled targeted antimicrobial therapy13. Importantly, these pathogens frequently demonstrate intrinsic resistance to beta-lactams and variable susceptibility to fluoroquinolones but remain sensitive to macrolides and tetracyclines, highlighting the clinical relevance of accurate microbiological identification14.

Despite this increasing recognition, the actual prevalence, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and clinical implications of atypical pathogens in women presenting with UTI symptoms remain insufficiently characterized, particularly in real-world practice. Addressing this diagnostic gap is essential to optimize empirical therapy, prevent recurrence, and improve patient outcomes.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility of U. urealyticum and Chlamydia trachomatis in sexually active women presenting with pyuria and LUTS, using a combined diagnostic approach that incorporated conventional culture, colorimetric assays, and molecular testing. We hypothesized that atypical pathogens represent a significant, yet under-recognized, etiological factor in culture-negative UTIs and should be routinely considered in the diagnostic evaluation beyond the standard urine culture.

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective multicenter observational study was conducted in the Urology Outpatient Clinics of Ataşehir Memorial Hospital and Hisar Intercontinental Hospital between September 2018 and September 2022 (decision no.: 2023/5, date: 25.05.2023). Sexually active women aged ≥18 years presenting with symptoms suggestive of lower UTI, including dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, or suprapubic pain, were screened for eligibility.

Symptom severity was operationally defined as the number of urinary symptoms reported at presentation. The following symptoms were extracted from clinical charts: dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, hematuria, and malodorous urine. Based on the total number of symptoms, patients were categorized as having mild (1 symptom), moderate (2 symptoms), or severe (≥3 symptoms) symptom burden.

A Diagnosis of UTI was Defined as The Presence of at Least One Urinary Symptom Accompanied by One or More of The Following Laboratory Findings

• Positive conventional urine culture [≥105colony forming unit (CFU)/mL], or

• Pyuria defined as ≥10 leukocytes/ high-power field (HPF), regardless of culture result.

Recurrent UTI was defined as ≥2 symptomatic UTI episodes within the preceding 6 months or ≥3 symptomatic episodes within the preceding 12 months, as documented in medical records and/or patient history. For consistency with the study case definition, each episode was considered a UTI if it was associated with urinary symptoms and supported by pyuria [≥10 white blood cell (WBC)/high-power field (HPF)] and/or significant bacteriuria (≥105 CFU/mL), and had been treated as a UTI by a physician15.

This approach ensured the inclusion of symptomatic culture-negative patients, consistent with commonly used definitions in contemporary UTI research16.

Inclusion Criteria

• Patients were included if they:

• were sexually active women aged ≥18 years,

• presented with ≥1 lower urinary tract symptom,

• provided a midstream clean-catch urine specimen,

• met the UTI definition based on either significant bacteriuria or pyuria,

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria were:

• pregnancy,

• congenital/acquired urinary tract abnormalities,

• active urolithiasis or known obstruction,

• indwelling/intermittent catheterization,

• symptoms or signs suggesting upper UTI (fever ≥38°C, flank pain, systemic involvement),

• antibiotic exposure within the preceding 72 hours,

• incomplete data or inadequate specimen,

Classification of UTI

Only lower UTI (acute cystitis) cases were included.

• lower UTI: Symptoms confined to the lower tract without systemic findings,

• upper UTI (pyelonephritis): Excluded to eliminate confounding infection sources,

Sample Collection and Microbiological Evaluation Urinalysis and Pyuria

Urine was centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 minutes, and the sediment was examined at ×400 magnification.Pyuria (≥10 WBC/HPF) was used as supportive evidence of infection, particularly in culture-negative symptomatic patients.

Conventional Bacterial Culture

Specimens were inoculated onto blood gar and eosin methylene blue agar plates, and incubated at 37°C for 24-48 hours.

Identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the VITEK® 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), and the results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing 2023 criteria.Significant bacteriuria: ≥105 CFU/mL17.

Detection of Atypical Pathogens

A colorimetric system (Mycoplasma IST2, Bio-Rad, France) was used to detect U. urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis. Growth was quantified in CCU, and a bacterial load of ≥104 CCU/mL was considered clinically significant for U. urealyticum.

Detection of Chlamydia Trachomatis

Multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing (Femoflor® Screen, CE-IVD certified) was used. Internal controls were included to ensure analytical validity.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Susceptibility of U. urealyticum was tested using:

1. Qualitative colorimetric testing

2. Broth microdilution interpreted per CLSI M43-A2

Antibiotics tested: Minocycline, levofloxacin, erythromycin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual methods (histograms and Q-Q plots) and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%).

Patients were categorized into three groups: (i) culture-positive cases for classical pathogens, (ii) cases positive for atypical pathogens, and (iii) pathogen-negative cases.

• For three-group comparisons, continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (normal distribution) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (non-normal distribution). When a statistically significant difference was detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-adjusted tests (ANOVA: Bonferroni; Kruskal-Wallis: Dunn-Bonferroni).

• For two-group comparisons, where applicable, continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data.

• Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test; Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were <5.

To evaluate independent risk factors while accounting for confounders, multivariable multinomial logistic regression was performed with pathogen-negative cases as the reference category, reporting adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In addition, to directly compare atypical pathogen-positive cases with culture-positive classical pathogen cases, a binary logistic regression model was constructed (outcome: atypical vs classical), and aORs (95% CIs) were again reported. Candidate variables for multivariable models were selected based on clinical relevance or a univariate p-value threshold (e.g., p<0.10), and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors.

All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics and Conventional Culture Findings

A total of 185 sexually active women met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the cohort was 32.8±7.4 years (range: 18-52), and 29.7% (n=55) reported a prior history of recurrent UTI.

Conventional urine culture was positive in 33.5% (62/185) of patients. Escherichia coli was the most frequently isolated uropathogen, accounting for 66.1% (41/62) of positive cultures (22.2% of the overall cohort), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (12.9%, 8/62) and Enterococcus faecalis (8.1%, 5/62) (Table 1). No no conventional bacterial growth was observed in 66.5% (123/185) of samples.

Detection of Atypical Pathogens

Among patients with negative conventional cultures (n=123), 48.8% (60/123) tested positive for at least one atypical pathogen, while 51.2% (63/123) remained pathogen-negative. Within this culture-negative subgroup, U. urealyticum was detected in 47.2% (58/123) and Chlamydia trachomatis in 4.1% (5/123); Ureaplasma-Chlamydia co-detection occurred in 2.4% (3/123) (Table 2). No clinically significant Mycoplasma hominis growth was detected in any sample.

Co-presence of Atypical Pathogens with Conventional Uropathogens

Co-presence of atypical pathogens and conventional uropathogens was identified in 12.9% (8/62) of culture-positive cases. Symptom severity could not be analyzed according to co-presence status because a standardized symptom severity score was not available for this subgroup in the dataset.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility of U. Urealyticum

U. urealyticum demonstrated the highest susceptibility rates to minocycline (86.7%), levofloxacin (86.7%), and erythromycin (83.3%). Moderate susceptibility was observed for tetracycline (79.6%) and ciprofloxacin (50.0%). The highest resistance rate was recorded against clindamycin(77.3%) (Table 3).

Comparative Analyses Between Pathogen Groups

Comparisons among the three groups (culture-positive classical pathogens, atypical pathogen-positive, and pathogen-negative) are summarized in Table 4. Age differed significantly across groups (overall p=0.014), with the atypical pathogen-positive group being younger on average (29.4±6.3 years) than the classical pathogen group (35.2±8.1 years) and the pathogen-negative group (33.1±7.8 years). A history of recurrent UTI also differed across groups (overall p=0.041) and was more prevalent among atypical pathogen-positive patients (34.4%) than among culture-positive patients (22.6%) (Table 4). Pyuria rates did not differ significantly between groups (overall p=0.317).

Importantly, the overall diagnostic yield increased from 33.5% (62/185) using standard culture alone to 65.9% (122/185) when atypical pathogen testing was incorporated. This improvement was primarily driven by the additional detection of U. urealyticum in culture-negative symptomatic patients, while PCR contributed to identifying C. trachomatis, supporting the value of an integrated diagnostic approach in routine evaluation.

DISCUSSION

The present study highlights the clinical importance of atypical sexually transmitted pathogens, particularly U. urealyticum and C. trachomatis, in sexually active women with LUTS and pyuria, especially when conventional urine cultures are negative6, 7, 8, 11. Our findings demonstrate that 48.8% of culture-negative symptomatic patients carried atypical pathogens, supporting previous reports suggesting that PCR-based diagnostics can reveal pathogens missed by standard culture3, 19, 20, 21.

Concomitant detection of atypical and classical uropathogens was observed in a subset of culture-positive cases. This finding suggests that the presence of a conventional uropathogen does not necessarily exclude atypical organisms. Therefore, identification of a conventional uropathogen should not preclude evaluation for atypical pathogens, particularly in women with persistent or recurrent LUTS.

The relatively high prevalence of U. urealyticum in our cohort (31.4%) compared with some population-based studies (e.g., 1.9% reported by Pereyre et al.18) likely reflects differences in target population and diagnostic methodology. Our study enrolled sexually active symptomatic women with pyuria, a high-risk population for atypical pathogens6, 7, 8. Additionally, the combined use of multiplex PCR and quantitative colorimetric culture with a ≥104 CCU/mL threshold increases diagnostic sensitivity while minimizing misclassification of colonization as infection9, 13. These factors provide a clear explanation for the higher, yet clinically meaningful, prevalence observed.

Integration of molecular diagnostics increased the overall diagnostic yield from 33.5% to 65.9%3, 13, 19. Given that atypical pathogens lack a peptidoglycan cell wall and therefore exhibit intrinsic resistance to beta-lactams5, 14, incorporation of NAAT-based methods into routine UTI evaluation may enhance antimicrobial stewardship and improve clinical outcomes.

Our antibiotic susceptibility findings showed that U. urealyticum remained highly susceptible to tetracyclines and macrolides, while clindamycin resistance and emerging fluoroquinolone resistance mechanisms involving gyrA and parC mutations have been documented20, making these classes less reliable for empirical therapy. Pathogen-directed treatment based on susceptibility results may reduce recurrence and unnecessary antibiotic exposure.

Although C. trachomatis prevalence was low (2.1%), its well-known reproductive health consequences (e.g., pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility) justify early detection and intervention21. The association between significant U. urealyticum loads (≥104 CCU/mL) and symptomatic infection challenges its traditional categorization as a benign commensal9 and aligns with evolving evidence supporting its uropathogenic potential.

Collectively, our results support the routine consideration of atypical pathogens in women with LUTS and pyuria when standard cultures are negative or symptoms persist despite antibiotic therapy, ensuring more accurate diagnosis and appropriate management3, 6, 11, 19.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include its multicenter real-world clinical design, enabling generalizability, and its comprehensive diagnostic strategy integrating conventional culture, PCR, and quantitative colorimetric assays9, 13.

• However, several limitations must be acknowledged: Retrospective design, introducing potential selection bias

• No asymptomatic control group, limiting full differentiation between infection and colonization6

• Resistance analysis limited to U. urealyticum

• No partner screening or longitudinal follow-up, preventing reinfection assessment

• Lack of investigation of molecular resistance markers that are increasingly important for surveillance20, 22, 23

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that atypical sexually transmitted pathogens—particularly Ureaplasma urealyticum—represent a substantial and frequently overlooked cause of LUTS in sexually active women, especially when standard urine cultures are negative. The combined diagnostic approach incorporating conventional culture, PCR, and quantitative colorimetric assays significantly increased pathogen detection, reducing diagnostic uncertainty and guiding appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

Our findings support the routine consideration of atypical pathogens in women presenting with pyuria and persistent LUTS despite sterile cultures, in alignment with current CDC and ECDC recommendations. Recognition of these pathogens is also essential for antimicrobial stewardship, as their intrinsic resistance profiles differ fundamentally from those of classical uropathogens.

In clinical practice, tetracycline- or macrolide-based therapy should be prioritized when an atypical infection is suspected, while reliance on beta-lactams or fluoroquinolones may result in therapeutic failure and recurrence.

Prospective studies including asymptomatic control populations, partner evaluations, and molecular resistance surveillance are needed to further clarify pathogenic thresholds, prevent reinfection, and refine patient-specific management strategies.

Broadening diagnostic algorithms beyond standard urine culture may lead to earlier detection, reduced morbidity, and more precise care for women affected by culture-negative UTI.
