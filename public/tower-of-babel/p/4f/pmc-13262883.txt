SimSmoke simulation models by educational status: past and future US trends and the potential role of policy
Authors: David T Levy, James H Buszkiewicz, Zhe Yuan, Yameng Li, Rafael Meza, Nancy L Fleischer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSimulation models are helpful in anticipating future trends and developing effective tobacco control policies to reduce smoking-related inequities. However, few models have systematically analysed smoking trends by education group.MethodsWe developed four separate SimSmoke models by educational group: less than high school, high school, some college and college and above. Education status is based on the US Census estimates, and smoking prevalence is based on the Current Population Survey-Tobacco Use Supplement (CPS-TUS). Following a first-order Markov process, smoking prevalence evolves through yearly initiation, cessation and relapse, subject to tobacco control policies. The models begin in 2006 and incorporate the impact of tobacco control policies implemented through 2023. They are used to project trends in smoking prevalence and smoking-attributable deaths (SADs) and the impact of policies. The smoking prevalence estimates from the model have also been compared with the CPS-TUS in recent years.ResultsAdults with higher educational attainment had the lowest smoking prevalence with the greatest relative decline from 2006 to 2023. Per capita SADs were highest among adults with less education. Price increases, Tobacco 21 laws and smoke-free air laws were most effective in reducing long-term smoking prevalence. The models underestimate the reduction in smoking prevalence relative to CPS-TUS estimates in recent years, especially among youth.DiscussionWe found major differences in the initial levels and rates of decline in smoking prevalence by education, leading to widening health inequities. Further study is warranted on education-related policy impacts and the relationship of electronic nicotine delivery systems and other non-cigarette product use to cigarette use.

INTRODUCTION

Despite major decline in cigarette use in the USA since 1964,1 smoking disparities persist.2 Smoking prevalence is highest among less-educated adults and lowest among those most highly educated, with disparities widening in recent years.3–8 While local, state and federal tobacco control policies have reduced cigarette smoking,9–12 literature reviews have found limited effectiveness of policies in reducing smoking disparities, with only cigarette prices yielding consistent evidence of reducing smoking disparities.9–13 Greater attention needs to be directed to the equity impact of tobacco control policies.9–12 14 15

Simulation models provide a valuable tool to examine long-term trends and the impact of tobacco control policies on education-related smoking disparities. However, prior studies have given limited attention to these issues.16 17 SimSmoke is a well-validated compartmental simulation model that focuses on the impact of cigarette-oriented policies on smoking prevalence and smoking-attributable deaths (SADs), but previous versions do not distinguish by education.18–29

In this study, we develop separate US SimSmoke models30 by education status. Building on prior work,27 we consider the impact of tobacco control policies on smoking prevalence and SADs by educational attainment. Due to the complex and evolving relationship between cigarette and electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) use and the limited knowledge regarding those interactions by educational status, we do not explicitly incorporate ENDS use in the models. Instead, we infer potential transitions by applying an indirect method previously used for the USA,27 England28 and Canada;29 we compare model projections of smoking prevalence without ENDS to observed estimates from a national survey.

METHODS

We developed four separate SimSmoke models corresponding to different levels of educational attainment distinguished by age and sex. Each model includes separate components for population, smoking prevalence, tobacco control policies and SADs. The models generally begin in 2006, during a period of relative stability in tobacco control policies and smoking prevalence, and project through 2060. Smoking prevalence evolves through initiation, cessation and relapse, subject to changes in cigarette-oriented policies.

Population

As shown in figure 1, individuals in each education group may remain at the same education level or progress to higher levels, for example, from less than high school (LTHS) to high school (HS) or some college (SC), from HS to SC, and from SC to college and above (CA). Educational attainment is defined as the highest level of school completed or degree received at a certain age.

Flowchart for switching between education groups

In online supplemental appendix 1, we develop a population education transition model that underlies each of the education models. We begin the LTHS, HS and SC models at age 15 years in 2006, since smoking initiation occurs almost exclusively at ages 15 years and above27 31 and more than 99% of individuals at that age have less than a HS degree.32 Since few individuals reach CA before age 19 years, the CA population begins at age 19 years. Using 2006 Census data,32 we first distinguish the population33 34 in terms of levels of educational attainment by single age and sex. We then estimate the initial education-related switching rate between education groups by tracking the proportional change between adjacent single ages (eg, if LTHS population is 65% at age 18 years but declines to 27% at age 19 years, we infer that 59% [=1–(27%/65%)] progressed to HS).

Based on our analysis of the 2006 Census, we apply the 2006 LTHS, HS and SC population at age 15 years to all future cohorts and limit educational transitions to ages 15–30 years. However, based on trends in educational transitions from the 2007 to 2020 Census,35 we also estimate an annual drift rate of the percentage of the population progressing to higher levels of educational attainment over time. We map the relevant populations into each education model based on inflows and outflows from each education level.

Smoking

We obtained estimates of initial smoking prevalence by education level, age (15 years and older) and sex from the 2006/2007 Current Population Survey-Tobacco Use Supplement (CPS-TUS). Current smoking is defined as having smoked more than 100 lifetime cigarettes and currently smoking cigarettes either daily or on some days. Former smoking is defined as those who do not currently smoke among people who smoked more than 100 lifetime cigarettes and is further distinguished by the number of years quit through 15 years. Smoking prevalence was linearly interpolated for each age using a moving average (MA-3) process, as shown by age and sex for each education level in online supplemental appendix 2 figures A2:1.a. and A2:1.b.

Following a first-order Markov process, smoking prevalence evolves through yearly initiation, cessation and relapse rates. Due to empirical challenges in measuring initiation rates and to ensure model stability, we measured initiation net of cessation as the absolute change in 2006 smoking prevalence between contiguous ages relative to never smoking prevalence at the younger age. We set the last age of male (female) initiation at the first age (in years) where smoking prevalence reached a maximum: ages 22 (21) for LTHS, 29 (22) for HS, 29 (29) for SC, and 27 (27) for CA.

Smoking cessation occurs after the last age of net initiation. We measure sex, age and education level-specific cessation rates by the percentage of individuals who quit smoking within the last 3–12 months among those who smoked 12 months ago,36 since those who quit in the first 3 months and remain quit tend to offset former smoking adults who quit but later relapsed in the last 3–12 months.37 38 Non-education-specific relapse rates by years quit from previous SimSmoke models30 39–41 are applied to former smoking adults. After 2006, the estimated net initiation and cessation rates are maintained subject to policy-induced changes, as detailed in online supplemental appendix 3.

Mortality rates by educational attainment are further distinguished for never/current/former smoking status using Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network estimates.37 38 The mortality rates (MR) by smoking status for the USA are scaled by the ratio of education-specific MR42 to average MR. Former smoking rates are based on the proportional decline in the relative risk of current smoking by years quit.30 39–41 Differences in the smoking-related mortality ratio by education level are not distinguished, implying the same education gradient across current, former and never smoking status. A recent study suggests a higher smoking-related mortality ratio among those of higher educational attainment,43 but another study found no association.44

For each education model, current SADs by age and sex among adults who smoke are calculated as the number of adults who currently smoke multiplied by their excess mortality risks (current smoking MR–never smoking MR), and similarly for former smoking. Total SADs are summed over people who currently and formerly smoked.45 To assess health equity impacts, we calculate per capita SADs by dividing 2023 SADs for each education group by their respective 2022 population aged 40 years and older, the ages when SADs typically occur.30 39–41

Tobacco control policies

Smoking prevalence, initiation and cessation rates change over time via changes in cigarette-oriented policies since 2006. For a particular policy, the effects of a change in policy are estimated as the percentage change in smoking rates from implementing the new policy relative to the effect size of the previous level, that is, ((post-policy effect size–pre-policy effect size)/pre-policy effect size). When more than one policy is simultaneously implemented, effect sizes are multiplicatively applied, that is, (1+policy effecti)×(1+policy effectj) for policies i and j.

SimSmoke policies include cigarette prices (taxes), smoke-free air laws that limit smoking in public and private workplaces, mass media campaigns that target smoking, advertising and other tobacco marketing restrictions, smoking cessation treatment policies, Tobacco 21 policies, and further youth access restrictions. Table 1 provides policy descriptions and effect sizes as applied to all education groups. The level of and changes in policy are described in Levy et al,27 and the policy effect sizes used in this and in previous SimSmoke models are based on previous reviews as summarised in Levy et al.46 Based on prior reviews,9–13 we consider all policies except price to have the same effect size across education groups. We reviewed studies examining the effect of price on smoking prevalence by education level in online supplemental appendix 4.

The impact of a price change between time periods t-1 and t is generally estimated by the price elasticity, which is measured by the relative change in prevalence divided by the relative change in cigarette price, that is, (Smoking prevalencet–Smoking prevalencet-1)/Smoking prevalence t-1)/((Pricet–Pricet-1)/Pricet-1). We apply the elasticities estimated by Kalousova et al.47 That study adopts the same four education categories as in our models, is the most comprehensive study in terms of distinguishing by age group and obtains results consistent with other education-related elasticity studies. Based on their findings, we scaled the original SimSmoke elasticities by the ratios: 2.7 for LTHS, 1.1 for HS, 1 for SC, and 0.15 for CA (online supplemental appendix 4, table 2). Since the effects of price by education level are inconsistent across the reviewed studies, we present results with both equal and differential price elasticities.

Calibration

After incorporating the impact of tobacco control policies, we calibrate each of the education models by adjusting initiation, cessation and relapse rates. Based on comparisons by age and sex, we calibrated each of the models so that projected 2006–2010 relative reductions were within 2% of the 2006–2010 and half of the 2010–2014 CPS-TUS relative reduction, and future predictions were not less than the survey estimates (online supplemental appendix 5).

Analyses

We apply four types of analysis to each education model: (1) projecting near-term (2006–2023) and long-term (2023–2060) trends in smoking prevalence and SADs (2023–2060); (2) estimating measures of health equity; (3) estimating the impact of past tobacco control policies individually and in combination; (4) comparing recent model projections to survey estimates of smoking prevalence to evaluate model error due to omitted factors, such as the impact of increased ENDS and other nicotine products use.

RESULTS

SimSmoke smoking levels and projected trends

Table 2 presents results from the separate LTHS, HS, SC and CA models using the equal (designated by ‘eq.’) and differential (‘diff.’) price elasticities. We consider the 2006 smoking prevalence (third column) for those aged 18 years and older and trends in terms of relative reductions from 2006 to 2023 (second last column) for the LTHS, HS and SC models. For the CA model, prevalence is reported for those aged 19 years and older due to the lack of Census observations for those aged 18 years. However, the age 19–24 years CA projected prevalence is comparable to the age 18–24 years estimate from CPS-TUS.

The 2006 male (female) smoking prevalence for those aged 18 years and older is 28% (22%) for LTHS, 28% (22%) for HS, 22% (18%) for SC and 9% (8%) for CA. Applying equal price elasticities across education levels, the projected 2006–2023 relative reductions in male (female) prevalence are 24% (29%) for LTHS, 32% (27%) for HS, 37% (29%) for SC and 48% (57%) for CA. As shown in figure 2a,b, those of lower educational attainment generally begin adult smoking prevalence at higher initial levels and typically show less decline than those of higher educational attainment. In comparison, on applying different education-related price elasticities, the 2006–2023 relative reductions increased for LTHS and decreased for other educational attainment groups: 28% (33%) for LTHS, 30% (26%) for HS, 35% (27%) for SC and 45% (54%) for CA.

The effect of policies implemented through 2023

Online supplemental appendix 7, tables A7:1–A7:4 also show the implied effect of policies implemented between 2006 and 2023 by education group. Results are derived by comparing relative changes in smoking prevalence incorporating policies (individually or in combination) to a counterfactual in which all policies are maintained at their 2006 levels. The relative impact of a specific policy is estimated by the relative difference from the counterfactual for that policy alone compared with the summed impact of all policies.

Applying equal price elasticities by education level, SimSmoke projects relative reductions in smoking prevalence from 2006 to 2023 averaged over both sexes as a result of all policies implemented by 2023 as approximately 16% for LTHS, 16% for HS, 16% for SC and 15% for CA. By 2060, the relative reductions in smoking prevalence increase to about 34% for LTHS, 34% for HS, 33% for SC and 33% for CA as the policies continue to reduce smoking rates through increased cessation and reduced initiation. Thus, similar impacts are projected for the different education groups. As a result of all policies, the models project that total 2006–2060 SADs are reduced by 218,240 for LTHS, 576,626 for HS, 313,630 for SC and 118,444 for CA. Applying different education-level price elasticities, the reduction in SADs with all policies implemented increases to 310,280 for LTHS, but decreases to 530,831 for HS, 279,572 for SC and 75,496 for CA.

In terms of individual policies, much of the public health gain results from the 34% inflation-adjusted price increase from 2006 ($4.95) to 2023 ($6.64). With equal price elasticities, SimSmoke projects that prices alone reduced smoking prevalence for each educational group by 7%–8% across education groups by 2023 and by 12%–24% by 2060, reducing premature deaths from 2006 to 2060 by 98,525 for LHTS, 265,078 for HS, 142,512 for SC and 52,216 for CA adults. With different elasticities, price alone reduces the prevalence by 13% for LTHS, 7% for HS, 6% for SC and 1% for CA by 2060, thereby reducing cumulative deaths by 199,070 for LHTS, 214,514 for HS, 104,943 for SC and 5269 for CA adults (online supplemental appendix 7, tables A7:1–4). Increasing the minimum legal purchasing age from 18 to 21 years yields 1%–4% reduction in smoking prevalence by 2023, but 16%–19% reduction by 2060. Implementing smoke-free air laws yields relative reductions of 4%–5% by 2023 and 5%–6% by 2060. Cessation treatment, marketing restrictions, youth access enforcement and media campaigns yield <2% reduction by 2060.

Comparison of SimSmoke and CPS-TUS smoking prevalence estimates

Table 2 shows the levels and relative reduction in smoking prevalence in those aged 18 years and older from SimSmoke compared with 2006–2023 CPS-TUS smoking prevalence estimates. We focus on the periods 2014–2018 and 2018–2023 (not subject to calibration) as a gauge of omitted factors. Applying equal education-level price elasticities, the 2023 LTHS male smoking prevalence from SimSmoke and the CPS-TUS in those aged 18 years and older are close (21.2% SimSmoke vs 21.5% CPS-TUS for males and 15.2% SimSmoke vs 15.3% CPS-TUS for females), and the 2006–2023 relative reductions are reasonably close (24.0% vs 21.5% for males and 29.3% vs 25.4% for females). SimSmoke underestimates the reductions in smoking prevalence for HS. Compared with the CPS-TUS, the model projects 2023 smoking prevalence of 19.1% versus 17.2% for males and 15.8% versus 12.8% for females, and relative reductions of 31.5% versus 37.5% for males and 27.2% versus 39.3% for females. Similarly, the model projects a 2023 smoking prevalence for SC of 13.9% versus 12.2% for males and 12.7% versus 11.3% for females with relative reductions of 36.5% versus 43.5% for males and 29.1% versus 35.8% for females. SimSmoke overestimates the reduction in 2023 CA prevalence: 4.9% versus 5.4% for males and 3.3% versus 5.1% for females with relative reductions of 47.9% versus 41.9% for males and 56.6% versus 32.0% for females. Projections allowing for different price elasticities yield similar rankings in terms of the model versus CPS-TUS estimates, in some cases showing greater variations (eg, at lower education levels).

Table 2 also shows relative reductions for all adults in both the 2014–2018 and the 2018–2023 time periods. SimSmoke predicts slightly better for the 2014–2018 period compared with the 2018–2023 results.

Online supplemental appendix 6, tables A6:1–A6:4 show the relative reductions from SimSmoke and CPS-TUS by age group. For all education groups, the primary difference from the patterns for all adults occurs in the 18–24 years age group and, to a lesser extent, in the 25–44 years age group, where SimSmoke underestimates reductions in smoking prevalence compared with the CPS-TUS.

DISCUSSION

Four separate SimSmoke models by educational attainment showed major differences in smoking trends and SADs across these groups. Adult smoking prevalence was initially highest in 2006 and had the slowest decline through 2023 among those with LTHS and was initially lowest with the steepest decline among those CA. In general, the models project that current and future smoking prevalence remains highest among those with the lowest levels of educational attainment.

Our results are broadly consistent with a recent age-period-cohort analysis by Cao et al.3 While they also found that initiation probabilities were lowest among individuals with at least a college degree and cessation probabilities increased with education in recent cohorts, they also found smoking declined most rapidly among individuals with ≤8th grade education. Other studies in the USA have found that smoking prevalence is highest among those less educated and lowest among those most highly educated, and that these disparities have increased over time.4–8 Similar results have been found for other high-income countries.48–50

The higher rates of smoking among the lower educational attainment groups indicate the potential to lower health inequities by reducing their smoking prevalence. Our analysis projected 2023 SADs of 4.4 per 1000 in those aged 40 years and older with LTHS education compared with 0.9 for those CA, indicating important impacts of smoking on health equity. While our analysis assumes the same risk of smoking-related mortality across education groups and that the socioeconomic gradient in mortality is consistent across smoking statuses, our analysis is consistent with studies that find smoking prevalence in and of itself plays a major role in the higher overall mortality rates among those of low socioeconomic status.51–53 The health risks of smoking by educational status and the consistency of the socioeconomic gradient in mortality across smoking status merit further attention.

Our analysis also shows the importance of specific tobacco control policies to improve health equity by reducing smoking disparities. We found that cigarette prices can play an important role, due to the large impact that taxes generally have on smoking prevalence46 and their greater impact on those of lower educational attainment. Our projections also indicate that Tobacco 21 and smoke-free air laws have been effective policies in reducing LTHS, HS and SC smoking prevalence, but smoke-free air laws and cessation treatments yielded more immediate reductions in SADs than Tobacco 21. However, based on previous reviews,9–13 we do not distinguish by education level the effects of policies other than price. We estimated price effect sizes using studies that obtained positive equity effects, but our literature review indicated a wide variation in estimates across studies. Additional study is warranted on tobacco policy impacts as differentiated by educational status. In particular, it will be essential to distinguish any interactive impacts of ENDS-oriented with cigarette-oriented policies.

While our results suggest clear differences in smoking prevalence and health disparities by educational attainment, SimSmoke smoking prevalence projections differed from the estimates from 2018/2019 and 2022/2023 CPS-TUS. As applied to the USA,27 England28 and Canada,29 the differences in predictions between the model and CPS-TUS provide an indirect estimate of the implicit impact of ENDS and other omitted factors. We found the greatest difference in relative reductions from the model compared with those from CPS-TUS for those of higher educational attainment, suggesting the greater importance of omitted factors such as the use of ENDS and, more recently, oral nicotine pouches for those groups. As such, SimSmoke may underestimate the reductions in recent years by those of HS and CS but overestimate the reductions for those of CA, suggesting less diversity in trends by education group than predicted by our models. The results also indicate relatively large reductions for those aged 18–24 years, especially among those with a CA, a group most inclined to use ENDS.54 55 However, the results generally varied considerably by age, sex and educational attainment, indicating the need for further study of the relationship of ENDS use to cigarette use by levels of educational attainment. In addition, cigar,56–59 smokeless tobacco56 60 (especially oral nicotine pouches61 and heated tobacco product62 use) merit consideration.

The differences in model projections and CPS-TUS estimates in recent years may also occur due to limitations arising from model assumptions, input data quality or random variation. Initiation and cessation rates were differentiated by education level, but we did not incorporate changes in behaviour by recent cohorts, for example, due to the unstable patterns of use in recent years.45 63–65 While the models are calibrated through 2014, the models are not validated. Because we did not explicitly model the influence of ENDS use in the post-2014 period, the calibration through 2014 may bias our analysis of the impact of post-2014 policies. Future modelling efforts should incorporate ENDS use and ENDS-oriented policies and validate those impacts to ensure model accuracy.

Our base analyses were conducted for ages 18 years and older, but many transitions to CA do not occur until age 25 years or later. A similar table using age 25 years and older smoking prevalence is included in online supplemental appendix 8 and yields substantively similar results. Another limitation is that we focus on educational disparities in isolation.

We did not distinguish the role of other factors, such as race/ethnicity, on smoking trends and policy impacts.56 66 67 SimSmoke models by race/ethnicity also show health disparities and do not distinguish by educational attainment.31 Due to the complex interaction between education and race/ethnicity,6 14 15 56 the education models do not distinguish the role of race and ethnicity from that of education. However, racial/ethnic identity (eg, the separate vs intersectional impacts with education)14 15 56 may differentially affect smoking trends and the impact of policies on education groups. Further research is warranted on the interrelationship between racial/ethnic and education-related disparities. We also did not consider changes in the income distribution as a separate indicator of socio-economic status.

Finally, our analysis did not consider industry behaviour. The cigarette industry may influence smoking trends and the impact of policies through lobbying68 and product marketing (via differential pricing and promotion through media and other outlets).69 70 These pathways of influence merit further research.

CONCLUSION

Our paper provides the first set of separate simulation models distinguished by educational attainment. Our analyses indicate major differences in the level and rates of decline in smoking prevalence by educational attainment, with health disparities widening over time between education groups. Our study also highlights the complexity involved in modelling separate education groups and the information is needed to improve model predictability. In particular, it will be important to better understand the impacts of ENDS use relative to cigarette use and the impacts of tobacco control policies on cigarette and other product use.
