Hydrothermal fenton oxidation for sustainable degradation of bioplastics, PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS
Authors: Vikash Singh, Seon Yeong Park, Chang Gyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global production of bioplastics (BPs) continues to rise owing to their perceived environmental advantages; however, their end-of-life management pathway remains uncertain. The present study investigated the combination of hydrothermal conditions with the Fenton reaction (hydrothermal Fenton reaction - HFR), reinforcing the effective oxidation of BPs such as Polylactic acid (PLA), Polybutylene adipate-co-terephthalate (PBAT), Polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), and Polybutylene succinate (PBS). There was observed to be the weight loss of 85.37%, 80.19%, 93.54% and 89.09%, respectively. Quenching study suggests that hydroxyl radical (•OH) mediated oxidation and ester bond hydrolysis under hydrothermal conditions act synergistically to drive the degradation of BPs. SEM morphologies confirm that the polymer surfaces have deteriorated significantly. The DSC results confirm that HFR promotes chain scission followed by secondary crystallization, yielding a material with reduced melting stability but enhanced crystallization behavior. Hence, HFR will stipulate a background for further exploration to resolve the plastic pollution in the ecosystem, and ultimately it will address the United Nations Sustainability Goals (SDG 12 - Responsible Consumption and Production and SDG 14 - Life Below Water).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45868-5.Keywords: Bioplastics, Hydrothermal Fenton Reaction, Hybrid Process, Oxidation, Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)Subject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The exponential growth of global plastic production and consumption has led to severe ecological and waste management challenges, primarily due to the persistence of conventional plastics in natural ecosystems. Despite advances in recycling and waste valorization technologies, less than 10% of plastic waste is effectively recycled, while the rest accumulates in landfills or aquatic systems, contributing to microplastic pollution and greenhouse gas emissions during degradation1,2. Bioplastics (BPs) have surfaced as a promising substitution for petroleum-based polymers because of their biodegradability and renewable origin. The global BPs production is more than 2.4 million tons annually as per the current estimations, and the packaging application alone contributes nearly 60% of the total consumption3. The four biopolymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA), polybutylene adipate-co-terephthalate (PBAT), polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA), and polybutylene succinate (PBS), contribute to the 55% of the global BPs production capacity4.

Even though BPs offer lowered carbon footprints with potential for biological degradation, their end-of-life management remains a decisive concern. In practice, decomposition is often hindered or delayed under natural or industrial conditions because of insufficient composting infrastructure, contamination from mixed plastic waste streams, and the semi-crystalline structure of many bioplastic polymers5,6. Consequently, BPs often persist in the environment or enter conventional recycling systems, where they can interfere with petroleum-based plastics from being properly managed. To enable sustainable material circularity and effective degradation, there is an urgent need for practically feasible technology to be developed1.

In this context, the advanced oxidation process has recently gained attention as a promising technology for degrading/oxidizing plastic waste effectively. The combination of heat, pressure, and catalytic agents facilitates polymer chain scission, hydrolysis, and oxidation, producing organic acids and monomers that can be valorized7–9. Hydrothermal carbonization and liquefaction are specifically efficient for mixed or contaminated waste streams, resulting in aqueous fractions rich in platform molecules/respective monomers such as lactic acid, succinic acid, and 1,4-butanediol10. Furthermore, the hydrothermal Fenton reaction (HFR), which induces hydrothermal catalytic oxidation via Fe2+ and H2O2, results in a rapid and eco-efficient approach for degrading both conventional and biodegradable plastics11,12. In this hybrid reaction, hydroxyl radicals are generated through Fenton chemistry, which violently attack polymer backbones, leading to depolymerization and oxidation of carbonaceous intermediates into biodegradable compounds, H2O, CO2, and CH4.

Our previous work successfully demonstrated the effectiveness of the HFR process for conventional plastics, reaching over 90% oxidation to convert into low-molecular-weight polymers and other nontoxic products under mild reaction conditions11. Extending this methodology to biodegradable polymers such as PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS is therefore essential to evaluate their degradation pathways and distinguish the environmentally feasible routes. The chemical structure of these polymers, comprising aliphatic and aromatic ester linkages, renders them susceptible to hydrolysis and oxidative cleavage under hydrothermal and radical-mediated conditions. Moreover, by controlling temperature and oxidant concentration, the process can be tailored to selectively depolymerize amorphous structures while enhancing crystallinity in residual solids, providing insights into the transformation kinetics of biodegradable polyesters.

Hydrothermal and oxidative degradation studies of bioplastics have reported the formation of individual monomers such as lactic acid from PLA, 2-butenoic acid from PHA, and succinic acid from PBS, validating that the cleavage of ester bonds leads to the regeneration of their fundamental building blocks of monomers13,14. These reaction products can serve as precursors for bio-based chemical synthesis, closing the loop within the circular bioeconomy framework15. Additionally, the valorization of biowaste and bioplastics through thermochemical conversion aligns with global climate mitigation targets by reducing dependence on fossil feedstocks and minimizing carbon emissions16,17.

Therefore, this study aims to explore the HFR degradation behaviour of four typical BPs (PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS), focusing on their structural changes, thermal transformation, and byproduct formation mechanisms. By combining hydrothermal and oxidative processes, the present work seeks to bridge the research gap between bioplastic degradation kinetics and sustainable waste valorization strategies. Eventually, these findings contribute to the broader objective of establishing closed-loop management solutions for biodegradable polymers under the circular bio-economy paradigm.

Experimental section

Materials

The bioplastic samples of pellets were purchased from Serim B&G Co., Ltd. (Republic of Korea). Polylactic acid (PLA), polybutylene adipate-co-terephthalate (PBAT), polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), and polybutylene succinate (PBS) were shown in Fig. 1, which were used for the HFR experiment of sole oxidation. Additionally, they are cryogenically milled and sieved from the Korea Testing & Research Institute (Republic of Korea). BPs in the 125–200 μm size fraction were utilized for comparing their oxidation between sole and mixed phases. The additional details of the materials used are explained in the Supporting Information (SI).

Hydrothermal fenton experiment

All the specifications associated with the experimental setup and the calculations used to determine weight loss in BPs were explained in detail in our previous study on conventional plastics oxidation by this system11. Effect of time (2–16 h for PLA & PBS; 2–20 h for PBAT & PHA), effect of temperature (40–160 °C), effect of H2O2 dose (50–500 mM), effect of Fe2+ dose (100–2000 mg L–1, and effect of BPs dose (0.25–2.5 mg L–1 were studied for all the four BPs. The solution pH for all the reactions was kept constant (pH = 1 ± 0.1). Previous studies confirmed that increasing the pH does not show a positive effect on oxidation11,18. Rather, the acidity of the reaction exerts a decisive influence on the Fenton-hydrothermal oxidation process. That is, under strongly acidic conditions (pH ~ 1), the HFR system generates hydroxyl radicals (•OH) efficiently. Therefore, we decided to keep such an acidic pH for all the reactions. All experiments were performed in triplicate, which is statistically represented by their mean value and corresponding standard deviation.

Characterization of Altered BPs

The morphological change in the BPs was evaluated by the scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4300SE, Hitachi (High-Tech), Japan). The surface functional groups on BPs were explored by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, bench-top Nicolet iS5, Thermo Scientific, USA). The spectra were recorded as an average of 32 scans in the spectral range of 400–4000 cm− 1. The thermal behavior of BPs was accessed by the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, STA 409 PC, Netzsch, Germany) in N2 atmosphere. PLA was heated from 30 °C to 200 °C, PBAT and PHA from − 60 °C to 200 °C, and PBS from − 45 °C to 200 °C. In addition, LC/MS analysis was performed to identify the degradation products using a Vanquish HPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), equipped with a Hypersil Gold™ C18 HPLC Column (Thermo Scientific, USA). Samples were eluted using a gradient elution consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (B), which was run as follows: 90/10% A/B for 3 min, ramped to 0/100% A/B over 14 min; and then returned to 90/10% A/B for 3 min. The flow rate was kept at 0.3 mL min− 1, and the column temperature was set at 40 ℃ throughout the analysis. Eluents were ionized using electrospray ionization (ESI), and mass spectra were scanned over an m/z range of 70-1000. Both positive and negative ion modes were recorded simultaneously.

Results and discussion

Hydrothermal fenton experiment

Effect of reaction time

The degree of BPs oxidation was quantified using weight loss as the primary indicator, and the influence of reaction time on this parameter is presented in Fig. 2. During the initial 2 h of exposure, PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS showed minimal weight loss (~ 1–5%), primarily due to the time required for the reactor vessel to reach the target temperature in the pre-heated oven. After 4 h, PBS showed a substantially higher oxidation of 47.8%, which increased to 91.28% at 12 h and approached near-complete oxidation (~ 99%) by 16 h. For PBAT, the weight loss was 16.2% at 12 h, followed by a sharp rise to 80.1% at 16 h, after which it showed only a slight increase of about ~ % at 20 h (82.2%). PHA displayed a similar trend to PBAT, reaching 93.5% of weight loss at 16 h and increasing marginally to 94.8% at 20 h. PBS achieved 89.0% of weight loss within 6 h and subsequently reached almost complete oxidation (~ 9%) at 8 h. The faster degradation of PBS can be attributed to its relatively low melting point (118 ℃; Fig. 6), which is further associated with higher chain mobility and lower steric hindrance19. Furthermore, the flexible polyester backbone with a fully aliphatic structure facilitates attack by hydroxyl radicals generated under HFR conditions, promoting rapid hydrogen abstraction and β-scission20.

DSC curves for the BPs (PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS) before and after the HFR.

Effect of temperature

Temperature plays a critical role in governing the oxidation of BPs within the HFR system. Initial experiments were conducted below 100 °C, showing negligible oxidation, which is expected because the melting point of all the BPs is higher than this temperature21,22. With the increase in temperature, particularly above ~ 135 °C, the polymer transitions from solid state to molten form. This phase of change greatly enhances the accessibility of the polymer chain to •OH radicals, thereby facilitating oxidation attack18. Moreover, elevated temperatures accelerate the generation of •OH radicals, as demonstrated by established kinetic and thermodynamic principles23,24. The influence of temperature on BPs oxidation, assessed over the range of 40–160 °C, is illustrated in Fig. 3. Significant weight loss was observed above 135 °C for PLA, above 140 °C for PBAT, PHA, and PBS. At 160 °C, PLA, PHA, and PBS exhibited ~ 98% weight loss, while PBAT reached ~ 85%. Collectively, these findings indicated that higher temperatures not only promote polymer chain expansion, but also enhance •OH radical production, resulting in intensified oxidative degradation. Consequently, the synergistic action of the hydrothermal and Fenton processes at elevated temperatures leads to markedly increased weight loss.

Effect of temperature on weight loss for (a) PLA, (b) PBAT, (c) PHA, and (d) PBS (Control conditions: Time = 6 h (PLA & PBS) and 16 h (PBAT & PHA); Fe2+ = 1000 mg L–1; H2O2 = 200 mM; BPs dose = 1 g L–1; pH = 1 ± 0.1).

Effect of H2O2, Fe2+, and BP dosages

The effect of H2O2, Fe2+, and BP dosages on BPs oxidative degradation was examined over the range of 50–500 mM, 100–2000 mg L–1, 0.25–2.5 g L–1, respectively, as are summarized in Fig. S1-S3. At 50 mM H2O2, the weight loss was 58.35%, 22.36%, 74.66%, and 59.02% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively (Fig. S1). Maximum oxidation for all BPs occurred at 200 mM H2O2, beyond which further increases in H2O2 produced a decline in degradation efficiency. At 500 mM H2O2, the weight loss decreased to 60.85%, 45.26%, 72.86%, and 50.62% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively. A comparable pattern was observed for Fe2+ to be 85.37%, 80.19%, 93.54%, and 89.09% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively, at 1000 mg L–1 Fe2+ (Fig. S2). The decline at higher doses is attributed to (i) scavenging of •OH radical by excess Fe2+ and (ii) self-quenching of •OH by H2O2 (H2O2 + •OH → H2O + HO2•)11,25. The effect of BPs dose on weight loss is shown in Fig. S3. Weight loss remained stably unchanged (~ 85%, ~ 80%, ~ 93%, and ~ 89% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively) within a BPs dosage of 0.25–1 g L–1. Increasing the BP loading to 1.5–2.5 g L–1 led to a progressive reduction in oxidation, likely due to insufficient Fe2+ ions and H2O2 concentration to generate the reactive species required for efficient polymer oxidation18,25.

Quenching study and synergy

A quenching study was conducted to elucidate the underlying mechanism of relevant phenomena behind the degradation of BPs under the HFR system. Methanol (CH₃OH) was employed as an oxidation inhibitor due to its ability to rapidly scavenge the •OH radicals immediately upon their formation in the reaction. The outcomes of CH₃OH quenching for all four BPs are summarized in Fig. 4. The CH₃OH concentration was varied from 50 to 1000 mM. At a concentration of 50 mM, the weight loss was 79.7%, 78.5%, 91.5%, and 85.5% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively. A gradual and steady decline in the oxidative degradation was observed with the increasing CH₃OH concentration up to 1000 mM. However, even at 1000 mM, the four BPs still exhibited oxidative degradation of 46%, 53.1%, 82.8%, and 53.9% for PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS, respectively. In contrast, the same HFR system showed only 3.8% weight loss for the conventional plastic (medium density polyethylene, MDPE) under the 500 mM CH₃OH quenching11. Moreover, the experiments were conducted under heat-free, Fe2+-free, and H2O2-free conditions to gain deeper insight into the factors responsible for oxidative degradation. The results for all BPs are summarized in Fig. S4 (SI). Under heat-free conditions, a weight loss of approximately 5–15% was observed, which might be attributed to the presence of •OH radicals even at room temperature26. In the absence of Fe2+, noticeable oxidation still occurred, suggesting that under hydrothermal conditions, H2O2 alone can generate •OH radicals. These results confirm that the •OH radical is not the sole reactive species responsible for the oxidative degradation of BPs; other reactive intermediates or mechanisms may also contribute to the degradation process.

Hydroxyl radical quenching experiment conducted with different concentrations of CH3OH for (a) PLA, (b) PBAT, (c) PHA, and (d) PBS.

All four BPs examined in the present study fall under the category of biodegradable polyesters. Specifically, PLA, PHA, and PBS are classified as aliphatic polyesters, whereas PBAT is an aliphatic-aromatic polyester1. Under hydrothermal conditions, ester bond cleavage is accelerated via hydrogenolysis, leading to chain scission, molecular weight reduction, and mass loss. Chai et al. (2024) demonstrated that PBAT undergoes ester bond cleavage, which is directly correlated with a loss in tensile strength and an increased carbonyl index, confirming hydrogenolysis-driven degradation27. Similarly, Muthuraj et al. (2015) also reported that PBS and PBAT exhibit progressive ester bond hydrolysis under the high temperature and moisture rich conditions28. They also concluded that degradation occurs more rapidly in the amorphous region of the polymer, as diffusion is easier in that region. Furthermore, Im et al. (2024) found that hydrothermal pretreatment at elevated temperatures induces the breakdown of ester linkages in PLA, PHA, PBAT, and PBS, significantly enhancing methane production potential during anaerobic digestion29. Collectively, this body of evidence suggests that •OH radical-mediated oxidation and ester bond hydrolysis under hydrothermal conditions act synergistically to drive the degradation of BPs in the HFR system.

Morphological changes

The SEM micrographs in Fig. 5 illustrate the morphological transformations of PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS before and after exposure to the HFR system. Prior to treatment, all bioplastics exhibited smooth, compact, and relatively homogeneous surfaces characteristic of semi-crystalline polyesters. Following HFR exposure, pronounced morphological alteration was observed across all polymers, with surfaces showing cracks, pits, voids, and significant roughening, indicating strong oxidative attack by hydroxyl radicals and thermally induced hydrolysis.

SEM images of before (B) and after (A) HFR: (a, b) PLA, (c, d) PBAT, (e, f) PHA, and (g, h) PBS. Scale bars are 50 μm for PLA, PBAT, and PHA, and 250 μm for PBS.

For PLA, the originally dense structure (Fig. 5a) evolved into a porous, eroded morphology (Fig. 5b), consistent with oxidative cleavage of ester bonds and depolymerization of amorphous domains. Similar •OH-induced fragmentation and pore formation in PLA have been reported during Fenton-like and thermo-oxidative treatments30. PBAT displayed a transition from a uniform surface to one with collapsed cavities (Fig. 5c, d), confirming scission of aliphatic segments and partial decomposition of aromatic units3,31.

PHA and PBS, which initially appeared to be compact and continuous surfaces (Fig. 5e, g), but developed extensive fissures and holes after treatment (Fig. 5f, h), reflecting accelerated ester bond hydrolysis and oxidative decarboxylation behaviour consistent with other studies on aliphatic polyester degradation under oxidative or hydrothermal environments32,33.

Overall, the SEM observations confirm that HFR triggers combined radical oxidation and hydrolytic fragmentation, producing extensive morphological changes and polymer chain scission. They provide direct visual evidence of oxidative degradation pathways and demonstrate the feasibility of HFR as a practical strategy for bioplastic degradation under realistic environmental conditions.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS before and after the HFR are presented in Fig. S5, S6 (SI). Characteristic absorption bands of ester carbonyl (C = O) stretching at 1740 –1720 cm–1 and C-O-C stretching between 1180 –1080 cm–1 were observed for all samples prior to oxidation, possessing their aliphatic polyester backbones. After HFR treatment, noticeable changes were detected in both the intensity and position of these bands, signifying extensive oxidative modification and hydrolysis. For PLA, a reduction in the C = O peak intensity and the emergence of a broad O-H stretching band at ~ 3400 cm–1 indicated hydrolytic cleavage of ester bonds and formation of hydroxyl-terminated fragments as similar spectral shifts in oxidized PLA have been attributed to chain scission and carboxylic acid generation30. PBAT exhibited weakening of aromatic C = C peaks (around 1600 cm–1) and broadening of the C = O signal, suggesting both oxidative attack on aromatic moieties and cleavage of aliphatic segments34. In the case of PHA, significant loss of ester and C-O peaks along with increased O-H stretching confirmed oxidation-driven β-oxidation and formation of shorter carboxylic species35. Similarly, PBS displayed a pronounced decrease in the 1715 cm–1 of carbonyl band and the appearance of new peaks near 1650 cm–1, corresponding to conjugated C = O groups and oxidized intermediates.

Overall, the spectral transformations confirm that HFR induces simultaneous oxidative and hydrolytic degradation across all bioplastics, leading to cleavage of ester linkages and formation of oxygenated groups (-OH, -COOH). These chemical alterations, corroborated by morphological observations, strongly support radical-driven chain scission and partial degradation under hydrothermal Fenton oxidation36,37.

DSC and LC/MS analysis

The DSC analysis was performed for the pristine BPs and after the HRF treatment to know the changes that happened in the thermal behavior, molecular structure, and processing characteristics. The results are summarized in Fig. 6. After HFR, PLA shows a slight increase in Tg (from ~ 67 °C to ~ 73 °C) and a reduction in Tm (from 151 °C to 147 °C). The marginal rise in Tg suggests removal of amorphous oligomers and increased rigidity due to limited cross-linking, while the reduction in Tm reflects decreased molecular weight and disrupted crystalline regions38. Hydroxyl radicals attack the ester linkages, promoting chain scission and random oxidation that hinder regular crystallization. The oxidative or photo-Fenton degradation of PLA decreases crystallinity and thermal stability by destabilizing the α-form crystal structure6.

PBAT shows a slight drop in Tg (from − 27 °C to − 39 °C), minor Tc shift ( ∼52 °C to ∼50 °C), and Tm increase (∼120 °C to ∼124 °C). The reduction in Tg implies increased chain mobility due to oxidative depolymerization of the soft aliphatic segments. However, the rise in Tm suggests that, following oxidative degradation of PBAT, the resulting smaller polymer fragments reorganize into more ordered crystallites. These trends align with Chen et al. (2023) and Mtibe et al. (2022), who showed that Fenton and hydrothermal aging promote selective chain scission in the adipate phase while enhancing aromatic crystallinity33,34.

For PHA, Tg and Tc increase (− 43 °C to − 39 °C and 94 °C to 102 °C) and Tm rises (149 °C to 158 °C) after HFR. The increase in all transitions indicates oxidative removal of amorphous impurities and formation of shorter, more crystallizable chain fragments. Hydroxyl radicals preferentially oxidize secondary alcohols in hydroxyalkanoates, leading to denser crystal packing and higher crystallinity. These results correlate with Mondol et al. (2025), who found that oxidative hydrolysis enhances the structural order of PHAs through chain-end condensation and recrystallization phenomena39.

After the HFR, PBS exhibited slight increases in Tg (− 43 °C to − 41 °C) and Tc (86 °C to 98 °C), accompanied by a notable decrease in Tm (118 °C to 105 °C). These shifts indicate oxidative chain scission caused by hydroxyl radicals, leading to shorter polymer chains that crystallize more easily, but form fewer perfect lamellae. The Tg rise suggests restricted amorphous mobility took place due to oxidation-induced hydrogen bonding, while the higher Tc reflects enhanced nucleation by low-molecular-weight fragments. The reduced Tm confirms partial degradation of crystalline order, consistent with literature reports on hydrothermally or oxidatively aged PBS40–43. Overall, the DSC data confirm that HFR promotes chain scission followed by secondary crystallization, yielding a material with reduced melting stability but enhanced crystallization behavior.

LC/MS analysis was performed to identify the degradation products after the HFR. Several peaks in the leachate obtained under optimal oxidation conditions were assigned by matching the mass spectrum and molecular weights of oligomers derived from the esterification reactions (chromatograms are shown in Fig. S7-S9 (SI)). After the degradation of PHA and PBAT, the monomers of 3-Hydroxybutyric acid (RT = 1.30 min) and adipic acid (RT = 3.18 min) were detected, respectively. In addition, ethyl myristate (RT = 14.07 min), steric acid (RT = 14.38 min), and other intermediate products were commonly identified among the degradation products. The detection of these lower molecular weight organic compounds indicates that oxidative chain scission occurred during the HFR, decomposing the polymer backbone into smaller fragments and confirming the effective oxidative depolymerization of the BPs under the applied conditions.

Effect of BPs size and mixing ratio on oxidation by HFR

The oxidative degradation of bioplastics, PLA, PBAT, PHA, PBS, and their respective mixtures (PLA+PBAT and PHA + PBS), was evaluated under the HFR system using particle sizes in the range of 125–200 μm (Fig. 7). The observed weight loss values demonstrate substantial oxidations for all samples, with variations depending on polymer type and blending ratio. Pure PLA, PHA, and PBS exhibited the highest weight losses (> 90%), whereas PBAT showed comparatively lower oxidation efficiency, consistent with their higher aromatic and crystalline content. Notably, mixing PLA with PBAT and PHA with PBS reduced the overall degradation efficiency, suggesting diffusion limitations in the radical attack across heterogeneous polymer domains.

Weight loss by HFR in BPs powder (125–200 μm) and their mixtures in different ratios (a) PLA+PBATs and (b) PHA + PBS.

The observed results align with trends reported for conventional plastics such as medium-density polyethylene (MDPE) under similar HFR systems11, where smaller particle sizes facilitated more rapid and complete oxidation. This enhancement is attributed to the increased specific surface area, which improves the contact between the polymer surface and reactive species (•OH and HO2• radicals). Similar findings were reported by Hu et al. (2021), who demonstrated that microplastics with smaller particle sizes underwent faster decomposition due to enhanced radical diffusion and better Fe2+/H2O2 accessibility within the polymer matrix18. Furthermore, Ortiz et al. (2022) emphasized that surface modification, pore formation, and oxygenated group incorporation are more pronounced in smaller particles, which accelerate decomposition44.

From a mechanistic perspective, the degradation kinetics of bioplastics under HFR can be described by a shrinking-particle model, where the oxidation front progresses from the surface toward the polymer core45. As the particle size decreases, diffusion resistance diminishes, enabling uniform radical penetration and promoting near-complete weight loss. The temperature and pressure conditions of the hydrothermal environment further enhance polymer chain mobility and oxygen solubility, synergistically improving oxidation rates46.

Overall, the present findings indicate that the particle size of 125–200 μm used in this study provided an optimal balance between experimental reproducibility and effective radical diffusion. Smaller size fractions are expected to further enhance oxidative conversion, as corroborated by previous studies on both petrochemical and biobased polymers. These insights underscore the crucial role of particle morphology in controlling the kinetics and mechanism of plastic and bioplastic degradation in hydrothermal Fenton systems.

Influence of water matrices on bioplastic degradation

To evaluate the practical applicability of the HFR for real environmental systems, degradation experiments were conducted using various water matrices (Fig. 8). The details of the water collected and their properties are summarized in Table S1 (SI). The weight loss results indicate that DI water consistently produced the highest degradation efficiencies; however, comparable oxidation performance was also achieved in natural water matrices, particularly for PLA and PHA, where weight loss exceeded 85%. These results confirm that the HFR system maintains high oxidative activity even in complex aqueous environments containing natural solutes, minerals, and organic matter.

Weight loss by HFR in BPs for different water matrices (a) PLA, (b) PBAT, (c) PHA, and (d) PBS (*SG: Seunggicheon water stream).

The variation in degradation efficiency among the different matrices can be attributed to the presence of dissolved inorganic ions such as Cl–, SO42–, HCO3–, and Ca2+, which can influence the formation and lifetime of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Chloride ions are known to react with •OH to produce less reactive chlorine radicals (Cl•, Cl2•⁻), which can reduce the direct oxidation capacity18,45. Similarly, carbonate and bicarbonate species act as •OH scavengers, forming carbonate radicals (CO3•⁻) that possess lower redox potentials44. Despite these inhibitory interactions, the hydrothermal environment enhances Fe2+/Fe3+ cycling and promotes the continuous in situ generation of •OH and HO2• radicals, effectively compensating for radical quenching and sustaining oxidation rates11.

Moreover, elevated temperature and pressure under hydrothermal conditions improve radical diffusion, oxygen solubility, and polymer chain mobility, enhancing the accessibility of reactive species to the polymer surface13. This synergistic effect explains the observed high oxidation levels in all tested water types, even those rich in competing ions or natural organic matter.

These findings are consistent with earlier studies on polyethylene and polystyrene degradation under similar advanced oxidation conditions, where natural waters showed only minor differences from pure systems18,44. Overall, the comparable performance across DI and natural waters highlights the real-world applicability of the HFR system for bioplastic degradation. Its resilience against matrix-induced radical scavenging demonstrates its potential for in situ or large-scale environmental treatment of plastic and bioplastic wastes in freshwater and marine environments.

Conclusion

This study establishes the HFR system as an effective and sustainable method for oxidizing major bioplastics (PLA, PBAT, PHA, and PBS) and their blends. By combining hydrothermal conditions with Fenton-based advanced oxidation, this hybrid system efficiently depolymerized bioplastics into low-molecular-weight intermediates, indicating significant oxidation and chemical transformation of the polymer backbone. The synergistic interaction of heat, pressure, and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) promoted both hydrolytic and oxidative cleavage of ester bonds, accelerating the degradation of these biopolymers that are otherwise resistant to natural breakdown under given conditions.

In a broader context, the findings from this study extend the applicability of the HFR process beyond conventional plastics to biodegradable polymers, bridging a critical gap in sustainable end-of-life management for emerging bio-based materials. The demonstrated ability of this system to mineralize or valorize diverse polymer types highlights its potential integration as a tertiary treatment stage within existing waste or wastewater treatment infrastructures. Such integration could substantially reduce the environmental accumulation of bioplastic residues or microplastics, contributing to cleaner aquatic ecosystems and supporting the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).
