Liquid biopsies for risk stratification of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms
Authors: Nicolas Jorek, Ingmar F Rompen, Louisa Bolm, Thomas Hank, Martin Loos, Christoph W Michalski, Nerma Crnovrsanin, of the Young Surgeons Publications Committee
Published: 2025

Pancreatic cysts are increasingly detected as incidental findings, with a prevalence of 13.5 % in the general population, rising to over 40 % in individuals older than 70 years [1]. Among these, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) represent a common subtype with malignant potential. While many IPMNs remain indolent, a subset progresses to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) through different stages of dysplasia. The central clinical challenge is to accurately identify IPMNs that will progress to or already harvest high-grade dysplasia or invasive cancer to guide timely surgery, while avoiding overtreatment of low-risk lesions. According to current international consensus, including the Kyoto guidelines, risk stratification relies on clinical assessment and imaging-based criteria defined as high-risk stigmata and worrisome features [2,3]. However, these criteria have limited sensitivity and specificity [4]. Moreover, a considerable number of patients with low-risk lesions and ambiguous risk of malignant progression are subjected to long-term surveillance, which is associated with psychological burden and increased healthcare costs [5].

Emerging biomarker-based approaches, including liquid biopsies, may help refine patient selection and support a shift toward individualized IPMN-management [6,7]. Liquid biopsies are minimally invasive tools that detect molecular markers such as circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), tumor-educated platelets (TEPs), and circulating free RNA (cfRNA), providing insights into the tumor's genetic landscape, disease status, and progression. While blood and urine are the most commonly used specimens, alternative sources such as saliva, peritoneal fluid, pancreatic juice, or cyst fluid can also be utilized—particularly in the context of pancreatic lesions [8,9].

In this editorial, the authors aimed to present two most recent studies that are focusing on liquid biopsy approaches to detect IPMN lesions with high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma before surgery. Patients at risk for malignant transformation could be identified before radiological or clinical signs of worrisome features emerge, allowing for earlier surgical intervention and improved survival. Conversely, low-risk patients may avoid unnecessary surgery, related complications, and frequent imaging. It should provide a rationale for the potential transition of IPMN management from consensus-based guidelines to individualized precision medicine. While additional studies [10] have also investigated the role of liquid biopsies in this context, a comprehensive discussion of all available evidence falls beyond the scope of this editorial.

The prospective, multicenter study by Paniccia et al. [6] presents a comprehensive molecular characterization of pancreatic cysts, and additionally answers important clinical questions regarding risk stratification. Through targeted next-generation sequencing of 22 genes, PancreaSeq-Testing, the authors analyzed cyst fluid obtained from endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)–fine-needle aspiration (FNA) and surgical specimens. The primary objective was to improve the classification of pancreatic cyst subtypes and to enhance the detection of lesions harboring high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma.

Mutations in MAPK pathway genes and GNAS were identified as highly accurate markers for differentiating mucinous from non-mucinous cysts, achieving a sensitivity of 89 % and a specificity of 100 %. In addition, the detection of MAPK or GNAS loss of heterozygosity or co-occurring alterations in TP53, SMAD4, and/or mTOR with similar allele frequencies, yielded a sensitivity of 86 %, specificity of 95 %, positive predictive value of 91 %, and negative predictive value of 92 % for the identification of advanced neoplasia in IPMNs. The inclusion of CTNNB1 mutations further enhanced diagnostic accuracy, increasing the sensitivity to 88 %. The authors incorporated the PancreaSeq testing into the American Gastroenterological Association and International Association of Pancreatology criteria, and the sensitivity and specificity for detecting advanced neoplasia increased from 72 to 96 % and from 86 to 98 %, respectively. Notably, the study identified a subgroup of IPMNs harboring non-V600E BRAF mutations, classified as class II and class III variants. These mutations were exclusively observed in IPMNs, frequently in cysts communicating with the main pancreatic duct, suggesting distinct molecular subtype.

Despite the promising diagnostic performance of this liquid biopsy approach, one has to consider remaining challenges. Discordance between preoperative and postoperative molecular testing, particularly in IPMN patients, raises concerns regarding the optimal timing and methodology for cyst fluid collection. As test results are influenced by the cellular content of the aspirated fluid, questions remain about how much material is required and who should undergo cyst fluid sampling. Importantly, the acquisition of cyst fluid relies on endoscopic ultrasound-guided aspiration, an invasive procedure that requires specialized expertise and is not universally available or feasible. This introduces a practical barrier to broad implementation and contributes to selection bias in the study population.

In a separate study, Zhang et al. investigated a more accessible liquid biopsy strategy by analyzing patient-derived blood samples to identify biomarkers capable of discriminating low-risk IPMNs from those with high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma [7]. A total of 390 serum samples from healthy donors and patients diagnosed with IPMN were tested on microarrays of 2977 antibodies. Furthermore, profiling of 179 miRNAs was performed on the same serum samples. Machine learning regression models were used to evaluate the best candidate biomarkers. However, the best discrimination with an AUC of 97 % in the validation cohort was yielded by a combination of five proteins (EEF1A1, RPH3AL, NCOR1, L1CAM, TMEM161A) and three miRNAs (miR-146a-5p, miR-155–5p, miR-375). Furthermore, this study reported an impressive AUC of 1.0 for distinguishing healthy individuals from patients with IPMN-derived PDAC, highlighting a potential additional application of this assay in blood-based screening and early detection of PDAC.

We are enthusiastic about future interventional studies to evaluate whether the PancreaSeq-Testing and the blood-based combination of proteins and miRNAs proves clinical utility by improving patient outcomes, enabling more accurate identification of high-risk lesions, and supporting the refinement of surgical decision-making in patients with IPMN.

However, there is still a lot of work to do. In order to enhance the management of IPMN and pancreatic cysts through the implementation of liquid biopsies in clinical practice, future research endeavors should prioritize the harmonization of biobanking procedures for cyst fluids and plasma samples. It is imperative that the results of studies conducted on plasma samples be validated in multi-center prospective cohorts and in interventional trials. Additionally, the optimal approach for incorporating molecular testing into established surveillance protocols remains to be defined. Furthermore, a major barrier to the broader implementation of liquid biopsies in clinical practice is the need for complex bioinformatics pipelines and specialized molecular knowledge, which are often not readily available or widely shared among clinicians.

Challenges that could potentially be addressed through closer collaboration between research and clinical practice—ideally within the framework of comprehensive cancer centers. These centers offer the necessary infrastructure to foster interdisciplinary exchange, enable high-quality biobanking and standardized data collection, and support the implementation of advanced diagnostic tools such as liquid biopsies. By bridging the gap between bench and bedside, comprehensive cancer centers play a pivotal role in translating molecular innovations into clinically meaningful strategies—not only for IPMN management, but for oncology more broadly [11].

With increasing analytic precision inevitably increasing costs in the healthcare system, it is crucial to ensure universal access to these diagnostic approaches beyond academic institutions through adequate reimbursement. Which lead us to one critically important point and that is the cost of additional molecular testing and its cost-effectiveness.

While robust data on cost-effectiveness in the described clinical setting are still lacking, there is existing evidence from other cancer types such as colorectal and lung cancer regarding the use of liquid biopsies for early detection and disease monitoring. However, the results and interpretations across studies remain inconsistent [12,13]. To maximize clinical value, liquid biopsies should be applied to well-defined and validated clinical questions. These technologies are also subject to ongoing technical and procedural refinement, which may further improve their accuracy and precision and ultimately reduce costs. Consistent digital documentation of diagnostic data and clinical outcomes is essential to assess clinical relevance and define appropriate usage from an economic perspective. When evaluating the socioeconomic impact of routine implementation, potential benefits such as reduced imaging frequency, avoidance of unnecessary surgeries, and earlier detection of pancreatic cancer should be taken into account [12].

If the implementation of liquid biopsies into clinical routine proves to be successful, management of cystic pancreatic lesions and IPMNs could shift from a clinical dilemma to a model of precision oncology and cancer prevention.
