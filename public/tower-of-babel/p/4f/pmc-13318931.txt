Association of Triglyceride‐Glucose Index, Triglyceride/HDL Cholesterol Ratio and Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance With Asymptomatic Organ Damage in Previously Untreated Patients With Hypertension
Authors: Ionas Papasotiriou, Anastasios Tentolouris, Sotiria Spiliopoulou, Theodoros N Sergentanis, Efstathios Manios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe aimed to examine the association between insulin resistance (IR) and target organ damage (TOD) in patients with hypertension. Three indices of IR (Triglyceride‐Glucose index: TyG; triglyceride‐to‐HDL ratio: TG/HDL‐C; Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance: METS‐IR) were examined for their association with TOD in 614 newly diagnosed patients with hypertension. Left ventricular mass index (LVMI), the intima‐media thickness of the common carotid artery (IMT‐CCA) and estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) were used as indices of cardiac, artery and renal damage. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis performed to estimate optimal cut‐off for each index, while simple and multiple logistic regression models were applied to examine the indices as binary predictors of organ damage. Linear and non‐linear associations were examined with continuous variables using linear regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) models. After adjusting for confounders, logistic regression showed that high TG/HDL‐C (OR:1.50, 95%CI:1.03–2.18) and METS‐IR (OR:2.17, 95%CI:1.50–3.13) were associated with increased risk of left ventricular hypertrophy, while only high METS‐IR was associated with increased risk of carotid plaque (OR:2.07, 95%CI:1.17–3.68) and only high TG/HDL‐C was associated with renal damage (OR:2.33, 95%CI:1.02–5.36). Adjusted linear regression showed that none of the indices were significantly associated with LVMI. However, all indices were associated with CCA‐IMT (p < 0.05), but none with eGFR (p > 0.05). Adjusted RCS models showed a significant non‐linear association between TyG and CCA‐IMT (p for non‐linearity = 0.009). In conclusion, IR biomarkers are of clinical value in newly diagnosed patients with hypertension.Keywords: hypertension, insulin resistance, METS‐IR, organ damage, TyG

Introduction

Hypertension, a remaining public health issue in many societies, constitutes the precursor of many serious health problems, such as cardiovascular disease, stroke and kidney diseases [1]. Chronic elevated blood pressure (BP) can cause damage to several organs, including the brain, heart, arteries and kidneys. The mechanisms by which elevated BP causes damage to specific organs are well‐known and described, and due to this selectivity of hypertension to cause damage to specific organs this effect is also called target organ damage (TOD). Therefore, it is very common for hypertensive patients to be found with subclinical signs of TOD, such as left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), plaque in the carotid arteries and/or reduced renal function [2]. In addition, since hypertension is very common among middle‐aged and older adults, these patients often have metabolic comorbidities, such as obesity, diabetes or dyslipidemia, causing an additional health burden [3].

Since cardiovascular diseases, plaque formation and renal function can be independently influenced by metabolic factors, their subclinical course in hypertensive patients in relation to these factors remains clinically relevant. Today there is a better, but not complete, understanding of the relation between cardiovascular diseases and glucose metabolism [4]. Both dysglycemia and IR share some common pathophysiological mechanisms with CVDs, but also the first two conditions are an independent mechanism for the latter [5]. For example, IR can increase lipolysis and subsequently, the accumulation of lipids in the blood, leading to higher levels of triglycerides, and the unfavorable lipoproteins LDL and VLD. This atherogenic environment is the basis for plaque formation in the arteries and coronary artery disease [6]. On the other hand, glucose metabolism disturbances have been also linked to structural and functional changes in myocardium, including LVH, fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction [7]. Beyond the cardiovascular system, diabetes and subclinical glucose metabolism decline has been also associated with renal damage, mostly through hemodynamic and metabolic mechanisms [8]. However, these mechanisms have not yet been adequately explored in hypertensive adults.

Last decades, several indices of glucose metabolism and IR have emerged, with many of them being good predictor for several cardiovascular and other health outcomes [8]. Blood biomarkers using triglycerides and glucose, such as the triglyceride‐to‐glucose ratio (TyG), triglyceride‐to‐HDL‐cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL) and the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS‐IR) have been shown to be among the most reliable indices of IR and glucose metabolism [8, 9]. However, the clinical utility of these indices has not been sufficiently evaluated in hypertension, particularly in relation to its principal complication, target organ damage. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to examine the association between surrogates of IR and TOD in newly diagnosed patients with hypertension.

Methods

Study Design and Population

In this cross‐sectional study, 614 adults without prior diagnosis of hypertension, that visited the Hypertension Center of our Department and underwent full blood pressure assessment were included. BP assessment included office BP measurements and a valid 24‐h ambulatory BP monitoring, followed by imaging and laboratory evaluation. Inclusion criteria for this study were: 1) adults ≥18 years old, 2) 3 valid office BP measurements, 3) valid 24‐h BP monitoring, 4) free history of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular or renal diseases. Patients were excluded if: 1) had prior diagnosis of hypertension, 2) had ever been prescribed or used antihypertensive treatment, 3) evidence of secondary causes of arterial hypertension. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study and the study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Alexandra Hospital.

Office and Ambulatory BP Measurements

BP in the office was measured using an appropriate cuff around the non‐dominant arm with a validated automated sphygmomanometer (Omron 705IT; Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan). Three consecutive BP measurements were taken at 1‐min intervals, with participants seated with their arms supported and placed at the level of the heart. Diastolic and systolic office BP was determined as the average value of the last two recordings. 24‐h ambulatory BP monitoring was applied on a usual working day, and participants were instructed to act and work as usual. Ambulatory BP was recorded using oscillometric Spacelabs 90207 equipment (SpaceLabs, Redmond, WA). The device was set to automatically obtain BP recordings at 20‐min intervals throughout the 24‐h study period. Daytime was defined as the interval between 09:00 h and 21:00 h and nighttime as the interval between 01:00 h and 06:00 h. Participants were encouraged to rest and sleep at night, while maintaining their usual activities during the day.

Target Organ Damage

Echocardiography was performed with a complete M‐mode two‐dimensional and color‐flow Doppler examination, using a commercially available Hewlett Packard machine (Sonos 1000; Hewlett Packard, Andover, Massachusetts, USA) and a 2.5 MHz phased‐array transducer. Pulsed Doppler data were monitored at a sweep speed of 50 or 100 mm/s using a Panasonic AG‐7350 videocassette recorder (model AG‐MD830E; Panasonic, Osaka, Japan) during 4–6 cardiac cycles. Measurements of the left ventricular posterior wall, septum, and left ventricular internal diastolic and systolic dimensions at the level of the chordae tendineae were obtained. Then, left ventricular mass (LVM) was calculated using the Devereux formula according to the Penn Convention Protocol [10], and the LVM index (LVMI) was calculated as LVM divided by the body surface area (LVM/BSA). The presence of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) was determined if LVMI >115 g/m2 for men or >95 g/m2 for women [11].

The left and right common carotid arteries (CCA) were assessed using a high‐resolution Doppler ultrasound system (Acuson 128XP) equipped with a 7 MHz linear array transducer, with measurements obtained from the anterolateral, posterolateral, and mediolateral orientations. A single experienced sonographer performed all CCT imaging. The intima–media thickness (IMT) was calculated by averaging the IMT measurements from the right and left CCAs. For each artery, ten measurements were recorded at a point 10 mm before the carotid bifurcation using the M‐line technique, and the mean of these values was used [12]. A CCA‐IMT value ≥0.9 mm was used as the threshold for carotid damage [13].

Τhe estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR), from the 2021 CKD‐EPI creatinine equation, in accordance with the suggestions of the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology, was used as index of renal function and damage [14]. The use of this equation is in accordance with the suggestions of the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology. eGFR value <60 mL/min/1.73m2 was considered indicative of renal damage and used to create the binary variable [15].

Statistical Analysis

To describe the basic demographic and clinical characteristics of study's sample, the mean ±standard deviation values are presented for quantitative variables, while the absolute and percentage frequencies for qualitative variables. To determine the optimal cut‐off values of TyG, TG/HDL‐C and METS‐IR for each organ damage, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed, and the value corresponding to highest Youden's index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1) was used as cut‐off point. These cut‐off values were used to create a binary variable for each index (TyG, TG/HDL‐C or METS‐IR) for each organ damage (LVH, carotid damage, renal damage) and later inserted into simple and multiple logistic regression models as predictors of organ damage. Multiple regression models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, BMI and mean 24‐h systolic blood pressure for TyG and TG/HDL‐C, or age, sex, smoking status, diabetes, hyperlipidemia and mean 24‐h systolic blood pressure for METS‐IR. BMI was not inserted as confounder when METS‐IR is examined as predictor, to avoid collinearity, since it is already used in METS‐IR equation. TyG, TG/HDL‐C and METS‐IR were also examined as both continuous and ordinal variables (categorized into Tertiles) in simple and multiple linear regression models, where LVMI, CCA‐IMT and eGFR were inserted as the dependent variables. Multivariate linear regression models were adjusted for the same confounders described for logistic regression. To explore possible non‐linear patterns between IR indices and organ damage indices, Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) models were also applied. To create the cubic splines for TyG, TG/HDL‐C, and METS‐IR, the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile values were used as the knot locations. Two RCS models were performed for each index: an unadjusted model including the spline terms of the index as independent variables and each organ damage index as the dependent variable, and a fully adjusted model including the same covariates described for the multiple logistic and linear regression models. The joint effect of the spline terms was assessed using the Wald test, while overall model non‐linearity was examined using the likelihood ratio test. RCS curves were generated to graphically represent the non‐linear association between each IR index and each organ damage index. Statistical analysis and figure creation were performed using STATA 18.0 for Windows, and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Prognostic Value of TyG, TG/HDL‐C Ratio and METS‐IR

ROC analysis of each index for LVH showed that METS‐IR had the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.536, 95%CI: 0.491–0.582), followed by TyG (AUC: 0.524, 95%CI: 0.478–0.569) and TG/HDL‐C (AUC: 0.501, 95%CI: 0.455–0.547). The optimal cut‐off point for LVH prediction was 7.89, 1.94 and 42.08 for TyG, TG/HDL‐C and METS‐IR, respectively. The association between each index and LVH using these cut‐off points are presented in Table 2, where in univariate logistic regression only METS‐IR was significantly associated with LVH with 70% increased odds of having LVH in patients with high METS‐IR. In multivariate analysis both TG/HDL‐C and METS‐IR found to significantly associate with LVH, with the odds of LVH being 50% and 117% higher in patients with high TG/HDL‐C or METS‐IR, respectively.

For CCA damage, ROC analysis also showed that METS‐IR had the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.565, 95%CI: 0.511–0.620), followed by TG/HDL‐C (AUC: 0.554, 95%CI: 0.496–0.613) and TyG (AUC: 0.524, 95%CI: 0.467–0.581). In multivariate analysis, only METS‐IR was still significantly associated with CCA damage, where patients with high METS‐IR had 107% higher odds of CCA damage in comparison to those with low METS‐IR.

For renal damage, ROC analysis showed that TG/HDL‐C had the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.555, 95%CI: 0.453–0.658), followed by TyG (AUC: 0.494, 95%CI: 0.397–0.592) and METS‐IR (AUC: 0.467, 95%CI: 0.364–0.571). The optimal cut‐off point for renal damage prediction was 8.01, 2.83 and 45.40 for TyG, TG/HDL‐C and METS‐IR, respectively. The association between each index and renal damage using these cut‐off points are presented in Table 1, where in univariate logistic regression no index was significantly related to renal damage. However, in univariate analysis it was found that TG/HDL‐C was significantly associated with renal damage, with patients with high TG/HDL‐C having 133% higher odds of damage in comparison to the reference group. Linear Associations

Results from simple linear regression showed that among the three indices, only METS‐IR, either as continuous or ordinal variable, was associated with LVMI (Table 3, Model 1). However, this association was not significant in the multivariate model, after adjusting for confounders (Table 3, Model 2). Simple linear regression for mean CCA‐IMT, showed that TyG, both as continuous and ordinal variable, was significantly associated with CCA‐IMT (Table 3, Model 1), but after adjusting for confounders only the ordinal variable was still significantly associated with this organ damage index (Table 3, Model 2). The TG/HDL‐C, as ordinal but not as continuous variable, was also found to significantly associate with CCA‐IMT in both simple (Table 3, Model 1) and multiple linear regression model (Table 3, Model 2). METS‐IR, both as a continuous and ordinal variable was significantly associated with CCA‐IMT in simple (Table 3, Model 1) and multiple linear regression (Table 3, Model 2). Results from linear regression for renal damage showed that only TyG as ordinal variable was associated with eGFR, and this association was significant in the univariate (Table 3, Model 1), but not in the multivariate model (Table 3, Model 2).

Non‐Linear Associations

RCS regression did not reveal any non‐linear association between TyG, TG/HDL‐C, or METS‐IR with LVMI (Table 4). For CCA‐IMT, a significant non‐linear association was found with TyG cubic splines (Table 4, Model 1). This association was also significant for the joint effect of splines (p < 0.001), while the likelihood ratio test revealed a significant superiority of the non‐linear model (p for non‐linearity = 0.001). This association was still significant after adjusting for confounders (Table 4, Model 2). TG/HDL‐C splines were also significantly associated with CCA‐IMT in the simple regression model, with their joint effect and the non‐linear model being also significant (Table 4, Model 1). However, none of these associations were significant after adjusting for confounders (Table 4, Model 2). METS‐IR spline was also found to be significantly associated with CCA‐IMT in simple regression model, where the joint effect of splines was significant in both simple and adjusted regression model. However, the non‐linear models were not superior to linear models, either in simple or adjusted regression (p for non‐linearity >0.05, Table 4, Model 1 & 2). Also, RCS analysis showed that neither TyG, TG/HDL‐C or METS‐IR splines alone, or their joint effect, was significantly associated with eGFR (Table 4, Model 1 & 2). Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between budget‐friendly and clinical meaningful indices of IR and subclinical organ damage in untreated patients with hypertension. The main findings of data analysis indicate that indices of IR are associated with cardiovascular and renal damage in this population, with this relation being independent of BP and other cardiometabolic factors. Another important finding of this study is that non‐linear associations between these indices and organ damage were also found, with TyG having a clear and consistent reverse U‐shaped association with left ventricular mass.

These findings are consistent with similar evidence in several populations, where IR has been found to associate with structural changes of the heart, including left ventricular geometry and hypertrophy [7], atherosclerosis and plaque formation in the carotids [16], as well as renal decline [17], The association between IR and LVH in hypertensive patients has been already indicated by some old studies. A study by Lind et al. (1995) showed that in a very small sample of hypertensive adults (n = 50) IR, measured with the gold standard method of hyperinsulinaemic‐euglycaemic clamp, was associated with LVH [18]. However, methodological limitations such as small sample sizes and simplistic statistical approaches are some drawbacks in most older studies.

The introduction of non‐insulin indicators of IR, which are often more budget‐friendly and less invasive, have found widely acceptance among researchers and clinicians, due to their high clinical significance [19]. TyG, HDL‐C and METS‐IR are three indices, that have been found to closely relate with insulin‐derived indicators of IR, correlate with several cardiovascular and other health outcomes [20, 21]. Therefore, the examination of the predictive ability of these indices in hypertension is still clinically relevant. In this direction, recent studies have indicated the importance of these indices, especially TyG, in cardiovascular implication in hypertensive and non‐hypertensive populations. A retrospective study of 4861 young and older adults with hypertension, showed that TyG, TG/HDL‐C, and METS‐IR were associated with major adverse cardiovascular events [22]. Such associations between these IR indices and hard cardiovascular outcomes have been confirmed in several cohort studies as well [23, 24]. However, our study provides more information about the background of these possible associations, and its link with organ‐specific damage in hypertension, which is a continuous clinical issue in this population.

Besides LVH, our results also showed associations of IR indices with carotid and renal damage. A cohort study by Lu et al. (2022) showed that both HOMA‐IR and TyG were associated with IMT thickening and carotid plaque formation in a sample of 589 adults, followed up for a mean of 4 years [25]. In support of the vascular effects of IR, a study by Catena et al. (2025) showed that HOMA‐IR was correlated with arterial stiffness, while hypertensive patients with impaired glucose tolerance had higher HOMA‐IR and IMT values [26]. Our study also showed a non‐linear association between TyG and CCA‐IMT, with the patterns of this association following a reverse U shape. Lu et al. (2022) also examined the non‐linear association between HOMA‐IR and TyG with carotid plaque incidence, by applying RCS analysis, but no significant non‐linear associations were found.

The association between IR and renal function has been adequately examined, at least in diabetic population and patients with kidney diseases. A prospective study by Yang et al. (2022) also showed an association between IR and adverse renal outcomes in a general population [27]. A cross‐sectional study in Chinese found an association between METS‐IR and eGFR [28], while a large cohort study in Australia showed a relation between TyG and risk for end stage kidney disease [29]. In our study, such associations were not strong or consistent, and only TG/HDL‐C found to have a significant positive association with risk of renal damage (eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73m2) in the logistic regression model.

The vascular effects of IR are mostly those that link IR with carotid damage, plaque formation and atherosclerosis. Despite common comorbidities that often accompany IR, including obesity, low‐grade inflammation and dyslipidemia, there are also other important pathways, independent of these factors. For example, clinical data have also shown that hyperinsulinemia may stimulate proliferation and migration of the vascular smooth muscle cells, causing structural changes of the arteries and wall thickening [30]. In addition, IR promotes a prothrombotic state, characterized by increased platelet activation, elevated fibrinogen levels, and higher concentrations of plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1), which impair fibrinolysis and increase the risk of thrombotic events [31, 32]. Finally, IR often leads to chronic hyperglycemia, a process well established in diabetes mellitus, from which much of the current understanding of hyperglycemia‐induced vascular injury has been derived [33]. Persistent hyperglycemia promotes the formation and accumulation of advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs), which cross‐link proteins and interact with their receptors (RAGE), thereby enhancing oxidative stress, inflammation, and vascular stiffness [33, 34].

In contrast to its well‐documented association with atherosclerosis, the relationship between IR and cardiac structural changes remains less studied. The association between IR indices and LVH can be explained by the fact that chronic hyperinsulinemia can stimulate cardiomyocyte growth. In hyperinsulinemia, it is observed an activation of both insulin and insulin‐like growth factor pathways, that result in increased protein synthesis and cellular hypertrophy [35]. IR is also associated with sympathetic nervous system activation and renal sodium retention, leading to increased cardiac workload, plasma volume expansion, and ultimately greater left ventricular mass and concentric remodeling [36]. Altered myocardial energy metabolism and myocardial fibrosis, due to IR–related endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress, have also been suggested as possible mechanisms for structural changes in the heart [37].

On the other hand, IR also causes glomerular hyperfiltration, which is a key mechanism for renal damage, alongside with stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system that increases intraglomerular pressure, resulting in damage of the glomerular capillaries [38]. In addition, IR impairs insulin signaling in podocytes, leading to their apoptosis, foot process effacement, and disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier, further contributing to albuminuria and glomerulosclerosis [39]. The relationship between IR and renal dysfunction appears to be bidirectional: IR may promote the development and progression of renal damage, while declining renal function further aggravates metabolic disturbances that worsen IR [40]. For example, IR is present in patients with chronic kidney disease both with and without diabetes, irrespective of the underlying etiology of kidney disease, and its severity tends to increase as kidney function declines [40].

As with any study, this also has its own limitations, with the main drawback being its cross‐sectional design that prevents accusations regarding possible causal relationship among the findings. Although a very careful strategy for patient collection was followed, with all necessary inclusion and exclusion criteria to avoid misconceptions, as well as a very analytical statistical analysis, this limitation still exists. Another limitation relates to the assessment of IR. The gold standard method for diagnosing IR is the hyperinsulinaemic‐euglycaemic glucose clamp technique; however, due to its complexity and limited feasibility in large‐scale clinical studies, IR in the present study was estimated using surrogate indices, including the TyG index, the TG/HDL‐C, and the METS‐IR score. Although these indices have been widely validated and used in epidemiological studies, they may not fully capture the complexity of IR. It should also be noted that, since this study was designed to align with usual clinical practice for screening adults with hypertension, the collection of additional data regarding organ damage and glucose–insulin metabolism was avoided to respect available resources and patient convenience.

Conclusion

This study showed that in patients with untreated essential hypertension, indices of insulin resistance are associated with hypertension's organ damage. The results also indicate the clinical significance of these indices in predicting cardiovascular and renal damage in this population, with this association being independent of blood pressure. Therefore, more detailed metabolic screening in patients newly diagnosed with hypertension seems mandatory.
