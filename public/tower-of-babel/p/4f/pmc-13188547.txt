Nitric oxide exacerbates systemic inflammation in adults with severe acute respiratory
Authors: Yong Bai, Minglang Liao, Ruiling Shang, Lehong Zhou, Haili Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInhaled nitric oxide (iNO) is used as rescue therapy for severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), but its dose-dependent effects on systemic inflammation and outcomes remain controversial. This study aimed to compare clinical outcomes between high- and low-dose iNO in adults with severe ARDS.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, 117 patients with severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤100 mmHg) treated with iNO between January 2020 and July 2024 were categorized by the maximum iNO concentration received: high-dose (≥ 10 ppm, n = 59) and low-dose (< 10 ppm, n = 58). Baseline characteristics, inflammatory markers (white blood cells, interleukin-6 [IL-6], tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]), thromboelastography maximum amplitude (MA) value, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, vasopressor requirement, and 30-day survival were analyzed.ResultsBaseline characteristics were balanced between groups. Compared to the low-dose group, the high-dose group had significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers (WBC, IL-6, TNF-α; all P < 0.001), lower MA values (P < 0.001), higher SOFA scores (P = 0.031), greater norepinephrine requirements (P = 0.026), longer mechanical ventilation duration (P < 0.001), and fewer 30-day survival days (P < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis indicated good discriminatory power (AUC > 0.7) for these parameters in identifying high-dose exposure. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly worse 30-day survival in the high-dose group (Log-rank P = 0.019).ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort, high-dose iNO (≥ 10 ppm) was associated with exacerbated systemic inflammation, impaired coagulation function, greater organ dysfunction, and increased 30-day mortality compared to low-dose iNO in severe ARDS. These findings suggest cautious use of higher iNO concentrations, emphasizing monitoring of inflammatory and coagulation responses.Keywords: Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Nitric oxide, Dose-response, Systemic inflammation, Thromboelastography, Mortality

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a frequent cause of respiratory failure in critically ill patients, is biologically characterized by disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier due to uncontrolled inflammation [1].Although lung-protective ventilation and prone positioning have significantly improved outcomes, mortality remains high in severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ <100 mmHg) [2].

Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), a selective pulmonary vasodilator, has been employed as a rescue therapy for severe ARDS based on its potential to improve ventilation-perfusion matching and transiently enhance oxygenation [3].However, the clinical benefit of iNO on hard endpoints remains controversial. Numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have failed to demonstrate a reduction in 28-day mortality with iNO therapy, and some studies even suggest an increased risk of renal injury [4, 5].This discrepancy between physiological effect and clinical outcome highlights the complex, dual-edged role of NO in critical illness.

Beyond vasodilation, NO is a pivotal signaling molecule involved in infection, inflammation, and immune responses [6, 7]. Its effects appear to be critically dose-dependent. Experimental and some clinical data suggest that at low concentrations, NO may exert anti-inflammatory effects, potentially by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [8]. Conversely, higher concentrations of NO can promote the generation of reactive nitrogen species (RNS), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of pro-inflammatory pathways (e.g., elevating cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α), and exacerbation of oxidative stress [9, 10]. This dose-dependent dichotomy may explain the variable outcomes observed with iNO therapy.

Despite these insights, there is a scarcity of clinical evidence directly comparing the impact of different iNO dosage ranges on systemic inflammatory and coagulation profiles in patients with severe ARDS. In particular, the interaction between iNO dose, systemic inflammation, and coagulation function—as assessed by viscoelastic tests like thromboelastography (e.g., maximum amplitude, MA value)—remains poorly characterized. Clarifying these relationships is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind iNO’s potential harm and for optimizing its therapeutic application.

Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a dose-response analysis to evaluate and compare the effects of high-dose (≥ 10 ppm) versus low-dose (< 10 ppm) iNO therapy on systemic inflammation, coagulation function, organ failure, and 30-day survival in adults with severe ARDS.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval No.: SYRMYY-177). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature. We analyzed data from 117 adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with severe ARDS who were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and received inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) therapy between January 2020 and July 2024.

Patients and grouping

The diagnosis of severe ARDS followed the 2023 criteria of the American Thoracic Society and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine, defined as a PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≤ 100 mmHg or a SPO2/FiO2ratio ≤ 148 (with SPO2≥97%) under invasive mechanical ventilation [11].Patients under 18 years of age or those who did not receive iNO therapy were excluded.

To investigate the dose-response relationship, patients were classified based on the maximum stable iNO concentration achieved during treatment: a high-concentration group (≥ 10 ppm, n = 59) and a low-concentration group (< 10 ppm, n = 58). Two factors are the main reasons for using 10 ppm as the grouping threshold: First, it cites earlier research in which this concentration is linked to higher dangers such hypoxia rebound after withdrawal and is commonly used to differentiate between moderate and high doses [21].Second, From a pharmacological perspective, concentrations exceeding this threshold may be more likely to induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. To validate the rationality of this threshold post-hoc and avoid data-driven bias, we employed a Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) model combined with the Maximum Selection Statistic method for exploratory analysis. Results revealed a nonlinear relationship between iNO concentration and the risk of log-transformed key inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6), with a distinct trend inflection point near 10 ppm (Fig. 1). This provides objective support from our cohort data for establishing this clinical threshold.

Dose-response relationship between inhaled nitric oxide concentration and risk ratio. The odds ratio (OR) for unfavorable outcomes (such increased inflammation levels) is less than 1 when iNO concentrations are below 10 ppm, suggesting a lower risk. The OR score dramatically rises when concentrations above 10 ppm, indicating a correlation with worse clinical indications.Both the overall association and nonlinear trend are statistically significant (overall association P = 0.022; nonlinear P = 0.049)

Due to the study’s retrospective observational design, the attending physician chose the iNO dosage based on the clinical conditions of each patient (such as the degree of hypoxemia and indications of right ventricular failure) rather than by random assignment. As a result, patients with more serious diseases might be more likely to get higher doses of iNO, which could lead to indication bias in the trial results. Although we used multivariate models to account for known baseline confounders in our statistical analysis, the impact of unmeasured confounders cannot be completely ruled out.

The flow of patient selection, inclusion, and group allocation is illustrated in Fig. 2. Briefly, during the 54-month study period, a total of 500 critically ill patients admitted to our ICU were screened. Among them, 165 met the Berlin definition for severe ARDS. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 117 patients were included in the final analysis. These patients were subsequently stratified into two groups: the high-concentration group (n = 59), comprising patients who received a maximum iNO concentration of ≥ 10 ppm, and the low-concentration group (n = 58), comprising those who received a maximum concentration of < 10 ppm.

Intervention and data collection

All patients received iNO therapy via a BG-95 nitric oxide gas flow controller connected to the ventilator. The attending physician determined the initial iNO dose and subsequent adjustments based on the patient’s clinical status, including the degree of hypoxemia and signs of pulmonary hypertension.The highest concentration must be consistently maintained for at least two hours (or one full clinical evaluation cycle) in order to be divided into high/low concentration groups.

Data were extracted retrospectively from the electronic medical record system. Collected variables included: Baseline characteristics: age, sex, APACHE II score at ICU admission, presence of pulmonary hypertension, and time from ARDS diagnosis to iNO initiation.Treatment parameters: iNO concentration and cumulative duration, norepinephrine dosage.Laboratory and physiological indices were collected within 24–48 h after iNO initiation, after patients had achieved stable iNO concentration for at least 2 h. These included: oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂) post-iNO, inflammatory markers (white blood cell count, interleukin-6 [IL-6], tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]), and thromboelastography parameter (maximum amplitude, MA value). This time window was selected to capture the potential inflammatory response during stable iNO exposure while minimizing confounding from late-stage complications (e.g., secondary infections).Outcome measures: duration of mechanical ventilation, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and 30-day survival.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the χ² test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to identify optimal cutoff values for significant continuous variables. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.For continuous variables demonstrating significant differences between groups (P < 0.05), we further plotted receiver operating characteristic curves to evaluate their ability to distinguish between high- and low-concentration iNO groups, and determined optimal diagnostic cut-off points using the Youden index. Thirty-day survival rates were compared using the Kaplan-Meier method, with the Log-rank test employed to assess the significance of intergroup differences.

Multivariable Cox regression model specification

To evaluate the independent association between iNO dose and 30-day survival, we constructed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for clinically relevant covariates.The model included the following variables, entered as continuous or categorical predictors as specified: iNO dose group: categorical (0 = low-dose [< 10 ppm], 1 = high-dose [≥ 10 ppm]), Age: continuous (per 1-year increase), Sex: categorical (0 = female, 1 = male), APACHE II score: continuous (per 1-point increase), SOFA score: continuous (per 1-point increase), Pulmonary hypertension: categorical (0 = absent, 1 = present), Time from ARDS diagnosis to iNO initiation: continuous (per 1-hour increase), Cumulative iNO duration: continuous (per 1-hour increase).All continuous variables were tested for linearity and met the proportional hazards assumption (Schoenfeld residuals global test P > 0.05). Results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

Baseline characteristics and patient grouping

A total of 117 patients with severe ARDS were included in this retrospective analysis. Based on the iNO concentration, they were divided into a high-concentration group (≥ 10 ppm, n = 59) and a low-concentration group (< 10 ppm, n = 58). The baseline characteristics of the two groups, including age, APACHE II score, incidence of comorbid pulmonary hypertension, time from ARDS diagnosis to iNO initiation, post-iNO oxygenation index, and cumulative iNO duration, were well-balanced and showed no statistically significant differences (all P > 0.05). Comparison of treatment outcomes and inflammatory/coagulation profiles

Significant differences were observed between the two groups in key clinical outcomes and laboratory parameters (Table 1). Within 24–48 h after iNO initiation, compared to the low-concentration group, patients receiving high-concentration iNO (≥ 10 ppm) had: A significantly longer duration of mechanical ventilation (154.78 ± 15.99 vs. 134.97 ± 18.37 h, P < 0.001).Markedly elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers, including white blood cell count, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (all P < 0.001).A lower maximum amplitude (MA) value on thromboelastography (25 [19–27] vs. 39.5 [35.8–43.3] mm, P < 0.001), indicating impaired platelet function and clot strength.A higher requirement for norepinephrine support (4.21 ± 0.53 vs.4.01 ± 0.42 µg/(kg●min), P = 0.026).A higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (18.3 ± 3.5 vs.16.7 ± 4.4, P = 0.031).Fewer 30-day survival days (28 [19–30] vs. 30 [27–30] days, P < 0.001).Indicating that high-dose iNO exposure was associated with a more intense systemic inflammatory response during the early phase of treatment.

Diagnostic value of key differentiating parameters (ROC analysis)

To identify which parameters best distinguished the high-concentration group from the low-concentration group and to determine their clinically relevant cutoff values, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis on the five significantly different continuous variables. As shown in Fig. 3A-E, all parameters demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values greater than 0.7.The optimal cutoff values, along with their sensitivity and specificity, were as follows: Mechanical ventilation time: 145 h (Sensitivity: 0.690, Specificity: 0.828).White blood cell count: 17.36 × 10⁹/L (Sensitivity: 0.780, Specificity: 0.724).Interleukin-6 (IL-6): 672 pg/mL (Sensitivity: 0.847, Specificity: 0.983).Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α): 19.65 pg/mL (Sensitivity: 0.966, Specificity: 1.000).Thromboelastography MA value: 34 mm (Sensitivity: 0.983, Specificity: 0.793).These cutoff values may serve as potential clinical indicators for monitoring patients at risk of exacerbated inflammation and coagulopathy associated with higher-dose iNO therapy.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in 30-day survival between the two groups (Log-rank test, P = 0.019). Patients in the high-concentration iNO group had a lower probability of survival within the 30-day period compared to those in the low-concentration group (Fig. 3F).

Multivariable Cox regression analysis

After adjusting for potential confounders in a multivariable Cox model, high-dose iNO (≥ 10 ppm) remained independently associated with increased 30-day mortality (HR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.18–4.65, P = 0.015). The full model results for all covariates were as follows: age (HR = 1.02 per year, 95% CI: 0.98–1.06, P = 0.312), male sex (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.56–1.42, P = 0.627), APACHE II score (HR = 1.03 per point, 95% CI: 0.97–1.09, P = 0.289), SOFA score (HR = 1.05 per point, 95% CI: 0.99–1.12, P = 0.108), presence of pulmonary hypertension (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.73–2.01, P = 0.456), time from ARDS diagnosis to iNO initiation (HR = 1.01 per hour, 95% CI: 0.98–1.04, P = 0.563), and cumulative iNO duration (HR = 1.00 per hour, 95% CI: 0.99–1.01, P = 0.892). A forest plot visualizing these results is provided in Fig. 4.

Analysis of sensitivity

We redefined the high- and low-dose groups using various concentration thresholds (8 ppm and 12 ppm) and repeated comparisons of primary outcome measures (SOFA score, 30-day survival rate, inflammatory markers) to ensure the robustness of study conclusions regarding threshold selection. When compared to studies using a 10 ppm threshold, the results consistently demonstrated directionality and significance, suggesting that the core finding—that high-dose iNO is linked to worse outcomes—is resilient and unaffected by threshold selection.

Discussion

Physiological background and rationale for dose selection

Nitric oxide (NO) is an important signaling molecule in both physiology and pathophysiology. Studies have demonstrated that NO regulates bronchial smooth muscle tone and pulmonary blood flow, improves ventilation-perfusion matching, reduces pulmonary hypertension, enhances arterial oxygenation, and may decrease lung infiltrate [9, 10].In recent years, NO inhalation therapy has been widely employed in the clinical treatment of ARDS patients, and it has demonstrated clinical success [12, 13].

However, NO also plays a complex role in infections, inflammatory reactions, and immune responses [14, 15].Research has implicated NO as a mediator in inflammatory processes and tissue damage [16–18].Elevated NO concentrations may trigger inflammatory cascades and have been associated with septic shock and multiorgan dysfunction in experimental studies [19, 20].Additionally, NO inhibits platelet aggregation and affects platelet function [21].These dual effects highlight that inhaled NO therapy represents a two-edged sword in critically ill patients.

The selection of 10 ppm as the cutoff concentration in this study was based on previous work by Lavoie A et al., who found that hypoxic rebound occurs when NO concentrations ≥ 10 ppm are abruptly discontinued [22].This threshold was further supported by our restricted cubic spline analysis, which identified an inflection point near 10 ppm for the relationship between iNO concentration and inflammatory markers (Fig. 1).

Inflammatory response, hemodynamic support, and clinical outcomes

This study analyzed changes in inflammatory markers among 117 patients receiving different concentrations of iNO. ROC analysis demonstrated strong correlations between high-concentration iNO therapy and elevated inflammatory markers, with cutoff values of WBC > 17.36 × 10⁹/L, IL-6 > 672 pg/mL, and TNF-α > 19.65 pg/mL. The concurrent elevation of multiple inflammatory markers suggests a more pronounced systemic inflammatory response state in patients receiving high-dose iNO. These findings align with studies demonstrating NO’s role in regulating leukocyte activation, migration, and vasodilation [23].

The mean dose of norepinephrine used in patients with high concentrations of NO inhalation in this study was significantly higher than that in the low-concentration group, which could be related to the generation of large amounts of inflammatory mediators to worsen the vascular response to septic shock after inhalation of high concentrations of NO [24].

Regarding coagulation function, thrombus maximal amplitude on thromboelastography serves as a sensitive measure of platelet aggregation and function [25].Patients receiving high-concentration iNO exhibited significantly lower MA values, with an ROC-derived cutoff of < 34 mm, suggesting an association between high-dose iNO and impaired platelet function that may increase the risk of coagulation dysfunction. MA value primarily reflects final clot strength, determined by the interaction of fibrinogen levels, platelet count, and function [26].The observed reduction in MA values may indicate hypofibrinogenemia or platelet dysfunction, potentially related to coagulation factor depletion from systemic inflammation or direct NO-mediated platelet inhibition. Nevertheless, a lower MA value alone does not necessarily indicate clinical coagulopathy or bleeding risk, but rather necessitates comprehensive assessment alongside other clinical and laboratory parameters.

Consistent with previous research demonstrating that inhaled NO only transiently enhances oxygenation [27, 28], our study found no statistically significant difference in oxygenation index between patients receiving different NO concentrations. The combination of leukocyte activation, inflammatory mediator production, and impaired coagulation may explain the observed clinical outcomes: patients in the high-dose group experienced significantly longer mechanical ventilation (> 145 h), higher SOFA scores, greater organ dysfunction, and increased 30-day mortality.

Comparison with previous studies and etiologic considerations

High-dose iNO was found to be significantly correlated with increased levels of TNF-α and IL-6, consistent with experimental studies suggesting that elevated NO concentrations can promote pro-inflammatory cytokine release [29]. Similarly, the observed decline in MA values, indicating compromised platelet function, may reflect the combined effects of NO on platelet activation and the systemic inflammatory state [30].These mechanistic explanations, however, require future studies to confirm through direct measurement of relevant molecular markers. Notably, the lack of improvement in oxygenation indices may be attributed to NO’s bidirectional physiological effects, including potential counteracting mechanisms such as increased alveolar edema and reduced oxygen transport [31].Although Lavoie A et al. identified the risk of withdrawal rebound with high NO concentrations [22], the current investigation suggests that dose-dependent exacerbation of inflammation may be an equally important concern.

The apparent disagreement with the data of Padayachee S et al. [27], may arise from differences in study populations: COVID-19 ARDS is characterized by microthrombosis, where the oxygenation benefit of high-dose NO may be more pronounced, whereas our cohort was predominantly bacterial ARDS, in which pro-inflammatory effects may be amplified. Although the current study demonstrates that ≥ 10 ppm iNO is associated with exacerbated systemic inflammation, it is important to note that ultra-high doses (≥ 80 ppm) may have salvage value in selected extreme conditions [32, 33], such as biofilm infections with drug-resistant bacteria [34], or severe hypoxia during ECMO transition [35].These observations underscore the importance of developing iNO strategies based on ARDS etiologic stratification.

Clinical implications and future directions

This study demonstrates that in patients with severe ARDS, low-dose iNO (< 10 ppm) was associated with improved oxygenation and a favorable safety profile, whereas high-dose iNO (≥ 10 ppm) was associated with exacerbated systemic inflammation, impaired platelet function, increased vasopressor requirements, prolonged mechanical ventilation, higher organ failure scores, and increased 30-day mortality. Based on these findings, we suggest that iNO concentrations ≥ 10 ppm be used cautiously, particularly in patients with bacterial ARDS. When higher doses are clinically necessary (e.g., for refractory hypoxemia or right ventricular failure), close monitoring of inflammatory markers, coagulation parameters (including TEG/ROTEM MA values), vasopressor requirements, and organ function is recommended. The ROC-derived cutoff values identified in this study (e.g., IL-6 > 672 pg/mL, MA < 34 mm) may serve as early warning thresholds for dose-related toxicity.

Future research should focus on optimizing low-dose iNO strategies (timing, duration, withdrawal protocols) and exploring combinations with other lung-protective or anti-inflammatory therapies. Ultra-high iNO doses (≥ 80 ppm) may have a role in select salvage scenarios (e.g., drug-resistant bacterial biofilms, severe hypoxia during ECMO transition), but their risk-benefit profile requires rigorous evaluation in prospective studies. All iNO therapy should follow standardized gradual withdrawal protocols to mitigate rebound phenomena.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center, non-randomized design. First, selection of iNO dose was based on clinical judgment rather than randomization, introducing potential indication bias. Although we adjusted for measured confounders (age, APACHE II, SOFA, etc.) in multivariable analysis, unmeasured factors—such as the precise severity of refractory hypoxemia, right ventricular dysfunction, or other unrecorded resuscitative interventions (e.g., ECMO)—may have influenced both treatment selection and outcomes. Second, the lack of detailed data on concomitant therapies (e.g., antibiotics, corticosteroids) could confound the observed associations. Third, while inflammatory markers were measured 24–48 h after iNO initiation, establishing a temporal sequence, reverse causation cannot be excluded; patients with more severe baseline inflammation may have been preferentially selected for higher iNO doses. Fourth, coagulation assessment relied solely on TEG MA values without more specific immunothrombosis markers (e.g., platelet-leukocyte aggregates). Fifth, the sample size (n = 117) limited our ability to perform subgroup analyses by ARDS etiology (e.g., bacterial vs. viral). The absence of independent prognostic value for baseline variables in multivariable analysis may reflect limited statistical power, collinearity, or the strong risk-stratifying effect of iNO dose grouping itself. These limitations underscore the need for larger, prospective, multicenter studies with pre-iNO baseline inflammatory profiling and serial post-exposure measurements to establish causality and optimize dose selection based on individual patient characteristics and ARDS etiology.

Conclusions

This study indicates that, in comparison to low-concentration therapy, inhaling high-concentration nitric oxide (≥ 10 ppm) in adult patients with severe ARDS is linked to more severe systemic inflammation, impaired coagulation function (lower MA values), and increased vasopressor needs. Additionally, it is linked to increased 30-day mortality, more severe organ dysfunction, and longer periods of artificial breathing.According to this data, systemic toxicity should be prevented by dynamically monitoring coagulation and inflammation parameters, and the lowest effective dose of iNO in the treatment of ARDS should be tightly regulated below 10 ppm. This approach has immediate practical implications for ARDS patients’ survival and prognosis.
