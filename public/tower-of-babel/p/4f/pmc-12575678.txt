Functional evaluation of cis-vaccenic acid in cordia dichotoma with tissue-specific biochemical insights
Authors: Shilpa Raina, Zahid Nabi Sheikh, Parshant Bakshi, Saud Alamri, Manzer H. Siddiqui, Khalid Ul Islam Rather, Kamran Ashraf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study systematically elucidates the biochemical composition and tissue-specific pharmacodynamic properties of Cordia dichotoma, with a particular emphasis on the distribution and functional implications of cis-vaccenic acid across distinct plant parts, including pulp, seeds, leaves and bark. Comprehensive phytochemical analyses were performed to quantify total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC), alongside in vitro assessments of antioxidant, antidiabetic, antibacterial and neuroprotective activities. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiling identified cis-vaccenic acid as a predominant bioactive compound, especially abundant in seeds and bark. This monounsaturated fatty acid, recognized for its pivotal role in lipid metabolism and cardiovascular health, was found to significantly contribute to the bioactivity of these tissues. Notably, the ethyl acetate fraction of seeds showed the highest α-amylase (68.25%) and α-glucosidase (84.68%) inhibition. Pulp ethyl acetate and acetone fractions also demonstrated strong α-glucosidase inhibition (81.53% and 80.63%, respectively). Leaf and bark fractions showed moderate activity. Overall, ethyl acetate and acetone fractions were the most active across plant parts. The ethyl acetate fractions of C. dichotoma seed, leaf and bark showed the strongest antioxidant activity with low DPPH IC₅₀ values (21.82, 19.98, and 16.97 µg/mL, respectively) and high FRAP and metal chelation values, comparable to ascorbic acid. These results highlight their potent free radical scavenging potential. The study demonstrated significant acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity, with methanolic extracts of pulp (45%) and bark (43%) showing the highest inhibition. Acetone and chloroform fractions also displayed notable activity, particularly in seed (51% and 26% respectively), while hexane fractions showed no activity. Hierarchical clustering and correlation matrix analysis indicated strong positive correlations between FRAP values and metal ion-chelation activity, suggesting potential synergistic antioxidant mechanisms. Extracts and fractions of C. dichotoma exhibited mild antibacterial activity, with the ethyl acetate and chloroform fractions showing the highest inhibition zones (up to 1.3 mm) against K. pneumoniae, S. aureus and B. subtilis, though less effective than the positive control. These findings highlight the multifaceted bioactive potential of different plant tissues and their role in mitigating oxidative stress and microbial proliferation. The study integrates these results with existing literature, positing that the cardiovascular and lipid-regulatory effects of cis-vaccenic acid may extend to tissue-specific bioactivities in C. dichotoma. This integrative functional assessment provides a robust foundation for future exploration of the plant’s therapeutic potential, emphasizing its applicability in developing antioxidant, antibacterial, antidiabetic and neuroprotective interventions.

Introduction

C. dichotoma, a member of the Boraginaceae family, is a deciduous tree native to tropical and subtropical regions, including India and Sri Lanka. Traditionally, various parts of the plant such as the bark, leaves, fruits, and seeds have been utilized in indigenous medical systems to treat a range of ailments including fever, cough, wounds, inflammation, ulcers, and digestive disorders1,2. The Boraginaceae family itself comprises approximately 2,740 species across 148 genera, many of which have been reported to contain potent bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential3. Ethnopharmacological interest in this family continues to grow, especially in the context of chronic disease management, due to the rich presence of phenolic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids and alkaloids compounds known for their wide-ranging biological activities4,5. With increasing global interest in natural remedies and plant-based therapeutic agents, C. dichotoma stands out as a promising candidate for scientific investigation, particularly in the treatment of metabolic diseases and oxidative stress-related disorders. Medicinal plants are central to the healthcare systems of over 80% of the global population, especially in low- and middle-income countries where access to conventional pharmaceuticals is limited6. Moreover, approximately 25% of conventional drugs are derived directly or indirectly from plants, underlining the immense potential of botanical sources in drug development7,8.

Several recent studies have reported diverse pharmacological properties of C. dichotoma, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and antidiabetic effects3,9. These therapeutic effects are attributed to the presence of secondary metabolites capable of modulating biochemical pathways associated with disease progression. In the context of diabetes mellitus, these properties are particularly relevant. Diabetes, especially Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), is a complex metabolic disorder involving insulin resistance and impaired glucose uptake in key tissues such as muscle and liver10,11. Though pharmaceutical options are available, their effectiveness is often hindered by limited efficacy, side effects such as hypoglycemia and gastrointestinal discomfort, and poor patient compliance12,13. Furthermore, oxidative stress marked by an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and compromised antioxidant defenses plays a pivotal role in both the onset and complications of diabetes, including cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, nephropathy and retinopathy14–16. Therapeutic interest are natural inhibitors of carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes such as α-amylase and α-glucosidase, which play a central role in postprandial glucose regulation by hydrolyzing dietary starch and disaccharides into absorbable monosaccharides17,18. Although synthetic inhibitors such as miglitol, acarbose and voglibose are clinically used, they are frequently associated with gastrointestinal adverse effects, including bloating, flatulence and diarrhea19. In addition to its antidiabetic and antioxidant properties, C. dichotoma has demonstrated modest antibacterial activity, further supporting its therapeutic versatility. Various extracts and fractions, particularly those derived using ethyl acetate and chloroform, exhibited inhibitory effects against both Gram-positive (S. aureus, B. subtilis) and Gram-negative (E. coli, K. pneumoniae) bacterial strains. Though the zones of inhibition were generally smaller compared to standard antibiotics, these findings indicate the presence of bioactive compounds with potential antimicrobial efficacy. The interrelationship between antioxidant, antidiabetic and antibacterial activities is particularly noteworthy, as oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation often exacerbated by microbial infections are central to the pathogenesis and complications of diabetes. Antioxidants may indirectly support glycemic control by mitigating oxidative damage to insulin-producing β-cells and enhancing insulin sensitivity, while antibacterial agents can reduce the risk of secondary infections, which are common in diabetic individuals due to compromised immunity. Therefore, a plant like C. dichotoma, which demonstrates all three bioactivities, offers a comprehensive therapeutic profile, making it a valuable candidate for the development of multifunctional phytopharmaceuticals targeting complex metabolic disorders.

This underscores the urgent need for safer and more tolerable alternatives derived from natural sources. In this context, C. dichotoma offers a compelling research opportunity. Preliminary phytochemical screening of its extracts has revealed the presence of various phenolic and flavonoid compounds, which are known for both antioxidant and enzyme-inhibitory activities. Investigating the methanolic extract and solvent-partitioned fractions (n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate and acetone) allows for the isolation and identification of specific bioactive constituents with dual antidiabetic and antioxidant potential. This dual mechanism regulation of carbohydrate metabolism and attenuation of oxidative stress makes the plant a particularly attractive candidate for the development of integrated therapeutic strategies for T2DM.

This study, therefore, aims to evaluate the antioxidant, antibacterial, antidiabetic and anti-Alzheimer, activities of various extracts from C. dichotoma, with a focus on identifying specific compounds responsible for these effects. Such findings could contribute significantly to the search for novel, plant-based therapeutics with fewer side effects, supporting both traditional knowledge and modern pharmacological innovation.

Materials and methods

Chemical

Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA) provided the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, gallic acid, quercetin, ascorbic acid, 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), sulforhodamine B (SRB), crystal violet blue, Rhodamine 123, fetal bovine serum (FBS) and DCFDA (2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate). All additional chemicals and solvents were of analytical quality and obtained from standard sources.

Collection of plant material and Preparation of extract and fractions

Enough quantity of fruit, leaves, and bark of C. dichotoma was collected and authenticated from SKUAST KVK located in District Samba of the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir, India. To eliminate soil pollutants, the plant material was carefully rinsed under running tap water. The plant parts were cut into small pieces, dried in the shade, and then crushed into a fine powder. Methanolic extracts were prepared by percolating the powdered plant material in 95% methanol for 24 h with intermittent shaking. The mixture was filtered using Whatman filter paper, and the residue was again soaked in methanol for an additional 24 h to ensure exhaustive extraction. The combined filtrates were then concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure to obtain the crude methanolic extract.

To separate the complex mixture of phytochemicals based on polarity, the crude methanolic extract was subjected to successive solvent fractionation using n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and acetone. These solvents were chosen strategically to partition compounds across a polarity gradient: n-hexane, a non-polar solvent, extracts lipophilic constituents such as fatty acids, waxes and some terpenoids; chloroform, moderately non-polar, is effective in extracting compounds like alkaloids, some phenolics and sterols; ethyl acetate, a medium-polarity solvent, is particularly suitable for polyphenols and flavonoids; and acetone, which is more polar, helps isolate more polar compounds including certain glycosides, tannins, and sugars. This systematic fractionation increases the likelihood of isolating bioactive compounds in relatively pure forms for further pharmacological evaluation. All fractions were concentrated and stored at − 20 °C until further investigation.

Sample Preparation

Stock solutions of 20 mg/ml were produced by dissolving methanolic extract and subsequent fractions in DMSO. While as antioxidants, phenolics and flavonoids were produced at a stock concentration of 3 mg/ml in HPLC grade methanol. Stock solutions were prepared at least one day in advance.

Phytochemical analysis

Standard assays were used to conduct preliminary phytochemical analysis of the extract/fractions for the presence of secondary metabolites such as phenolics and flavonoids.

Determination of total flavonoid contents

The total flavonoid content was measured using a slightly modified version of the aluminium chloride colorimetric technique used by Sembiring et al., 201820. 50 µL of extract solution was mixed with 10 µL of 10% aluminium chloride solution, followed by 150 µL of 96% ethanol. In a 96-well plate, 10 µL of 1 M sodium acetate was added to the mixture. As a control, 96% ethanol was utilized. The same technique was used to create a standard solution of Quercetin (0.78, 1.562, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 µg/ml, R2 = 0.987) in methanol. The total flavonoid content was calculated as mg Quercetin equivalent/g DW.

Determination of total phenolic contents

The total phenolic content of various extracts and fractions was measured on a 96-well plate using the Folin-Ciocalteu method by Sembiring et al., 201820 with slight modifications. In a flat-bottom 96-well microplate, 50 µL of extract solution from each part and their fractions were combined with 100 µL of 1:4 diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagents and agitated for 1 min. After 5 min, 75 µL of 20% sodium carbonate solution was added and the mixture was shaken at medium continuous speed for 1 min. After 2 hours of incubation at room temperature, the absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M Nano Elisa plate Reader, Austria). As a calibration standard, Gallic acid (0.78, 1.562, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 µg/ml, R2 = 0.993) was serially diluted. The results were reported as mg/g of gallic acid equivalents in milligrams per gram of dry extract (mg GAE/g).

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The 1, 1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) tests was used to evaluate the free radical scavenging activity of crude methanolic extracts of each plant component and their fractions. The test was carried out in a 96-well plate in the manner described by Thida et al. (2018)21with minor modifications. In each 96 well, 100 µL of extracts and fractions of various plant components at varied concentrations (15.62–1000 µg/ml) were mixed with 100 µL of a methanol solution of DPPH (0.1 mM). The reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, and absorbance at 517 nm was measured using a micro-plate reader (Tecan Infinite M Nano Elisa plate Reader, Austria). Ascorbic acid was utilized as the positive control. All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the radical scavenging ability (%) was determined as follows:

% scavenging = [(A0 – A1)/(A0)] ×100.

Where A0 represents the absorbance of the control reaction (containing all reagents except the test extract or standard) and A1 represents the absorbance of the test extract or standard.

Determination of ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

Ferric reducing antioxidant power of extract and fractions was determined according to the method of Benzie and Strain (1996)22. The principle of this method is based on the reduction of a ferric-tripyridyltriazine complex to its ferrous, colored form, in the presence of antioxidants. 132 ml FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing 100 ml of 300 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 ml of 10 mM TPTZ [2,4,6-tri-(2-pyridyl)−1,3,5- triazine] solution in 40 mM HCl plus 10 ml of 20 mM FeCl3 and 12 ml of distilled water. The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared every time. The samples of extracts were taken from their respective stock solutions (1 mg/ml) and mixed with 3 ml of FRAP reagent. After that absorbance was taken at 593 nm after incubating the reaction mixture for 10 min. The FRAP activity was calculated from the calibration curve of ferrous sulphate (FeSO4 0.2 H 2 O). FRAP value was determined for each sample and expressed as µM FeSO4 0.2 H2O eq./g of the dried extract.

Determination of percent metal ion chelation

The chelating effect of ferrous ions in extracts was estimated by the method followed by Dinis et al., 199423 with slight modifications. Briefly, 50 µL of extract/essential oil was added into 2950 µL of methanol and mixed with 60 µL of 2 mM FeCl2 (ferrous chloride). The reaction was initiated by the addition of 120 µL of 5 mM ferrozine into the reaction mixture, which was then shaken vigorously and left standing at ambient temperature for 4 min. After that the absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 562 nm. Each test sample was repeated thrice. The ratio of inhibition of ferrozine-Fe 2+ complex formation was calculated as follows:

% inhibition = [(absorbance of control – absorbance of sample)/absorbance of control]

×100.

Determination of antibacterial activity

Bacterial strains used in the study

Three gram positive (Staphylococcus aureus MTCC7443, Micrococcus luteus MTCC4821, Bacillus subtilis MTCCCC2389) and two gram negative (Escherichia coli MTCC2127, Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC7172) bacterial strains used in the study were procured from IMTECH Chandigarh. The bacterial cultures were maintained in the nutrient agar slants and kept at 4 °C during the entire study and used as stock cultures.

Preparation of inoculum

The selected bacterial strains were inoculated in Muller Hinton broth (MHB) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Turbidity of the resulting bacterial suspension of active cultures was measured by taking absorbance at 600 nm using spectrophotometer and the optical density was adjusted equivalent to 0.5 Macfarl and standard by dilution with MHB.

Determination of antibacterial activity

The study used gram positive (S. aureus, M. luteus, B. subtilis) and gram negative (E. coli and K. pneumoniae) species of bacteria to determine the antibacterial activity of extracts and fractions of different parts of C. dichotoma according to the standard disc-diffusion method24. Sterile discs loaded with different concentrations (1 mg/mL) were aseptically kept in the middle of petri dishes previously swabbed with broth solution of test bacteria. Furthermore, ampicillin (1 mg/mL) was utilized as a positive control, whereas, silver nitrate solution and CSE were used as negative controls. The clear zones surrounding the discs were scored for antibacterial activity.

Determination of α -amylase inhibitory activity

α -amylase inhibitory activity of the Methanolic extracts and subsequent fractions were measured according to the methods of Miller et al., 195625 with slight modifications. Porcine pancreatic α -amylase (2U/ml) was dissolved in phosphate buffer (0.02 M), NaCl (0.006 M) at pH 6.9. 200 µL of α -amylase was mixed with 200 µL of the methanolic extract and subsequent fractions and was incubated for 10 min at 30o C. After incubation 200 µL of 1% starch solution (w/v) was added to the reaction mixture. Reaction was stopped after 10 min by adding 200 µL of DNS (3, 5-dinitrosalicylic acid) reagent and boiled in water bath for 10 min. After this volume of the reaction mixture was made upto 5 mL by adding double distilled water. The absorbance was taken at 540 nm. Blank solution was prepared with the replacement of enzyme concentration and inhibitor concentration with 200 µL of buffer. Rest all the ingredients are same. Positive control solution was prepared with the replacement of inhibitor concentration with 100 µL of buffer. Rest all the ingredients are same that of reaction mixture. The inhibitory activity was calculated as inhibitory percentage by the explanation as follows.

α-amylase inhibition (%) = (A control - A sample/A control) x 100.

Where A = Absorbance.

α -glucosidase inhibitory activity

α- glucosidase inhibitory activity was measured by adopting the technique of Dewi et al., 2007)26 with slight changes. The enzyme reaction consisted of 10 µL of extract and 10 µL of α-glucosidase solution (1.0 U/ml prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer pH 6.9). The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. 40 µL of substrate (5mM p-nitrophenyl- α-D glucopyranoside solution in 0.1 M phosphate buffer pH 6.9) was added after incubation. The reaction was again incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The absorbance at 405 nm. Readings were compared with control (containing 10 µL of buffer solution instead of extract sample). The inhibitory activity was estimated as inhibition % and calculated as follows:

α- glucosidase inhibition (%) = (A control - A sample/A control) x 100.

Where, A = Absorbance.

Determination of acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity

Acetylcholinesterase enzymatic activity of methanolic extracts and subsequent fractions was measured by following the procedure described by Rhee et al., 201127 with slight modifications. 180 µL 50 mM Tris-HCL buffer pH 8. 60 µL sample and 15 µL acetylcholinesterase solution containing 0.26 U/mL were mixed in a microplate and left to incubate for 15 min. Subsequently, 45 µL of the solution of acetylcholinesterase (0.023 mg/mL) and 284 µL of 3 mM DTNB were added. The absorbance at 405 nm was read when the reaction reached equilibrium. A control reaction was carried out using water instead of extract and it was considered 100% activity.

I% = 100 - (A sample/A control) × 100

Where A sample is the absorbance of the extract containing reaction and A control the absorbance of the reaction control. Tests were carried out in triplicate and a blank with Tris-HCL buffer instead of enzyme solution was used.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the findings were computed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPAD prism 5.0 was used to calculate IC50 and analyse variation in concentration of total phenol and total flavanoids content by two ways ANOVA at 95% confidence level followed by correlation plots.

Results and discussion

In the present investigation, crude extract as well as fractions of different parts of C. dichotoma showed TFC, TPC, antioxidant, antibacterial, antidiabetic and anti-Alzheimer potential.

Total flavanoid content

Flavonoids are secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties and their efficacy is influenced by the number and positioning of hydroxyl (OH) groups on their structure28. Flavonoid concentrations in selected plant extracts and fractions were determined using an aluminum chloride colorimetric method. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (A), the total flavonoid content of all extracts and fractions (20 in total) from various parts of C. dichotoma was expressed in Quercetin equivalents (QE) per gram of dry extract weight. The flavonoid content in different extracts and fractions from C. dichotoma parts showed significant variation (P < 0.001). The highest flavonoid content was observed in the ethyl acetate and hexane fractions of leaves and the acetone fraction of bark (74.34 ± 0.56, 56.49 ± 0.60, and 48.59 ± 1.10 mg QE/g, respectively). Conversely, the lowest flavonoid contents were recorded in the chloroform fraction of bark, hexane fraction of pulp and acetone fraction of seeds (1.01 ± 0.45, 1.02 ± 0.87, and 1.16 ± 0.96 mg QE/g, respectively), as shown in Fig. 1 (A). Comparatively, Imam et al. (2015)29 reported a total flavonoid content (TFC) of 3.91 mg QE/g in the methanolic extract of bark, which aligns closely with our findings of 3.73 ± 0.71 mg QE/g. Additionally, Prabu et al. (2010)30 observed a total flavonoid concentration of 5.65 ± 0.43 µg/mL in the leaves of C. dichotoma. It is well-established that genetic diversity, biological factors, environmental conditions, and seasonal variations significantly influence flavonoid content (Veberic, 2016)31.

Total phenolic content

Phenolic compounds are essential plant components with antioxidant activity, primarily due to their redox characteristics. The hydroxyl groups in plant extracts contribute to free radical scavenging32. As shown in Fig. 1 (B), the total phenolics in all extracts and fractions from C. dichotoma were expressed in gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry extract weight. The phenolic content in extracts and fractions from various parts of C. dichotoma varied significantly (P < 0.001). The highest phenolic contents were found in the methanolic extract of bark, the ethyl acetate fraction of seed and the methanolic extract of pulp (230.72 ± 0.73, 223.50 ± 2.61, and 222.09 ± 1.049 mg GAE/g, respectively). In contrast, the lowest phenolic contents were observed in the hexane fraction of seed and pulp and the acetone fraction of leaves (25.93 ± 0.59, 54.23 ± 2.08, and 83.10 ± 2.40 mg GAE/g, respectively), as indicated in Fig. 1 (B). The extraction methods and solvents are crucial in dissolving the plant’s endogenous components. Plant constituents can be either polar or non-polar, and phenolic compounds are more soluble in polar organic solvents due to their hydroxyl groups. As a result, methanol was selected as the extraction solvent14,33. Rahman et al. (2015)34 reported a total phenol content (TPC) of 176.46 mg GAE/g in a methanolic extract of C. dichotoma leaves. Similarly, Ibrahim et al. (2016)35 found a TPC of 112.71 ± 8.40 mg GAE/g dry extract in the fruit of C. dichotoma. Hussain et al. (2012)36 also reported a TPC of 48.75 mg GAE/g in the bark. The differences between the phenolic content results in this study and those reported in the literature may be attributed to varying levels of carotenoids, ascorbic acid, geographical differences, or differences in extraction techniques, which can influence phenolic content.

Antioxidant activity assessments

The DPPH assay is widely used to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of plant extracts, based on their ability to reduce the stable, purple-colored DPPH radical to its yellow-colored non-radical form. The antioxidant activity of different parts of C. dichotoma (pulp, seeds, leaves and bark) was evaluated using various solvent extracts, revealing significant variation in their capacity to neutralize free radicals, reduce ferric ions and chelate metal ions. The results indicate that extracts obtained using polar and moderately polar solvents (methanol, ethyl acetate and chloroform) exhibit superior antioxidant activity compared to non-polar solvent (hexane) extracts, which consistently show weaker performance. Among the plant parts studied, the bark demonstrates the highest antioxidant potential, particularly in its ethyl acetate fraction, which exhibits the lowest IC50 value in the DPPH radical scavenging assay (16.97 µg/mL), a high FRAP value (229 µM), and significant metal ion chelation activity (75.22%). These results suggest that the bark contains a high concentration of bioactive compounds capable of efficiently neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing oxidative stress. The methanolic extract of the bark also shows strong antioxidant activity, reinforcing the bark’s prominence as a source of potent antioxidant compounds. The seeds also show considerable antioxidant activity, particularly in the ethyl acetate fraction, which demonstrates the highest FRAP value (413 µM), a low IC50 value (21.82 µg/mL) and the strongest metal ion chelation capacity (90.39%) across all plant parts tested. These findings indicate that the seeds possess compounds with substantial electron-donating capacity and metal ion-binding properties, key mechanisms in mitigating oxidative damage. The pulp and leaves display moderate antioxidant activity. The ethyl acetate fraction of the pulp shows relatively low IC50 (29.11 µg/mL) and a high FRAP value (104 µM), indicating good radical scavenging and reducing potential. Similarly, the methanolic extract of the leaves performs reasonably well, though the overall antioxidant activity of the leaves is lower compared to the bark and seeds, as indicated by higher IC50 values and moderate FRAP values. The hexane fractions from all parts consistently exhibit weak antioxidant activity, with high IC50 values and lower FRAP and metal chelation results, indicating that non-polar compounds contribute minimally to the overall antioxidant potential of C. dichotoma. In contrast, polar and moderately polar compounds are the primary contributors to the plant’s antioxidant properties.

Chemical compounds identified in various parts of C. dichotoma through GCMS Analysis.

The GC-MS analysis of C. dichotoma reveals a diverse array of bioactive compounds in its bark, leaves, pulp and seeds, each contributing to its medicinal properties. The bark shows a high concentration of fatty acids, particularly palmitic acid (18.34%) and cis-vaccenic acid (28.81%), which are known for their anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. These fatty acids, combined with phenols like 4-vinylguaiacol and benzylamine derivatives, suggest that the bark has strong antioxidant and healing properties. This supports the traditional use of C. dichotoma bark in treating inflammatory conditions and wounds. Similarly, the leaves contain significant amounts of neophytadiene (7.57%), a sesquiterpenoid with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects, along with fatty acids and phenylpropanes, reinforcing their role in protecting against infections and promoting skin health. The pulp of C. dichotoma is rich in both alcohols, like [R, R]−2,3-butanediol (9.21%), and fatty acids, such as n-hexadecanoic acid (19.57%), which contribute to its antimicrobial and skin-soothing properties. The seed, on the other hand, is a valuable source of cis-vaccenic acid (33.76%) and methyl linoleate (11.36%), both known for their potential to improve cardiovascular health and reduce inflammation. These bioactive lipids may explain the seed’s use in traditional medicine for heart health and metabolic benefits. The results obtained from the GC-MS analysis of C. dichotoma align with findings from various studies that have investigated the phytochemical composition and biological properties of this plant. In terms of the pulp’s composition, Rahman et al. (2021)60 found high levels of alcohols and fatty acids in methanolic extracts of C. dichotoma, aligning with the current study’s observation of [R, R]−2,3-butanediol (9.21%) and n-hexadecanoic acid (19.57%). Their research suggested that these compounds possess antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, further supporting the use of C. dichotoma pulp in traditional remedies for skin health and wound healing60. Lastly, Cis-vaccenic acid, a monounsaturated omega-7 fatty acid, has been increasingly recognized for its role in supporting lipid metabolism, cardiovascular health and anti-inflammatory responses. Found prominently in C. dichotoma seeds, where it comprises 33.76% of the fatty acid profile, cis-vaccenic acid has been linked to numerous health benefits, particularly concerning cardiovascular risk factors and cholesterol regulation. Additionally, research suggests that cis-vaccenic acid can help reduce triglyceride levels and potentially minimize the risk of atherosclerosis, partly due to its influence on hepatic lipid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation processes61. Furthermore, cis-vaccenic acid has shown anti-inflammatory properties, which could help mitigate chronic inflammation, a contributing factor in the development of various metabolic disorders, including cardiovascular disease. Studies indicate that cis-vaccenic acid may inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduce oxidative stress markers in cells, offering protection against inflammatory damage62. This dual action of lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory effects makes cis-vaccenic acid an attractive candidate for functional foods and nutraceutical formulations aimed at improving metabolic and cardiovascular health. The high concentration of cis-vaccenic acid in C. dichotoma seeds could indeed explain the heightened antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities observed in these seeds in comparison to other plant parts. Its presence in these seeds likely contributes to their enhanced bioactivity, which could be particularly beneficial in lipid metabolism and in reducing oxidative damage. This is supported by recent studies suggesting that cis-vaccenic acid can also exhibit radical scavenging activity, which aligns with the high antioxidant profile observed in the seed extracts63. Additionally, cis-vaccenic acid’s interaction with other bioactive compounds, such as phenolic acids and flavonoids present in C. dichotoma seeds, might further enhance the seed’s overall antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential through synergistic effects64. We, Raina et al., (2022)64 reported the presence of cis-vaccenic acid in C. dichotoma bark for the first time, which is significant as it expands our understanding of the plant’s chemical composition. The bark’s observed bioactivity, potentially bolstered by cis-vaccenic acid, could be attributed to this compound’s role in modulating cellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. The identification of cis-vaccenic acid in both seeds and bark suggests that C. dichotoma may serve as a promising natural source of this fatty acid, with applications not only in traditional medicine but also in modern health supplements aimed at cardiovascular and metabolic health. This discovery emphasizes the potential pharmacological relevance of C. dichotoma, supporting its further exploration as a valuable source of bioactive lipids that could enhance therapeutic outcomes in the management of oxidative stress and inflammatory-related conditions65. The identification and concentration of cis-vaccenic acid within C. dichotoma highlight the plant’s value in pharmacological research and functional food development, given this fatty acid’s well-documented benefits on lipid metabolism, antioxidant defense, and anti-inflammatory effects. Further studies focusing on the isolation and clinical evaluation of cis-vaccenic acid from C. dichotoma seeds and bark could provide more concrete evidence of its therapeutic potential and contribute to the development of effective, natural health-promoting formulations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the multifaceted pharmacological potential of C. dichotoma, driven by its diverse phytochemical composition. The significant antioxidant, antidiabetic, antibacterial and neuroprotective activities observed across various plant parts highlight its potential as a source of natural bioactive compounds. The high phenolic and flavonoid content in the leaves and bark correlates with robust antioxidant activity, while the seeds demonstrated notable enzyme-inhibitory properties, emphasizing their relevance in antidiabetic applications. Additionally, neuroprotective effects, particularly from pulp extracts, suggest potential therapeutic roles in managing neurodegenerative conditions. GC-MS profiling revealed key bioactive compounds, such as cis-vaccenic acid, which likely contribute to the observed bioactivities. The unique biochemical and functional properties of cis-vaccenic acid, alongside phenolic compounds, warrant further investigation for their integration into pharmaceutical and nutraceutical applications. These findings support the exploration of C. dichotoma as a source of bioactive compounds for innovative therapeutic strategies.
