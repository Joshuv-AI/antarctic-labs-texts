Cellular receptors and host factors mediating orthoflavivirus entry
Authors: Shuangshuang Yang, Meng Zhang, Xingyong Yang, Jinxi Yue, Xue Li, Peiyue Zhang, Hong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthoflaviviruses are positive-sense, enveloped RNA viruses that include dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), yellow fever virus (YFV), West Nile virus (WNV), and Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV). Viral entry is a multistep process governed by the envelope (E) glycoprotein, virion lipid composition, and host-cell molecules that act at distinct mechanistic stages. A major limitation of the current literature is that attachment factors, internalization receptors, immune-modulatory receptors, and membrane-associated cofactors are often collectively termed “receptors”, which can obscure the strength and interpretation of the underlying evidence. This review therefore reorganizes reported orthoflavivirus entry-associated molecules into four functional classes: attachment factors, bona fide or candidate internalization receptors, indirect immune-modulatory receptors, and membrane-associated or post-entry cofactors. We place particular emphasis on DENV because it imposes a major clinical burden among orthoflaviviruses and has been the subject of the most extensive receptor-related research. By integrating evidence from binding assays, genetic loss-of-function studies, and in vivo models, this review highlights conserved and virus-specific entry mechanisms. This mechanism-based framework may guide the development of receptor decoys and host-targeting antivirals, although clinical translation will require rigorous validation of antiviral breadth, delivery, safety, and resistance potential.

Background

Orthoflaviviruses, formerly classified within the genus Flavivirus, are members of the family Flaviviridae and include several medically important arthropod-borne viruses, such as DENV, ZIKV, YFV, WNV, and JEV [1, 2]. Most human-pathogenic orthoflaviviruses cycle between hematophagous arthropod vectors and vertebrate hosts. DENV, ZIKV, and YFV are transmitted primarily by Aedes mosquitoes, whereas WNV and JEV are commonly associated with Culex mosquitoes. The geographic expansion of competent mosquito vectors is shaped by urbanization, travel, vector adaptation, and climate-sensitive ecological change [3, 4].

Orthoflaviviruses continue to impose a substantial global disease burden. WNV was first recognized in New York City in 1999 and subsequently became established across North America [5]. The ZIKV epidemic in the Americas was linked to congenital infection and severe fetal neurodevelopmental abnormalities, including microcephaly [6]. DENV remains the most clinically important mosquito-borne orthoflavivirus, with large at-risk populations and recurrent outbreaks in tropical and subtropical regions [7]. These epidemiological patterns make viral entry biologically and clinically relevant because receptor usage contributes to host range, tissue tropism, and the earliest determinants of productive infection.

The clinical spectrum of orthoflavivirus infection ranges from asymptomatic infection or mild febrile illness to hemorrhagic disease, encephalitis, congenital abnormalities, and long-term sequelae. Broadly effective virus-specific antivirals are not available for routine clinical treatment [8]. Vaccines are available for selected orthoflaviviruses, including YFV, JEV, and DENV in defined populations or settings; however, ZIKV still lacks a licensed vaccine or specific preventive drug. Because antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) can complicate antibody- and vaccine-based strategies for DENV and related viruses, host-targeting antiviral approaches that block entry are attractive but require careful evaluation.

A central challenge is both terminological and mechanistic. Many reported host molecules enhance infection, but not all are bona fide entry receptors. Some molecules increase virion attachment, some recruit endocytic machinery and mediate internalization, some signal through immune-modulatory pathways, and others organize membrane microdomains or support post-entry replication. This review preserves the overall structure of a receptor-focused review while reorganizing the content by mechanism. Throughout the text, the term “receptor” is used conservatively and is distinguished from attachment factors, indirect immune modulators, and post-entry cofactors.

Life cycle of orthoflaviviruses and rationale for receptor classification

Orthoflaviviruses are enveloped, positive-sense RNA viruses with genomes of approximately 11 kb and virions of about 50 nm in diameter (Fig. 1A). The genome encodes three structural proteins (capsid [C], premembrane [prM], and envelope [E]) and seven nonstructural proteins. Mature virions contain 180 copies each of the E and M proteins, arranged as 90 E dimers on a host-derived lipid membrane [9]. The E protein mediates receptor engagement and low-pH-triggered fusion, whereas structural heterogeneity and virion “breathing” can expose different epitopes and potentially alter receptor accessibility (Fig. 1B, C) [10].

The infection cycle can be divided into attachment, endocytic uptake, endosomal fusion, genome release, translation, replication, assembly, maturation, and egress. For DENV, fusion is favored in late endosomes and depends on compartment-specific lipids, whereas endocytic trafficking requirements can vary across viral strains and cell types [11, 12]. After fusion, viral RNA is translated and nonstructural proteins remodel intracellular membranes to form replication organelles [13–17]. These post-entry events are distinct from receptor-mediated uptake and should not be conflated with the initial receptor step (Fig. 2).

For clarity, this review classifies host factors into four categories. Attachment factors increase virion concentration at the cell surface but do not necessarily mediate internalization. Bona fide or candidate internalization receptors promote uptake, often through cytoplasmic motifs or recruitment of endocytic adaptors. Indirect immune-modulatory receptors enhance infection by suppressing antiviral signaling or altering cell-survival pathways. Membrane-associated and post-entry cofactors organize entry complexes, lipid microdomains, replication, or egress. This classification is particularly important for DENV, where DC-SIGN, the mannose receptor, TIM-1, AXL, integrins, HSP70/HSP90, and lipid rafts have all been described as “receptors” in different contexts despite their mechanistically distinct roles.

Attachment factors

Glycosaminoglycans and sialylated glycans

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), especially heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), are among the best-established attachment factors for orthoflaviviruses. HSPGs are broadly expressed and negatively charged, allowing electrostatic interactions with positively charged residues in the viral E glycoprotein. HSPG-dependent attachment has been reported for JEV, DENV, YFV, and other flaviviruses [18–20]. In DENV, early work showed that infectivity depends on E-protein binding to target-cell heparan sulfate, and subsequent studies in human endothelial cells supported a dominant role for heparan sulfate-containing proteoglycans in productive infection [19, 21].

The evidence for HSPGs is strongest for initial attachment rather than for a universal internalization receptor function. Because HSPGs are ubiquitous and can be selected during cell-culture adaptation, their in vivo importance may differ between laboratory-adapted and clinical strains. For DENV, this distinction is important: heparan sulfate can enhance attachment and endothelial infection, but it does not fully explain serotype-specific tropism, immune-cell infection, or severe disease phenotypes. Thus, HSPGs should be classified as low-affinity attachment factors rather than bona fide entry receptors.

Sialylated glycans provide another example of an entry-associated factor that is not clearly receptor-like. α2,3-linked sialic acid facilitates ZIKV internalization because neuraminidase treatment reduces infection in Vero cells and neural progenitor cells [22]. However, competitive inhibition experiments have not demonstrated strong direct binding between ZIKV and sialic acid. The most cautious interpretation is that sialic acid supports receptor-complex assembly or membrane organization. Comparable DENV-specific evidence is less developed; therefore, sialic acid should not be generalized as a DENV entry receptor without additional biochemical and genetic validation.

C-type lectin attachment factors: DC-SIGN and mannose receptor

C-type lectins recognize glycans on the viral E protein and are especially relevant in myeloid-lineage cells. DC-SIGN/CD209 interacts with DENV E-protein glycans and promotes infection of dendritic cells [23, 24]. The macrophage mannose receptor (MR) binds envelope glycoproteins from all four DENV serotypes and enhances DENV infection of macrophages [25]. These observations are clinically important to DENV pathogenesis because dendritic cells, monocytes, and macrophages are early targets after mosquito inoculation and contribute to systemic dissemination and immune activation.

Nevertheless, DC-SIGN and MR should be described primarily as attachment factors or attachment-associated entry enhancers. The evidence for these molecules is strong for glycan-dependent binding and infection enhancement in defined cell types but weaker for direct recruitment of endocytic machinery. This distinction matters therapeutically: blocking lectin-glycan binding may reduce early infection in dendritic cells or macrophages, but such interventions may not provide broad protection across tissues where lectin expression is low or where DENV uses alternative attachment factors. Future studies should compare lectin blockade with genetic rescue in primary human myeloid cells and skin explant models (Fig. 1C) [26].

Bona fide or candidate internalization receptors

TIM-family phosphatidylserine receptors

TIM-family proteins provide one of the clearest mechanistic examples of phosphatidylserine (PS)-dependent internalization. During virion biogenesis, host-derived PS can be incorporated into the viral envelope. TIM proteins bind exposed PS, allowing enveloped viruses to exploit an “apoptotic mimicry” mechanism [27–29]. In DENV, TIM-1 is one of the best-supported internalization receptors because it does more than bind virions: it localizes to clathrin-coated pits, co-internalizes with DENV, and requires ubiquitination of its cytoplasmic domain and STAM-1 interaction for efficient uptake. TIM-1 facilitates the direct binding of DENV particles and mediates their internalization via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, which constitutes the primary entry pathway for DENV. Crucially, mutating the highly conserved amino acids lining the metal-ion-dependent ligand binding site (MILIBS)—specifically N114A or D115A in TIM-1, and N121A in TIM-4—completely abrogates the infection [27].

The evidence for TIM-1 is therefore stronger than that for many reported “receptors”, although it remains model-dependent. TIM-1 expression in keratinocytes and epithelial cells suggests a plausible role at the mosquito inoculation site, whereas its relevance in monocytes, endothelial cells, and neural cells may differ [26, 27]. For JEV, TIM-1 also promotes entry and infection [28], and TIM-family proteins can enhance multiple enveloped viruses through PS recognition [29]. Thus, TIM-1 should be classified as a bona fide PS-dependent internalization receptor in defined cellular settings, while avoiding the claim that it is universally required for all orthoflavivirus infections.

Integrins and cell-type-specific internalization

Integrins are candidate internalization receptors whose functions appear strongly virus-, tissue-, and model-dependent. For DENV-2, β3 integrin expression is upregulated in human dermal microvascular endothelial cells, and soluble αvβ3 integrin or β3-integrin knockdown can inhibit viral entry [30]. This finding provides DENV-specific evidence that integrins may contribute to endothelial entry. However, it does not establish β3 integrin as a universal DENV receptor, because other endothelial studies have found that heparan sulfate-containing proteoglycans can dominate DENV attachment and productive infection [21].

For ZIKV, integrin β4 (ITGB4/CD104) has been identified as a candidate entry factor, and anti-ITGB4 antibodies reduced placental infection and fetal disease in an animal model [31]. Integrin αvβ5 has also been reported to mediate ZIKV internalization in neural stem cells [32]. For WNV, αvβ3 integrin promotes viral entry in certain cell systems [33]. Taken together, integrins should be presented as candidate internalization receptors or entry-associated factors rather than as a shared orthoflavivirus entry platform. For DENV, the key unresolved question is whether integrin involvement is restricted to endothelial or dermal models or contributes to clinically relevant infection in primary human tissues.

Neural, endothelial, and tissue-tropism-associated receptors

Several host molecules have been proposed to contribute to neural or endothelial tropism. NCAM1 was identified by chemical proteomics as a ZIKV attachment and internalization factor and was functionally validated in cell culture [34]. In JEV infection, the laminin receptor and CD4 have been implicated in viral entry into microglia [35]. PLVAP and GKN3 have been associated with JEV infection in the central nervous system and may contribute to viral burden and traversal of the blood-brain barrier [36]. TLR3 has been implicated in WNV neuroinvasion and pathogenesis, but it should not be classified as a canonical entry receptor because its primary role relates to innate immune signaling and disease progression [37].

DENV-specific neural receptor evidence is more limited but important. PHB1/2 has been proposed as a putative DENV-3 entry factor in human neuroblastoma and microglial cell models [38]. This study extends receptor discussion beyond classical myeloid and endothelial targets, but its relevance to natural DENV neuroinvasion remains uncertain because severe neurological DENV disease is less common than systemic vascular and immune manifestations. Therefore, PHB1/2 should be classified as a cell-type-specific candidate entry factor that requires validation in primary neural cells, organoids, and in vivo models.

Lipid-metabolism-associated receptors

Lipid-metabolism-associated receptors provide an emerging link between flavivirus entry and host endocytic trafficking. LRP1 has recently been reported to facilitate DENV infection by interacting with domain III of the viral E glycoprotein; depletion of LRP1 markedly reduces infectious virus production, and purified LRP1 can block DENV infection in vitro [39]. These findings identify LRP1 as a mechanistically interesting DENV receptor candidate because it is an endocytic receptor with broad ligand-binding capacity.

LRP4 is a member of the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family, a group of endocytic receptors involved in lipoprotein uptake and related trafficking pathways. A recent CRISPR-based study identified LRP4 as a critical entry receptor for YFV; broader screening further suggested that multiple LDLR-family members, including LRP1, LRP4, and VLDLR, can support YFV entry [40]. These findings indicate that LDLR-family receptors may constitute a lipid-metabolism-associated entry route for YFV and potentially other orthoflaviviruses, but the breadth and in vivo relevance of this pathway, especially for neurotropic orthoflaviviruses, require further validation.

Indirect immune-modulatory receptors

TAM-family receptors and the AXL controversy

AXL/TAM receptors have been implicated as host factors in DENV and ZIKV infection [41]. TAM-family receptors, including Tyro3, AXL, and MerTK, are mechanistically distinct from TIM proteins. TIM proteins directly bind PS exposed on viral envelopes, whereas TAM receptors usually require bridging ligands, such as Gas6 or Protein S, to link PS-containing virions to the receptor [42]. AXL expression patterns in neural progenitor and skin cells further made an entry-promoting model for ZIKV biologically plausible [43, 44].

Subsequent genetic studies challenged the view that AXL is an indispensable physical entry receptor. AXL ablation did not protect human neural progenitor cells or cerebral organoids from ZIKV infection, and AXL deficiency did not substantially reduce ZIKV replication in interferon-deficient mice [45, 46]. These findings indicate that AXL can enhance infection in some cellular contexts but is not universally required for viral internalization. For DENV, AXL is best described as an infection-enhancing host factor whose role may involve entry-associated binding, intracellular signaling, or innate immune modulation rather than a single receptor function [41, 42].

The most balanced interpretation is that AXL/TAM receptors primarily function as indirect immune-modulatory receptors. TAM activation can dampen type I interferon signaling and create a cellular environment that is more permissive to viral replication [42, 47]. In JEV infection, NS2B-3 can promote AXL degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, disrupting PI3K/Akt signaling and facilitating apoptosis-associated viral release [48]. This example underscores a broader conceptual point: a host factor that enhances infection may act at entry, immune evasion, replication, or egress. Future DENV studies should separate these stages experimentally by combining synchronized entry assays, AXL kinase-dead mutants, interferon-rescue experiments, and time-of-addition inhibition.

Membrane-associated and post-entry cofactors

HSP70/HSP90 and lipid rafts

Heat shock proteins and lipid rafts are often discussed alongside receptors but are more accurately classified as membrane-associated and post-entry cofactors. HSP70 has been reported to support ZIKV entry, replication, and egress [49]. In DENV infection, HSP70 subnetworks are required for efficient viral replication, and HSP70/HSP90 have been reported to participate in viral entry as part of a receptor-associated complex in human cell lines and monocytes/macrophages [50, 51]. These findings support an important role for chaperone-associated membrane complexes but do not establish HSP70 or HSP90 as independent cell-surface receptors.

Lipid rafts are cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich microdomains that can cluster host factors, organize signaling, and promote endocytic uptake. For DENV, the reported HSP70/HSP90 receptor complex and its association with lipid rafts suggest that membrane microdomains can organize entry-associated complexes [51]. For JEV, HSP70 association with lipid rafts is required for infection in Huh7 cells, whereas HSP90 appears to function more prominently during late release than during initial uptake [52, 53]. This distinction is clinically relevant because targeting membrane cofactors may produce broad antiviral effects but may also carry host toxicity, as chaperones and lipid rafts regulate many essential cellular processes.

Translational implications: receptor decoys and host-targeting antivirals

Receptor usage is also closely linked to the antigenic state of the virion. The flaviviral E protein is not static; structural heterogeneity and virion “breathing” can transiently expose otherwise cryptic epitopes, thereby influencing both receptor accessibility and antibody recognition. In DENV, this dynamic surface organization is particularly relevant because antibodies that bind but do not fully neutralize virions may promote Fc receptor-mediated uptake and contribute to antibody-dependent enhancement. Thus, entry-associated host factors should be interpreted not only as determinants of cell tropism but also as components of a broader entry landscape shaped by virion maturation, E-protein epitope exposure, neutralizing-antibody binding, and enhancing-antibody-mediated uptake. Integrating receptor biology with these antigenic and immunological features may therefore improve mechanistic understanding of early infection and inform the rational design of entry-blocking or host-targeting antiviral strategies. Mechanism-based classification has direct translational implications. Attachment factors such as HSPGs and DC-SIGN are attractive targets for blocking early virion concentration, but redundancy may limit the efficacy of single-target strategies. Bona fide or candidate internalization receptors such as TIM-1, integrins, and LRP1 provide more specific entry targets, but their roles may be restricted by cell type, tissue, or virus species. Immune-modulatory receptors such as AXL may be better approached through signaling modulation than through receptor blockade alone. Membrane-associated cofactors such as HSP70/HSP90 and lipid rafts offer broad-spectrum possibilities but require careful toxicity assessment.

For DENV, receptor-based interventions could be valuable because ADE complicates antibody-based strategies. However, receptor decoys and host-targeting antivirals should be framed as future possibilities rather than established therapeutic solutions. High-priority validation steps include testing soluble decoys or blocking antibodies across all four DENV serotypes, comparing primary human skin, dendritic-cell, macrophage, endothelial, and neural models, and determining whether blockade remains effective in the presence of enhancing antibodies. Such studies would connect receptor biology to realistic clinical and vaccine-related contexts.

Conclusions

Orthoflavivirus invasion relies on a highly redundant receptor network that integrates low-affinity attachment factors (e.g., HSPGs), lipid-dependent apoptotic mimicry (e.g., TIM-1), and direct protein–protein interactions (e.g., integrins and NCAM1). Vector-borne orthoflaviviruses remain major sources of emerging infectious disease worldwide. This review summarizes recently reported host factors associated with attachment and entry across the flavivirus family (Table 1). A systematic account of cellular receptors and entry mechanisms will clarify how orthoflaviviruses engage host cells and may provide a mechanistic basis for developing broad-spectrum antiviral strategies.

Future research on flavivirus entry should address two key challenges. First, broadly expressed attachment factors must be distinguished from host molecules that directly promote virion internalization by recruiting endocytic adaptors or organizing receptor-associated entry complexes. Because orthoflaviviruses often exploit redundant and cell-type-specific host factors, the existence of universal core entry receptors remains uncertain and should be tested using complementary approaches, including loss-of-function genetics, rescue experiments, biochemical binding assays, and relevant in vivo models. Second, receptor-based antivirals, such as soluble receptor decoys or inhibitors targeting conserved virus-host interfaces, represent a promising but still exploratory strategy. Such approaches may provide alternatives to antibody-based interventions and could, in principle, reduce concerns related to ADE. However, their antiviral breadth, delivery efficiency, safety, and in vivo efficacy require rigorous validation before they can be considered clinically applicable.
