Takayasu arteritis in an adolescent with untreated Crohn's disease: A case report
Authors: Viven Solomon, Jessica Fennell, Lindsay M Griffin, Bella Zeisler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtraintestinal manifestations in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) most frequently involve the joints, skin, and organs such as the liver and pancreas. Inflammation of the aorta is less commonly described in pediatrics, although it has been described in adults. We report a case of an adolescent female with Crohn's disease who presented with worsening diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss, found to have aortitis on imaging. We review the pathogenesis and clinical features of Takayasu arteritis, as well as some of its similarities to IBD to raise awareness, as early detection of this less‐known extraintestinal manifestation has been associated with better outcomes.Keywords: aortitis, extraintestinal IBD, inflammatory bowel disease

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of disorders caused by a dysregulated immune system in predisposed individuals, resulting in symptoms such as abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea. Extraintestinal manifestations (EIM), most commonly arthralgias, occur in about one‐quarter of pediatric IBD and have been associated with increased disease severity. 1 Other organs associated in both adult and pediatric IBD include the liver, pancreas, and skin. 1 , 2 Aortitis is less common, described in case reports among adults with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. 3 , 4 Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a systemic inflammation primarily affecting large arteries including the aorta, primarily arterial stenoses, and can present as pulse inequalities or loss, or claudication, with most patients exhibiting symptoms when disease is severe. 5 Here, we describe a case of TAK in a pediatric patient with Crohn's disease initially treated with Infliximab who presented with new symptoms while off therapy.

Case report

A 17‐year‐old female with Crohn's disease diagnosed at age 12 years old presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with several weeks of nonbloody diarrhea occurring 8–10 times per 24 h period including nocturnal symptoms, as well as 1 day of multiple episodes of non‐bloody, non‐bilious emesis. She also had decreased appetite with associated seven‐pound weight loss over 2 weeks, and complained of pain in her legs and arms with increased activity. Initial vital signs showed a blood pressure of 98/74, pulse at 104, and normal respiration, temperature, and oxygen saturation. Physical exam revealed a tired‐appearing thin female with abdominal tenderness with guarding, and a delayed cap refill.

The patient's medical therapy included an initial course of Solumedrol at diagnosis, followed by Infliximab infusions and oral methotrexate. She had well‐controlled disease and was asymptomatic on monotherapy with anti‐TNF infusions since April 2017. However, when she developed right leg pain in November 2019, the family decided to stop infusions and opted for naturopathic interventions. During representation in November 2022, she had not been under our care for at least 15 months since last endoscopy in July 2021 showing worsening IBD.

Initial work‐up in the ED was significant for low hemoglobin of 11.7 g/dL, thrombocytosis at 713,000/uL, hypoalbuminemia at 3.3 g/dL, elevated CRP 6.8 mg/L and ESR 97 MM/HR, and positive Clostridium difficile stool antigen. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen with intravenous contrast showed pancolitis in addition to circumferential wall thickening of the infrarenal aorta with luminal narrowing, compatible with aortitis (Figure 1). These findings prompted a rheumatology consult who noted equal radial and dorsalis pedis pulses, left carotid bruit, and no bruits over the right external carotid, renal arteries, or femoral arteries.

Axial contrast‐enhanced CT showing circumferential wall thickening of the infrarenal aorta (arrow) with mild luminal stenosis, compatible with aortitis. CT, computed tomography.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with intravenous contrast showed focal, severe narrowing of the celiac and superior mesenteric artery origins (Figure 2) in addition to the narrowing of the infrarenal aorta seen in CT. MRA of the head and neck showed long segment wall thickening causing severe luminal narrowing of both common carotid arteries, left more than right (Figure 3). Carotid ultrasound supported these findings and showed elevated peak systolic velocities in regions of narrowing (Figure 4). The spectrum of imaging findings were compatible with large‐vessel vasculitis.

Coronal contrast‐enhanced time‐resolved angiography during arterial phase showing long segment narrowing of the left greater than right common carotid arteries (between white arrows), compatible with vasculitis.

During hospitalization, she received a pulse dose of steroids and was restarted on Infliximab infusions. An endoscopy a year later showed visually normal mucosa and no histopathologic abnormalities on biopsy. Her vasculitis improved as evidenced by decreased intensity of bruit on exam, reported improvement in exercise tolerance, and improvement on MRA about 6 months after diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

Aortitis, more specifically TAK, is a large‐vessel vasculitis affecting the aorta and its major branches that presents as circumferential wall thickening with subsequent aneurysm or stenosis/occlusion. 2 , 5 It is a rare but reported EIM in IBD, specifically Crohn's disease. The clinical presentation of TAK depends on disease severity and involved vessels, ranging from muscle fatigue with activity to pulse inequalities, bruits, and blood pressure discrepancies between extremities to claudication.

Martins et al. describe seven adult patients seen in Brazil and Israel initially diagnosed with IBD, five with CD and two with UC, and subsequently diagnosed with TAK. There was a spectrum of disease among their IBD‐phenotypes, from proctitis to pancolitis for UC, to ileocolonic Crohn's to penetrating disease requiring hemicolectomy. TAK also had varying severity and involvement, with two patients requiring surgery and mechanical valve replacement due to ascending aortic aneurysm, to an urgent surgery due to a ruptured descending aorta. 3 Similarly, Sy et al. in 2016 identified 12 patients with IBD and TAK; 6 were diagnosed with IBD before 21 years of age, three of which were diagnosed with TAK at age 17. In comparison to patients with IBD and other types of vasculitis, patients with IBD and TAK were noted to have more constitutional symptoms including headaches, limb claudication, decreased peripheral pulse, blood pressure differences between arms, and bruits. 4

The pathogenesis of TAK is poorly understood; however, like IBD, it has been associated with the HLA loci, specifically HLA‐B and HLA‐B52. 4 , 5 When biopsied, TAK lesions contain macrophages and lymphoid cells, which cause the progressive changes seen in the disease, from acute arterial wall inflammation causing dilation or stenosis to chronic fibrosis and luminal contraction. 5 TAK and IBD both have shown response to anti‐TNF and anti‐IL 6 blockade as well as other immunomodulatory therapies. Like in IBD, early recognition and disease control is essential, as there is minimal response to medications when fibrosis has occurred. 5

Recognition and treatment of vasculitis in patients with IBD might be particularly important as this population already demonstrates an increased cardiovascular risk, due to systemic inflammation, hypercoagulability, and endothelial dysfunction. 6 , 7 Physicians should ask about symptoms of claudication as part of screening for TAK as recognition as a possible EIM of IBD is essential to avoid delays in initiation, step up, or tailoring of medications to treat both IBD and vasculitis appropriately and promptly to minimize complications and long‐term damage.
