Emerging from the shadows of congenital TTP
Authors: Marie Scully, Catrin Cox
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Joly et al,1 from the French thrombotic microangiopathy registry, report on their congenital thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (cTTP) experience. They present data spanning over 25 years, including the demographics, treatment, and experience of complications in 88 cTTP cases. Recombinant ADAMTS13 (rADAMTS13) (Adzynma) received US Food and Drug Administration approval in 2023 and European Medicines Agency approval in 2024. This study includes the details of 39 patients with cTTP, who switched to the recombinant protein.

The cTTP cohort were divided into 3 groups, which was deemed necessary for prognostic and therapeutic impact. Firstly, there was pediatric-onset cTTP, more likely associated with consanguineous parentage, greater neurological events, and triggers including infection and vaccination, resulting in acute TTP presentations. The second group were pregnancy-onset cTTP cases, susceptible to neurological and abdominal symptoms, but surprisingly, within the French cohort, their cases did not present with TTP episodes outside of pregnancy. Finally, adult-onset TTP were liable to present with vascular complications, affecting the heart and kidney, reflecting delayed diagnosis.

Until recently, the sole treatment entailed plasma-based therapy. However, this is cumbersome, time consuming, and associated with significant rates of side effects. Indeed only 50% of the French cohort received regular plasma infusion (PI), with adverse events documented in 35% of these cases. Furthermore, there remained evidence of end organ damage, because PI does not achieve a significant and sustained increase in ADAMTS13 activity.2 Introduction of rADAMTS13 in the 39 cases of the French cohort was associated with a number of benefits including ease of administration, lower volumes infused, no reactions to the therapy and no antibodies directed to ADAMTS13. Minimal adverse events were reported in this group, some of which could be attributed to isolated TTP symptoms. Peak ADAMTS13 activity levels in comparison to that seen with PI, were significantly higher. Indeed, this was shown within the pivotal phase 3 study of rADAMTS13 compared to standard of care, demonstrated a fivefold higher ADAMTS13 activity level, maintained for a longer period compared to PI.3

ADAMTS13 replacement in cTTP requires a long-term prophylaxis regime that the authors have shown and improves relapse-free survival. Acute, subacute, and isolated symptoms were highest in the pediatric onset group, while accepting that retrospective reporting of events may not capture all episodes. However, they were significantly reduced in all patient groups receiving rADAMTS13, affirming a shorter follow-up time in these cases. The risk of acute TTP relapse was greatest in patients who were not on regular prophylaxis, and both PI and rADAMTS13 prophylaxis demonstrated a comparable improvement in reducing these events. The Hereditary TTP Registry has also shown a reduction in acute TTP episodes on a regular prophylactic PI regime compared to those on no prophylaxis, with a higher acute event rate in children.4 rADAMTS13 prophylaxis not only reduces acute TTP episodes, but has also been recognized to reduce subacute and isolated symptoms.5 Furthermore, the international Hereditary TTP Registry described the impact on stroke risk (50% cTTP cases in those aged over 40 years) if a prophylactic program is not adopted.6

Pregnant individuals comprised a specific group that required high volumes of plasma, including plasma exchange in some cases to maintain remission. Despite positive outcomes with plasma-based therapy, use of rADAMTS13 in pregnancy reduced the impact of adverse events. The use of rADAMTS13 as a rescue therapy has been described in a number of case reports, and is also now used throughout pregnancy, with superior fetal and maternal outcomes.7

Despite the role of PI in preventing acute TTP in the longer term, there remains a noteworthy impact on end organ manifestations, specifically cardiac conditions and stroke in all 3 groups. The effect on quality of life,8 mental health, and neurological effects of the condition and treatment cannot be overstated. Increased white matter changes identified on brain magnetic resonance imaging scans, particularly in the frontal and cingulate cortex9 (in immune TTP), correspond to, for example, memory issues, poor concentration, anxiety, and depression. This is compounded by post-traumatic stress disorder,10 the impact of a chronic condition requiring lifelong therapy.

A number of variants were identified on ADAMTS13 genetic analysis with a spread throughout the ADAMTS13 gene. There were 5 variants common to all groups, with p.Arg1060Trp particularly prominent in the pregnancy-onset cases. Particularly, the pregnancy-onset group also presented with acute TTP but identified only 1 heterozygote ADAMTS13 variant. Under continued physiological stress, development of acute TTP occurred with no TTP episodes outside of pregnancy. The full impact of heterozygote-only ADAMTS13 variants, in family members of cTTP cases, requires further investigation.

Since the publication of the pivotal study of rADAMTS13 in cTTP, presentation of real-world data11 of this new therapy expands our understanding of the disease and therapy but will also generate new questions for the optimization of treatment. What is the ideal frequency of dosing for recombinant ADAMTS13? Biweekly therapy will likely prevent acute TTP, although breakthrough subacute and isolated manifestations may not be completely avoided and the impact that different regimens have on long-term end organ function is unclear. What ADAMTS13 activity do we want to achieve?

But for now, let us enjoy the safety and efficacy of recombinant replacement therapy in cTTP.
