Prognostic Value of EGFR Mutation Subtypes After Linac-Based Stereotactic Radiosurgery or Fractionated Stereotactic Radiotherapy for Brain Metastasis From EGFR-Mutated Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Ryosuke Matsuda, Shigeto Hontsu, Tetsuro Tamamoto, Nobuyoshi Inooka, Akihiro Doi, Kaori Yamaki, Sachiko Miura, Ryosuke Maeoka, Tsutomu Nakazawa, Tomoko Ochi, Toshiteru Miyasaka, Yasuhiro Takeshima, Shuichi Yamada, Fumihiko Nishimura, Young-Soo Park, Fumiaki Isohashi, Ichiro Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the differences in common types of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with brain metastasis (BM) treated using linear accelerator-based stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (fSRT).MethodsBetween January 2011–December 2023, among 294 consecutive patients with BM from NSCLC, 84 patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC were enrolled in this study.ResultsThe median follow-up time after SRS/fSRT was 20.1 months (range: 0.6–109.7 months), while the median overall survival (mOS) after SRS/fSRT was 25.1 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 18.4–33.5 months). The mOS after initial treatment for NSCLC was 56.2 months (95% CI: 41.7–77.0 months). The mOS after SRS/fSRT in 34 patients with exon 19 deletions and 45 patients with exon 21 mutations with L858R was 20.4 months (95% CI: 13.6–55.9 months) and 28.5 months (95% CI: 16.2–33.5). The two groups showed no difference in the mOS. In univariate analyses using the Cox proportional hazards model, no prognostic factors associated with prolonged survival were identified except for good pretreatment Karnofsky Performance Status score and no prior tyrosine kinase inhibitor use before SRS/fSRT in EGFR-mutated NSCLC. There was no difference in both distant failure and local control in the two groups.ConclusionThe difference in survival between EGFR subtypes (exon 21 L858R mutations vs. exon 19 deletion) was not observed after SRS/fSRT in NSCLC.Keywords: Brain metastasis, Stereotactic radiosurgery, Non-small cell lung cancer, EGFR mutation

INTRODUCTION

Brain metastasis (BM) is the most common neoplasm among brain tumors. Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (fSRT) for BM are widely used [1,2,3]. Advances in chemotherapy and radiation therapy have increased BMs treatment options. The existing treatment modalities for BM include whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), surgical resection, SRS/fSRT, and systemic therapies such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Recent data on the use of SRS/fSRT for BMs, especially smaller BMs, show that it is most frequently used [3].

In cases with BM, the lung is the most common primary tumor site. Lung cancer is divided into small cell lung cancer and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which is further subdivided on the basis of mutations in driver genes. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations are the most frequent mutations in NSCLC, and EGFR-mutated lung cancer has a better prognosis than EGFR-wild type lung cancer in terms of both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [4]. Among genetic mutations in lung adenocarcinoma, EGFR mutations and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusions are known to occur more frequently in Asian countries than in the United States [5]. In lung adenocarcinomas, EGFR mutations are found in 53% of the cases in Japan, compared to only 11.3% in USA. While tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) for EGFR gene mutations are also effective for BM [6,7,8,9], in cases of BM from EGFR-mutated NSCLC, the treatment approach differs between patients who have already received TKI therapy and those who have not received any TKI therapy at all. The selection of SRS/fSRT for BM from lung cancer with EGFR gene mutations should take into consideration the use of TKIs and the status of BM.

The most common type of EGFR mutation in NSCLC is in-frame deletion of exon19 (approximately 45% of EGFR mutations), followed by L858R point mutation of exon 21 (approximately 40% of EGFR mutations) [10].

In previous report, patients with exon 19 deletions (exon 19 del) exhibit longer PFS and higher overall response rate compared with exon 21 with L858R point mutations (exon 21 L858R). However, no statistically significant difference in OS was observed between the two groups of patients [11]. Whether genetic mutations between exon 19 del and exon 21 L858R affect outcomes after SRS/fSRT remains a matter of debate. The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether the therapeutic efficacy of SRS/fSRT for metastatic brain tumors in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC differs between genetic mutations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient characteristics

This retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of Nara Medical University Hospital (No. 3333). Clinical data were retrospectively collected to evaluate the prognostic value of SRS and fSRT for BMs. Between January 2011 and December 2023, 315 consecutive patients with brain metastases from lung cancer were treated with SRS or fSRT at our institution. Among them, 21 patients with small cell lung cancer were excluded. Of the remaining 294 patients with NSCLC, 84 patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC were enrolled in this study. Radiotherapy protocol

SRS/fSRT planning was based on CT scans in supine position. To reduce intra-fractional error, all patients were immobilized using a thermoplastic mask (BRAINLAB AG). CT was taken on Aquilion LB (Toshiba Medical Systems) with a 1.0-mm slice thickness. The gross tumor volume (GTV) for each lesion was delineated on MRI scans with a slice thickness of 1 mm. The planning target volume (PTV) was created by adding 1–2 mm margin in all dimensions to GTV. Treatment was provided within 1 week after the planning CT scan. Treatment planning was performed using BrainSCAN or iPlan RT (BRAINLAB AG). The radiation dose was prescribed to isocenter with 2 mm multileaf collimator (MLC) margin, and PTV was covered with approximately 90% isodose line. Dose calculations were performed using a pencil-beam algorithm. SRS and fSRT were performed using linac with the micro-MLC Novalis (BRAINLAB AG) with a collimator width of 3 mm or TrueBeam STx (Varian Medical Systems) with a collimator width of 2.5 mm. Patients were treated with Novalis until November 2017, and TrueBeam STx from November 2017. Every patient was treated with X-rays with a beam energy of 6 MV. Patient positioning and verification were performed using BrainLab ExacTrac (BRAINLAB AG). This device consists of two infrared cameras and dual diagnostic kV X-ray tubes, which can be moved automatically into the treatment position to minimize setup errors [12,13]. Additionally, cone-beam CT was used to reduce setup errors in TrueBeam STx. Patients with neurological symptoms, BMs larger than 20 mm in size, or BMs in the eloquent area, including the basal ganglia and primary motor cortex, underwent fSRT at a dose of 35 Gy in five fractions [14]. Patients with brainstem metastasis were treated with doses of 24–40 Gy in 7–13 fractions [2]. Asymptomatic patients with BMs smaller than 20 mm were treated with SRS at a dose of 21–22.5 Gy in a single fraction, and the decision was based on tumor size, location, surrounding edema, and other factors [15].

All patients were treated using Novalis or TrueBeam STx via non-coplanar multi-beams, non-coplanar multi-arcs, or both. The treatment methods in SRS or fSRT included conformal beams, dynamic conformal arcs, intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), or hybrid arcs. Hybrid arc is a treatment technique that blends aperture-enhanced optimized arcs with discrete IMRT elements, allowing arc selection with a set of static IMRT beams [16].

Clinical and radiological follow-up

Follow-up evaluation with contrast-enhanced MRI was performed at 3-month intervals after the end of SRS/fSRT, if possible. Tumor volumes were calculated using BrainSCAN or iPlan RT before and after SRS/fSRT. Once recurrence or radiation necrosis is suspected, MRI was performed at intervals of 1–2 months. The decision to perform additional treatment for disease progression was based on evidence of clinical deterioration and associated imaging progression judged by the tumor review board for BM. Local failure was defined as a pathologically proven recurrence within the SRS/fSRT treatment field. Imaging progression that necessitated additional treatment (surgery for symptomatic radiation necrosis, SRS/fSRT, or medication, including steroid and bevacizumab) was counted as local failure without pathological confirmation. Distant failure was defined as intracranial failure at a site not previously treated with SRS/fSRT.

Statistical analysis

The mOS was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log-rank test was used for univariate analyses. Prognostic factors, including age (≥70 years vs. <70 years), sex, extracranial metastasis, control of the primary cancer, mutation status in NSCLC, the use of TKI, and pretreatment Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score (≥80% vs. <80%) were analyzed. Cox proportional hazards analysis at the univariate and multivariate levels was used to identify factors associated with survival. Competing risk analyses were performed to evaluate local and intracranial distant failure after SRS/fSRT, treating death without local or intracranial distant failure as a competing event. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated and compared using Gray’s test. The effects of covariates on local and intracranial distant failure were assessed using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models, and results were expressed as subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed with the EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [17], and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The characteristics of the 84 patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC are summarized in Table 1. The median follow-up duration was 20.1 months (range: 0.6–109.7 months), and the mOS from SRS/fSRT was 25.1 months (95% CI: 18.4–33.5 months). The 6-month, 1-year, and 2-year survival rates were 86.1%, 74.4%, and 50.3%, respectively (Fig. 1A). Also, the mOS from initial treatment for EGFR-mutated NSCLC was 56.2 months (95% CI: 41.7–77.0 months) (Fig. 1B). In the entire patient cohort, univariate analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model was performed in sex, age, KPS, control of primary cancer, extracranial metastasis, number of metastasis, and prior TKI use before SRS/fSRT. The prognostic factors associated with prolonged survival time were a good pretreatment KPS score and prior TKI use before SRS/fSRT. The prognostic factors in multivariate analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model were a good pretreatment KPS score and no prior TKI use (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Among the primary sites of BM, lung cancer is the most frequent and important. BM occurs in 20%–40% of NSCLC patients, with a particularly high rate of BM in EGFR/ALK-mutated NSCLC (up to 50%) [18,19]. BMs from EGFR/ALK-mutated NSCLC are smaller but more numerous than those from NSCLC without driver mutations. EGFR-mutated NSCLC is also more likely than other subtypes to cause meningeal carcinomatosis, which contributes to the higher frequency of central nervous system (CNS) death compared to other subtypes [20]. Patients in advanced stages of EGFR/ALK-mutated lung cancer show dramatically enhanced clinical responses and prolonged mOS following administration of TKIs [7,9,21,22]. TKIs have been reported to have promising blood–brain barrier permeability, and can improve the prognosis of driver mutation NSCLC with BM [8,23,24]. In particular, among TKIs, osimertinib has been shown to have superior efficacy against BM [25]. On the other hand, SRS/fSRT is a standard treatment for cases with small and few BMs. However, no clear evidence is available to indicate which therapy for BM should be administered first in the patients with EGFR TKI-naïve NSCLC [26,27]. In recent studies, EGFR mutations independently predicted better mOS in patients treated with SRS and fSRT [28,29,30]. Magnuson et al. [31] reported that upfront SRS was superior to upfront TKI and upfront WBRT in terms of OS in EGFR TKI-naïve patients with NSCLC with BM. They concluded that SRS followed by EGFR TKI resulted in the longest OS and allowed patients to avoid the potential neurocognitive sequelae of WBRT. Also, Chiou et al. [22] compared the outcomes between combined TKI therapy and SRS versus TKI therapy alone in patients showing EGFR-mutated NSCLC with BMs. Although the OS rate did not differ between TKI therapy with and without SRS, the addition of SRS to TKI therapy resulted in improvement of intracranial tumor control. They recommended that the potential benefits of combined therapy may include prevention of neurological deficits and seizures.

The most common type of EGFR mutation in NSCLC is exon 19 del (approximately 45% of EGFR mutations), followed by exon 21 L858R (approximately 40% of EGFR mutations) [10]. It has been reported that patients with the exon 21 L858R mutation are more likely to develop BM and leptomeningeal metastasis [32,33,34]. The mOS from BMs diagnosis in the patients with L858R was longer in the group with upfront TKI+SRS/fSRT than in the group with upfront TKI or upfront TKI+WBRT [35]. Patients with exon 19 del exhibit longer PFS and higher overall response rate compared with exon 21 L858R. However, no statistically significant difference in the mOS after the initial treatment for EGFR-mutated NSCLC was observed between the two groups of patients. This difference is assumed to be due to the different response of TKI used as the first line treatment between exon 19 del and exon 21 L858R [11]. Roh et al. [36] reported EGFR-mutated NSCLC with exon 19 del showed better distant brain control compared to those with exon 21 L858R mutations. He et al. [35] reported that the prognosis independently correlated with the EGFR mutation type in the multivariate analysis. EGFR-mutated NSCLC with exon 19 del showed a significant increase in the OS. On the other hand, in the other report, there was no difference in CNS failure between exon 19 and exon 21 in patients who used afatinib in the first line for EGFR-mutated NSCLC. However, CNS failure was significantly more common in uncommon mutations than in exon 19 del and exon 21 L858R [9]. Lee et al. [37] compared exon 19 and exon 21 for tumor control rates after Gamma Knife SRS and reported no difference between them. Similarly, Chiou et al. [22] similarly reported no difference in tumor control and survival after Gamma Knife SRS between exon 19 and exon 21. In this study, among patients with EGFR mutations, mOS after SRS/fSRT, intracranial distant failure and local failure did not differ significantly between patients with mutations in exon 19 del and exon 21 L858R. Based on our data and previous data, it remains unclear whether EGFR mutation subtypes affect overall survival or intracranial distant failure after SRS/fSRT for BM from EGFR-mutated NSCLC.

This study had several limitations since this was a retrospective analysis. The study had a small sample size of the patients treated with SRS/fSRT for BMs from EGFR-mutated NSCLC. Although similar clinical studies have been conducted recently, they involved different treatment strategies, such as those for treatment modalities, and showed differences in the first-line treatment for BM.

In this study, we present outcomes of patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC who were treated with SRS/fSRT for BM. The mOS after SRS/fSRT and after initial treatment for EGFR-mutated NSCLC was 25.1 and 56.2 months, respectively. For patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, prognostic factor associated with prolonged survival time included a favorable pretreatment KPS score and no prior TKI use. Patients with EGFR mutations in exon 19 del and exon 21 L858R showed no significant difference in the mOS after SRS/fSRT, intracranial distant failure, and local failure. The findings of our study highlight the efficacy of linac-based SRS and fSRT in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC.
