RGReco: a unified framework for automated R-group recognition in chemical publications
Authors: Yuanjie Xiang, Yanghong Luo, Renshuang Liu, Jiajun Tao, Wei Hu, Mei Ouyang, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The abundant R-group information available in chemical publications plays a crucial role in data-driven artificial intelligence (AI) research in the field of medicinal chemistry. In real-world publications, R-groups are expressed in various textual and graphical forms, thereby rendering their manual integration labor-intensive and inefficient. Although automated tools exist for R-group recognition, they remain underdeveloped, creating a clear requirement for precise and comprehensive automated parsing tools. This paper presents RGReco, a novel framework that combines deep learning and chemical rules to parse and integrate R-group information from images and text through a multistage pipeline. In addition, a new process for recognizing substituent structures and parsing-related text is proposed. To evaluate the performance of RGReco, a dataset containing common types of R-group images was constructed from real-world scientific literature. Using this dataset, RGReco achieved a precision of 86.4%, a recall of 79.7%, and an F1 score of 82.9%. These results demonstrate that RGReco effectively handles the diversity of R-group images in real-world scenarios, offering researchers a new technological tool for accelerating the extraction of chemical information.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-026-01179-5.

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated transformative potential in the field of chemistry, particularly medicinal chemistry [1–3]. However, the performance of AI systems is constrained by the quality and scale of the training data. Traditional data collection methods rely on manual extraction, rendering it difficult to capture the vast amount of research generated in recent years. Consequently, a significant portion of valuable chemical knowledge remains widely distributed in unstructured forms across the scientific literature and patent documents. Existing automated extraction solutions primarily focus on standard molecular structures and chemical reactions, leading to an output quality that still falls short compared to manually curated databases. This gap hinders the ability of AI to meet the demand for high-quality data, creating a major bottleneck for the deep application of AI in chemistry [4–6].

In drug design and development, the choice of R-group critically influences key pharmaceutical properties, including biological activity, selectivity, metabolic stability, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity [7, 8]. In target-based virtual screening, R-groups can be systematically adjusted to fine-tune the interaction patterns between candidate molecules and target proteins, greatly affecting the binding affinity and selectivity. In toxicity predictions, R-groups can influence the formation of toxic metabolites and off-target effects, rendering the rational design of R-group structures crucial for mitigating potential safety risks. In the design of synthetic routes, the size, polarity, and electronic effects of the R-groups directly affect the feasibility and selectivity of the final pathway. Thus, systematic characterization and an in-depth understanding of R-groups have become critical tasks that span multiple stages of modern drug development.

Markush structures [9] generally exhibit four types of variability: substituent, position, frequency, and homology. The R-group structure specifically refers to the subset of Markush structures characterized by substituent variability. In this representation, the R-group can be replaced with substituents from a designated set, allowing a single R-group to represent a large number of compounds. Consequently, substituents information can be conveyed through heterogeneous representations, including tables, text list, and substituent structure. Additionally, R-group images face multiple challenges such as combinatorial complexity, diverse drawing styles, and inconsistent text annotation styles. In recent years, R-group recognition technologies have made significant advances across multiple subtasks, including molecular structure segmentation from document images [13–15], identification of molecular structure images containing R-group symbols [16–25], and conversion of textual descriptions (e.g., International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) names [26]) into machine-readable molecular representations [27–30]. These advancements have laid a critical foundation for the development of R-group image recognition methods. While specialized tools like MarkushGrapher [40] successfully focus on Markush structure recognition in patents and effectively extract substituent information from natural language sentences, there is currently no tool capable of processing the substituent information expressed in the table formats and substituent structures mentioned above. By integrating existing tools, it should be possible to develop a comprehensive framework capable of efficiently and accurately parsing scaffold structure and their substituent information from images. This would be expected to contribute to the automation of chemical information extraction and the creation of structured databases, thereby providing solid data support for AI-driven chemical research.

To address the challenge of parsing complex and diverse R-group images in authentic publications, the aim of this study is to develop a novel framework (RGReco) for accurately recognizing R-group images in real-world scenarios. The task diagram for this model is illustrated in Fig. 2. To ensure process stability, a systematic comparison is performed and multiple open-source tools are integrated to enhance the recognition accuracy. Furthermore, to address the inadequacies in the existing molecular structure and document recognition steps, specialized recognition modules are designed. These modules should improve the accuracy of recognizing R-group scaffold structures and enable the recognition of both substituent structures and substituent text. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of this framework for identifying R-groups from journal images, a high-quality R-group evaluation dataset is constructed. This dataset supports subsequent research and contributes to advancements in the development and application of AI technologies in this field. Related work

The relevant work for this study falls into two primary categories, namely molecular structure segmentation and recognition, and document content recognition. Molecular structure segmentation and recognition involves segmenting structures from images and serializing them in machine-readable formats. Document content recognition encompasses general document recognition, R-group information recognition, and techniques for converting substituent text within the R-group information into machine-readable formats.

Molecular structure segmentation and recognition

Molecular structure segmentation

Molecular structure segmentation is a crucial pre-processing step in molecular structure recognition. Currently, open-source methods such as YoDe segmentation [13] and DECIMER-Segmentation [14] are available for this purpose. YoDe-segmentation initially employs an object detection model for the coarse localization of molecular structures, followed by a segmentation model to eliminate noise within the detection boxes. This method is more suitable for an entire document page, but its excellent annotation accuracy results in high annotation costs, and segmentation precision is more dependent on the training data. DECIMER-Segmentation directly uses segmentation models to cover the main regions of molecular structures, and employs morphological postprocessing operations to extract complete molecular masks. This method achieves a higher segmentation accuracy, but its morphological parameters are difficult to adapt to images of varying scales.

Molecular structure recognition

In recent years, deep learning-based optical chemical structure recognition (OCSR) methods have followed two main research directions. The first involves end-to-end models that typically extract image features through an encoder and convert them into simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) sequences through a decoder. The advantages of this approach include its end-to-end efficiency and outstanding generalization performance. However, its drawbacks include a strong dependence on large-scale datasets and heavy reliance on synthetic data, resulting in limited effectiveness in real-world natural scenarios. Relevant methods include Image2SMILES [17], SwinOCSR [19], DECIMER [21], MPOCSR [23], and MolParser [25]. The second direction involves graph-based methods that detect molecular components and reconstruct molecular graphs using manually designed image processing rules. These methods are advantages due to their interpretability and recognition accuracy; however, they are complex, rendering large-scale manual annotation difficult. Moreover, their heavy reliance on synthetic data further limits their generalizability. Related approaches include MolScribe [20], MolGrapher [22], and MolNexTR [24].

Machine-readable R-group molecular representation

The results of molecular structure recognition must be converted into machine-readable strings for effective processing. Although the SMILES format is one of the most widely used molecular representations, it does not directly support the description of substituent identifiers in R-group molecules. To address this issue, studies such as Image2SMILES and MolParser have proposed customized and optimized SMILES variants to represent more complex molecular structures. However, their compatibility with mainstream tools remains uncertain. Consequently, Chemaxon-extended SMILES (CXSMILES) [32] is a more commonly used variable molecular representation method. It introduces additional fields based on standard SMILES strings to accurately describe information, such as attachment points between molecular fragments, atomic coordinates, bond directions, and R-group positions. Additionally, it offers greater information density and is well supported by the RDKit molecular processing tool [33].

Document content recognition

General document recognition

General document recognition is the preliminary step in document content recognition. In recent years, general document recognition tools have shown a clear trend toward unification, meaning that a single tool can accomplish multiple tasks, such as layout analysis, text detection and recognition, and table detection and recognition. Current mainstream approaches fall into two categories. The first relies on multiple specialized deep learning models working in tandem, wherein the corresponding methods employ various specialized deep-learning models to collaborate on document recognition tasks, with each model being optimized for a specific task. As a result, higher recognition accuracy is often achieved for individual tasks. However, this approach results in higher system complexity and slower recognition speeds, rendering it difficult to meet real-time processing demands. Representative projects in this category include Surya [34] and PaddleOCR 3.0 [35]. The second category employs unified approaches, which use a single model to handle multiple tasks. This significantly simplifies the system architecture, enhances the processing speed, and facilitates practical deployment. Although single-model methods offer advantages in terms of speed and complexity, they suffer from insufficient generalization capabilities for complex images, along with challenges related to their alignment with specific requirements. GOT OCR 2.0 [36] is representative of this category.

R-group information recognition

The recognition of R-group information aims to identify relevant R-group content in the document text, primarily by focusing on recognizing the identifiers of substituents and their specific references. Existing studies have mainly targeted substituent information in textual labels and natural language expressions, with approaches being broadly categorized into rule-based and deep learning-based methods. Rule-based methods primarily handle the substituent information in image text labels. ChemSchematicResolver [37] pioneered this approach by systematically recognizing four common representations of substituents in text labels, thereby establishing a paradigm for subsequent research. OpenChemIE [38] further extended this approach to identify substituent representations in tables, along with homologous segments across multiple molecular structures. Additionally, deep-learning-based methods focus on recognizing the substituent information in natural language sentences. For instance, Wang et al. [39] first applied deep learning models to recognize substituent information in natural language. More recently, MarkushGrapher [40] was proposed specifically for extracting Markush structures from patents, where substituent information is described in natural language sentences. It adopts a multimodal deep learning architecture that takes both molecular structure images and their corresponding textual descriptions as input, and employs an end-to-end model to directly generate the corresponding Markush structures. This approach simplifies the recognition process and handles a wide range of complex Markush representations.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain regarding research into R-group information recognition. Firstly, existing studies are limited to recognizing R-group information in textual descriptions, and no research has focused on recognizing R-groups defined by chemical structure diagrams. In addition, R-group information has not been sufficiently explored in more information-dense table formats. Given the diverse and complex representations of R-groups in real-world chemical publications, current rule-based and deep-learning approaches still face limitations. Effectively addressing the challenges associated with diversity and complexity therefore remains an urgent issue for R-group document recognition.

Substituent text recognition

Substituent text refers to the symbolic representation of substituents in the R-group information, and its recognition currently presents a significant challenge. The main difficulty lies in the fact that, in real-world chemical publications, substituent text appears in various forms, such as chemical abbreviations, molecular formulae, or IUPAC names, and no unified method for recognition currently exists. In the context of chemical abbreviations, MolGrapher and MolScribe rely on previously defined abbreviation lists and map them to generate machine-readable SMILES strings. MolScribe incorporates a depth-first search algorithm to convert molecular formulae into SMILES sequences, although this method is limited to relatively simple linear molecular formulae. In terms of IUPAC name recognition, existing methods can be broadly classified into rule-based and deep-learning-based approaches. Specifically, rule-based methods offer high recognition accuracy, but rely on manual prior knowledge, rendering it difficult to cover all IUPAC names. Notable examples include OPSIN (Open Parser for Systematic IUPAC Nomenclature) [27] and CIR (Chemical Identifier Resolver) [28]. Deep learning-based methods such as STOUT (SMILES-to-IUPAC-name translator) [29] and STOUT V2 [30] employ encoder–decoder architectures to facilitate mutual conversion between SMILES and IUPAC names. However, end-to-end deep-learning methods may suffer from hallucinations, and current deep-learning approaches are not fully effective in recognizing IUPAC names with substituents.

Evaluation dataset

RGReco represents the first recognition framework designed specifically for diverse R-group images. However, no publicly available dataset currently exists for evaluating the R-group image recognition performance. Therefore, in the current study, an evaluation dataset was constructed that included multiple subsets to assess the molecular segmentation, attachment point detection, molecular recognition, and substituent text parsing. The dataset contained 82 images, all cropped from the Journal of Medicinal Chemistry, and featured 640 molecules. To accurately reflect the real-world challenges outlined previously, the images encompass diverse R-group representations, such as tables, text lists, and various substituent structures. The dataset also features specific attachment point notations, including wavy lines and asterisks, alongside diverse substituent text expressions like abbreviations, molecular formulae, IUPAC names, and combinations thereof, thereby ensuring a realistic representation. All annotations were performed manually, specifically covering molecular masks, rotated rectangles for attachment points, molecular structure recognition results, and substituent information. Module I: Molecular structure and document separation

In R-group images from real-world publications, the content requiring recognition includes molecular structures, tables, and residual text. Tables may contain molecular structures, whereas molecular structures may contain textual information, rendering it challenging for a single model to accurately identify nested tables, molecular structures, and text amid such interference. To enhance recognition accuracy across different content types, RGReco initially employs the molecular segmentation model DECIMER-Segmentation to perform the segmentation of molecular structures within images. It replaces pixels in the corresponding molecular structure regions with the identifier "STRUCT.” Subsequently, through molecular segmentation, the pixel information for molecular structures is separated from the remainder of the original image, enabling distinct subsequent processing workflows and effectively reducing recognition errors.

Module II: Molecular structure recognition

Challenges in molecular structure recognition

Molecular structure recognition in the R-group images faces two major challenges. The first is the difficulty in identifying substituent structures. Specifically, in chemical publications, substituent structures often include attachment points, such as wavy lines, broken lines, and asterisks, to indicate where the substituent connects to the scaffold structure. However, existing molecular structure recognition tools struggle to identify these attachment points. Furthermore, the identification of superscript and subscript identifiers for substituents in the scaffold structure poses another challenge. Identifiers such as R1 and R' are depicted in various styles, resulting in high error rates for current tools when recognizing these superscript and subscript structures.

Attachment point and substituent identifier detection model

To address the above challenges, this study fine-tunes a rotated bounding-box detection model specifically for detecting attachment points and substituent identifiers in molecular structures, adapting to the unique shapes of the substituent bonds. Given that each image in this task contains relatively few targets, the YOLOv11-OBB model [41], which maintains a high detection accuracy while offering a lightweight structure and fast inference speed, is selected for fine-tuning. The fine-tuning process is illustrated in Fig. 5. The training data are sourced from PubChem [42] and converted into molecular structure images using RDKit. During the image creation process, attachment points such as wavy lines, zigzag lines, and asterisks are simulated in the most common forms found in publications. In addition, 22 common superscript and subscript substituent identifiers are included. To enhance the generalizability of the model, a custom method is employed to randomly introduce substituent identifiers, attachment points, stereochemistry, and common functional group abbreviations into the molecules. This is accompanied by various image sizes, font types, font sizes, and bond thicknesses, among other rendering parameters.Fig. 5Fine-tuning method for the YOLOv11-OBB model. The original data from the PubChem database were processed using RDKit and data augmentation to generate training data for fine-tuning the YOLOv11-OBB model

Post-processing of attachment point and substituent identifier detection

To convert molecular structures containing attachment points into formats compatible with existing recognition tools, this study proposes a pixel replacement-based post-processing method for molecular structure images. This method initially masks the regions of attachment points detected within the image and then draws specific substituent identifiers at those locations. Comparative experiments revealed that the "X" character structure is simple and exhibits higher stability and compatibility across multiple recognition tools. Therefore, the "X" character was adopted as the standard replacement character.

Molecular structure recognition method

The input for molecular recognition is a noise-free molecular structure image processed via molecular segmentation. The specific method for molecular structure recognition, as shown in Fig. 6, includes a number of steps. Firstly, a detection model is used to detect attachment points and substituent identifiers in the image, allowing classification of the molecular structure into scaffold and substituent structures. For substituent structures containing attachment points, a post-processing method replaces these points with "X" characters, and MolScribe recognizes the processed images. For the scaffold structures, the MolScribe recognition results are further refined based on the detection of substituent identifiers. This repair is achieved by identifying cases where the bounding box of a substituent identifier predicted by the detection model overlaps with the coordinates of an atom in the MolScribe output. In such instances, the atom label is corrected to match the detected identifier. The final output includes the corresponding CXSMILES string and a classification indicator specifying whether the molecule possesses a scaffold structure.Fig. 6Molecular structure recognition workflow in RGReco. Module III: Document parsing

Substituent text recognition algorithm

Substituent texts in real-world publications often appear in the form of abbreviations, molecular formulae, IUPAC names, or combinations thereof. Existing methods are unable to handle the recognition of these texts independently. Thus, in the current study, an abbreviation dictionary from MolGrapher is introduced, and the molecular formula recognition algorithm in MolScribe is improved. Finally, OPSIN, which recognizes IUPAC names, is integrated. A comprehensive algorithm is then designed to recognize substituent texts, covering most types of substituent texts found in real-world publications. The specific steps of the algorithm are listed in Algorithm 1, which outlines the entire process from raw text input to the final output. The raw substituent text is input and subjected to six key steps, namely abbreviation dictionary lookup, text normalization, text tokenization, IUPAC name conversion, SMILES recognition, and fragment integration. The final output is a SMILES string corresponding to the original substituent text.

The overall time complexity of the algorithm is O(n + k), where n is the length of the input substituent text, and k is the number of tokens after tokenization. The time complexity arises from two main components, namely text processing and token manipulation. Text normalization and tokenization each have a linear complexity of O(n), whereas the IUPAC conversion, SMILES recognition, and fragment integration steps each scale linearly with the number of tokens, contributing to O(k). Consequently, complexity is dominated by these two linear steps. The space complexity is O(k), as the primary space usage arises from storing tokens during the tokenization process and SMILES fragments during recognition and integration. Since the size of the dictionaries used for abbreviations and IUPAC name conversion is independent of the input size, the space complexity is determined by the number of tokens.

Document parsing method

The document parsing method is illustrated in Fig. 7. It comprises three steps, including Surya document recognition, substituent extraction, and substituent text recognition. In the content recognition step, the text recognition, table detection, and table recognition models from Surya are used to identify the text and tables. Subsequently, the coordinates of the detected cell and text boundaries are mapped, and the text content within the table is filled accordingly. In the substituent extraction step, the Jaccard similarity algorithm is applied to match columns containing substituent identifiers in the table header row, whereas empty rows are filtered out to obtain structured substituent information. The remaining text is processed using a rule-based algorithm similar to that used in ChemSchematicResolver to extract potential substituent information. The substituents represented as molecular structures are recorded with temporary identifiers for use in the subsequent substituent-mapping module. Module IV: Substituent mapping

The substituent-mapping module operates alongside the molecular structure and document separation module, retaining the separated and recognized molecular structures in the original document to supplement the substituent information extracted by the document-parsing module. The inputs to the substituent mapping module include the molecular structure information output by the molecular structure recognition module, and the textual information extracted by the document parsing module. The molecular structure information includes the corresponding CXSMILES string and its bounding box, whereas the textual information comprises the extracted text and its corresponding bounding box from the document. The time complexity is composed of two parts, i.e., filtering the "STRUCT" labeled texts, which takes O(m) time, and mapping the "STRUCT" labels to molecular structures based on bounding-box relations, which requires O(k * n) time. Module V: Molecular integration

The molecular integration module generates a complete molecular list by integrating the scaffold structures and their substituents. This module initially matches the substituent identifiers of the scaffold structures with substituent identifiers in the R-group information. This step prioritizes rule-based methods for the exact matching between identifiers in scaffold structures and R-group information. To address potential character recognition errors in the existing tools, the integration module attempts fuzzy matching when exact matches are missed. For example, if an unmatched substituent identifier "R1" exists in the scaffold structure and an unmatched "RI" exists in the R-group information, the fuzzy matching mechanism associates the two. Following successful matching, molecular assembly is performed. This step utilizes RDKit tools to convert both the scaffold structure and substituent CXSMILES strings into molecular graph structures. The substituent identifiers within the scaffold structure are sequentially replaced with their corresponding substituent structures, yielding the final complete compound.

Result and discussion

Overall performance evaluation

The overall performance of RGReco was evaluated using exact matching criteria based on canonical SMILES representations generated by RDKit. A prediction was considered correct only if the canonical SMILES string of the final merged molecule was identical to that of the ground truth annotation. The evaluation metrics included the precision, recall, and F1 score. Specifically, the precision measures the accuracy of the predicted results, the recall assesses the rate at which the true results are correctly predicted, and the F1 score is the harmonic mean of the two. It is important to note that the evaluation was conducted at the molecular level, treating each of the 640 molecules with equal weight. Consequently, images containing a larger number of molecules had a proportionally higher influence on the aggregate results. Using the current dataset, RGReco achieved a precision of 86.4%, a recall of 79.7%, and an F1 score of 82.9%. Furthermore, while the entire pipeline consists of several macro-modules, we independently evaluated its specific core components based on the availability of manually annotated ground truth for key sub-tasks. Substituent text recognition

We evaluated the performance of our proposed method for recognizing substituent text against two established baselines: the “molscribe.chemistry._parse_formula” function of MolScribe and the OPSIN. The test set was curated from authentic chemical literature and subjected to several filtering steps: removal of typographical errors, exclusion of independent non‑substituent fragments such as “6‑Cl”, deduplication, and elimination of overly simple substituents like “Me” and “H”. This process yielded a final dataset of 76 valid substituent samples. All SMILES outputs generated by the three methods were manually verified for chemical correctness. As summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1, our integrated approach correctly converted 74 out of 76 samples, achieving an accuracy of 97.4%. In contrast, OPSIN and MolScribe managed only 45 (59.2%) and 11 (14.5%) correct conversions, respectively. These results demonstrate that our method substantially outperforms current tools in interpreting substituent text, a critical yet challenging component of chemical structure extraction from literature.

Error case analysis

To elucidate the limitations of the current framework in practical applications, an in-depth analysis of two representative error cases was conducted, as shown in Fig. 9. Case (a) involves a complex R-group image containing tables and substituents. Although the molecular scaffold was correctly recognized, subsequent processes encountered errors in two aspects. Firstly, the table detection model failed to locate the complete table area, resulting in a partial loss of R-group information. Secondly, the critical substituent symbol "X" was omitted during molecular structure recognition, leading to errors in identifying some molecular results. Case (b) presents an R-group image lacking tables and substituent structures. During molecular structure recognition, the critical R-group identifier "R2" was omitted, directly invalidating all subsequent predictions. Additionally, the text recognition module misidentified the substituent text "OCH3" (containing a subscript numeral) as "OCH," causing subsequent substituent text recognition errors. A comprehensive analysis of the above cases reveals that the currently used open-source molecular structure recognition model, MolScribe, lacks robustness when handling small-scale R-group identifiers near molecular structures, leading to the omission of some critical substituent identifiers. Furthermore, the open-source tool currently employed by Surya exhibits imperfections in understanding complex page layouts and recognizing numeric subscripts/superscripts. Conclusion

This study proposes RGReco, a collaborative framework for the multimodal analysis of R-group representations in the chemical publications. By integrating deep learning with chemical rules, a comprehensive automated processing pipeline was developed, which was capable of extracting and integrating molecular structures and textual information from publication images containing R-groups, ultimately generating complete compound lists. By employing a multimodule pipeline, this framework effectively achieved content separation within images using dedicated modules for recognition before integrating R-group-related compounds. An F1 score of 82.9% was obtained using the constructed dataset, validating its effectiveness and practicality in real-world scenarios. Furthermore, while the existing method MarkushGrapher focuses on extracting substituent information from natural language sentences in patents, our proposed framework is designed with a focus on the diverse and complex formats prevalent in chemical literature. It is capable of recognizing substituent information represented as tables, text lists, and substituent structures, thereby filling a critical gap in current automated chemical document parsing (a detailed comparison of the two methods’ respective capabilities is provided in Table S2).

Although RGReco performed well on most standard samples, its overall capability was inherently constrained by the current limitations of the underlying open-source tools. It is also worth noting that R-group definitions provided in free text, which are common in patents and often involve homology groups (e.g., alkyl), are not currently supported. Future studies will therefore focus on expanding the recognition capabilities of RGReco to address other types of Markush variability, such as position variability and frequency variability. This also includes learning how substituent structures with multiple connection points integrate with the scaffold. Additionally, more advanced optical character recognition technologies will be integrated to enhance the text recognition accuracy and strengthen the semantic parsing module, which should allow the system to address broader and more intricate R-group expressions. Simultaneously, the computational efficiency of the pipeline will be optimized to facilitate high-throughput processing. Overall, the R-group auto-parsing framework RGReco developed in this study provides a viable technical pathway for chemical information mining and has the potential to play an active role in drug discovery, material design, and chemical database construction.
