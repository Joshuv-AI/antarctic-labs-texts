Dofetilide for the treatment of premature ventricular complexes and ventricular tachycardia in patients with structural heart disease
Authors: Amrish Deshmukh, Miki Yokokawa, Daniel McBride, Jamie Simpson, Andrew Chou, Michael Ghannam, Jackson J. Liang, Mohammed Saeed, Ryan Cunnane, Hamid Ghanbari, Rakesh Latchamsetty, Thomas Crawford, Krit Jongnarangsin, Frank Pelosi, Aman Chugh, Fred Morady, Frank Bogun, Hakan Oral
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDofetilide is a class III antiarrhythmic agent approved for the treatment of atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter. Given the efficacy of other class III agents, it has been used off‐label for the treatment of premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and ventricular tachycardias (VTs).ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to determine the efficacy and safety of dofetilide for ventricular arrythmias (VAs).MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 81 patients (59 men; age = 60 ± 14 years; LVEF = 0.34 ± 0.16) were admitted for dofetilide initiation to treat PVCs (29), VTs (42) or both (10). A ≥ 80% decrease in PVC burden was defined as a satisfactory response. An ICD was present in 72 patients (89%). Another antiarrhythmic was previously used in 50 patients (62%). Prior catheter ablation had been performed in 33 patients (41%).ResultsDuring intitiation, dofetilide was discontinued in 12 patients (15%) due to QT prolongation (8) and inefficacy to suppress VAs (4). Among the 32 patients with PVCs who successfully started dofetilide, the mean PVC burden decreased from 20 ± 10% to 8 ± 8% at a median follow‐up of 2.6 months (p < .001). PVC burden was reduced by ≥80% in only 11/32 patients (34%). During 7 ± 1 years of follow‐up, 41/69 patients (59%) continued to have VAs and received appropriate ICD therapies for monomorphic VTs (35) and polymorphic VT/VF (6) at a median of 8.0 (IQR 2.6–33.2) months. Dofetilide had to be discontinued in 50/69 patients (72%) due to inefficacy or intolerance. The composite outcome of VT/VF recurrence, heart transplantation, or death occurred in 6/12 patients (50%) without dofetilide and 49/69 patients (71%) with dofetilide. The event free survival was similar between patients treated with and without dofetilide (log‐rank p = .55).ConclusionsTreatment with dofetilide was associated with a decrease in PVCs, however clinically significant suppression occurred in a minority of patients. Dofetilide failed to suppress the occurrence of VTs in a majority of patients.

INTRODUCTION

Class III antiarrhythmic agents such as amiodarone and sotalol, have been used to suppress premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and ventricular tachycardia (VT), either as a stand‐alone therapy or in conjunction with catheter ablation. However, these antiarrhythmic agents can be ineffective or not well tolerated in patients with ventricular arrhythmias (VAs). Although not primarily indicated for the treatment of VAs, dofetilide is also a class III antiarrhythmic agent that is safe and effective for treatment of atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter, including in patients with structural heart disease. 1 , 2 , 3 It is a selective blocker of the rapid component of the outward delayed rectifier K+ current (I Kr) and exerts its antiarrhythmic effects through prolongation of the action potential and refractory period. 4

Dofetilide may also be effective in treating PVCs or VTs, particularly in patients who have failed or could not tolerate other class III antiarrhythmics. However, the efficacy of dofetilide for the suppression of VAs has not been well established. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dofetilide in the treatment of VAs in patients with structural heart disease.

METHODS

Study subjects

The study population consisted of 81 consecutive patients who received dofetilide exclusively for suppression of VAs between 2009 and 2023 (Table 1). There were 59 men and 22 women, and the mean age of the patients was 60 ± 14 years (range: 18–94 years). The mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 0.34 ± 0.16 (range: 0.10–0.65). Among the 81 patients, 23 (28%) had ischemic cardiomyopathy, 54 (68%) had a nonischemic cardiomyopathy (32 idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, 7 LV non‐compaction, 3 myocarditis, 3 congenital heart disease, 1 arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia, 5 sarcoidosis, 2 chemotherapy‐induced cardiomyopathy, and 1 peripartum cardiomyopathy), and 3 (4%) had a mixed cardiomyopathy. An implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) had been previously implanted in 72 patients (89%) for primary prevention (20) or secondary prevention (52), of whom 25 (35%) also had cardiac resynchronization therapy. Forty‐nine of 72 patients (68%) had received ICD therapies, including ICD discharges (46) or antitachycardia pacing (3) before dofetilide initiation.

Prior antiarrhythmic drug therapy

Fifty patients (62%) had failed other antiarrhythmic drugs (AAD) previously. Amiodarone had been administered without and with concomitant mexiletine in 36 (72%) and 15 patients (30%), respectively. Sotalol had previously been administered in 23 (46%) patients and with mexiletine in 5 patients (10%). Twelve patients (24%) had been treated with both amiodarone and sotalol.

Among the 36 patients with prior amiodarone therapy, amiodarone was discontinued due to limited efficacy in 3 patients (8%) and adverse events in 28 patients (78%), including pulmonary toxicity (11), thyroid toxicity (7), liver toxicity (6), ocular effects (1), photosensitivity (1), persistent gastrointestinal upset (1), and tremor (1). Among the 23 patients with prior sotalol therapy, sotalol was discontinued due to limited efficacy in 14 patients (61%), excessive QT prolongation in 3 patients (13%), and adverse events in 9 patients (26%), including fatigue (5), bradycardia (1), worsening heart failure (2), and others (3).

In 31 patients who did not receive an AAD previously, dofetilide was selected on the basis of young age (7), associated comorbidities including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with reduced diffusion capacity (7), after failed VA ablation (n = 7), provider preference (7), and patient preference after shared decision making (2), and as a bridge to transplant in a patient with LVAD (1).

Prior catheter ablation of VAs

Catheter ablation for VAs was previously performed in 33 patients (41%). Among the 29 patients who underwent ablation at the University of Michigan, targeted VAs were PVCs in 20 (69%), VTs in 7 (24%), and both VAs in 2 (7%) patients. Prior ablation was ineffective due to intramural VAs (10), infrequent PVCs at procedure (4), parahisian VAs (2), epicardial VAs (1), papillary muscle VAs (1), pleimorphic PVCs (1), proximity to the left main coronary artery (1), and aborted procedure due to pericardial tamponade (1).

Study protocol

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan and informed consent was waived. Patients were identified by systemic search of the electronic health records using DataDirect (https://datadirect.med.umich.edu/). Patients who received dofetilide for treatment of atrial fibrillation or other atrial arrhythmias were excluded.

Dofetilide was initiated per U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommendations. Patients stayed in the hospital for 6 doses of dofetilide loading with continuous telemetry monitoring. Baseline QTc interval was determined on the latest interpretable electrocardiogram (ECG) before dofetilide initiation. Contraindications for dofetilide initiation included baseline QTc ≥ 440 ms or ≥500 ms (with ventricular conduction abnormality) and/or creatinine clearance ≤20 mL/min. Dofetilide was started at doses of 500, 250, or 125 mcg every 12 h, based on creatinine clearance of >60 mL/min, 40 to 60 mL/min, or 20 to 40 mL/min, respectively. QT was measured by ECG 2 to 3 h after each dose. Whenever the QTc increased by ≥15% or QTc ≥500 ms, the next dose was then reduced by 50%. QTc after dofetilide loading was determined on an ECG between 60 and 72 h after dofetilide initiation. Dofetilide was discontinued if there was a further increase in the QTc after the dose reduction or if polymorphic VAs occurred.

The primary end point of the study was the time from dofetilide initiation to first VT/VF associated with cardiac arrest or ICD therapy during follow‐up. The time to a composite endpoint of VT/VF recurrence, heart transplantation, or death was also evaluated. In patients treated for PVCs, a ≥ 80% decrease in PVC burden was defined as a sufficient response. Patients were followed until death, cardiac transplantation, loss to follow up, or the end of the review period by February 2024. LVEF was measured via echocardiogram in all 81 patients before dofetilide initiation.

Follow‐up

Patients were closely monitored by a dedicated AAD Clinic every 3 months or when there were recurrent arrhythmias after the discharge. Clinic visits included assessment of the QTc, renal function, and serum electrolytes. Dose adjustments were made as necessary. In patients treated for PVCs event monitoring was performed at 2–3 months after dofetilide initiation. Patients with an ICD were followed in the device clinic every 3‐6 months in addition to remote home monitoring.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± 1 standard deviation (or median and interquartile range [IQR] for non‐normally distributed variables) and were compared by the Student's t‐test. Wilcoxon paired analysis was used to evaluate sequential continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test, but if the expected count was smaller than 5 in a given cell of the 2 × 2 table, the Fisher's exact test was used. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess event‐free survival. Differences in event‐free survival in the subgroups were tested with log‐rank testing. Cox regression analyses were used to assess association of dofetilide use and clinical characteristics with the composite outcome of recurrence of VT/VF, heart transplantation, and death. A 2‐tailed p < .05 indicated statistical significance.

RESULTS

Dofetilide initiation

Among the 81 patients, dofetilide was administered to suppress PVCs in 29 (36%), VTs in 42 (52%), and both VAs in 10 patients (12%). Initial dose of dofetilide was 500 mcg in 63 patients (78%), 250 mcg in 15 patients (18%), and 125 mcg in 3 patients (4%). Twenty‐five patients (31%) were in a paced rhythm and 22 patients (27%) had a pre‐existing ventricular conduction abnormality. Baseline QRS width and QTc interval were 96 ± 14 ms and 438 ± 28 ms in patients with normal conduction, 137 ± 19 ms and 449 ± 38 ms in patients with ventricular conduction abnormality, and 162 ± 20 ms and 492 ± 35 ms in patients with paced rhythm, respectively.

During dofetilide loading, dose adjustment was required in 27 patients (33%) due to QT prolongation. Dofetilide was successfully initiated in 69 patients (85%). The effective maintenance dose of dofetilide was 500 mcg po bid in 28 (41%), 250 mcg po bid in 32 (46%), and 125 mcg po bid in 9 patients (13%). In one patient, the maintenance dose of dofetilide was 375 mcg po bid in 1 (1%) at the discretion of the prescribing physician. QTc interval prolonged at steady state to 466 ± 23 ms in patients with normal conduction, 479 ± 32 ms in patients with ventricular conduction abnormality, and 516 ± 32 ms in patients with paced rhythm after 6 doses, respectively (p < .001 for each compared to baseline).

During the index hospitalization, dofetilide was discontinued in 12 patients (15%) due to further QT prolongation after the dose adjustment (5), ICD therapies for monomorphic VT (3), increased PVC burden (1), QT prolongation and ICD therapies for monomorphic VT (1), QT prolongation and increased PVC burden (1), and QT prolongation and episodes of non‐sustained VT (1).

Dofetilide efficacy in VAs

Among the 69 patients who started dofetilide therapy, 38 patients (55%) had appropriate ICD shocks, 3 patients (4%) had antitachycardia pacing, and 2 patients (3%) required external cardioversion for VAs before the dofetide initiation. During a mean follow up of 7 ± 1 years, 41/69 patients (59%, P = 1.0) had VAs with appropriate ICD therapies for monomorphic VTs (35) and polymorphic VT/VF (6). Significant delayed QT prolongation was observed in 2 of 6 patients who had polymorphic VT/VF and median QT prolongation was greater for this group after initiation (IQR 10–13%) compared to the remainder of the cohort (IQR 0–10%). One patient with delayed QT prolongation had nonischemic cardiomyopathy possibly related to an undescribed PKP2 splice site mutation. Initial QTc increase after dofetilide initiation was 12% and in 3 month follow up further increased to 25% from baseline (follow up QTc 470 ms) with polymorphic VT in the setting of mild hypomagnesemia (1.7 mg/dL) and stable renal function. The other patient had sarcoid cardiomyopathy with baseline interventricular conduction delay, QTc prolongation of 21% at initiation, and further increase to 35% (to 608 ms) at 3 years. After initiation, the patient had episodes of myocarditis and subsequent initiation of biventricular pacing. Patients received ICD therapies at a median of 8.0 months (IQR 3–33) after dofetilide initiation (Figure 1). No patient without ICD had cardiac arrest. Among 52 patients who initiated with dofetilide for VTs, 15/46 patients (33%) with successful dofetilide initiation and 2/6 patients (33%) without were free from VT/VF during the follow‐up period (P = 1.0, Figure 2A).

Among the 32 patients (40%) who were successfully initiated with dofetilide for frequent PVCs, the mean PVC burden decreased from 20 ± 10% to 8 ± 8% (p < .001) after dofetilide treatment at a median follow‐up of 2.6 months. Successful suppression of PVCs (≥80% reduction in PVC burden) was achieved in 11/32 patients (34%). The efficacy for PVC supression was similar to the patients with failed dofetilide initiation for PVCs (2/7 patients [29%]; P = 1.0, Figure 2B). LVEF was unchanged before and after treatment with dofetilide (34.4 ± 12.5% to 35.6 ± 15.8%, p = .68).

Long term tolerability of dofetilide

Dofetilide was eventually discontinued in 50/69 patients (72%) at a median of 17 months (IQR 4–58 months) after initiation due to recurrent VAs (26), QTc prolongation (4), or both (5, Figure 3). Five patients had deterioration in renal function during the follow‐up. In the remaining 10 patients, dofetilide was discontinued due to successful catheter ablation for VAs (2), side effects (2), heart transplantation (2), LVAD implantation (1), noncompliance (1), or cost (2).

Catheter ablation after dofetilide initiation

At a median of 13 months (IQR 5–36 months) after the initiation of dofetilide, catheter ablation was performed in 22/69 patients (32%) for frequent PVCs (4), VTs (11), or both (7). Catheter ablation had been performed in 8 of these patients previously and was unsuccessful. PVCs were successfully ablated in 10/11 patients (91%). The site of origin was parahisian in 3, intramural in 2, in papillary muscles in 2, near the left bundle in 2, along the mitral annulus in 1, and right ventricular outflow tract in 1 patient. In 2/18 patients (11%) who underwent VT ablation, the ablation procedure was ineffective. One patient underwent bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy. Dofetilide was re‐loaded after the catheter ablation in 5/22 patients (23%).

Long‐term clinical outcomes

During a mean follow up of 7 ± 1 years, the composite outcome of VT/VF recurrence, heart transplantation, or death occurred in 6/12 patients (50%) in whom dofetilide could not be initiated, and in 49/69 patients (71%) who were discharged on dofetilide (Figure 4). Four patients on dofetilide underwent heart transplantation. There were 4 patients on dofetilide who died during follow up, 2 from progressive heart failure, and 2 due to noncardiac causes. There were 2 deaths due to noncardiac causes in patients who were not treated with dofetilide. Event free survival from VT/VF, heart transplantation, or any cause of death was similar between two groups (log‐rank p = .55, Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

The main findings of this study are; (1) Dofetilide can be successfully initiated for the treatment of VAs in ~85% of the patients. However, during long‐term follow‐up, dofetilide was discontinued in 70% due to inefficacy or intolerance; (2) Although dofetilide may suppress PVCs, a clinically meaningful ≥80% reduction in the PVC burden was achieved in only a minority (34%) of patients; (3) Dofetilide was associated with freedom from VTs in ~40% of patients, however dofetilide was not associated with a significant decrease in device therapies for VAs.

The results of our study are similar to an observational series by Shantha et al. who also found limited utility of dofetilide in suppression of VT and PVCs during a follow up of 2 years. 5 In contrast to the present study, the patients in this series more frequently had ischemic cardiomyopathy and a signficant faction of patients had arrythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy or left ventricular non compaction. In addition, the present study had a greater number of patients who had not failed other antiarrythmic medications. 5

Safety

Compared to the DIAMOND trial which enrolled patients with heart failure and structural heart disease, discontinuation of dofetilide due to polymorphic VT or QT prolongation was more frequent in this cohort (22% vs. 12%). 6 In contrast to prior studies in which polymorphic VT occurred primarily during drug intiation, six patients had polymorphic VT in follow up. 2 , 6 Because these patients had greater QT prolongation after intiation and two had delayed further QT prolongation, a lower tolerance for QT prolongation during drug loading should be considered. In addition, 5 patients had dofetilide dicontinuation in follow up for excessive QT prolongation alone highlighting the importance of continued QT monitoring with dose adjustments based on renal function.

Efficacy of dofetilide for VT

In patients treated with dofetilide for VT, long term freedom from VT requiring ICD shocks was approximately 50%. In comparison, prior trials of sotalol and amiodarone have reported VT free survival rates of >70% suggesting dofetilide is inferior to these agents. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, unlike the present study, the patients in these prior studies had not failed catheter ablation.

One randomized crossover trial has directly compared dofetilide and sotalol. In this study these agents demonstrated similar efficacy in suppression of VT (35%). 11 Importantly, the efficacy of dofetilide and sotalol were not concordant in the majority of randomized patients. The included patients also differ from the present study in that all patients had ischemic heart disease often with ventricular aneurysms (35%). 11

Efficacy of dofetilide for PVCs

The efficacy of dofetilide in treatment of PVCs in this study was lower than in a prior randomized trial in which intravenous dofetilide was associated with a > 80% reduction in PVCs in 50% of patients as compared to placebo. 12 Importantly, this study employed a higher dosage of intravenous dofetilide (7.5 µg/kg) and assessed short term efficacy. In addition, the patients in the current study had failed treatment with catheter ablation and other antiarrhythmic therapies and may be more treatment resistant. As prior investigations demonstrate a higher efficacy of PVC suppression with class I and other class III agents, dofetilide may have utility only in the narrow population of patients who cannot tolerate these medications. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is the retrospective nature. As the included patients are primarily those who have failed prior antiarrhythmic agents or catheter ablation, direct comparison of the safety and efficacy of dofetilide to other antiarrhythmic agents is not feasible. It is possible that in a cohort of patients who had not been previously treated dofetilide may be more efficacious. In addition, because of the retrospective nature of the study, the included patients are heterogenous with regard to underlying substrate and prior treatments. Similar to prior series examining treatment of PVCs or VT with antiarrythmic agents we did not observe a differential effect of dofetilide between ischemic and nonischemic substrates. 16 , 17 , 18 However, the cohort size may also limit the power to draw conclusions regarding the efficacy of dofetilide within subgroups. While the protocol for dofetilide initiation is standardized at our institution, thresholds for medication withdrawal due to inefficacy or intolerance were at the discretion of the treating electrophysiologist. Finally the results of this study may not have bearing on the treatment of patients in countries where dofetilide is not available.

CONCLUSIONS

In patients with structural heart disease, dofetilide had limited efficacy in the treatment of VAs. In patients who have not responded to other antiarrhythmics or catheters ablation and when treatment options are limited, dofetilide can be considered to determine if there will be a reponse. When closely monitored, long‐term dofetilide use appears to be safe in patients with structural heart disease and VAs.
