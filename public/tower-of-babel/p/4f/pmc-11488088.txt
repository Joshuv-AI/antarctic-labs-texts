The associations between modifiable risk factors and constipation: a comprehensive mendelian randomization study
Authors: Hong Chang, Jing Ouyang, Meng Tian, Jin Yang, Jie Gao, Mengjiao Yang, Meng Zhang, Hao Yuan, Ya Zheng, Yuping Wang, Zhaofeng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEarly identification of modifiable risk factors is crucial for the prevention of constipation. This study systematically investigated the relationship between genetically predicted modifiable risk factors and constipation.MethodsThe inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was employed as the primary analytical approach. For similar exposure indicators, the multivariate Mendelian randomization (MVMR) method was used to adjust for potential biases in univariate MR analysis. The robustness of the results was further evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test, Cochran’s Q test, and leave-one-out analysis. Bonferroni correction was applied to reduce the false positive rate in the results.ResultsThe IVW analysis indicated a significant causal association between genetically predicted gastroesophageal reflux disease [OR (95% CI) = 1.192 (1.079–1.315), P = 0.0005], atorvastatin use [OR (95% CI) = 16.995 (3.327–86.816), P = 0.0007], and constipation. Additionally, there was a potential causal association between education level [OR (95% CI) = 0.859 (0.767–0.964), P = 0.009], major depressive disorder [OR (95% CI) = 1.206 (1.041–1.399), P = 0.013], hypothyroidism [OR (95% CI) = 2.299 (1.327–3.985), P = 0.003], and aspirin use [OR (95% CI) = 4.872 (1.174–20.221), P = 0.029] with constipation. No causal associations were found for the other included indicators. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated the absence of evidence for heterogeneity and pleiotropy in any positive results.ConclusionThis study identified several risk factors that could be targeted for the prevention of constipation, offering valuable insights for public health policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03384-8.Keywords: Constipation, Risk factors, Mendelian randomization, Genome-wide association data

Introduction

Chronic constipation is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal disorder, with a prevalence ranging from 0.5 to 32% in children and 2.5–79% in adults [1, 2]. The prevalence of constipation in children is 0.5–32%, while in adults it is 2.5–79% [3]. The incidence of constipation is higher in non-white populations, among women, and increases with age, particularly in the elderly [4]. Constipation is associated with impaired quality of life and various medical burdens, making it an important public health issue [5]. Characteristic symptoms include hard stools, infrequent bowel movements, abdominal pain, and a sensation of incomplete defecation. The latest diagnostic criteria for constipation, namely the Rome IV criteria, stipulate that patients should have symptoms for at least 6 months and at least two of the following symptoms for 3 months or longer, then chronic constipation can be diagnosed: (1) Straining more than 25% of defecations. (2) Lumpy or hard stools (BSFS type 1 or 2) more than 25% of defecations. (3) Sensation of incomplete evacuation more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations. (4) Sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations. (5) Manual maneuvers to facilitate more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations. (6) Fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week [6]. Initial treatments for chronic constipation typically involve dietary and lifestyle changes, while other options include medication, biofeedback therapy, and surgery. Despite extensive research on the nutritional management of constipation, focusing mainly on increasing fiber and water intake, there remains a lack of high-quality evidence regarding the effectiveness of dietary interventions [7, 8]. Currently, the treatment of constipation mainly uses laxatives, osmotic agents or stimulants [9] as well as secretagogues and prokinetic agents [10]. However, these therapies often come with significant side effects, such as watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea, and bloating, as well as issues like high drug dependence, high recurrence rates, poor long-term efficacy, and high costs, which limit their application [11].

Various risk factors, including demographic factors (income, education level, employment status) [12–14]、lifestyle factors and behaviors (physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable intake) [15–17], and health-related factors (including medical conditions and medications), are associated with the risk of constipation. However, the existing evidence for most factors is either conflicting or insufficient. Evaluating the causal impact of these modifiable factors on constipation is challenging because traditional case-control studies based on observational data can only describe associations between risk factors and outcomes without determining causality due to confounding factors [18]. Mendelian randomization (MR) has been increasingly used to study causal relationships between exposure and outcome. The basic principle of MR is to utilize genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to proxy for the exposure of interest and make causal inferences about the outcome. Compared to traditional observational studies, MR has the advantage of using genetic variations that are randomly assigned at conception, offering a method that is less likely to be influenced by confounding factors and reverse causality [19]. Therefore, in this study, we used genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to investigate the risk factors for constipation and employed a two-sample MR approach to test the causal relationship between 39 genetically predicted potential risk factors and constipation.

Methods

Study design

The main purpose of this study was to systematically assess the causal association between multiple exposure factors and constipation. The major steps for detailed analysis are as follows: First, exposure factors correlated with constipation were filtered from epidemiological studies, and relevant instrumental variables (IVs) were obtained from different gene sequencing databases. Then, a two-sample Mendelian randomization (TSMR) method was used to verify their causal relationships with constipation [20]. For similar exposure indicators, a multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) method was adopted to adjust for potential biases in univariate MR analysis [21]. Sensitivity analyses were performed on the subsequent results to evaluate the presence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy affecting the robustness of the results [22]. For heterogeneous results, the MR-PRESSO method was further employed to remove outliers in the MR analysis, and the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was used again to obtain the causal effect estimate [23]. Finally, Bonferroni correction was used to reduce the false-positive rate in the results. All data included in this study were from freely available online gene sequencing databases. Exposure factor-related instrumental variables

In this study, 39 exposure factors related to constipation identified from traditional epidemiological studies were divided into four categories: (1) Socioeconomic factors: income level, education level, unemployment, and employment; (2) Dietary and lifestyle factors: including smoking, alcohol consumption, carbohydrate, milk, vegetables, fruits, pork, beef, mutton, fish, nuts, beans, coffee, tea, carbonated beverage intake, and sleep duration, exercise duration; (3) Metabolic factors: body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, blood sugar (Glu), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL); (4) Disease and drug factors: gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), multiple sclerosis (MS), Parkinson’s syndrome (PD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), stroke, anxiety, major depressive disorder (MDD), hypothyroidism, morphine use, aspirin use, and atorvastatin use. The exposure factors included in this study were all of the European population from the GWAS database. The obtained IVs were strictly screened. First, all included SNPs had to meet the significance of genomics (P < 5 × 10 − 8). For some exposure factors with a small number of effective SNPs (< 3), the screening conditions were appropriately relaxed (P < 5 × 10 − 6). Secondly, the selected SNPs had to be independent of each other, with no linkage disequilibrium (LD). Therefore, linkage disequilibrium was removed from the preliminary screening data (Clump = 10000 kb, r2 = 0.001). Finally, the F-statistic was used to quantify the strength of the association between genotypes and each putative risk factor, and the F-statistic for each SNP (F = beta2/se2) was evaluated, and SNPs with F < 10 were excluded [24]. If F > 10, it is considered that the correlation between IVs and exposure was strong enough to prevent MR analysis results from being affected by weak instrumental bias.

Outcome factor-related instrumental variables

In the open database IEU Open GWAS Project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), the search was conducted with “constipation” as the gene phenotype keyword, and the GWAS data set with the largest available sample size was selected for the study. Considering that most of the exposure factors originated from the UK Biobank (UKB), to avoid sample overlap, constipation data from the UKB was excluded. Therefore, in the study, to ensure the independence and randomness between exposure and outcome, constipation data from the Finnish database was finally selected for MR analysis, which included 17,246 patients with constipation and 201,546 controls.

Mendelian randomization analysis

In this study, the MR analysis satisfied three basic assumptions: (1) IVs are strongly correlated with exposure factors such as socioeconomic and dietary lifestyles (association assumption); (2) IVs variables are not affected by confounding factors (independence assumption); (3) IVs are not related to constipation (exclusivity assumption) [25]. The IVW method was selected as the primary analysis method, which calculates the effect estimates of each SNP on exposure and outcome, assigns weights to each SNP using the variance of the effect estimates, and then combines the weighted effect estimates to obtain a total causal effect estimate [26, 27]. MR-Egger regression method and weighted median method (WM) were used as auxiliary analysis methods in this study. The MR-Egger regression method is a summary data instrumental variable MR method based on the IVW method, which corrects the requirement for the requirement of no pleiotropy among genetic variations in the IVW method [20, 28]. This method uses the effect estimates of all SNPs for regression analysis, and obtains the corrected causal effect estimate through regression analysis. MR-Egger also provides an intercept term that can be used to test and correct for certain biases. The WM method provides a relatively robust and consistent causal estimation method by weighting the effect estimates of multiple genetic variations and combining these estimates using the median [20]. The above three analysis methods have their own advantages and disadvantages, and the results need to be interpreted according to specific circumstances. Generally speaking, under the condition of fully meeting the three core assumptions, the results of the IVW method are the most accurate. When there is pleiotropy, the results obtained by the IVW method may be biased, and in such cases, the results of the WM and MR-Egger methods can be considered. In general, Mendelian randomization analysis primarily uses the IVW method for testing, while the WM and MR-Egger methods are more used to test the robustness of the results and can only provide limited reference value. The MR- PRESSO method is used to eliminate outliers, utilizing the sum of squares of residuals in the regression equation to assess the heterogeneity of causal estimates, more comprehensively considering the influence of genetic heterogeneity on the research results [23]. This method is of great significance in MR research and can help researchers more accurately evaluate the causal impact of exposure on the outcome.

MVMR research is based on the principles of Mendelian genetics and the concept of randomized experiments, using the random assignment of genotypes to simulate randomized experiments to assess the causal impact of multiple exposure factors on specific outcomes [29]. MVMR research differs from typical TSMR research. When too many indicators are included in the MVVR research, it may cause inaccuracies in the results. There should be a certain correlation between several exposures used for multivariate analysis, such as HDL, LDL, and TG. Therefore, only MVMR analysis was performed on red meat and lipid exposure indicators in the subsequent study.

Sensitivity analysis

This study further employed MR-Egger intercept test, Cochran’s Q statistic, and leave-one-out analysis to detect the presence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy, and to evaluate the robustness of the results [23, 30, 31]. Finally, Bonferroni correction was used to correct the Type I error rate in the multiple comparison problem [32].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis of this study was mainly carried out in the R (version 4.1.3) software, using the “TwoSample MR” and “MR-PRESSO” packages. The odds ratio (OR) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to describe the final results. When OR > 1, the exposure factor is regarded as a risk factor for the outcome; while OR < 1, the exposure factor is considered a protective factor for the outcome. Additionally, for the primary outcome, results are defined based on the P-value; P < 0.0013 has significant statistical significance, and when 0.0013 < P < 0.05, it has potential statistical significance.

Results

In this study, a total of 39 clinical risk factors were included to study the causal relationship between them and constipation, and all risk factors were divided into four categories: socioeconomic factors, dietary lifestyle factors, metabolic factors, and disease drug factors. The number of SNPs of these factors ranged from 3 to 461, and the F-statistics of all SNPs were > 10, indicating that there were no weak instrumental variable affecting the stability of the results, and the exposure factor information and F-statistics can be seen in Table 1. After Bonferroni correction, a significant causal association was found between genetically predicted GERD and atorvastatin use with constipation. Additionally, there were a potential causal association between education level, MDD, hypothyroidism, and aspirin use with constipation. No causal association were detected between other clinical risk factors and constipation. For any positive results, no evidence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy was found (Table 2).

TSMR of the Association between socioeconomic factors and constipation

Regarding the study of the relationship between socioeconomic factors and constipation, we identified a potential causal association between genetically predicted educational attainment and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 0.859 (0.767–0.964), P = 0.009], and the same conclusion were also drawn by the WM method. However, income level, employment status, and unemployment status did not show MR correlations with constipation. MR-Egger intercept test and Cochran’s Q test did not detect significant heterogeneity and pleiotropy, and the leave-one-out method also did not detect a single SNP that affected the direction of the results (Fig. 2).

TSMR of the Association between Dietary Lifestyle factors and constipation

Regarding the study on the relationship between dietary lifestyle factors and constipation, univariate MR analysis showed that there were a causal association between genetically-predicted beef intake [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 0.352 (0.198–0.624), P < 0.001] and lamb intake [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 0.498 (0.271–0.915), P = 0.025] with constipation. In order to correct the bias of the univariate analysis, we performed MVMR analysis. However, after adjusting for the intake of beef, lamb and pork, the association between beef intake [MVMR: OR (95% CI) = 0.439 (0.173–1.118), P = 0.084] and lamb intake [MVMR: OR (95% CI) = 0.699 (0.249–1.959), P = 0.496] with constipation no longer existed (Fig. 3). No causal correlations were observed between other dietary factors, lifestyles and constipation in this study.

Forest plot of MVMR analysis for red meat

TSMR of the Association between metabolic factors and constipation

Regarding the study of the relationship between metabolic factors and constipation, univariate MR analysis showed a potential causal association between genetically- predicted HDL and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 0.924 (0.864–0.988), P = 0.020]. Subsequently, we performed MVMR analysis. However, after adjusting for HDL, LDL and TG, the association between HDL and constipation no longer existed [OR (95% CI) = 0.932 (0.863–1.006), P = 0.071] (Fig. 4). No causal correlations were observed between BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, Glu, LDL, TG and constipation in this study. In the sensitivity analysis, Cochran’s Q test for BMI, systolic blood pressure, and LDL suggested the presence of heterogeneity. Subsequently, using the MR-PRESSO method to remove outliers, it was found that the directionality of the IVW result was not affected, so its presence could be ignored.

TSMR of the Association between Disease drug factors and constipation

For the study on the relationship between disease drug factors and constipation, we identified that there are a relatively significant causal association between genetically predicted GERD and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 1.192 (1.079–1.315), P = 0.0005], and the WM method also reached the same conclusion; there are a potential causal association between genetically-predicted MDD and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 1.206 (1.041–1.399), P = 0.013]; there are a potential causal association between genetically predicted hypothyroidism and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 2.299 (1.327–3.985), P = 0.003]; there are a potential causal association between genetically predicted aspirin use and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 4.872 (1.174–20.221), P = 0.029]; there are a relatively significant causal association between genetically predicted atorvastatin use and constipation [IVW method: OR (95% CI) = 16.995 (3.327–86.816), P = 0.0007]. Although positive results for MDD, aspirin use, and atorvastatin use with constipation were not observed in the WM and MR-Egger methods, considering that there are no heterogeneity or pleiotropy in these factors, we use the results obtained by the IVW method as the final conclusion. In this study, no causal correlations were observed between MS, PD, AD, stroke, anxiety, and constipation. In the sensitivity analysis, Cochran’s Q test for stroke suggested the existence of heterogeneity. After removing outliers using the MR-PRESSO method, it was found that the directionality of the IVW result was not affected, so it was considered that this heterogeneity does not affect the robustness of the result. The MR-Egger intercept test and Cochran’s Q test for the remaining indicators did not detect significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy, and the leave-one-out method did not detect a single SNP that affected the directionality of the result (Figs. 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9).

Discussion

This is an MR study analyzing the clinical risk factors of constipation. The results indicate that genetically-predicted GERD, MDD, hypothyroidism, aspirin use, and atorvastatin use may be risk factors for constipation. Higher educational attainment may serve as a protective factor against constipation. Among these, genetically predicted GERD, hypothyroidism, and atorvastatin use show stronger causal associations with constipation. These findings are crucial for the early identification of risk factors, as well as for the prevention and treatment of constipation.

Education and constipation

As human society develops, people pay more and more attention to education. There is ample evidence that people with a higher level of education are healthier in terms of physical, mental, functional abilities, and cognitive performance [33–35]. In the past few decades, a large number of epidemiological studies on the relationship between educational attainment and various diseases have been conducted, and more and more evidence shows that educational attainment is closely related to chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and certain cancers [36–40]. In addition, the educational attainment also has a non-negligible impact on constipation. A cross-sectional study found that the prevalence of constipation among illiterate participants were 9.0% (95% CI: 8.0–10.0) [41], which were significantly higher than that of non-illiterate participants. In our study, we also found a causal association between genetically-predicted educational attainment and constipation. The IVW method showed that a higher level of education may be a protective factor for constipation [OR (95% CI) = 0.859 (0.767–0.964), P = 0.009]. The reasons why educational attainment affects constipation may include: (1) Dietary habits: People with a higher level of education tend to have healthier dietary habits, such as consuming more dietary fiber, liquids, and plant-based foods, which can help promote intestinal motility and normal defecation [42]. (2) Lifestyle: People with a higher level of education may attach more importance to a healthy lifestyle, including regular exercise, reducing sedentary behavior, and avoiding unhealthy habits, all of which can help promote intestinal peristalsis and prevent constipation [43].((3) Psychological stress: People with a higher level of education have better mental health and coping abilities. Currently, mental health problems such as anxiety and depression are regarded as risk factors for constipation, and people with a higher level of education may have better coping abilities, thereby reducing the likelihood of constipation [44, 45]. (4) Cognitive factors: People with a higher level of education may have more knowledge and awareness of health issues, including the prevention and management of constipation. They are more likely to take active measures to maintain good bowel habits and lifestyles, thereby reducing the risk of constipation [46–48]. From this, it can be seen that generally improving the educational level of the population has a positive impact on the prevention and treatment of constipation.

Diet and constipation

The benefits of consuming adequate amounts of fruits and vegetables on health have been demonstrated by a large number of studies. The dietary fiber contained in them is defined as “the remnants of plant constituents that resist hydrolysis by human digestive enzymes,” and is a type of carbohydrate that can tolerate gastric acid and cannot be digested in the human body [49]. Many studies have shown that supplementing with insoluble dietary fiber can improve the symptoms of constipation patients [50, 51]. A randomized trial of 275 primary-care constipation patients receiving bran treatment found that the bran-consuming group had better constipation treatment effects than the placebo group after 3 months [52]. In our study, no significant causal relationship was found between diet, exercise, and constipation. The potential reasons for this may include: (1) Residual confounding: Residual confounding may come from a variety of factors, including individual differences, environmental factors, measurement errors, etc. In MR studies, even with random allocation, differences in individual physical characteristics and metabolic processes may lead to individual differences, thereby influencing the effects of diet and exercise on constipation. For example, some people may be more sensitive to specific dietary components, while for others, exercise has a greater impact on their intestinal motility and digestive processes. Individual differences and their possible influencing factors need to be considered in study design and data analysis. Currently, residual confounding may account for part of the instability of MR study results [53]. (2) Complexity of physiological pathways: The occurrence of constipation involves multiple physiological pathways, including intestinal peristalsis, fecal water content, and intestinal flora, and their regulation can be very complex. Although diet and exercise can affect the function of these physiological pathways, in some individuals, there may be other physiological mechanisms that mask the effects of diet and exercise on constipation, resulting in less obvious effects of diet and exercise on constipation [54, 55]. Subsequent studies need to consider these potential causes and take corresponding measures to optimize the study design, sample selection, and data analysis methods, in order to more accurately understand the relationship between diet, exercise, and constipation.

GERD and constipation

There are cross-sectional studies indicating a correlation between the prevalence of GERD and constipation [OR (95% CI): 1.51 (1.28–1.78)] [12], and 28.3% of GERD patients develop constipation symptoms [56]. A study on childhood constipation found that 39% of children experienced reflux symptoms before the occurrence of constipation [57]. Baran et al. found that there may be a certain overlap between GERD and functional constipation [58, 59]. Constipation patients may also have symptoms such as acid regurgitation and heartburn [60, 61]. The results of these observational studies are consistent with the direction of our study, and our study also found that genetically predicted GERD may increase the risk of constipation [OR (95% CI) = 1.192 (1.079–1.315), P = 0.0005]. The reason for this phenomenon may be related to the following reasons. Patients with constipation often experience difficulties in defecation and excessive straining. This straining and difficulty in evacuation can increase intra-abdominal pressure and gastric pressure, leading to a reduction in lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure. This phenomenon is similar to what is observed in other situations where intra-abdominal pressure is elevated, such as coughing, sneezing, or weightlifting [62]. Furthermore, constipation can affect normal gastrointestinal motility, resulting in the accumulation of gas or intestinal contents in the bowel. Through gastrointestinal reflex mechanisms, such as vagal nerve regulation, this condition may induce transient relaxation of the LES [63]. Constipation is typically associated with decreased intestinal motility, and the normal rhythmicity of gastrointestinal motility is a critical regulator of motilin secretion. When intestinal motility is reduced, the secretion of motilin may be inhibited, thereby affecting the function of the migrating motor complex in the gastrointestinal tract. This dysfunction can not only lead to constipation but also exacerbate GERD symptoms by delaying gastric emptying and impacting LES function. This dual effect suggests that abnormalities in motilin regulation may play a role in both constipation and GERD. Studies have also found that patients with constipation often exhibit abnormal bile acid metabolism, which can affect normal intestinal transit [64]. Dysregulated bile acid metabolism may lead to delayed gastric emptying, increased gastric pressure, and consequently make gastric contents more prone to reflux into the esophagus. These factors collectively contribute to the relaxation of the LES, thereby worsening GERD symptoms. Constipation is one of the lower gastrointestinal problems with the greatest correlation with GERD, and the presence of GERD should be considered in the prevention of constipation [65].

MDD and constipation

Depression is considered one of the most pressing mental health issues currently. MDD is associated with a significant reduction in lifespan, partially due to suicide, and the remaining reasons are a significantly increased susceptibility to major diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, strokes, autoimmune diseases, diabetes, and cancer [66–68]. Patients with depression are not only more susceptible to these problems but also lead to worse treatment outcomes for these diseases [69]. In our study, genetically predicted MDD was found to be another potential risk factor for constipation [OR (95% CI) = 1.206 (1.041–1.399), P = 0.013]. This is consistent with the results of a Mendelian randomization study on the relationship between depression and constipation [70]. Observational studies also support our view, as they found that constipation associated with depression is widespread in the Asian population [71], and individuals with depression have a higher risk of constipation compared to non-depressed individuals [72, 73].

The mechanisms by which depression increases the risk of constipation may include the following: (1) Gut-brain peptides: In patients with depression, the secretion of stress hormones such as cortisol and catecholamines increases, which can directly or indirectly affect the synthesis and release of gut-brain peptides. If the secretion of these peptide substances decreases or their receptor functions are abnormal, it may lead to weakened intestinal motility, prolonged residence time of feces in the intestine, and cause constipation symptoms. (2) Neuroendocrine peptides: Patients with depression are usually in a state of continuous stress, which may lead to excessive activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, resulting in an increase in the release of the important regulatory factor of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, corticotropin-releasing factor (Corticotropin releasing factor, CRF) [74, 75]. The increase in CRF release may lead to effects such as slowed intestinal motility, increased intestinal mucosal permeability, and imbalance in neurotransmitter regulation, thereby affecting constipation [76, 77]. (3) Antidepressants: For example, tricyclic antidepressants (Tricyclic antidepressant, TCA) are more likely to cause gastrointestinal symptoms than placebos in the treatment of major depression [78]. Because TCA has an inhibitory effect on acetylcholine, and acetylcholine can promote intestinal peristalsis and defecation in the intestine, the anticholinergic effect of TCA causes a slowdown in intestinal peristalsis, resulting in constipation.

Hypothyroidism and constipation

The most common symptoms of hypothyroidism are dry skin, coarse hair, myxedema, pale complexion, intolerance to cold, constipation, anorexia, etc. It is well known that hypothyroidism is associated with a decrease in the frequency of bowel movements, and many patients with hypothyroidism have constipation symptoms [79–82]. Back in 1999, Deen et al.’s prospective study used anorectal manometry, rectal balloon sensation, and total intestinal transport markers to evaluate the anorectal physiology of constipation patients with hypothyroidism and found that 33% of the hypothyroidism patients have symptoms of intestinal dysfunction [83]. Our study also supports this view. We found that genetically predicted hypothyroidism is a risk factor for constipation [OR (95% CI) = 2.299 (1.327–3.985), P = 0.003]. The intestine is a target organ of thyroid hormone, and it functions through nuclear thyroid hormone receptor α1 (TRα1) expressed by intestinal epithelial cells. It can directly act on intestinal smooth muscle cells, change the activity of their ion channels, regulate the contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle, and thereby affect intestinal peristalsis. T3 controls the homeostasis of the intestinal epithelium by interacting with TRα1 (the main TR isoform in the intestine). This homeostasis depends on the strict regulation of local T3 concentrations, which are regulated by specific TH transporters and deiodinases in the intestine. Patients with dominant-negative mutations in TR and experimental mouse models show severe abnormalities in the morphology of the intestinal epithelium and suffer from severe symptoms of gastrointestinal dysfunctions, supporting the critical role of TRα-mediated T3 in intestinal function [84]. In addition, changes in thyroid hormone levels may affect the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota, thereby affecting intestinal function and peristalsis [85, 86].

Aspirin and constipation

Aspirin has become one of the most widely used nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) due to its unique antiplatelet effect, and it has important value in the prevention of atherosclerotic diseases. Therefore, we selected aspirin as the representative of NSAIDs for our study. Observational studies have found a correlation between the use of aspirin and constipation [OR (95% CI) = 1.70 (1.00-2.70)] [16]. NSAIDs are associated with an increased risk of constipation [OR (95% CI) = 1.54 (1.17–2.03), P = 0.002] [87]. Similarly, our study reached the same conclusion, indicating that genetically predicted aspirin use increases the risk of constipation [OR (95%CI) = 4.872 (1.174–20.221), P = 0.029].

The inhibition of PG may explain the reason for constipation in NSAIDs users [88], including the following: (1) Inhibiting intestinal smooth muscle contraction: PG can bind to specific receptors on intestinal smooth muscle cells, activate intracellular signal transduction pathways, resulting in an increase in intracellular calcium ion concentration, thereby activating actin and troponin, playing an important role in the contraction of intestinal smooth muscle. PG can also affect the intracellular second messenger system, such as by activating adenylate cyclase to increase intracellular cyclic AMP levels, thereby affecting the contraction of intestinal smooth muscle. Therefore, when PG production is reduced, it will lead to weakened intestinal smooth muscle contraction, thereby causing constipation. (2) Imbalance of intestinal secretion: PG can stimulate Cl-channels on intestinal mucosal cells to promote the secretion of Cl-ions. The secretion of Cl-ions will cause water to follow the ions to flow out, increasing the water content in the intestinal contents. Therefore, when PG production is reduced, it will lead to a decrease in water absorption in the intestine, thereby causing constipation. In addition, NSAIDs-mediated PG depletion plays a crucial role in the early stages of intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction. In case of barrier damage, when the Gram-negative intestinal microbiota invades the intestine, it will cause an inflammatory cascade reaction, activate Toll-like receptor 4, resulting in an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines and the activation of the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-associated protein 3 inflammasome, while attracting a large number of neutrophils to accumulate, eventually leading to oxygen chronic inflammation and apoptosis, triggering intestinal diseases [89]. It is worth emphasizing that the role of the intestinal microbiota in indirectly regulating NSAIDs-induced enteropathy is a promising research area.

Atorvastatin and constipation

Since the launch of simvastatin in 1987, statins have become a widely used class of lipid-lowering drugs. Statins lower cholesterol levels in the blood by inhibiting the activity of cholesterol synthesis enzymes in the body. Statins are highly effective in delaying the progression of atherosclerosis and reducing the incidence of vascular lesions [90]. Our study found that genetically predicted atorvastatin use increased the risk of constipation [OR (95% CI) = 3.86, (1.08–13.77), P = 0.036] [91]. And several large-scale observational studies have also found that 2–3% of statin users will experience constipation. In a clinical trial review involving 2,361 patients with primary hypercholesterolemia receiving statin therapy (56% receiving the maximum daily dose), with a median follow-up of 1 year, the most common drug-related adverse events were constipation (2.2%) and abdominal pain (2.5%) [92].

Statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis by inhibiting hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase. Cholesterol is the precursor substance of bile acids. Therefore, when the synthesis of cholesterol is inhibited, the generation of bile acids will also decrease [93]. Bile acids are a compound synthesized by the liver and stored in the gallbladder, which plays an important role in the digestive process. As a G-protein-coupled receptor, G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (Takeda G-protein-coupled receptor 5, TGR5) is widely expressed in intestinal tissues. Bile acids can act as agonists of TGR5, and after binding to the TGR5 receptor, it triggers intracellular G protein-related signaling pathways, resulting in an increase in the concentration of intracellular Ca2 + in intestinal smooth muscle cells, thereby causing the contraction of intestinal smooth muscle [94, 95]. In addition, the colonic tissue morphology of patients with constipation is significantly damaged, and their impaired intestinal function may be related to the abundance and stability of specific bacterial groups in the intestine. Bile acids have a certain antibacterial effect and have a bactericidal or inhibitory effect on some intestinal bacteria. This antibacterial effect helps maintain the stability of the microbial community in the intestine and prevents the overgrowth of harmful flora. However, statins inhibit the synthesis of bile acids, which may disrupt the stability of the microbial community and lead to flora imbalance. This disorder may also affect intestinal peristalsis and water absorption, resulting in the occurrence of constipation [96].

Innovation and limitations

This study uses large-scale GWAS data and MR studies to analyze the causal relationship between clinical risk factors and constipation. One of the main advantages of this study is its large sample size, which helps to reduce sampling errors and can help eliminate the influence of confounding factors, thereby more accurately assessing the causal relationship between variables and constipation. In addition, this study conducted a number of sensitivity analyses and carried out comprehensive quality control of the pleiotropy and heterogeneity of the MR analysis, so the conclusions obtained are robust.

The present study has the following limitations. Firstly, the research data comes from the aggregated-level data, so it is impossible to eliminate the impact of covariates on the results through stratified studies. Despite the finding in epidemiological investigations that women and advanced age are risk factors for constipation, due to data limitations, this study did not conduct stratified analysis according to gender and age to explore their impact on the causal relationship. Secondly, when conducting research on the relationship between drug use and constipation, only the TSMR study was performed, and no drug-targeted MR analysis was carried out, so there is still some uncertainty in the obtained results. Additionally, the sample size of some of the selected exposure data in this study is relatively small, so the obtained results may be biased. Moreover, in the study of the relationship between blood pressure and constipation, the results differ from those of Wang et al., and we consider that this may be related to the difference in sample selection [97]. We separately selected systolic and diastolic pressure data, and did not include hypertension diagnosis data, and subsequent studies can use updated and larger whole-genome sequencing data for further verification. Finally, the data of this study comes from the database in Europe, and the research results may not be applicable to other races. Therefore, our results cannot be regarded as absolute, and subsequent studies are still needed to verify the inference of the results.

Conclusion

A higher level of education may be a protective factor for constipation; GERD, MDD, thyroid hypothyroidism, aspirin use, and atorvastatin use may be risk factors for constipation. This can provide some reference information for public health policies.
