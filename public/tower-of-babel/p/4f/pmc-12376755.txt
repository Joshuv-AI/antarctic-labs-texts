Inhibition of Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli adhesion via aptamers prevents infection in IPEC-J2 cells
Authors: Zefeng Wang, Tao Xu, Jianxi Tan, Yan Zhou, Qiyi Li, Zhihui Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) is a well-known strain associated with post-weaning diarrhea. Consequently, strategies to prevent and inhibit ETEC infections are critically important. Aptamers are single-stranded DNA or RNA sequences that exhibit high affinity and specificity for binding to target molecules. In this study, we used the intestinal porcine epithelial cell line IPEC-J2 as an in vitro model to demonstrate that two aptamers, K88-Apt A04 (a fimbrial-specific aptamer targeting ETEC K88) and K88-Apt 37 (a cell-specific aptamer targeting ETEC K88) effectively inhibited the adhesion of ETEC K88 to intestinal epithelial cells. We established an ETEC K88-IPEC-J2 interaction model by exposing the IPEC-J2 cell line to ETEC K88 at a multiplicity of infection of 10. Our findings revealed that the aptamers inhibited ETEC K88 adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells in a concentration-dependent manner, primarily through adherence inhibition and prevention. Furthermore, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and mRNA expression analyses indicated that the aptamers reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines triggered by ETEC K88 infection. In conclusion the aptamers demonstrated the ability to reduce ETEC K88-induced cytotoxicity in IPEC-J2 cells in vitro.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04233-8.

Introduction

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) is a major cause of diarrhea in neonatal and young pigs, leading to significant economic losses, increased treatment costs, and reduced production efficiency. The key virulence factors of ETEC-mediated diarrhea include (i) adhesins, which mediate bacterial attachment to host enterocytes and initiate Escherichia coli (E. coli) colonization, and (ii) enterotoxins, which disrupt fluid homeostasis in the small intestine, causing fluid hypersecretion. ETEC strains expressing F4 (K88) fimbriae, heat-labile entero-toxin (LT), and heat-stable enterotoxin (ST) are highly prevalent. Previous cell culture studies have indicated that reducing the adhesion of F4+ ETEC on epithelial cells requires the repression of both adhesin and LT [1]. Using the undifferentiated piglet jejunum intestinal epithelial cell line IPEC-J2 as an in vitro cell model [2], it was found that the flagellum of F4ac+ ETEC is an important virulence factor that can act in concert with F4 fimbriae to facilitate adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells in vitro [3]. Additional support for the importance of F4 fimbriae in ETEC infection of IPEC-J2 cells originates from Geens and Niewold, who used isogenic strains to demonstrate that only the strain possessing F4 fimbriae was able to efficiently bind IPEC-J2 cells [4].

Research into feeding strategies to improve piglet health after weaning has become a high priority. However, animal testing is demanding and can raise animal welfare concerns. Cell culture is considered an alternative to in vivo experiments. The IPEC-J2 cell line has emerged as the most relevant porcine cell line for in vitro challenge studies with enteric pathogens. Furthermore, IPEC-J2 cells are increasingly used in microbiological studies to examine interactions between various animal and human pathogens—including pathogenic E. coli and Salmonella enterica—and intestinal epithelial cells.

Antibiotics have been used for decades to combat gastrointestinal disorders caused by ETEC pathogens. However, over the past two decades, public health concerns have increased due to the rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and their impact on the environment [5–7]. Significant efforts have been made to find alternatives that can reduce or replace antibiotic use in animal production [8, 9]. Over the past two decades, the administration of pharmacological doses of zinc oxide (ZnO) has been the most efficacious approach for mitigating diarrhea and intestinal injuries in swine. ZnO effectively diminished the incidence of recurrent diarrhea and intestinal damage in pigs [10]. Nonetheless, due to environmental concerns associated with elevated zinc excretion, the use of ZnO has been phased out since June 2022 [11]. This shift has consequently spurred mounting interest in identifying cost-effective alternatives that offer comparable growth-promoting effects and long-term benefits. The aptamers, selected through systemic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment, are a unique class of ssDNA or RNA molecules capable of binding to their targets with high selectivity and affinity [12]. Moreover, aptamers offer several advantages over antibodies in identifying protein ligands due to their in vitro selection process, fully controlled chemical synthesis, and high specificity [13, 14]. These properties give them strong potential for a wide range of applications, including therapy [15–17], targeted drug delivery [18], sensors [19, 20], and diagnostic reagents [21, 22]. Over the last decade, aptamers have transitioned from basic research to commercial applications, owing to their ability to be extensively modified for improved in vivo stability and pharmacokinetics [23].

Adhesion behavior is fundamental to microbial colonization and a key prerequisite for pathogen infection. ETEC adheres to receptors on the brush border surface of small intestinal enterocytes and releases toxins that cause cellular damage and fluid secretion [24]. Aptamers can bind to their targets with high specificity and affinity. When bound to bacteria, aptamers can block some specific sites, thereby impairing bacteria motility and adhesion. To mitigate the harmful effects of pathogenic microorganisms in the intestines, most researchers have focused on the use of antimicrobial agents or probiotics rather than anti-adhesive agents. Although vitamins and probiotics have demonstrated potential as modulators of gastric microbiota in weaned piglets, the potential of aptamers to inhibit the adhesion of pathogenic bacteria to intestinal epithelial cells in pigs has not been assessed.

The intestine immune system of pigs serves as the first line of defense against microbial invasion and plays a crucial role in host metabolism. Studies have demonstrated that intestinal epithelial cells are central to the immunomodulatory functions of innate immune responses, primarily regulated by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) and inflammatory cytokines [25]. PRRs mainly include Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and intracellular NOD-like receptors (NLRs). TLRs recognize bacterial components at the cell surface or with endosomes and trigger immune responses by promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the expression of costimulatory surface molecules [26]. Continuous contact between pathogenic bacteria and epithelial cells can lead to the down-regulation of TLR expression, a mechanism that helps prevent overstimulation by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and the resulting abnormal inflammatory reactions in some tissues. When cells become infected, PAMPs are transferred into the cytoplasm, where they interact with NLRs to initiate defensive immune responses [27].

NLRs, also known as nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing proteins (NODs) or nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain and leucine-rich repeat-containing proteins, mediate innate immune responses by detecting bacterial components within the cytosol [28, 29]. Among them, NOD1 and NOD2 play a key role in recognizing peptidoglycan (PGN) fragments generated during bacterial cell wall synthesis or degradation, particularly within the gut microbiota [30, 31]. NOD1 specifically recognizes PGN-related molecules containing meso-diaminopimelic acid, a compound commonly found in Gram-negative and select Gram-positive bacteria [32]. NOD2 is activated by muramyl dipeptide, a conserved motif present in virtually all PGN types [33]. Upon activation, NOD1 and NOD2 induce gene transcription through the nuclear factor-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, initiating innate and adaptive immune responses in the host [34].

Inflammatory cytokines mainly include tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukins (IL6, IL8, and IL12), anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL10 and others), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ1), and interferons. During pathogenic bacteria infection, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukins increases significantly, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokines enhance the body’s inhibition of inflammation. TGFβ1 contributes to cell differentiation and reinforces anti-inflammatory response, whereas interferons inhibit viral replication by disrupting the viral life cycle.

In this study, to explore the effect of aptamers on inhibiting ETEC K88 adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells IPEC-J2, we investigated the affinity of the ETEC K88 strain for K88-Apt A04 (a fimbrial aptamer against ETEC K88) and K88-Apt 37 (a cell aptamer against ETEC K88). We also studied the effect of K88-Apt A04 and K88-Apt 37 on the low-concentration bacteria adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells using assays for adhesion inhibition rate and cytotoxicity, under three modes: adherence inhibition, adherence prevention, and treatment. Each condition corresponded to different methods of aptamer application. In addition, to assess changes in the inflammatory response, we examined the regulation of immunomodulatory factors in intestinal epithelial cells following bacteria challenge in the presence of aptamers. Overall, our objective was to assess the potential of aptamers as therapeutic and preventive agents for piglet infectious diarrhea and other mucosal inflammation-related diseases, while noting that the tests were conducted in a cellular model, and the results may not necessarily translate directly to animals.

Cells lines and culture conditions

The neonatal jejunal epithelial cell line IPEC-J2 was derived from a newborn, unabsorbed piglet. These cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. A.T.B Liks Lager’s Laboratory at North Carolina State University (USA). Cell growth was maintained in DMEM–F12 medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Wisenting, USA) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Invitrogen). IPEC-J2 cells were transferred from culture flasks to 96-well plates or 6-well plates (Costar Snapwells). Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sangon Biotech, China) and treated with trypsin (Genview, USA). In this in vitro system, average cell density was determined by hemacytometer counts of trypsinized cells. For the experiments, cells were seeded onto 12 mm, 96-well Costar Snapwells at a density of 4–6 × 104 cells per well in a 0.1 mL medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h before being washed. Cells were used for experiments within two days of seeding. Cells were washed 24 h before the experiment, and the medium was replaced with the same formulation described above; however, without antibiotics. All experiments were repeated three times in triplicate.

Bacteria for IPEC-J2 cell challenge

ETEC K88 (CVCC 216) was obtained from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control. ETEC K88 was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB, Sangon Biotech, China) at 37 °C, and bacterial populations were estimated by measuring optical density at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer. Growth curves of the bacterial population were established from overnight cultures. To prepare ETEC K88 without fimbriae using a heat-activated method, the bacterial cells were suspended in a 0.09 mol/L Na3PO4 solution (pH = 7.2) at a ratio of 1:10. The mixture was incubated in a 60 °C water bath for 40 min, followed by centrifugation. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium. All bacteria samples were individually pelleted, washed three times with PBS, and then resuspended in an antibiotics-free DMEM/F12 growth medium for use in the IPEC-J2 challenge. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Affinity determination of aptamer to ETEC K88

Excessive 5-Carboxyfluorescein-labeled (FAM-labeled) aptamers were added to the bacteria in 200 µL of binding buffer (Sangon Biotech, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To remove the unbound ssDNAs, we washed the sediment three times with 1 mL of PBS after centrifugation at 8000×g for 10 min, discarding the supernatant each time. The resulting bacterium–ssDNA complex was then resuspended in 200 µL PBS. The binding affinities of the two aptamers to the bacteria were determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity of the solution. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was measured using the Luminescence Spectrometer 55 (Perkin–Elmer, Germany) under the following conditions: CW-lamp energy of 15,000, prompt fluorimetry, excitation filter at 480 nm, emission filter at 514 nm, and a measurement time of 1 s). A saturation curve was generated from the data, and the binding dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated using the equation Y = BmaxX/(Kd + X) (Origin 8.0), where ‘‘Bmax’’ represents the maximal fluorescent intensity, ‘‘Y’’ represents the mean fluorescent intensity, and ‘‘X’’ denotes the concentration of the added aptamer.

Adherence assays

Bacterial adhesion assays (K88 + cell)

Treatment groups included uninfected controls and experimental groups infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1, 10, 102, 103, and 104. Confluent IPEC-J2 cells in 96-well Costar Snapwells were washed twice with PBS and then overlaid with 0.1 mL of antibiotic-free DMEM/F12 medium. Washed bacteria were added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h to allow bacterial adhesion. After incubation, unattached bacteria were removed, and wells were washed twice with 100 µL PBS. For each MOI, one set of wells was stained with crystal violet (Sangon Biotech, USA), washed five times with PBS, and examined by microscopy to assess cell morphology. A parallel set of wells was treated with 120 µL of 0.5% Triton X-100 (Solabio, USA) at room temperature for 5–10 min; cells were then fully dispersed by repeated pipetting to complete lysis. The lysates containing total cell-associated bacteria were plated onto LB agar and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to enumerate different bacteria. The adhesion rate of ETEC K88 to IPEC-J2 cells was calculated to determine the optimal bacterial concentration. At this concentration, cells were either left uninfected (control) or infected for 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 h. Adhesion rates at each time point were determined using the same plating procedure. Adherence prevention assays (Apt + cell + K88)

The adherence prevention experiment included a control group (uninfected cells) and an experimental group treated with aptamers at concentrations of 0, 20, 50, 100, and 200 nM. The aptamers were co-incubated with IPEC-J2 cells at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1.5 h. Subsequently, ETEC K88, diluted with a MOI of 10, was added to the cells pretreated with aptamers. The 96-well Costar Transwells were further incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for another 1.5 h. After incubation, unattached bacteria were removed, and the wells were washed twice with PBS. The follow-up procedures were performed according to “Adherence inhibition assays (Apt+K88+cell)”.

The treatment assays (K88 + cell + Apt)

The treatment assay design included a control group (uninfected cells) and an experimental group treated with aptamers at concentrations of 0, 20, 50, 100, and 200 nM. Before starting the experiment, IPEC-J2 cells were exposed to ETEC K88 at 37 °C for 1.5 h, after which aptamers at different concentrations were added. The 96-well Costar Transwells were further incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1.5 h. After incubation, unattached bacteria were removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The follow-up procedures were performed according to “Adherence inhibition assays (Apt+K88+cell)”.

Flow cytometry to detect bacterial adhesion rate

ETEC K88 was stained with the fluorescent dye 5,6-carboxymethyl fluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFDA-SE, Sangon Biotech, USA). A 10 µL aliquot of CFDA-SE (100 mM), diluted with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sangon Biotech, USA), was added to 1 mL of bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The bacteria were then washed five times and resuspended in PBS. A drop of the suspension was observed under a fluorescence microscope to confirm bacteria fluorescence. The stained bacteria were added to the cells at a MOI of 100 (as the low bacterial concentration resulted in the fluorescence value being too small to calculate a meaningful difference) and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After incubation, the suspension was centrifuged, and the precipitate was washed three times with PBS. This procedure ensured that only adherent bacterial cells were measured during subsequent flow cytometry analysis. The cells were detached using trypsin treatment, centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, and resuspended in 300 µL of PBS. Flow cytometry was performed using a Gallios™ instrument (Beckman counter Gallios, USA) equipped with a 15 mW argon ion laser emitting at a fixed wavelength of 488 nm. Cells were analyzed at a rate of 100–250 events per second until 1000 events in the region of interest were collected. Noise, cell debris, and non-adherent bacteria outside of the gate were excluded from the analysis. Fluorescence values were recorded using the instrument’s built-in software, and histograms were generated using Flowjo (version 10). The signal-to-background ratio (S/B) was calculated based on the fluorescence values, where ‘‘S’’ represents the fluorescence value of the experimental group and ‘‘B’’ represents the fluorescence value of the cell-free control.

Crystal violet staining and apoptosis detection

Crystal violet staining

After the adhesion assays, the supernatant solution of the 96-well plate was removed, and the wells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, a 100 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each well and incubated for 10 min. The fixative was then removed, and cells were washed with PBS four times. Crystal violet was then added and incubated for 3 min to ensure full contact with the cells, after which the excess dye was removed, and the cells were washed again. Finally, cell morphology was observed and recorded using a bright-field microscope at 400× and 1000× magnifications.

Apoptosis detection

Cell climbing slices were placed into 24-well plates, and 400 µL of cell suspension was added to each well. The cells were then cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator until confluence was reached. Serum-free medium was added to both the control and experimental groups. In addition, the experimental group was treated with K88 (MOI = 10), an aptamer, or both. After 1.5 h, CFDA-SE (final concentration: 1 µg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The dye was then removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Propionate iodine (final concentration: 2.5 µg/mL) was then added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After removing the dye and washing the cells twice, the slides were observed under a fluorescence microscope at 400× magnification.

Cytotoxic properties

Cytotoxicity was assessed using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity test kit (Beyotime, China) by measuring LDH release. Cell culture procedures followed the experimental steps described in “Cells lines and culture conditions”. Each well was assigned to one of the following groups: background control group (cell-free wells), control group (untreated cell wells), sample maximum enzyme activity control group (cells subjected to subsequent lysis), and experimental group (drug-treated cell wells). Drug treatments were administered according to the procedures described in “Adherence assays”. Since cytotoxicity was minimal at 1.5 h—making it difficult to distinguish between the experimental and control groups—the exposure time of ETEC K88 to the cells was extended from 1.5 to 3 h. All groups were then cultured under standard conditions. After 1.5 h, the culture was transferred to the Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. A total of 100 µL of the cell culture supernatant was collected and transferred to the corresponding wells of a new 96-well plate. Subsequently, 60 µL of LDH test solution was added to each well. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Dual-wavelength absorbance was measured at 492 nm and the reference wavelength at 620 nm every 3 min until 20 min. For calculating cytotoxicity, absorbance values at the 12-minute time point were used. The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula: Cytotoxicity (%) = (absorbance of treated sample– absorbance of sample control)/(absorbance of maximum enzyme activity control − absorbance of sample control well) × 100.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of TNF-α and IL-8

In this study, we followed the steps described in 2.8 and incubated the mixture with cells for 1.5 h. The cells were then centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 100 µL of the cell culture supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.25-µm filter. The protein expression levels of TNF-α and IL-8 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Sangon Biotech, China) to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Relative gene expression

IPEC-J2 cells were transferred from the culture flasks into 6-well plates. Cells were treated under the optimal conditions described in “Adherence assays”. The cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by the addition of 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) per well. They were lysed at room temperature for 5 min and then completely transferred to RNase-free Eppendorf tubes. The samples were stored at -80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

The total RNA of IPEC-J2 cells was extracted according to the instructions provided with the TRIzol reagent. The quality and quantity of RNA expression was determined by using the ultraviolet spectrophotometer. The cDNA transcription was achieved by using Reverse Transcription Master Mix (EZ Bioscience), and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was implemented by using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix. β-actin was selected as housekeeping genes and used for data normalization. All assays were performed in quadruplicate using QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis software (version 1.3.1), and a dissociation curve was analyzed after amplification. The 2–△△CT method was used to normalize the relative quantification results [35].

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance in Origin software (version 8.0). Relative gene expression data were analyzed using Image J and QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis software (version 1.3.1). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using Flow Jo (version 10).

Results

Affinity and influence of different aptamers on ETEC K88 growth

K88-Apt 37 and K88-Apt A04 were selected in our laboratory. The affinity of these aptamers for ETEC K88 was determined to be 21.68 and 34.51 nM, respectively (Fig. 1B, C). Results from the laser confocal microscope also demonstrated that both aptamers had a high affinity for ETEC K88 (Fig. 1A). However, when both aptamers were present simultaneously, their combined affinity decreased (Fig. 1D), suggesting that the reduction was likely due to competitive interference. Therefore, only one aptamer was used in the subsequent experiments. When bacteria were cultured at a concentration of 105–106 CFU/mL (Fig. 1E), there was no difference in the plate counting results, regardless of the presence or absence of the aptamers. This indicates that the two aptamers did not damage the bacteria.

Bacterial adhesion assays

The IPEC-J2 cell line is an ideal in vitro model for studying E. coli infection [4]. In the preliminary experiment, we co-cultured four concentrations of ETEC K88 with IPEC-J2 cells for 1 h. Crystal violet staining and microscopic analysis revealed that the ETEC K88 can adhere to cells (Fig. 2B).

Subsequently, we investigated the mechanism of IPEC-J2 cell damage caused by ETEC K88. Bacterial adhesion assays revealed that the adhesion rate decreased with increasing bacterial concentration (Fig. 2A, C). The adhesion rate was significantly higher at an MOI of 10, suggesting the possibility of quorum sensing at high bacterial concentrations. However, this observation may also be influenced by receptor saturation or other factors. Thus, further experimental validation is required to confirm this hypothesis. Cytotoxicity was calculated by detecting LDH release, and the assays exhibited that the cytotoxicity percentage increased with the rise in bacterial concentration (Fig. 2C). In conclusion, due to the high adhesion rate, low cytotoxicity, and the absence of reference data at extremely low concentrations, the ETEC K88-IPEC-J2 interaction model simulating pig intestinal infection was established at an MOI of 10.

To determine the optimal time of action, cytotoxicity (Fig. 2D) and crystal violet staining (Fig. 2E) were observed in 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h, respectively, at a concentration of 10 MOI. When the bacterial adhesion rate was too high, the cells detached, which interfered with the statistical results of adhesion. Conversely, when the adhesion rate was too low, the cytotoxicity differences were too small to be statistically significant. Based on these observations, we selected 10 MOI for subsequent assays, measuring the adhesion rate of 1.5 h and cytotoxicity at 3 h after incubation to simulate the early stage of intestinal infection in piglets.

Effect of aptamers on ETEC K88 adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells

It is well known that medications can treat piglet diarrhea either by directly killing pathogens or by disrupting their interaction with the intestinal epithelium. Aptamers have been explored as targeting molecules by modifying specific drugs to combat pathogenic bacteria; however, their role in inhibiting bacterial adhesion has not yet been reported. Therefore, we studied the effect of aptamer in preventing ETEC K88 adhesion to cells, focusing on three modes of action—adherence inhibition, adherence prevention, and therapeutic intervention—each corresponding to a different method of aptamer application.

In the adherence inhibition assays (Fig. 3A, B), when the aptamers were incubated with ETEC K88, the inhibition rate of bacterial adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells increased progressively with higher aptamer concentrations. The highest inhibition rate (79.04%) was observed at an aptamer concentration of 100 nM. Meanwhile, cytotoxicity decreased with increasing aptamer concentration. K88-Apt 37 revealed the lowest cytotoxicity (8.77%) at 50 nM, whereas K88-Apt A04 indicated the lowest cytotoxicity (5.18%) at 100 nM. We speculated that aptamers at optimal concentration can bind to ETEC K88 and inhibit its adhesion to cells. However, aptamers may exhibit slight cytotoxicity due to their oligonucleotide nature, as they can come into direct contact with cells rather than binding exclusively to bacterial targets. When the aptamer concentration exceeds the optimal level, this non-specific interaction may increase cytotoxicity.

Subsequently, we did the adherence prevention experiment to investigate whether pre-treatment with aptamers could prevent intestinal epithelial cells from pathogenic bacterial attack. Similar to the adherence inhibition assays, the inhibition rate increased progressively with higher aptamer concentrations and peaked at 100 nM (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, cell cytotoxicity was lowest at an aptamer concentration of 20 nM, but was increased sharply when the concentration exceeded 20 nM (Fig. 3D). We speculated that in the pre-addition approach, where cells were incubated with the aptamer for 1.5 h, higher aptamer concentrations caused greater cell damage due to the inherent cytotoxicity of the aptamer. This finding suggests that aptamers should be administered at low concentrations when used as preventative agents. In this in vitro model, decreased concentrations of aptamers were correlated with diminished cytotoxicity, which suggests their potential as prophylactic agents, though further in vivo validation is required.

To verify the therapeutic potential of the aptamers in reducing bacterial adhesion, we conducted treatment assays. The results found that the adhesion inhibition rate increased with higher aptamer concentrations. Although the inhibition rate was lower compared with the previous two assays, it remained notable, reaching up to 40%. However, cytotoxicity levels were relatively low, likely because ETEC K88 had already adhered to the cell surface and caused a significant cytotoxicity effect before the aptamer was introduced. These findings also suggest that K88-Apt A04 may more effectively displace ETEC K88 and induce less cytotoxicity than K88-Apt 37 (Fig. 3E, F).

In summary, the experimental results from the three different aptamer application methods indicate that aptamers can inhibit bacterial adhesion to cells and reduce cytotoxicity. In terms of adhesion inhibition, the effect followed this order: adherence inhibition assays (79.04%) > adherence prevention assays (69.79%) > treatment assays (48.14%). A similar trend was observed for cytotoxicity reduction using K88-Apt A04: adherence inhibition assays (5.8%) > adherence prevention assays (13.57%) > treatment assays (15.18%). K88-Apt 37 indicated a slightly different pattern: adherence prevention assays (7.14%) > adherence inhibition assays (8.77%) > treatment assays (13.55%).

Subsequently, we explored whether fimbria plays a vital role in the adhesion of ETEC K88 to cells. Following the protocols described in “Adherence inhibition assays (Apt+K88+cell)”, we performed the assay using ETEC K88 strains with all fimbriae removed. The number of adherent bacteria was extremely low—approximately 10 bacteria per well—rendering the adhesion inhibition rate statistically significant. Additionally, the cytotoxicity remained minimal after 3 h of co-incubation. These findings suggest that the absence of fimbria greatly reduced or even eliminated ETEC K88’s ability to adhere to cells (Fig. S1). Notably, K88-Apt 37 combined with fimbria-deficient ETEC K88 caused higher cytotoxicity to cells than K88-Apt A04 under the same conditions—an outcome that contrasts with our previous assays. This suggests that K88-Apt 37 may be inherently more cytotoxic to cells than K88-Apt A04, although the other factors may also contribute to this difference.

We calculated the affinity of the two aptamers for ETEC K88 lacking fimbria to explain why K88-Apt 37 caused higher cytotoxicity (Figs. S2, S3). K88-Apt A04 maintained a high affinity for the no-fimbrial strain, similar to that observed with the standard strain, whereas K88-Apt 37 indicated increased curve error. This suggests that the higher cytotoxicity of K88-Apt 37 may stem from its inability to bind effectively to fimbrial-deficient ETEC K88, unlike K88-Apt A04, which may still interact through cell-targeting mechanisms.

Finally, we compared the cytotoxicity resulting from different orders of adding two aptamers. As the adhesion inhibition rate was higher, a slightly excess concentration (100 nM) of aptamers was selected for subsequent experiments. Under different aptamer addition sequences at concentrations of 10 MOI, the cytotoxicity revealed a similar trend after 1.5 and 3 h of treatment (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, the pre-addition of K88-Apt 37 produced even higher cytotoxicity than the control group at 10 MOI (Fig. 4A), whereas the cytotoxicity was significantly reduced at 100 MOI (Fig. 4C). Among the various addition methods, the order of cytotoxicity inhibition was as follows: adherence inhibition assays > adherence prevention assays > treatment assays (Fig. 4E). Moreover, both K88-Apt 37 and K88-Apt A04 showed greater effectiveness at lower bacterial concentrations. At higher concentrations, however, K88-Apt 37 outperformed K88-Apt A04. We then compared the cytotoxicity of different interaction models at various time points, and the results supported these observations (Fig. 4D). In summary, both aptamers reduce the cytotoxicity caused by ETEC K88, with K88-Apt 37 being more effective at low concentrations and K88-Apt A04 at high concentrations.

Flow cytometry to detect bacterial adhesion rate

The adhesion rate of ETEC K88 to IPEC-J2 cells was measured by flow cytometry to evaluate the potential protective effect of the aptamer against bacterial challenge. Fluorescence microscopy was then used to observe the fluorescently stained ETEC K88 (Fig. 5A) and the adhesion of the bacteria to IPEC-J2 cells (Fig. 5B). The results indicated that the bacteria were successfully stained and retained their ability to adhere to cells, causing the cells to become fluorescent. Moreover, Fig. 5C and D illustrate that both the aptamers reduced bacterial adhesion to the cells, with K88-Apt 37 demonstrating a stronger inhibitory effect. S/B-1 represents the relative adhesion after correction with controls, indicating the ratio of bacteria adhering to the cells. A higher S/B indicates greater bacteria adhesion to the cells. In Fig. 5E, a significant decrease was observed in the number of bacteria adhering to the cells. As illustrated in Fig. 5E, a significant decrease in adhesion was observed 1.5 h after the addition of aptamers. In contrast, pre-treatment aptamers resulted in the weakest inhibition of adhesion, likely due to the mild cytotoxicity effect causing cell damage. However, co-incubation for 3 h (Fig. 5F) indicated a stronger inhibitory effect, indicating that the aptamers exert their influence primarily during the early stages of bacterial adhesion.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for detecting TNF-α and IL-8

We measured the levels of TNF-α and IL-8 using TNF-α ELISA and IL-8 ELISA Kits. Currently, IL-8 is a well-studied pro-inflammatory cytokine, and previous studies have indicated that IL-8 is involved in the inflammatory response of intestinal cells to pathogenic infection [36, 37]. TNF-α is another key cytokine known for its role in increasing the permeability of intestinal epithelial cells. Our results revealed that higher bacterial concentrations or prolonged exposure time led to increased TNF-α expression and a slight decrease in IL-8 levels (Fig. 6A, B).

Future research should explore the effects of K88-Apt 37 and K88-Apt A04 on early bacterial adhesion to cells by measuring IL-8 and TNF-α expressions induced in IPEC-J2 cells. As illustrated in Fig. 6C, ETEC K88 significantly increased TNF-α expression in IPEC-J2 cells compared to untreated cells, indicating its ability to stimulate an inflammatory response. In the absence of bacterial challenge, both aptamers induced mild cytokine expression, which may be related to non-specific cellular activation. ETEC K88 induced lower TNF-α levels when incubated with K88-Apt 37 or K88-Apt A04 compared to the bacteria alone. Moreover, K88-Apt A04 resulted in lower TNF-α expression than K88-Apt 37, indicating that K88-Apt A04 has lower cytotoxicity. Furthermore, the adherence prevention assays were more effective than adherence inhibition or treatment assays. In addition, even in the presence of aptamer, the expression level of IL8 slightly decreased (Fig. 6D).

Gene expression of PPRs and inflammatory factors

Finally, we analyzed several major inflammatory factors produced by intestinal epithelial cells based on their mRNA expression levels. PRRs play a key role in identifying microbial stimuli and are recognized by different intracellular and extracellular receptors depending on the type of microorganism [38]. This recognition can lead to the overexpression of inflammatory factors in intestinal epithelial cells, leading to inflammatory damage. To examine the expression of PRRs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, we measured the mRNA expression levels of TLR6, NOD1, NOD2, IL-6, IL-8, and TGFβ1 using PCR (Figs. S4, S5, S6) and quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (Fig. 7). For PRRs, as indicated in Fig. S5, increasing the concentration of ETEC K88 significantly decreased the expression of NOD1 and NOD2 while increasing TLR6 expression. For pro-inflammatory cytokines, as exhibited in Fig. S6, IL6 expression was the lowest at a MOI of 100. IL8 and TGFβ1 exhibited similar expression trends, with both indicating slightly higher levels at an MOI of 10. However, IL8 expression (approximately 1.5-fold) was generally higher than that of TGFβ1 (approximately 1-fold), suggesting that TGFβ1 may be weakly expressed or even suppressed. Overall, the expression levels of PRRs and pro-inflammatory cytokines peaked at an MOI of 104. As demonstrated in Fig. S6, compared with the 100 MOI group, most genes (except NOD1) exhibited higher expression of and greater differences at 10 MOI when the incubation time was extended.

To assess the effect of K88-Apt-37 and K88-Apt-A04 on ETEC K88 adhesion to cells under different interaction modes, we obtained cycle threshold (Ct) values and calculated gene expression levels using qPCR to determine the expression of PRRs and pro-inflammatory cytokines. The Ct values for the kinetic amplification plot of β-actin, TGFβ1, NOD2, IL8, TLR6, NOD1, and IL6 mRNA were mostly below 30 cycles, indicating good repeatability (Fig. S6). Additionally, the melt curve plot indicated single peaks, confirming primer specificity (Fig. S7). These results suggest that the fluorescence quantification result was credible.

For the fimbrial aptamer, the addition of K88-Apt-37 to IPEC-J2 cells alone significantly increased the expression level of TLR-6, NOD1, and IL-6. However, when co-infected with bacteria, K88-Apt-37 downregulated the expression of TLR-6, NOD1, NOD2, and IL8 in IPEC-J2 cells (Fig. 7A, B). This suggests that although K88-Apt-37 exhibits cytotoxicity, it can reduce bacterial adhesion to cells. In contrast, for cell-targeting aptamers, K88-Apt-A04 alone did not significantly induce inflammatory factors (Fig. 7C, D). However, its protective effect against bacterial-induced damage in IPEC-J2 cells was weaker than that of K88-Apt-37, except in the adherence prevention of assays.

In conclusion, our analysis of major inflammatory factors in IPEC-J2 cells induced by ETEC K88 found that both K88-Apt A04 and K88-Apt 37 could reduce the cytotoxicity caused by bacterial attack. Although the aptamers exhibited mild toxicity, they were able to slow the rate of bacterial adhesion to cells, thereby significantly reducing the secretion of pattern recognition receptors and pro-inflammatory cytokines in IPEC-J2 cells.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed two aptamers (K88-Apt 37 and K88-Apt A04) as potential therapeutic agents for inhibiting bacterial adhesion to the intestinal epithelial cell line IPEC-J2. We employed three aptamer administration methods—adhesion inhibition assays, adhesion prevention assays, and therapeutic assays—which referred to three typical treatment methods: pre-infection administration, post-infection administration, and optimization administration.

Some studies have reported that high concentrations of aptamers exhibit bactericidal effects [39]. However, the concentration of aptamers used in our experiments was too low to exert a significant bactericidal effect, while excessively high concentrations could cause cellular damage. The observed reduction in bacterial adhesion is likely due to the ability of cell-targeting aptamers to bind specific sites on the bacterial surface, thereby interfering with certain physiological functions of the ETEC K88. In contrast, the fimbrial-targeting aptamer may bind directly to fimbriae, affecting bacterial motility and attachment. Since there are many fimbriae on the surface of ETEC K88, the binding sites of fimbrial aptamers are also more, which explains why the inhibitory adhesion effect of K88-Apt 37 is better than K88-Apt A04.

Both aptamers tested in this study were able to reduce the adhesion of ETEC K88 to cells. Because adhesion is the initial step in the infection process, reducing it can delay and weaken the subsequent attack of pathogenic bacteria on cells, thereby mitigating cytotoxic effects. Nevertheless, additives that reduce ETEC adhesion do not necessarily prevent the destruction of the cell monolayer [40]. Thus, a reduction in bacterial adhesion alone is not a decisive indicator of an aptamer’s potential to prevent infection. The aptamers can be modified with different drugs to reduce bacterial adhesion and enable targeted delivery of drugs to specific sites for the elimination of pathogenic bacteria. In this study, we investigated the role of aptamers in inhibiting bacterial adhesion on cells, providing preliminary data to support the potential of aptamers as carriers for functional drugs in clinical treatment. In future work, we plan to conjugate aptamers with high-efficiency drugs to modify drug utilization and reduce bacterial resistance.

We evaluated the effects of aptamers on the morphological structure and cellular response of IPEC-J2 cells after bacterial exposure. Although these in vitro assays provide preliminary insights into the effects of aptamers on the intestinal epithelium, in vivo validation is necessary to confirm these findings. This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted entirely in vitro, limiting the applicability of the findings to in vivo systems, particularly the porcine gastrointestinal system. Second, the effective concentrations of aptamers observed in vitro may not directly translate to in vivo conditions due to physiological differences. Third, while aptamers demonstrated the ability to reduce bacterial adhesion, their potential for non-specific interactions with epithelial cells could lead to off-target effects that were not assessed herein. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate the impact of aptamer treatment on the intestinal microbiota or the integrity of the intestinal barrier, which are critical factors for assessing the safety and practicality of aptamer-based therapies. Addressing these challenges could pave the way for the development of aptamers as nucleic acid-based therapeutics for clinical use.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that both aptamers (K88-Apt 37 and K88-Apt A04) are capable of inhibiting bacterial adhesion to cells. We further investigated the inhibitory effects of three distinct aptamer administration approaches. In addition, we measured changes in pattern recognition receptors and cytokines levels to understand how aptamers influence the secretion of inflammatory factors in cells infected with ETEC K88. Prior to the in vitro challenge, we studied the impact of aptamers on pathogenic bacteria. Our results demonstrated that neither aptamer was effective in inhibiting ETEC K88 growth. However, K88-Apt37 reduced bacteria adhesion to cells by up to 80%, while K88-Apt A04 reduced the cytotoxicity by approximately 70% (from 19.89 to 5.80%). Remarkably, although K88-Apt 37 was more effective at reducing bacterial adhesion than K88-Apt A04, the latter exhibited lower cytotoxicity. Therefore, a low concentration of K88-Apt 37 or a high concentration of K88-Apt A04 can be effectively used to reduce bacterial adhesion. Among the three aptamer addition methods tested, the adherence inhibition assay indicated the strongest effect, followed by adherence prevention assay and then the treatment assay. In addition, we measured the expression levels of pattern recognition receptors and pro-inflammatory cytokines in cells challenged with ETEC K88 strains to understand how aptamers affect bacterial adhesion at the gene expression level. The results indicated that K88-Apt A04 and K88-Apt 37 have different effects on the adhesion of ETEC K88 to cells, and both aptamers were able to downregulate the expression of PRRs and pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that co-incubation with aptamers can reduce ETEC K88 adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells and modulate inflammatory responses in vitro. Based on our findings, we recommend administering the aptamers before infection, as the highest adhesion inhibition rate of 69.79% was observed in the adherence prevention assay. Furthermore, increasing the concentration of aptamers can reduce the adhesion rate of bacterial adhesion to cells after infection.
