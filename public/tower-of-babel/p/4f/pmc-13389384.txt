BRCA1P1 immunotherapy requires pseudogene-aware transcriptomics
Authors: Hao Chi, Jiayu Xu, Youping Deng
Published: 2026

Han et al. identify BRCA1P1 as an immunoregulatory pseudogene-derived RNA whose inhibition activates antiviral programs and antitumor immunity in human cancers (1, 2). The study is important because it moves pseudogene biology from genomic annotation toward therapeutic mechanism. We suggest, however, that the path from BRCA1P1 biology to a broadly targetable cancer immunotherapy axis requires a validation framework that is explicit about isoform identity, tumor-cell origin, and RNA-perturbation specificity.

First, BRCA1P1 is an unusually demanding target for transcriptomic interpretation. It is a fusion pseudogene related to BRCA1 and RPLP1, and Han et al. report that most BRCA1P1 transcripts are circular RNAs. Short-read RNA sequencing can be uncertain for both pseudogene-derived transcripts and circular RNAs because homologous sequence, multimapping reads, backsplice assignment, library selection, and reverse-transcription artifacts can affect apparent abundance (3, 4). For therapeutic development, the relevant unit should therefore be the targetable BRCA1P1 isoform or junction, not only locus-level expression. Junction-specific assays, RNase R and poly(A)-selection comparisons, targeted long-read sequencing, and rescue with isoform-defined constructs would make the target identity more robust.

Second, patient-tumor transcriptomes need cell-type resolution before BRCA1P1 expression is interpreted as a tumor-cell-intrinsic immune rheostat. Tumor purity, immune infiltration, stromal abundance, copy-number state, and molecular subtype can all reshape associations between noncoding RNA abundance and immune programs in bulk samples (5, 6). Single-cell or single-nucleus RNA profiling, spatial transcriptomics, and tumor-cell deconvolution could test whether BRCA1P1-high malignant cells are the same cells in which nuclear factor kappa B driven antiviral genes are suppressed, and whether BRCA1P1 depletion rewires malignant cells before macrophage phagocytosis and T cell recruitment.

Third, RNA-targeting experiments and therapeutics can themselves engage innate immune sensors. Synthetic small interfering RNAs and related RNA perturbations may induce interferon and inflammatory programs in a sequence-, chemistry-, and delivery-dependent manner (7). Orthogonal perturbations, chemistry-matched controls, dose–response studies, and junction- or isoform-specific rescue are therefore essential to separate BRCA1P1-specific biology from generic RNA-perturbation-induced antiviral signaling.

These requirements do not weaken the significance of Han et al.’s discovery. They define the evidentiary path that could make BRCA1P1 clinically actionable: a targetable isoform, a malignant-cell source, preserved antiviral pathway competence, and a spatial immune context in which BRCA1P1 inhibition can plausibly convert tumor-intrinsic viral mimicry into antitumor immunity.
