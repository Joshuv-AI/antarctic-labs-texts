Multienzyme supplementation alleviates the performance of hens fed diets with lower crude protein and energy levels during peak to late production phase
Authors: Jun Young Mun, Habeeb Tajudeen, Priscilla Neves Silvestre, Sang Hun Ha, Abdolreza Hosseindoust, Anushka Lokhande, Santosh Ingale, Jin Soo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of supplementing a multienzyme complex on the laying performance, nutrient digestibility, cecal microflora, and digesta viscosity of laying hens fed corn-soybean meal (corn–SBM) and wheat diets. In the 12 weeks trial, 240 37-week Hy-Line Brown layers with an average body weight of 1.99 + 0.5 kg sourced from GANONGBIO, 151, Mageum-gil, Gasan-myeon, Pocheon-si, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea were partitioned into 4 treatments as 1. CON-: basal diet without multienzyme. 2. CON+: basal diet with 0.035 % multienzyme. 3. Low nutrient-: basal diet reduced by 100 Kcal/kg ME and 0.05 % CP without multienzyme. 3. Low nutrient+: basal diet reduced by 100 Kcal/kg ME and 0.05 % CP with 0.035 % multienzyme. Each treatment consisted of 15 replicates with 4 hens per replicate in a completely randomized design. Results showed body weight, average daily feed intake, and average egg weight remained unaffected across treatments. However, hens in the Low nutrient+ group showed higher (p < 0.05) hen-day egg production and egg mass, with a lower feed conversion ratio, comparable to birds in CON+. Egg and eggshell quality parameters were not influenced by the treatments. Nutrient digestibility results revealed higher (p < 0.05) dry matter digestibility in the Low nutrient+ and CON+, while no differences were observed for gross energy, crude protein, ether extract, ash, or amino acid digestibility. Non-starch polysaccharide digestibility and inositol release were improved (p < 0.05) in the Low nutrient+, although xylan digestibility remained unchanged. Cecal Lactobacillus spp. counts increased (p < 0.05), and digesta viscosity was reduced (p < 0.05) in the Low nutrient+ and CON+ groups, indicating enhanced gut health. We concluded that the multienzyme complex at 0.035 % improved performance and nutrient digestibility in hens fed corn-wheat-SBM diets reduced by 100 kcal ME and 0.5 % CP. This suggests multienzyme supplementation can lower feed cost without affecting productivity.Keywords: Egg production, Enzymes, Digesta viscosity, Microbiota

Introduction

Feed cost constitutes the largest expenditure in poultry production, accounting for up to 70 % of total operational costs (Akter et al., 2025; Tiruneh et al., 2025). Among feed components, metabolizable energy (ME) and crude protein (CP) are critical for optimizing the productivity of laying hens, particularly during the peak production phase when nutrient demands are the highest (Hwang et al., 2025; Tajudeen et al., 2025). However, rising costs and increasing competition for feed resources have spurred interest in strategies that can maintain performance while reducing dietary energy and protein levels (Jat et al., 2025; Prates et al., 2025).

Non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) including arabinoxylans, β-glucans, mannans, pectins, and cellulose are structurally diverse components of plant-based feed ingredients, making a single exogenous enzyme insufficient to hydrolyze all NSP fractions present in the diet. Therefore, the use of a multi-enzyme complex is necessary to target the different types of NSP simultaneously. Exogenous multienzyme supplementation is a promising approach to enhance nutrient utilization under dietary restrictions (Mun et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2024). Enzymes containing amylase, β-glucanase, cellulase, mannanase, pectinase, phytase, protease, and xylanase have been widely shown to improve feed efficiency in hens by degrading anti-nutritional NSP, and increasing amino acid (AA) availability (Huang et al., 2024; Valente et al., 2024).

Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of multienzymes in hens under low-nutrient conditions, showing improvements in egg production, feed conversion ratio (FCR), and nutrient retention (Choi et al., 2024; Juan et al., 2024; Mun et al., 2024). However, limited data are available on the impact of multienzyme supplementation in laying hens fed diets simultaneously reduced in both energy and protein during peak production. Given that nutrient demand and metabolic stress are the highest during this period, optimizing nutrient efficiency without compromising performance is both economically and biologically crucial (Choi et al., 2024; Mun et al., 2024)

We hypothesize that multienzyme supplementation will mitigate the adverse effects of nutrient reduction by enhancing nutrient availability and intestinal efficiency. Thus, the present study aims to investigate the effect of a multienzyme complex containing blends of mannanase, xylanase, cellulase, pectinase, β-glucanase, amylase, protease, and phytase on the performance, egg quality, cecal microflora, digesta viscosity, and nutrient utilization of laying hens fed diets reduced by 100 kcal/kg ME and 0.5 % CP during their peak to late production phase.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

This experiment was conducted at Kangwon National University, Chuncheon-Si, South Korea. The procedures were ethically approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (KW-240411-2).

Enzyme information

The multienzyme used in this study was produced through the fermentation of selected strains of Bacillus licheniformis, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Trichoderma citrinoviride, Pichia pastoris, Aspergillus niger, and Trichoderma citrinoviride. The powdery DigeGrain Delta Plus donated by Advanced Enzyme Ltd, Thane India contains 800,000 BAU/kg amylase, 500,000 BGU/kg ß-glucanase, 3,000,000 CMC/kg cellulase, 2,500,000 MNU/kg mannanase, 1,000,000 PBU/kg pectinase, 1,000,000 FTU/kg phytase, 1,000,000 PCU/kg protease, and 600,000 XU/kg xylanase.

Experimental design, animals, housing, and diet formulation

This experiment involved a sum of 240, 37 weeks Hy-Line Brown layers with an average body weight (BW) of 1.99 ± 0.5 kg sourced from GANONGBIO, 151, Mageum-gil, Gasan-myeon, Pocheon-si, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea. The birds were partitioned into 4 treatments as CON- (basal diet without the multienzyme), CON+ (basal diet + 0.035 % multienzyme supplementation), Low nutrient– (diet reduced by 100 Kcal/kg ME and 0.05 % CP without multienzyme), Low nutrient+ (diet reduced by 100 Kcal/kg ME and 0.05 % CP + 0.035 % multienzyme). In the enzyme supplemented group, the diets were formulated to contain the following expected enzyme activity per kilogram of diet: 280 BAU amylase, 175 BGU β-glucanase, 1050 CMCU cellulase, 875 MNU mannanase, 350 PBU pectinase, 350 FTU phytase, 350 PCU protease, and 210 XU xylanase. Enzyme recovery analysis confirmed that the supplemented enzymes were retained in the diets. The recovery values were 291 BAU amylase, 179 BGU β-glucanase, 1125 CMCU cellulase, 944 MNU mannanase, 381 PBU pectinase, 375 FTU phytase, 405 PCU protease, and 236 XU xylanase for the CON+ diet, and 305 BAU amylase, 201 BGU β-glucanase, 1154 CMCU cellulase, 916 MNU mannanase, 375 PBU pectinase, 406 FTU phytase, 358 PCU protease, and 255 XU xylanase for the Low nutrient+ diet. Each treatment consisted of 15 replicates with 4 hens per replicate in a completely randomized design. The 12-week experiment was managed in accordance with Hy-Line (2023). In brief, the birds were kept in pens with temperatures between 20 and 22 ℃, relative humidity of 65 %, 16 h light cycle and 8 h dark period. Spaces of 1225 cm2/hen were provided and enriched with sand bath, next, and perches. The nutritional composition of the feeds supplied in marsh form is shown in Table 1 and was supplied ad libitum with water.

The egg quality parameters including Haugh unit, yolk color, yolk weight, and albumin weight were evaluated using the egg multi-tester (Touhoku Rhythm Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

For eggshell qualities, the eggshell thickness was measured using the micrometer eggshell thickness gauge (Baxlo PRECISION, Barcelona, Spain). Nutrient digestibility

To evaluate the effects of the treatments on nutrient digestibility, the diets were incorporated with a 0.25 % chromium oxide indigestible marker during the last 7 days of each phase. Excreta samples were afterwards collected randomly during the last 3 days from each replicate (4 hens/replicate). All feed and excreta samples were promptly stored at −20 °C until further analysis. For each treatment, samples were oven-dried at 55 °C for 72 h using a forced-air system, then ground with an electric feed grinder (Thomas Model 4 Wiley Mill, Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, USA) and passed through a 1-mm mesh sieve. These processed samples were subsequently analyzed in triplicate for dry matter (DM) (930.15), CP (990.03), ash (942.05), and ether extract (EE) (920.39) using standard AOAC (2007) procedures. Gross energy (GE) was determined using a bomb calorimeter (Model 1261, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA), while AA profiles were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (Waters 486, Waters Corp., Milford, MA) following acid hydrolysis as described by Ha et al. (2024). Methionine and cysteine levels were analyzed post-performic acid oxidation (Moore, 1963), and tryptophan content was determined via alkaline hydrolysis (Ha et al., 2024). The NSP, including xylan, were quantified using the method by Englyst et al. (1987). In addition, myo-inositol concentrations in both feed and excreta were quantified using high-performance anion exchange chromatography coupled with pulsed amperometric detection, following acid hydrolysis as described by Ellingson et al. (2012). Chromium concentration was assessed with an automated spectrophotometer (Jasco V-650, Jasco Corp., Tokyo, Japan) following Fenton and Fenton (1979). Cecal microflora composition

At the end of the experiment, the hens were restricted from feeds for 12 h, and (4 hens/replicate) were selected at random for euthanasia. The hens were anesthetized by intramuscular injection of medetomidine (50 μg/kg; Domitor, Pfizer, Finland). Immediately full sedation was achieved; euthanasia was carried out via cervical dislocation. The entire procedure was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to the guidelines set by the American Veterinary Medical Association for the humane euthanasia of animals. The ceca were afterwards aseptically removed, and their contents were collected for microbiological analysis. Cecal contents from each replicate group were pooled and transferred into sterile flasks containing 90 mL of pre-reduced salt medium under a continuous CO₂ stream to preserve anaerobic conditions. Serial dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁷) of the samples were prepared and plated on selective media to enumerate bacterial populations. Culture media and reagents were sourced from Difco Laboratories (Detroit, USA). Bacterial enumeration was conducted as follows:

Lactobacillus spp.: Cultured on De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar supplemented with 0.200 g/L sodium azide and 0.500 g/L L-cystine hydrochloride monohydrate; incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Bifidobacterium spp.: Cultured on MRS agar enriched with nalidixic acid, paromomycin, neomycin sulfate, and lithium chloride (MRS-NPNL); incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Enterococcus spp.: Grown on Slanetz and Bartley agar under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. Clostridium perfringens: Cultured on Tryptose Sulfite Cycloserine (TSC) agar (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK); incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Escherichia coli (E. coli) and coliforms: Both were cultured on Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial populations were reported as log₁₀ colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of cecal content, following the procedure described by Hosseindoust et al. (2017).

Digesta viscosity

The viscosity of digesta collected from euthanized hens was assessed based on the procedure outlined by Laerke et al. (2015). In summary, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for analysis. Measurements were conducted using a Brookfield DV-II Cone/Plate Viscometer (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories Inc., Stoughton, MA), maintained at 39 °C. Shear rates ranged from 2.25 to 450 s⁻¹, with viscosity specifically recorded at 45 s⁻¹ and reported in millipascal seconds (mPa·s) for consistency across samples.

Statistical analysis

A two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary nutrient levels (standard vs. nutrient-deficient) and multienzyme supplementation (0 % vs. 0.035 %) on laying performance and egg quality parameters of 37-weeks Hy-Line Brown laying hens over a 12-week experimental period. Measurements were taken at multiple time points from the same experimental units to assess the main effects of diet, enzyme inclusion, and their interactions. For other parameters such as gut microflora and digesta viscosity, a two-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate nutrient and enzyme effects. Where significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), Tukey multiple range test was used to separate means. Results are reported as means ± standard error.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that multienzyme supplementation alleviated the negative impacts of reduced dietary ME and CP on laying hen performance during the peak production phase. Despite a deliberate dietary reduction of 100 kcal/kg ME and 0.5 % CP, hens receiving the multienzyme-supplemented nutrient-deficient diet maintained comparable HDEP, EM, and FCR to those on the standard diet, highlighting the efficacy of enzyme inclusion in supporting production under nutritional constraints. Similar improvements in performance with multienzyme supplementation under restricted diets were reported by Mun et al. (2024), suggesting that enzymes play a compensatory role when nutrient supply is limited.

The observed improvements in the ATTD of DM and NSP in the enzyme-supplemented groups align with previous findings that multienzymes can disrupt plant cell wall matrices, facilitating enhanced access to encapsulated nutrients (Palafox-Rivera et al., 2025; Whiting et al., 2025). These beneficial effects are attributable to the enzymatic activities of the multienzyme complex, which contains carbohydrase (xylanase, β-glucanase, cellulase, mannanase, pectinase), amylase, protease, and phytase. These enzymes act synergistically to hydrolyze complex polysaccharides, proteins, and phytate-bound compounds, improving nutrient availability and utilization in hens (Mun et al., 2024; Valente et al., 2024).

In this study, the basal diet contained corn, wheat, and SBM ingredients known to contain substantial levels of NSP including arabinoxylans, β-glucans, mannans, cellulose, and pectin, along with phytic acid and protein-bound nutrients. These fibrous and anti-nutritional components can limit nutrient digestibility and absorption, especially under nutrient-deficient conditions (Gaviley et al., 2025; Ha et al., 2024). Our experimental diets were analyzed to contain 13.15 % and 13.12 % total NSP, including measurable amounts of mannose (0.19 % and 0.21 %) and xylose (0.22 % and 0.20), which are substrates targeted by several of the supplemented enzymes. Notably, wheat contributes soluble arabinoxylans that increase digesta viscosity and hinder nutrient absorption (Laerke et al., 2015; Boukid et al., 2024). These effects are particularly important when birds are fed suboptimal diets, as enzymes can compensate for nutrient deficiencies by improving digestibility and nutrient release (Ebeid et al., 2025; Ha et al., 2024; Mun et al., 2024). The enzyme complex used in this study was specifically designed to hydrolyze these polysaccharides, thereby improving gut health and nutrient utilization. Xylanase, a major component of the enzyme blend hydrolyzes these arabinoxylans, reducing digesta viscosity and enhancing nutrient diffusion (Huang et al., 2025). β-Glucanase further contributes to the enzymatic hydrolysis of NSP by targeting β-glucans in wheat (Gaviley et al., 2025). Mannans and pectin, prevalent in SBM, are effectively degraded by mannanase and pectinase, respectively, improving access to entrapped nutrients (Mun et al., 2024). Cellulase facilitates the breakdown of cellulose in plant cell walls, releasing encapsulated starches, proteins, and lipids (Shuaib et al., 2025). Amylase enhances starch hydrolysis from corn and wheat, increasing glucose availability and energy utilization (Liu et al., 2025). The Protease component of the multienzyme can improve protein digestibility, particularly of proteins tightly bound within SBM matrices, while phytase hydrolyzes phytate in the corn and SBM, releasing bound phosphorus and other chelated minerals (Chisoro et al., 2025; Kayan et al., 2025).

Interestingly, while xylan digestibility was not significantly altered, the release of myo-inositol was markedly improved in both standard and nutrient-deficient enzyme-supplemented diets. This suggests that enzyme-induced hydrolysis of phytate or NSP-bound inositol improved its bioavailability, which may enhance energy metabolism and antioxidant capacity in laying hens (Ellingson et al., 2012; Park et al., 2024). Moreover, multienzyme supplementation supported gut health, evidenced by an increased cecal population of Lactobacillus spp. A healthier microbial balance is known to promote nutrient digestion and immune modulation (Hosseindoust et al., 2017), reinforcing the holistic benefits of enzyme inclusion.

Although the egg quality and eggshell characteristics were not significantly affected by enzyme supplementation or nutrient reduction. Our finding suggests these traits are either resilient to moderate nutritional stress or that enzyme supplementation preserved egg integrity under such conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the multienzyme complex containing amylase, β-glucanase, cellulase, mannanase, pectinase, phytase, protease, and xylanase supplemented at 0.035 % mitigated performance losses and improved nutrient digestibility in laying hens fed corn-wheat-SBM-based diets reduced in 100 kcal ME and 0.5 % lower CP. These results support our hypothesis that enzyme supplementation is a promising nutritional strategy for reducing feed costs without compromising productivity during the peak laying period. Future work should evaluate whether smaller enzyme combinations could provide similar benefits with greater economic efficiency.
