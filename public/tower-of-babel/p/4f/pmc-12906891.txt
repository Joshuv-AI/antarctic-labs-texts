Analysis of the Risk of Neoplasm and Malignancy of Salivary Gland Neoplasm of Uncertain Malignant Potential (SUMP): From the Perspective of Cytomorphological Subgroups
Authors: Enver Yarikkaya, Senay Erdogan Durmus, Elis Kangal, Selma Sengiz Erhan, Cem Comunoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the risk of neoplasm (RON) and the risk of malignancy (ROM) in cases diagnosed as salivary gland neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential (SUMP), with subclassification based on cytomorphologic features.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on salivary gland fine-needle aspiration (FNA) specimens diagnosed as SUMP between 2018 and 2022 in a tertiary care setting. Each case was re-evaluated by two pathologists and subclassified into basaloid, oncocytic, or clear cell/other categories according to the dominant cytomorphologic pattern. RON and ROM were calculated for the overall SUMP category and for each cytomorphologic subgroup.ResultsAmong 41 SUMP cases, the basaloid subtype was the most common (70.7%), followed by the oncocytic (19.5%) and the clear cell/others (9.8%). Histopathological confirmation was available in 65.9% of cases, all of which were neoplastic, resulting in a RON of 100%. The overall ROM was 29.6%. The ROMs by subgroup were 25% for basaloid, 40% for oncocytic, and 50% for clear cell/others. Pleomorphic adenoma was the most frequently encountered benign neoplasm (33.3%), and basal cell carcinoma was the most common malignant diagnosis (18.5%). The majority of malignant cases (87.5%) were located in the parotid gland.ConclusionThis study confirms the consistently high neoplastic potential of SUMP lesions and highlights the variation in malignancy risk among different cytomorphologic subtypes. Although the ROM differed among the subgroups, they all demonstrated clinical relevance, supporting the utility of cytomorphologic subclassification in salivary gland cytopathology. These findings may contribute to more refined risk assessment and patient management within the Milan System framework.

Salivary gland neoplasms are relatively rare lesions, accounting for approximately 6% of all head and neck tumors and 0.3% of all malignancies.[1,2] They most frequently arise in the parotid gland, followed by the submandibular gland and the minor salivary glands.[1,3] Although the majority of salivary gland neoplasms are benign, such as pleomorphic adenoma (PA) and Warthin tumor, a significant proportion may represent malignant tumors, including mucoepidermoid carcinoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma.[1]

Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology has become a cornerstone in the preoperative evaluation of salivary gland masses owing to its minimal invasiveness, cost-effectiveness, and relatively high diagnostic accuracy.[1, 4, 5] The specificity of FNA is reported to be high for detecting neoplasia (up to 98%) and malignancy (approximately 96%), although its sensitivity is relatively lower, ranging up to 96% for neoplasia and up to 79% for malignancy.[2,6] Diagnostic challenges arise particularly from tumor heterogeneity and the overlapping cytomorphologic features between benign and malignant lesions.[1]

To standardize salivary gland cytopathology reporting and improve risk stratification, the Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology (MSRSGC) was introduced in 2018.[1, 4, 7] This system organizes FNA results into six main diagnostic categories—nondiagnostic, nonneoplastic, atypia of undetermined significance, neoplasm (subdivided into benign and salivary gland neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential [SUMP]), suspicious for malignancy, and malignant—with each category associated with an estimated risk of malignancy (ROM).[4, 6, 7]

Among these, the SUMP category (Milan IVb) was specifically created for neoplasms where a definitive benign or malignant classification is not possible based on cytology alone.[1,8] Cases classified as SUMP often display morphologic features such as basaloid, oncocytic, or clear cell patterns, which pose significant diagnostic challenges due to their substantial cytologic overlap with both benign and malignant entities.[8–10] Consequently, the SUMP category has emerged as a critical but challenging group, with reported risks of malignancy varying between 20% and 40%.[1, 8, 10]

Recent studies have emphasized that subclassifying SUMP cases based on dominant cytomorphologic features—such as basaloid, oncocytic, or clear cell patterns—may aid in refining ROM estimates and improving clinical decision-making.[1,5,8,9] Particularly, SUMP lesions with clear cell morphology have been associated with a significantly higher ROM.[5,9]

Given the variability of ROM across SUMP subgroups and the clinical implications for surgical management, a more nuanced understanding of the cytomorphologic heterogeneity within SUMP is essential. The aim of this study is to investigate the risk of neoplasm (RON) and ROM among different cytomorphologic subgroups (basaloid, oncocytic, clear cell/others) within the SUMP category diagnosed on salivary gland FNA, based on histopathologic correlation.

Methods

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of our institution on September 26, 2023, under approval number 176. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. For case selection, the electronic pathology database was retrospectively reviewed to identify salivary gland FNA cases performed between 2018 and 2022. Cases categorized as SUMP were included in the study. We obtained demographic data, including patient age, sex, and lesion location, from pathology reports. Histological follow-up information and immunohistochemical study results were recorded in available cases.

Cytological Preparation

Conventional smears were prepared and stained using May-Grünwald-Giemsa (MGG) and Papanicolaou (PAP) stains. Slides were prepared accordingly in cases where liquid-based cytology (LBC) was performed. Cell blocks were obtained by the conventional formaldehyde-alcohol method.

Cytopathological Assessment

Two pathologists independently reviewed all cases and classified them according to the MSRSGC into six diagnostic categories.[7] Only cases diagnosed as SUMP were selected for detailed analysis. SUMP cases were further subclassified based on dominant cytomorphologic features into basaloid, oncocytic, clear cell, or other subgroups. Histological follow-up was available in 27 cases. We calculated the ROM for each cytomorphologic subgroup.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Office Excel 365 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In evaluating results, descriptive statistics were shown as mean, nominal variables as the number of cases, and percentage (%). The RON and ROM were calculated for each cytomorphologic subgroup as follows:

RON=(Number of neoplastic cases)/(Total number of cases with histologic follow-up)×100

ROM=(Number of malignant cases)/(Total number of cases with histologic follow-up)×100

Results

A total of 538 salivary gland FNA samples were evaluated at the Department of Pathology of our institution between 2018 and 2022. Of these, 41 cases (7.6%) were diagnosed as SUMP and included in the study cohort. The study population consisted of 22 men and 19 women, with a mean age of 59.7±13.8 years (range, 29–89 years). Lesions were located in the parotid gland in 35 cases and in the submandibular gland in 6 cases (Table 1).

Based on cytomorphologic evaluation, 29 cases (70.7%) exhibited basaloid features, 8 cases (19.5%) showed oncocytic features, and 4 cases (9.8%) demonstrated clear cell features with or without other accompanying features (Table 1). Histological follow-up was available in 27 cases, including 20 basaloid (74.1%), 5 oncocytic (18.5%), and 2 clear cell (7.4%) cases (Fig. 1). The overall RON in the SUMP category was 100% (27/27 cases), while the overall ROM was 29.6% (8/27 cases). Subgroup analysis revealed a ROM of 25.0% in basaloid cases, 40.0% in oncocytic cases, and 50.0% in cases with clear features. No statistically significant differences in ROM were observed between cytomorphologic subgroups (p>0.05) (Table 3).

Among cases with available histological correlation, PA was the most common benign neoplasm, accounting for 33.3% of all cases. Basal cell carcinoma was identified as the most frequent malignant neoplasm, observed in 18.5% of cases. Notably, seven out of eight malignant cases (87.5%) arose in the parotid gland, while one case of metastatic carcinoma was located in the submandibular gland.

Immunohistochemical studies were performed in 36.5% of the cases. The most commonly used markers were P63 (n=12) and Cytokeratin 7 (n=10). According to preliminary diagnoses, CD117, P40, TTF-1, Pancytokeratin, DOG1, Gata3, SOX10, and PAX8 were less commonly used markers in immunohistochemical studies.

Discussion

This study evaluated the cytomorphologic subgroups within the SUMP category, assessing their associated RON and ROM based on histopathologic outcomes. Our findings reinforce the highly neoplastic nature of SUMP lesions, while also highlighting the variability in malignancy risk across cytomorphologic subtypes.

Consistent with previous reports, we observed that all SUMP cases with histologic follow-up (27/27) were confirmed as neoplastic, resulting in an overall RON of 100%. This aligns with literature values reporting RON rates between 93.5% and 100% across various cohorts.[5, 8, 9] Thus, the SUMP designation reliably indicates a true neoplastic process, despite the uncertainty regarding malignant potential.

Regarding ROM, our study demonstrated an overall malignancy rate of 29.6% among SUMP cases, comparable to rates documented by Agarwal (29.0%) and Arisi et al.[9] (36.7%).[5] However, when stratified by cytomorphology, clear differences emerged, underlining the significance of subcategorization in refining risk stratification.

The basaloid subgroup, representing the majority of cases (70.7%), exhibited a ROM of 25.0%. This finding is consistent with the lower malignancy risks reported in previous studies, where basaloid SUMP cases demonstrated ROMs ranging between 17.1% and 20%.[8,9] These relatively low values support a more conservative clinical approach in selected cases, especially when ancillary studies and clinical correlation do not raise additional concerns.

Oncocytic SUMPs comprised 19.5% of our cohort and demonstrated a ROM of 40.0%. This aligns with the literature, where oncocytic/oncocytoid subtypes exhibit intermediate malignancy risks, with ROMs reported between 20.5% and 42.8%.[5, 8, 9] The relatively higher risk associated with oncocytic features compared to basaloid morphology suggests the need for careful evaluation, and in many cases, definitive surgical excision.

Although representing a smaller proportion of our sample (9.8%), clear cell features carried the highest malignancy risk (50.0%). Previous multi-institutional studies, notably by Xia et al.[8] and Arisi et al.,[9] documented a ROM of 100% for clear cell SUMPs, underscoring their strong association with malignancy. Our ROM for clear cell lesions was somewhat lower than that reported in multi-institutional studies, but the trend toward higher malignancy risk remained evident.

Histopathologic correlation revealed that PA was the most common benign neoplasm, consistent with prior observations.[5,11] Among malignant neoplasms, basal cell carcinoma was the most frequent, mirroring the pattern of salivary gland malignancies typically associated with basaloid features.

Our findings emphasize the clinical utility of subcategorizing SUMP cases according to cytomorphologic features, as recommended by the MSRSGC.[7] The MSRSGC recognizes that while the overall ROM for SUMP lies in an intermediate range (20%-40%), the specific risk for individual patients can be better predicted through a detailed cytologic assessment.

Several factors may contribute to the variability of reported ROM values across studies, including institutional practices, interobserver variability, and differences in criteria for case selection.[8,12] Moreover, a consensus review among multiple pathologists has been shown to increase ROM estimates, suggesting that expert panel evaluations can detect subtle features indicative of malignancy.[9]

Ancillary techniques, including immunohistochemistry and molecular studies, represent critical adjuncts in refining diagnoses. Markers such as PLAG1, DOG1, P63, CK7, and CD117, as well as the detection of genetic alterations like MAML2 rearrangements, have demonstrated value in distinguishing between benign and malignant salivary gland neoplasms.[11, 13–15] Ancillary tests were performed in 36.5% of the cases in our study. The most commonly used marker was P63, especially in basaloid neoplasms. Immunohistochemistry is important for differential diagnosis, particularly in challenging subtypes such as oncocytic and clear cell lesions. In our study, the majority of the cell blocks did not have adequate cells for additional studies. This limited the number of cases in which immunohistochemistry was performed. This may have caused the FNA cases to be diagnosed as SUMP rather than a more definitive diagnosis.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small—especially in the oncocytic (n=5) and clear-cell (n=2) subgroups with histologic follow-up—thereby limiting the statistical power to detect meaningful differences in ROM across cytomorphologic subtypes. Second, histologic confirmation was available for only 27 out of 41 cases (65%), which raises the possibility of selection bias. Cases that proceeded to surgery may represent a subset with higher clinical suspicion, potentially inflating ROM estimates. Third, subclassification relied on meticulous cytomorphologic assessment by two pathologists, but ancillary studies were limited to selected immunohistochemical stains, and no molecular testing was performed—measures that could have further refined the diagnostic classification. Finally, this was a single-institution, retrospective study, limiting generalizability. Multi-institutional prospective studies with systematic ancillary testing and larger cohorts are needed to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the importance of recognizing cytomorphologic subgroups within SUMP as meaningful predictors of malignancy risk. Basaloid features are associated with relatively lower malignancy rates, while oncocytic and especially clear cell features portend a higher risk. Subclassification of SUMP lesions thus provides critical prognostic information, aiding in tailored clinical management and optimizing patient outcomes.

Recognizing cytomorphologic subtypes during FNA evaluation may guide surgical decision-making and avoid overtreatment of low-risk lesions. Future multi-institutional studies with standardized subcategorization criteria and integration of ancillary techniques are warranted to validate and refine risk estimates.
