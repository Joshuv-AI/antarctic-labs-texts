Ankle Arthrodesis Associated With Risk of Progression to Subtalar Arthrodesis Compared to Total Ankle Arthroplasty
Authors: Gloria Coden, Kristian Efremov, Philip Hanna, Colin Wood, Matthew Beckles, Jay Modi, Kurt Hofmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:It is unknown how the increased range of motion and improved gait mechanics provided by total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) affects the progression of subtalar arthritis compared with ankle arthrodesis (AA). We hypothesized that patients treated with TAA would have a lower incidence of postoperative subtalar arthrodesis (SA) compared to AA.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed a matched cohort of 2105 AA and 2105 TAA surgeries performed between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022, using a commercial claims database. Patients with a history of preoperative ipsilateral SA were excluded. Patients were matched 1:1 for gender, age, indication for ankle surgery, and obesity at time of ankle surgery. The primary endpoint was the cumulative incidence of subtalar arthrodesis following the index ankle procedure. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed. Significance was set at P <.05.Results:Demographics were similar between cohorts, including age, gender, presence of obesity, and length of follow-up. At the 5-year time point, the cumulative incidence of subtalar arthrodesis was 42 SAs for every 1000 AAs (4.2%, absolute risk [AR] = 1.425%), compared with 14 SAs for every 1000 TAAs (1.4%, P = .018; AR = 0.713%, absolute risk reduction [ARR] = 0.71%, CI = 0.09%-1.33%). A univariate analysis demonstrated that AA was associated with increased risk of SA (OR = 2.01, CI = 1.08-3.76), whereas factors such as age, gender, laterality, obesity, and diagnosis did not significantly affect SA risk (all P > .05). In a multivariate analysis, AA remained associated with increased risk of requiring SA (OR = 1.90, CI = 1.01-3.56).Conclusion:In this study, we found a significantly higher incidence of subtalar arthrodesis following AA compared with TAA. Surgeons may consider the risk of progression to SA when deciding between TAA and AA for patients with advanced ankle arthritis, particularly in preoperative counseling for patients without existing subtalar arthritis, as TAA may offer an opportunity to mitigate this risk.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: total ankle arthroplasty, ankle arthrodesis, gait mechanics, subtalar arthrodesis, subtalar arthritis, post-traumatic arthritis

Introduction

The ankle joint is notably less susceptible to arthritis compared to other joints like the knee and hip. This is partially due to the fact that the ankle is a hinge joint that maintains weight distribution at high load. 1 The etiology of ankle joint arthritis is unique compared to knee and hip OA, as it is commonly a post-traumatic condition, arising from fractures in the ankle region. Other common causes include chronic instability and Progressive collapsing flatfoot deformity. In addition to its post-traumatic etiology, ankle joint arthritis tends to occur in a younger population. 2 As a result, this patient population suffers from a progression to more severe stages of ankle joint arthritis during peak functional years of their lives.3,4 Patients with ankle arthritis proceed with conservative treatment involving activity modification, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, and hyaluronic acid injections. 4 In patients with advanced ankle arthritis not responsive to conservative treatment, both ankle arthrodesis (AA) and total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) are considered valid treatment options.5,6 Ankle arthrodesis has been considered the gold standard for treatment of advanced ankle arthritis. 7 However, with advances in implant design, more patients are being offered TAA, with TAA being the preferred treatment option in certain settings. 8 Alongside improvements in implant design, TAA has demonstrated advantages including preserved range of motion and maintaining a more natural gait pattern while allowing for a more even distribution of load on the ankle joint.9-11

Because AA and TAA alter the hindfoot and gait mechanics differently, it is important to understand how these interventions affect the adjacent subtalar joint. Many pathologies affect the ankle and subtalar joints simultaneously, resulting in concomitant ankle and subtalar arthritis (STA). 12 Diagnosis of STA is based on radiographs, often accompanied by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, as well as guided diagnostic injections, to confirm that the pain is coming from the subtalar joint. It is also important to note that fusion rates of the subtalar joint differ after cases of TAA vs AA, with patients undergoing subtalar arthrodesis after AA having significantly lower rates of fusion compared to those who had a prior TAA.13,14

Although early ipsilateral STA may remain clinically asymptomatic and managed conservatively, the presence of a coexisting ankle arthritis that necessitates surgical intervention with TAA or AA can affect the progression of STA in the long term.15,16 Furthermore, symptomatic STA after either TAA or AA may require subsequent subtalar arthrodesis. The mechanical advantages of TAA, particularly the better preservation of normal joint kinematics compared with AA, may decrease the risk of subsequent STA. 17 In spite of these theoretical considerations, the comparative incidence of subtalar arthritis requiring surgical intervention after TAA vs AA remains incompletely characterized. Understanding these differences is of great clinical interest as it can inform both preoperative decision making and postoperative expectations for patients undergoing treatment for advanced ankle arthritis. Therefore, we sought to compare the incidence of subtalar arthrodesis (SA) after TAA compared to AA.

Materials and Methods

After approval from our institutional review board, a retrospective cohort study was conducted using the MarketScan Commercial Claims Database (Merative). This database utilizes employer-sourced insurance claims but is limited in the clinical data it provides as it is not linked to patient electronic medical records. A total of 9029 primary AA and 6708 primary TAA claims were identified between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022, based on the Current Procedural Terminology and International Classification of Diseases Tenth Edition (ICD-10) Procedural Coding System codes listed in Supplementary Table 1. Obesity at time of ankle surgery and indication for ankle surgery, including osteoarthritis, posttraumatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, or another indication for ankle surgery was also determined based on the ICD-10 codes listed in Supplementary Table 1. Claims were excluded based on the criteria described in Figures 1 and 2, leaving 2564 AAs and 3014 TAAs in the final study population.

Flowchart demonstrating study design and exclusion criteria for total ankle arthroplasty claims.

Patients were subsequently matched 1:1 by Mahalanobis nearest neighbor propensity score matching with a caliper of 0.25 based on gender, age, indication for ankle surgery, and obesity at time of ankle surgery. This left 2105 AAs and 2105 TAAs that could be matched and were included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Python (Python Software Foundation) was used for statistical analysis. R (R Core Team) was used for matching and to calculate cumulative incidence of postoperative SA. Gray’s test was used to compare the cumulative incidence of postoperative SA between groups. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to evaluate risk factors for progression to SA. Multivariate analysis excluded year of ankle surgery due to collinearity concerns, as subtalar arthrodesis rates were higher earlier in the study period, resulting in correlation between year of surgery and SA. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were calculated. Independent samples t test was used to compare follow-up. Significance was set at P <.05.

Results

Demographics

Demographics were similar between AA and TAA, including age (OR = 1.00, CI = 0.99-1.00), gender (OR = 1.03, CI = 0.91-1.16), and obesity (OR = 1.14, CI = 0.96-1.35). Follow-up was also similar between the TAA (1.94 years) and AA (1.88 years, P = .197) cohorts, with an average overall follow-up time of 1.91 years. Patients diagnosed with posttraumatic arthritis (OR = 0.88, CI = 0.77-0.99) or rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 0.49, CI = 0.29-0.81) compared with osteoarthritis were more likely to undergo TAA. Patients who required surgery on their right ankle (OR = 1.17, CI = 1.04-1.32) and those with another indication for ankle surgery (OR = 1.87, CI = 1.44-2.42) were more likely to have an AA. Patients who underwent surgery in the Midwest were more likely to receive an AA compared with patients in the Northeast (OR = 1.23, CI = 1.00-1.51), but the rates of TAA and AA were similar between other geographical regions (Table 1).

Risk Factors for Progression to Postoperative SA

The AA cohort had a higher cumulative incidence of undergoing SA (4.2%, absolute risk [AR] = 1.425%) compared with the TAA cohort (1.4%, AR = 0.713%, absolute risk reduction = 0.71%, CI = 0.09%-1.33%) at 5 years after ankle surgery (P = .018, Figure 3). A univariate analysis showed that AA compared with TAA (OR = 2.01, CI = 1.08-3.76) and earlier years of ankle surgery from 2018 to 2019 (OR = 0.79, CI = 0.67-0.94) were associated with increased risk of requiring SA. Age (OR = 0.98, CI = 0.95-1.01), women (OR = 1.18, CI = 0.66-2.12), right laterality (OR = 0.55, CI = 0.30-1.01), obesity (OR = 1.80, CI = 0.91-3.57), and diagnosis were not associated with increased risk of undergoing SA (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, AA was associated with a significantly higher risk of progression to SA compared to TAA. This finding remained true after multivariate analysis, accounting for age, gender, obesity, and preoperative diagnosis. Our results are consistent with prior studies such as SooHoo et al 18 who found a higher rate of subtalar fusion in patients who underwent AA (2.8 %) compared with patients who underwent TAA (0.7%) at 5 years postoperatively. Similarly, Stavrakis and SooHoo 19 found that patients who underwent TAA had lower risk of requiring subtalar arthrodesis, reporting a hazard ratio of 0.45 at similar follow-up duration. Importantly, in our cohort this corresponded to a 3-fold difference in cumulative 5-year incidence of subtalar arthrodesis (4.2% vs 1.4%). Although the absolute risk reduction of progression to SA when choosing TAA over AA is more modest (0.71%), we believe that collectively these findings hold clinical significance. Surgeons may opt for TAA given its association with a reduced rate of a subsequent major surgical intervention. By reducing the frequency of patients requiring subtalar arthrodesis after ankle surgery, patient quality of life is improved, health care costs are reduced, and risks associated with surgery are mitigated.

The observed differences between cohorts also provides insight into surgical decision making. Patients with posttraumatic and rheumatoid arthritis were less likely to undergo AA, which may reflect concerns about bone quality or an attempt to preserve movement in these cohorts. Prior studies have demonstrated that inflammatory arthritis and posttraumatic deformity significantly influence procedure selection, with surgeons preferring motion-preserving procedures when preoperative status such as bone structure and alignment are favorable. 20 Furthermore, regional variation was observed, with higher rates of AA in the Midwest, highlighting the potential role of geographic practice in surgical decision making. National trend analyses have also demonstrated varying relative rates of TAA and AA across regions, illustrating that patterns of procedural choice are not uniform geographically. 21

From a functional standpoint, TAA has been found to improve gait analysis parameters of the arthritic ankle, with no significant difference in most of the parameters compared with normal subjects at 1 year after surgery. 22 Moreover, TAA showed more improvement in postoperative total sagittal plane range of motion when compared with AA. 9 A systematic review including 17 studies showed improvement of spatio-temporal, kinematic, and kinetic gait patterns after TAA, whereas AA patients demonstrated improvement in only walking speed and ankle moment in addition to increased hip ROM, which is thought to be compensatory to decreased ankle ROM. 10 Collectively, these findings suggest improved functional biomechanics following TAA and may help explain the lower observed risk of adjacent joint degeneration compared with AA. 23 Preservation of ankle motion following TAA may reduce compensatory loading across the subtalar joint, providing a plausible functional mechanism for the lower incidence of secondary subtalar arthrodesis observed in our study.

In a biomechanical study using a 3-dimensional finite element analysis, Wang et al 24 found a decrease in the subtalar joint contact pressure during first-peak, midstance, and second-peak phases of gait after simulated ankle arthrodesis. However, a cadaveric study by Jung et al 25 found significant increase in the subtalar contact pressure and contact surface area between 0° and 20° of dorsiflexion after AA. In an effort to find a consensus regarding the association between AA and the development of hindfoot arthritis, Ling et al 26 performed a systematic review including 18 clinical studies, 5 biomechanical studies, and 1 gait analysis study. The authors reported no consensus on this matter. Our findings provide clinical evidence supporting an increased long-term risk of subtalar degeneration following AA, suggesting that biomechanical models may not fully capture joint loading over time.

Many factors can contribute to the development of concomitant ankle and subtalar arthritis, like inflammatory arthritis, talus fractures, talus avascular necrosis, advanced age, and obesity, to name few. Our analysis showed no significant difference between the TAA and AA groups in terms of baseline characteristics, but patients with preoperative diagnosis of posttraumatic arthritis or rheumatoid arthritis were less likely to undergo AA. However, in our multivariate analysis, none of the baseline characteristics were found to be associated with increased risk of SA, and AA remains the only variable significantly associated with an increased risk of requiring SA. This suggests that procedure-associated biomechanics may play a larger role in subtalar degeneration than patient factors that are traditionally considered high risk. Although year of ankle surgery was associated with an increased risk of SA in the univariate analysis, it was not included in the multivariate analysis due to its collinearity with progression to SA. Subtalar arthrodesis was performed more frequently earlier in the study period which introduced instability when included alongside the primary covariates of interest.

In our cohort, patients who underwent subtalar fusion after TAA tended to proceed with surgery earlier in the postoperative period compared with the AA group. In an effort to further investigate this finding, the authors looked at other risk factors including preoperative diagnoses that could have contributed to early STA, with no statistically significant findings. This observation could also reflect a staged surgical plan where subtalar fusion is intentionally completed shortly after TAA. It may be beneficial to concomitantly address STA with TAA regardless of its severity, especially since hindfoot fusions following TAA have demonstrated a lower nonunion rate compared to subtalar fusions following subtalar arthrodesis. 14 However, this finding warrants further investigation. In one large cohort of primary TAA patients, secondary subtalar fusion was performed because of symptomatic STA, suggesting that unaddressed subtalar pathology at the time of primary surgical intervention may later become clinically significant. 27

Limitations

Although this study provides useful insights on the incidence of subtalar arthrodesis after TAA and AA, it is important to acknowledge its limitations given our study design. Because this study uses a commercial claims database, there are several inherent weaknesses. These data sets rely on proper administrative coding and may contain inaccuracies, missing entries, and inconsistent practices across providers and institutions. Additionally, the claims data used in this study captures billing information rather than clinical detail, which may prevent us from knowing important clinical context such as degree of preexisting subtalar pathology. Because the study population is derived from patients with commercial insurance and does not include uninsured or publicly insured individuals, there is also limited generalizability of our findings beyond those covered by commercial health insurance.

Choosing between TAA and AA is a multifactorial decision that incorporates surgeon preference and experience, patient comorbidities, and the unique pathology of each patient’s ankle. With an average age of 55 years, most patients during the study period would likely be candidates for TAA. Thus, it is possible that only patients with more severe pathology would be chosen to undergo AA. This may introduce a selection bias into the study, which makes it more difficult to discern causation between the specific ankle surgery chosen and the risk of subsequent subtalar arthrodesis. Furthermore, once patients unenrolled from the database, we were unable to follow up on them. This may underestimate the actual incidence of subtalar arthrodesis in either cohort. The 5-year period post surgery may also not adequately capture the gradual, longer-term progression of STA and need for SA.

Last, this study does not account for any variation in surgical technique, implant type and design, or postoperative rehabilitation protocols. These additional variables can affect patient outcomes and may influence the development and progression of arthritis in the subtalar joint that requires subsequent surgical intervention.

Conclusions

Understanding the risk of progression to subtalar arthrodesis after AA and TAA is important for preoperative planning and managing patient expectations. Our study demonstrates a significantly higher incidence of subtalar arthrodesis after AA compared to TAA. Surgeons may consider these findings in the context of each patient’s clinical picture and individual preferences when choosing between TAA and AA, particularly for patients without existing subtalar arthritis, as TAA may reduce the likelihood of requiring a subsequent major hindfoot procedures.
