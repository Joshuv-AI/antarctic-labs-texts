Enhanced spectral purity of WSe2 quantum emitters via conformal organic adlayers
Authors: Riddhi Ananth, M. Iqbal Bakti Utama, David D. Xu, Albert F. Vong, Tumpa Sadhukhan, Hongfei Zeng, Rafael López-Arteaga, Wei Wang, Seok Daniel Namgung, S. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Quantum emitters in solid-state materials are typically embedded in the bulk of their hosts, making their electronic transitions inaccessible to surface modification. In contrast, two-dimensional materials, with their all-surface nature, offer a platform for tuning quantum emitters via chemical functionalization. Because of its semiconducting properties that enable electrical addressability, monolayer WSe2 is a promising candidate for quantum emission, although the complex interplay between point defects and the localized strain needed to activate quantum emission leads to poor spectral purity. Here, we demonstrate that functionalizing monolayer WSe2 with conformal adlayers of 3,4,9,10-perylenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (PTCDA) improves quantum emission spectral purity. Optical spectroscopy reveals that PTCDA functionalization lowers defect activation energies by 10 meV and induces a 30 nm redshift in quantum emission wavelength, while preserving the bright and dark exciton energies of monolayer WSe2. First-principles calculations corroborate these findings, thus providing molecular-level insight into the underlying mechanism of enhanced spectral purity.

INTRODUCTION

Robust quantum emitters of single photons are of central importance to the development of emerging quantum information technologies (1, 2). To facilitate transduction between electrical and photonic quantum systems, single-photon generation is particularly desirable in semiconducting materials. In this regard, the family of monolayer (1L) transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) (3–7) is of high interest due to their direct bandgap semiconducting electronic structure, high light extraction efficiencies, and ease of monolithic integration into devices and cavities (8–12). However, similar to other solid-state single-photon sources, efforts to tune quantum emission from TMDs have achieved limited success. Existing methods for modifying quantum emission, such as the use of electric fields (6, 13–16), defect engineering (17–20), and strain tuning (21, 22), have shown some promise, but additional strategies are needed to realize highly versatile and scalable tunability.

Because of their all-surface nature, chemical functionalization is an effective strategy to tailor the electronic and optical properties of 1L TMDs (23, 24), suggesting a pathway for tuning TMD-based quantum emitters (25, 26). Quantum emitters in 1L TMDs typically have electronic transitions involving states within the bandgap that originate from point defects (27–29). Because of the atomically thin nature of 1L TMDs, these quantum emitters are located at the surface, allowing direct interaction between the energy levels of defect states and the orbitals of organic molecules used for chemical functionalization. When organic adlayers are conformal, they are also compatible with the nonplanar substrate geometries that are commonly used to introduce strain to 1L TMDs, which is necessary to activate quantum emission from their point defects.

Here, we present a chemical functionalization scheme using conformal adlayers of the organic molecule 3,4,9,10-perylenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (PTCDA) to tailor quantum emission in strained 1L WSe2. With solution-free deposition, noncovalent interaction with TMDs, and high spatial homogeneity, PTCDA adlayers provide a well-defined chemical functionalization strategy that reproducibly modifies quantum emission characteristics. In particular, PTCDA functionalization results in a 30 nm redshift of quantum emission wavelengths, a 10 meV reduction of defect activation energies, and overall simplification of the quantum emission spectrum that improves single-photon spectral purity. The absence of significant changes in the free exciton states and quasiparticle bandgap, which is investigated using near-field and far-field optical spectroscopy, suggests that PTCDA functionalization directly hybridizes with the defects that underpin the quantum emitting electronic transitions in 1L WSe2. Molecular-level insight into these experimental findings is provided by density functional theory (DFT) calculations of the electronic structure of PTCDA/WSe2 heterostructures. Overall, this work highlights the effectiveness of chemical functionalization for tuning quantum emission in 1L TMDs, thus motivating efforts to use mixed-dimensional heterostructures in quantum information technologies (30).

RESULTS

Noncovalent interactions between PTCDA adlayers and 1L WSe2

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) allows the nature of the chemical interaction between PTCDA and 1L WSe2 to be assessed. Following the deposition of 5 nm of PTCDA on 1L WSe2 by vacuum deposition, we observed the previously reported C 1s XPS spectrum of PTCDA (31), which consists of intramolecular O─C═O and C─O─C bonds (fig. S1). Since the deposition of PTCDA does not introduce significant changes to the XPS lineshapes of the W 4f and Se 3d transitions of 1L WSe2 (Fig. 1A, also see fig. S2), it can be concluded that covalent bonding is absent between PTCDA and 1L WSe2 (i.e., PTCDA is physisorbed onto the surface of WSe2).

We fabricated the heterostructure shown in Fig. 1B by transferring micromechanically exfoliated 1L WSe2 onto an array of prefabricated nanopillars, which produces localized strain for spatially deterministic quantum emitter generation (32, 33). Next, 10 nm of PTCDA was deposited on the top surface of the strained WSe2 via vacuum sublimation (<2 × 10−5 torr) with a deposition rate of 0.01 to 0.05 Å/s. To have an internal comparison between the optical response of 1L WSe2 with and without the PTCDA adlayer, we functionalized only a portion of the 1L WSe2 sample by using a shadow mask during the deposition of PTCDA (Fig. 1C). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) of the PTCDA film deposited on strained 1L WSe2 shows uniform and conformal molecular coverage (fig. S3).

At room temperature and 532 nm excitation, the A exciton photoluminescence (PL) intensity is quenched by 90% in the strained and flat regions of 1L WSe2 functionalized with PTCDA (Fig. 1, D and E). This finding is consistent with the formation of a type II heterojunction between PTCDA and 1L WSe2, as previously investigated using tunneling spectroscopy (34, 35). In addition, a 10-fold increase is observed in the intensity of the Raman modes for PTCDA on WSe2 compared to PTCDA on SiO2 (Fig. 1, G and H). This Raman enhancement has been previously attributed to noncovalent hybridization between PTCDA and 1L WSe2, leading to the exchange of oscillator strength between the B exciton of WSe2 and the Raman modes of PTCDA (36). These observations confirm that our method for fabricating strained PTCDA/WSe2 heterostructures enables effective coupling between PTCDA and 1L WSe2.

Flexibility in heterostructure design and the resulting modifications to quantum emission

Functionalization via vacuum sublimation provides substantial flexibility in PTCDA/WSe2 heterostructure design. For example, PTCDA can be deposited on only one side of 1L WSe2, either on the top of the strained 1L WSe2 as previously described (Fig. 2B, left) or on the bottom by depositing PTCDA on one side of the as-exfoliated 1L WSe2 on a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp before transferring the sample onto nanopillars (Fig. 2B, right). By combining these approaches, PTCDA can also be deposited on both sides of 1L WSe2 (Fig. 2C). This flexibility in heterostructure design is difficult to achieve through solution-based functionalization methods. Vapor-phase functionalization also preserves the tent-like morphology of 1L WSe2 flakes draped over the nanopillars (fig. S3) in contrast to solution-based functionalization methods that often result in partial tearing and puncturing of 1L WSe2 flakes at the nanopillar sites (fig. S4).

Similar to previous reports, the low-temperature (T = 3.5 K) PL spectra of bare 1L WSe2 with 633 nm excitation show multiple defect-related sharp emitters that are localized around the nanopillar-strained portion of the flake (Fig. 2D) (32, 33). PTCDA deposition on the top side of the flake quenches some of these emitters, as expected for a type II heterojunction, but bright emitters are retained from ~60% of the nanopillar sites. These bright emitters present a simplified emission spectrum with fewer spectrally isolated emitter peaks. Similarly, 1L WSe2 flakes that are functionalized with PTCDA on both sides also exhibit PL spectra with nanopillar-localized emitters that are more spectrally isolated (Fig. 2E) than typical nanopillar-strained bare 1L WSe2. The spectrally isolated emitters after PTCDA functionalization are confirmed to be quantum emitters with g(2)(0) < 0.5 (Fig. 2F and figs. S6 and S7). Following PTCDA functionalization on one or both sides, the emitters exhibit brightness levels comparable to bare 1L WSe2.

Statistical analysis of how emitter properties are modified by PTCDA functionalization

The characteristics of the quantum emitters before and after PTCDA functionalization were quantified using an automated peak analysis algorithm (19, 37). Specifically, from the histogram of the number of emitters as a function of emitter wavelength (Fig. 3A), we observe a systematic redshift of the weighted average of the distribution by ~60 meV (30 nm) as we increase the degree of PTCDA functionalization. This redshift does not originate from the dielectric screening of the coulomb potential of excitons in WSe2, as a similar analysis of emitters in 1L WSe2 strained by pillars coated with a high-k HfO2 dielectric did not produce a shift in the distribution of emitter wavelengths (fig. S10). Moreover, the low dielectric constant of PTCDA (ε = 2.13 ± 0.06, see fig. S11) is not expected to notably enhance screening of the coulombic interactions for excitons involved in quantum emission (38).

The spectral simplification after PTCDA functionalization on the top or both sides of 1L WSe2 as shown in Fig. 2 (D and E) can be analyzed statistically (Fig. 3B). We quantify the spectral simplification by calculating the spectral purity, which is given by the integrated intensity of a given localized emitter divided by the total integrated intensity of all emitters in a low-temperature PL spectrum. The average spectral purity of the emitters increases by 32% after functionalization on one side and by 87% after functionalization on both sides (Fig. 3B). In contrast, we do not observe a similar increase in the spectral purity of the emitters when 1L WSe2 is functionalized only on the bottom side of strained 1L WSe2 (fig. S12). The spectral purity of the emitters is directly correlated to an increase in single-photon purity as isolated emitters with high spectral purity after functionalization show g(2)(0) < 0.5, while spectrally complicated emitters in bare WSe2 with low spectral purity show g(2)(0) > 0.5 (figs. S5 and S8). In addition, the linewidth of the emitters does not significantly change with functionalization (fig. S9).

To gain an understanding of how PTCDA functionalization modifies the defect potential wells, we investigated the activation energy ( Ea) of the mid-gap defect potential responsible for quantum emission in 1L WSe2 (Fig. 3C). This analysis was accomplished by fitting the temperature evolution of the integrated PL intensities of the emitters to an Arrhenius equation (fig. S13). We observe that the mean activation energy decreases as we increase the degree of functionalization from PTCDA on one side to PTCDA on both sides, indicating a shallowing of the potential well that localizes excitons associated with quantum emission (Fig. 3D).

For comparison, we also performed low-temperature (T = 3.5 K) PL measurements and statistical analysis of emitters on nanopillar-strained 1L WSe2 following functionalization with 1,4,5,8-naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (NTCDA; fig. S14). NTCDA shares a similar chemical structure to PTCDA and also forms a type II heterojunction with 1L WSe2 (fig. S14A). After NTCDA deposition on the top surface of 1L WSe2, we also observe a similar redshift of the emitter wavelength (fig. S14D) and a decrease in the activation energy of the quantum emitter potential (fig. S14F). However, NTCDA functionalization on WSe2 does not result in spectral simplification or improved spectral isolation of the emitters (fig. S14E). This observation can likely be attributed to the difference in adlayer morphology for NTCDA and PTCDA on WSe2. Unlike PTCDA (fig. S3), the vacuum sublimation of NTCDA results in the deposition of molecular aggregates, formation of cracks in the adlayer, and ultimately nonuniform coverage on 1L WSe2 (fig. S14C). As we shall discuss later, complete and conformal coverage of the molecular adlayer on 1L WSe2 is critical to achieve spectral isolation of the emitters.

Investigating the mechanism of emitter modifications following PTCDA functionalization

To understand the origin of the redshift of the quantum emitters following PTCDA functionalization, we monitored the changes in the bright and dark exciton energies as well as in the quasiparticle bandgap of 1L WSe2 (Fig. 4, A to D). Quantum emission in 1L WSe2 involves the funneling of spin-forbidden dark excitons into potential wells formed by the strain-mediated hybridization of the conduction band with mid-gap defect states (28, 39). The dipole moment of spin-forbidden dark excitons is oriented in the in-plane direction with respect to the 1L WSe2 sample (40), and therefore the dark exciton can be observed via optical spectroscopy techniques that capture the associated in-plane emission (41–43). Toward this end, we used tip-enhanced PL (TEPL) to excite and collect the in-plane emission of the dark excitons (Fig. 4A) and compare their energies before (Fig. 4B) and after functionalization (Fig. 4C). The TEPL measurements were performed on a 1L WSe2 flake exfoliated onto a template-stripped gold substrate with an alumina spacer layer (~0.6 nm thick; fig. S15). Tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (TERS; fig. S16, A and B) was also performed to confirm the near-field enhancement and the presence of PTCDA at the measurement location. We extracted the energy of the exciton species by fitting the near-field-enhanced TEPL spectrum that was gathered when the gold tip was engaged with the surface.

PL is obtained only from the far-field (“FF only”) when the Au tip is retracted, whereas the near-field contribution (“FF + NF”) is also obtained when tip is engaged with the surface. (B and C) Room-temperature TEPL spectra of 1L WSe2 before (B) and after (C) deposition of PTCDA. Negligible changes are detected in the dark (XD) and bright (X0) exciton positions after PTCDA functionalization. (D) Low-temperature (T = 3.5 K) reflectance spectra from bare 1L WSe2 (blue) and 1L WSe2 functionalized with PTCDA (pink). Negligible changes are detected for the X0 position and energy separation between the 1s (X0) and 2s Rydberg excitons (Δ12) after PTCDA functionalization, indicating that PTCDA does not affect the electronic bandgap or binding energy of the bright exciton in WSe2.

The feature in the TEPL spectra near 1.64 eV, observed both before and after functionalization, is attributed to the bright A exciton (X0) because this feature remains prominent when the tip is retracted. The feature at 1.61 eV is assigned to a bright trion peak (XT), consistent with previous TEPL measurements (44). The lowest energy feature at 1.59 eV corresponds to the dark exciton (XD). The energy difference between the X0 and XD excitons is approximately 48 meV, which is consistent with previous reports (40, 43, 45, 46) and is preserved after PTCDA deposition. Similarly, the energy shifts of X0 and XD following PTCDA functionalization are minimal (≤6 meV, also see fig. S15 and table S1).

In addition, we probed the binding energy of the bright A exciton by reflectivity measurements on 1L WSe2 transferred onto transparent quartz substrates (Fig. 4D, also see fig. S16). Negligible changes are detected in the 1s A exciton (X0) position and the energy separation between the 1s and 2s Rydberg exciton levels (Δ12) after PTCDA functionalization. Since Δ12 scales proportionally with the binding energy of the bright exciton (38, 47), we conclude that PTCDA functionalization does not change the electronic bandgap (Eg) or the binding energy of the bright A exciton. Along with the TEPL results, the reflectivity spectra suggest that the energetics of the spin-orbit-split conduction band edges (Fig. 5A) remain unchanged after PTCDA functionalization and do not contribute significantly to the redshift of the quantum emitter wavelength. Instead, the increase in spectral purity and systematic emission redshift likely arises from direct modification of the defect states and their interaction with localized excitons in the presence of PTCDA.

To corroborate this mechanism, we performed first-principles calculations of the electronic structure of strained 1L WSe2 in proximity to a Se vacancy (Fig. 5, B to E) since Se vacancies are the predominant defect species responsible for quantum emission in 1L WSe2 (18, 28, 39). In particular, the electronic transitions of 1L WSe2 quantum emitters involve mid-gap states associated with Se vacancy defects. Strain shifts the conduction band of WSe2 to align with the defect state, leading to hybridization and quantum emission (39). Our DFT calculations show an evident change in the Se vacancy states following PTCDA functionalization (Fig. 5, C and E). Specifically, a decrease occurs in the splitting of the Se vacancy mid-gap defect states at the K/K′ point due to the lowering of the upper band of the two Se vacancy states that are split at the K points (at 1.504 and 1.323 eV). As the Se vacancy states closest to the conduction band lower by 40 meV, the quantum emitter wavelength also redshifts as these defects hybridize with the WSe2 conduction band.

Figure 5 (F to H) summarizes the overall mechanism of the modification of 1L WSe2 quantum emission with chemical functionalization by incorporating the key observations of type II heterojunction formation (Fig. 5F), reduction of the trapping potential from defects and redshifting of emission (Fig. 5G), and spectral simplification (Fig. 5H). Our first-principles calculations also corroborate the formation of a type II heterojunction between WSe2 and PTCDA. On the basis of the experimental optical highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) gap of the deposited PTCDA molecules from optical absorbance measurements (fig. S10A) and the calculated LUMO energy of PTCDA, the HOMO level of PTCDA is situated lower in energy than the valence band edge of WSe2.

To explain the spectral simplification following PTCDA functionalization, we hypothesize that the conformal vacuum deposition of PTCDA minimizes the interaction of adventitious surface adsorbates with defect sites on 1L WSe2 (Fig. 5H). Previous reports have shown that the binding of atmospheric adsorbates to chalcogen vacancy defects in TMDs leads to additional localized emission features as a result of the chemical interaction between defects and adsorbates (20, 29, 48), which can be minimized via annealing followed by hBN encapsulation. Vacuum-deposited conformal molecular adlayers can offer similar benefits by displacing adsorbates and providing encapsulation. In this regard, the ability of vacuum-deposited PTCDA functionalization to form conformal adlayers even on nonplanar substrates offers clear benefits for quantum emission spectral purity since strained TMDs are difficult to encapsulate with rigid, multilayer hBN flakes. This mechanism also explains the absence of improved spectral isolation of quantum emitters in cases where the top surface of strained 1L WSe2 remains exposed to adsorbates, such as when PTCDA functionalization is only performed on the bottom surface (fig. S12) or in the case of nonconformal functionalization with NTCDA (fig. S14), despite the presence of redshifted emitter wavelengths and reduced activation energies in these cases.

Previously investigated solution-based diazonium functionalization also simplifies the emission spectrum and improves single-photon purity of strained 1L WSe2 by quenching competing radiative transition channels via charge transfer from defect states (25). However, diazonium functionalization results in the spontaneous and uncontrolled formation of polydisperse nitrophenyl oligomers with a broad distribution of molecular orbital energy levels (49), which compromises the uniformity and reproducibility of this approach. In contrast to poorly controlled nitrophenyl oligomers, PTCDA functionalization has distinct advantages, in particular the reproducible formation of dense and conformal adlayers with well-defined HOMO-LUMO levels with considerable band offsets to the conduction and valence band edges of 1L WSe2. Moreover, since spectral isolation from PTCDA functionalization is not reliant on quenching radiative transitions by polydisperse charge transfer mechanisms, we are able to perform statistically significant analysis to determine that PTCDA directly alters defect energies to modify emission wavelengths and defect trapping potentials. In single-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which are another prominent nanomaterial system where chemical functionalization has been explored for quantum emission, the role of molecular adsorbates is to create new potential wells to localize excitons either through covalent sp3 defect creation (50–52) or noncovalent alteration of the exciton energy landscape (53). In contrast, PTCDA modifies localized exciton-defect interactions, which allows tunability of quantum emission characteristics such as wavelength shifting and spectral purity enhancement. The ability to deposit conformal molecular adlayers on both sides of 1L WSe2 also provides opportunities for heteromolecular Janus functionalization that is inaccessible to one-dimensional (1D) CNTs.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have demonstrated gas-phase deposition of a conformal PTCDA molecular adlayer on nanopillar-strained 1L WSe2 that results in redshifted quantum emitters with enhanced spectral purity. Through comprehensive characterization and first-principles calculations, the observed redshift to the quantum emission characteristics can be attributed to hybridization between PTCDA and mid-gap Se vacancy defect states as opposed to changes in the bandgap or exciton energies in 1L WSe2. In addition, vacuum deposition of a dense PTCDA adlayer isolates the defects in 1L WSe2 from atmospheric adsorbates, which results in high spectral purity for quantum emission. To achieve similar quantum emitter benefits as PTCDA-WSe2 in other organic-2D heterostructures, an appropriate combination of organic molecules and semiconducting 2D materials should be selected such that: (i) a type II heterojunction is formed to quench undesirable excitonic emission features; (ii) the molecules do not strongly fluoresce at the wavelength range of quantum emitters in the target 2D material, thereby preserving single-photon purity; (iii) the molecules form conformal organic adlayers on the surface of the target 2D material upon vacuum deposition to passivate against atmospheric adsorbates. Since many combinations of organic molecules and semiconducting 2D materials meet these criteria, organic-2D heterostructures provide a flexible platform for tuning single-photon emission that can be applied to diverse quantum information technologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

WSe2 flakes were micromechanically exfoliated from bulk single crystals (flux grown, 2D Semiconductors) using the scotch tape method. After identifying monolayer flakes on PDMS stamps, 1L WSe2 was transferred onto prefabricated nanopillar arrays or quartz substrates using a transfer stage inside a N2 glove box (<0.1 parts per million H2O and O2) following an established viscoelastic transfer method at room temperature (54, 55). PTCDA and NTCDA (sublimation grade, Lumtec and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively) were deposited onto 1L WSe2 by thermal evaporation in vacuum (<2 × 10−5 torr, down to 2 × 10−6 torr) with a deposition rate of 0.01 to 0.05 Å/s as monitored in situ using a quartz crystal microbalance. For typical low-temperature PL studies, the thickness of the deposited PTCDA film was 10 to 15 nm. A 10-nm-thick film ensured complete coverage of PTCDA molecules over 1L WSe2. Complete PTCDA coverage is beneficial for displacing adventitious adsorbates from the surface of WSe2. In the case of complete coverage, local variation of the PTCDA film thickness should not affect the spectral isolation of emitters as the electronic coupling with WSe2 is an interfacial phenomenon determined by the first monolayer of the PTCDA film (fig. S18). Selective-area PTCDA deposition on nanopillar-strained WSe2 (Fig. 1C) was achieved using a hard shadow mask (PrMat) that is aligned with a transfer stage to cover a portion of the sample. Meanwhile, the deposition of NTCDA was performed on a flat 1L WSe2 flake that is partially masked with a PDMS-transferred hBN flake (fig. S14A).

Room-temperature characterization

For XPS measurements, gold-assisted exfoliation (56) of large-area 1L WSe2 was performed on template-stripped gold substrates (57). The PTCDA film was limited to a thickness of 5 nm to ensure that the photoelectrons ejected from WSe2 could be collected. XPS measurements were performed in high vacuum (<3 × 10−7 mbar pressure) using a Thermo Scientific Nexsa G2 XPS system in charge compensation mode with a nominal x-ray spot size diameter of 400 μm, analyzer pass energy of 20.0 eV, 50 ms dwell time, and 0.02 eV step size. The XPS peaks were analyzed with Avantage 6.6.0 software and fitted with a Smart background. The binding energy of all levels was calibrated to the Au 4f7/2 level of the substrate at 84.0 eV.

Confocal Raman spectroscopy, PL spectroscopy, and their spatial mapping in ambient conditions (T ≈ 296 K) were performed with a Horiba XploRA Plus setup using a laser excitation focused with an objective (×100, 0.9 numerical aperture). The Raman map in Fig. 1 (F and G) was collected with a 532-nm laser, 1% laser filter, 1800 gr/mm grating, and 0.6 s acquisition time per point. The PL map in Fig. 1D was collected with a 532 nm laser, 0.1% laser filter, 600 gr/mm grating, and 0.05 s acquisition time per point. Meanwhile, PL maps for the NTCDA-functionalized sample in fig. S14A were collected with a 473 nm laser, 1% laser filter, 600 gr/mm grating, and 0.1 s acquisition time per point.

For TEPL and TERS experiments, 1L WSe2 was directly exfoliated onto a ~0.6-nm-thick alumina film that was deposited using atomic layer deposition (ALD) onto template-stripped gold substrates (46). ALD deposition (Anric AT-410) was performed by 5 cycles of consecutive pulsing of TMA and H2O precursors at a substrate temperature of 50°C and purging time of 60 s for each pulse. TEPL and TERS measurements were performed at ambient conditions with a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution AFM-Raman system using 633-nm laser excitation at 3.2% power filter and focused on a gold-coated probe tip (AppNano, OMNI TERS-SNC-Au). The PL and Raman spectra were dispersed with a 600 gr/mm grating and detected with a Synapse EMCCD detector with a 150 EM gain.

AFM imaging was performed with an Asylum Cypher S instrument in tapping mode using NCHR-W (NanoWorld) probes at a 0.5 Hz scan rate. Ellipsometry measurements using a J.A. Woollam M2000U Spectroscopic Ellipsometer were performed on PTCDA films deposited with various thickness on a standard microscope glass slide. Optical absorbance measurements using an Agilent Cary 5000 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer were performed on a 31-nm-thick PTCDA film deposited on a microscope glass slide, with the spectrum baseline referenced to a blank glass slide without any film.

Capacitance measurements

The dielectric constant of PTCDA was extracted through capacitance measurements of a uniform PTCDA film in a parallel plate capacitor at a temperature of 7 K using a LakeShore Probe Station CRX 4 K. Capacitance was extracted through impedance spectroscopy of devices in the range of 10 Hz to 1 MHz with an AC signal amplitude of 50 mV using a Solartron 1260a system.

Low-temperature optical microscopy

Low-temperature PL measurements were performed in a closed-cycle Helium cryostat (Montana Instruments) at 3.5 K using an optical setup consisting of a mirror mounted on a scanning S-335 Piezo platform (Physik Instrumente), a scan lens (Thorlabs LSM03-VIS), a 100-mm tube lens (Thorlabs TTL100-A), and an optical cryostat (Montana Instruments Cryostation S100) with a built-in objective (Zeiss Epiplan-Neofluar 100×/0.90 numerical aperture). To excite the PL emission from 1L WSe2, a 633-nm continuous-wave laser (REO) was directed to the sample using a 640-nm dichroic mirror (Chroma). The average laser excitation power before the cryostat objective (located before the sample) was 1 μW. Sample emission was directed through the same dichroic mirror into an imaging spectrometer (Andor, Shamrock SR-303i-B). A 700-nm long-pass filter (Thorlabs) was used to filter outgoing excitation light. For reflectivity measurements, a broadband tungsten-halogen lamp (Thorlabs SLS201L) was used as the light source. The dichroic mirror was replaced with a pellicle beamsplitter (Thorlabs BP145B1) for reflectance measurements. A grating of 300 lines mm−1 dispersed the emitted or reflected light onto an air-cooled scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera (Andor Zyla 4.2P).

A large spectrometer slit size (200 μm) and relatively long exposure time (10 s) were used to collect the spectra shown in the main text, which can cause all peaks to appear relatively broad due to spectral diffusion. A peak-finding algorithm was adapted from previous work for the statistical analysis and spectral purity calculations of the emitters (37). Emitter statistics were collected from eight flakes of 1L WSe2 before and after PTCDA deposition on top, four flakes with PTCDA on both sides, four flakes with PTCDA on the bottom only, one flake strained with pillars coated with HfO2, and two flakes with NTCDA functionalization. For fluorescence imaging and antibunching experiments, the emission was sent to a Hong-Ou-Mandel interferometer with two avalanche photodiodes (Micro-Photon-Devices PDM) at both outputs and processed using commercial software (SymphoTime 64, PicoQuant). The emission peak that is measured in the antibunching experiment was filtered with a band-pass filter (Thorlabs, 10 nm full width at half maximum bandwidth). The raw coincidence counts were fitted and normalized to produce a second-order correlation function, g(2)(τ) , using a model that accounts for a three-level system: g(2)(τ)=1−ae−∣τ∣/τ1−be−∣τ∣/τ2 , where a and b are constants. No background correction was performed to produce the g(2)(τ) plots shown in Fig. 2F and figs. S5 to S8.

First-principles calculations

1L WSe2 was modeled using a 4 × 4 × 1 supercell. An isolated PTCDA molecule was then noncovalently interfaced on the top surface of 1L WSe2. All calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) based on spin-polarized DFT with a plane-wave basis set and projector-augmented wave (PAW) technique (58). For geometry optimization, the generalized gradient approximation refined by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) (59) was used with Grimme’s DFT-D3BJ correction. The energy and force convergence parameters were 1 × 10−6 eV/cell and 1 × 10−2 eV/Å, respectively. The energy cutoff was 520 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled using the Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack k-grid scheme with a 4 × 4 × 1 k-mesh. A 20-Å vacuum above the surface along the c axis was added to avoid interimage interactions. The isolated molecules were simulated using a cubic box of 50 × 50 × 50 Å and at the Γ point (1 × 1 × 1). Biaxial and isotropic tensile strains of 1% were applied to pristine 1L WSe2, 1L WSe2 with Se mono-vacancy, and PTCDA-functionalized 1L WSe2 along the a and b axes. All of the band structures along the high symmetry points in the Brillouin zone and the energy level alignments were obtained using the range-separated hybrid functional HSE06 (60) coupled with spin-orbit coupling (HSE06/SOC) on the PBE-D3BJ geometry. Band structures and other postprocessing were carried out using VASPKIT (61).
