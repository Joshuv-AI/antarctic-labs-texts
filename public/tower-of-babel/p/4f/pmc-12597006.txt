Cloning and expression of outer membrane proteins from Vibrio alginolyticus, and evaluation of their immune response in Epinephelus coioides
Authors: Ngo Thi Huyen, Dong Van Quyen, Tran Nhu Quynh, Le Thi Tuoi, Vu Thi Bich Huyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVibrio alginolyticus and Vibrio parahaemolyticus pose significant threats to various marine fish species, with mortality rates reaching as high as 90 %. Outer membrane proteins represent promising antigens for creating immune responses. The bacterial strains V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 were isolated from diseased grouper samples collected in Vietnam. The two bacterial strains, V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, were identified as highly virulent, causing disease in Epinephelus coioides. This study assesses the protective efficacy of the recombinant proteins OmpK and OmpU, derived from the ompK and ompU genes of V. alginolyticus A2, in E. coioides against the V. parahaemolyticus N9 and V. alginolyticus A2 strains. The ompK and ompU genes were 798 bp and 1023 bp in length, respectively, encoding polypeptide chains of 265 and 340 amino acids. The amino acid sequences of OmpK and OmpU proteins demonstrated high conservation across the other Vibrio species. The relative percent survival (RPS) for the OmpK protein in orange-spotted groupers challenged with V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 was 89.18 % and 72.22 %, respectively. Similarly, the group of fish injected with OmpU protein exhibited the RPS values of 82.14 % and 61.11 % against V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, respectively. Both recombinant antigenic proteins, OmpK and OmpU, induced a strong immune response in E. coioides to V. alginolyticus A2. Most interestingly, OmpK could also cross-protect against V. parahaemolyticus. This result suggests that OmpU and OmpK protein could be used as potential candidates for vaccine development targeting V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus.Keywords: Vibrio alginolyticus, Epinephelus coioides, OmpK, OmpU, vaccine candidates

Introduction

Vibrio spp. are serious pathogens for a variety of marine fish species, leading to substantial economic losses in numerous countries. Vibrio alginolyticus and Vibrio parahaemolyticus are two prevalent pathogens in marine fish species throughout the Asian region. The diseased marine fish caused by V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus has the symptoms: dark skin, ulcers on the skin surface, caudal fin necrosis and septicemia, hemorrhaging. The Vibrio disease spreads rapidly and can cause mortality rates of up to 90 % of a fish population1. Using antibiotic to treat diseased fish is one of the common methods used by fish farmers to control Vibrio spp. in aquaculture2. However, the frequent use of antibiotics has led to a rise in antibiotic-resistant pathogenic bacteria, with instances of antibiotic resistance in Vibrio spp. recorded in Vietnam3, 4. The use of antibiotics in aquatic animal feed directly affects food safety by leading to antibiotic residues in aquatic products. Therefore, it negatively impacts the marketability and exportability of aquatic products, poses health risks to consumers, and contributes to higher rates of antibiotic resistance in patients5. Therefore, vaccinating aquatic animals is an effective strategy for controlling diseases and reducing the reliance on antibiotics in aquaculture6. There are currently very few commercial vaccines available for fish diseases caused by Vibrio spp. Additionally, due to differences in climate and marine fish species composition, European and American countries often have commercial vaccines to prevent V. anguillarum and V. salmonicida strains7, meanwhile, Asian countries are focusing on developing vaccines against V. parahaemolyticus, V. alginolyticus, V. harveyi1.

To develop a vaccine that can simultaneously against multiple bacterial species within Vibrio spp., identifying potential antigens is crucial. The outer membrane proteins (OMPs) of Vibrio spp. are potential antigens for vaccine development8. These OMPs play a crucial role in infection and pathogenicity within the host. The major OMP could serve as a valuable component in a vaccine against Vibrio spp. and may also be useful for diagnostic purposes6. The ompK genes from 19 strains of Vibrio spp. (11 strains of V. harveyi, 6 strains of V. alginolyticus and 2 strains of V. parahaemolyticus) were cloned and sequenced. Analysis of the deduced amino sequence of OmpK between V. harveyi EcGs929892 and other Vibrio strains revealed that they shared 71.7 to 99.2 % sequence. The authors proposed that the recombinant OmpK protein from V. harveyi EcGs929892 could offer cross-protection against V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus, with an RPS rate ranging from 40 % to 65.4 %. Additionally, it provided protection against other strains of V. harveyi, with an RPS rate of 66.7 % to 100 %9. The extracted crude OMP (approximately 35.6 kDa) from Vibrio alginolyticus shows high sequence homology (92 %) with OmpU proteins from other Vibrio species. These Lutjanus erythropterus vaccinated with crude OMP were protected against virulent V. alginolyticus10. In addition, previous studies have shown that outer membrane proteins such as VP0802, VP2309, VP0887, VPA0548, and VP1019 of V. parahaemolyticus11, as well as OmpU, OmpW, and DLD of V. alginolyticus12, 13, 14 are potential vaccine candidates. Currently, commercial vaccines for Vibrio spp. are primarily inactivated vaccines. Additionally, difference in climate and the composition of marine fish species may explain why European and American countries often have vaccines targeting V. anguillarum and V. salmonicida6, 7 while Asian countries are making efforts to produce vaccines against V. parahaemolyticus, V. alginolyticus, V. harveyi15.

In Vietnam, a range of economically important marine fish species are being raised. Grouper refers to a group of marine fish commonly cultivated in coastal waters, including popular species such as the Malabar grouper (Epinephelus malabaricus), orange-spotted grouper (Epinephelus coioides), red-spotted grouper (Epinephelus akaara), Bleeker's grouper (Epinephelus bleekeri), greasy grouper (Epinephelus tauvina)… These fish are widely farmed in several coastal provinces of Vietnam, including Hai Phong, Quang Ninh, Nam Dinh, Thanh Hoa, Phu Yen, Khanh Hoa, Vung Tau16, 17,18. V. parahaemolyticus and V. alginolyticus have been reported to cause disease in many species of marine fish, shrimp and shellfish in Vietnam19, 20, 21, 22.

The OMPs of Vibrio spp. show promise as potential antigens for vaccine development. This study aimed to create recombinant vaccines from OMPs targeting V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus, focusing on three main objectives: (1) Isolation the high virulent Vibrio strains, (2) Evaluate the similarity of the ompK and ompU gene sequences in the V. alginolyticus strain compared to those found in other Vibrio spp. strains; (3) Clone and express the antigen genes ompK and ompU from V. alginolyticus in E. coli BL21; (4) Purify the recombinant OmpK and OmpU proteins, introduce them into orange-spotted grouper (E. coioides), and assess the RPS of the vaccine against both V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus strains.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The diseased grouper fish (E. coioides) were collected from the aquaculture area in Van Don district, Quang Ninh province, Vietnam. They exhibited symptoms such as ulcers, hemorrhaging on the fins, fin rot, tail rot, pale liver, liver and kidney necrosis. The diseased grouper samples were collected in June and July of 2022. The fish were transported directly to the laboratory (Molecular Biology Laboratory, Faculty of Biology, Hanoi National University of Education, Hanoi City, Vietnam) in ice-cooled containers (below 10 °C) and analyzed within 24 h of collection.

Experimental fish

2730 grouper fish fingerlings (E. coioides), with an average length of 60 ± 0.5 mm and weight of 5.3 ± 0.5 g, were sourced from the Aquaculture Breeding Center in Cat Hai district, Hai Phong province, Vietnam. The fish were acclimated in laboratory tanks under controlled condition for a minimum of two weeks before the experiment. They were kept in seawater at a temperature range of 26–28 °C and fed commercial dry pellets three times daily.

Vectors and bacterial strains

The pGEM-T-Easy vector (Promega, USA), pCE2 TA/Blunt-Zero vector (Vazyme, China) and their host E. coli DH5α were used to clone ompK and ompU genes, respectively. The pET-28a(+) vector (Novagen, USA) with His-tag used for subcloning and expressing the ompK and ompU genes in E. coli strains BL21 (DE3), were provided by Molecular Biology Laboratory, Faculty of Biology, Hanoi National University of Education). The E. coli strains were cultured on Luria-Bertani (LB) medium containing 1.5 % (w/v) agar at temperatures of 37 °C, with added 50 μg/mL kanamycin (SERVA, USA) whenever necessary.

Isolation of Vibrio strains

Suspensions from the liver and kidney, ulcers skin of diseased groupers, were placed in Thiosulfate-Citrate-Bile-Salts-Sucrose Agar (Himedia, India) and Chromogenic Vibrio Agar (Titan Biotech, India). The plates were incubated overnight at 28 °C. Green and yellow colonies from TCBS plates or white and purple colonies from Chromogenic Vibrio Agar, were transferred to BHI agar with 1 % NaCl for purity. The single isolated colony was placed in Blood Agar Base (Himedia, India) supplemented with 7 % sterile sheep blood to study haemolytic reactions.

Virulence assessment of isolated Vibrio strains for fish

The virulence of the bacterial strains was evaluated through a intraperitoneal injection challenge. Each group of E. coioides fish (n = 30) received a intraperitoneal injection of 100 μL/fish of bacterial suspension at different concentrations ranging from 101 to 109 CFU/mL corresponding to nine groups. A control group of thirty fish was injected with 100 μL/fish of sterile PBS (common control for the entire experiment). Mortality rates were monitored daily over a 14-day period to record the proportion of fish that died. The LD50 values of the bacterial strains for E. coioides were determined using the Reed and Muench method23.

Identification of Vibrio spp. by 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA from the isolated strains was extracted using i-genomicTM BYF DNA Extraction Mini Kit (INtRON, Korea) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Universal primers, 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCATGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), were used to amplify an approximately 1.5 kb fragment of the 16S rRNA gene. Each 50 μL PCR reaction mixture contained 80 ng of DNA template, 25 μL of 2 × PCR Master mix Solution (iNtRON, Korea), and 20 μM of each primer. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 60 s, 57 °C for 60 s, and 72 °C for 90 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified PCR products were visualized on a 1 % agarose gel, subsequently purified, and sequenced by 1st BASE Sequencing Company (Seri Kembangan, Selangor, Malaysia). The resulting sequences were analyzed for homology using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast). Based on the results of the virulence assessment on E. coioides and the identification of bacterial strains, two highly virulent strains, belong V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus, were selected for the next steps.

Identification of ompK and ompU antigen gene sequences

The genomic DNA of both V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus strains was extracted using i-genomicTM BYF DNA Extraction Mini Kit (INtRON, Korea) follwing the manufacturer’s protocol. The primers (Table 1) used for amplification of ompK and ompU were designed based on the published sequences of ompK and ompU genes of V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus in GenBank (Genbank accession no. CP082319.1 and CP065369.1, respectively).

Cloning of the ompK and ompU genes

After determining ORFs of the ompK and ompU genes in V. alginolyticus, primers OmpK-F/OmpK-R and OmpU-F/OmpU-R (shown in Table 1) were designed to add sequences for the BamHI and HindIII restriction enzymes at 5′ ends. The underlined sequences indicate the BamHI and HindIII restriction sites, with the BamHI site present in the forward primers and the HindIII site in the reverse primers. The amplification of the ompK and ompU genes was conducted using the designed primers under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 5 min to complete amplification.

PCR products of ompK and ompU genes were purified using the MEGAquick-spinTM Plus Total Fragment DNA Purification Kit (iNtRON, Korea), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified ompK gene was ligated into the pGEM-T-easy vector (3015 bp), while the ompU gene was ligated into the pCE2 TA/Blunt-Zero vector (3957 bp). The ligation mitxture was subsequently transformed into E. coli DH5α cells using the heat shock method. The recombinant cells containing pGEM-ompK were plated on LB agar supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin, 0.5 mM IPTG and 80 µg/mL X-Gal; while the recombinant cells containing pCE2-ompU were plated on LB agar with 50 µg/mL kanamycin, 100 µg/mL ampicillin, 0.5 mM IPTG and 80 µg/mL X-Gal at 37 °C. Colonies were then screened for insert genes using colony PCR with specific primers. Positive recombinant plasmids were purified using the DNA-spinTM Plasmid DNA Purification Kit (iNtRON, Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocol and digested with two restriction enzymes (BamHI and HindIII). Additionally, a basic PCR was conducted using specific primers to amplify the ompK and ompU genes, and the PCR products were sequenced by 1st BASE Sequencing Company (Seri Kembangan, Selangor, Malaysia). These sequences were compared to the ompK and ompU gene sequences of V. alginolyticus to assess the cloning results.

Construction of recombinant expression plasmid

The ompK and ompU gene fragments were excised from the pGEM-ompK and pCE2-ompU plasmids using the restriction enzymes BamHI and HindIII. These purified fragments were then separately subcloned into the pET-28a(+) expression vector (5369 bp), which had also been digested with the same enzymes. Finally, the constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21 cells on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. The colonies were screened for target genes using colony PCR with specific primers. Positive recombinant plasmids were purified using the DNA-spin™ Plasmid DNA Purification Kit (iNtRON, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The pET-ompK and pET-ompU plasmids were then digested with the BamHI and HindIII enzymes. Beside, PCR was performed using specific primers to amplify the ompK and ompU genes, and the PCR products were sequenced by 1st BASE Sequencing Company (Seri Kembangan, Selangor, Malaysia). These sequences were compared to the ompK and ompU gene sequences of V. alginolyticus strain to evaluate the subcloning results.

Gene expression and extraction of recombinant protein

E. coli BL21 strains containing the recombinant plasmids (pET-ompK and pET-ompU) were grown overnight in LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C until the absorbance at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6. The IPTG was added to the medium of recombinant E. coli BL21 to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM, and the strains were then cultured for an additional 5 h at 37 °C.

The bacterial cells were centrifuged at 9,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell pellet was washed three times with the washing solution (50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5) and resuspended in NPB 1X buffer (1 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 0.2 M NaCl; 10 % v/v glycerol; 20 mM imidazole). The cells were disrupted by intermittent sonication at 350 W for a total of 20 min, with 10-second intervals on ice. The disrupted cell extract was then centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected while discarding the pellet. Recombinant proteins with His-Tag were purified using ProBond™ Nickel-Chelating Resin (Thermo Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity of the target proteins was assessed by SDS-PAGE using a 12 % polyacrylamide gel. Protein concentrations were determined using the Lowry method24.

Vaccination

The OmpK and OmpU proteins, each recombinantly produced and purified at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, were mixed separately with an equal volume of Freund's incomplete adjuvant (Sigma) to create emulsions. E. coioides were randomly assigned to two control groups and four experimental groups and were then acclimatized for a period of two weeks. Two experimental groups were given intraperitoneal injections of recombinant protein OmpK, while the other two groups received injections of recombinant protein OmpU, each at a volume of 100 µL per fish. The two control groups were administered 100 µL of PBS (pH 7.4) per fish. Each group consisted of 30 fish. After vaccination, the fish were raised in separate tanks with fresh running marine water at 26–28 °C and fed an amount of food equal to 10 % of their body.

Bacterial challenge experiments

At 30 days post-immunization, one OmpK vaccinated- group, one OmpU vaccinated- group, and one control group were challenged with intraperitoneal injections of V. alginolyticus (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 100 µL per fish). Additionally, another OmpK vaccinated-group, another OmpU vaccinated- group, and a second control group were challenged with intraperitoneal injections of V. parahaemolyticus (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 100 µL per fish). In the challenge experiments, the bacterial concentration used was significantly higher than the LD50 values to ensure consistent infection across experimental groups and effectively assess the pathogenicity of the strains under controlled conditions. Using a higher dose minimizes variability and increases the likelihood of disease manifestation within a short period, thereby facilitating reliable observations of bacterial virulence and host response. These fish groups were monitored for survival over a 14-day period. Dead fish from the experimental tanks were autopsied to check for the presence of V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus in the tissues using bacterial culture on TCBS agar medium. Green colonies on TCBS were classified as V. parahaemolyticus, whereas yellow colonies were identified as V. alginolyticus. The experiment was repeated three times. The RPS was calculated from the cumulative mortalities: RPS = (1 − [Mortality of vaccinated fish/ Mortality of unvaccinated control fish]) × 100.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS software (version 22.0, SPSS Inc.), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Isolation and virulence assessment of Vibrio strains

From diseased grouper fish collected in Quang Ninh, Vietnam, a total of 8 strains were identified as Vibrio based on their characteristic patterns, including: (1) green and yellow colonies on TCBS plates or white and purple colonies on Chromogenic Vibrio Agar; (2) hemolytic activity (Fig. 1A, B); and (3) approximately 99 % similarity in 16S rRNA sequences with Vibrio spp. strains in the NCBI database. Based on colony morphology and 16S rRNA gene sequences, the 8 isolated Vibrio strains include 4 strains of V. alginolyticus and 4 strains of V. parahaemolyticus. LD50 values of these 8 isolated Vibrio strains were determined in E. coioides (Table 3; Fig. S1, S2). V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, the strains with the lowest LD50 values, indicating high virulence, were selected for further studies. Fish infected with the two bacterial strains exhibited quite similar symptoms, including ulcers, fin hemorrhaging, fin rot, tail rot, pale liver, and liver and kidney necrosis (Fig. 1C, D). The 16S rRNA gene sequences of V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 were submitted to GenBank with accession no. PP403904 and PP404004, respectively. The two strains were deposited in Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology Culture Collection of Microorganisms (VCCM), with VCCM numbers 15623 for V. alginolyticus A2 and VCCM numbers 15622 for V. parahaemolyticus N9.

Identification of ompK and ompU genes

To identify the open reading frames (ORFs) of ompK and ompU genes, PCR products amplified with the designed primers (Table 1) were sequenced and analyzed. The ompK and ompU genes of the V. alginolyticus A2 strain were 798 bp (GenBank accession No. PP350534) and 1023 bp (GenBank accession No. PP350535), respectively, while those of V. parahaemolyticus N9 were 819 bp (GenBank accession No. PP475473) and 1014 bp (GenBank accession No. PP475474). The deduced amino acid sequences for the ompK and ompU genes from V. alginolyticus A2 comprised 265 amino acids (GenBank accession No. WWE87340) and 340 amino acids (GenBank accession No. WWE8734), respectively. In contrast, the ompK and ompU genes from V. parahaemolyticus N9 encoded 272 amino acids and 337 amino acids, respectively.

Analysis of amino acid sequences using BLAST revealed that the OmpK protein of V. alginolyticus A2 shows high similarity to several Vibrio species, with identity percentages of 99.25 % with V. alginolyticus (ACK36938), 85.98 % with V. parahaemolyticus (WP_140247526), 90.91 % with V. harveyi (AKL71399), 85.56 % with V. vulnificus (AGL46604), 91.67 % with V. cholerae (WP_046127003), and 86.40 % with V. parahaemolyticus N9 (Fig. 2). For the OmpU protein of V. alginolyticus A2, the deduced amino acid sequence identities were 99.71 % with V. alginolyticus (ACI97461), 90.00 % with V. parahaemolyticus (WP_020841237), 31.20 % with V. harveyi (CAK6715719), 73.43 % with V. vulnificus (AIL71513), 62.50 % with V. cholerae (AVK79118), and 83.67 % V. parahaemolyticus N9 (Fig. 3). The alignment analysis results indicated that OmpK protein is highly conserved among V. alginolyticus A2 and various Vibrio species. Furthermore, the identity of the OmpU protein between V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 is higher than that observed between V. alginolyticus A2 and other Vibrio species.

InterPro analysis suggests OmpK is a member of the Tsx-like channel family and acts as a nucleoside transport channel in Gram-negative bacteria. It has the important characteristics of a Tsx-like channel protein, a signal peptide, and a non-cytoplasmic region, indicating its outer membrane location. Its participation in membrane transport is also supported by Gene Ontology (GO) annotations. Sequence alignment of the NRG-leucine transporters reveals multiple conserved regions (residues 30–70, 120–170, 210–270), which map onto the functional domain (22–265) where they likely play an important role in its transport activity.

Similarly, OmpU from V. alginolyticus, there is a conserved region (30–70, 120–170, 210–270, 310–340) in 22–340, which also has functional domain. These are probably necessary for the formation of the porin β-barrel. InterPro search places OmpU in the YDDL-related protein family having characteristic.

Cloning of the ompK and ompU genes

Using the designed primers (OmpK-F/OmpK-R and OmpU-F/OmpU-R) listed in Table 1, PCR products of approximately 810 bp and 1035 bp were successfully amplified. These products were then inserted into the pGEM-T-easy vector (3015 bp) and the pCE2 TA/Blunt-Zero vector (3957 bp) to create the recombinant plasmids pGEM-ompK and pCE2-ompU, respectively. Both plasmids were successfully transformed into E. coli DH5α cells. Fig. 4 shows the restriction map of the recombinant plasmids pGEM-ompK and pCE2-ompU digested with BamHI and HindIII. Plasmids containing the target genes were used as DNA templates in PCR with specific primers (OmpK-F/OmpK-R and OmpU-F/OmpU-R) for nucleotide sequencing. A comparison of the nucleotide sequences of the PCR products with the original ompK and ompU gene sequences amplified from the V. alginolyticus A2 strain revealed 100 % similarity.

The pET-ompK and pET-ompU plasmids were successfully transformed into E. coli BL21, enabling the expression of the target recombinant proteins. Induction with 1 mM IPTG at 37 °C triggered protein production, which commenced at 2.5 h and peaked at 5 h post-induction. The SDS-PAGE analysis presented in Fig. 6 demonstrates the expression levels of the recombinant proteins OmpK and OmpU in E. coli BL21 cells, thereby confirming the successful expression of the target genes.

Relative percent survival

Table 3 presents the cumulative mortality and relative percent survival of orange-spotted grouper (E. coioides) following challenges with the virulent strains V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9. After challenge with these virulent strains, the fish mortality rate in the OmpK and OmpU recombinant vaccinated groups was significantly lower than in the control groups. Mortality was monitored for 45 days post-challenge (Fig. S3).The cumulative mortality of the PBS-injected control fish challenged with V. alginolyticus A2 reached 97.33 %, while the mortality with V. parahaemolyticus N9 was 96.67 %. The recombinant protein OmpK provided immune protection against both V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, resulting in the RPS of 88.89 % and 72.22 %, respectively. Similarly, the recombinant protein OmpU offered comparable protection, achieving an RPS of 82.14 % against V. alginolyticus A2 and 61.11 % against V. parahaemolyticus N9. Blue-pigmented and yellow-pigmented bacteria were re-isolated from the dead fish after challenges on TCBS agar medium. The phenotypic characteristics of these bacteria closely resembled those of V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus.

Our data showed significant differences in mortality and the RPS among treatment groups (p < 0.05) (Table S1). Tukey's post hoc test indicated that both OmpK and OmpU vaccines significantly reduced mortality compared to the control group after challenges with V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 (Table S2). Furthermore, the OmpK vaccine resulted in significantly higher RPS than the OmpU vaccine (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Vibrio spp. are halophilic bacteria found in both marine waters and marine organisms. Pathogenic species of Vibrio represent substantial threats to aquaculture, food safety, and consumer health25. Several Vibrio spp., including V. alginolyticus, V. parahaemolyticus, V. harveyi, V. owensii, V. anguillarum, and V. campbellii, are common pathogens responsible for mass mortality events among marine animals in aquaculture26. In Vietnam, V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus are bacterial strains known to cause diseases in various marine fish species, including grouper21,20. In this study, the bacterial strains V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus were isolated from diseased grouper samples collected in Vietnam. The two bacterial strains, V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, were identified as highly virulent, causing disease in grouper. These strains were determined the wild-type strains for cloning and expressing potential surface antigen genes.

Outer membrane proteins (OMPs) of Vibrio spp. are important surface proteins involved in bacterial infections and represent potential antigens for vaccine development. They play critical roles in bacterial survival, including sensing environmental signals and facilitating the infection of host cells6. Among the OMPs of Vibrio spp., OmpK and OmpU proteins are promising candidates for vaccine development against Vibrio pathogens due to their immunogenicity, effectiveness, and safety12, 27. OmpK is widely distributed and contains conserved sequences among various Vibrio spp9. Qian et al.14 compared the amino acid sequence of the OmpK protein from the V. alginolyticus strain ZJ04107 with those of other Vibrio species, revealing considerable similarity: 81.2 % with V. parahaemolyticus, 75.2 % with V. vulnificus, 73.7 % with V. cholerae, and 78.1 % with V. harveyi. The ompK gene, which encodes 266 amino acids of V. harveyi, was found to have 96 % similarity with the gene sequences of V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus28. In this study, the OmpK protein of V. alginolyticus A2 showed high similarity with several Vibrio species: 99.25 % with V. alginolyticus (ACK36938), 86.40 % with V. parahaemolyticus N9, 85.98 % with V. parahaemolyticus (WP_140247526), 90.91 % with V. harveyi (AKL71399), 85.56 % with V. vulnificus (AGL46604), and 91.67 % with V. cholerae (WP_046127003). OmpU isolated from Vibrio species induces an immune response in multiple aquaculture hosts12. The OmpU protein showed a high similary in amino acid sequences between V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 (83.67 %). It can be seen that OmpU and OmpK proteins are the potential antigens for creating cross-protection for these two species.

In the present study, following the identification of the ompK and ompU gene sequences from V. alginolyticus A2, these two genes were cloned and expressed in E. coli BL21. The resulting recombinant proteins, OmpK and OmpU, demonstrated protective antigenic properties against V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus in orange-spotted grouper (E. coioides). The expression of the recombinant proteins was achieved with IPTG induction. In this study, the ompK gene from V. alginolyticus A2, which consists of 798 bp and encodes 265 amino acids, was cloned and expressed using the pET28a(+) vector in E. coli BL21. In contrast, the ompK gene from V. parahaemolyticus N9 comprised 819 nucleotides, encoding 271 amino acids. Although there were differences in the number of amino acids per chain and variations in the sequences of certain amino acids in the OmpK protein between the two strains, OmpK can still be considered a conserved protein in both strains. This study demonstrated that a strong immune response of OmpK was elicited, providing significant protection for vaccined fish against V. alginolyticus A2, with an RPS rate of 89.18 %. Additionally, cross-protection against V. parahaemolyticus N9 was observed, resulting in an RPS rate of 72.22 % in orange-spotted groupers (E. coioides). The RPS of vaccinated fish receiving the OmpU antigen protein against V. alginolyticus A2 was 82.14 %, while the cross-protection rate against V. parahaemolyticus N9 was 61.11 %. Both rates were lower than those observed in fish vaccinated with OmpK. Mao et al29 cloned and expressed the ompK and ompU genes from V. parahaemolyticus zj2003 as N-terminal His(6)-tagged proteins in E. coli. Recent advances have shown promising results for oral vaccines using microencapsulation techniques to protect antigens from degradation in the gastrointestinal tract30,31. Immersion vaccination has also gained attention, particularly for small or juvenile fish, and formulations incorporating nanoparticle carriers or mucosal adjuvants have been reported to enhance antigen uptake and immune responses32, 33. The recombinant proteins were used to immunize large yellow croaker (Pseudosciaena crocea) via intraperitoneal injection9, 29,28. The RPS for the fish group vaccinated with OmpU and OmpK was 80 % and 90 %, respectively, whereas all 10 fish in the control group died29. Two outer membrane proteins, OmpK and OmpW, from Vibrio alginolyticus were generated through the cloning of their respective genes into the pET32 Ek/LIC vector and expressed in E. coli. Following intraperitoneal vaccination with recombinant proteins OmpK and OmpW the fish demonstrated RPS of 100 % and 63 %, respectively, when challenged with V. alginolyticus at a concentration of 109 CFU/mL for juvenile hybrid groupers, Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × Epinephelus lanceolatus34. It can be seen that outer membrane proteins OmpK and OmpU as candidates for vaccine development and OmpK had high cross-protection between V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9 in E. coioides. This could be attributed to the greater similarity of the ompK gene between the two strains, V. alginolyticus A2 and V. parahaemolyticus N9, compared to the similarity of the ompU genes in these strains.

Conclusion

The study isolated V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus from diseased groupers and identified two highly virulent strains. The sequence of the ompK gene of V. alginolyticus A2 showed high similarity to several Vibrio species. The ompK and ompU genes from V. alginolyticus A2 were successfully cloned and expressed in E. coli. Both recombinant proteins, OmpK and OmpU, generated a strong immune response against V. alginolyticus in orange-spotted groupers (E. coioides). The OmpK protein also exhibited cross-protective effects against V. parahaemolyticus in orange-spotted groupers.
