PDZK1 is downregulated in ovariectomized mice with angiotensin II-induced hypertension and interacts with β2-AR to regulate endothelial ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling
Authors: Haoran Zhang, Yan Meng, Wenjing Zheng, Wenjie Bu, Wenxiu Liu, Xiaohui Zhang, Zhiyu Shi, Chunnan Liu, Meng Zhao, Huiying Wang, Yufeng Bai, Dechao Zhao, Jinyu Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstrogen deficiency is a major risk factor for postmenopausal hypertension. PDZK1, an estrogen-responsive scaffold protein, may interact with the β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) to regulate vascular function, but this axis has not been characterized in the context of estrogen-deficient hypertension.MethodsWe used ovariectomized and PDZK1 knockout mice infused with angiotensin II to model hypertension in vivo, and HUVECs for mechanistic studies in vitro. Techniques included hemodynamic measurements, histology, molecular interaction assay, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and functional cellular assays.ResultsOvariectomy exacerbated Ang II-induced hypertension and vascular remodeling, which correlated with reduced PDZK1 expression. PDZK1 KO mice showed enhanced hypertensive responses and impaired endothelium-dependent relaxation. Mechanistically, PDZK1 interacted with β2-AR via its PDZ1 and PDZ4 domains, stabilizing β2-AR and activating the downstream ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway to promote endothelial proliferation. PDZK1 knockdown or β2-AR inhibition attenuated this signaling and suppressed endothelial repair.ConclusionOur study identifies a novel PDZK1/β2-AR/ERK1/2/c-Fos axis that is essential for maintaining endothelial function under estrogen-sufficient conditions. Disruption of this pathway in the context of estrogen deficiency contributes to hypertension, highlighting PDZK1 as a potential therapeutic target for postmenopausal hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00897-y.

Introduction

Hypertension is a major contributor to the global burden of disease, driving cardiovascular morbidity and premature mortality [1]. In aging women, menopause represents a critical transition, during which cardiometabolic alterations, vascular aging, and hormonal shifts collectively elevate the risk of hypertension [2]. Postmenopausal women experience a unique and accelerated rise in cardiovascular risk factors with the loss of estrogen [3]. Estrogen analogs have been shown to prevent angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced hypertension in ovariectomized mice by compensating for the endogenous estrogen loss [4]. Their therapeutic effects and safety are influenced by multiple factors, including the type and route of estrogen administration, dose, timing of treatment initiation relative to menopause, and the presence of preexisting cardiovascular conditions [5]. Thus, the precise molecular mechanisms linking estrogen deficiency to hypertension in postmenopausal women remain incompletely understood [6].

PDZ domain containing 1 (PDZK1), a member of the Na+/H+ exchanger regulatory factor family, contains four PDZ domains and functions as a scaffold protein [7]. Studies have shown that the expression level of the PDZK1 gene is significantly reduced in postmenopausal women [8]. Additionally, PDZ-domain proteins can interact with transcriptional co-activators such as YAP/TAZ, which are known to modulate endothelial cell behavior and angiogenesis—processes relevant to cardiovascular homeostasis [9]. Enhanced YAP/TAZ activity has been shown to protect endothelial cells from ischemia/hypoxia-induced damage, improve the angiogenic activity of endothelial cells, and promote cardiac angiogenesis in the ischemic heart [10].

The β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) is one of the most abundantly expressed β-ARs in endothelial cells and plays a key role in regulating endothelium-dependent vasoconstriction and vasodilation [11]. Notably, β-AR expression is modulated in hypertensive conditions [12, 13], suggesting a potential association with blood pressure regulation. Mechanistically, β-AR signaling is known to activate downstream pathways such as ERK and NF-κB, which are critical for cell proliferation, migration, and survival—processes that are often impaired in hypertension [14–16]. Furthermore, β-AR contains a canonical type-I PDZ-binding motif (D-S-L-L) at its carboxyl terminus [17], a structural feature that enables its interaction with PDZ-domain scaffold proteins such as PDZK1. This interaction may regulate receptor localization, stability, and downstream signaling efficiency. Given that β-AR possesses a canonical PDZ-binding motif at its carboxyl terminus, we hypothesized that PDZK1 may interact with β-AR to regulate endothelial function in an estrogen-dependent manner.

While the role of Ang II in hypertension has been confirmed, the specific mechanism involving PDZK1 and β2-AR in the context of estrogen deficiency-induced hypertension remains unknown. Among the various hypertensive models, the combination of OVX with Ang II infusion offers a unique advantage for investigating postmenopausal hypertension, as it recapitulates the dual pathological features of estrogen withdrawal and renin-angiotensin system overactivation commonly observed in postmenopausal women [4]. This model is particularly suited for studying β2-AR signaling, given that β2-AR expression and function are known to be modulated by both estrogen and Ang II [18, 19]. Thus, exploring whether PDZK1 coordinates β2-AR-mediated endothelial protection under these conditions may provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of hypertension associated with estrogen deficiency.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether estrogen deficiency reduces PDZK1 expression, thereby inducing β2-AR-mediated endothelial dysfunction and contributing to hypertension.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Anti-β2-AR, anti-ERK1/2, anti-p-ERK1/2, and anti-c-Fos were purchased from Cell Signaling (Danvers, MA, USA). The anti-PDZK1 and FITC‑conjugated secondary antibody were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). Anti-His antibody was purchased from MBL (Nagoya, Japan). The anti-myc antibody was purchased from Applygen (Beijing, China). Ang II and ICI-118,551 were purchased from Sigma (St Louis, MO, USA). Anti-GAPDH and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were obtained from ZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China).

Cell culture

SV40T-transformed human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and HEK293T cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% FBS and gentamicin (20 µg/mL) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. For drug treatment experiments, cells were treated with estrogen (E2) at concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 µM for 8, 12, or 24 h. For β2-AR inhibition experiments, cells were pretreated with 1 µM ICI-118,551 for 30 min prior to Ang II stimulation (100 nM for 24 h).

Experimental animals

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China), and the PDZK1 KO mice were kindly provided by the Diabetes Research Institute of Beijing Luohe Hospital, Affiliated with Capital Medical University. All animal experiments were performed according to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Use of the Harbin Medical University and conformed to the guidelines for ethical principles in the care and use of animals adopted by the Harbin Medical University (IACUC NO. 2023083).

6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (body weight 18–22 g) were randomly divided into four groups: control (saline infusion), Ang II (1.44 mg/kg/day), ovariectomy (OVX), and OVX + Ang II. OVX was performed 14 days before Ang II infusion to allow for estrogen depletion. Mice in the Ang II and OVX + Ang II groups received subcutaneous infusion of Ang II (1.44 mg/kg/day for 18 days) via osmotic minipumps; control mice received saline infusion in the same manner. PDZK1 KO mice underwent the same modeling procedure [20].

For in vivo PDZK1 overexpression experiments, 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice received a tail vein injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding PDZK1 (AAV-OE-PDZK1) or a negative control (AAV-OE-NC) at a dose of 50 µL per mouse (titer: 1 × 10¹² vg/mL) four weeks before OVX surgery. All mice in this experiment underwent OVX followed by Ang II infusion as described above. Two weeks after OVX, osmotic minipumps containing Ang II (1.44 mg/kg/day) were subcutaneously implanted for 18 days.

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured using a tail-cuff system (BP-2010 A, Softron, Tokyo, Japan) in conscious mice. Mice were acclimated to the apparatus for 5 consecutive days before baseline measurements. Blood pressure was recorded every 3 days during the 18-day infusion period, with 6 consecutive measurements per time point per mouse, and the average value was used for analysis.

At the end of the 18-day infusion period, mice were euthanized, and thoracic aortas were collected for histological and functional analyses. PDZK1 KO mice underwent the same modeling procedure.

Lentivirus and plasmid constructions

Knockdown lentiviruses against PDZK1 were synthesized by Genechem (Shanghai, China). Their sequences were as follows: PDZK1 siRNA: sense 5′-GGG CAG CAA TGG CTA CGG TTT′ and anti-sense 5′-AAA CCG TAG CCA TTG CTG CCC-3′. The control lentivirus was obtained from Genechem. Lentivirus for PDZK1 overexpression was also synthesized by Genechem (Shanghai, China), and the sequences were as follows: sense 5′-GAA TTC CCC CGG GCG GGA TCC GCC ACC ATG GCC TCC ACC TTC AACCC-3′ and anti-sense 5′-TCG CGG CCG CTA CGC GTC GGG GTA CCC ATC ACC GTG TCT TCA GAGT-3′. The control lentivirus was obtained from Genechem.

The GV712 vectors encoding individual PDZ domains of PDZK1 (His-PDZK1, His-PDZK1-del1, His-PDZK1-del2, His-PDZK1-del3, His-PDZK1-del4), GST-β2-AR-CT (the last 83 amino acids of β2-AR, β2-AR-CT) were constructed by Genechem (Shanghai, China). Constructs of myc-PDZK1 were kindly provided by Dr. He (Capital Medical University, Beijing, China).

GST pull‑down assay

GST pull-down assays were performed using HEK293T cells transfected with His-tagged PDZK1 domain constructs. Equal amounts of GST and GST-β2-AR-CT fusion proteins conjugated to glutathione-sepharose 4B were added to IPTG-induced bacterial lysates containing equal amounts of His-PDZK1-del1, His-PDZK1-del2, His-PDZK1-del3, or His-PDZK1-del4. The lysate was incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. Then, glutathione-sepharose 4B was collected by centrifugation and washed four times with Rinse Buffer I and once with Rinse Buffer II, respectively. Finally, the proteins were eluted from the precipitated complex by boiling for 5 min in an appropriate amount of Protein Electrophoresis Sampling Buffer, centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected to obtain samples for immunoblotting analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP assays were performed using HEK293T cells overexpressing myc-PDZK1 and β2-AR, as well as HUVECs, to detect endogenous protein interactions. HUVECs were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer and incubated at 4 °C for 40 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, and an aliquot was saved as an input control. The remaining sample was divided equally into two tubes, one containing 5 µL of anti-PDZK1 antibody and the other containing 5 µL of anti-β-actin antibody as a control. After a 3-hour incubation, 30 µL of protein A/G agarose beads were added, and incubation continued overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Beads were washed three times with wash buffer, resuspended in SDS loading buffer, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The eluted proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE and immunoblotted with anti‑β2‑AR antibody (1:1000, 8513). For Co-IP experiments using overexpressed proteins, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with myc-PDZK1 and β2-AR plasmids, and the same protocol was applied with anti-myc antibody for immunoprecipitation.

Western blot analysis

Lysed cell samples were denatured, separated by SDS-PAGE, and then transferred to PVDF membranes at 300 mA for 1–2 h. After blocking the PVDF membrane with 5% non-fat dry milk (Millipore, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin solution in TBST at 4 °C overnight. The following antibodies were used for Western blot: anti-β2-AR (1:1000, 8513), anti-PDZK1 (1:1000, ab121248), anti-ERK1/2 (1:1000, 9102), anti-p-ERK1/2 (1:1000, 9101), anti-c-Fos (1:1000, 2250), anti-myc (1:1000, c1302), anti-His (1:1000, D291-7), and anti-GAPDH (1:2000, TA-08). For target proteins with molecular weights close to that of GAPDH (such as c-Fos, 41 kDa), membrane stripping was performed after initial detection. The PVDF membrane was incubated in stripping buffer (containing β-mercaptoethanol and SDS) at 50 °C for 30 min to remove bound antibodies, then re-blocked and re-probed with the next antibody. For target proteins with molecular weights distinct from GAPDH, the membrane was directly cut into strips corresponding to the molecular weight range, and each strip was incubated with the corresponding antibody. After incubation with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000, ZB-2305), proteins were visualized using ECL.

Histological staining

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Thoracic aortas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted and observed under a light microscope (Leica, Germany). Vascular wall thickness was measured using ImageJ software (NIH).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1% sodium citrate solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PDZK1 antibody (1:100, ab121248). Cells were then incubated with FITC‑conjugated secondary antibody (1:100, ab6785) for 30 min at room temperature. Fluorescence images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Negative controls were processed without the primary antibody.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

HUVECs were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, and quenched with 125 mM glycine. Cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer and sonicated to shear chromatin to 200–500 bp fragments. After pre-clearing with protein A/G magnetic beads, chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with anti-ERα antibody (1:1000, 8644) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control). Beads were washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and TE buffers. DNA was eluted in elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3), reverse cross-linked at 65 °C overnight, and purified using a PCR purification kit. Enrichment of the PDZK1 promoter region was quantified by quantitative PCR using specific primers. Data are presented as a percentage of input.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

For cell proliferation assays, HUVECs were seeded in 96‑well plates (3,000 cells/well) and cultured for 1–5 days. CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an EnSpire microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses and graph plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 and SPSS 20.0. Band intensities from Western blots were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by Student’s t‑test; comparisons among multiple groups (three or more) were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Sample size for animal experiments (n = 6 per group) was determined based on preliminary experimental data on SBP, with a power analysis indicating that this sample size provides 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference at a significance level of α = 0.05. A p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ovariectomy enhanced Ang II-induced blood pressure response and decreased PDZK1 expression

Consistent with previous reports [21, 22], chronic Ang II infusion elevated SBP and MAP in mice; OVX further potentiated the hypertensive response to Ang II (Fig. 1A-B). H&E staining and quantitative analysis of the thoracic aorta revealed that Ang II and/or OVX treatment significantly increased vascular wall thickness (Fig. 1C). To assess endothelial function, we examined the vasodilatory response of thoracic aortic rings to acetylcholine. As shown in Fig. 1D, Ang II and OVX treatment significantly impaired acetylcholine-induced endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation; endothelium‑dependent relaxation was markedly impaired in the OVX + Ang II group compared with the Ang II group. Next, ChIP assay was used to verify whether ERα is a transcription factor of PDZK1. Figure 1E shows a positive signal in the IP samples, indicating that ERα can bind to the PDZK1 promoter, suggesting that PDZK1 is an estrogen-responsive protein. Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated a significant decrease in PDZK1 expression in the thoracic aorta of the OVX + Ang II group (Fig. 1F), suggesting that PDZK1 may be involved in the pathogenesis of hypertension associated with estrogen deficiency.

PDZK1 deficiency enhanced Ang II-induced blood pressure elevation response

To investigate the role of PDZK1 in hypertension, PDZK1 knockout (KO) mice were infused with Ang II to establish a hypertensive model. Tail-cuff measurements revealed that PDZK1 KO mice exhibited enhanced SBP and MAP responses to Ang II infusion (Fig. 2A-B). H&E staining revealed that Ang II significantly increased the wall thickness of the thoracic aorta. Similarly, aortic wall thickness was also markedly increased in the PDZK1 KO + Ang II group (Fig. 2C). Vascular response to acetylcholine was further examined. Figure 2D shows impairment of the endothelium-dependent response to acetylcholine in the thoracic aorta of PDZK1 KO + Ang II group compared to the Ang II group mice. Taken together, these results indicate that PDZK1 deficiency exacerbates Ang II-induced hypertension-related responses and increases thoracic aorta thickness.

PDZK1 knockdown inhibited estrogen-induced HUVEC proliferation and ERK1/2 signaling

To assess PDZK1 expression in response to estrogen (E2), HUVECs were treated with increasing concentrations of E2, followed by Western blot analysis. The results showed that the protein expression level of PDZK1 was increased in a dose‑dependent manner with E2 treatment (Fig. 3A). The scratch assay and the CCK-8 assay further demonstrated that E2 promoted HUVEC migration and proliferation (Fig. 3B-C). To investigate whether PDZK1 mediates E2‑induced effects, HUVECs were transfected with si-PDZK1. The results showed that E2 treatment significantly increased PDZK1 protein expression level, ERK1/2 phosphorylation level, and cell viability. However, following si-PDZK1 treatment, these effects were markedly suppressed (Fig. 3D-E). In the Ang II stimulation model, E2 treatment effectively increased PDZK1 protein expression level, ERK1/2 phosphorylation level, and cell viability. However, following si-PDZK1 treatment, these parameters were significantly suppressed in both the si-PDZK1 + Ang II and si-PDZK1 + Ang II + E2 groups (Fig. 3F-G). The above results suggest that PDZK1 is a mediator of estrogen-regulated vascular endothelial cell proliferation and the ERK1/2 signaling pathway.

The carboxyl terminus of PDZK1 protein interacted with β2-AR

It has been reported that the β2-AR carboxy-terminal D-S-L-L sequence conforms to the type I PDZ-binding motif, which can mediate the interaction between β2-AR and PDZK1 proteins. To test direct binding, we performed GST pull-down assays using HEK293T cells overexpressing His-tagged PDZK1 domain constructs. As shown in Fig. 4A, the His-PDZK1 protein could be detected in the GST-β2-AR deposition complex, indicating that β2-AR could bind to PDZK1 protein. To confirm this interaction under more physiological conditions, we performed Co-IP assays using HUVECs to detect endogenous PDZK1 and β2-AR proteins. The result further confirmed the interaction between PDZK1 and β2-AR (Fig. 4B). To further identify the critical binding region on β2-AR for PDZK1 interaction, we constructed truncated β2-AR mutants. GST-β2ARCT, containing only the C-terminal region, successfully pulled down PDZK1, whereas GST-β2-AR-del DSSL, lacking the C-terminal DSSL motif, lost its binding capacity (Fig. 4C). This indicates that the DSSL motif at the C-terminus of β2-AR is the core site for its interaction with PDZK1. To map the PDZ domains responsible for β2-AR binding, we generated His-tagged PDZK1 deletion mutants lacking each of the four PDZ domains. The results showed that upon deletion of PDZ1 and PDZ4, GST-β2-AR could not interact with His-PDZK1, suggesting that PDZK1 could bind to β2-AR proteins through the PDZ1 and PDZ4 structural domains (Fig. 4D).

PDZK1 regulated β2-AR expression and its downstream ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway

HUVECs were transfected with si-PDZK1 or OE-PDZK1. Western blot results showed that β2-AR protein expression level was significantly downregulated in the si-PDZK1 group (Fig. 5A). In contrast, PDZK1 overexpression significantly upregulated β2‑AR expression level (Fig. 5B). As a signaling pathway downstream of β2-AR, ERK1/2/c-Fos mediates the regulation of cell growth and proliferation. We further investigated the regulatory mechanism by which PDZK1 modulates this pathway after promoting β2-AR expression using the β2-AR agonist Fenoterol. Western blot results showed that the β2-AR, p-ERK1/2, and c-Fos protein expression levels were remarkably increased in the β2-AR+PDZK1 group compared with the β2-AR group. Following fenoterol treatment, these molecules in the β2-AR+PDZK1 + fenoterol group were significantly higher than those in the β2-AR+fenoterol group (Fig. 5C-E). The above results indicated that PDZK1 positively regulated β2-AR protein expression, and PDZK1 overexpression enhanced the activation of the ERK1/2-c-Fos signaling pathway.

PDZK1 knockdown or ICI‑118551 treatment attenuated Ang II-induced ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling pathways and endothelial cell proliferation

To verify the effects of β2-AR on PDZK1 and ERK1/2/c-Fos, Ang II-stimulated HUVECs were treated with si-PDZK1 or/ and β2-AR inhibitor (ICI-118551). Western blot results showed that compared with the Ang Ⅱ group, ICI‑118,551 or si-PDZK1 treatment downregulated β2‑AR, p‑ERK1/2, and c‑Fos expression levels and suppressed cell viability. However, upon combined treatment with si-PDZK1 and ICI‑118,551, the inhibitory effects on the markers showed no significant difference compared to the single treatment groups (Fig. 6A-D). These results demonstrated that both PDZK1 knockdown and ICI‑118,551 treatment effectively suppressed ERK1/2/c‑Fos pathway activation and cell proliferation.

PDZK1 overexpression alleviates hypertension and vascular remodeling in OVX mice

To further validate the protective role of PDZK1 in estrogen-deficient hypertension, we performed in vivo gain-of-function experiments using AAV-mediated PDZK1 overexpression in OVX mice subjected to Ang II infusion. Western blot analysis confirmed successful overexpression of PDZK1 in thoracic aortic tissues of mice receiving AAV-OE-PDZK1 compared with the AAV-OE-NC control group (Fig. 7A). Tail-cuff measurements revealed that PDZK1 overexpression significantly reduced both SBP and MAP in OVX + Ang II mice (Fig. 7B-C). Moreover, H&E staining showed that PDZK1 overexpression markedly attenuated the Ang II-induced increase in thoracic aortic wall thickness (Fig. 7D). These results demonstrate that PDZK1 overexpression effectively alleviates hypertension and vascular remodeling in the context of estrogen deficiency, providing in vivo gain-of-function evidence to support its protective role in this pathological condition.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that OVX enhanced Ang II-induced blood pressure responses and decreased PDZK1 expression. In mice, we observed enhanced Ang II responses and significantly attenuated endothelium-dependent relaxation in PDZK1 knockout mice. In HUVECs, estrogen promoted endothelial cell proliferation and migration, effects attenuated by PDZK1 knockdown, which also suppressed ERK1/2 activation. PDZK1 regulates β2-AR expression and the downstream ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway by interacting with the β2-AR. β2-AR inhibitor ICI-118,551 inhibited the β2-AR expression, supressed the ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling pathway, and inhibited endothelial cell proliferation. Thus, the regulatory effect of PDZK1 on β2-AR may play a role in estrogen deficiency-associated hypertension.

Endothelial dysfunction, characterized by impaired angiogenesis and cell loss, is a well‑established contributor to hypertension pathogenesis [23–25]. Xu et al. emphasized that endothelial cells, as ubiquitous components of tissues, regulate a wide range of vascular behaviors, and their dysfunction represents a major hallmark of cardiovascular disease [26]. Similarly, several studies have shown that estrogen deficiency is strongly associated with increased cardiovascular risk, impairing both endothelium-dependent and -independent vascular functions [27, 28]. This concept was corroborated by our observation of impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation in response to acetylcholine in the thoracic aortae of ovariectomized mice combined with Ang II infusion. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of endothelial dysfunction has significant potential for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases caused by estrogen deficiency.

The choice of the OVX + Ang II infusion model in this study was based on its relevance to postmenopausal hypertension. Unlike Ang II infusion alone, which primarily induces renin-angiotensin system-dependent hypertension, the addition of OVX introduces the component of estrogen withdrawal, allowing for the investigation of how hormonal deficiency interacts with pressor stimuli to exacerbate vascular dysfunction. This model has been widely used to study estrogen-related cardiovascular pathophysiology [29], as it mimics the clinical scenario in which postmenopausal women, already facing declining estrogen levels, may be more susceptible to the hypertensive effects of an overactivated renin-angiotensin system. Our findings demonstrate that PDZK1 downregulation upon OVX + Ang II treatment is associated with impaired β2-AR-mediated ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling, suggesting that this axis may serve as a critical link between estrogen deficiency and aggravated hypertensive vascular remodeling. The loss of estrogen-mediated PDZK1 maintenance may render endothelial cells more vulnerable to Ang II-induced injury, thereby amplifying the pressor and remodeling responses. These observations underscore the value of the OVX + Ang II model in uncovering pathway-specific mechanisms that might not be apparent in models of single-factor hypertension.

Although estrogen deficiency is a recognized risk factor for hypertension in postmenopausal women, the precise molecular pathways involved remain complex and incompletely defined [30, 31]. Studies have shown that understanding the expression patterns of estrogen receptors in aging females is critical for developing targeted hormonal therapies to mitigate cardiovascular damage [32]. In addition, stimulation of estrogen receptor (ER) activation helps to restore estrogen imbalance, thereby preventing endothelial dysfunction in postmenopausal women and reducing cardiovascular disease-induced deaths in older women [33]. Estrogen receptors include ERα, ERβ, and the G protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 (GPER1), which are involved in the regulation of a variety of complex physiological processes and have been implicated in the pathophysiology of hypertension [34, 35]. Studies have shown that ERα plays an important role in regulating mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation associated with hypertension, and activation of ERα not only prevents the progression of age-related nephropathy [36], but also effectively attenuates arterial and renal changes induced by aging and hypertension in female mice [37]. Our study identified PDZK1, whose promoter is bound by ERα, as a downstream effector of estrogen signaling. PDZK1 protein levels were significantly reduced in ovariectomized mice. The response to Ang II-induced hypertension was increased, and the endothelium-dependent response to acetylcholine was significantly attenuated in PDZK1 KO female mice combined with Ang II infusion. Transfection of si-PDZK1 in HUVECs suppressed the ERK1/2 pathway and inhibited cell proliferation, suggesting that the reduction of PDZK1 expression under ovariectomy combined with Ang II infusion conditions may be associated with endothelial dysfunction and elevated blood pressure.

β2-AR, a key receptor of the sympathetic nervous system, is expressed at lower levels in the thoracic aorta of hypertensive patients [38]. Studies have found that β2-AR expression in endothelial cells of hypertensive patients is significantly reduced, resulting in impaired endothelial proliferation, and β2-AR overexpression restores endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and adhesion [39, 40]. It has been reported that β2-AR carboxyl-terminal D-S-L-L sequence is consistent with the type I PDZ binding motif, so it was considered that there was an interaction between β2-AR and PDZK1 protein. It is worth noting that the mechanism by which PDZK1 increases the stability of β2-AR via the ubiquitination pathway has been validated in our previous work [41]. In this study, the interaction between β2-AR and PDZK1 was confirmed by GST pull-down, and Co-IP experiments, and the interaction sites were mapped to the PDZ1 and PDZ4 structural domains of PDZK1 and the carboxyl-terminal DSSL motif of β2-AR. The effects of PDZK1 on β2-AR expression and the downstream ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway were further investigated. PDZK1 was found to positively regulate β2‑AR expression and enhance downstream ERK1/2/c‑Fos signaling. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PDZK1 interacts with β2-AR via its C-terminal PDZ1 and PDZ4 domains, a process that may be affected under ovariectomy combined with Ang II infusion conditions, thereby contributing to endothelial dysfunction and the development of hypertension.

The downstream mechanisms of this study focus on the ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling axis. Our data show that in an AngⅡ-induced endothelial cell model, knockdown of PDZK1 or inhibition of β2-AR significantly reduced the expression of p-ERK1/2 and c-Fos, accompanied by weakened cell proliferation and migration abilities. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that the ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway serves as a core downstream effector regulating endothelial cell growth, survival, and vascular homeostasis [42]. Notably, c-Fos, as an immediate early response gene, is rapidly induced by ERK1/2 phosphorylation-driven activation and participates in cell growth and development [43]. This study further reveals that PDZK1 enhances the activation of this pathway by interacting with β2-AR, whereas under conditions of estrogen deficiency or downregulation of PDZK1 expression, this positive feedback loop is disrupted, leading to attenuation of ERK1/2/c-Fos signaling, impaired endothelial repair, and ultimately promoting vascular remodeling and increased blood pressure. Notably, Ang II primarily activates the ERK1/2 pathway via the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) [44]. However, our finding that PDZK1 knockdown or β2-AR inhibition attenuates Ang II-induced ERK1/2/c-Fos activation suggests crosstalk between the PDZK1/β2-AR axis and AT1R signaling. As a scaffold protein, PDZK1 may coordinate signaling complexes downstream of both receptors, synergistically regulating this pathway. Future research could further explore the regulatory roles of other key molecules in this pathway and evaluate the effects of estrogen replacement therapy on the PDZK1/β2-AR axis, in order to more comprehensively elucidate its downstream network in endothelial dysfunction and assess its therapeutic potential.

In addition, β2-AR signaling involves a diverse array of downstream effectors, including NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and various inflammatory pathways, which play critical roles in vascular inflammation, cell survival, and endothelial function. It has been reported that β2-AR activation can regulate PI3K/Akt signaling via G protein-dependent pathways, contributing to cell survival and angiogenesis [45]. Meanwhile, β2-AR agonists have been reported to inhibit NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing macrophage activation in cardiovascular complications [46]. As a scaffold protein, PDZK1 may coordinate the activation of multiple signaling pathways. Recent studies have shown that PDZK1 mediates the activation of the ERK and NF-κB pathways in the kidneys of rats with heart failure [47]. Although the present study focuses on the ERK1/2/c-Fos axis, it is plausible that PDZK1/β2-AR signaling may intersect with NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and other pathways to collectively contribute to endothelial homeostasis and the pathogenesis of hypertension. Future studies are warranted to explore the roles of these additional pathways in PDZK1-mediated vascular protection.

Although this study identified PDZK1 as a potential mediator of estrogen-deficient hypertension via the β2-AR/ERK1/2 signaling pathway, several limitations remain. First, in vivo experiments primarily relied on Ang II infusion and OVX mouse models, which may not fully replicate the progressive hormonal and vascular changes occurring in postmenopausal women; moreover, in vivo estrogen replacement experiments were not performed, so the phenotypes observed in OVX mice cannot be fully attributed to estrogen deficiency alone. Second, although we demonstrated the PDZK1-β2-AR interaction and its functional effects in HUVECs, the mechanism of action of this axis in other vascular beds or in humans remains unclear. Third, this study primarily focused on the ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway; other downstream effectors of β2-AR or PDZK1 (e.g., NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, or inflammatory pathways) were not examined. Fourth, histological analysis of vascular remodeling was limited to H&E staining. Additionally, all in vitro experiments were performed using SV40T-immortalized HUVECs rather than primary HUVECs, which may affect cell cycle checkpoints, receptor signaling, and ERK pathway activation.

Future studies should validate the translational significance of these findings in clinical cohorts, explore tissue-specificity and gender differences in PDZK1 expression and function, incorporate comprehensive histological assessments including elastin fiber integrity, smooth muscle cell phenotype, and inflammatory infiltration, elucidate additional signaling cascades mediating PDZK1-mediated vascular protection, evaluate potential aortic complications in Ang II infusion models, perform in vivo estrogen replacement experiments to validate the causal relationship between estrogen deficiency and PDZK1-mediated vascular dysfunction, and use primary endothelial cells to confirm the findings obtained from SV40T-immortalized cells. Furthermore, developing PDZK1-targeting drugs or gene editing strategies in preclinical models will help assess its therapeutic potential for preventing or treating estrogen-deficient hypertension.

This study suggests that PDZK1-mediated regulation of β2-AR may play a role in endothelial dysfunction associated with estrogen deficiency. We demonstrated that PDZK1 can interact with β2-AR through its carboxy-terminal PDZ1 and PDZ4 structural domains and regulate its downstream ERK1/2/c-Fos pathway. Our study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of OVX AngII hypertension. These findings position PDZK1 as a promising target for developing novel therapeutics aimed at preventing or treating hypertension in postmenopausal women.
