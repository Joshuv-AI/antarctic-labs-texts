Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase 1 mutation impairs pulmonary surfactant homeostasis by disrupting alveolar macrophage function
Authors: Weitao Zhou, Dan Dai, Yao Chen, Xiao Han, Xinran Dong, Yunfei Liao, Tong Chen, Huixian Wang, Chen He, Yilei Lu, Chenxin Zheng, Yufeng Zhou, Liling Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiallelic isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (IARS1) variants are associated with an IARS1-related phenotype characterized by growth retardation, intellectual disability, muscular hypotonia, infantile hepatopathy, and interstitial lung disease. This rare, early-onset disorder is often lethal. Although several variants have been identified in recent years, their pathogenicity and underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we generated Iars1Y148C/R444* compound heterozygous mice, which exhibited pulmonary alveolar proteinosis - like lesions, growth retardation, and muscular hypotonia. Lipid metabolomic profiling of these mice revealed dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism pathways. Furthermore, the Iars1 mutation increased surfactant deposition in alveolar macrophages by impairing lysosomal degradation and disrupting lysosome-autophagosome fusion. We also found that partial loss of IARS1 activity interfered with the post-translational processing of cathepsin Z by inhibiting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Importantly, exogenous expression of cathepsin Z improved surfactant clearance in macrophages with partial loss of Iars1 activity. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that partial loss of IARS1 activity induces lysosomal dysfunction in alveolar macrophages and promotes pulmonary surfactant accumulation in the alveoli, thereby contributing to the development of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03792-2.Keywords: Gene variants, Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase 1, Cathepsin Z, Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

Introduction

Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (IARS1) is a member of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) family, which is universally expressed and essential for cellular growth, differentiation, and the development of multiple organs [1, 2]. Like other ARSs, IARS1 catalyzes the ATP-dependent ligation of isoleucine to its cognate tRNA [3]. Beyond its canonical translational role, IARS1 also exhibits noncanonical functions, including regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, transcription, and signal transduction [4–6]. Pathogenic variants in IARS1 have been associated with growth retardation, muscular hypotonia, infantile hepatopathy, and interstitial lung disease in affected individuals [7–15]. However, these studies have primarily described hepatic and neurodevelopmental manifestations, and the specific role of IARS1 in the respiratory system remains poorly understood, particularly in pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP). A few reports have described respiratory involvement associated with IARS1 variants, including recurrent respiratory infections [7] and two patients died at an early age and exhibited pulmonary pathological features consistent with PAP [8].

PAP is a rare but potentially fatal disorder characterized by abnormal accumulation of pulmonary surfactant. The condition may be inherited, secondary, or acquired [16]. Pulmonary surfactant is composed mainly of phospholipids, neutral lipids (predominantly cholesterol), and surfactant proteins (SPs) including SP-A (encoded by SFTPA1), SP-B (encoded by SFTPB), SP-C (encoded by SFTPC), and SP-D (encoded by SFTPD). Surfactant is produced, secreted, and recycled by alveolar type 2 epithelial cells (AEC2), and a portion is phagocytosed and degraded by alveolar macrophages (AMs) [17, 18]. Inherited PAP is caused by pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in genes encoding proteins critical for surfactant production (e.g., SFTPB, SFTPC, ABCA3, and NKX2-1), surfactant clearance (e.g., CSF2RA, CSF2RB, GATA2, SLC7A7, CCR2), or interferon-mediated alveolar macrophage dysfunction (e.g., OAS1 and ADA), ultimately leading to impaired surfactant homeostasis and PAP [12, 19–23]. Interestingly, pathogenic variants in methionyl-tRNA synthetase 1(MARS1) and leucyl-tRNA synthetase 1(LARS1), which are two other ARS family members, have also been implicated in pediatric multisystem disorders associated with PAP [24–27]. Notably, MARS1 is now recognized as an important genetic cause of PAP with systemic manifestations, and functional studies have demonstrated that disease-associated MARS1 variants impair MetRS catalytic activity and methionine recognition, with partial rescue by methionine supplementation in model systems [28, 29]. In addition, pathogenic variants in phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase genes (FARSA and FARSB) have been associated with childhood interstitial lung disease and multisystem disorders [30, 31]. However, despite these advances, the mechanisms linking ARS dysfunction to pulmonary disease, particularly alveolar macrophage dysfunction and PAP pathogenesis, remain incompletely understood.

Macrophages perform multiple roles, including phagocytosis of pathogens and apoptotic cells, antigen presentation, tissue repair, inflammation, and maintenance of alveolar homeostasis [32–34]. In healthy individuals, macrophages degrade approximately 30% of pulmonary surfactant, breaking down the lipid-protein complex into free cholesterol, fatty acids, and amino acids within lysosomes [35, 36]. Lysosomal dysfunction has been observed in both mutant AEC2 cells and macrophages, where proper lysosome-autophagosome fusion is essential for autophagosome degradation [37, 38]. Impaired lysosomal acidification can lead to defective autophagic flux, inefficient clearance of cellular debris, and increased susceptibility to inflammation and infection [38].

Cathepsin Z (CTSZ) is a cysteine protease that constitutes an important component of the lysosomal proteolytic system. It degrades polyglutamine proteins and peptides in neurodegenerative diseases and is recruited to the murine phagosome following phagocytosis [39–41]. CTSZ participates in early phagosomal maturation and may contribute to tissue injury by degrading extracellular matrix components [42]. Beyond proteolysis, CTSZ also regulates brown adipocyte function and inflammation [43, 44]. Notably, the related lysosomal protease cathepsin D has been shown to induce surfactant abnormalities through early lysosomal stress in interstitial pneumonia [45].

Multiple biallelic IARS1 variants have been identified in human IARS1-related disorders. In this study, we identified the compound heterozygous variants c.443G > A (p.Y148C) and c.1330 C > T (p.R444*) by exome sequencing (ES) (Table 1), a genotype that was recently reported in a patient with IARS1- related disease [14]. Both c.443G > A (p.Y148C) and c.1330 C > T (p.R444*) are single nucleotide variants; however, c.443G > A results in a missense variant, whereas c.1330 C > T introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in a nonsense variant.R444*. Both variants are extremely rare in the gnomAD (Table 1). The missense variant p.Y148C was predicted to be deleterious by multiple computational tools, including CADD, REVEL, and AlphaMissense. In contrast, p.R444* is a nonsense variant predicted to introduce a premature termination codon, resulting in truncation of the IARS1 protein and likely loss of normal protein function (Table 1). Previously, a mouse model carrying the Iars1-V79L mutation exhibited growth retardation but no PAP phenotype [46], suggesting that compound heterozygosity may provide an effective tool to recapitulate the pulmonary mechanisms through which IARS1 and its pathogenic variants influence the PAP phenotype.

In this study, we generated a biallelic variant mouse model that developed PAP-like lesions, growth retardation, and muscular hypotonia. The Iars1 mutation promoted surfactant accumulation in alveolar macrophages by impairing lysosomal clearance of surfactant proteins and lipids. Lipid metabolomic analysis further revealed that IARS1 interacts with CTSZ and protects it from ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Loss of CTSZ led to increased cholesterol and SP-C deposition within alveolar macrophages. Our findings demonstrate that IARS1 pathogenic variants impair surfactant clearance through macrophage lysosomal dysfunction, resulting in alveolar surfactant accumulation, whereas exogenous Ctsz expression ameliorates these abnormalities in Iars1Y148C/R444* macrophages.

Results

Generation of the Iars1Y148C/R444* biallelic mice using CRISPR/Cas9

To examine the effects of IARS1 depletion, we initially attempted to generate Iars1-null mice; however, complete knockout resulted in embryonic lethality. Because one of our patients carried compound heterozygous variants (Y148C and R444*; Table 1), we sought to generate Iars1Y148C/R444* mice by crossing Iars1Y148C/Y148C with Iars1+/ R444* mice using a CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in technique (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis confirmed decreased Iars1 protein levels in the lung tissue of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared with those in Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 1B, C). The fluorescence intensity of Iars1 immunostaining in lung tissue sections (Fig. 1D, E) and Iars1 mRNA expression (Fig. 1F) were also reduced, further verifying successful establishment of the compound heterozygous line.

The compound heterozygous Iars1Y148C/R444* mice were born with low birth weight and developed severe growth retardation by 8 weeks of age, consistent with previous findings (Fig. 1G, H). Approximately 40% of these mice died by 8 months, which was significantly higher than that observed in wild-type mice (Fig. 1I). Beyond growth and developmental abnormalities, about 10% exhibited sparse hair, especially around the neck, beginning at 21 days of age, and 12% showed a short-limb phenotype (Fig. 1J). Blood cholesterol levels were higher in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice than in Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 1K). Seizures occurred in 45% of heterozygous animals beginning at 2 months of age, and 20% of neonatal mice died with dyspnea. However, Nissl staining revealed no significant structural abnormalities in the brains of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared to Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 1L). To evaluate the metabolic consequences of partial loss of Iars1 activity, we quantified all 20 amino acids in venous blood. Isoleucine concentrations were reduced, though not significantly (Fig. 1M). Concentrations of glutamine, lysine, and histidine were decreased, while those of glycine and serine were increased; no significant differences were observed for other amino acids (Fig. 1M). Collectively, these results confirm that Iars1Y148C/R444* mice represent a robust model for investigating the pathophysiological mechanisms associated with biallelic Iars1 variants.

Iars1Y148C/R444* biallelic mice develop PAP-like phenotypes

Since some patients with IARS1-related disease present with PAP, we further investigated the pulmonary phenotypes of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from PAP patients typically shows positive staining with both periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and oil red O (ORO), indicating the accumulation of surfactant and lipids [47]. In our study, BALF from the Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibited more PAS-positive and ORO-positive cells compared with Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 2A and B). The BALF of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice also showed significantly higher total protein content and greater numbers of secreted cells than that of Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 2C and D). We next evaluated the expression of surfactant proteins in the lungs of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice. Western blot analyses of BALF from Iars1 Y148C/R444* mice revealed elevated levels of SP-A, proSP-B, and SP-D (Fig. 2E, F). Lipid quantification of BALF demonstrated increased concentrations of total cholesterol, total lipids, and total phospholipids in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared with Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 2G-I). Collectively, these findings confirm that the accumulated material in the alveolar spaces of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice consists of pulmonary surfactant and demonstrate that these mice develop a PAP-like phenotype.

Dysfunction of alveolar macrophage in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice

Because previous studies have shown that Iars1 pathogenic variants affect lipid metabolism in the lung and other organs [9–11], we analyzed the lung lipidomic profiles of Iars1+/+ and Iars1Y148C/R444* mice mice using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) at postnatal day 28 (Fig. 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of downregulated metabolites revealed significant enrichment in fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, and sphingolipid signaling pathways (Fig. 3A). These results suggest that altered lipid metabolism may involve dysfunction of AMs.

Previous studies have established that functional impairment of alveolar macrophages leads to defective surfactant clearance and the development of PAP [48, 49]. Immunofluorescence staining showed no significant difference in the number of alveolar macrophages between Iars1 Y148C/R444* and Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 3B, C). However, flow cytometric (FC) analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in the percentage of alveolar macrophages in the lung of Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared with Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. S1A, S1B). Although Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibited spontaneous PAP-related abnormalities under basal conditions, the pulmonary phenotype was relatively mild. To further evaluate alveolar macrophage responses under inflammatory stress and to enhance PAP-like pathological manifestations, mice were challenged with intratracheal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) prior to macrophage subset analysis. Then, Iars1Y148C/R444* and Iars1+/+ mice were administered LPS intratracheally, and alveolar macrophages were isolated from the lung tissue 48 h post-treatment. FC analysis showed a significant decrease in the percentage of alveolar macrophages in PAP-like Iars1Y148C/R444* mice and Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 3D and E). Because the pulmonary surfactant is phagocytosed and degraded by alveolar macrophages, we next assessed the phagocytic capacity of macrophages isolated from the lung tissue and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). There was no significant difference in the uptake of latex beads between Iars1Y148C/R444* groups and Iars1+/+ groups (Fig. 3F). However, confocal imaging revealed greater deposition of NBD-cholesterol (NBD-CHOL) within 10 min in alveolar macrophages and BMDMs from Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared with Iars1+/+ mice (Fig. 3G, H). Additionally, SP-C protein levels were significantly elevated in both AMs and BMDMs after exposure to exogenous SP-C in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice relative to Iars1+/+ groups (Fig. 3I, J). Together, these findings indicate that alveolar macrophages in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibit impaired clearance of pulmonary surfactant lipid-protein complexes.

Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibit lysosomal degradation defects in alveolar macrophages

Classical autophagy (macroautophagy) begins with the formation of a double-membrane structure known as the phagophore, which engulfs cytosolic material and closes to form an autophagosome [50]. Autophagosomes subsequently fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, where cellular debris and metabolic byproducts are degraded [38, 51]. To assess lysosomal pH, we used the fluorescent probes LysoSensor Green DND-189 and LysoTracker Red DND-99. Confocal imaging revealed that the fluorescence intensity of both dyes was reduced in BMDMs in Iars1 Y148C/R444* mice with partial loss of Iars1 activity compared with that in Iars1+/+ mice, indicating defects in lysosomal acidification. (Fig. 4A). When BMDMs with partial loss of Iars1 activity were subjected to 24-h starvation, western blot analysis revealed increased levels of P62 and LC3-II/I (Fig. 4B, C), and electron microscopy revealed a marked increase in autolysosomes (Fig. 4D). We then transduced Iars1+/+ and Iars1Y148C/R444* BMDMs with a lentiviral vector encoding mRFP-GFP-LC3 to monitor autophagic flux. Following autophagy induction, the GFP signal was quenched in control cells, while most Iars1Y148C/R444* cells retained both GFP and RFP fluorescence, indicating the accumulation of autophagosomes without efficient degradation (Fig. 4E). Together, these findings demonstrate that partial loss of Iars1activity leads to defective autophagosome-lysosome fusion in macrophages, impairing cholesterol catabolism and disrupting pulmonary surfactant clearance.

Iars1 deficiency impairs lysosome degradation in macrophages through the regulation of CTSZ

Inherited forms of PAP can arise from pathogenic variants in key genes such as CSF2RA, CSF2RB, or GATA2, which affect the number or function of alveolar macrophages [20, 52]. Western blot analysis of lung tissue from 2-month-old Iars1Y148C/R444* and Iars1+/+ mice revealed no significant differences in Csf2ra or Gata2 protein expression (Fig. 5A). To explore how partial loss of Iars1 activity impairs lysosomal degradation at the translational level, we performed proteomic analysis of BMDMs from Iars1Y148C/R444* and Iars1+/+ BMDMs (Fig. 5B). Among the 132 upregulated and 98 downregulated proteins identified in the Iars1 Y148C/R444*-deficient group, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in autophagy and endocytosis pathways (Fig. 5B). Of the 77 significantly downregulated proteins (fold change > 1.2, p < 0.05), only CTSZ and TPCN1 (two-pore channel 1) were directly involved in lysosomal function (Fig. 5C). However, western blot analysis only demonstrated a significant decrease in Ctsz protein levels in BMDMs from Iars1Y148C/R444* mice compared to those in Iars1+/+ controls (Fig. 5D). The protein level of Ctsz was reduced in BMDMs derived from Iars1Y148C/R444* mice, which exhibit partial loss of Iars1 activity, compared with BMDMs from Iars1+/+ (Fig. 5E). Ctsz mRNA expression in primary BMDMs was slightly increased in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice, but this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 5F), consistent with observations in Iars1 Y148C/R444* BMDMs (Fig. 5G).

To further investigate the role of CTSZ in surfactant metabolism, three distinct Ctsz siRNA constructs (siCtsz) were transfected individually into BMDMs, each significantly reducing Ctsz mRNA expression relative to siNC controls (Fig. 5H). Co-transfection of all three siCtsz constructs further decreased Ctsz protein levels, resulting in BMDMs with reduced Ctsz expression (Fig. 5I). Fluorescence imaging showed progressive accumulation of NBD-cholesterol and increased SP-C protein levels in Ctsz-knockdown BMDMs following exposure to exogenous SP-C compared with siNC-treated cells (Fig. 5J). Additionally, confocal imaging of Ctsz- knockdown BMDMs stained with LysoSensor Green DND-189 and LysoTracker Red DND-99 revealed lysosomal acidification defects (Fig. 5K). Together, these results indicate that reduced CTSZ expression causes lysosomal degradation defects in alveolar macrophages. Thus, CTSZ may act as a downstream mediator of IARS1 and likely contributes to the regulation of pulmonary surfactant clearance in macrophages.

IARS1 regulates the deubiquitination and stability of CTSZ

Recent studies have revealed multiple noncanonical functions of ARSs in processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, transcriptional regulation, and signal transduction [53, 54]. IARS1 has been identified as directly binding to BRCA1 (breast cancer 1, early onset) and regulating its ubiquitination and stability [6]. Because CTSZ protein levels were reduced without changes in CTSZ transcription, we next examined post-translational regulation. To investigate whether IARS1 regulates CTSZ degradation, BMDMs with partial loss of Iars1 activity were treated with cycloheximide, and CTSZ stability was assessed. Ctsz stability was markedly decreased in Iars1-knockdown cells compared with that in control cells (Fig. 6A). Conversely, in the presence of Iars1, Ctsz ubiquitination was significantly reduced, and the decrease in Ctsz levels associated with partial loss of Iars1 activity was restored by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. (Fig. 6B). To confirm the interaction between CTSZ and IARS1, we performed immunofluorescence staining for endogenous Ctsz in BMDMs. Ctsz was found to co-localize with Iars1 in primary Iars1+/+ BMDMs, but mutations in Iars1 reduced this interaction in primary Iars1Y148C/R444* BMDMs (Fig. 6C). To explore the potential effects of individual IARS1 variants, we generated Flag-tagged constructs encoding WT IARS1, IARS1-Y148C and IARS1-R444* and co-transfected HEK293T cells with HA-tagged CTSZ together with Flag-tagged WT or variant IARS1 constructs. The Y148C variant exhibited protein expression comparable to that of WT IARS1, whereas the R444* variant was not detectablly expressed (Fig. 6D). To determine whether IARS1 interacts with CTSZ, wo co-transfected HEK293T cells with HA-tagged CTSZ and Flag-tagged WT or variant IARS1 constructs. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis demonstrated that CTSZ interacted with IARS1. Expression of WT or the Y148C variant did not significantly alter CTSZ protein levels or ubiquitination, whereas cells transfected with the R444* construct exhibited markedly reduced CTSZ levels and ubiquitination (Fig. 6D and E). These findings suggest that the two variants may have distinct molecular consequences and support a role for IARS1 in the regulation of CTSZ stability and ubiquitination.

To assess whether Ctsz could rescue the functional deficits caused by Iars1 mutation, Iars1-depleted BMDMs were transduced with lentivirus expressing WT Ctsz. The fluorescence intensity of SP-C was significantly reduced following the addition of exogenous SP-C in Iars1Y148C/R444* BMDMs transduced with lentivirus-Ctsz (WT) compared with those receiving vector control (Fig. 6F). Similarly, the fluorescence intensity of cholesterol ester was significantly decreased after cholesterol ester treatment in Iars1Y148C/R444* BMDMs expressing lentivirus-Ctsz (WT) compared with vector controls (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results indicate that CTSZ degradation is primarily regulated by IARS1 and that reduced interaction between these proteins decreases CTSZ stability.

Discussion

IARS1 is a conserved enzyme that performs nontranslational roles in processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, mitochondrial regulation, and post-translational modification [6, 46]. In this study, we describe a mouse model carrying compound heterozygous Iars1 variants that exhibits PAP-like lesions, postnatal growth retardation, and cholestasis, features that are largely consistent with recently reported human cases [9–11]. Functional studies demonstrated that the biallelic pathogenic variants cause loss of function and may contribute to de novo dominant presentations. Our findings provide insight into the mechanisms underlying IARS1-associated PAP and support a model in which partial loss of IARS1 activity is associated with impaired autophagic and lysosomal function in alveolar macrophages, potentially involving CTSZ dysregulation.

Iars1 Y148C/R444* biallelic mutant mice in our study exhibited postnatal growth retardation, hypotonia, sparse hair, and PAP-like lesions, thereby recapitulating several key clinical features observed in patients and more closely resembling the human phenotype than previously reported mouse or Japanese black cattle models [46, 55]. Nevertheless, this model does not fully reproduce the spectrum of manifestations associated with IARS1-related disease. For example, liver disease, endocrine abnormalities, and immune dysfunction were not comprehensively evaluated or reproduced in the current study. Homozygous missense mutation in exon 3 (V79L) [46] and exon 4 (Y148C) did not alter the protein level of Iars1 in Iars1V79L and Iars1Y148C mice. However, biallelic mutations significantly decreased Iars1 protein expression in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice. These findings suggest that the Y148C and R444* variants have distinct molecular consequences. Whereas the Y148C variant exhibited protein expression levels comparable to those of WT IARS1, the R444* variant showed markedly reduced expression, suggesting that truncation of the C-terminal region may impair protein stability. However, the precise functional consequences of these variants require further investigation. To determine whether partial loss of IARS1 activity alters systemic isoleucine homeostasis, plasma isoleucine concentrations were measured. No significant differences were observed between Iars1Y148C/R444* mice and Iars1+/+ mice.

Our results demonstrate that mice with partial loss of Iars1 activity exhibit PAP-like accumulation of surfactant proteins within the alveoli. PAS and ORO staining of BALF revealed alveoli filled with proteinaceous and lipid-rich material. Immunoblot analysis of BALF confirmed SP accumulation, while measurements of total cholesterol, lipids, and phospholipids indicated abnormal lipid deposition within alveolar spaces. These findings parallel those reported in PAP patients, including congenital forms caused by pathogenic variants in ABCA3, SFTPB, and SFTPC [56–60]. Similar to SFTPC pathogenic variantsthat cause aberrant SP-C accumulation, Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibited elevated SP-C levels in the alveolar compartment, indicating a PAP-like phenotype.

Metabolomic analysis of lungs from mice harboring Iars1 variants revealed lysosomal pathway abnormalities in macrophages. Macrophages are critical for lipid storage, clearance of apoptotic or damaged cells, pathogen defense, and maintenance of tissue homeostasis [61–64]. The relatively mild spontaneous pulmonary phenotype observed in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice required an inflammatory challenge to more clearly reveal macrophage dysfunction and PAP-like pathological alterations. Because monocyte-derived macrophages originate from circulating monocytes [65], and given our single-cell RNA sequencing data showing monocyte dysfunction, we hypothesize that partial loss of IARS1 activity in pulmonary macrophages contribute to PAP development. Consistent with this, pulmonary macrophages in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice exhibited lipid accumulation and surfactant protein deposition. Furthermore, the percentage of alveolar macrophages was significantly reduced, consistent with previous findings that pathogenic CSF2RB variants impairs surfactant clearance and increases susceptibility to pulmonary infections [49].

Proteomic profiling of BMDMs revealed abnormalities in lysosomal pathways, and functional assays confirmed lysosomal acidification defects in Iars1Y148C/R444* BMDMs. Lysosomes are essential degradative organelles that fuse with endosomes and autophagosomes [51] and mediate pulmonary surfactant processing and turnover [66]. In vitro, partial loss of Iars1 activity led to defective autophagosome-lysosome fusion, resulting in impaired lysosomal degradation. CTSZ, a cysteine protease involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, fatty acid degradation, and cancer progression [41, 43, 67], was identified as a candidate mediator of the cellular phenotypes associated with partial loss of IARS1 activity. Ctsz knockdown led to the accumulation of surfactant proteins and cholesterol in macrophages, while Ctsz overexpression rescued these phenotypes in BMDMs with partial loss of Iars1 activity.

IARS1 is classified as a class Ia ARS and contains a UNE-I domain at its C-terminal end. The UNE-I domain consists of tandem repeats of ubiquitin regulatory X (UBX) motifs, which function to protect BRCA1 stability by preventing ubiquitin-mediated degradation. In our study, the Iars1 variants exhibited distinct IARS1 protein expression patterns. The Y148C variant was expressed at levels comparable to WT, suggesting that this missense variant does not substantially affect IARS1 protein stability. In contrast, the R444* variant showed markedly reduced expression, likely due to truncation of the C-terminus. In Co-IP experiments, CTSZ co-precipitated with WT IARS1 and the Y148C variant, whereas substantially less CTSZ was detected in cells transfected with the R444* construct. These results demonstrate an interaction between IARS1 and CTSZ; however, the precise domains mediating this interaction and the individual contributions of the Y148C and R444* variants remain to be determined.

There are several limitations to our study. The Iars1Y148C/R444* mouse model was generated based on the phenotype of our patient cohort. Thus, it remains uncertain whether other combinations of Iars1 variants would produce similar phenotypes. While Ctsz overexpression in BMDMs showed potential to rescue the effects of Iars1 deficiency, further in vivo validation is required. A limitation of the present study is that most functional analyses were performed using compound heterozygous Iars1Y148C/R444* mice or cells with partial loss of Iars1 activity. Although these models closely recapitulate the genotype and associated cellular phenotypes, they do not fully distinguish the individual effects of the Y148C and R444* variants. Additional studies using variant-specific models will be necessary to clarify the contribution of each variant to the observed phenotype. Future studies should explore therapeutic strategies such as small-molecule administration, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene delivery, and other modalities to better understand the potential for translational intervention. Although we observed a significant reduction in the proportion of alveolar macrophages in PAP-like Iars1Y148C/R444* mice, we did not comprehensively characterize alveolar macrophage subsets. Therefore, the relative contributions of tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (TR-AMs) and monocyte-derived alveolar macrophages (Mo-AMs) to this phenotype remain unclear. Future studies incorporating more refined macrophage subset analyses will be required to determine how partial loss of IARS1 activity affects alveolar macrophage heterogeneity and function during PAP pathogenesis. A recent report indicated that amino acid supplementation in patients with ARS deficiencies can promote growth, prevent infections, and reduce oxygen dependency [12]. Based on these findings, our next objective is to investigate whether dietary isoleucine supplementation can ameliorate the phenotypes observed in Iars1Y148C/R444* mice. Although our findings support a model in which partial loss of IARS1 activity is associated with altered CTSZ ubiquitination and stability, the precise molecular mechanisms and the specific domains mediating this interaction remain to be determined.

In summary, we generated a compound heterozygous Iars1Y148C/R444* mouse model to investigate the pathogenic mechanisms associated with partial loss of IARS1 activity. The compound heterozygous mice exhibited PAP-like accumulation of SPs within alveolar spaces, postnatal growth retardation, and neurodevelopmental abnormalities. We demonstrate that partial loss of IARS1 activity disrupts the post-translational regulation of CTSZ by impairing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Moreover, exogenous expression of Ctsz restored surfactant clearance in Iars1Y148C/R444* alveolar macrophages. Collectively, our findings support the role of IARS1 as a PAP-associated gene and provide mechanistic insight into how partial loss of IARS1 activity may contribute to PAP pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study involving human participants was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians for participation in this study. All animal procedures, including animal husbandry, LPS administration, and tissue collection, were approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Approval No. 2021 − 172) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

BALF collection and processing

BALF obtained from mice via sequential lung lavages using five 1-ml aliquots of sterile saline was prepared for analysis following the previously outlined procedure [68]. Cell pellets recovered from centrifugation were used for PAS or ORO staining, alveolar macrophage phagocytosis, differential cell count, and transmission electron microscopy. Supernatant was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo, USA) and further concentrated using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, USA) for western blot.

Transmission electron microscopy

Alveolar macrophages were isolated from the lungs of mice subjected to a 7-day isoleucine-deficient diet, followed by LPS stimulation and fixation in glutaraldehyde. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were then prepared, post-fixed in osmic acid, dehydrated, infiltrated with propylene oxide (Aladdin, China), and embedded using an SPI-PonTM Kit (SPI Science, USA). The samples were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and captured using an HT-7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo).

Quantification of internalized lipid

AMs, RAW264.7 (RRID: cvcl_0493), and BMDM cells that underwent specific treatment were treated with 2 µM NBD-cholesterol or 4 µM cholesterol ester in a 24-well dish [69]. The dishes were rinsed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 1 min, 5 min and 10 min respectively at room temperature. DAPI was stained for 10 min. The fluorescence intensity of fluorescent cholesterol conjugates or cholesterol ester in cells was observed at 488–555 nm.

Mouse studies and interventions

All of the animal experiments were performed in accordance with guidelines and complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal testing and research approved by the Committee on Animal Research and Ethics of Fudan University. All animals were maintained in the C57BL/6J background, and bred under pathogen-free conditions. Male mice aged 1 day to 8 weeks were used in this study. Animals were housed in plastic cages under temperature − (24 ± 2 °C) and humidity-controlled (52 ± 10%) conditions with a 12-h/12‐h light/dark cycle and free access to food and fresh water. Iars1Y148C/Y148C with Iars1+/R444* mice were generated in Biocytogen, Ltd (USA). (XM000975) by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. The Y148C point mutation was induced using the crRNA guiding sequence (5′-ATTAGTTATTCGCTTCATTAAGG-3′) targeting exon 5 of Iars1 (NCBI accession number: NM_172015), along with a single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN) for targeted insertion (5′-CTATTTCTAGCTCTAAAACTAATGAAGCGAATAACTAATCCTATAGTGAGTCGTATTA-3′). This strategy resulted in a point mutation causing a single amino acid alteration. Similarly, the R443X point mutation was generated using the crRNA guiding sequence (5′-AGTTTGAGTCCCTGGTATGTGGG-3′) targeting exon 14 of Iars1, along with a ssODN for targeted insertion (5′-CTATTTCTAGCTCTAAAACACGCTAGTGGAAGTTAAGGTCCTATAGTGAGTCGTATTA-3′). Iars1Y148C/R444* mice were produced by crossing Iars1Y148C/Y148C with Iars1+/R444* mice. The genotyped of Iars1Y148C/Y148C, Iars1+/R444*, and Iars1Y148C/R444* mice were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic DNA. To induce the PAP-like phenotype, Iars1Y148C/R444* mice were administered LPS intratracheally at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Forty-eight hours after LPS administration, the mice were sacrificed and lung tissues were collected for further analyses.

Body weight and survival rate curves

Iars1+/+ and Iars1Y148C/R444* mice were bred and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility. The body weight of the mice was monitored weekly from birth until they reached 8 weeks of age. Averaged body weight curves were measured for both Iars1+/+ and Iars1Y148C/R444* mice. The body weight curves and the survival rate curves were generated using Graphpad Prism software version 10.

Histology

BALF of mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained by periodic acid-Schiff according to our previous study [68]. The main procedure of ORO staining involved incubation in isopropanol, staining with Oil Red O, followed by washing in isopropanol, and a final staining with hematoxylin.

Nissl Staining

Mouse brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (in PBS) overnight. The tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned coronally at 10 μm thickness. The slices were stained with Nissl in accordance with our previously established protocol [70].

LC/MS metabolomics

Untargeted lipidomics relative-quantitative analysis was conducted by Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China) [71]. Samples were homogenized using an MP homogenizer (24 × 2, 6.0 M/S, 60s, twice) with methanol. They were then mixed with methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and subjected to ultrasound, followed by centrifugation and drying under nitrogen. The lipid extracts were redissolved in isopropanol/acetonitrile, centrifuged, then sampler was set to a reverse-phase CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters) using a high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Nexera LC-30 A, SHIMADZU). Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted using a Q-Exactive Plus instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in either negative (ESI-) or positive (ESI+) electrospray ionization mode. LipidSearch software version 4.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to determine lipid species for raw data processing, peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction.

Flow cytometry

The isolated alveolar macrophages from adherent cells of CD45 + cells from lung tissue were incubated with the following antibodies: allophycocyanin-cyanine 7-conjugated anti-FSV 780 (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) used for the detection of dead cells, Fluorescein Isothiocyanate-conjugated anti-CD45 (Biolegend, USA), phycoerythrin-conjugated anti-F4/80 (Biolegend, USA), allophycocyanin-conjugated anti-CD11c (Biolegend, USA). Alveolar macrophages were identified as CD45+F4/80+CD11c+ [72] (Fig S2). Cell analysis was conducted using a FACSCalibur with CellQuest software (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar, USA).

Cell culture

The RAW264.7, MLE12 (CVCL_3751) and HEK293T (CVCL_0063) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Va, USA). HEK293T and MLE12 cells were incubated in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), penicillin (100 IU/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RAW264.7 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (4.5 g/L) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 IU/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml). All experiments were performed using mycoplasma-free cells.

AM cells were sorted by CD45+ from the lung of mice as CD45+, cultivated in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), penicillin (100 IU/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 2 h, cell cultivated medium was discarded and added fresh.

BMDM cells were isolated from Iars1+/+ and Iars1Y148C/R444* mice as previously described [73] and were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum and 10 ng/ml GM-CSF (Bio-Techne, USA).

Bone marrow, blood and tissue leukocyte isolation

Blood was obtained by retro-orbital plexus bleeding from mice anesthetized with diethyl ether, followed by perfusion with 10 ml of PBS via the right ventricle. Lungs, femurs, and tibias were subsequently dissected. Bone marrow cells were flushed out from the opened distal and proximal ends of bones. After passing through a 70 μm cell strainer, cell suspensions were centrifuged and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Biolegend, USA). Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 ng/ml GM-CSF, and assayed after 5–7 days [74]. AM cells were generated from mouse lung tissue using the lung dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) in conjunction with the gentleMACS™ Dissociators (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the provided protocol. The resulting single-cell suspensions were filtered through both a 70 μm cell strainer and a 40 μm cell strainer, followed by centrifugation and separation using CD45-positive microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) in combination with LS Columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). CD45+ cells, cultivated with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, were seeded on 6-well plates and 24-well plates for 2 h. The percentage of alveolar macrophages of adherent cells was identified using anti-CD11c and F4/80 antibodies by flow cytometry analysis.

Proteomics

Primary BMDM cells were cultivated with DMEM for 24 h and lysed in 6 M GndHCl per 50 mM TEAB buffer via sonication. Further, the concentration of protein was assessed by Bradford assay; then, the proteins were digested overnight in Trypsin (MS grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), subsequently acidified with TFA, deionized using SepPak C18 cartridges and freeze-dried in a vacuum dryer. Peptide fractionation was performed by Oasis MCX Cartridges [75]. Briefly, lyophilized sample peptides dissolved in 900 µL loading buffer were loaded onto SCX-SPE column, and subsequently washed with elution buffer. All four fractions were lyophilized in a vacuum drier, reconstituted in 900 µL 0.1% TFA (v/v), desalted with SepPak C18 cartridges and quantified using the BCA assay. LC-MS/MS analysis of a total of 1 µg peptides was carried out on a nano-HPLC chromatography system coupled to a hybrid trapped ion mobility spectrometry quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TIMS-TOF Pro, Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA) via a CaptiveSpray nanoelectrospray ion source. PEAKS Online Xpro Software (v1.4) was used to determine peptide and protein identifications for raw data processing from the Swiss-Prot database.

Phagocytosis

BMDM cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 24-well culture plates, treated with DMEM for 24 h, and 7.5 µl latex bead solution added (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 3 h, the dishes were washed with PBS for three times and fixed with paraformaldehyde. Then nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min and images were scanned using a Lecia confocal microscope.

Lysosome acid assay

BMDM cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 24-well culture plates, obtained from both Iars1Y148C/R444* mice and Iars1+/+ mice, and then transfected with siRNA. Following 48-hour incubation, LysoSensor Green DND-189 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) along with Hoechst 33,258 were added, and the cells were subsequently cultured for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity of live cell was captured using a Leica confocal microscope.

Western blot

Cells and lung tissues were extracted in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplied with protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010, MCE, NJ, USA) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was assessed by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4℃ against SP-B (1:800; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), proSP-C (1:2000; Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), SP-A (1:2000; Millipore, USA), SP-D (1:2000; Abcam, USA), P62 (1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), LC3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Ubiquitin (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Vinculin (1:4000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), IARS1 (1:1000; Bethyl Laboratories, USA), CTSZ (1:1000; Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China), and β-tubulin (1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The secondary antibodies, conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following this, the bands were detected using the Pierce™ ECL western blot substrate kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cell immunohistochemistry

Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for subsequent analyses. Tissues were cut into 4–5 μm, and followed by deparaffinization and antigen retrieval. The dishes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and then incubated with antibodies overnight at 4℃ against SP-A (1:800; Millipore, USA), proSP-B (1:800; Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), proSP-C (1:800; Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), mature SP-B (1:800; Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), mature SP-C (1:800; Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), SP-D (1:800; Abcam, USA), IARS1 (1:800; Bethyl Laboratories, USA), CTSZ (1:800; R&D, USA), LAMP1 (1:800; Abcam, USA) OR F4/80 (1:800, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase were detected with matched primary antibodies, such as 555-carbocyanine-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:800 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA), 488-carbocyanine-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:800 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA), 488-carbocyanine-conjugated Donkey anti-goat (1:800 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA), 647-carbocyanine-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:800 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA).

Quantification of internalized SP-C

AMs and BMDM cells, which had undergone the specific treatment mentioned above, were treated with 100ng SPC (Cloud-Clone Corp, China) in a 12-well dish (1 mL). After 1 h, the dishes were washed with PBS and fixed with paraformaldehyde. The primary antibody anti-SP-C was incubated at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with 555-carbocyanine-conjugated goat anti-rabbit as described above, and DAPI.

Plasmids and DNA transfection

The flag tag was amplified by polymerase chain reaction and connected with pPB vector (gift from Weijun Feng, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China) using one step cloning kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. FLAG-tagged full-length IARS1 was constructed by cloning the corresponding human cDNA into pPB vector. The pPB-N-Flag-IARS1 plasmid variants were generated by KOD-plus mutagenesis kit (TOYOBO CO., LTD, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The HA tag was amplified by PCR and cloned into pLVX vector (YouBia, China) using one step cloning kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Shanghai, China) mentioned above. HA-tagged full-length CTSZ was constructed by cloning the corresponding human cDNA into pLVX vector. Indicated plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using PolyJet Transfection Reagent (SL100688, SignaGen Laboratories, Rockville, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA constructs, lentivirus production, and infection

The plasmid pLVX-GFP-RFP-LC3, acquired from YouBia Company (Hunan, China), was co-transfected with packaging vectors pSP-AX2 and pMD2.g into HEK293T cells to generate lentivirus. At 48–72 h following transfection, the cell culture supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Millipore, USA). Subsequently, it was transferred to a 100k ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, USA) and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C to produce a high-titer lentivirus. pLVX-GFP was used as control. To detect the autophagolysosome, BMDM cells were transduced with lentivirus concentrated using PEG 8000 in the presence of 10 µg/ml polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After 72 h, the cells were incubated in DMEM for 24 h and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde.

Immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and transfected with the indicated expression plasmids. After 48 h, the cells were lysed using a mild lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were then subjected to ultrasound and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatants were incubated with Anti-DYKDDDDK Affinity Beads or Anti-HA Affinity Beads (Smart Lifesciences, Changzhou, China) overnight at 4 °C. The beads were rinsed four times with ice-cold mild lysis buffer before being boiled with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer for western blot analysis. For ubiquitination analysis, cells were pretreated with 20 µM proteasome inhibitor MG132 (MCE, China) for 4 h prior to sample collection.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10. To ensure the reproducibility of each trial, three independent experiments were performed. Group comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test and two-tailed Mann-Whitney tests. The bars depict the means ± SEM. The survival data were statistically compared using the Mantel-Cox (log-rank) test. Statistically significant results were defined as P values < 0.05.
