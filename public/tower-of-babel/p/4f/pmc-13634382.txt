Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) affects up to 10%-15% of hospitalized patients and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.1,2 Acute decompensated heart failure (HF) commonly occurs concurrently with AKI. Intact kidney function is essential for recovery from acute HF. However, acute decompensated HF can cause or worsen AKI through kidney hypoperfusion and sodium and fluid retention. These pathways create a vicious cycle of worsening cardiac and renal function.3,4 It is unclear why certain individuals with comparable clinical trajectories of acute HF develop AKI. There are few reliable tools to identify individuals vulnerable to cardiorenal syndrome. Early identification of these individuals could lead to strategies to mitigate or prevent AKI in the setting of HF.

Current assessments of kidney function mainly focus on glomerular metrics like serum creatinine (SCr) levels and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). However, these measures often overlook kidney tubular health, which is vital for overall kidney function. Emerging biomarkers such as the levels of uromodulin (UMOD), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), alpha-1 microglobulin (A1M), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) show potential in the detection of kidney tubular injury.5, 6, 7, 8 Recent studies indicate these biomarkers can predict AKI risk in patients with acute HF, especially under pre-renal stressors.5

In this study, we sought to evaluate the associations between markers of kidney tubule dysfunction and injury and future risk of AKI after acute HF.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection – The Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke Study

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Alabama at Birmingham. We performed a case-control study using data from the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study. The REGARDS study is a longitudinal cohort encompassing 30,239 community-dwelling adults aged ≥45 years from across the continental United States.9 The study enrolled participants between January 2003 and October 2007. Baseline assessments included an interview that collected demographics, health behaviors, diagnosed medical conditions, and medication usage. Research personnel collected physiologic measurements and obtained blood and urine samples from each participant. The REGARDS study identified health and hospitalization events through telephone follow-up conducted with participants at 6-month intervals. The REGARDS study has followed participants since baseline.

Selection of Participants

The identification and adjudication of acute HF hospitalizations were performed by a separate REGARDS study effort and team distinct from our renal research team. Based on information from the 6-month follow-ups, the REGARDS study personnel retrieved medical records for hospitalizations related to acute heart disease.10 Identification of HF as a cause of hospitalization was based on signs and symptoms (eg, orthopnea, exertional dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, nocturnal cough, rales on pulmonary examination, cardiomegaly, jugular vein distension, central venous pressure > 16 mm Hg, peripheral edema, pleural effusion, hepatomegaly, and heart rate > 120 bpm), laboratory results (eg, B-type natriuretic peptide levels), imaging (eg, echocardiogram or contrast ventriculography), and ≥ 4.5 kg of weight loss with diuresis. Trained clinician-investigators reviewed the medical records in duplicate, with disagreements resolved by discussion.

Biomarker Analysis

Study personnel obtained blood and urine samples during the initial in-home visit. The samples were centrifuged within 2 hours of collection, placed on ice, and shipped overnight to the REGARDS study central laboratory at the University of Vermont. Upon arrival, laboratory researchers centrifuged the samples at 30,000 g and 4 °C.

The biomarker level measurements for the current study were performed by Brigham and Women’s Hospital BioCon Central Laboratory. KIM-1 and EGF levels were measured using a microbead-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay from the Bio-Rad Bio-Plex Luminex 200 reader (Hercules). The lower limit of detection (LLOD) and interassay coefficients of variation (CV) for the KIM-1 level were 1.98 pg/mL and 7.8%, respectively. For the EGF level, the LLOD value was 0.313 pg/mL, and the CV value was 9.2%. We measured A1M levels using the SIEMENS BNII Nephelometer (Tarrytown) with an LLOD value of 5.63 mg/L and an intra-assay CV value of 1.2-4.0%. We measured UMOD levels using the R-Plex Protocol on the Meso Scale Discovery platform (Rockville) with an LLOD value of 244.14 pg/mL and an interassay CV value of 6.4%. We measured all KIM-1, EGF, and UMOD biomarker levels twice and averaged the results to improve precision. We measured A1M levels only once because of its low CV value.

Outcomes—AKI

The primary outcome was AKI during acute HF hospitalization. Trained research personnel reviewed medical records for each HF hospitalization, abstracting the date and time of hospitalization, history of end-stage kidney disease before hospitalization, performance of coronary artery bypass graft surgery, all values of SCr levels, hemoglobin concentration, blood urea nitrogen levels, and albumin values when available throughout the admission. We conducted quality checks of all records, including verification of timing and values of the SCr level. SCr level measurements occurred according to local practice. Based on the Acute Kidney Injury Network (AKIN) criteria, we defined AKI as either (1) a change in SCr levels of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL between the lowest and highest SCr levels or (2) treatment with acute hemodialysis.11,12 We used the Acute Kidney Injury Network rather than other AKI systems because the analysis focused on change from the first inpatient creatinine levels, not the prehospitalization baseline. To ensure a more robust assessment of AKI, we did not limit ascertainment to SCr levels obtained within a 48-hour window.13 Because they were not consistently documented, we did not define AKI based upon urine output.

Covariates

Questionnaires were used for participants to report age, sex, and race. Research personnel calculated body mass index (BMI) using height and weight obtained at the baseline in-home visit. Diabetes history consisted of self-reported use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents, fasting serum glucose concentration ≥ 126 mg/dL, or a nonfasting serum glucose concentration ≥ 200 mg/dL. Hypertension history consisted of self-reported use of blood pressure-lowering medications or a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg at the baseline visit. History of coronary heart disease included evidence of myocardial infarction on the baseline electrocardiograph, self-report of a prior history of a cardiac procedure, or self-reported history of myocardial infarction.

The parent REGARDS study determined urine albumin excretion levels using nephelometry using the BNII ProSpec nephelometer (now Siemens AG); urine creatinine excretion levels using the rate Jaffé method using the Modular-P chemistry analyzer (Roche/Hitachi); and baseline eGFR from SCr level measured using isotope dilution mass spectrometry-traceable methods using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation without race.14

Data Analysis

Among all the REGARDS study participants, we identified the first acute HF hospitalization through 2018. We then identified all acute HF hospitalizations with potential AKI, excluding those without biomarkers available and where the patient reported a history of kidney transplantation or chronic dialysis. We created a frequency-matched sample with approximately equal numbers of randomly selected AKI cases and non-AKI controls. We summarized baseline participant demographic characteristics, health behaviors, and kidney function using descriptive statistics.15 We evaluated interrelationships between biomarker levels using Spearman correlations. We determined the associations between each tubule dysfunction marker level and AKI using logistic regression. We modeled the marker levels as a continuous linear exposure (per log2 increase). We also categorized each biomarker level into quartiles. We repeated the models adjusted for age, sex, race, urine creatinine level (to account for differences in urine tonicity), time from the REGARDS study baseline visit to admission for heart failure, diabetes, hypertension, and BMI. We fitted another model with additional adjustments for eGFR and urine albumin level measured at the baseline. We set statistical significance at 0.05 (2-tailed). We performed all analyses using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

From 1,846 participants with hospitalizations for probable or possible acute HF, we identified a cohort of 600 AKI and 600 non-AKI (Fig 1). After excluding participants without available urine specimens, there were 422 with AKI and 422 without AKI. After excluding those with a history of chronic dialysis (n=23) or kidney transplant (n=2) before AHF, we retained 400 with AKI and 419 without AKI in the analysis.

Median time from the REGARDS study enrollment (when blood and urine were sampled for biomarker level measurements) to HF hospitalization was 5.6 (interquartile range [IQR], 2.6-9.4) years. Time to HF hospitalization was similar between participants with 5.7 (IQR, 2.9-9.1) years versus without AKI, 5.4 (IQR, 2.5-10.4) years, on the basis of frequency matching on this variable. The median minimum SCr level during hospitalization was similar between participants with AKI (1.2; IQR, 0.9-1.7 mg/dL) and without AKI (1.0; IQR, 0.8-1.3 mg/dL). Among those developing AKI, the median maximum SCr level was 1.9 (IQR, 1.4-2.7) mg/dL. Among participants with AKI, hemodialysis was performed in 10. Among the 40 AKI participants, AKI severities were stage I, n=290 (72.5%); stage II, n=61 (15.3%); and stage III, n=49 (12.3%). Approximately half (55.8%) of AKI occurred within the first 3 SCr level measurements during hospitalization.

Higher urine EGF level was associated with a lower risk of AKI in the unadjusted model (odds ratio [OR], 0.29; 95% CI, 0.19-0.44; Table 4). The results minimally changed after adjustment for age, sex, race, time from baseline to HF hospitalization, urine creatinine, diabetes, hypertension, and BMI (OR comparing the fourth to the first quartile: 0.27; 95% CI, 0.16-0.46) and after further adjustment for eGFR and urine albumin (OR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.27-0.91). The results were similar when EGF level was analyzed as a continuous variable, though the results were no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (OR per 2-fold higher EGF level, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.77-1.04). No other urine biomarker levels were consistently associated with incident AKI in multivariable-adjusted models, though a higher UMOD level was directionally associated with lower risk of AKI in fully adjusted models.

Discussion

In this study, using data from the REGARDS cohort, higher EGF was associated with a lower risk of future AKI following hospitalization for HF. Other measures of kidney tubular function and injury did not exhibit consistent associations with future risk of AKI after adjustment for potential confounders. These results are in line with prior studies in the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) blood pressure trial, which showed that higher levels of EGF and UMOD were associated with future development of AKI in a subgroup of individuals with CKD.5,16, 17, 18, 19 In aggregate, these data suggest that biomarker levels of kidney tubule function provide important insights into future vulnerability to AKI in those admitted for acute HF exacerbation.

EGF level is a key mediator of renal tubular repair, promoting epithelial cell proliferation and survival after AKI.20 Urinary EGF level, primarily produced by distal tubular cells, declines during AKI and correlates with injury severity, making it a potential marker of tubular integrity and recovery capacity.20,21 Baseline EGF levels may plausibly reflect intrinsic tubular health and regenerative potential, with higher EGF suggesting preserved nephron integrity and a lower propensity for maladaptive repair. Prior studies have linked EGF levels with hypertension and chronic kidney disease progression.22, 23, 24 Studies linking higher urinary EGF excretion levels to favorable renal outcomes after cardiac surgery suggest its relevance in HF populations where recurrent AKI episodes are common.25

An interesting observation was that the odds of AKI differed inconsistently across biomarker quarters. Specifically, the third—but not the fourth—quartile of UMOD level was associated with lower odds of AKI. Quartile-based analyses are inherently subject to random fluctuation and are provided primarily to evaluate the functional form of the point estimates across quartiles. Even if the true relationship is monotonic, the observed ORs across categories may not decrease in a perfectly ordered fashion because of chance. The overlapping CIs between Q3 and Q4 would suggest that the difference between them may not be statistically meaningful. In addition, the association between UMOD level and AKI risk may not be strictly linear. It is possible that the strongest protective association occurs in the mid-to-high range (Q3), with attenuation at the extreme high end (Q4), potentially reflecting measurement variability at the high end of concentrations or residual confounding that differs across quartiles.

These results add to knowledge regarding risk factors detectable at times of health that can inform future risk of AKI. An understanding of the long-term ability of the kidney to withstand stressors could provide new opportunities for closer surveillance and prevention, particularly in vulnerable high-risk individuals. The identification of biomarkers associated with future AKI risk is an important first step toward profiling cumulative AKI risk. As has occurred with like acute conditions such as myocardial infarction and stroke, the early identification of individuals vulnerable to AKI could lead to novel measures to mitigate the effects of or prevent AKI.26

There are important strengths and limitations of this study. We used a novel design that enabled us to elucidate connections between baseline kidney health and AKI occurring in the distant future. We leveraged the REGARDS study, which is 1 of the nation’s largest cohorts with comprehensive health information over 2 decades of follow-up. Specially trained research personnel identified AKI using structured abstraction methods. Billing codes and administrative data have poor sensitivity for capturing AKI events in hospitalized patients.

Limitations of this study include a focus on adults over 45 years old. Although we identified AKI based upon the initial measured SCr level, AKI may have been in progress before hospital presentation. We did not limit AKI ascertainment to SCr levels obtained within a 48-hour window because shorter time windows may bias toward lower AKI detection.13 Urine output information was insufficient for classifying AKI. We had limited information on the course or acuity of HF hospitalizations. Although changes in SCr levels may result from fluid overload or over-diuresis, there are no validated methods for adjusting AKI classification to account for the course of HF treatment. We focused on printed medical records for specific hospitalizations; other hospitalizations or health data may be available with linkage through the electronic health records system. The REGARDS study used spot urine samples, which may not capture intraindividual or interindividual variability in the excretion of individual solutes and may have biased results toward the null. We selected modeling approaches that we felt were appropriate for the available data, including adjusting for baseline kidney function. Other studies have used alternate model approaches such as normalizing biomarker levels by urinary creatinine levels, but we do not expect markedly different results with alternate modeling strategies.27 Our study highlighted potential linkages between select tubule dysfunction marker levels and long-term AKI risk, but AKI may occur through separate pathways. We did not link to long-term mortality.

Renal functional reserve testing using protein load or stress ultrasonography has been shown to predict AKI risk. We were unable to compare renal functional reserve testing using protein loading or stress ultrasonography to the urine biomarker levels examined in this study. Renal functional reserve testing lacks standardized protocols and is difficult to implement retrospectively or at scale, limiting feasibility in both large epidemiologic cohorts and clinical practice, unlike urine biomarker levels.28

In conclusion, among community-dwelling adults, higher urine EGF levels were associated with future risk of AKI during acute HF hospitalization.
