Assessment of knowledge, attitudes and behaviors related to self-medication practices among the adult population during the COVID-19 pandemic in Italy
Authors: Concetta Paola Pelullo, Chiara Lombardi, Giorgia Della Polla, Francesco Napolitano, Gabriella Di Giuseppe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors about self-medication practices during the COVID-19 pandemic and any changes from the period before the current health emergency. A cross-sectional study was conducted from June to December 2021 in the Campania region, Southern of Italy. Of the 590 questionnaires distributed, 525 returned for an overall response rate of 89%. Overall, 66.8% of respondents had a good knowledge of self-medication and adverse drug reaction (ADR). Moreover, 58% of respondents stated that they had used an oral medication without the physician’s prescription in the last year. Multiple logistic regression analyses showed that respondents with a lower perception of their health status (p = 0.05), who knew the definition of self-medication (p = 0.034), who practiced self-medication before COVID-19 pandemic (p < 0.001), and who reported at least one suspected symptom for COVID-19 disease in the last year (p = 0.002), were significantly more likely to practice self-medication. Our findings supported the needs to limit the risks of self-medication through the active involvement of physicians and pharmacists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22124-w.Keywords: Adverse drug reaction, COVID-19, Italy, Self-medication, SurveySubject terms: Health care, Public health

Introduction

According to WHO definition, self-medication refers to the use of medication to treat self-diagnosed symptoms or intermittent use or continued use of a prescribed medication for chronic or recurrent illnesses or symptoms1. Several studies conducted in many areas and different populations have shown that self-medication, when inappropriate, exposes especially the frailest individuals to an increased risk of side effects, adverse reactions, and drug interactions2–7. In addition, other studies have examined self-medication antibiotic practices (SMA) as a public health threat, such as increased risk of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and increased risk of adverse effects for patients8.

The COVID-19 pandemic, with the resulting measures to contain the contagion, has affected the organizational and care structure of the National Health Service. Since the World Health Organization declared that an outbreak of pneumonia caused by a new strain of Coronavirus never before identified in humans, classified as SARS-CoV-29, the increased attention given to public health protection has taken precedence over the care and specific needs of patients who have seen a decrease in contact with health care personnel. In particular, during the early epidemic phases, the effort to protect the health of the population entailed the attempt to contain morbidity and mortality by optimizing resources and strategies with consequent restrictions on access to general practitioners or specialists10. A study conducted by Gualano et al.11 has shown that indirect effects of COVID-19 have been important for vulnerable population that have suffered delays and interruptions of medical services. The difficulties in accessing health services and other factors such as, for example, lack of knowledge regarding side effects, adverse reactions and drug interactions, socio-economic status, fear of COVID-19 disease, beliefs and previous experiences/behaviors of patients, might have influenced the self-medication practices.

In literature, several studies have been conducted with the aim of exploring self-medication practice and related determinants12–16. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, several observational studies have been published exploring the determinants related to self-medication practices during the current health emergency among adults17–20, healthcare personnel21, and some professional groups22. Furthermore, less is known about self-medication practices during COVID-19 pandemic in Italy, which is known to have been one of the first in Europe to face the impact of the pandemic. However, the results of previous studies focused on specific populations or on antibiotic consumption during the pandemic period.

This study adds to previous studies by evaluating the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of the Italian adult population towards self-medication practices during the COVID-19 pandemic, namely by characterizing self-medication changes from the period pre-pandemic, the self-medication practices to prevent COVID-19 disease, and the related determinants.

Material and methods

Study setting and population

The present cross-sectional study was carried out between June and December 2021, in the cities of Naples, Benevento, and Salerno located in the Campania region in the south of Italy, among a sample of adults who attended the Public clinics to receive SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. In particular, sample inclusion criteria comprised being ≥ 18 years old, being able to give their consent to the survey, and being able to understand and fill in a self-administered questionnaire.

The minimum sample size required for the study was estimated at 323 subjects, assuming expected of self-medication practices during COVID-19 pandemic as 30%11,18,19,21–23, a relative precision of 5%, and a 95% confidence interval. Furthermore, assuming a response rate of 70%, the study would require a sample size of 461 adults.

Ethics approval and informed consent

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Teaching Hospital of the University of Campania ‘‘Luigi Vanvitelli” (approval number:0022403/i/2021). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study instrument

The information was collected through a standardized, self-administered, anonymous, and structured questionnaire. The survey instrument (Supplementary material) was built ad hoc for this investigation based on items of questionnaires used in several previous studies carried out by several research groups [12,13]. This consisted of 37 questions, including 20 multiple-choice questions, 6 statements involving 3 or 5-point Likert scale responses, and 11 socio-demographic questions. In particular, the questionnaire comprised the following four sections: (1) socio-demographic and anamnestic characteristics of the participants; (2) knowledge of self-medication, adverse drug reactions (ADR), and COVID-19 main symptoms, attitudes related to perceived risk of ADRs associated with self-medication practices, and questions regarding the reduction of medical visits due to fear of COVID-19 and perceived difficulty to access health services; (3) self-reported practices related to antibiotic use during COVID-19 pandemic; and (4) sources and needs of information regarding COVID-19. In the first section, socio-demographic characteristics and history of the study population have been acquired with open-ended and multiple-choice questions, including age, gender, marital status, number of sons/daughters, number of cohabiting, education level, employment status, a family member employed in health care, previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, comorbidities, and self-rated health status measured on a 10-point scale, with values ranging from 1 (low) to 10 (high). The second and third sections included questions with multiple-choice responses, while attitude related to perceived risk of ADRs associated with self-medication practices was measured on a 3-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from “agree” to “disagree”. In addition, participants were asked regarding the reduction of doctor visits or emergency room visits due to fear of contracting SARS-COV2 and the perceived difficulty of contacting the primary care physician or making hospital visits; these items were explored on a 3-point Likert-type scale with responses ranging from “agree” to “disagree.” The fourth part contained questions regarding behaviors towards self-medication practices with a multi-option response format. The last section explored the sources and needs for information on COVID-19 disease with the format of multiple-choice answers and open questions.

Study pilot

A pilot study was conducted on 50 subjects with the same inclusion criteria as in the main study to assess the clarity, content validity, and comprehensibility of the questionnaire, and no changes were made to the survey instrument. Data from the pilot study were included in the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were analyzed using STATA software, version 15.024. First, data were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical and dichotomic data and mean and standard deviations for continuous data. Second, bivariate appropriate analyses (t-test and χ2 test) were conducted to assess the relationships between several independent variables and the outcomes of interest. Then, in accordance to Hosmer and Lemeshow model building strategy25, the variables that in the univariate analysis showed an association with the outcomes of interest with a p value ≤ 0.25 were included in the multiple logistic regression models. A backward elimination procedure models with p values of > 0.2 and < 0.4 was applied for the inclusion and exclusion of the variables in the final models. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the analyses and to estimate the strength of association adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The following outcomes of interest were investigated: knowledge of self-medication and adverse drug reaction (no = 0; yes = 1), belief that self-medication increases the risk of ADRs (no = 0; yes = 1), self- medication practices in the last year (no = 0; yes = 1), and self-medication practices to prevent COVID-19 disease (no = 0; yes = 1). A description of the independent variables included in all models is reported in an additional file (Supplementary material).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Of the 590 questionnaires distributed, 525 were returned for an overall response rate of 89%.

The characteristics of the respondents are summarized in Table 1. More than half of the sample (61.4%) were females, with ages ranging from 18 to 93 years with an average of 44.5 years, 46.5% of the respondents were married or cohabited, and 49.4% had at least one son/daughter. A little more than half of the participants had a high school diploma (56%) and less than half were employed (44.6%). The average self-rated health status was 7.6 out of a score of 1 to 10, 47.6% had at least one chronic disease, 30.3% of respondents reported at least one suspected symptom of COVID-19 in the last year and, at the time of the survey, 11.1% of the sample had had a SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Participants’ knowledge and attitude

The respondents’ knowledge and attitude regarding self-medication practice and COVID-19 are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 66.8% of respondents had a good knowledge of self-medication and ADR. Multiple logistic regression analyses showed that respondents aged 39–49 years compared with those aged 18–30 years and females were significantly more likely to have this knowledge. Furthermore, educational level has an impact on the level of knowledge, since respondents with middle school or lower and high school were less knowledgeable compared to those had university degree (Model 1 in Table 3).

Participants’ behaviors

The self-medication behaviors before and during the COVID-19 pandemic of respondents are described in Table 1. Of the 525 respondents, 292 (58%) stated that they had used an oral medication without the prescription of the physician in the last year. Multiple logistic regression analyses showed that respondents with a lower perception of their health status, who knew the definition of self-medication, who practiced self-medication before COVID-19 pandemic, and who reported at least one suspected symptom for COVID-19 disease in the last year were significantly more likely to practice self-medication in the last year (Model 2 in Table 3).

Among those who had had at least one suspected symptom for COVID-19 in the last year, 79.2% had contacted physicians or had undergone a swab for SARS-CoV-2, whereas 14.1% had practiced self-medication or went to the emergency room or waited for the end of the symptoms. For fear of COVID-19, 31.9% and 36% of the sample reduced medical examinations and avoided going to the emergency room respectively, while 20.5% and 28.4% had difficulty contacting the physician and had difficulty making visits to the hospital. Moreover, 29.1% of the sample practiced self-medication to prevent COVID-19 disease, and in particular, 88% and 12% using vitamins or supplements and antibiotics, respectively. This behavior was significantly higher in those employed, in those who had previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, in those who knew main COVID-19 symptom, in those who were not agreed that self-medication increases the risk of ADR, in those who reported at least one suspected symptom for COVID-19 disease in the last year, and among respondents who have practiced self-medication with drugs advertised on social media, internet, TV before COVID-19 pandemic. Age has an impact on self-medication to prevent COVID-19 disease; indeed, respondents aged 50–65 years and more than 65 years were less likely to practice self-medication to prevent COVID-19 disease (Model 4 in Table 3).

Table 4 reports self-medication behaviors before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. While during the pandemic the intake of drugs on pharmacists/friends’ advice, as well as the intake of drugs already present at home had decreased, there was an increase in the use of drugs advertised on social media, the Internet and TV (9.6% before pandemic vs. 11.1% during the pandemic).

Participants’ main sources of information

Overall, more than half of the sample had received information from TV and journals (67.5%), 52.4% from the Internet and social media, and only 46.2% from physicians. Moreover, 35.4% of the respondents declared the need for more information about COVID-19 disease (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides relevant information regarding the attitudes and behaviors related to self-medication practices among the adult population in Italy, particularly in the context of COVID-19 pandemic.

Analyzing the results of previous studies, the prevalence of self-medication in our investigation (58%) was higher than the values of 43.3% among adults in Peru during COVID-19 pandemic19, and 39.5% in respondents of an online survey on self-medication before and during the COVID-19 lockdown in Poland18, whereas the prevalence observed was lower than that reported in Dhaka city, Bangladesh (71.4%)20. Moreover, regarding self-medication with prescription medicines, 65.1% took steroids, 91.7% took heparin, and 82.8% took antibiotics. It should be noted that a large majority of the sample agreed that self-medication increases the risk of adverse drug reactions although self-medication is a common practice in more than half of the participants.

It is well known that, above all, self-medication with antibiotics is a significant public health problem due to its association with inappropriate medication use, adverse drug reaction, and the possibility of emergence of antibiotic resistance and many previous investigations have reported a worrying use of antibiotics without a prescription in different population groups12,13,26–28. Indeed, among the respondents of this study nearly one in five of those who self-medicated reported taking antibiotics without a prescription and this result is in line with that found in a previous study conducted in Australia where 19.5% reported using antibiotics as self-medication against COVID-19. Self-medication carries the risk of adverse reactions, represents an important cause of morbidity and mortality, and imposes a significant burden on healthcare resources29,30.

It is also be highlighted that, if practiced appropriately, self-medication can free up resources that can be invested in the treatment of other pathologies and to support public health. For example, a study conducted in Italy showed that, during COVID-19 pandemic, the use of self-medication was fundamental with 65.4% of Italians used it to manage their health31. Moreover, the results of our investigation revealed that non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were the most frequently used medications without a prescription and similar results were reported in Pakistan32 and Jordan33.

A main objective of the study was to explore the factors associated with self-medication during the COVID-19 pandemic. The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that several predictors were significantly associated with the different outcomes of interest. Indeed, self-medication to prevent COVID-19 was significantly lower among older participants, and this result is confirmed by previous research conducted in Ethiopia34, whereas it is in contrast with other studies which found that older participants were more likely to self-medicated35,36. Moreover, a worryingly result was that self-medication was more common among respondents who had previously practiced self-medication with drugs advertised on social media, Internet, and TV before COVID-19 pandemic. This is an alarming finding because patients may not have appropriate knowledge about diseases and their treatments, and may be influenced by misleading messages found on social media and on the Internet. Indeed, it is known that the Internet can spread misleading information on health issue and can increase inappropriate practices among the public. Thus, it is imperative that policy makers and healthcare providers understand the public knowledge needs regarding self-medication and implement educational interventions to promote appropriate medication use and encourage the consultation of websites certified by healthcare authorities.

The results of this research must be interpreted in light of several potential limitations related to the study design and methodology. First, it is not possible to establish causal and effect relationships direction between the outcomes of interest and associated factors. Second, the data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire, and it is possible that socially appropriate attitudes and behaviors may have been overestimated. To address this limitation, the survey instrument was anonymous preventing having information on the identity of the participants and the confidentiality of the answers was ensured. Moreover, more women than men voluntarily participated in the study, in line with another study37, which can limit generalization of results for this subgroup. Information about previous prescription of steroids, heparin, and other chronic medication was not collected, and we cannot exclude that patients could have continued this medication during the pandemic, by physician indication. Finally, participants were asked to report self-medication practices throughout the pandemic period and a recall bias could have occurred.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths as an appropriate sample size and high response rate, and the findings of this survey provide relevant new information on self-medication during COVID-19 pandemic in Italy. Moreover, the study sample is well representative of the surveyed population. The average age of less than 50 years as well as the high average level of education and the low birth rate, evidenced by the fact that fewer than half of the sample had at least one child, are superimposable with data from the three cities in the Campania region from which the sample was drawn.

Conclusions

Self-medication was high in Italy during the COVID-19 pandemic, and self-medication to prevent COVID-19 (with vitamins or antibiotics) was associated with lower awareness of the risk of adverse drug reactions and with advertisements on social media, besides other factors related to previous COVID-19 experience.

Promoting responsible self-medication is essential for improving health outcomes, and community pharmacists, general practitioners, and primary care pediatricians may play a key role, as they are best positioned to communicate accurate medicine and health information with the public. Therefore, it is essential to improve the public knowledge and responsible use of self-medication to increases public health outcomes and a well distribution of healthcare resources. To achieve these goals, community pharmacists, general practitioners, and primary care pediatricians have a key role and they are in the best position to transfer correct information to population in order to improve appropriate self-care and health and economic outcomes.

Implications for behavioral health

Self-medication in our sample is high and it is disturbing because it is present for medications that require a physicians’ prescription. Therefore, efforts are needed to limit the risks of self-medication through the active involvement of physicians and pharmacists, in order to increase patients’ knowledge on medications, limit concerns about illnesses, and counteract self-medication for medications that should be used after a physician’s prescription.
