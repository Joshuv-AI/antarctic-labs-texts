The association between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable dietary behaviors among university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hatice Tuğçe Berberoğlu, Fatma Çiftci, Ecenur Özkul Erdoğan, Yunus Akdoğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnsustainable dietary patterns contribute substantially to climate change, biodiversity loss, and the growing global burden of non-communicable diseases. University years represent a critical transitional period during which lifelong eating behaviors are shaped. Understanding whether positive attitudes toward healthy eating are associated with sustainable dietary behaviors may inform integrated public health strategies targeting both health and environmental outcomes. This study examined the relationship between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors among Turkish university students and explored associated demographic factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2023 and October 2024 among 610 university students aged 18–26 years. Data were collected using structured self-report questionnaires. Healthy eating attitudes were assessed using the Attitude Scale for Healthy Nutrition (ASHN), and sustainable dietary behaviors were measured using the Behaviors Scale Towards Sustainable Nutrition (BSSN). Non-parametric tests, Spearman correlation analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and structural equation modelling (SEM) were performed.ResultsParticipants demonstrated generally high levels of healthy eating attitudes. A weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between overall healthy eating attitudes and sustainable dietary behaviors (r = 0.182, p < 0.001). SEM indicated that healthy eating attitudes were positively associated with sustainable nutrition behaviors (β = 0.542, p < 0.001). Non-smokers and female students exhibited significantly higher sustainable nutrition behavior scores compared to smokers and male students (p < 0.05).ConclusionsHealthy eating attitudes were positively associated with sustainable dietary behaviors among university students. Although the strength of the association was modest, the findings suggest that health-oriented attitudes may support environmentally responsible dietary practices. Public health interventions that integrate sustainability principles into university-based nutrition education and campus food policies may represent a promising strategy to advance both human and environmental health. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways.

Introduction

The global food system is increasingly recognised as a major driver of climate change, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion, while simultaneously contributing to the rising burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Unhealthy dietary patterns are among the leading modifiable risk factors for morbidity and mortality worldwide. Consequently, the concept of sustainable nutrition has emerged as a framework that integrates human health, environmental protection, economic viability, and social equity within food systems [1].

Sustainable nutrition encompasses dietary patterns that are nutritionally adequate, culturally acceptable, affordable, and environmentally responsible, while preserving biodiversity and optimising natural resources [2]. Despite growing international attention to sustainable diets, awareness and behavioral adoption remain limited, particularly among young adults [3]. University students represent a critical population group, as the transition to university life is often accompanied by increased autonomy in food choices, exposure to new social environments, and long-term habit formation [4]. Dietary behaviors established during this period may persist into adulthood and influence both individual health trajectories and environmental outcomes.

Previous research has explored knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to sustainable nutrition, often focusing on specific behaviors such as food waste reduction or local food consumption [5–7]. Previous studies have also examined attitudes toward healthy eating and their association with dietary quality and health literacy [8–10]. However, the intersection between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable dietary behaviors remains insufficiently investigated. Understanding whether individuals who hold positive attitudes toward healthy eating are also more likely to engage in sustainable nutrition behaviors may inform integrated public health strategies that simultaneously address chronic disease prevention and environmental sustainability.

In Türkiye, studies examining sustainable nutrition among university students are limited and often region-specific [5, 11]. Moreover, to our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively evaluated the structural relationship between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors using validated multidimensional scales within a university student population.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors among university students in Türkiye. Additionally, we explored whether selected demographic characteristics, including gender, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and socioeconomic indicators, were associated with these constructs. We hypothesised that stronger healthy eating attitudes would be positively associated with higher levels of sustainable nutrition behaviors.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted to examine the association between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors among university students. The study was carried out at KTO Karatay University in Konya Province, Türkiye, between December 2023 and October 2024.

A convenience sampling method was used. Students from different academic disciplines were invited to participate based on their availability and willingness to volunteer. Data were collected using structured face-to-face questionnaires administered to students on campus. Participation was voluntary, and students who met the eligibility criteria and provided informed consent were included in the study.

Eligible participants were university students aged 18–30 years. Although the eligibility criterion allowed participation up to 30 years of age, the final sample consisted of students aged 18–26 years. Students with communication disabilities or eating disorders, those who declined to participate, and those who withdrew from the study after enrolment were excluded.

The sample size was estimated using the finite population proportion formula, with a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error. Based on this calculation, the required sample size was determined as 610 participants [12].

Variables of interest and data collection

Information on the general characteristics of the participants was collected using a structured questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews. This questionnaire was developed by the researchers and included items on age, gender, height, body weight, department of education, monthly budget, marital status, housing situation, smoking status, alcohol consumption, parental education level, and general disease status. However, it did not specifically assess chronic diseases requiring medical nutrition therapy. The data collection instrument consisted of two components: (i) a researcher-developed section assessing sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, and (ii) standardised scales measuring healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors. No new scale was developed for this study. Healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors were assessed using previously validated and published instruments, namely the ASHN and the BSSN.

The Attitude Scale for Healthy Nutrition (ASHN), developed by Tekkurşun Demir and Cicioğlu [8], is a 21-item instrument designed to assess attitudes toward healthy eating in four subdimensions: Information on Nutrition (IN), Emotion for Nutrition (EN), Positive Nutrition (PN), and Malnutrition Perception (MP). The total scores on the scale range from 21 to 105, with higher scores indicating a more favourable attitude toward healthy nutrition.

Behaviors Scale Towards Sustainable Nutrition (BSSN) was developed by Garipoğlu, Koç, and Özlü [13]. This 29-item scale evaluates sustainable nutrition behaviors in four subdimensions. Food Preference (FP), Food Waste (FWR), Seasonal and Local Food Consumption (SLFC), and Food Purchase (FPP). Scores range from 29 to 145, with higher scores reflecting more sustainable eating habits.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed via the SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) 22.0 and SPSS AMOS 22.0 package programmes. Among the normality tests, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the suitability of the data for the normal distribution. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were used to analyse demographic data. Since the scales previously tested in the literature were used, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to verify the scales and dimensions. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated for the reliability analysis of each scale. As a result of the CFA, the factor structure of the scales was evaluated based on fit indices and factor loadings, and total scale scores were calculated accordingly. No items were removed during the CFA process, and the original structure of the scales was preserved. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett sphericity tests were used to determine the suitability of the sample for factor analysis. To examine whether the mean values of the basic scales differed according to the demographic characteristics of the participants, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied in paired groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied in groups of three or more, and the findings obtained are presented below. Furthermore, the Spearman correlation coefficient was used for relationship analysis. A SEM was used to determine the relationships among the scales.

Results

Before factor analysis, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test was applied to determine the suitability of the data for this analysis. The KMO coefficient of each scale (ASHN = 0.821; BSSN = 0.926) was above the acceptable threshold of 0.50, indicating sampling adequacy. Bartlett’s test (for ASHN, p < 0.001, X²=4559.066; for BSSN, p < 0.001, X²=7279.158) yields a significance value of less than 0.001 for the scales. These results show that the variables are suitable for factor analysis.

CFA was used to verify the validity of the scales, as shown in Figs. 1 and 2.

The CFA model was tested using maximum likelihood estimates. The goodness-of-fit of the model was evaluated via multiple criteria, including the following: X²/df indicates the Chi-square fit test 3, the root mean square error of the approximation (RMSEA) 0.08, GFI (goodness of fit index) indicates goodness of fit index 0.90, comparative fit index (CFI) indicates comparative fit index 0.90 [9]. Although both the scales have already been validated among Turkish populations having similar demographic features, CFA analysis was performed in this study to determine whether the factor structure was intact in this particular group, taking into account that different samples could affect the construct validity. For this reason, CFA was performed to check the appropriateness of the measurement model. In line with the outcomes of the CFA analysis, it can be concluded that the goodness-of-fit indices for both the BSSN and ASHN scales demonstrated that there was an adequate level of fit for the proposed scales (BSSN: χ²/df = 2.619; RMSEA = 0.066; CFI = 0.962; GFI = 0.956; ASHN: χ²/df = 2.326; RMSEA = 0.062; CFI = 0.964; GFI = 0.915). Also, the reliability of the two scales was evaluated using the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which was calculated as follows: the Cronbach’s alpha values for the subdimensions of the BSSN were as follows: FP α = 0.835 (α from the original study = 0.92); FWR α = 0.827 (α from the original study = 0.91); SLFC α = 0.850 (α from the original study = 0.91); FPP α = 0.780 (α from the original study = 0.91). Regarding the ASHN, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subdimensions were: IN α = 0.892 (original study: α = 0.90), EN α = 0.784 (original study: α = 0.84), PN α = 0.707 (original study: α = 0.75), and MP α = 0.766 (original study: α = 0.83). As can be seen, all of the scales and their subdimensions have demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency within the existing sample.

When the gender distribution of the questionnaire participants was analysed, women were predominant. 360 (59.0%) of the participants were women, and 250 (41.0%) were men. Data on the marital status revealed that 596 (97.7%) were single and 14 (2.3%) were married. The number of smokers was 218 (35.8%), while the number of never smokers was 370 (60.7%). The number of alcohol users was 100 (16.5%), and the number of never-drinkers was 500 (81.9%).

No significant differences were found between the groups according to gender, housing situation, and marital status according to the ASHN scale and its subdimensions (IN, EN, PN, MP) (p > 0.05). Regarding the monthly budget, a significant difference was found between the groups according to the MP subdimension (p < 0.05), and no significant differences were found between the groups according to other subdimensions and the ASHN scale (p > 0.05). Similarly to the monthly budget variable, a significant difference in smoking status was found between the groups only according to the subdimension of the MP subdimension (p < 0.05). Alcohol consumption was significantly different between the groups according to the MP subdimension and the ASHN scale (p < 0.05). When participants were compared according to the source of the nutrition programme for body weight maintenance, a statistically significant difference was observed only in the IN subscale (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found in the EN, PN, MP subscales or the ASHN total score (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study examined healthy eating attitudes and their association with sustainable nutrition behaviors among university students in Türkiye. Overall, participants demonstrated high levels of healthy eating attitudes, and a positive, statistically significant association was identified between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors. Although the magnitude of the correlation was modest, the findings suggest that individuals who value healthy eating may also be more inclined to engage in environmentally responsible dietary practices.

Consistent with previous studies, university students in this sample demonstrated generally favourable attitudes toward healthy nutrition [9, 14]. The presence of positive attitudes during the university years is particularly important, as this life stage represents a transitional period in which long-term dietary habits are formed [4]. Supporting healthy attitudes during this period may have long-term public health implications.

Gender differences in healthy eating attitudes were not statistically significant in the present study, although women reported slightly higher mean scores. The literature presents mixed findings in this regard. Some studies indicate that women are more likely to prefer healthier foods and possess greater nutrition knowledge [15, 16], whereas others report no significant differences [14] or even higher scores among men [17]. These inconsistencies may reflect sociocultural factors or sampling differences. Given the gender imbalance in the current sample, findings related to gender should be interpreted cautiously.

Socioeconomic status is frequently cited as a determinant of dietary behavior, particularly because nutrient-dense foods may be more costly [18]. Lê et al. demonstrated that negative attitudes toward healthy eating among individuals with low socioeconomic status may be an important factor in unhealthy food choices [19]. However, a recent systematic review also reported that when individuals have a strong intention to adopt healthy eating habits for health-related reasons, socioeconomic status may not substantially differentiate healthy eating behaviors [20]. Similarly, consistent with Seref et al. [10], no significant association was observed between monthly income and healthy eating attitudes in this study. The relatively homogeneous socioeconomic characteristics of the university student sample and the possibility of continued family support may partially explain this finding. Further studies involving more socioeconomically diverse populations are needed to clarify this relationship.

Living arrangements may also influence dietary attitudes. Students living with friends reported relatively higher healthy eating attitude scores compared to those living alone, although differences were not statistically significant. Previous research has suggested that students residing with their families may exhibit healthier dietary habits than those living independently [21]. Social context, shared meals, and household norms may influence both attitudes and behaviors related to food.

Smoking and alcohol consumption are commonly associated with less healthy dietary patterns [10, 22]. While no significant association was observed between smoking status and healthy eating attitudes in this study, prior research has reported lower nutrient intake and poorer dietary quality among smokers [22]. Similarly, Canbolat et al. reported that smokers had lower total ASHN scores and lower scores in the scale subdimensions [23]. On the other hand, Demirbaş et al. [24] did not find a significant association between smoking and healthy eating attitudes among university students. These findings suggest that attitudes toward healthy eating may not always align with broader health behavior patterns.

With regard to sustainable nutrition behaviors, significant gender differences were observed. Female students demonstrated higher overall sustainable behavior scores and higher scores in the FP and FPP subdimensions. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Türkiye and internationally, which report greater sustainability awareness and engagement among women [6, 7]. In a recent study conducted among young adults, women were reported to be more likely to engage in climate-conscious dietary practices motivated by animal ethics and health concerns. However, despite the high level of awareness among women regarding this issue, the study emphasized that priorities differed according to physical activity level: performance-related concerns were more prominent among highly active individuals, whereas economic considerations were more influential among moderately active individuals [25]. Another study by Culliford et al. reported that women showed a greater tendency than men to prioritise plant-based proteins in their dietary habits [26]. Hashim et al. found that female students were more likely to adopt plant-based food choices and demonstrate greater concern regarding food waste [27]. However, awareness does not always translate directly into behavior change, as individuals may still generate food waste despite reporting concern [27]. This highlights the complexity of sustainable behavior adoption. In the regional context, earlier findings from Konya province indicated limited prior awareness of sustainable nutrition concepts among university students [11]. Despite this, the relatively high sustainable behavior scores observed in the present study may reflect increasing public discourse on sustainability or the influence of health-oriented motivations on environmental practices.

Marital status was associated with sustainable nutrition behaviors, with married participants reporting higher sustainability scores. Although this finding aligns with previous literature suggesting that structured meal patterns and home food preparation may increase sustainable practices [28], the small number of married participants in the present sample limits interpretability.

Smoking and alcohol consumption were significantly associated with sustainable nutrition behaviors, with non-smokers and non-drinkers reporting higher sustainability scores. This may reflect behavioral clustering, whereby individuals who avoid risky health behaviors are also more likely to adopt environmentally responsible dietary practices. Vegetarian or plant-forward dietary patterns, often linked to sustainability, have also been associated with lower rates of smoking and alcohol consumption [29]. From a public health perspective, these findings support the integration of sustainability messages into broader lifestyle promotion strategies.

Importantly, a positive association was observed between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors. The nutrition information and PN subdimensions were positively associated with sustainability behaviors, whereas MP demonstrated a negative association. These findings suggest that knowledge-based and constructive health attitudes may facilitate sustainable food practices. Similar relationships between nutrition knowledge and sustainable dietary behaviors have been reported in previous studies [4, 30, 31]. Educational interventions have been shown to be associated with improvements in both health-oriented and sustainability-oriented dietary behaviors [4]. Similar findings have also been reported in recent public health research demonstrating that higher levels of nutrition literacy are associated with improved diet quality and greater adherence to sustainable dietary practices [32].

Nevertheless, the modest strength of the association indicates that healthy eating attitudes alone are unlikely to fully explain sustainable dietary behaviors. Structural and contextual determinants—including food availability, affordability, cultural norms, and environmental values—also shape sustainable food choices [18, 33]. Evidence from population-based studies published in BMC Public Health has highlighted the role of food environment characteristics, ecological awareness, and socioeconomic constraints in shaping sustainable food choices [34, 35]. Furthermore, Claessens et al. [28] demonstrated that healthy food preferences do not always coincide with sustainable choices, particularly in out-of-home eating contexts. These findings underscore the importance of multilevel public health approaches that combine individual education with environmental and policy-level strategies.

However, since this study was conducted among students from a single university in Konya, Türkiye, regional and cultural characteristics may have influenced the present findings. It has been reported that each of the seven geographical regions of Türkiye has a distinct food culture. In particular, in the Eastern and Central Anatolia regions, where Konya is located, meat-based dietary patterns differ substantially from the Mediterranean dietary model. These cultural variations have been shown to shape dietary behaviors and may limit the generalizability of the findings [36]. Moreover, a recent study conducted among nutrition and dietetics students demonstrated that geographical regional differences influence both the Planetary Health Diet Index and the Healthy Eating Index [37]. In this context, when making generalizations to university students in different regions of Türkiye, regional characteristics and sociocultural context should be considered, as these factors may influence students’ healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors.

Universities provide a strategic setting for such interventions. Integrating sustainability principles into nutrition curricula, improving campus food environments, promoting plant-based and locally sourced options, and implementing food waste reduction initiatives may support more consistent sustainable dietary behaviors among students. Previous intervention-based research in university and institutional settings suggests that modifying food service environments and incorporating sustainability-focused education can be associated with improvements in both dietary quality and environmentally responsible food behaviors [38].

Limitations and Strengths

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have overreported socially desirable behaviors related to healthy or sustainable eating. Second, the use of convenience sampling from a single university limits the generalizability of the findings. Although the eligibility criterion allowed participation of university students aged up to 30 years, the final sample consisted of students aged 18–26 years. Therefore, the potential heterogeneity related to older students in their late twenties was limited in the present sample. Nevertheless, differences in academic year, living arrangements, and socioeconomic conditions may still have introduced variability in dietary attitudes and behaviors. Another limitation is that chronic diseases requiring medical nutrition therapy were not specifically assessed. Such conditions may have influenced participants’ eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors and may represent a source of unmeasured confounding. The gender imbalance within the sample may also have influenced subgroup comparisons. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although a significant association was identified between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors, the direction of this relationship cannot be determined. Unmeasured confounding variables, such as environmental awareness or food environment characteristics, may also have influenced the observed associations. Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The relatively large sample size provided adequate statistical power for subgroup analyses and SEM. The use of validated and multidimensional instruments (ASHN and BSSN) enhanced measurement reliability and construct validity. Furthermore, this study contributes novel data from a regional university context and advances understanding of the intersection between health promotion and environmental sustainability.

Conclusions

This study identified a positive association between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition behaviors among university students in Türkiye. Although the association was modest, the findings suggest that health-oriented attitudes may support environmentally responsible dietary practices. From a public health perspective, integrated strategies that promote both healthy eating and sustainability may offer synergistic benefits. Universities represent strategic settings for implementing multilevel interventions, including nutrition education, campus food policy improvements, increased availability of plant-based and locally sourced foods, and food waste reduction initiatives. However, attitudes alone are unlikely to fully determine sustainable dietary behaviors. Structural, social, and environmental determinants must also be addressed to achieve meaningful and sustained behavioral change. Longitudinal and multi-centre studies are warranted to clarify causal pathways and further explore contextual influences on the relationship between healthy eating attitudes and sustainable nutrition practices.
