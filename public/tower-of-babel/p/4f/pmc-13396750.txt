Ultra-processed and High fat-salt-sugar (HFSS) foods in India: a dual-axis framework for regulation
Authors: Sneha Chakraborty, SubbaRao M Gavaravarapu, Samarasimha Reddy N, Bharati Kulkarni
Published: 2026

The recent Lancet Series on ultra-processed foods (UPFs) advanced understanding of UPFs expanding role and the commercial, economic, political, and health determinants shaping food environments,1, 2, 3 linking UPF consumption to diet-related non-communicable disease (NCD) risk, particularly in high- and middle-income countries.2 India remains under-represented due to limited data on UPF consumption, health outcomes and market size.

The Global Burden of Disease analysis identifies unhealthy diets as a key modifiable risk factor for NCD disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in India.4 The National Family Health Survey-6 (NFHS-6) (2023–24) shows rising obesity among men and women (15–49 years) (23% vs. 27%; 24% vs. 31%) since NFHS-5 (2019–21).5 India's Household Consumption Expenditure Survey shows monthly per capita expenditure on processed foods rose from 7.9% to 9.8% (rural) and 9%–11.1% (urban) between 2011–12 and 2023–24.6 The Indian Council of Medical Research-India Diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB) study identifies high-calorie diets as a key NCD risk contributor7 and India's Economic Survey, 2025-26 highlights rising UPF consumption as a driver of obesity and NCDs.8 Despite these signals, policy progress remains slow, partly due to absence of a standard UPF definition. Often, terms like packaged, processed, pre-packaged and ultra-processed are used interchangeably.

Against the backdrop of expanding UPF markets and persistent concerns around high fat, sugar and salt (HFSS) foods, this commentary examines the future direction of food labelling regulation in India by integrating HFSS considerations within broader UPF policy efforts. HFSS foods are cooked or packaged foods containing excessive amounts of calories, saturated fat, added sugars or salt. These foods are generally energy-dense, may lack fibre and essential nutrients (like deep-fried snacks, sweets, sugary beverages, bakery products).9

According to NOVA10 and SIGA11 classifications, UPFs are defined based on the nature, extent and purpose of industrial processing4 and the presence of highly transformed ingredients, additives, and substances rarely used in home cooking, respectively. Definitions vary across studies, and no uniform operational definition exists in research or policy.2,3 However, globally many research studies rely on the NOVA classification to define UPFs, but this scenario presents practical challenges in India, where UPF and HFSS food regulation is at a nascent stage. While NOVA is valuable for research, regulatory frameworks require clearer definitions that account for both nutrients and degree of processing. In the absence of a formal HFSS definition, key policy instruments - such as school food standards, front-of-pack nutrition labelling (FOPNL) and restrictions on marketing of unhealthy foods to children, cannot sufficiently address UPFs.12 Nutrient thresholds underpin most FOPNL systems globally, yet rarely incorporate processing-related risks like use of cosmetic additives, highly refined ingredients (which are associated with certain NCDs) and alterations to the food matrix that compromise the nutritional quality.13 Consequently, India faces a dual policy dilemma: whether to prioritise regulation of HFSS foods alone or to simultaneously address the broader UPFs.

Several classification ambiguities illustrate the limitations of existing frameworks. Traditionally prepared homemade preparations are minimally processed, whereas industrial versions of these foods containing emulsifiers or flavouring agents qualify as UPFs.9 Similar challenges arise when home-prepared dishes rely heavily on packaged components such as instant noodles, premixed spice blends, ready-made gravies or flavoured oils, blurring distinctions between household and industrial foods. A further regulatory blind spot is the rapidly expanding category of “health-positioned” UPFs, including sugar-free biscuits, flavoured yoghurts, protein bars, multigrain snacks, plant-based milks, zero-sugar beverages. These products may or may not fall below HFSS thresholds; however, they remain classified as ultra-processed foods based on their formulation and structure.14 Reformulation strategies often reduce select nutrients of concern while retaining additives such as non-nutritive sweeteners, emulsifiers, and stabilisers that alter food matrices. Their marketing fosters a misleading perception of healthfulness despite resemblance to foods associated with increased cardiometabolic risk.

Adopting a two-axis definition that integrates processing levels, nutrient thresholds, and India-specific food contexts provides an opportunity to confront rising obesity and its complications in India. The Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) have presented a framework combining processing and nutrient considerations for the first time (Fig. 1).9 However, clearer demarcation across food categories would strengthen its utility for policy and research.

In India, reliance on classification based on ultra-processing alone may overlook substantial dietary hazards. Commonly consumed non-industrially prepared foods such as samosas, chaat, jalebi are HFSS and have refined carbohydrates posing health risks.9 However, such foods remain largely outside regulatory focus when ultra-processing is treated as the sole indicator of unhealthfulness.

India's evolving food system underscores the need for a pragmatic, integrated classification combining UPFs and HFSS foods. Defining HFSS foods first and UPFs later, or adopting NOVA without nutrient thresholds, risks delaying regulatory action. This juncture therefore offers a critical opportunity for India to articulate an integrated UPF-HFSS framework for FOPNL. Regulatory authorities, should adopt a definition that reflects global evidence while remaining responsive to national dietary patterns by incorporating: (i) level of industrial processing, (ii) nutrient thresholds for sugar, fat, and sodium, and (iii) Contextual features of the Indian food system including regional culinary diversity, multi-ingredient mixed dishes, packaged traditional foods and coexistence of informal and industrial food sectors (Fig. 2).

Despite growing evidence of UPF expansion in India, the absence of a regulatory definition hampers effective policy action. Recently, the Supreme Court of India directed the FSSAI to reconsider mandatory FOPNL for HFSS foods, citing the right to health.15 Therefore, a coherent framework is essential to enable consistent food classification, effective regulation, and protection of culturally important foods that do not share the structure or function of UPFs. This approach would also prevent reformulated products from circumventing regulation and support generation of robust evidence on UPF consumption and health outcomes in India, where data remain limited.
