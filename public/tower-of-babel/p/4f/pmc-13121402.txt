Osteoporosis is a risk factor for complications and reoperations at 1 and 3 years after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair
Authors: Cameron Brown, Andrea H Johnson, Jane C Brennan, Matthew A Peterman, Daniel E Redziniak, Justin J Turcotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis is associated with compromised bone quality and healing potential. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (RCR) restores shoulder function and reduces pain in patients with rotator cuff pathology. The influence of osteoporosis on complications and long-term outcomes following RCR remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate the relationship between osteoporosis and outcomes after arthroscopic RCR.MethodsThe PearlDiver Mariner 170 dataset was retrospectively analyzed. Patients were grouped by whether they had a diagnosis of osteoporosis within the year prior to RCR. The 2 groups were propensity score matched 1:1 on age, gender, and comorbidities. Outcomes were assessed at 1 year and 3 years post-operatively. The outcomes of interest included repeat RCR, Surgical Site Infection, frozen shoulder (FS), proximal humerus fracture, total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), and lysis of adhesions/manipulation under anesthesia.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, there were no differences in demographics and comorbidities between groups. At 1 year post-operatively, the osteoporosis group had increased rates of FS (3.7% vs. 3.1%; P = .004), proximal humerus fracture (0.6% vs. 0.4%; P = .002), and TSA (1.2% vs. 0.9%; P = .012). This trend persisted at 3 years post-operatively, with the osteoporosis group continuing to have increased rates of FS (4.8% vs. 4.0%; P < .001), proximal humerus fracture (1.0% vs. 0.7%; P = .001), and TSA (2.2% vs. 1.9%; P = .038).ConclusionPatients with osteoporosis are at increased risk of FS, proximal humerus fractures, and TSA at 1 and 3 years after arthroscopic RCR. Overall rates of complication were low and osteoporosis should not be considered a contraindication to arthroscopic RCR.Keywords: Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, Osteoporosis, Post-operative complications, Frozen shoulder, Proximal humerus fracture, Total shoulder arthroplasty

Rotator cuff tears are a common cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction and can lead to weakness, alterations in glenohumeral kinematics, and potential shoulder instability.9,21,22 For patients that do not respond to conservative treatment, rotator cuff repair (RCR) has become a common treatment option, with rates of RCR increasing over time.24 RCR is typically a safe and effective surgical procedure that improves function, range of motion, and provides pain relief, although complications can occur that affect the long-term success of the procedure.6,10,16,19 The success of RCR is contingent upon a multitude of factors, including tear size, patient age, surgical technique, and the quality of the surrounding tissue.10,18

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disease characterized by decreased bone mineral density and microarchitectural deterioration that most commonly occurs in older populations.13 This can lead to fractures and poor bone healing and may also compromise the structural integrity of the bone–tendon interface.5 Patients with osteoporosis undergoing RCR may be more prone to repair failure and increased problems with post-operative rehabilitation, although there has been limited study in this area.5,8 As the global population ages, the prevalence of both rotator cuff tears and osteoporosis is expected to rise, making the investigation of their interplay increasingly important for optimizing patient care.5 There are a number of medications used to improve bone density and reduce complications from osteoporosis, although it is unclear whether the use of these medications in patients undergoing RCR has any effect on the repair.1,13

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the relationship between a pre-operative diagnosis of osteoporosis and post-operative outcomes following RCR using a large national database. In addition, we will explore whether being prescribed an osteoporosis medication had a demonstrable effect on outcomes in patients with a diagnosis of osteoporosis. We hypothesize that patients with osteoporosis would have poorer post-operative outcomes when compared with patients without osteoporosis and that osteoporosis medication will improve post-operative outcomes.

Materials and methods

This study was deemed exempt by the institutional review board as retrospective review of a deidentified database.

Data source

The PearlDiver (PearlDiver Inc., Colorado Springs, Colorado, USA; www.pearldiverinc.com) Mariner 170 dataset was retrospectively analyzed. The database contains claims records from over 170 million patients across all payers, including commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, and self-pay. Data are searchable by Predefined Cohorts, International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9), ICD-10, and Current Procedural Terminology codes.

Study population

All patients included in this study underwent arthroscopic RCR and were active in the database for at least 1 year prior to RCR and 1 year post-operatively. Patients were grouped by whether they had a diagnosis of osteoporosis within the year prior to RCR. Coding definitions of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, independent variables, and outcomes can be found in the Supplementary Appendix.

Independent variables

Demographics and comorbidities (based on ICD-9/10 codes) of interest were age, gender, obesity, diabetes, congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension (HTN), tobacco use, coagulation defects, and pre-operative adrenal corticosteroid use. Pre-operative adrenal corticosteroid use was defined as having an adrenal corticosteroid prescription within the 6 months before surgery.

Outcome measures

Outcomes were assessed at both 1 year and 3 years post-operatively. The outcomes of interest included repeat RCR, Surgical Site Infection (SSI), frozen shoulder (FS), proximal humerus fracture, total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), and lysis of adhesions (LOA)/manipulation under anesthesia (MUA).

Statistical analysis

The 2 groups were propensity score matched 1:1 on age, gender, obesity, diabetes, CHF, HTN, COPD, tobacco use, coagulation defects, and pre-operative adrenal corticosteroid use. Univariate analyses (chi-square and independent samples t-tests) were performed to compare demographics, comorbidities, and outcomes between groups. Within the osteoporosis group, differences in outcomes were assessed between those who were on an osteoporosis medication (either bisphosphonate or anabolic) within the 6 months pre-operatively and those who were not. All statistical analysis was performed within the PearlDiver platform using R (R Studio PBC, Boston, MA). Statistical significance was assessed at P < .05.

Results

After propensity score matching, there were 17,498 patients in each group included in the study; there were no significant differences in demographics and comorbidities between groups. Overall, the population was 88.3% female with an average age of 66 year old. Discussion

The primary finding of this study demonstrates that a diagnosis of osteoporosis in patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR is associated with an elevated risk of adverse post-operative outcomes. Specifically, our analysis shows increased rates of FS, proximal humerus fracture, and TSA at both 1 year and 3 years post-operatively in osteoporotic RCR patients compared to nonosteoporotic counterparts, although the overall rate of complications in both groups was low. There were no differences in the rates of repeat RCR, SSI, or LOA/MUA between groups. In patients with a diagnosis of osteoporosis, there were no differences in complications between patients being treated with medication and those that were not.

There have been a number of studies that have identified osteopenia and osteoporosis as risk factors for revision surgery following RCR. Cancienne et al2 reported a substantial difference in the revision surgery rate after arthroscopic RCR in patients with osteoporosis compared with matched controls in a large database study. Xu et al23 also found that osteoporosis increases the revision rate of RCR in a rat model, highlighting impaired rotator cuff healing in osteoporotic models. Furthermore, a narrative review by Daher et al5 emphasizes that osteoporosis and osteopenia are prevalent in patients undergoing shoulder surgery, especially RCR, and that major complications include repair failure and revision surgery. In contrast to these studies, our study did not find an increased rate of repeat RCR or of LOA/MUA when comparing patients with osteoporosis to those without. It is possible that this difference is attributed to the larger sample size in our study or it may be related to differences in defining decreased bone density. However, we did identify a higher incidence of proximal humerus fracture and TSA in our osteoporotic cohort, although the overall rates of both were low in all groups. This underscores the detrimental impact of reduced bone quality on the long-term stability and functional recovery of the shoulder joint, which is a consistent theme across the literature when bone quality is compromised.3 It is worth noting that with a large database study such as this one, we are unable to determine the indication for conversion to TSA and whether or not there was a relationship between subsequent TSA and the initial RCR. In addition, while we were able to utilize the database to match all patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR with and without a recorded diagnosis of osteoporosis, we need to acknowledge the large percentage of patients with osteoporosis that do not receive a diagnosis. A recent study that used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that nearly 70% of patients in the United States with osteoporosis went undiagnosed, with the rates of underdiagnosis varying between groups; in some groups, nearly 90% of patients went undiagnosed.17

A noteworthy finding from this study was the increased predisposition to FS among osteoporotic patients following RCR. While the underlying mechanisms linking osteoporosis to adhesive capsulitis in this specific context warrant further investigation, it may be related to altered inflammatory processes or differences in tissue healing capabilities in individuals with diminished bone density.15 While patients with osteoporosis in this study did experience a higher rate of FS at both 1 and 3 years post-operatively, we did not see higher rates of LOA/MUA in patients with osteoporosis, and the overall rates of FS were low. This may be due to a reluctance of surgeons to aggressively treat FS surgically in patients who already have reduced bone density and a higher predisposition to fracture.

Research highlights the significant prevalence of osteoporosis among individuals with rotator cuff tears; however, screening and subsequent treatment for this condition are often overlooked prior to RCR.4 A recent study by Kawashima et al11 found that among individuals with rotator cuff tears and low bone mineral density, only a small percentage (12.9%) were actually undergoing osteoporosis treatment, despite the crucial need for diagnosis and treatment in this population. In alignment with this finding, only 15% of patients with a formal diagnosis of osteoporosis received pharmacologic treatment within the 6 months prior to surgery in our study. This oversight presents a missed opportunity for optimizing patient outcomes, as early identification and management of osteoporosis, including the use of targeted treatments that have shown to lower failure rates after RCR.11 A recent study by Sierra et al20 reported that, despite previous literature suggesting an adverse effect of osteoporosis on rotator cuff healing, they found no relationship between decreased bone mineral density and 2-year patient-reported outcomes, indicating that patients with low bone mineral density can still achieve favorable outcomes. Although we did note an increased rate of complications in patients with osteoporosis and were unable to examine patient reported outcomes in this population, it is encouraging to note that despite a potential for increased complications some patients with osteoporosis report equivalent satisfaction with the procedure over the longer term. A proactive approach by the osteoporosis patient population in both seeking and adhering to appropriate treatment regimens could lead to improved pre-operative bone health and reduced post-operative complications.

Our study observed no statistically significant difference in outcomes based on the utilization of bisphosphonate/anabolic medication within the osteoporotic group. This finding prompts a closer look at the broader literature concerning the role of antiosteoporosis drugs in RCR outcomes. While our study did not observe an overall improvement with the use of these medications, it is important to note that other research is actively exploring their potential benefits. A recent meta-analysis by Hao et al7 aimed to elucidate the effects of antiosteoporosis drugs in patients who experienced rotator cuff tears and underwent arthroscopic repair, indicating ongoing investigation into their efficacy. Kim et al12 investigated the effects of denosumab, a receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand inhibitor, on tendon healing, retear rates, and clinical outcomes post-RCR in women with osteoporosis, reporting a reduction in retear rates and improved functional outcomes. Similarly, Lee et al14 found that intravenous zoledronate may reduce the retear rate after RCR in older female patients with osteoporosis, suggesting positive effects on healing. These studies present a nuanced perspective, suggesting that while the general use of bisphosphonate/anabolic medication in our cohort did not yield a broad statistical difference in the measured adverse outcomes, specific pharmacological interventions may positively influence certain aspects of RCR success, such as retear rates. This area remains complex and warrants more targeted research to understand optimal pharmaceutical management strategies. Given the significant limitations in interpreting the medication data included in this study (identifying medication fills only, not compliance or duration of treatment), we would consider these findings exploratory, and additional specific research on osteoporosis medication use in the population needs to be performed.

This study does have a number of limitations that must be addressed. The study relied solely on an administrative database, which relies on the accuracy of the coded data and limits us to variables that are included within the database itself. This particular database pulls from insurance claims, which may limit the diversity of subjects included. Given the significant rates of underdiagnosis of osteoporosis, we cannot be certain that patients were categorized correctly and this may reduce the accuracy of our findings and the applicability of the results. We were unable to include data on the type of rotator cuff tear, the quality of the repair, or the severity of the osteoporosis, all of which may influence functional outcomes and complication rates. In addition, we were unable to assess important outcomes such as range of motion, quality of life, patient-reported outcome measures, and return to activity, all of which are vital to fully assess recovery and patient satisfaction with the procedure. For medication data, we only had access to whether the medication was filled or not and were unable to assess compliance. Despite these limitations, this study does provide an important overview of the interplay of 2 common conditions and provides a basis for future single or multi-institution studies to assess the role of osteoporosis in patients undergoing RCR.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that osteoporosis significantly influences the post-operative success of arthroscopic RCR, leading to increased risks of FS, proximal humerus fracture, and TSA, although the overall rates of these complications in both groups were low. These findings underscore the critical need for a thorough assessment of bone health in patients undergoing RCR and the development of specialized treatment protocols for individuals with osteoporosis, although osteoporosis does not appear to be a contraindication for proceeding with arthroscopic RCR. To advance the understanding of this relationship and improve patient outcomes, future research should consider several key areas, including mechanistic studies on specific complications, pharmacologic management optimization, the impact of early osteoporosis screening and intervention, and novel therapeutic approaches to surgical technique, implant design, and rehabilitation protocols.
