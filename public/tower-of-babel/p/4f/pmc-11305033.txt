Impact of COVID-19 on vaccine confidence and uptake: A systematic literature review
Authors: Ivo Vojtek, Marloes van Wouw, Angus Thomson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuring the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, scheduled vaccinations were postponed, mass vaccination programmes were suspended and opportunities for healthcare workers to administer vaccines ad hoc decreased. The aims of this systematic literature review were to determine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on vaccine confidence, intent and uptake in preexisting routine childhood or adult vaccination programmes, and to identify factors associated with changes in acceptance, intent and uptake of preexisting vaccines. Medline and Embase were searched for studies in Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, Japan, the USA, and European countries, published between 1 January 2021 and 4 August 2022. A complementary gray literature search was conducted between 11 and 13 October 2022, and supplemented with additional gray research in October 2023. In total, 54 citations were included in the review. Study design and geography were heterogeneous. The number of adults who received or intended to receive an influenza or pneumococcal vaccine was higher during the pandemic than in previous seasons (n = 28 studies). In addition, increased acceptance of adult vaccinations was observed during 2020–21 compared with 2019–20 (n = 12 studies). The rates of childhood vaccinations decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic across several countries (n = 11 studies). Factors associated with changes in intention to receive a vaccination, or uptake of influenza vaccine, included previous vaccination, older age, higher perceived risk of contracting COVID-19, anxiety regarding the pandemic and fear of contracting COVID-19. Acceptance and uptake of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines generally increased after onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic had an unprecedented impact on healthcare systems.1,2 With respect to immunization, scheduled vaccinations were postponed,3,4 mass vaccination programmes were suspended,5 opportunities to administer vaccines decreased6,7 and fear of COVID-19 infection made many people unwilling to visit health centers for vaccination.7,8 As a result, routine childhood vaccination programmes were disrupted9–15 and adult vaccination rates decreased.16,17 The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 25 million children missed vaccinations in 2021, 5.9 million more than in 2019;18 modeling suggested 27–30 million children missed certain vaccinations in 2020.15

It is unclear whether changes in vaccination rates as people adjusted to life in the COVID-19 pandemic primarily resulted from missed opportunities for vaccination and/or if they reflected a substantial shift in attitudes toward vaccination. We conducted a systematic literature review to evaluate the impact of the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic on preexisting vaccines against certain other vaccine-preventable diseases. We investigated vaccine confidence, intention to receive vaccinations and uptake of vaccinations in children and adults. We also identified factors associated with changes in vaccination intent or uptake in children and adults. As may be expected given the context of the pandemic, relevant studies were highly heterogeneous, particularly in terms of study geography and age groups included. We have therefore attempted to identify and describe underlying trends in the data that may inform strategies to sustain routine vaccine uptake in the early months of any future pandemic and efforts to rebuild routine immunization rates right now.

Materials and methods

After performing a feasibility assessment to identify the most appropriate databases, a systematic literature review was conducted by searching Medline and Embase for articles with abstracts, published in English between 1 January 2021 and 4 August 2022, that reported on routine childhood immunizations (including against measles, diphtheria, pertussis and tetanus) or adult vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal and herpes zoster). Inclusion criteria included country/region of study and study outcome. Studies conducted in Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, Japan, the USA and any European country were included, with these countries being chosen because they had adult vaccination programmes and representative healthcare systems. Outcomes of interest for the selected vaccines included immunization status before and during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic; vaccine acceptance and uptake rates; impact of the pandemic on willingness to vaccinate and uptake of vaccines; and factors associated with willingness to vaccinate or uptake of vaccines. More detail on the search strategy, and the data collection and selection processes, is provided in the Supplemental methods; eligibility criteria are outlined in Table S1.

A gray literature search was conducted between 11 and 13 October 2022 using Google and the following websites: United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), WHO, GOV.uk, GOV.br, Italian Ministry of Health, National Institutes for Food and Drug Control, the European Commission and some websites identified in the included peer-reviewed literature. Additional research in October 2023 led to the identification of relevant data and updates from the ECDC and CDC.

Results

Of 5607 unique publications identified by searching Medline and Embase, 54 were included in this review (Figure 1). A summary of these 54 publications is provided in Table S2. Data were also included from 11 sources identified by the gray literature search. A total of 37 studies reported data on the influenza vaccine alone, one reported data on the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine alone and four reported data on both vaccines. A total of 12 studies reported data on routine childhood vaccines (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio and Haemophilus influenzae type b [DTaP IPV Hib]; pneumococcal conjugate; rotavirus; meningococcal conjugate; measles, mumps and rubella [MMR]; and varicella).

Of the 54 included studies, 29 were conducted in Europe, 17 in North America, six in China and one in Japan. One additional study spanned continents. Most studies (n = 41) used surveys to obtain data, but some (n = 14) extracted data from electronic health records, registries or databases.

Adult vaccination uptake before the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–20) compared with uptake or intention to vaccinate during the pandemic (2020–22)

Uptake of seasonal recommended vaccines before the COVID-19 pandemic (i.e., during 2019–20 or earlier), and uptake or intention to receive vaccinations during the pandemic (2020–21 or 2021–22), were reported in 30 studies. Most studies included adults (n = 13) and healthcare workers (HCWs) (n = 10); others included students (n = 4), adults and children (n = 4), older adults (n = 2) and adults with cancer (n = 2), with some studies including multiple study populations.

Generally, irrespective of vaccine type, the intention of individuals to receive a vaccine was higher during the pandemic than in previous years. However, uptake of some adult vaccines decreased.

Influenza vaccine

All 17 European studies that assessed the proportion of adults who received or intended to receive an influenza vaccine in 2020–21 reported increases compared with prior seasons (Italy,19–26 Spain,27–29 Ireland,30,31 Greece,32 Malta,33 Switzerland34 and the UK35; Figure 2A). These analyses included data from HCWs,19,20,22,23,26,29,31,33 adults,20,24,25,27,30,32,34,35 students21,28 and adult caregivers of children.30,34 The two studies that measured actual vaccine uptake during the pandemic, using workplace vaccination registers, reported substantial increases in coverage among HCWs (from 33.5% to 57.7% and from 15.7% to 53.6% for 2020–21 versus 2019–20).23,26 A further two studies that assessed vaccine uptake during the pandemic using self-reported online questionnaires showed an increase in vaccination during the pandemic compared with 2019–20 (vaccination rates 41.4% versus 25.1% among the general population, and 35.0% versus 26.7% among university students [p < .001]).25,28 Figure 2.Seasonal vaccination uptake pre-COVID-19 pandemic, and uptake or intention to be vaccinated during the COVID-19 pandemic 2020/21 in: (a) Europe; (b) North America; and (c) Asia. Recent (October 2023) ECDC data also reported marked improvements in vaccination coverage rates (VCRs) from the 2018–19 and 2019–20 influenza seasons to the 2020–21 season (for instance, for adults aged ≥65 years; Figure 3).36 Among countries that provided data for all three seasons, the VCR increased between 2018–19 and 2020–21 (with the exceptions of Slovakia, where the rate was unchanged, and Latvia, where it decreased), suggesting that the COVID-19 pandemic may have motivated many people to get an influenza vaccination. However, even among those countries where coverage increased, there was substantial heterogeneity in rates – perhaps due to factors such as variability in vaccination availability (licensed formulations) and recommendations (target populations and age groups) – and only one country, Denmark, reached the European Union VCR target of 75%. Issues that may have had a detrimental impact on influenza vaccine uptake include COVID-19 lockdowns, limited population movement, vaccine supply problems or lack of storage, and hesitancy or lack of confidence in influenza vaccination. Seven studies conducted in the USA37–42 and Canada43 showed a consistent increase in the proportion of adults intending to receive an influenza vaccine in 2020–21 compared with previous seasons, although the magnitude of the increase was more modest than that observed in the European studies (Figure 2B). As in Europe, surveillance data (CDC, October 2023) support this trend, with coverage in adults ≥65 years increasing from 68.1% in the 2018–19 season to 75.2% in the 2020–21 season; however, data for the 2022–23 season appear more in line with historical averages, with 69.7% coverage in this age group.44 In China, two studies showed an increase in the intention of parents to vaccinate their children45 and the intention of university students46 to receive influenza vaccine during 2020–21 compared with previous seasons, whereas a third study showed no marked change in intentions among adult migrants (Figure 2C).47 A Japanese study conducted exclusively in adult patients with cancer showed an increase in the proportion of patients intending to receive an influenza vaccine in 2020–21 (Figure 2C).48

Pneumococcal vaccine

A study conducted in China using a self-administered questionnaire showed a large increase in the proportion of individuals aged >60 years willing to receive the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine in October 2020 compared with those who actually received the vaccine in the previous 5 years (91.5% versus 1.4%, respectively).49

Herpes zoster vaccine

The gray literature search identified information on herpes zoster vaccine uptake in the UK. Cumulative coverage with herpes zoster continued to increase in each quarter from April 2020 to March 2021 among adults aged 70–78 years. However, coverage was lower in 2020–21 compared with 2019–20, with a 6.5% decrease in coverage among those turning 70 years old.50

Change in acceptance of vaccination

Fourteen studies reported the proportion of individuals who changed their mind regarding receiving a vaccine between 2019–20 and 2020–21. The proportions of individuals with changes that were favorable and unfavorable were calculated for 12 studies (Figure 4). Two studies were excluded as they focussed on HCWs who were required to receive the influenza vaccine. A favorable change was calculated as the proportion of individuals who switched from not accepting vaccination to accepting/intending to accept vaccination or having not yet decided on accepting vaccination. An unfavorable change was calculated as the proportion of individuals who switched from accepting vaccination to not accepting/not intending to accept vaccination or having not yet decided on accepting vaccination. In all 12 studies, the proportion of individuals with favorable changes was higher than the proportion with unfavorable changes. Influenza vaccine

Eleven studies provided information on the proportion of individuals who changed their mind regarding receiving an influenza vaccine in 2020–21 compared with previous seasons in adults,20,25,27,32,35,39,49 HCWs19,23,29 and caregivers of children.40,51

Across all studies, the proportion of individuals who switched from an unfavorable to a favorable attitude (range, 25.5–56.3%) exceeded the proportion of individuals who switched from a favorable to an unfavorable attitude (range, 1.0–28.8%; Figure 4). There were no notable differences between countries or study populations.

Pneumococcal vaccine

Only one study, conducted in China, assessed the proportion of individuals who changed their mind regarding receiving a pneumococcal vaccine.49 Markedly more individuals exhibited a favorable change in attitude toward this vaccine than an unfavorable change (91.4% versus 3.4%; Figure 4).

Direct impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on acceptance of recommended vaccinations

In fifteen studies, participants were asked whether the COVID-19 pandemic had influenced their perceptions of recommended vaccinations and willingness to receive recommended vaccinations for the next season.

Influenza vaccine

Thirteen studies assessed attitudes toward influenza vaccination alone in HCWs,26,29,31,52 adults,37,42,53 adults and caregivers of children,30 university students,38,46 adults with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,54 adults with underlying illnesses27 or adult caregivers of children with congenital heart disease.55

Healthcare workers

The COVID-19 pandemic had a substantial impact on HCWs’ opinions of influenza vaccination, as reflected in higher vaccination rates in workplaces that did not impose mandatory vaccination. Considerable increases in the proportion of vaccinated HCWs were reported in the 2020–21 influenza season among emergency department employees at 12 Bavarian hospitals (59% versus 31% in the 2016–17 influenza season; p < .001)52 and employees at three Italian hospitals (53.6% versus 3.7% in the 2013–14 influenza season; p < .001).26 In contrast, similar proportions of staff at an Irish tertiary care hospital received an influenza vaccine in 2020 and 2019 (78% and 80%, respectively).31

A large proportion of HCWs reported that the COVID-19 pandemic had changed their opinion about the importance of influenza vaccination, including 30.5% of those surveyed at three Italian hospitals26 and 39% of the staff at an Irish hospital (rising to 51% among HCWs who had not received an influenza vaccine during the previous year).31 A survey of 610 Spanish HCWs from primary care teams, conducted between October 2020 and January 2021, showed that 65.7% intended to receive the influenza vaccine, among whom 6.7% reported that they would do so because of the COVID-19 pandemic.29

Adults and university students

Many surveys showed increases in uptake of influenza vaccination among adults and university students. In China, a November 2020 online study of attitudes toward and intention to receive influenza vaccination among 6922 university students showed that 62.9% reported increased intention to receive influenza vaccine because of COVID-19; overall, 4.7% had received the vaccine in 2019.46 In a survey of Chinese outpatients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, conducted at seven tertiary hospitals in Beijing in June and July 2021, 51.5% of respondents reported that the COVID-19 pandemic had influenced their intention to receive influenza vaccination; overall, 22.7% of respondents received the vaccine in the previous season (2020–21).54

Among patients attending an urban general practice clinic in Ireland (N = 465), uptake of influenza vaccine increased by 27.4% from 2019 to 2020; 75.9% of patients reported that the COVID-19 pandemic had made them more inclined to be vaccinated, and 48.9% considered the influenza vaccine to be more important than they had previously considered it to be.30 An October 2020 telephone survey of 434 Spanish adults with underlying health conditions found that 43.3% intended to receive influenza vaccine, 14.9% of whom decided to do so because of the COVID-19 pandemic.27

An online survey of 1001 Canadian adults (May 2020) showed that a greater proportion of those aged ≥65 years versus those aged 50–64 years planned to receive an influenza vaccine for the upcoming season (72.1% versus 47.0%). Among individuals who had originally planned not to receive an influenza vaccine, 8.1% and 20.0% of those aged ≥65 years and 50–64 years, respectively, planned to be vaccinated because of COVID-19.53 In addition, in a survey of 1000 US residents aged 18–64 years (June 2020), 27.9% of respondents were more likely to receive the influenza vaccine because of COVID-19; 49.3% of respondents planned to receive an influenza vaccination in the current season, whereas only 21.4% received the vaccine in each of the five previous seasons.37 Moreover, an online survey conducted between December 2020 and February 2021, among 206 adults who were recently seen at an academic internal medicine practice in the USA, showed that 88.3% of respondents had received the influenza vaccine; overall, 22 individuals (12.5%) reported that the COVID-19 pandemic changed their plans to be vaccinated against influenza, of whom 20 decided to have the vaccine.42 A total of 53% of students (N = 237) attending a US university influenza clinic reported that they were more likely to receive influenza vaccine because of the COVID-19 pandemic.38

Other

A Greek study in the 2019–20 and 2020–21 influenza seasons showed vaccination coverage was suboptimal among caregivers and household contacts of children with congenital heart disease, and that the COVID-19 pandemic had no significant impact on plans to receive influenza vaccine.55 Overall, 65.0% of children, 34.2% of mothers and 26.1% of fathers had previously received an influenza vaccine; only 9% of mothers and fathers cited COVID-19 as an influence to get vaccinated against influenza. Notably, among the unvaccinated parents, 55% of mothers and 28% of fathers stated that they were not interested in influenza vaccination.55

Influenza and pneumococcal vaccines

Two studies conducted in France assessed attitudes toward receiving both influenza and pneumococcal vaccination in adults with chronic medical conditions.56,57 In a study of adults at risk of infection (N = 1151) in three French hospitals between December 2020 and April 2021, 30.6% and 16.5% of patients who were not up to date on pneumococcal or influenza vaccines, respectively, were willing to update these vaccines as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic.56 Most participants in this survey had diabetes mellitus (714/1151, 62.0%). In an online survey of French adults at risk of infection (N = 2177), approximately two-thirds of patients (62.5%) stated that the COVID-19 pandemic convinced them to receive recommended vaccines; however, 30.2% and 27.9% reported that the pandemic had not convinced them to receive pneumococcal and influenza vaccines, respectively.57

Factors associated with intention to receive seasonal vaccines or uptake of seasonal vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic

Vaccine uptake

Five studies reported on associations between factors related to the COVID-19 pandemic and influenza vaccine uptake using self-reported questionnaires in China, Italy and Spain: fear of further COVID-19 outbreaks,46 a perception of high personal susceptibility to COVID-19,46,47,58 cohabiting with a person at risk for COVID-1928 and a consultation with a medical professional regarding COVID-1947 were all associated with a higher rate of influenza vaccination. Having a positive result for a COVID-19 test was not significantly correlated with receipt of influenza vaccine in Italian adults.25

Intention to receive vaccines

Diverse studies analyzed associations between parameters related to COVID-19 and the likelihood of intending to receive seasonal influenza or pneumococcal vaccines (Table S3). A significant positive association was observed between intention to receive an influenza vaccine and perceptions of increased risk of contracting COVID-19 in some studies.

Asia

In China, local COVID-19 cases did not influence the intention of citizens to receive influenza vaccination.45

Europe

Perceptions of high personal susceptibility to COVID-19 (among UK adults eligible for but previously unvaccinated with the influenza vaccine),35 and beliefs that influenza vaccination protects against the spread of COVID-19 (among the Greek general adult population),32 were associated with a greater intention to receive an influenza vaccine. Among Italian HCWs, the perception that influenza and COVID-19 share similar symptoms,59 and the belief that influenza vaccination is useful to distinguish influenza from COVID-19, were significantly associated with intention to receive an influenza vaccine.19

Adults in the UK who were eligible for routine influenza vaccination but had not received it in the past were not more inclined to receive influenza vaccination during the COVID-19 pandemic, even if they believed themselves to be highly susceptible to COVID-19.35 Concerns about contracting COVID-19 did not significantly influence Italian adults to receive influenza vaccination, although greater knowledge about COVID-19 was significantly associated with intention to receive an influenza vaccine.24 In contrast, in Greece, awareness of COVID-19 symptoms was not associated with intention to receive the influenza vaccine among caregivers and household contacts of children with congenital heart disease.32

Among Italian adults with suspected or confirmed COVID-19 who attended an infectious disease department between 1 March and 30 May 2020 (N = 599), older age (>65 years versus 18–44 years old), presence of a chronic disease and an increasing number of comorbidities were all significantly associated with acceptance of influenza vaccination.20 Prior admission to a hospital or intensive care ward for COVID-19 was also associated with a significantly higher likelihood of accepting influenza vaccination, whereas duration of symptoms or length of hospital stay was not.20 Intention to receive an influenza vaccine was highest among patients admitted to an intensive care unit (69.6%), followed by individuals admitted to a hospital ward (58.2%), and was lowest among those not admitted to hospital (40.3%).20

The strength of association between worry or anxiety regarding COVID-19, and intention to receive the influenza vaccine, varied. In Italy, university students who reported experiencing fear about the increase in positive COVID-19 cases or deaths had a significantly higher likelihood of receiving influenza vaccination.21 Polish adults who were afraid of COVID-19 were more likely to get vaccinated or declare an intention to receive an influenza vaccine compared with those who were not afraid.60 Among UK adults, the degree of anxiety over return from lockdown was generally not significantly associated with intention to receive an influenza vaccine, except for in patients with high anxiety over the return from lockdown who were newly eligible for influenza vaccination.35

North America

Knowing someone infected with COVID-19 did not alter the intention of US adults to receive an influenza vaccine.39 In Canada, prior COVID-19 infection, or having someone close who had died or was ill during the pandemic (any cause), did not significantly modify the intention of older adults (≥75 years versus <75 years old) to receive an influenza vaccine.43 However, the extent of the self-perceived impact of the pandemic had a significant impact on intention to receive an influenza vaccine in Canadian adults aged ≥50 years.43 A ‘negative impact’ was associated with a significantly greater likelihood of vaccination, whereas ‘very positive’ and ‘very negative’ impacts were associated with a significantly lower likelihood of vaccination.43 Adults who reported any degree of worry regarding COVID-19 were significantly more likely to receive an influenza vaccine compared with those who were not at all worried.43 Among the general population in the USA, COVID-19-related worry was not associated with an increased intention to receive influenza vaccination.39

Miscellaneous

Concerns (among caregivers in Canada, Israel, Japan, Spain, Switzerland and the USA) that a dependent child had contracted COVID-19 were associated with a greater intention to receive influenza vaccination.51

Hesitancy to receive an influenza vaccine

Several studies assessed COVID-19-related factors associated with hesitancy to receive an influenza vaccine: factors with significant associations included fear of COVID-19 infection (among Polish adults),61 self-rated risk of acquiring COVID-19 (among adults in the UK),62 a positive COVID-19 test result (among Italian adults)25 and the belief that spread of the novel coronavirus would be mitigated when a large percentage of the population had been infected (Greek caregivers of children with congenital heart disease).32 Polish individuals who believed that COVID-19 was an exaggerated or fictional threat were significantly more hesitant to receive an influenza vaccine than those who did not hold such beliefs.60

Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on childhood routine immunization rates

Gray literature search

Information from the gray literature search was collected for routine childhood vaccinations before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. According to WHO estimates, most national vaccination programmes had a decrease in coverage in 2021 compared with 2019, with the exception of rotavirus vaccine programmes.63 These decreases were coincident with COVID-19 pandemic-associated disruptions.

Childhood routine immunization data are available from the WHO for select countries.64 In China in 2020–21, the COVID-19 pandemic had no impact on vaccination coverage.64 Similarly, in France, Germany, Italy, the UK and the USA, coverage with all vaccines remained stable.64 In Japan, minor fluctuations were observed for some vaccines after onset of the pandemic; however, vaccination rates continued to exceed 90%.64 In Brazil, vaccination rates for all vaccines declined between 2018 and 2019; during the pandemic, vaccination rates increased for some vaccines and continued decreasing for others.64

Data from the UK COVER Programme are consistent with WHO/UNICEF data.65 Coverage with several vaccines decreased among infants and preschool children during the initial phase of the pandemic, with recovery during the last quarter of 2021.65,66 Fewer children received the first dose of the MMR vaccine (by age 18 months) from onset of the pandemic in 2020; however, an upward trend was observed from January 2021.66 Despite these fluctuations, vaccination rates generally exceeded 85%. Uptake of the influenza vaccine was lower in 2021–22 compared with 2020–21 in primary school age children in England, with vaccine uptake by year group being 55.8–58.9% of children in 2021–22.67

According to the US National Immunization Surveys, tetanus, diphtheria and acellular pertussis vaccine coverage in adolescents was similar before and after onset of the pandemic.68,69 Similarly, coverage with the MMR vaccine among adolescents was high and remained stable in 2019, 2020 and 2021.69,70 However, coverage with the MMR, DTaP IPV Hib and varicella vaccines decreased in the USA during the 2020–21 school year compared with the 2019–20 school year among kindergarten children.71

Systematic literature review

Of the citations identified in the systematic literature review, 11 reported detailed data on childhood routine immunization before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic had a negative impact on childhood vaccination programmes according to studies conducted in the USA,72–76 the UK,77,78 Canada79–81 and The Netherlands.82 Across all studies, actual vaccination rates decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic. In the USA, pediatric vaccination rates generally decreased after onset of the pandemic when ‘stay at home’ orders were implemented. Vaccination rates increased after these orders were lifted, but remained lower than 2019 levels.72–76 Routine childhood vaccination rates also decreased after the onset of the pandemic in Canada and recovery varied by province.79–81 In the UK, the COVID-19 pandemic had a negative impact on pneumococcal and MMR vaccination rates in children aged <19 years.77 In England, during the pre-lockdown period, the percentage of children vaccinated with the MMR vaccine and the 6-in-1 vaccine decreased, but then recovered gradually during lockdown.78 In contrast, in Scotland, immunization rates for these vaccines dropped immediately before the lockdown, then rose and exceeded pre-pandemic levels during lockdown. In The Netherlands, MMR vaccination rates in March–September 2020 were 6–14% lower than rates reported during the same period in 2019.82

Discussion

This analysis demonstrates that uptake of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines in adults generally increased in 2020–21 compared with the season preceding onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–20). Although the magnitude of the increase in intention to receive influenza vaccination varied, we identified a positive trend across geographic regions, including Europe, North America and Asia, and across the populations surveyed, including healthcare professionals, adults, students and caregivers of children.

The COVID-19 pandemic had a positive impact on intention to be vaccinated against influenza in several countries. Historically, uptake of the influenza vaccine has been low in many countries among people eligible to receive a vaccination for free or as part of their health insurance at no additional cost. For example, in the USA, 45.3% of adults aged ≥18 years received an influenza vaccine in 2018–19.83 Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, reasons for not getting vaccinated included perceptions of low susceptibility, organizational barriers, misconceptions and fear of vaccine-related adverse events.84–86 Data presented in this review were generally collected prior to the availability of COVID-19 vaccines, when citizens had a greater sense of susceptibility to COVID-19 infection. COVID-19 and influenza present with similar symptoms and affect similar high-risk groups, and vaccination against influenza was promoted in some jurisdictions to prevent concurrent COVID-19 and influenza epidemics.87,88 Thus, this review suggests that, in many countries, more people intended to receive an influenza vaccine and actual vaccination rates increased after onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Generally, adults who had previously received an influenza vaccine were more likely to state that they intended to receive an influenza vaccine during the COVID-19 pandemic. Older age (variably defined, but typically at least 60 years old) was generally associated with intention to receive an influenza vaccine. This is not surprising given older individuals are disproportionately susceptible to influenza and COVID-19, and older and at-risk individuals tend to be targeted in influenza vaccination campaigns. Higher perceived risk of contracting COVID-19, and greater anxiety regarding aspects of the pandemic, correlated with intention to receive an influenza vaccine in some countries but not in others. Although no clear causal factors for the variation between countries were identified, evidence from multicountry studies suggests that a lack of trust in domestic and international health institutions may have been associated with vaccine hesitancy.89–92 Trust in vaccines, vaccine producers, health authorities and governments contributes to vaccine acceptance and uptake.93,94 Level of trust in authorities has been previously linked to vaccine hesitancy,95 and the country-specific differences in pandemic response might explain the differences we have observed in our review. The pandemic has resulted in increased salience of vaccine-preventable disease, an unprecedented but varied focus globally on the use of non-pharmacological interventions (such as contact tracing, social distancing and lockdowns) to prevent the spread of disease along with the development of mathematical models to predict the possible impact of vaccination and other interventions,96–98 and variable levels of trust in healthcare systems.99 This might produce variable vaccine acceptance across countries but also in different populations within countries. An international survey in 2022 found variable levels of COVID-19 booster vaccine refusal ranging from 1.1% to 28.9%,100 which can be explained by different public health strategies deployed in response to the pandemic.

In contrast to the data on influenza vaccination and the limited data on pneumococcal vaccination in adults, routine childhood immunization rates tended to decrease during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic and then recover, but not to pre-pandemic levels. The timing of the decrease in immunization rates appeared to vary, with some countries reporting a decrease in immunizations immediately prior to lockdown, and others observing a decrease following the implementation of COVID-19 restrictions. The reasons for the incomplete recovery are unclear; barriers to vaccination may have persisted due to fears regarding the risk of COVID-19 infection during in-person visits, staffing shortages due to COVID-19 exposure or financial constraints.74 There is also the possibility that negative views regarding COVID-19 vaccines during the pandemic may have increased vaccine hesitancy in general, and subsequently reduced the uptake of childhood immunizations.101,102

Most studies included in this review were cross-sectional surveys in specific populations and therefore had important limitations. It is likely that formal validation of the questionnaires used was not performed in most cases. Multiple types of bias could have influenced study results, including selection, recall, self-report, social desirability and digital-divide bias. The timing of studies varied, as did the ways in which vaccine uptake, willingness to receive vaccines, vaccine confidence and vaccine hesitancy were measured across studies. Furthermore, studies varied in the quality of reporting of the methods and results. Overall, the studies reviewed exhibited marked heterogeneity, which led to some difficulties in data interpretation. This should be taken into account when considering the comparability and generalizability of some of the research findings.

Conclusions

Despite the diversity of the identified studies, there appeared to be a trend toward increased uptake of adult vaccines, in particular the influenza vaccine, across 13 countries after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Similarly, despite variations in the approaches used to measure vaccine acceptance and vaccine intent, positive trends were observed in most studies. The increase in vaccine acceptance may have been due to increased feelings of susceptibility to viral infections like COVID-19 and influenza, or to improvements in public trust. Routine childhood immunization rates, in contrast with rates for adult vaccines, tended to decrease during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic and then recover, but not to pre-pandemic levels.

The findings of this review may inform ongoing responses to the recent COVID-19 pandemic and future responses to any new pandemic. Well-established immunization programmes may be less impacted by pandemics than other immunization programmes, and a robust immunization policy combined with strong healthcare professional advocacy for vaccination have the potential to increase the resilience of immunization programmes during future pandemics. Healthcare professionals and others should be prepared to answer questions and concerns of the public that have increased relevance during a pandemic, for example, on the role of vaccination when non-pharmaceutical interventions, such as social distancing and face masks, have been mandated, and mitigating the risks associated with attending a clinic in person for a vaccination. Longitudinal studies should clarify the ongoing impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on attitudes regarding vaccination, particularly in the adult setting beyond influenza vaccination. Increased standardization of the methods used in such studies and coordination between research groups would help, for example, when comparing and interpreting findings, including in the context of different environments within different regions or countries, and when performing meta-analyses. The implementation of catch-up programmes for childhood routine vaccinations that have been negatively impacted by the pandemic may help to prevent outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases in the future. Expanded programmes on immunization in some high-risk groups, together with social behavior change approaches to enhance public trust in adult vaccination protocols, may further improve the robustness of future pandemic responses.
