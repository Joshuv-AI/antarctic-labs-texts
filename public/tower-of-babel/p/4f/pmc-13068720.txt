Barriers and facilitators for implementing patient-reported outcome measures in oncology practices: an umbrella review
Authors: Elke Rammant, Imogen Ramsey, Kelly M de Ligt, Carolyn Mazariego, Katarzyna Pogoda, Heike Schmidt, Kim Beernaert, Emma Lidington, Raymond Chan Javan, Bogda Koczwara, Monika Sztankay, Lonneke van de Poll-Franse, Mieke Van Hemelrijck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly valued for enhancing patient-centered care and clinical decision-making in oncology. However, routine use remains limited due to implementation challenges. This umbrella review synthesized evidence on barriers and facilitators to PROMs implementation in oncology care using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO CRD42024539970), we included systematic and scoping reviews published in English up to 25/11/2025 examining PROMs implementation in cancer care. Searches were conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, CINAHL, and Web of Science. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and conducted qualitative content analysis, categorizing findings within CFIR domains. Review quality was appraised with AMSTAR 2.ResultsEighteen reviews (2004–2024) were included. Most barriers and facilitators (n = 34) were mapped to Innovation Characteristics (i.e. features related to the intervention). Key barriers included limited perceived clinical value of PROMs and increased patient burden. Inner Setting (i.e. the context within which the implementation occurs) challenges included poor integration with electronic health records and workflow disruptions, while facilitators encompassed alignment with clinical guidelines and staff training. At the Individual level (i.e. attributes related to individuals involved), limited provider knowledge and low patient e-health literacy were noted.ConclusionSuccessful PROMs implementation requires raising awareness of their clinical value, seamless integration with health records, workflow optimization, and adequate staff training. Patient support should target e-health literacy and equitable access to digital tools. The overall low quality of existing reviews underscores the need for more rigorous evidence on implementation in oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11136-026-04205-z.Keywords: PROMS, Oncology, Clinical practice, Barriers, Facilitators, CFIR

Introduction

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have gained increasing recognition in oncology for their critical role in assessing the impact of cancer and its treatment from the patient’s perspective [1]. PROMs capture essential information about patients’ symptoms, functional status, and overall well-being, which can complement clinical assessments and provide a more holistic view of health [1]. Routine use of PROMs can significantly enhance patient-centered care by facilitating better communication between patients and healthcare providers, identifying unmet needs, and enabling timely interventions [2]. Potentially due to improved symptom management, the integration of PROMs into cancer care is associated with improved overall survival and quality of life [3]. “The Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer Supportive Care 2030 Movement” has recently declared that routine implementation of PROMs should be one of the 15 ambitions of the global cancer care community [4].

Despite the acknowledged benefits, the implementation of PROMs in daily clinical practice remains limited [5]. Several barriers at different levels, including organizational, provider, and patient-related challenges, hinder implementation. These barriers range from logistical and technical issues, such as lack of infrastructure and time constraints, to attitudinal barriers like healthcare providers’ skepticism about the relevance of PROMs and patients’ concerns about privacy and data security [2, 6–8].

Numerous systematic reviews have investigated these barriers and facilitators, providing valuable insights into the multifaceted challenges associated with PROMs implementation. These reviews collectively highlight the complexity of integrating PROMs into routine practice, but their findings are often dispersed and lack integration within a common framework. As a result, although the challenges are well-documented, there remains limited guidance on how to effectively address them in practice [7–9]. An umbrella review (i.e. review of reviews)[10] that provides a structured synthesis of the evidence within an implementation framework would help translate this body of evidence into practical, targeted strategies for implementation.

The use of implementation science frameworks can enhance our understanding of how to translate evidence into practice by identifying the factors that influence implementation success. Among these, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) offers a comprehensive, theory-driven structure to categorize barriers and facilitators influencing implementation across five domains: innovation characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and implementation process [11]. Applying the CFIR allows systematic and nuanced analysis of the diverse factors that influence PROMs uptake, which can inform the design of tailored strategies and interventions. [12].

This umbrella review therefore aimed to map existing evidence on barriers and facilitators to PROMs implementation in oncology using the CFIR. By synthesizing findings from existing reviews through this lens, this review will generate a high-level, theory-informed understanding of key factors influencing implementation. The insights gained will support and inform the development of targeted, evidence-based strategies for integrating PROMs into routine clinical practice.

Methods

Reporting of this umbrella review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [13]. This review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024539970).

Inclusion criteria and search strategy

Eligible study designs for this umbrella review were reviews with a clear description of an aim and methodology for systematically searching, selecting, and synthesizing studies (e.g. systematic reviews, scoping reviews, meta-analyses). We included reviews focusing on adult cancer populations (P) that investigated barriers and facilitators to the implementation of PROMs in routine cancer clinical practice (I), reporting barriers/facilitators influencing PROM integration, use, or uptake (O). As this review focused on implementation determinants, no comparator was required.

We excluded reviews that did not address PROM implementation (e.g. those focusing solely on psychometric validation or outcome measurement), those conducted outside clinical practice contexts, narrative reviews without a systematic methodology, and conference abstracts. Non-English articles were excluded. No exclusion was applied based on publication date.

Five databases were searched from inception until the search date (25/11/2025): MEDLINE (using the PubMed interface), EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, CINAHL and Web of Science (WoS). Key search terms were related to “cancer”, “PROMs”, and “implementation”. Furthermore, cited references of the included reviews were checked. The full search strategies are presented in Supplementary File 1.

Screening and study selection

After pooling search results and removing duplicates, two reviewers (ER and IR) independently screened titles and abstracts of all identified articles for relevance using the web application Rayyan (without the artificial intelligence features). The same reviewers retrieved and reviewed the full texts of all potentially relevant articles against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

To extract relevant data, a data extraction form was developed and pilot-tested with three studies. One reviewer (ER) extracted relevant systematic review characteristics (date, population, aim, number and type of included studies). Two independent reviewers (ER and one of the following reviewers: KL, CM, HS, KP) extracted findings on barriers and facilitators to PROM implementation and classified them within the domains of the CFIR.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias of the included reviews was assessed using the Assessing the Methodological quality of Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR 2) tool. The AMSTAR 2 tool contains fourteen items for systematic reviews and two additional items for meta-analyses. The tool does not provide a total numerical score but instead assesses the quality based on the individual items which are scored as ‘yes’, ‘partial yes’ or ‘no’. The more individual items are answered with ‘yes’ or ‘partial yes’, the higher the quality of the review. Note: it is the quality of the reviews that was assessed, not the quality of the individual studies within the reviews.

Data analysis

The consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR) 2.0

To guide the analysis, we used the updated 2022 version of the CFIR framework to categorize factors influencing implementation [11]. While the CFIR includes 5 domains (i.e. Innovation Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Implementation Process), we excluded the Implementation Process domain because it primarily addresses activities and strategies used to implement the innovation (i.e. implementation strategies), whereas the scope of this review was determinants of implementation (i.e., barriers and facilitators). Each CFIR domain encompasses multiple constructs (e.g. the innovation domain includes constructs such as innovation source, innovation evidence-base). Coding was conducted at the construct level, allowing detailed identification of specific factors influencing PROM implementation. Within the “Characteristics of Individuals” domain, reviewers first selected the relevant role (e.g., healthcare provider, patient) and then documented the characteristics applicable to that role. If included reviews used the CFIR framework to categorize barriers and facilitators, these categorizations were not retained in the analysis. Instead, barriers and facilitators were inductively extracted from all reviews without applying any predefined framework. Subsequently, all extracted data were deductively coded into the CFIR 2.0 domains and constructs to ensure a consistent analytical framework across all included reviews.

Innovation Characteristics encompass the “thing” being implemented and includes attributes to the intervention itself. The Outer Setting considers the setting in which the Inner Setting exists covering external influences. The Inner Setting addresses organizational factors like culture, workflow integration, resources, and infrastructure. Lastly, Characteristics of Individuals are based on the COM-B system and include needs, capability, opportunity, and motivation of key stakeholders, such as healthcare providers and patients. Together, these domains offer a comprehensive lens to identify and understand the multilevel factors that influence PROMs implementation [11].

Content analysis

One reviewer (ER) analyzed qualitative data on barriers and facilitators using content analysis in the software program NVivo. Qualitative data extracted from the included reviews were first coded inductively, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data without applying predefined categories. During this phase, codes were generated line-by-line and iteratively refined through constant comparison, resulting in a structured codebook that defined each code and illustrated it with examples from the source material.

In a second step, the inductively derived codes were deductively mapped onto the domains of the CFIR 2.0 framework to organize and interpret the findings within an established implementation model. This hybrid approach allowed for both data-driven identification of determinants and theory-informed structuring of results.

To ensure accuracy and consistency, four additional reviewers (KL, CM, HS, KP) independently cross-checked the coded data from each review against the CFIR domains and flagged any discrepancies. ER resolved disagreements by revisiting the original sources in light of the reviewers’ comments. In the results section, we distinguish between barriers, facilitators, and bidirectional factors. The latter refers to determinants that can function as either a barrier or facilitator depending on the context. The results of this umbrella review are presented thematically, organized according to the CFIR domains.

Characteristics of the included reviews

Eighteen reviews, published from 2004–2025 were included in this umbrella review. Two types of reviews were included: eight systematic reviews [6, 8, 9, 16–22] and four scoping reviews [23–26]. Two reviews described methods for systematically identifying studies but did not specify a type of review [7, 27]. The majority of the reviews focused on a mixed cancer population, while one focused on patients with lung cancer [24], one on patients with breast cancer [8] and another one on palliative cancer care [17]. The number of individual studies in each review ranged from 5–66 studies.

Overlap of individual studies included in the reviews

To evaluate the overlap of primary studies across the included systematic reviews, the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) was calculated. With a total of 560 study appearances (N), 422 unique primary studies (r), and 18 systematic reviews (c), the resulting CCA was 2%. According to established thresholds, this indicates a slight overlap between the reviews [15]. An overview of the primary studies in each review is shown in Supplementary File 2.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality assessment indicated that the methodological quality of the included reviews was low overall. Only 10 reviews achieved a score of 50% or higher on the AMSTAR tool. The lowest scoring quality domains across studies were: provision of a list of excluded studies with justification, reporting on the sources of funding for the included studies, justification of the included study designs, adequate technique for assessing risk of bias, and incorporating risk of bias into the interpretation/discussion of the results. Barriers and facilitators to implement PROMS in daily oncology practice

A comprehensive overview of all barriers and facilitators is presented in Table 3.

Innovation characteristics

Most identified barriers (n = 13), facilitators (n = 17) or bidirectional factors (n = 5) were categorized as Innovation Characteristics, which refers to implementation factors related to the intervention itself (i.e., the use of PROMs in clinical practice is the intervention). In this category, the most commonly reported barriers were the perceived uselessness of PROMs to either HCPs or patients (n = 10), and the additional patient burden to complete PROMs (n = 6). Common bidirectional factors were the user-friendliness of the ePRO system (n = 10) and difficulty interpreting PRO data (e.g. direction of symptoms, data visualization). The most common facilitators were e-mail reminders to clinicians or patients (n = 5), perceived relevance of PROMs to individual patient characteristics or disease/treatment (n = 6), and perceived benefits of PROMs on patient outcomes (n = 5) or work-related factors (n = 4).

Outer setting

Several barriers and facilitators were identified within the Outer Setting domain. Barriers included concerns about liability and legal issues (n = 4) (i.e., the potential creation of new standards of care through PROM use, with possible legal vulnerability if identified HRQOL problems are not adequately addressed), concerns regarding the security of patient data (n = 4), absence of policy support at the local or institutional level (n = 1), and lack of specialized funding models for PRO symptom monitoring (n = 1).

Facilitators included institutional approval and policies supporting PROM implementation (n = 1), implementation of PROs in compliance with existing guidelines and local legal norms (n = 1), establishing and strengthening partnerships with external organizations (n = 1), availability of external financial support (n = 1), and external performance-measurement pressure, such as the use of PROs as performance evaluation indicators (n = 1).

Inner setting

Within the Inner Setting domain, several barriers, facilitators, and bidirectional factors were identified. The most frequently reported barriers were lack of integration of the ePRO system with electronic health records or other healthcare IT systems (n = 9), disruption or delays in clinical workflows and routines (n = 8), and lack of time and/or staffing (n = 8). Additional commonly reported barriers included technology-related issues (e.g., login problems) (n = 6) and perceptions of increased workload, such as time required for data interpretation and administrative burden (n = 7).

Training and education on PROMs for healthcare professionals (e.g., interpretation of data, use of ePRO systems, and acting on scores) emerged as a bidirectional factor (n = 8), functioning as a barrier when insufficient and as a facilitator when adequately provided. Perceptions of workload similarly acted as a bidirectional factor, depending on the degree of integration and organizational support.

The most commonly reported facilitators were integration of PROMs into existing clinical practice guidelines (n = 5), clear workflow definitions or structures (n = 3), and automation of processes such as symptom flagging and referrals (n = 2).

Characteristics of individuals

Healthcare providers

Several barriers and facilitators related to the roles and characteristics of healthcare providers were identified. Capability-related barriers included lack of knowledge about PROMs (such as their benefits, interpretation, and how to act on results) (n = 3), low self-efficacy to use or discuss PRO results (n = 1), lack of knowledge on using digital (ePRO) systems (n = 1), and limited implementation experience (e.g. due to limited working hours) (n = 1). In terms of opportunity, lack of support from colleagues and management was reported as a barrier (n = 1). Motivation-related barriers included preference for face-to-face assessments (n = 2) and reluctance to change existing routines (n = 2).

Facilitators for healthcare providers included complete awareness, understanding, and familiarity with PROs (n = 1), a sense of ownership over PROM use (i.e. using PROMs is regarded as part of their job) (n = 1), buy-in from frontline staff and managers (n = 1), positive attitudes towards ePROMs (n = 1), and perceptions that using the ePRO system is an expected part of care (normative belief) (n = 1).

Patients

Multiple patient-related barriers and facilitators were identified. Capability-related barriers included low eHealth literacy (i.e. limited ability to use technology) (n = 6), low health literacy (i.e. difficulty understanding and completing PROMs) (n = 5), older age (n = 4), illness-related impairments (e.g. being too ill or having visual impairments) (n = 4), low socio-economic status (n = 2), lower education level (n = 2), language or cultural constraints (n = 2), and complex cancer types (n = 1). A capability-related facilitator was patients’ capacity for self-management (n = 1).

Opportunity-related barriers included lack of access to digital devices or internet connectivity at home (n = 4), non-working status (n = 1), rural living (n = 1), and low levels of social support (n = 1). Opportunity-related facilitators included access to kiosks with PRO applications or internet connectivity (n = 2) and availability of social support (n = 1).

Motivation-related barriers included preference for face-to-face symptom reporting to clinicians (n = 3), lack of comfort discussing symptoms (n = 2), lack of feedback from physicians (n = 1), and questions about the significance of PROs (n = 1). A key motivational facilitator was timely and appropriate clinical responses to patient-reported alerts (n = 1).

Discussion

This umbrella review aimed to provide a thematic synthesis of evidence on barriers and facilitators to PROMs implementation in oncology, mapped to the domains of the CFIR. Across 17 reviews of primarily low methodological quality, barriers identified included the perceived lack of value of PROMs by healthcare providers and patients, the additional burden on patients, and challenges with the user-friendliness of ePRO systems and interpreting PRO data. Workflow disruptions, poor integration with electronic health records, and a lack of time and staff were also significant obstacles. Facilitators included email reminders, the relevance of PROMs to specific conditions, alignment with clinical guidelines, and staff training. At the individual level, addressing the lack of knowledge among healthcare providers and improving e-health literacy in patients were perceived as key to overcoming barriers.

Several barriers and facilitators in this review were closely interrelated, collectively influencing the implementation of PROMs. For instance, the perceived lack of value of PROMs among healthcare providers and patients can stem from a lack of knowledge, often due to insufficient training, regarding the benefits of using PROMs. This knowledge gap not only diminishes the perceived benefits associated with PROMs but also hampers positive attitudes towards their use. A systems thinking approach, which considers the relationships between different elements within a healthcare system, can offer valuable insights into how such factors interact and influence implementation outcomes [28, 29]. Recognizing this complex interplay highlights the need for a comprehensive approach to PROMs implementation, that includes education on PROMs as well as training on how to interpret and act on PRO data, a need identified across multiple studies [30–32]. Targeted training and education of HCPs can improve their understanding of and attitudes towards PROMs, thereby enhancing the perceived value of PROMs and fostering a more supportive environment for implementation [30, 32].

Importantly, incorporating PROMs into the training of healthcare students (e.g., medicine, nursing) can improve their familiarity and confidence in using PROMs early in their careers [32]. A recent study by Sztankay et al.[33] explored HCPs’ attitudes and learning needs related to PROMs to inform the development of an e-learning course designed to support their clinical use [33]. However, training alone is unlikely to be sufficient. Additional implementation strategies, such as audit and feedback mechanisms and the use of clinical champions, will also be needed to drive behaviour change and promote sustained adoption of PROMs in clinical care[34]. In addition to knowledge and skills, motivational factors among healthcare providers, such as reluctance to change routines, preference for face-to-face assessments, and normative beliefs about whether PROM use is an expected part of care, also appear to shape implementation success, suggesting that behaviour change strategies should address not only competence but also professional identity and norms.

Another barrier influencing patient engagement was the additional patient burden of completing PROMs, a phenomenon often referred to as ‘survey fatigue’ [35, 36]. In fragmented healthcare systems, patients may receive multiple PROMs across different departments and contexts (e.g., also for clinical trials), often leading to survey duplication and increased burden. Integrating PROMs into electronic health records as well as improving coordination and communication between HCPs and departments may prevent this duplication and lower patient burden [36]. Another important strategy to alleviate patient burden is to educate patients about the purpose and value of PROMs. When patients understand how these tools can improve their care (e.g., by enhancing symptom monitoring or facilitating more informed clinical decision-making), the perceived burden may be reduced and PROMs may be seen as a meaningful part of their care journey [32, 37].

To enhance patient engagement, it is crucial to explore novel ways of presenting PROs, such as providing personalized feedback based on their scores. HCPs play a pivotal role in this process by clearly communicating the benefits of using PROMs and actively using PRO data in their consultations [37].

Other key barriers for patients included low e-health literacy, low health literacy, and limited access to digital devices or internet connectivity, while facilitators included patients’ capacity for self-management, access to social support, and timely clinical responses to reported symptoms. To support patient engagement, implementation strategies should offer flexible modes of PROM completion (e.g., digital, paper-based, or kiosk-supported), provide training or guidance for patients, and ensure timely feedback on reported outcomes. These strategies can help make PROMs more accessible, actionable, and meaningful, supporting patients in participating effectively in their own care. While the included reviews primarily reported barriers and facilitators related to literacy, access, and support, implementation strategies may also benefit from considering additional patient-centered factors, such as flexibility in completion and autonomy in reporting, to enhance engagement across diverse patient populations. Another crucial barrier identified was lack of time and staff. Without sufficient time for clinical encounters, HCPs struggle to integrate PROMs effectively into patient interaction [37]. Integrating PROMs into electronic health records as well as into practice guidelines can help minimize disruptions to clinical workflows and potentially save time for HCPs [30, 38]. Another aim should be to help clinicians understand that the active use of PROMs can save time and contribute to efficiency and efficacy in clinical routines. However, for this to occur, clinicians must adapt their attitudes, communication strategies, and routine behaviors to accept that patients’ views are meaningful and integral to clinical decision-making [39]. These issues reflect broader challenges within Patient-Centered Care (PCC), but our findings indicate that PROMs may pose a particularly salient challenge, as several participants expressed reservations about their added value and the practical difficulties of integrating them into routine workflows.

Adequate funding is necessary to facilitate the preparation and redesign of clinical workflows that support PROMs integration. As highlighted by Eng et al. [34], successful implementation also depends on the involvement of both administrators and frontline clinicians, who often perceive different barriers. Administrators may emphasize resource limitations, while clinicians are more concerned with clinical utility and workflow [34]. To secure the necessary investment, stronger real-world evidence is needed to demonstrate the tangible benefits of PROMs in enhancing patient care and outcomes. This evidence can inform health policy decisions and support sustained investment in PROM implementation across healthcare systems.

Our findings also highlight the importance of outer-setting determinants, such as legal, policy, and regulatory considerations, which have received comparatively limited attention in prior discussions of PROM implementation. Concerns about liability and the potential creation of new standards of care suggest that PROM use may alter professional accountability, particularly when clinically relevant issues are identified but not acted upon. Clear institutional policies, alignment with legal norms, and guidance on clinical responsibilities are therefore essential to mitigate legal uncertainty. At the same time, facilitators such as policy support, external funding, partnerships with external organizations, and performance-measurement pressure may help legitimize PROM use and embed it within routine oncology care. These findings underscore that successful PROM implementation depends not only on local workflows and individual behaviour but also on broader organizational and regulatory contexts.

As mentioned in the introduction, identifying barriers and facilitators is a first crucial step, but the next important step is to explore implementation strategies for addressing these factors. According to the ESMO guidelines, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are in progress comparing the effectiveness of different implementation strategies for implementing PROMs [40]. Since implementation strategies are rarely universally successful and vary according to the specific clinical context [41], further research must help clarify why specific implementation strategies work in some contextual conditions and others not. Understanding how and why implementation strategies work (or fail) will help move the field beyond simply identifying barriers and implementation strategies for implementing PROMs in clinical practice [41]. Realist evaluations can play a vital role in this process, by examining the contexts in which PROMs are implemented and the mechanisms that drive their success or failure [42].

In addition, future PROMs implementation efforts should adopt a strength-based approach, focusing not only on overcoming barriers but also leveraging facilitators. While current efforts mainly address barriers (i.e., a needs-based approach), conducting a combined strength and barrier assessment would enable healthcare teams to address challenges while building on existing positive factors. Incorporating this strength-based approach would foster a more balanced and proactive PROMs integration process [43].

Our review identified various facilitators, although this term was often not applied appropriately. For instance, some factors such as gradually increasing the patient population or involving end users, were sometimes labelled as facilitators, but are more accurately described as implementation strategies. Facilitators are inherent factors that make the implementation process easier, while implementation strategies are targeted actions designed to drive adoption [44, 45]. Correct use of these terms is vital for accurate interpretation and application of findings from implementation research, which in turn can affect the effectiveness of PROMs integration into clinical practice [45].

Several initiatives (e.g. PROTEUS, EORTC QLG projects) are aimed at supporting the integration of PROMs into daily clinical practice, and may help address some of the barriers identified in this review. For example, the EORTC manual for the use of EORTC measures in daily clinical practice [46, 47]. The CHES software platform provides practical tools for data collection and feedback [48], and the training course developed by Sztankay et al. [33] offers structured education for clinicians on how to interpret and use PROM results in practice. These efforts reflect a growing commitment to overcoming key implementation challenges.

This umbrella review has several strengths and limitations that should be acknowledged. A key strength is its comprehensive approach, synthesizing a wide range of studies to provide a holistic view of the barriers and facilitators associated with PROM implementation in oncology. Using the CFIR framework, we systematically categorized factors influencing implementation, which may help PROM users anticipate barriers, identify facilitators, and select or design appropriate implementation strategies [11].

However, the review also has several limitations. First, variability in how studies defined and reported barriers and facilitators may lead to inconsistencies in interpretation, limiting the generalizability of our conclusions. Second, the inclusion of primarily English-language studies may reduce the diversity of perspectives and implementation practices represented. Third, the poor methodological quality of some included reviews, such as unclear inclusion/exclusion criteria, potential funding biases, and limited justification of study design, reduces confidence in the findings. These limitations affect the strength of our recommendations, as identified facilitators and barriers may be incomplete, and strategies derived from these findings should be applied cautiously.

Additionally, some identified barriers may reflect process-related factors, such as a setting’s implementation capacity and capability, which are not fully captured by the CFIR domains included in this review. For example, challenges in planning, needs assessment, or engagement of stakeholders may manifest downstream as barriers related to training, workflow integration, or perceived fit of PROMs. Another limitation is the minimal reporting on administrator perspectives in the included reviews. Few studies captured the views of nurse managers, department heads, or other administrative roles, which may underrepresent determinants related to leadership, decision-making, and resource allocation that are critical for PROM implementation. Finally, it is possible that studies on PROM implementation in routine care remain unpublished, which may introduce publication bias.

Despite these limitations, this umbrella review provides valuable insights into the complex landscape of PROM implementation in oncology and underscores the need for more rigorous, transparently reported research to guide future practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the successful implementation of PROMs in oncology practice depends on addressing a wide range of barriers while leveraging existing facilitators. In addition to the need for higher quality evidence in systematic reviews, future efforts should prioritize developing tailored strategies that address the specific challenges within the innovation and inner settings, such as increasing staff awareness of the value of PROMs, integrating PROMs into electronic health records, optimizing clinical workflows, and enhancing staff training in PROM utilization and interpretation. Patient support efforts should focus on improving e-health literacy and ensuring equitable access to digital tools.
