Airport Deicers: An Unrecognized Source of Phosphorus Loading in Receiving Waters
Authors: Owen M Stefaniak, Steven R Corsi, Troy D Rutter, Greg G Failey
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAirport ice control products contributed to total phosphorus (TP) loadings in a study of surface water runoff at a medium-sized airport from 2015 to 2021. Eleven airport ice control products had TP concentrations from 1–807 mg L–1 in liquid formulas, while solid pavement deicer had a TP concentration of 805 mg kg–1. Product application data, formula TP concentrations, and surface water sampling results were used to estimate TP concentration and loading contributions from these ice control products to receiving streams. Airport ice control products were found to contribute to TP in 84% of the water samples collected at downstream sites during deicing events, and TP concentrations at those sites exceeded aquatic life benchmarks in 70% of samples collected during deicing. A receiving stream 6 km downstream had TP attributed to airport ice control sources in 78% of the samples. TP loadings at an upstream site and the receiving stream site were greatest during the largest runoff events as is typical in urban runoff, but this pattern was not always followed at airport outfall sites due to the influence of TP in deicer products. Products analyzed in this study are used at airports across the United States and abroad, and findings suggest that airport deicers could represent a previously unrecognized source of phosphorus to adjacent waterways.Keywords: airport, deicer, aircraft deicer, pavement deicer, total phosphorus, phosphorus loading, great lakes, surface water

Introduction

Aircraft and pavement deicing operations are an integral part of safe air travel at airports around the world.1,2 Commonly used aircraft deicer and anti-icing fluids (ADAF) include Type I deicers, which remove ice that is present, and Type IV anti-icers for inhibiting the formation of new ice on the aircraft. Runways, taxiways, and aprons must also be free of snow and ice, and a combination of liquid and solid pavement deicing materials (PDM) are used for this purpose. Liquid pavement deicer can be used to pretreat paved surfaces to limit ice adhesion to the asphalt. Solid pavement deicer is used to penetrate ice that is already present and may be followed by additional liquid product application before plows and brushes physically clear the snow and ice.3

ADAF products have a high biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) and can degrade aquatic ecosystems through oxygen depletion.4−6 The freezing point depressants (FPD) and additives in ADAF and PDMs can also be toxic to aquatic organisms3,7−11 and have been linked to the proliferation of heterotrophic bacteria biofilm growth that can adversely impact stream biota.12 The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has designated spent ADAF as a regulated industrial wastewater.13 New airports are required by law to collect 60% of their ADAF runoff while existing airports are handled by a site-specific permitting process.14 Any uncollected spent deicing products can run off to nearby surface waters or enter the local groundwater.15−17

The most common FPDs in ADAF are propylene glycol (PG) and ethylene glycol, and the most common FPDs in PDM are potassium acetate, sodium acetate, and potassium formate. The FPDs are typically 50–98% of the products, with the remainder including water and a suite of proprietary additives such as corrosion inhibitors, surfactants, pH buffers, and dyes.18 Benzotriazole-based corrosion inhibitors have previously been identified as a component that contributes to the aquatic toxicity of deicer formulations,9 but these corrosion inhibitors have been phased out in many deicer products.19 Corrosion inhibitors in these formulations may also include phosphate-based compounds.20 Additionally, phosphate buffers have been used in some ADAF formulations for pH control.21 Phosphorus is a potent driver of eutrophication22−24 and can cause numerous impacts on aquatic ecosystems, including nuisance plant growth,25,26 dissolved oxygen depletion and hypoxia,27−30 fish mortality and habitat loss,31−33 and a loss of ecosystem services.34,35 Excess phosphorus loading can also cause toxin-producing harmful algal blooms (HABs) in freshwater systems,36−38 and the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources (DNR) issued new phosphorus requirements for surface waters in 2010.39 Airports are commonly required to have a stormwater discharge permit, which includes a pollution prevention plan and control measures for various types of waste. While ADAF and PDM are important components of these permits, airport ice control products are not widely recognized as sources of phosphorus inputs, and therefore, this source of phosphorus is not commonly addressed in airport stormwater permits or recognized as a source of phosphorus loading to airport-adjacent waterways.

The objectives of this study are to (1) define the phosphorus content of 11 airport ice control products used between 2015 and 2021 at Mitchell International Airport (MKE) in Milwaukee, Wisconsin (WI), (2) monitor phosphorus concentrations in surface water samples collected around MKE during the same time period, (3) estimate how ADAF and PDM may have contributed to the TP observed in those water samples by using nonphosphorus-containing constituents of the products as markers to observe their occurrence in the streams and comparing product application records and TP content of each formula, and (4) estimate how these products contributed to phosphorus loading in the watershed during deicing events such as snowmelt and freezing precipitation by incorporating streamflow data during each sampling event.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

Mitchell International Airport (MKE) is a medium-hub commercial service airport covering roughly 880 ha, situated 9 km south of downtown Milwaukee, WI.40 Five sites were selected for water sampling (Figure 1 and Supporting Information (SI), Table S2). Four sites are within or immediately adjacent to the airport property. One site (Receiving Stream site) is at the end of Wilson Park Creek, where it drains into the Kinnickinnic River roughly 6 km from the airport. The Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary outfalls were selected to characterize airport runoff, while the Receiving Stream site was selected to characterize airport runoff to a downstream receiving water body. The Infall site is primarily upstream of major airport operations and was selected to characterize water quality parameters upstream of the airport environment, though small amounts of airport runoff may periodically reach the site due to the proximity of an Air National Guard base and two nearby runways.

Product Formulations

MKE airport personnel collected samples of 7 currently used Type I, Type IV, and pavement deicer and anti-icer products from storage in December 2020. Four additional products that were used previously at MKE, but discontinued prior to 2020, were obtained from a sample archive maintained by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Upper Midwest Water Science Center. All product samples (Tables 1 and S1) were sent to the Wisconsin State Laboratory of Hygiene (WSLH) in Madison, WI, for chemical analysis. Since 2015, MKE has used four different Type I deicer products, three of which were still in use in the final year of the current study. Five Type IV anti-icing products were used over the same period, only two of which were in use in the final year of this study. The PDM products consist of one liquid and one solid deicer, and these products were consistent over the study duration.

Surface Water Quality Samples

Surface water samples were collected between 2015–2021 during cold weather periods when airport ice control operations were necessary—typically November to April—which will subsequently be referred to as “deicing season” (SI, Table S3). Additional summer samples (n = 4) were collected to assess water quality parameters outside of the deicing season. Sample collection methods have been previously described41 and are summarized here. Flow-weighted composite water samples were collected during runoff or precipitation events using refrigerated autosamplers (Teledyne ISCO, Lincoln, NE) with Teflon sample lines at all sites except the Tertiary Outfall site, where manual grab samples were collected by dipping triple-rinsed polypropylene bottles in the center of the stream channel. The stream water level was measured with bubbler-type pressure transducers (Sutron Accubar, Sutron Corporation, Sterling, VA), and streamflow was subsequently calculated using a log–log relation between water level and flow.42 The Tertiary Outfall is the only site without permanent stream monitoring infrastructure, and no streamflow data are available for this location. Surface water samples were delivered to WSLH within 3 days of collection, and analysis times varied by analytes (SI, Table S3).

Chemical Analysis

TP concentration was analyzed in the ice control products as well as in the surface water samples. PG and acetate were measured in surface water samples as a means to assess the occurrence of ice control products. These analytes are subcomponents of the products that are used as FPDs, and while they do not contain phosphorus directly, they serve as chemical markers to detect and quantify ADAF and PDM in the environment. COD of the liquid products was measured to calculate the strength of products diluted with water prior to application. For TP analysis of liquid ice control products and surface water samples, WSLH used automated spectrophotometry for colorimetric analysis following method EPA 365.1. Sample matrix interference was above tolerable limits for two of the products (Type IV-4 and Type IV-3b), and these samples were subsequently analyzed with inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) following method EPA 200.7. TP analysis of the solid pavement deicer was conducted using ICP-AES following method SW846 6010B. Analysis of PG content was done with gas chromatography following EPA method 8015C. For COD analysis of liquid products, WSLH performed sealed digestion and spectrometry following method ASTM D1252–06B. Solid pavement deicer was not analyzed for COD. Acetate in pavement products and surface water samples was analyzed using ion chromatography as detailed in Corsi et al.3

Quality Assurance

Field blanks were collected annually at the four sites with autosamplers between 2015–2020 (SI, Table S6). No blanks were collected during the 2021 water year. Blank sample collection required two people, and internal bureau travel restrictions due to the COVID-19 pandemic made this untenable. Deionized reagent water was pumped through the autosampler system into collection bottles to simulate environmental sample collection. Of the 21 field blank samples collected, 2 had detections for propylene glycol (PG = 11 mg L–1 in both), and these detections were similar to the lowest PG concentrations observed in the environmental samples, which ranged from 10 mg L–1 to 13,000 mg L–1. Acetate was below the limit of detection in all blank samples. Phosphorus was analyzed in the field blanks beginning with the 2016 water year. Six of these blank samples had low-level detections for phosphorus (TP < 0.008 mg L–1) that were below the lowest concentration observed in any of the environmental samples.

Laboratory quality assurance measurements were all within acceptable limits as defined by the WSLH: Acceptable limits for duplicate analyses were 0–30% relative standard deviation (RSD) for PG, 0–20% RSD for COD, 0–10% RSD for TP, and 0–15% for acetate. Acceptable limits for matrix spike recoveries were 70–130% for PG and acetate and 80–120% for COD and TP.

Data Analysis

Detailed calculations used in the analysis, as well as the reporting limits for all compounds, are provided in Supporting Information and are briefly described here. Type I aircraft deicers are designed to be diluted prior to application. All Type I products sampled by MKE personnel for this study had already been diluted by deicer application technicians by an approximate amount of water to meet situational needs for weather conditions. The amount of concentrated Type I product in dilution was estimated using COD results for each product along with known COD of FPDs and known percent FPD of the products from material safety data sheets. Type IV ADAF and PDM products are not diluted prior to application, and these were assumed to be full strength as analyzed. A TP/FPD relationship (ratio of TP concentration to FPD concentration) was then established between the laboratory-reported TP content of each product and the fraction of FPD in each (88% for Type I, 50% for Type IV, 50% for liquid pavement deicer, and 97% for solid pavement deicer).

Contributions of TP from ice control products to observed concentrations in water samples were computed using the TP content in products, concentrations of PG and acetate in surface waters, and ADAF and PDM application inventories from the airport. The product TP content was reported during product analysis by WSLH. The PG and acetate concentrations in surface waters were included in all water quality analyses. These compounds are used to detect and quantify ADAF and PDM in the environment, with PG being the analytical marker for ADAF. Acetate was chosen as the analytical marker for PDM in water samples as it is present in both the liquid and solid products, and other extraneous sources of sodium (primarily road salt) preclude its use as a marker for PDM. Acetate mineralizes completely in the environment and has an estimated biodegradation half-life of 4.6 days.43−46 However, previous work has found that 5-day BOD for the acetate in PDMs decreases with water temperature.4 The travel time from the Primary Outfall site to the Receiving Stream site between November and April is typically between 3.7 and 20 h (computed from the 10th percentile to 90th percentile of daily flow and the travel time formula in Nott et al.)12 —well below the 5-day period used in the previous BOD tests. Given the short travel time and low water temperatures typically observed at this time of year, the degradation of acetate before reaching the Receiving Stream site is expected to be low.

MKE staff provided monthly data describing types and quantities of ADAF used by airlines between 2015–202147 (Table S4 and Figure S1), and this was used to determine masses of PG and TP that were applied each month using the PG and TP content of each formula. Runway deicing records contained the amount of PDM used on an annual basis (Table S5 and Figure S2).

The total mass of FPD applied was calculated by product type. For pavement products, the mass of FPD applied was converted to the mass of acetate applied. The mass of TP applied per FPD (or acetate) was then calculated using the TP/FPD ratio for each product. TP contributions to the watershed from ADAF and PDM sources were then estimated: Total phosphorus per unit of FPD (PG or Acetate) measured in water samples was estimated as a weighted mean value based on the volume of product application (monthly for ADAF, annually for PDM), and this weighted mean TP per FPD value was multiplied by the PG or acetate observed in the sample (see Supporting Information for details). Concentrations of TP, PG, and acetate at or below the reporting limit in the stream samples were reduced to zero when estimating TP contributions from ADAF and PDM sources. Site-specific streamflow data were incorporated for each sampling event to estimate phosphorus loading to the respective streams.

Results and Discussion

Phosphorus Content of Ice Control Products

The TP content of ice control products was highly variable, both between and among product types (Figure 2). Type I ADAF products ranged from 153–807 mg L–1 TP as measured by WSLH using spectrophotometry and ICP-AES. Two of these products were on the high end of this range (TP > 700 mg L–1), and two were on the low end (TP < 200 mg L–1). The Type IV ADAF products generally had lower TP concentrations than Type I products and ranged from 1–216 mg L–1 TP. PDM had a TP content of 805 mg kg–1 and 397 mg L–1 for solid and liquid products, respectively. Overall, the four products with the highest TP content (Type I-1, Type I-3, and both pavement products) are still in active use at MKE as of this writing, while some of the products with lower TP concentrations (Type I-4, Type IV-3b, Type IV-4) are no longer used. The two Type IV products still in use at MKE (Type IV-1 and Type IV-2) have the lowest TP concentrations among all actively used products.

Total phosphorus content of airport deicing and anti-icing products from samples collected at Mitchell International Airport. Total phosphorus in solid pavement deicer (Pav 2b) is reported as mg kg–1, while all other formulations are reported as mg L–1.

The role of phosphorus-containing compounds in these products is not directly disclosed by manufacturers; however, numerous formulations list nonspecific corrosion inhibitors as a constituent, and compounds such as phosphonic acids, orthophosphates, and other phosphorus-based chemicals are used in many industrial applications for this purpose.48−52 The compounds containing phosphorus in ADAF and PDM formulations were not identified for the current research, but additional chemical analysis for this purpose would be valuable to advance the knowledge in future research for the evaluation of potential effects of phosphorus-based compounds on the aquatic ecosystem.

Over the course of the study period, some airlines at MKE changed the ADAF products they were applying, with some being discontinued at this airport, but all 11 products that were analyzed were still commercially available at the time of this writing. Deicer products are selected by each individual airline, and these choices are most likely driven by some combination of market forces, effectiveness of the formula, and familiarity with the product. While the reasoning behind product shifts at MKE is beyond the scope of this investigation, it is unlikely that the TP content of the products was a factor as this issue has not been previously reported and is not widely understood. Additionally, the TP content of products is considered to be proprietary and not disclosed by manufacturers. The variability in TP content observed in ADAF formulations indicates that the ice control product choices of individual airlines could result in different outcomes to watershed TP loading from uncollected ADAF runoff. More work would be needed to determine if there is variability in TP content among different batches for individual formulations. Therefore, the only way for airport personnel to understand how product choices impact the phosphorus budget of an airport would be to test the specific formulations used.

Surface Water Quality

Surface water samples were collected from the sampling sites during 27 weather events: 23 during the deicing season and four during warmer months. Some samples were missed at the Infall (n = 3) and Secondary Outfall (n = 10) sites due to equipment problems or streamflow conditions that were too low during the sampling event. At the Primary Outfall, PG and acetate were reported in 21 of the 23 samples collected during the deicing season (91%) (Figures S3 and S4), indicating the presence of ADAF and PDM. TP concentrations up to 1.09 mg L–1 (Figure 3), as measured by spectrophotometry, were reported in these water samples. Nineteen of the deicing season samples (83%) at this site exceeded the TP water quality threshold of 0.075 mg L–1 set by the DNR for streams (SI, Figure S7).39 At the Secondary Outfall, 13 samples were collected during deicing season, all of which contained measurable PG (100%), while 10 contained acetate (77%). All 13 of these samples (100%) exceeded the DNR TP threshold for streams, and five of them exceeded the greatest TP concentrations observed at all other locations (Figure 3). At the Tertiary Outfall site, 15 of the 23 deicing season samples contained measurable PG (65%) and 16 contained acetate (70%). TP concentrations at the Tertiary Outfall were generally lower than samples from the Primary and Secondary Outfall. While all Tertiary Outfall samples had TP concentrations less than 0.3 mg L–1 (Figure 3), ten samples (43%) exceeded the DNR TP threshold for streams (SI, Figure S7). Samples from the Receiving Stream site had concentrations similar to the Tertiary Outfall, and 18 of the 23 samples collected during the deicing season had measurable PG (78%), while 11 had measurable acetate (48%). Here, 15 samples (65%) had TP concentrations that exceeded DNR guidelines. The upstream Infall site had TP concentrations mostly in the range of those from the Receiving Stream site. PG was reported in 7 of the 20 deicing season samples (35%), while acetate was reported in only 2 (10%). PG was below the laboratory reporting limit at every site in all four of the off-season samples. Acetate was reported at low concentrations in two samples at the secondary outfall in the four warmer month samples (acetate <14 mg L–1).

ADAF and PDM Contributions to TP Concentrations of Surface Water Samples

Overall, 82 samples were collected at the four downstream sites during the deicing season and 69 of these (84%) contained TP that was attributed to ice control products. The Primary and Secondary Outfall sites are located nearest to and downstream of areas with aircraft operations. These two sites had the greatest phosphorus concentrations attributable to ADAF and PDM sources. The top five ADAF contributions to TP concentrations were all recorded at the Secondary Outfall site, while the Primary and Secondary sites together comprised 17 of the top 20 TP concentrations attributable to PG-based ADAF (Figure 3). The top 20 TP concentrations attributable to PDM were also mainly from samples collected at the Primary and Secondary sites; however, 3 of the top 20 were observed in samples from the Tertiary Outfall. Additionally, for 26 of the 44 samples (59%) collected at the Primary and Secondary Outfall sites, ADAF and PDM were the dominant (>50%) sources contributing to TP concentrations (Figure S6 and Table S7). The Infall site is located upstream of most major airport operations, resulting in relatively low concentrations of TP attributed to ADAF and PDM, with 6 of the 24 samples having contributions from ADAF and only 2 with TP contributions from PDM. Seventeen of the 27 samples collected at the Tertiary Outfall contained TP that was attributable to airport sources. Of these 17 samples, 13 had greater concentrations of TP contributions from PDM than ADAF. A portion of the TP concentrations at the Receiving Stream site was attributed to ADAF and PDM in 18 of 27 samples. ADAF and PDM were the dominant (>50%) sources of phosphorus in eight of these samples.

Total Phosphorus Loading during Sampled Events

More than 50% of TP loading was estimated to be from ADAF and PDM for 19 of 27 monitored events sampled at the Primary Outfall, 6 of 17 events at the Secondary Outfall, and 6 of 27 events at the Receiving Stream site (Figure 4). The TP loading contributions to the receiving stream from the Primary Outfall were greater than that from the Secondary Outfall even though TP concentrations were commonly greater at the Secondary Outfall: the mean TP event load at the Primary Outfall was roughly 100 times greater than that from the Secondary Outfall. This was a result of a smaller drainage area and smaller resulting streamflow volumes from the Secondary Outfall. The event-based streamflow was often two to 3 orders of magnitude lower here than at other sites, and at times, water levels were too low in this underground culvert to collect samples. The TP loads at the upstream Infall site were primarily influenced by sources other than ADAF and PDM and were typically more than an order of magnitude lower than the loadings observed at the Primary Outfall and Receiving Stream. The sampling events at the Receiving Stream site, where ADAF and PDM contributed a substantial proportion of the loading, had low relative streamflow volumes (Figures 4 and S5).

A recent meta-analysis of studies on phosphorus transport in surface waters found the average median concentrations of TP in world rivers to be between 28–85 μg L–1.53 When examining the deicing season samples collected at the four downstream sites, 62% were found to have TP concentrations that exceeded this range, and within this elevated group, 90% had contributions from ice control products. Of the 82 samples that were collected during deicing season at downstream sites, 57 (70%) were found to have TP concentrations that exceed phosphorus aquatic life guidelines for streams, indicating potential for harm to the aquatic ecology at the four sites receiving airport runoff.39 Additionally, a previous lake study found that the maximum risk for the development of HABs occurred when TP concentrations exceed 60 μg L–1, and this threshold was exceeded in 76% of samples from downstream sites during deicing events.54

Hydrologic conditions play a major role in determining phosphorus transport and annual loading, with high flow conditions often accounting for most of the TP transport to receiving water bodies.55,56 This trend is evident in the current study at the Infall and Receiving Stream sites, with higher flow events generally correlating with the largest TP loading (Figures 4 and S5). This pattern, however, is not always the case for the Primary and Secondary Outfalls, likely due to the larger influence of TP presence in airport ice control products. The Infall and Receiving Stream sites, by comparison, have larger portions of their drainage areas transported from urban runoff beyond the airport. Even so, when large runoff events coincide with periods of airport deicing, TP loadings can be substantial: During one of the highest flow events in the study (S143), the estimated TP loading from ice control products at the Primary Outfall was 40 kg of phosphorus. This one event comprised 12% of the permitted annual waste load allocation of phosphorus for this site.57

The variability of TP content in ice control products coupled with variations in application amounts, water runoff volumes from the different nature of winter precipitation events, and variable temperatures among events with respect to the freezing point results in a complex application-to-runoff dynamic.

Spatial Distribution

The Receiving Stream site was located to quantify the sum of influence from the Primary and Secondary Outfall sites flowing west and north from the airport and is therefore situated well to assess the potential for TP from airport ice control operations to impact downstream portions of the watershed. This site is at the mouth of Wilson Park Creek where it joins the Kinnickinnic River, part of the Milwaukee Estuary of Lake Michigan. The result that ADAF and/or PDM components (propylene glycol and acetate) were detected in 18 of the 23 deicing season samples at the Receiving Stream site—with more than half of the TP attributed to ADAF and PDM in 44% of these samples—indicates that airport runoff had a substantial influence on the Wilson Park Creek contributions to the TP loading for the Kinnickinnic River Watershed during deicing periods. Previous work has demonstrated that phosphorus can be transported substantial distances downstream to larger water bodies: Research on phosphorus transport to Lake Erie indicated that during storms, most tributaries transported phosphorus tens of km, and that at one site, 24 km from the river mouth; the authors estimated that 100% of the TP in transit was reaching the lake.58 The Tertiary Outfall site was situated on the south side of the airport, draining to Oak Creek and ultimately to Lake Michigan roughly 9 km downstream. This location had TP contributions from ice control sources in 74% of the samples collected during deicing periods, again indicating that airport ice control products can transport phosphorus to the broader watershed.

Sources of Uncertainty and Future Direction

There are several sources of uncertainty in data and computations leading to the current study results. The exact timing of deicer component runoff from the airport is difficult to predict a-priori. Sampling is conducted to capture major deicer application events, but the timing of runoff to receiving water bodies is dependent on the complex hydrology surrounding the airport and during freezing precipitation and melt periods. To fully characterize runoff from deicer application events, continuous monitoring would be required but was not practical for the current study. Quantifying the amount of ADAF applied is also a challenge: Airlines applied ADAF and reported a monthly inventory. Milwaukee County personnel applied PDM and reported an annual inventory. The proportion of these ice control products was estimated for each monitored event using these numbers, introducing uncertainty. In addition, there is no chemical marker available to differentiate between different ADAF formulations present in samples or to differentiate between sources of acetate in PDM. With variable TP in each formulation, it is currently not possible to precisely determine contributions from each formulation. Chemical analysis of airport ice control products was limited to those used at MKE, which did not include all ADAF and PDM on the market. Additional chemical analysis would be needed to extend this analysis to airports that used additional products. Last, the phosphorus contained in deicers is thought to originate from phosphate-based corrosion inhibitors. Future work on this topic could benefit from a more detailed analysis of phosphorus-containing compounds to provide additional insight into potential ecological effects.

Broader Implications for Other Airports

While this work represents a specific case study airport, the ice control products analyzed in this study and other products on the market have been used throughout the United States and other countries. Findings from the current study indicate a clear contribution of TP to the receiving watersheds, with numerous exceedances of phosphorus water quality benchmarks that were established to protect the aquatic environment. This also indicates potential for broad implications for phosphorus loading to airport-adjacent waterways at other airports that apply these products—any airports that experience freezing conditions during the year could have elevated TP runoff as a result of ice control product application. A study conducted by EPA in 2000 identified 212 airports in the United States that engaged in “significant deicing operations.”59 This same study also reported that after the full implementation of stormwater permit regulations that were proposed at that time, the estimated volume of ADAF that would be discharged to U.S. surface waters would still be at least 64 million liters per year.59 A 2011 review paper by Sulej et al.60 compiled data from 10 different studies covering 17 airports in the United States and Europe. Multiple airports had water quality parameters that indicated the presence of ice control products in the surrounding watershed, including elevated concentrations of COD, BOD, PG, potassium, ethylene glycol, and total organic carbon, suggesting that ADAF and PDM presence in surface waters around airports is not uncommon. Additionally, a market research study published in 2015 identified Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Russia, France, Finland, Norway, Switzerland, China, and Japan as revenue hotspots where demand for aircraft deicers was expected to grow.61 While airport wastewater permits can include components of runoff of ice control products and may even include monitoring of phosphorus runoff, it is not commonly recognized that ADAF and PDM are sources of phosphorus, and the true source of TP in airport runoff may not be accounted for in many situations.
