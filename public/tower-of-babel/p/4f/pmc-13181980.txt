Machine learning-based radiomics analysis of PET imaging for early prediction of concurrent chemoradiotherapy response in stage II–III cervical squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Dinghua Pang, Hong Yang, Shilai Zhang, Wenming Qiu, Weiwei Pu, Ziya Liu, Zhi Yang, Ning Li, Hai Liao, Guoyou Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical squamous cell carcinoma is a major global health burden, with many patients presenting with locally advanced disease requiring concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT). Early assessment of treatment response (TR) is critical for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes; however, conventional imaging parameters provide limited predictive value. This study aimed to develop a PET radiomics model using machine learning (ML) to predict early TR after CCRT in patients with stage II–III locally advanced cervical squamous cell carcinoma (LACSC).MethodsThis retrospective study included 184 patients with LACSC who received CCRT (2018–2021) and underwent pre-treatment 18F-FDG PET/CT within 1 week before CCRT initiation. The patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 128) or an internal test set (n = 56). The ROIs were delineated using ITK-SNAP. Radiomic features were extracted using Pyradiomics and selected using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator. Five ML algorithms (random forest, logistic regression, light gradient boosting machine, extremely randomized trees, and multilayer perceptron) were evaluated in this study. The performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsEight radiomic features were identified. The random forest model performed best, with AUCs of 0.877 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.810–0.943) in the training set and 0.783 (95% CI: 0.648–0.918) in the test set. The sensitivity was 0.833 in both sets, and the specificity was 0.783 (training) and 0.682 (test).ConclusionIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, a pre-treatment 18F-FDG PET radiomics model using random forest showed the best performance among the five evaluated ML algorithms for predicting early CCRT response (test-set AUC = 0.783). External validation is required before clinical implementation.

Background

In women, cervical cancer (CC) is the fourth most prevalent malignancy worldwide and a major cause of cancer-related deaths [1], with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) accounting for nearly 80% of cases [2]. At diagnosis, most patients present with locally advanced disease, defined as International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage IB3–IVA [3, 4]. Although concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) has significantly improved local control and survival in locally advanced CC (LACC), some patients still experience poor survival outcomes owing to resistance to CCRT [5]. Therefore, accurate prediction of CCRT response in patients with LACC is critical for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes.

Imaging is the primary method for assessing the treatment response (TR) in solid tumors [6]. The response of patients with LACC to radiotherapy is associated with their clinical outcomes [7–9]. Patients who demonstrated a favorable response to CCRT at one month exhibited significantly lower rates of tumor recurrence and distant metastasis than non-responders [10]. Previously, some researchers [11, 12] attempted to use tumor imaging features or traditional quantitative parameters on pre-treatment MRI or 18F-FDG PET/CT to predict TR after CCRT; however, the performance was often unsatisfactory. These limitations stem from the variability in parameter measurements caused by human factors, inconsistent cutoff values owing to different quantitative methods and techniques, and the limited amount of data available for visual analysis.

Recently, advances in radiomics technology have introduced new opportunities for tumor diagnosis and therapy. Radiomics can efficiently and accurately extract high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images, such as MRI and 18F-FDG PET/CT, which can then be transformed into mineable data for bioinformatic analyses. This enables comprehensive and detailed characterization of tumor heterogeneity in terms of morphology, structure, metabolism, and other aspects. Machine learning (ML) algorithms play a key role in this process. With their powerful data processing capabilities, ML algorithms can deeply mine and analyze the extensive feature data extracted from radiomics and subsequently construct highly personalized prediction models [13]. Currently, radiomics is widely used in the diagnosis, staging, and prognosis of LACC. However, few studies have focused on predicting treatment efficacy in LACC, and most available studies are based on MRI [14–16]. 18F-FDG PET/CT plays a key role in the diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer. It provides functional information through metabolic imaging. Since the revision of the FIGO staging in 2018, it has become an indispensable tool for the evaluation of lymph node and distant metastases. It not only provides a basis for precise staging but also directly affects the selection of treatment strategies, delineation of radiotherapy target areas, and monitoring of treatment efficacy, thereby significantly promoting individualized and precise treatment of cervical cancer [17].

Accordingly, we aimed to investigate PET radiomics features associated with early CCRT TR in patients with locally advanced cervical SCC (LACSC) and construct a radiomics model combined with an ML algorithm to offer additional reference for optimizing clinical therapeutic decisions.

Methods

Research participants

This study was approved by the Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. KY2023863). The requirement for written informed consent was waived in accordance with the national guidelines for retrospective studies.

We selected 184 patients with cervical cancer (CC) who underwent pre-treatment PET/CT examinations at our hospital (January 2018–December 2021). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) CC biopsy pathologically confirmed as SCC; (2) FIGO stage IB3-IVA; (3) completion of CCRT; (4) no anticancer therapy prior to PET/CT; and (5) Pelvic MRI was performed within 2 weeks before CCRT initiation and again 4 weeks after completion of CCRT for response assessment; pre-treatment 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed within 1 week before CCRT initiation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of other malignant tumors, (2) poor quality PET/CT or MRI images unsuitable for analysis, and (3) incomplete clinicopathological data. Clinical staging for all patients was based on the 2018 FIGO staging system [3]. The 184 patients with CC were aged 34–76 years, with a mean age of 55.51 ± 9.18 years. Based on a 7:3 ratio, patients were randomly assigned to a training (128 cases) or a test set (56 cases) (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy

Patients underwent total pelvic external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) or extended-field radiotherapy to the para-aortic region. Depending on the clinical status, daily doses of 1.8–2.4 Gy were delivered 5 days per week for a total of 50–67.2 Gy. Subsequent high-dose-rate brachytherapy was administered after EBRT, totaling 28–42 Gy in 4–6 fractions once weekly at 7 Gy/fraction. All patients received weekly cisplatin or nedaplatin chemotherapy at a dose of 50 mg/m2.

TR assessment

TR was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (v1.1) [6]. The efficacy of CCRT in patients with LACSC was evaluated four weeks after treatment. Patients with complete disappearance of the lesion were classified as having complete remission (CR). Patients with a ≥ 30% decrease in the maximum lesion diameter were classified as having partial remission (PR). Patients with a ≥ 20% increase in maximum lesion diameter or newly detected metastasis were classified as having disease progression (PD). Patients with lesion changes between PR and PD were classified as having a stable disease (SD). Two radiologists (each with > 5 years of experience) independently reviewed the MRI images before and after treatment and reached a consensus in cases of disagreement. Patients were then categorized into two groups based on TR: (1) sensitive group (CR and PR) and (2) resistant group (SD and PD).

Equipment and methods

All patients underwent scanning using a GE Discovery 710 PET/CT system (GE HealthCare Technologies, Chicago, IL, USA). The 18F-FDG imaging agent was produced and synthesized using a medical cyclotron (HM-10HC, Sumitomo Heavy Engineering Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and an 18F-FDG synthesizer system (F300E-2, Sumitomo Heavy Engineering Co.). The radiochemical purity was > 95%. Patients fasted for a minimum of 6 h before the examination. After confirming a blood glucose concentration below 11.1 mmol/L, 18F-FDG was administered through the elbow vein at 3.70–5.55 MBq/kg of body weight. Systemic imaging was performed 60 min after the injection. The scanning range was extended from the top of the skull to the mid-femur. The CT scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 110 mA; and slice thickness, 3–5 mm, depending on the protocol. PET imaging was performed in the 3D acquisition mode with 7–8 beds (1.5 min/bed). After nonuniform attenuation correction, the ordered subset expectation maximization algorithm was used for image reconstruction and fusion. On the Medex workstation, the primary tumor ROI was semi-automatically segmented on PET images using a 40% SUVmax threshold to derive conventional metabolic parameters. The SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV, and TLG were calculated. These threshold-based ROIs were used only for metabolic parameter analysis; radiomics features were extracted from separate manual ROIs, as described in Sect. 2.5.1.

Radiomics analysis

The radiomics analysis workflow included four components: lesion segmentation, feature extraction and screening, model construction, and evaluation (Fig. 2).

Radiomics workflow diagram

Lesion segmentation

The PET images (DICOM format) were imported into ITK-SNAP (v3.8) [18] for radiomics segmentation. Two nuclear medicine physicians (each with > 5 years of experience) independently performed manual delineation of the primary-tumor ROI for radiomics feature extraction, including the lesion as comprehensively as possible while avoiding necrotic regions and parametrial tissue. In cases of uncertain lesion boundaries, the contour was refined by adjusting the image contrast and reviewing the axial slices at multiple levels to improve delineation accuracy. Interobserver agreement was assessed in 30 randomly selected cases using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Both physicians were blinded to the patient’s clinical data during annotation.

Feature extraction

PyRadiomics, an open-source tool, was used to configure the image parameters and extract the radiomic features following the Initiative for Standardization of Imaging Biomarkers [19]. The PET images were preprocessed, and the voxel spacing in the X-, Y-, and Z-directions was resampled to 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm. Absolute intensity resampling was set to 0–25 SUV, and intensity discretization was set to 0.25 bin width. Eight filters were applied: Gaussian (LoG), wavelet transform (Wavelet), square (Square), square root (Square Root), logarithm (Logarithm), exponential (Exponential), gradient (Gradient), and three-dimensional local binary patterns (LBP3D). These filters were applied to each patient’s image. The features extracted from the original and filtered images included first-order, shape, gray-level size zone matrix/gray-level run length matrix (GLSZM/GLRLM), neighborhood gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM), and gray-level co-occurrence matrix/gray-level dependence matrix (GLCM/GLDM).

Feature screening

To prevent model overfitting, we adopted a four-step process for feature screening. First, to evaluate the impact of image segmentation on radiomics feature stability, the ICC was used to assess the radiomics features extracted from ROIs delineated by the two physicians. The ICC values ranged from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater interobserver reproducibility. An ICC ≥ 0.75 indicated good reproducibility, and only features meeting this threshold were retained for subsequent analyses. After preliminary screening with the ICC, feature standardization was performed using the Z-score method to ensure a consistent feature magnitude. Second, Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney U test were used to assess significant differences between CCRT-sensitive and CCRT-resistant patients. Furthermore, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to calculate the correlations among the radiomics features. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was applied to retain one feature when the correlation coefficient between any two features exceeded 0.9. LASSO regularization with 10-fold cross-validation (lambda = 0.01) was used to select the optimal features.

Model construction and evaluation

To construct a risk model, the selected optimal predictive features were input into five ML algorithms: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Extremely Randomized Trees (ERT), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). To address the class imbalance, SMOTE was applied to the training set only during model development to balance the CCRT-sensitive and CCRT-resistant classes. Finally, the ROC curve, AUCs, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, F1 value, and Brier score were used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model.

Statistical method

IBM SPSS (v26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R software (v4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Onekey AI One-Stop Intelligent Research Platform (https://www.medai.icu) were used for statistical analyses. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and the independent samples t-test was used for group comparisons. Non-normally distributed data are presented as medians (interquartile range), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison between groups. Enumeration data are reported as n (%), and group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The DeLong test was applied to compare the AUC differences between the models. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Prediction performance of five ML models in training and test sets

The models were ranked in descending order of AUC values as follows: RF, LightGBM, ERT, MLP, and LR in the training set, and RF, LR, LightGBM, ERT, and MLP in the test set. The DeLong test showed no significant differences in the AUC between the training and test sets for any of the five models (LR, RF, ERT, LightGBM, and MLP) (P > 0.05). In the training set, the AUC differences between the LR, ERT, MLP, and RF models were significant (P < 0.05). In the test set, no significant AUC differences were found between RF and LightGBM, ERT, MLP, or LR (P > 0.05; Fig. 4). A comprehensive evaluation showed that RF exhibited the best classification performance. In the training and test sets, the AUC values were 0.877 (95% CI: 0.810–0.943) and 0.783 (95% CI: 0.648–0.918), respectively; sensitivities were 0.833 and 0.833; specificities were 0.783 and 0.682; positive predictive values were 0.600 and 0.417; negative predictive values were 0.923 and 0.937; F1 values were 0.698 and 0.556, and Brier scores were 0.186 and 0.153, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, eight radiomics features related to CCRT efficacy in patients with LACSC were extracted from PET images and input into five different ML algorithms to construct the models. In the internal test set, model discrimination was moderate, with the random forest model showing the best overall performance (AUC 0.783; sensitivity 0.833; specificity 0.682; Table 4) and an acceptable calibration (Brier score 0.153). These results should be considered preliminary and require external validation before being used clinically.

Many researchers have devoted efforts to extracting quantitative parameters from PET images and exploring the correlations between these imaging parameters and CCRT-TR. Min et al. [11] showed that the pre-treatment SUVmax can help predict early response to CCRT in CC. Moreover, MTV and TLG before treatment were predictors of TR [20]. However, although these findings provide valuable insights for predicting LACC-TR, it should be noted that the research cohorts were small, which greatly limits the generalizability and reliability of the results and hinders their broader application in clinical practice. In our cohort, conventional PET metabolic parameters (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV, and TLG) did not differ significantly between the CCRT-sensitive and CCRT-resistant groups (Table 2). This indicates that PET/CT metabolic parameters have limited value in predicting CCRT response in patients with CC. Thus, potential new imaging features or biomarkers should be explored further for enhancing the prediction accuracy and reliability.

As a noninvasive technique, radiomics can extract quantitative features from medical imaging and reflect tumor cell heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment [21]. According to Shen et al. [22], the short-zone emphasis from the 18F-FDG PET/CT gray-scale region matrix (GLSZM) was statistically significant between SCC and non-SCC. Mu et al. [23] explored the value of texture features from 18F-FDG PET images in CC staging. The results showed that run percentage was a highly stable feature that could accurately distinguish early (I–II) and advanced (III–IV) stages of CC. This study focused on patients receiving CCRT and explored the interactions between 18F-FDG PET-related features and TR (Table 3). The results showed that among the selected features, the three first-order statistical features, three GLCM features, and two GLSZM features were closely related to the early TR of patients with LACSC after CCRT, particularly the wavelet_HLH_glcm_Idn feature, which had the most significant influence on the output of the RF model. At present, the correlation mechanism between PET radiomics features and TR of LACC requires further exploration. Cepero et al. [24] used longitudinal FDG-PET radiomics analysis and found that radiomics features extracted in the middle of treatment (rather than before treatment) can more accurately predict the TR of CCRT. This finding suggests that dynamic changes during treatment may better reflect the true response of tumors to treatment than baseline characteristics. In addition, the differences between different research results may also be due to the methodological challenges of radiomics analysis, including the standardization of image acquisition, repeatability of feature extraction, and strictness of model verification [25, 26]. Therefore, future research should adopt standardized image acquisition protocols and feature extraction methods and verify them in a multicenter cohort to ensure the reliability and clinical applicability of the results.

The ML algorithm is a powerful method for processing and analyzing high-dimensional image data. Combining this with radiomics features may provide additional evidence for individualized diagnosis and treatment in patients with cancer. To evaluate the impact of algorithm choice, we compared five commonly used classifiers (LR, RF, ERT, LightGBM, and MLP) using the same selected PET radiomics features. In our internal test set, the RF model achieved the highest AUC and a balanced sensitivity/specificity profile (Table 4). RF is an ensemble learning technology that relies on supervised learning and works by constructing multiple decision trees, where each tree is trained individually using a bootstrapping algorithm (bootstrapping), and the final classification decision is defined by summarizing the voting results of each decision tree. This method can effectively minimize processing complexity and shorten training time while improving the model’s generalization performance, avoiding overfitting, and demonstrating strong resistance to noise [27]. However, its ‘black box’ characteristics limit its clinical interpretability [28]. Although we attempted to enhance the transparency of the model through feature importance analysis, the internal decision-making mechanism of random forests is still relatively complex, and it is difficult to provide an intuitive clinical interpretation, which may affect its acceptance and application in clinical practice.

Multiple limitations should be considered, including the single-center retrospective nature of the study and the relatively small sample size. In addition, this study used an internal training–test split without external validation or nested/repeated cross-validation; thus, the reported performance may be optimistic. The class distribution was imbalanced (CCRT-sensitive vs. CCRT-resistant), although SMOTE was applied during model training. Treatment response was assessed using MRI-based RECIST 1.1 at 4 weeks after treatment, which is clinically relevant for early evaluation but is not a validated surrogate for progression-free or overall survival, and no PD cases were observed in our cohort. In the future, we plan to collect more data for independent testing through multicenter, large-scale prospective studies using different scanning devices to further evaluate and adjust our models. Second, the traditional method of manually delineating the region of interest used in this study is both time-consuming and labor-intensive and may introduce interobserver variability despite ICC-based feature screening. Therefore, it is not suitable for large-scale data processing. In the future, we plan to explore the use of deep learning techniques to enable automatic segmentation and extend the application of artificial intelligence in cervical cancer research. Finally, this study included only single-modality radiomic features. In the future, radiomics research involving fusion images such as PET/CT or PET/MRI will be necessary to fully understand the complementary role of multimodal imaging.

Conclusion

The RF model utilizing pre-treatment PET radiomics features shows potential in differentiating between CCRT-sensitive and CCRT-resistant groups, providing a basis for early individualized treatment planning in patients with LACSC.
