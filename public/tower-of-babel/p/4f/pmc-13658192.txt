Multiscale structural connectome eigenmodes constrain human brain functional dynamics
Authors: Jie Xia, Siqi Yang, Jinpeng Niu, Xinyue Huang, Jiao Li, Huafu Chen, Fang Zeng, Wei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human brain is an intricate network structurally organized across multiple scales. Multiscale structural architecture arises from complex biological mechanisms and provides the anatomical substrate for functional interactions. However, the extent to which the multiscale structural connectome constrains functional activity remains unclear. Here, we investigate the structure-function relationship by constructing an in vivo multiscale structural connectome that integrates white matter tractography, microstructural similarity, and cortico-cortical proximity. Multiscale structural connectome eigenmodes outperform conventional approximations of structural connectivity in capturing spontaneous and task-evoked functional activity. Moreover, multiscale structure-function decoupling reveals an organizational axis spanning from coupled unimodal sensory to decoupled transmodal association cortices, recapitulating microstructure and macroscale functional hierarchies. These decoupled patterns spatially align with opioid neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase, and colocalize with transcriptomic signatures enriched in synaptic structure and signaling regulation. Collectively, our findings highlight the pivotal role of multiscale structural wiring in shaping brain functional dynamics and provide novel neurobiological insights into the structure-function relationship.Subject terms: Network models, Biophysical models

Introduction

The human brain is an intricate network structurally organized across multiple scales1–3, ranging from microscale interactions among neuronal populations to macroscale interconnected networks connecting distant brain regions4. These neuroanatomical connections constitute a complex structural architecture that serves as the fundamental substrate for neural communication and information processing5,6, supporting brain function and diverse cognitive behaviors7,8. Deciphering how neuroanatomical architecture shapes functional dynamics across different scales remains a central challenge in neuroscience9,10.

The brain’s neuroanatomical scaffold intrinsically constrains the spatiotemporal patterns of functional dynamics11–13. Although structural and functional connectivity are closely interrelated, their relationship extends beyond a direct one-to-one mapping9,14. High-order interactions and polysynaptic communication among distributed neuronal populations may lead to intricate and imperfect links between structure and function15,16. Graph signal processing has emerged as an innovative approach for exploring these high-order structure–function interactions17–19. Connectome eigenmodes, derived from structural connectivity reconstructed using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) tractography17,20, provide a powerful formalism for bridging the brain’s anatomy with functional activity21–23. Spatiotemporal neural activity is decomposed into structurally-informed components, revealing the extent to which neural activity deviates from the underlying anatomical scaffold24,25. Moreover, the structural-decoupling index has been proposed to assess the regional coupling strength between structure and function26, recapitulating the canonical “sensorimotor-association” hierarchy27,28. Structure–function coupling holds promise for brain-state decoding29 and detecting disorder-related perturbations30–32. However, given the inherent limitations of dMRI tractography in mapping structural connectivity in living humans, particularly within intracortical axonal collaterals and superficial white matter fibers33–35, the degree to which the neuroanatomical connectome constrains functional dynamics remains poorly understood.

The multiscale structural connectome (MSC) represents a significant advancement in characterizing neural organization across multiple biological scales36–38. Recently, the MSC model was proposed by integrating complementary structural wiring features36, incorporating both microscale structural information from intracortical microstructural profile covariance (MPC) and macroscale properties derived from dMRI tractography strength (TS) and cortico-cortical geodesic distance (GD). Diffusion MRI tractography effectively detects deep white matter fibers. The GD enables estimation of short-range connectivity within the gray matter and superficial white matter by quantifying cortical spatial proximity, thereby serving as a proxy for intrinsic wiring cost39–41. Furthermore, microstructural similarity strongly correlates with inter-regional connection probability in non-human animals42,43, consistent with the classical “structural model” of cytoarchitecture-dependent similarity44,45. The MPC quantifies this microstructural similarity in vivo by comparing myelin-sensitive imaging profiles across cortical depths46. Notably, by integrating these complementary features, the MSC provides a comprehensive representation of the brain’s structural wiring diagram, capturing cellular cytoarchitecture, cell-type-specific gene expression, and functional interactions36,37. Thus, this advanced structural wiring provides a powerful framework for probing how multiscale structural organization constrains functional dynamics.

In this study, we aimed to decipher the complex relationship between the MSC and functional activity in humans. First, we derived frequency-specific brain eigenmodes of the MSC by applying Laplacian decomposition to its graph representation. Second, we showed the superiority of MSC eigenmodes for capturing spontaneous and task-evoked cortical activity acquired from functional MRI (fMRI) data. Third, we developed the multiscale structure–function decoupling (MSFD) index to measure the degree of local (de)coupling between functional activity and the underlying multiscale structural wiring. Fourth, we assessed the spatial relationships between the regional MSFD patterns and hierarchical organization across both microstructural and macroscale functional levels. Finally, we explored the molecular fingerprints underpinning the MSFD pattern by integrating microscale data on neurotransmitter receptors, mitochondrial features, and gene transcriptomic profiles.

Results

Multiscale structural connectome (MSC) eigenmodes

Structural wiring was derived from a “discovery” cohort of 100 unrelated healthy subjects in the Human Connectome Project (HCP)47, which provides high-resolution T1-weighted (T1w), diffusion-weighted, and T2-weighted (T2w) MRI images. Using these datasets, we constructed an in vivo multiscale structural wiring model36–38 by integrating three complementary structural features (TS, GD, and MPC) based on the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas48 (Fig. 1a). Here, “multiscale” refers to the integration of structural features spanning multiple biological scales—from microstructural to macrostructural, rather than to multiple spatial resolutions in the parcellation scheme. To integrate these features into a fused structural network, the interregional matrices were normalized and concatenated horizontally. A similarity matrix was then constructed using row-wise normalized angle similarity. The resulting fused MSC quantified the strength of cortical wiring between region pairs, thereby capturing shared and complementary contributions from distinct structural modalities36,37.

The MSC eigenmodes, also called eigenvectors, were obtained by performing an eigen-decomposition of the normalized graph Laplacian matrix, forming a series of orthogonal basis sets. Each eigenmode exhibited smoothly varying cortical patterns spanning positive to negative polarities17,25. The corresponding eigenvalues quantified the spatial smoothness of each eigenmode and could be interpreted as graph frequencies. These MSC eigenmodes were ordered in ascending order according to their corresponding eigenvalues (or frequencies). Low-frequency eigenmodes captured global spatial gradients across the neocortex, varying along principal anatomical axes, such as anterior–posterior, left–right, ventral–dorsal, and medial–lateral (Fig. 1b). In contrast, high-frequency eigenmodes represented irregular and localized patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1).

MSC eigenmodes constrain human brain functional activity

We investigated the extent to which MSC eigenmodes explain various aspects of human brain functional activity. Spatiotemporal cortical activity was decomposed into a weighted linear combination of orthogonal MSC eigenmodes. Based on this decomposition, we assessed the reconstruction accuracy of MSC eigenmodes in explaining spontaneous cortical activity and task-evoked activation maps derived from fMRI data in 100 unrelated healthy adults from the HCP, gradually increasing the number of eigenmodes from 1 to 20011,19.

We found that reconstruction accuracy continuously improved with the number of included MSC eigenmodes for both spontaneous and task-evoked conditions (Fig. 2). Notably, a substantial proportion of the reconstruction performance was achieved with the first 50 eigenmodes, indicating that functional activity patterns were predominantly composed of low-frequency eigenmodes. The first 200 MSC eigenmodes attained 87.3% reconstruction accuracy of activity concentration, significantly outperforming reconstructions based on rewired MSC connectomes49,50 (Prewired < 0.001, false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected; Fig. 2a), noise-fused networks combining the TS matrix with rewired GD and MPC connectomes (Prewired < 0.001, FDR-corrected; Supplementary Fig. 2a), and spatial permutation-based surrogate cortical activity maps51,52 (Pspin < 0.001, FDR-corrected; Fig. 2a). Similarly, the first 200 MSC eigenmodes achieved 83.3% reconstruction accuracy of intrinsic functional connectivity (Prewired/spin < 0.001, FDR-corrected; Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, these results were consistent across all 47 task-evoked activation patterns (Supplementary Table 1), with the first 200 MSC eigenmodes yielding a mean reconstruction accuracy of 90.7% (Prewired/spin < 0.001, FDR-corrected; Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Collectively, these results indicate that MSC eigenmodes provide a compact, biologically meaningful representation that captures both spontaneous neural activity and diverse task-evoked activation patterns.

We next examined the contribution of distinct structural wiring features to shape human brain functional dynamics. We found that TS made the strongest contribution to the reconstruction of functional dynamics across all task contrasts and in the resting state, followed by GD, while MPC contributed relatively less (Supplementary Fig. 4). All three structural features provided non-redundant information, and their integration yielded improved reconstruction performance, suggesting that functional dynamics arise from the complementary interplay of multiple structural features rather than any single feature alone.

We further evaluated the reconstruction performance of MSC eigenmodes relative to those derived from individual structural wiring features and their alternative combinations. Differences in reconstruction accuracy were assessed using a nonparametric paired test53. Spatial comparisons revealed that low-frequency MSC eigenmodes were more similar to those derived from other networks, whereas high-frequency eigenmodes were less similar (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, MSC eigenmodes consistently achieved significantly higher accuracy than those derived from alternative combinations or subsets of structural wiring features in reconstructing human brain functional dynamics. This superior performance was observed for resting-state activity concentration, intrinsic functional connectivity, and task-evoked activation patterns (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7; all P < 0.05, FDR-corrected). Together, these findings suggest that integrating structural wiring features across multiple scales parsimoniously captures fundamental aspects of human functional dynamics.

Hierarchical organization of multiscale structure–function decoupling (MSFD)

To investigate regional variations in multiscale structure–function relationships, we introduced the MSFD index (Fig. 1d). Specifically, MSC eigenmodes were divided into low- and high-frequency eigenmodes using a median-split of the energy spectral density (ESD) from spontaneous functional activity26, with the cut-off frequency set to C = 87. These eigenmodes were then used to decompose functional activity at each temporal frame into low- and high-frequency components24. Low- and high-frequency components revealed the extent to which functional activity is strongly or weakly constrained by the underlying anatomical architecture24,25,54. To quantify MSFD for each brain region, we calculated the binary logarithm of the ratio of high- to low-frequency component norms over the temporal course23,26. The MSFD index reflected the degree of (de)coupling between functional activity and the underlying MSC. The term coupling denoted the dependency of functional activity on the structural wiring as measured by activity smoothness on the MSC graph. Lower MSFD values indicated that functional activity is strongly coupled to multiscale structural wiring, whereas higher MSFD implied the reverse. Regional MSFD values were statistically evaluated using nonparametric permutation testing to localize areas where MSFD significantly deviated from null distributions generated by graph spectral randomization25,26.

We found that the spatial distribution of MSFD was regionally heterogeneous and varied continuously across the neocortex (Fig. 3a, left panel). Regional MSFD revealed a hierarchical axis spanning from tightly coupled primary sensorimotor to decoupled higher-order association cortices. Brain regions exhibiting significant coupling were primarily concentrated in the occipital and parietal lobes, superior temporal gyrus, and motor and premotor cortices (Fig. 3a, middle panel). In contrast, brain regions with significant decoupling were predominantly located in the frontal pole, posterior cingulate, insular, and orbitofrontal and medial temporal cortices (Fig. 3a, right panel).

We further examined whether the MSFD pattern recapitulates the macroscale layout of functional systems and laminar attributes. Regional MSFD values were assigned to the Yeo’s intrinsic functional networks55 (Fig. 3b), and Mesulam’s laminar differentiation classes56 (Fig. 3c). Multiscale structure–function coupling was predominantly observed in primary unimodal cortices, encompassing the visual and somatomotor networks, and idiotypic class. In contrast, MSFD was pronounced in transmodal cortices affiliated with the frontoparietal network, default mode network, and limbic/paralimbic systems. Collectively, these findings indicate that multiscale structure–function relationships are gradually decoupled along the hierarchical axis from unimodal to transmodal cortices.

We performed sensitivity analyses to examine the impact of filter cut-off parameters on regional MSFD patterns. We found that the MSFD patterns were highly consistent across different filter cut-off settings (Supplementary Fig. 8), with spatial correlation coefficients ranging from 0.886 to 0.996 (mean ± SD = 0.955 ± 0.038; all Pspin < 0.0001). Furthermore, we assessed reproducibility using an internal dataset (HCP REST-2) and an independent dataset from the Microstructural Information Connectome of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MICA-MICs)57. Regional MSFD patterns were robustly replicated in both internal validation (ρ = 0.99, Pspin < 0.001) and external validation (ρ = 0.66, Pspin < 0.001) analyses (Supplementary Fig. 9). Together, these findings confirm the sensitivity and reproducibility of the MSFD patterns across parameter settings and datasets.

Regional MSFD reflects microstructure and macroscale functional hierarchies

To understand the potential biological implications of regional MSFD patterns, we examined its spatial concordance with various neurobiological attributes. At the microscale level, we investigated whether regional variability in MSFD aligns with the histological cytoarchitectural hierarchy. Microstructural intensity profiles derived from the BigBrain post-mortem human histology were used to compute the cytoarchitectural similarity gradient58. The MSFD pattern was positively associated with BigBrain-derived histological cytoarchitectural gradient46 (Fig. 4a; ρ = 0.51, Pspin = 0.02), which reflects spatial trends in laminar differentiation and cytoarchitectural complexity laminar46,59.

At the macroscale level, we assessed whether regional variability in MSFD aligns with a spectrum of increasingly abstract cognitive functions. Regional MSFD was significantly correlated with the first principal component of NeuroSynth cognitive function60 (Fig. 4b; ρ = 0.47, Pspin = 0.02), linking structurally coupled regions to visual, auditory, and multisensory processing. In contrast, structurally decoupled regions were associated with complex cognition functions, including affective processing, emotion, and reward decision-making (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, we examined whether regional MSFD follows a macroscale functional hierarchy by assessing its association with the intrinsic functional connectivity gradient. Regional MSFD showed a significant association with the principal functional gradient28 (Fig. 4c; ρ = 0.43, Pspin = 0.003), indicating stronger coupling in unimodal sensory cortices and greater decoupling in transmodal association cortices at the apex of the functional hierarchy. Although the spatial patterns of MSC-derived MSFD resembled previously reported TS-derived SFD26 (Supplementary Fig. 11), MSC-derived MSFD showed stronger associations with microstructure and macroscale functional hierarchies (Supplementary Fig. 12). Collectively, these results suggest that the spatial layout of MSFD is systematically aligned with hierarchical organization across both micro- and macro-scale levels.

Associations with neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial profiles

Neurotransmitter receptors regulate synaptic plasticity, shape functional dynamics, and ultimately facilitate brain-wide communication61. However, whether the spatial distributions of specific neurotransmitter receptors contribute to the observed MSFD pattern remains unclear. To this end, we applied a multilinear regression model to assess associations between regional MSFD patterns and nine neurotransmitter systems61, including acetylcholine, cannabinoid, dopamine, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), glutamate, histamine, norepinephrine, opioid, and serotonin. This model explained 51.47% of the variance in the MSFD pattern (F(9, 350) = 41.24, Pspin < 0.001). Regional MSFD was positively associated with neurotransmitter systems (Fig. 5a; ρ = 0.69, Pspin < 0.001). Notably, MSC-derived MSFD explained more variance and exhibited stronger spatial associations with neurotransmitter systems than TS-derived SFD (Supplementary Fig. 13). Dominance analysis further indicated that the opioid (PFDR < 0.001), norepinephrine (PFDR < 0.001), GABA (PFDR = 0.002), and histamine (PFDR = 0.012) systems significantly contributed to the model’s overall fit, with the opioid neurotransmitter system accounting for the largest dominance (27.55%; Supplementary Table 2). These results remained robust after regressing out the principal functional gradient from both the MSFD map and each neurotransmitter map prior to model fitting, indicating that the observed correlations were not driven by the cortical hierarchy (Supplementary Fig. 14a and Supplementary Table 3).

Mitochondrial phenotypes play an indispensable role in the molecular energetic landscape that sustains physiological functional activity62. To assess their contribution to the MSFD pattern, we constructed a multilinear regression model relating regional MSFD to six mitochondrial features62, comprising NADH–ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I), succinate dehydrogenase (complex II), cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV), mitochondrial density (MitoD), mitochondrial respiratory capacity (MRC), and tissue respiratory capacity (TRC). This model explained 35.86% of the variance in the MSFD pattern (F(6, 353) = 32.9, Pspin < 0.001). Regional MSFD was significantly correlated with mitochondrial features (Fig. 5b; ρ = 0.58, Pspin < 0.001). MSC-derived MSFD explained more variance and showed greater spatial associations with mitochondrial features than TS-derived SFD (Supplementary Fig. 13). Dominance analysis further identified mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase (PFDR < 0.001) as the most significant contributor, accounting for 44.21% of the relative contribution (Supplementary Table 4). Consistent results were found after controlling for the principal functional gradient (Supplementary Fig. 14b and Supplementary Table 5). Overall, these findings suggest that both neurotransmitter systems and mitochondrial phenotypes serve as potential molecular substrates of the MSFD pattern.

Transcriptomic decoding of the MSFD pattern

Gene expression fundamentally governs the brain’s synaptic wiring and neuronal activity63. To examine the relationship between regional MSFD and gene expression, we utilized partial least squares (PLS) analysis to relate the MSFD pattern and transcriptional signatures of 8653 genes obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA)64. The first component (PLS1) captured the optimally weighted combinations of gene expression levels associated with regional MSFD pattern, which explained 50.25% of the variance (Pspin < 0.001; Fig. 6a). Regional MSFD was correlated with the spatial layout of PLS1 weighted gene expression (ρ = 0.68, Pspin < 0.001; Fig. 6b). MSC-derived MSFD explained more variance and exhibited stronger spatial associations with gene transcriptional signatures than TS-derived SFD (Supplementary Fig. 15). The PLS1 weighted map reflected a sensorimotor-association gradient, indicating that regional MSFD variation aligns with transcriptional architecture of the human brain.

To further elucidate the molecular biological signatures underlying the MSFD pattern, we ranked genes by their normalized PLS1 weights and then assessed their significance65,66 (Fig. 6c). Genes with significantly positive or negative PLS1 weights (|Z| > 5, PFDR < 0.001) were subjected separately to gene enrichment analysis using the Metascape toolbox67. Negatively weighted genes were predominantly enriched in biological processes associated with synaptic structure and signaling regulation (Fig. 6d), such as “postsynapse,” “transmembrane transporter activity,” “dendrite,” and “kinase activity” (all PFDR < 0.05). These genes were expressed specifically in excitatory (Ex4, n = 107, PFDR < 0.05) and inhibitory neurons (In6b, n = 84, PFDR < 0.05; Fig. 6e). Additionally, positively weighted genes were primarily enriched in synaptic organization and metabolic function (Supplementary Fig. 16), such as “postsynapse,” “presynapse,” “oxidoreductase activity,” and “regulation of trans-synaptic signaling” (all PFDR < 0.05).

Discussion

By integrating multiple representations of structural wiring, we investigated how MSC constrains functional dynamics in the human brain. We showed that MSC eigenmodes better explain functional dynamics than conventional approximations of structural connectivity. Furthermore, the multiscale structure–function relationships exhibited a graded decoupling along a hierarchical axis from primary sensorimotor to transmodal association cortices, resembling microstructure and macroscale functional hierarchies. The decoupled patterns were spatially correlated with the distributions of opioid neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase, and colocalized with transcriptomic signatures enriched in synaptic structure and signaling regulation. Collectively, these findings emphasize the crucial role of multiscale structural wiring as the anatomical scaffold that shapes human brain function.

Extending previous approaches that examined structure–function relationships at a single scale12,13,15, we applied eigenmode decomposition to reveal how multiscale structural organization shapes the brain’s functional dynamics. Eigenmodes offer a novel frequency-specific framework for linking neuroanatomical architecture with spatiotemporal patterns of functional activity17–19. Previous studies indicate that connectome eigenmodes, derived from structural connectivity mapped using dMRI tractography, account for local gray matter and long-range white matter axonal connections, which collectively underlie the intrinsic functional networks in humans17,20. Similarly, cellular connectome eigenmodes derived from neuronal tracing connectome reflect projection directionality and edge density, providing a fundamental description of spontaneous cortical activity in marmosets23. Moreover, geometric eigenmodes derived from cortical shape capture local spatial relations and curvature, highlighting the critical role of geometry in constraining neural dynamics11. In our work, we adopted a similar framework but focused on distinct anatomical features. Given that human brain networks are coordinated across multiple spatial scales1–3, a comprehensive understanding of how structural organizations constrain functional dynamics requires mapping multiple levels of brain organization in vivo36–38.

The core of our study was an advanced MSC model that combines multiple comprehensive dimensions of structural wiring36,37, including dMRI tractography strength, spatial proximity, and microstructural similarity. Each dimension captures distinct aspects of brain organization, consistent with structural wiring principles established in classical neuroanatomy studies36,37. By synergistically integrating these features, the MSC may provide a comprehensive representation of the structural connectome, enabling systematic investigation of the anatomical constraints on brain function. We decomposed the MSC network into orthogonal eigenmodes, forming a complete basis for representing spatiotemporal functional activity patterns. Extensive comparisons between fused MSC eigenmodes and those derived from conventional structural features showed the superior performance of MSC eigenmodes in reconstructing spontaneous and task-evoked functional activity. Spatial proximity and microstructural similarity emerged as key contributors to these reconstructions, underscoring the vital role of intracortical and cytoarchitectonic connections in shaping macroscale functional dynamics6,36. These results indicate that the structural organization across multiple scales collectively shapes diverse aspects of human neocortical activity. Therefore, MSC eigenmodes may serve as fundamental building blocks for describing spatiotemporal functional activity, promoting the establishment of correspondences between brain function and multiscale structural wiring.

We provided an important perspective on how multiscale structure–function relationships vary across different regions. Previous studies have employed linear correlation analysis37,38 and supervised machine learning36 to evaluate local or global correspondences between multiscale structural wiring and functional connectivity. However, functional activity correlations computed over time periods captured relatively limited information24. In contrast, our work utilized the topographic organization of the MSC to decompose functional activity, thereby potentially enhancing the statistical properties of activity fluctuations22. Critically, we developed the MSFD index to measure the coupling strength between functional activity and multiscale structural wiring. Our analyses revealed that the spatial layout of MSFD was nonuniform and varied continuously across the cortical mantle. Consistent with numerous studies10,13,26,68, regional MSFD patterns recapitulated a hierarchical axis, ranging from tightly coupled unimodal sensorimotor regions to decoupled transmodal association cortices. Specifically, the tight coupling in the sensorimotor cortices, including the occipital and parietal lobes, superior temporal gyrus, and motor cortices, suggests that functional communication is directly supported by multiscale structural wiring, likely ensuring rapid and accurate responses to external stimuli68. In contrast, the strong decoupling in the transmodal cortices, including the prefrontal pole, posterior cingulate, insular, and temporal cortices, indicates that functional processing is untethered by structural constraints, potentially supporting functional flexibility and diverse functional roles68. Hence, these results advance our understanding of the relationships between multiscale structural wiring and large-scale brain function.

Cortical hierarchy constitutes a fundamental principle for interpreting the multiscale organization of structure and function in primate brains27,28. Our findings of regional MSFD variability were consistent with recent studies that reported a similar organization axis spanning microstructural and functional hierarchies28,46,69. At the microscale level, the spatial layout of MSFD was closely aligned with the histological microstructure similarity gradient46, highlighting the role of cortical cytoarchitecture in shaping structure–function interaction. Greater dissociations between microstructural and functional gradients were exhibited in transmodal cortices, suggesting that cytoarchitectonic and laminar differentiation are critical determinants of neural dynamics46. At the macroscale level, regional heterogeneity in MSFD was associated with macroscale functional hierarchies, including cognitive function and functional connectivity gradients28,60. Regional MSFD patterns may reflect functional specialization along a cognitive representational hierarchy, ranging from low-level sensory processing (visual, auditory, and motion) to higher-order cognitive functions (affective processing, emotion, and reward decision-making)10,26. Collectively, these results suggest that cortical hierarchies are closely linked to how functional dynamics are constrained by multiscale structural architecture.

Advanced whole-brain maps of neurotransmitters and mitochondria provide novel avenues to characterize the molecular fingerprints associated with the MSFD pattern70. Neurotransmitter receptors regulate synaptic plasticity, modulate neural dynamics, and ultimately coordinate the communication of functional information61,71. We found that regional MSFD patterns are spatially aligned with distributions of neurotransmitter receptors, indicating that chemoarchitecture may play a crucial role in structure–function integration61,72. Notably, regional variability in MSFD was significantly associated with the spatial arrangement of multiple neurotransmitter systems, including GABA, histamine, norepinephrine, and opioids. GABA mediates rapid synaptic inhibition across neural circuits, enhancing the efficiency of information processing73. Histamine contributes to diverse signaling processes and flexible neuronal communication74. Norepinephrine broadly affects arousal, alertness, and pain sensation, facilitating the reconfiguration of brain functional dynamics75. In addition, opioids are related to brain structural and functional abnormalities in regions implicated in affect regulation, impulse control, and reward processing76. The simultaneous release of these neurotransmitters may enhance synaptic flexibility and support complex behaviors and cognition. Furthermore, mitochondria are specialized to support distinct cellular demands, orchestrate subcellular activities, and supply the energy necessary for large-scale functional communications62. We found a strong correspondence between regional MSFD and mitochondrial distributions, suggesting that molecular energetic landscapes are closely associated with structure–function interactions. Notably, mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase (complex II) emerged as a key contributor to regional MSFD variability. Succinate dehydrogenase transfers electrons from succinate to the respiratory chain ubiquinone pool, generating energy that supports neuronal metabolism, excitability, and brain homeostasis77. Together, these findings highlight neurotransmitter receptor and mitochondrial signatures as potential molecular substrates associated with multiscale structure–function decoupling.

Neuroimaging-transcription association studies provide a critical opportunity to bridge micro-level transcriptome profiles with macroscale regional variability in MSFD65,66. Our transcriptome analysis revealed that regional heterogeneity in MSFD was colocalized with the gene transcriptional profiles enriched in synaptic structure, signaling regulation, and metabolic function, such as “postsynapse,” “presynapse,” “transmembrane transporter activity,” “kinase activity,” “oxidoreductase activity,” and “regulation of trans-synaptic signaling.” Synapses underlie the anatomical foundation of neuronal communication within the central nervous system. Synaptic transmission relies on complex structural specializations in pre- and postsynaptic cells, while their dynamic architecture enables flexible regulation of synaptic signaling78. Metabolic function is critical for maintaining structural integrity and diverse functional demands. Notably, the emergence of higher cognitive functions in humans is associated with gene expression involved in energy metabolism79. Thus, these results enhance our understanding of the potential transcriptomic mechanisms underlying the links between structure and function in humans.

Several methodological limitations merit further consideration. First, accurately mapping white matter connections with dMRI tractography remains challenging, as the method is susceptible to false positives and negatives, underestimates fiber length, and struggles to resolve crossing fibers33–35. Second, previous studies have documented that structure–function relationships may vary among individuals depending on age, sex, cognitive performance, and disease conditions9,10,80. Understanding how these individual differences affect regional variability in MSFD would require further investigation. Third, molecular attributes were derived from publicly available datasets in separate cohorts, which may not adequately reflect individual variability. Future studies should integrate comprehensive phenotyping of the human brain across multiple biophysical scales and modalities from the same individuals81. Fourth, our analyses employed a uniform parcellation atlas for all participants, implicitly assuming consistent spatial mapping of brain regions across individuals13. Future studies would account for individual-specific functional boundary mapping82. Finally, the spin test does not explicitly account for spatially heterogeneous autocorrelation, which may introduce biases in statistical inference70. Future studies could adopt spatial null models that accommodate nonstationarity, such as geometric eigenmodes83 and Bayesian spatial regression84.

In summary, we quantified the strength of correspondence between functional activity and the underlying multiscale structural wiring. MSC eigenmodes effectively constrain brain functional dynamics. The multiscale structure–function relationships were gradually decoupled along the unimodal-transmodal hierarchy. These decoupled patterns were colocalized with molecular fingerprints, including neurotransmitter receptors, mitochondrial features, and gene transcription profiles. Collectively, our results provide comprehensive insights into how multiscale structural wiring constrains functional dynamics and further elucidate its neurobiological underpinnings.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

We included 100 unrelated healthy adults (n = 100; 54 females; 22–36 years) from the HCP (https://db.humanconnectome.org/)47. All participants underwent multimodal MRI scans on a 3 T Siemens Skyra scanner, including structural MRI, microstructurally sensitive T1w/T2w images, diffusion MRI, and resting-state and task-evoked fMRI. All volunteers provided informed consent. The open-access data collection followed protocols approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. Detailed acquisition procedures were available in Supplementary Method S1.

MRI data were processed through the HCP’s minimal preprocessing pipeline85. High-resolution T1w images handled with Freesurfer 5.3-HCP, including bias field correction, tissue segmentation, and surface reconstruction. After correcting for intensity nonuniformity, T1w images were divided by co-registered T2w images to generate volumetric T1w/T2w (myelin) maps, providing a contrast sensitive to cortical microstructure69. Diffusion MRI data were denoised and corrected for motion and eddy current distortions. Furthermore, spontaneous (resting-state) fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were corrected for gradient nonlinearity, geometric distortions, head motion, high-pass filtering, and residual confounds. We utilized rs-fMRI data from the first session (HCP REST-1) to conduct the main analyses, while the second session (HCP REST-2) was used as internal validation to assess reproducibility. Additionally, we analyzed 47 task-evoked fMRI data across seven key contrasts (Supplementary Table 1). Both spontaneous and task-evoked fMRI data were mapped into the fsLR-32k CIFTI space and parcellated into the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas48. Further preprocessing details were provided in Supplementary Method S2.

Generation of multiscale structural features

We derived complementary structural wiring features from three imaging sequences36–38, GD from T1w images, MPC from T1w/T2w images, and TS from diffusion MRI. All features were parcellated into the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas48.

Geodesic distance

GD matrices were generated for each participant’s native cortical mid-thickness surface using workbench commands. For each cortical parcel, the centroid vertex was identified as the vertex with the minimal Euclidean distance to all other vertices within the assigned parcel28,86. Dijkstra’s algorithm was then applied to calculate the GD between centroid vertices and all other vertices. We mirrored the GD to account for the inability to cross the hemispheres. Finally, vertex-level GD values were averaged within each parcel, yielding a symmetric parcel-wise GD matrix.

Microstructure profile covariance

Following previous procedures36,46, we generated 12 equivolumetric intracortical surfaces from the pial surface and white matter surface to sample T1w/T2w intensities across cortical depth. These profiles capture the intracortical microstructural composition. Vertex-wise intensity profiles were averaged within the parcels, excluding outliers. Microstructural similarity was calculated as the pairwise partial correlation of intensity intensities, adjusted for the mean intensity across cortical regions. The resulting matrix was thresholded at zero, log-transformed, and generated a symmetric MPC matrix.

Tractography strength

Structural connectivity was derived from dMRI tractography using MRtrix3 software87. Fiber orientation distributions were estimated via constrained spherical-deconvolution, and whole-brain tractography was generated with 40 million streamlines using anatomically constrained tractography. Spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT2) was applied to optimize streamline reconstruction88. Streamlines were mapped into the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas to construct individual TS matrices. Connection weights were defined as the weighted streamline count between regions. A group-consensus structural network was generated using distance-dependent thresholding89. This approach varies the consensus threshold as a function of inter-regional distance, thereby mitigating the over- and underexpression of short-range and long-range connections. The resulting group-level TS matrix preserves the connection-length distributions across individual subjects and integral organizational features. Finally, the weights of TS were log-transformed to reduce the variance in connectivity strength.

Construction of multiscale structural connectome

The comprehensive MSC model was constructed by integrating three complementary structural features (TS, GD, and MPC) on the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas. Consistent with previous studies36–38, the nonzero values in these interregional matrices were rank-normalized. Notably, rank normalization was applied to the inverted form of the GD matrix, so that larger values corresponded to closer interregional proximity. Secondly, the dense matrices (i.e., MPC and inverted GD) were rescaled to match the numerical range of the sparse matrix (i.e., TS), which could balance the contributions of each structural feature. Thirdly, we horizontally concatenated these normalized matrices and constructed a similarity matrix using row-wise normalized angle similarity. Graph energy spectral density and activity concentration

Graph ESD represents the energy distribution at each connectome eigenmode during dynamic functional activity. For a given connectome eigenmode ψk, ESD was calculated as the absolute square of the amplitudes of its temporal coefficient: ESD(ψk,t)=∣wk(t)∣2.

Moreover, to measure spontaneous functional activity concentration at each brain region vi, we computed the L2-norm of the fMRI signal fvi(t) across time points: Evi=∣∣fvi(t)∣∣2, which reflects the overall energy contributed by each brain region across the temporal domain24,26.

Reconstruction of spontaneous and task-evoked functional activity

We evaluated the reconstruction accuracy of connectome eigenmodes in explaining spontaneous and task-evoked cortical activity. For spontaneous fMRI, we reconstructed each individual’s cortical activity at each time frame using an increasing number of eigenmodes. We then generated an interregional functional connectivity matrix from the reconstructed activity. Reconstruction accuracy was quantified by the proportion of the empirical and reconstructed spontaneous activity concentration and the correlation between the empirical and reconstructed functional connectivity matrices. For task-evoked fMRI, we reconstructed each individual’s 47 task-based activation maps and evaluated accuracy by computing the correlation between the observed and reconstructed activation patterns.

We further investigated the uniqueness of MSC eigenmodes in explaining functional activity91,92 (Supplementary Method S3). First, to illustrate the significance of the MSC’s specific topology for capturing empirical brain activity, we generated eigenmodes from rewired connectomes that preserve the original MSC’s weight, degree, and strength distributions49,50. Second, to evaluate whether the improved reconstruction performance of MSC eigenmodes arises from specific biological information rather than the addition of extra feature matrices, we constructed a “noise fusion” model by combining the TS matrix with rewired GD and MPC connectomes. Third, to test whether empirical MSC eigenmodes explain observed activity patterns more effectively than randomly oriented activity patterns, we derived surrogate cortical activity maps using the spatial auto-correlation preserving permutation (spin) test51,52. The empirical reconstruction accuracy was then compared against these null distributions to determine statistical significance.

To examine the contribution of distinct structural wiring features to the reconstruction of functional activity, we varied the relative weights of TS, GD, and MPC using a weighted fusion strategy. Specifically, we assigned weighting coefficients βTS, βGD, and βMPC to each structural feature, such that βTS + βGD + βMPC = 1. These weights were sampled in steps of 0.05 over the interval [0, 1], generating a series of weighted MSCs. For each weighting configuration, we evaluated their performance in reconstructing both spontaneous and task-evoked functional activity.

Quantifying multiscale structure–function decoupling

We applied graph signal filtering to decompose spontaneous functional activity into low-frequency (i.e., more coupled with the MSC) and high-frequency components (i.e., more decoupled from the MSC)23,26. The cut-off frequency C was determined using a graph spectral dichotomization approach26, which divided the spectrum into two halves with equal energy (median-split) according to the mean ESD. We adopted the binary logarithmic form to ensure interpretability23,26: an MSFD value of 0 indicates the perfect balance between coupled and decoupled components. MSFD values near −1 reflect that functional activity is strongly coupled to the MSC network, whereas values approaching 1 indicate predominant decoupling.

To identify cortical regions exhibiting significant coupling or decoupling, we performed a nonparametric permutation test26. Specifically, we set the significance threshold at α=1/(19+1)=0.05 and generated 19 surrogate activity maps for each individual via the graph spectral randomization method25,26 (Supplementary Method S3). Furthermore, the statistical significance of the group average MSFD across individuals was determined using a binomial distribution P(n) with n = 100 independent tests, controlling for multiple comparisons across 360 regions.

Relation to microstructure and macroscale features

To investigate the biological basis underlying regional variability in MSFD, we further examined its associations with micro- and macro-scale features. Spatial relationships between two brain maps were estimated using Spearman correlation, and significance was assessed with the spin test to account for spatial autocorrelation.

First, microstructural intensity profiles were derived from the 3D BigBrain histological atlas of a post-mortem human brain (male, 65 years)58. The map of histological cytoarchitectural gradient was acquired from the “fsaverage” space utilizing the BigBrainWarp toolbox93. The BigBrain gradient of MPC captures topographical variation in cytoarchitectural similarity across cortical layers, reflecting laminar differentiation and microstructural complexity46,59. Second, NeuroSynth maps were derived from large-scale text-mining analyses of neuroimaging studies, which capture brain regions associated with specific cognitive processes or behaviors60. The first principal component of NeuroSynth meta-analytic decodings represents the dominant axis of variation across brain activation maps associated with cognitive terms27. We also performed functional decoding analysis of the MSFD pattern (Supplementary Method S4). Finally, macroscale functional hierarchy was characterized through the principal gradient of functional connectivity28. These functional hierarchy maps were obtained using the neuromaps toolbox94, projected into the fsLR-32k CIFTI space, and parcellated according to the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas48.

Associations with neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial profiles

To investigate the potential molecular fingerprints underlying regional MSFD patterns, we examined relationships between MSFD patterns and neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial profiles.

Neurotransmitter receptors

Neurotransmitter receptors and transporters were obtained from publicly available positron emission tomography (PET) tracer data61. These receptors combine into nine neurotransmitter systems: acetylcholine (α4β2, M1, and VAChT), cannabinoid (CB1), dopamine (D1, D2, and DAT), histamine (H3), GABA (GABAA), glutamate (mGluR5, NMDA), norepinephrine (NET), opioid (MOR), and serotonin (5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HTT). Volumetric PET images were spatially normalized to standard MNI space. PET images were parcellated into the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas and z-scored, generating a 360 (region) × 9 (system) matrix of relative densities.

Mitochondrial profiles

Mitochondrial data were derived from a 54-year-old neurotypical male who died of myocardial infarction62. The frozen brain slab was partitioned into 703 voxels (3 × 3 × 3 mm) using the physical voxelization method. To register mitochondrial values into standard MNI space, voxel values were assigned to their corresponding locations and subsequently aggregated to generate brain-wide maps for six mitochondrial features, including NADH–ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I, CI), succinate dehydrogenase (complex II, CII), cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV, CIV), mitochondrial density (MitoD), MRC, and TRC. These maps were parcellated into the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas, yielding a 360 (region) × 6 (feature) matrix that quantifies regional variation in mitochondrial respiratory function and metabolic activity across the human cortex.

Dominance analysis

Multivariate linear regression models were constructed to bridge regional MSFD patterns and molecular features. The responder variable was MSFD values, while the predictor variables included nine neurotransmitter receptors or six mitochondrial profiles. The relative contribution of each molecular feature to the overall model fit was subsequently assessed using dominance analysis95.

To account for potential confounding by the principal gradient of functional connectivity, we regressed this gradient from both the MSFD map and each molecular map prior to model fitting. Dominance analysis was then repeated using the resulting residuals to assess whether the observed associations were independent of the canonical cortical hierarchy.

Neuroimaging-transcription association analysis

Gene expression profiles

Regional microarray transcriptomic data were derived from six post-mortem healthy adult donors (one female, 24–57 years) provided by the AHBA64. Transcriptomic data were processed with the abagen toolbox96 using the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas. A total of 8563 genes showing differential stability above 0.1 were retained for further analysis.

Gene enrichment analysis

The PLS regression97 was utilized to probe the relationships between regional MSFD patterns and microscale transcriptomic profiles. The first PLS component (PLS1) captured the combination of gene expression levels exhibiting the greatest covariance with the MSFD pattern. The significance of latent variables was assessed using the spin test (10,000 times). Subsequently, bootstrapping resampling was conducted to estimate the contribution of each gene (10,000 times). The z-scores were computed as the ratio of each gene’s weight to its bootstrap-derived standard error65,66. Genes were then ranked by z-score, and significance was evaluated using one-sample Z tests65,66. Genes that survived after FDR correction (PFDR < 0.001) were identified as MSFD-related genes.

To reveal biological processes of MSFD-related genes, we performed gene function enrichment analysis using the Metascape toolbox67 (http://metascape.org). The Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases were used to perform functional annotation and pathway enrichment. Significance was set at P < 0.05 with FDR correction.

Cell type analysis

To assign MSFD-related genes to cell types, we extracted 25 cell-type categories98, including astrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, oligodendrocyte precursors, oligodendrocytes, and neuronal subtypes. Cell-type specificity of MSFD-related genes was assessed using the clusterProfiler package99. The P values were obtained by comparing the enrichment scores of gene sets against null distributions derived from permutation tests, and subsequently FDR-corrected (P < 0.05).

Sensitivity and reproducibility analyses

To assess the stability of regional MSFD patterns to variations in the cut-off frequency parameter, MSC eigenmodes were categorized into low-, medium-, and high-frequency bands24. Functional activity was then decomposed into low- and high-frequency components using different combinations of the lowest KL and the highest KH MSC eigenmodes. The resulting MSFD patterns were spatially correlated with those from the original analysis to evaluate stability.

To verify the reproducibility of the MSFD patterns, we included multimodal MRI data of 41 healthy adults from the MICA-MICs (n = 41; 21 females; 15–45 years; 2 left-handed; https://osf.io/j532r/)57. The ethics committee of the Montreal Neurological Institute and Hospital approved all procedures. Subjects underwent multimodal MRI scans on a 3 T Siemens Magnetom Prisma-Fit, including T1w, quantitative T1 (qT1) relaxometry, diffusion MRI, and rs-fMRI. Detailed procedures for data acquisition and preprocessing were available at ref. 36,57. The preprocessing steps and MSC construction closely followed the protocols established for the original HCP dataset, with microstructural features derived from qT1 images. Regional MSFD pattern was replicated in this dataset, and its spatial correspondence with the original analysis was evaluated.
