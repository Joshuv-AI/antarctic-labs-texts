The Use of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist in the Democratic Republic of the Congo: A Exploratory Survey
Authors: Florent Tshibwid A. Zeng, Gauthier Bahizire Murhula, Marie‐Etienne Lusasi Masesi, Sifa Nganza Katungu, Alexandre Amini Mitamo, Hervé Tshikomba Mbuyamba, Daniel Safari Nteranya, Dieumerci Kaseso Wasingya, Erick Namegabe Mugabo, Nestor Menelik Mulamba Ebondo, Nérée Akonkwa Bapolisi, Hervé Monka Lekuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimSince its establishment by the World Health Organization (WHO), the surgical safety checklist (SSC) is variably adopted worldwide despite evidence of advantages related to its use. To our knowledge, no data exists about the use of the SSC in DRC. We aimed to report the frequency of its use and reasons for its non‐use.MethodsUsing Google Forms, a survey was conducted in the Democratic Republic of the Congo public hospitals for 20 days (25 April to 15 May 2024).ResultsFifty‐nine hospitals were included in the survey, and 32 (54.2%) are using the SSC for a mean time of 4 (± 2) years. Of these, 16 (50%) use it regularly, with a surgeon coordinating the checklist process in 14 hospitals (43.8%). Twenty‐seven hospitals do not use the SSC due to the absence of training for its use in 16 hospitals (59.3%) or due to the unavailability of the SSC in 15 hospitals (55.6%).ConclusionHalf of the public hospitals in DRC have used the SSC for an average of 4 years. Lack of previous training and unavailability of the SCC are the main reasons of its non‐use. Seminars and training on the use of SSC would allow its use in these health facilities.

Introduction

Since 2008, the World Health Organization (WHO) has launched the Surgical Safety Checklist (SSC) as a tool that helps improve perioperative outcomes by increasing the safety of surgical procedures and reducing related morbidity and mortality [1].

The SSC has been implemented and used worldwide, especially in low‐income countries where it has shown valuable positive outcomes [2]. However, its use remains limited in low and middle‐income countries due to several barriers, including unavailability, lack of training, high staff turnover, work overload, lack of prioritization of SSC by trained specialists (surgeons and anesthesiologists), poor perception of SSC objectives, economic, language, and cultural issues, low implementation rates, and a lack of leadership among stakeholders in surgical patient management [3, 4, 5].

Several approaches have been designed to increase the implementation rate of the SSC in Low and Middle‐Income Countries (LMICs), with progress being noted when the approach is multidisciplinary, mixed, and team‐based [6, 7, 8].

As far as the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is concerned, there is a scarcity of studies showing what has been done regarding SSC. We aimed to evaluate the frequency of the SSC use in Congolese hospitals and to identify reasons of its non‐use.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional Ethical committee of the “Université Catholique de Bukavu” under the number UCB/CIES/NC/009/2024.

Study Design and Setting

A 20‐day survey was conducted from April 25 to May 15, 2024, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, a Central African country spanning 2,345,410 km and organized into provinces. The country has 26 provinces regrouped by linguistic regions [9]. The health system is organized in cooperation between the public and private systems. Study Population

Stratified sampling technique was used, with strata being constituted by three groups of provinces: the West, the North, and the South and Central. In a context of an exploratory survey, within each stratum, 26 hospitals were randomly selected, making a total of 78 hospitals. We determined the sample size to 26 by convenience. The exhaustive sample was constituted of responders.

In each of the 78 selected hospitals, a contact was established with a medical staff member acceding to the Operating Room (ORm): scrub nurse, anesthetist anesthesiologist, or surgeon. In case the contact was made with a responder who had less than a year in the institution, or in case they did not give consent to participate, another team member was contacted. That member filled out the survey in collaboration with the ORm team including scrub nurses, anesthetists/anesthesiologists, and surgeons.

For each stratum, the following participation rates were noted: in the West, 21/26 (80.7%), in the North 19/26 (73.1%), and in the south and center, 19/26 (73.1%).

Data Collection

Using Google Forms as a tool language, the survey was developed and validated by authors. The form was electronically sent to responders, and their replies were automatically registered on Google Forms in an Excel spreadsheet. Studied variables were the hospital (province, city, name, level), the operating room health workers (presence of a surgeon – a medical doctor with Master's Degree in Surgery, an anesthetist – health worker with specialized formation in anesthesia, presence of an anesthesiologist – a medical doctor with a Master's Degree in Anesthesia, presence of 2 ORm nurses, organization of a residency program in surgery) and about the SSC (previous seminar on its use, status of its use, since when it has been used, frequency of its use, qualification of the coordinator of the SSC, if it practiced out loud and if there is interaction between anesthetist, surgeon and ORm nurses, reasons of SSC lack of use, and availability for an online formation on the use of the SSC). The SSC use was defined as the utilization of either the WHO SSC or a locally adapted version. Collected data were validated before their analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Collected data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences® (IBM, SPSS Inc.) version 26. Continuous variables are expressed as mean (± Standard Deviation) or median (IQR) and categorical variables as frequencies.

Results

In this survey, we received 59 responses from the 78 contacted hospitals. The 59 included hospitals were located in 26 cities from 18 of the 26 provinces of DRC, as represented in Figure 2.

Location of surveyed hospitals: Among the 59 hospitals, 14 were in Kinshasa, 8 in Lubumbashi, and 4 in Goma. Other cities contributed with one or two hospitals.

Among the 59 hospitals, 44 (74.6%) were General Referral Hospitals. Thirty‐five hospitals (59.3%) had at least one surgeon. Data related to hospitals and their personnel in ORm are detailed in Table 1. Concerning the SSC, 20 hospitals (33.9%) had a previous seminar about its use. For the 59 institutions, 32 hospitals (54.2%) were using the SSC. The mean duration of SSC use was 4 ± 2 years. The frequency of use was often in 16 hospitals (50%), coordinated by the surgeon in 14 hospitals (43.8%) and practiced‐out loud in 23 hospitals (71.9%). Among the remaining 27 hospitals (45.8%) not using the SSC, three groups of reasons were evoked with absence of previous seminar about the use of SSC in 16 hospitals (59.3%). Details about the use of the SSC is reported in Table 2. All responders from hospitals not using the SSC agreed to attend an online training on the use of the SSC.

Discussion

The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist has demonstrated value in surgical practice by enhancing patient safety [2, 11, 12, 13]. Moreover, it has, through the decade, decreased the risk of avoidable post‐operative complications, including sepsis [14, 15, 16]. It has been observed that the SSC use is associated with reduction of surgery‐related morbidity and mortality [6, 13, 16]. Furthermore, Investing in the SSC could save the patient and the health system from unnecessary expenses [17].

However, the SSC remain underused, particularly in many LMICs with low utilization rates [18]. In DRC, our study revealed a use frequency of 54.2%, which is still low considering that around 4 to 10 surgeries are performed in unsafe conditions. Some Sub‐Saharan African countries like South Africa have shown progress in the use of SSC with a rate approximating 80% [11], and other African countries like Nigeria and Madagascar, whose utilization rate is 65% and 62.5%, respectively [7, 19]

Among reason of not using the SSC, the lack of training on the use of the SSC was reported in in 59.3%. The lack of training in SSC has already been found in other studies to be the main reason for the non‐use of SSC [3, 4, 20]. In fact, in DRC, few public hospitals staff benefit from continuous medical education (CME) due to restricted health budget in a context of continuous war in the Eastern part of the country. However, University Teaching Hospitals (UTH), whose staff members regularly benefit from CME could be focal points to disseminate SSC implementation in other public hospitals by organizing teaching sessions. In fact, a recent study in Somalia reported SSC acceptance from 37% to 98.8% [21]. By using a comprehensive training program in the SSC using the WHO model, several authors reported increased acceptance of the SSC [2, 5, 21, 22].

The second reported reason of not using the SSC was the unavailability of the SSC, reported in 55.6%. This was also reported in a recent review in Senegal, among pediatric surgeons [23]. Since the WHO SSC model is freely accessible on the internet, printable or paintable on ORm walls for daily use, many authors perceive this reason as a lack of interest in using the SSC [2, 23, 24, 25]. In DRC, this perception could also be acceptable. In fact, despite the low internet penetrance of the country, we were able to conduct an online study, which confirm that in all of the surveyed hospitals, at least one person had access to internet. This reason can also be a façade of individual, interprofessional and environment/organizational (excessive workload and distractions) barriers to the SSC implementation [2, 24, 25].

The existence of another checking procedure was reported to be re reason of not using the SSC in 18.5%. In fact, in such situations, the use of the SSC was perceived as a loss of time [23, 25]. In the Congolese context, most of ORm staff combine working in public and private hospitals. Therefore, each activity perceived as time‐consuming will hardly be accepted.

In DRC, implementation of the SSC in public hospitals should take advantage of the fact that each province has a public university with a faculty of medicine. The main institutional actors should be: 1) the national ministry of health, 2) international experts, 3) faculties of medicine of each public university by province, 4) the Provincial Health Direction (a local ramification of the National Minister of Health (NMH)), and 5) the public hospitals. A suggestion of how the SSC could be implemented by province is demonstrated in Figure 3. Taking into account that the implementation of a checklist through SSC training with a multidisciplinary mixed team‐based approach has shown its efficiency [7, 8, 15, 26, 27], we recommend that approach. To prevent long‐term decline of the SSC use [6, 28], we recommend endorsement by the NMH through the provincial direction of health by including report on the SSC use among the routine monthly reports required from each hospital in each province.

Suggestion of implementation of the SSC in DRC. Implementation of the SSC with involvement of the National Minister Health and international experts in SSC implementation in LMICs. With local universities in each province, online seminars could be organized, then followed by in‐person seminars between faculties' members and ORm staff of each hospital for adaptation and demonstration of the SSC to the local context, including the language adaptation. After SSC implementation, an official announcement to including the report on the SSC in the monthly report transmitted by all hospitals. To ensure permanent implementation of the SSC, yearly evaluation of SSC should be conducted by the Provincial Health Direction in each hospital.

Limitations

This study has faced some limitations related first to the design of the survey and to the small amount of data collected in countries with 26 regions within several hospitals that were considered for the study but did not respond to authors, including those of the North‐West region. The use of “always,” “often,” and “rarely” to describe SSC use are subjective and may vary between respondents. Thus, although the results of this study paint a picture of DRC hospitals, they cannot be generalized to the whole country. Further inclusive studies are needed to give the most appropriate results.

Conclusion

This study shows that in surveyed public hospitals in DRC, half of them use the SSC, with a surgeon mainly coordinating the checking. The other half of hospitals do not use the SSC, citing a lack of training on its use, lack of the SSC, or the existence of another checking system similar to the SSC as reason for non‐use.
