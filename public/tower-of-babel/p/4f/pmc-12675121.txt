Can safety and innovation coexist in the global expansion of laser balloon ablation?
Authors: Jose Eric M Lacsa, Doctor in Religious, Values Education
Published: 2025

To the Editor: I read with great interest the article by Kawase et al,1 which draws attention to the increased incidence of periprocedural stroke associated with laser balloon (LB) ablation compared with radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation. Despite this elevated stroke risk, the study reports that other critical safety outcomes, including in-hospital mortality and emergency readmission rates, are largely comparable across ablation techniques. This nuanced finding carries important implications beyond Japan’s borders, particularly for countries such as the Philippines, where electrophysiology is a rapidly developing specialty, but health care resources remain unevenly distributed.

The Philippines is experiencing a growing burden of atrial fibrillation, driven by an aging population and a rising prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes.2 As more Filipino patients become candidates for catheter ablation, the introduction of advanced technologies such as LB ablation offers opportunities for improved arrhythmia control through more precise pulmonary vein isolation. However, the elevated risk of stroke linked to LB ablation demands careful balancing of benefits and harms, especially in health care environments where stroke prevention, acute management, and rehabilitation services are limited or unevenly accessible.3

Many provincial hospitals and public health facilities in the Philippines lack widespread availability of intracardiac imaging and neurodiagnostic tools critical for mitigating and managing periprocedural complications. The adoption of LB ablation without parallel investments in safety protocols and postprocedural care risks exacerbating health inequities. This scenario is not unique to the Philippines; other low- and middle-income countries face similar challenges in safely integrating cutting-edge cardiac therapies within resource-constrained settings.4

Encouragingly, the Japanese data indicate that most patients who experienced stroke after LB ablation were ultimately discharged home.1 This outcome underscores the importance of timely recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary care involving cardiology, neurology, and rehabilitation teams. For the Philippines, this highlights a critical need to strengthen clinical training, interdepartmental collaboration, and infrastructure to support comprehensive stroke care alongside electrophysiology advancements.5

The broader lesson here is that innovation in cardiac rhythm management must be implemented with keen awareness of local health system capacities and patient safety imperatives. Global efforts to improve AF outcomes should emphasize not only the dissemination of novel technologies but also the development of tailored protocols, workforce capacity, and equitable access to supportive care.

In the context of the Philippines, embracing LB ablation’s potential should be accompanied by a commitment to robust data collection, outcome monitoring, and health system strengthening. Only through such an integrated approach can new therapies be safely and effectively incorporated, ensuring that advances in electrophysiology benefit all patients, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status.
