Prognostic value of preoperative CT-derived fractional flow reserve after transcatheter or surgical aortic valve replacement in patients with severe aortic stenosis
Authors: Xirui Duan, Xudong Jin, Shiying Tang, Shuaiyan Zuo, Wan Shen, Lei Yang, Ting Yang, Xiaolan Du, Feifei Zhou, Xinyan Zhou, Rong Qian, Na Tan, Guifang Sun, Chengde Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with aortic stenosis (AS) often have concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD), and coronary CTA (CCTA) is performed for anatomical evaluation to inform coronary revascularization decision-making prior to aortic valve replacement (AVR). However, the role of coronary hemodynamic assessment in pre-AVR is not well established. We aim to evaluate the prognostic value of preoperative CT angiography–derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) in patients with severe AS undergoing transcatheter (TAVR) or surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR).MethodsThis consecutive retrospective observational cohort (July 2023–January 2025) included patients with severe AS referred to TAVR or SAVR. CT-FFR was obtained fully-automatic using an on-site machine learning algorithm, and a low value was defined as CT-FFR ≤ 0.80. The composite endpoint was a patient-oriented composite of all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) (nonfatal myocardial infarction, unstable angina, cardiac death, cardiovascular-driven admission or heart failure admission).ResultsAmong 329 patients (mean age 61.4 ± 13.1 years), composite endpoint occurred in 14.3% and all-cause mortality in 4.0% over a median follow-up of 17 months. After adjustment, CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 remained independently associated with the composite endpoint (HR 3.83; 95% CI 2.04–7.20; P < 0.001) and with MACE (HR 4.84; 95% CI 2.40–9.78; P < 0.001), but was not independently associated with all-cause mortality (P = 0.071). Adding CT-FFR to clinical models substantially improved risk discrimination for MACE (C-index 0.74) and significantly improved reclassification (NRI 0.346; P < 0.001), whereas adding anatomical CCTA alone provided minimal incremental value.ConclusionIn patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR or SAVR, preoperative CT-FFR was associated with an increased risk of post-AVR adverse cardiac events. Introduction

Patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) often have concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD), with the prevalence of 40–70% [1–3]. The ESC/EACTS 2025 guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease recommend coronary artery evaluation when valve surgery or intervention is planned, but functional hemodynamic assessment of CAD in these patients is not well established [4]. Notably, the coronary physiology of severe AS is distinct, characterized by left ventricular hypertrophy, microvascular dysfunction, and a blunted hyperemic response, all of which complicate invasive hyperemia-based assessment and are directly relevant to the interpretation of any FFR-based index in this population [5, 6]. Although the pre-TAVR diagnostic workflow commonly includes invasive coronary angiography (ICA) to exclude significant CAD, the routine use of invasive FFR before TAVR is not universal and remains debated in severe AS [6]. Similarly, pre-SAVR pathways generally rely on anatomy-based thresholds and concomitant CABG decisions, with only limited and inconsistent incorporation of preoperative hemodynamic assessment. Therefore, incorporating coronary hemodynamic assessment into pre-AVR risk stratification is of clinical importance.

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is the preferred method to assess the coronary arteries noninvasively [7]. To standardize anatomic assessment on CCTA, the Coronary Artery Disease–Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS) has been widely adopted [8]. Yet CAD-RADS essentially classifies disease by stenosis severity, with limited coronary hemodynamic assessment. Although CAD-RADS 2.0 introduced optional CT angiography–derived FFR (CT-FFR) extensions [9], their application is inconsistent and has not been systematically validated in patients with severe AS undergoing AVR.

CT-FFR has been validated against ICA [10–12] in hemodynamic assessment and has gained increased favor and wider clinical application due to its noninvasive nature. In large prospective multicenter studies, CT-FFR has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for identifying hemodynamically significant coronary stenosis using invasive FFR as the reference standard [13, 14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CT-FFR is associated with postoperative clinical outcomes in patients undergoing TAVR, whereas data in SAVR are rare [15–17]. However, since patients selected for TAVR versus SAVR differ substantially in baseline characteristics and treatment selection criteria, comprehensive evidence is still lacking to clarify whether the ischemic burden assessed by CT-FFR incrementally improves risk stratification beyond anatomical CAD severity for adverse outcomes after valve replacement.

To address these limitations, we aimed to evaluate the prognostic association between preoperative CT-FFR and post-AVR outcomes, and its value for risk stratification, in patients with severe AS. The clinical composite endpoint was a patient-oriented composite endpoint consisting of all-cause mortality and MACE after AVR. We further quantified the incremental prognostic value of CT-FFR beyond clinical risk factors and anatomical coronary assessment on CCTA using discrimination and reclassification metrics, both in the overall cohort and separately in the TAVR and SAVR cohorts.

Methods

Study population

This single-center consecutive observational retrospective study included patients with severe AS referred to AVR after CCTA between July 2023 and January 2025. Severe AS was confirmed by transthoracic echocardiography and defined as a peak aortic jet velocity (Vmax) ≥ 4.0 m/s, a mean transvalvular gradient ≥ 40 mmHg, or an aortic valve area (AVA) ≤ 1.0 cm² (indexed AVA ≤ 0.6 cm²/m²). Low-flow, low-gradient AS was adjudicated using a stepwise algorithm: in patients with Vmax < 4.0 m/s and mean gradient < 40 mmHg, AVA and stroke-volume index (SVI) were assessed; in those with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (< 50%) and SVI ≤ 35 mL/m², low-dose dobutamine stress echocardiography was used to distinguish true-severe from pseudo-severe AS; and in paradoxical low-flow, low-gradient AS (LVEF ≥ 50%), the aortic-valve calcium score on CT was used as a supportive criterion. Consecutive patients with severe AS referred for AVR were enrolled irrespective of coronary status, which was characterized noninvasively on CCTA. Obstructive CAD was defined as a CAD-RADS category ≥ 3. The date of AVR was defined as the study enrollment date (time zero) for each patient, and clinical events were analyzed at the follow-up. The interval between CCTA and AVR was less than 7 days. All patients had severe AS and a CT scan obtained by using pre-AVR protocol and the choice of TAVR or SAVR was determined by the Heart Team in accordance with current guideline recommendations and the volition of the patient. The Heart Team evaluated routine clinical information, including symptoms, cardiovascular risk factors, transthoracic echocardiographic findings, laboratory results, surgical risk scores, and anatomical coronary assessment on CCTA. ICA was considered when clinically indicated, particularly in patients with suspected obstructive CAD on anatomical CCTA, symptoms suggestive of myocardial ischemia, high clinical risk, or when coronary anatomy required further clarification before AVR. CT-FFR results were calculated retrospectively for research purposes and were not available to the Heart Team; therefore, CT-FFR did not inform clinical management. In our center, all patients undergoing pre-AVR CT also underwent CCTA. Exclusion criteria included prior percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting, lack of follow-up and poor image quality or coronary anatomical variants that precluded coronary artery segmentation (Fig. 1). Patients with prior PCI or CABG (> 30 days) were excluded because coronary stents and bypass grafts produce metallic and beam-hardening artifacts and alter coronary geometry, precluding reliable coronary segmentation and CT-FFR computation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Yan’an Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University (Kunming Yan’an Hospital) (Ethical Number 2025-343-01), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the same ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical end points

The clinical composite end point was a patient-oriented composite of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) (nonfatal myocardial infarction, unstable angina, cardiac death, cardiovascular-driven admission or heart failure admission) and all-cause mortality at post-AVR. Specifically, cardiac death was included within all-cause mortality for calculation of the composite endpoint and was not double-counted as a separate MACE event within the same composite endpoint. The endpoint definitions were based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 initiative criteria [18]. Given that large RCTs such as NOTION, SURTAVI, and the Evolut Low Risk Trial traditionally excluded patients with left main coronary artery disease, high SYNTAX scores, or recent PCI, resulting in limited representation of revascularized patients [19–21], in this study, an exploratory endpoint was defined as concomitant PCI or CABG at the time of AVR and/or PCI/CABG performed within 30 days before AVR. Clinical events were ascertained from the electronic medical record and structured follow-up and were reviewed by two researchers (X.D. and X.J.) blinded to the CT-FFR results, with any discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Pre-AVR coronary CTA acquisition

All retrospectively ECG-gated coronary CT angiography acquisitions were performed on a second-generation dual-source CT system (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers). β-blockers and nitroglycerin were not administered before CCTA owing to aortic stenosis. A bolus of 85 mL iodinated contrast medium was injected intravenously at 5 mL/s, followed by a saline chaser. Tube voltage was 100 kVp with automated tube-current modulation (CARE Dose 4D). Images were reconstructed in the best systolic phase at a fixed absolute delay using an iterative reconstruction algorithm (SAFIRE; kernel I26f) at 0.75 mm slice thickness.

Coronary CTA data analysis

All CCTA images were transferred to cardiac research software (CoronaryDoc, v5.1.2, ShuKun Technology; skCT-FFR version v0.7.1) for further segmentation and calculation. Briefly, CT-FFR was automatically calculated using computational fluid dynamics modeling, in which a multilayer neural network learned the relationship between coronary anatomy and hemodynamics, followed by automatic extraction of vessel geometry (centerline, lumen contour, and stenosis) from CCTA images to provide CT-FFR values along the entire coronary tree [12]. CT-FFR values at 2 cm distal to the stenosis (or distal end of the middle arterial segment if no stenosis) on the corresponding CCTA image were automatically analyzed (Fig. 2). The patient-level CT-FFR used for classification was the lowest of these per-vessel distal values; a patient was classified as having a low CT-FFR when this lowest vessel value was ≤ 0.80. Coronary stenosis severity was assessed on a dedicated post-processing workstation (ShuKun) by two radiologists with more than 15 years of cardiovascular imaging experience (C.L. and G.S.), using axial images and multiplanar reconstruction to assign the CAD-RADS category for each major coronary artery. The lesion characteristics of discordant cases were discussed to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Descriptive data were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means ± standard deviations or medians (Q1-Q3) for continuous variables. Analysis was analyzed on a per-patient basis for clinical characteristics. Missing data were limited to NT-proBNP (10/329, 3.0%) and were handled by complete-case analysis without imputation. Time-to-event data were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Univariable regression was used to analyze the predictive value of CCTA and CT-FFR individually for MACE and all-cause mortality. Candidate covariates were pre-specified on clinical grounds; within this set, variables associated with the outcome at P < 0.10 on univariable analysis were retained to maintain parsimonious models given the limited number of events. The proportional hazards assumption was verified graphically with use of the log-log survival plot. Cox regression analysis was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Cumulative event rate curves were created. Analyses were pre-specified to be performed separately for the TAVR and SAVR cohorts in addition to the overall population. To account for baseline-hazard differences between procedures, a procedure-stratified Cox model was additionally fitted, and effect modification was assessed by adding a CT-FFR × procedure interaction term. As a sensitivity analysis, the primary models were repeated after excluding patients who met the exploratory revascularization endpoint.

Four models were constructed to assess the prognostic value of hemodynamic parameters (1) model 1 clinical risk factors (including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, NT-proBNP and left ventricular ejection fraction); (2) model 2 clinical risk factors + CCTA; (3) model 3 clinical risk factors + CT-FFR; (4) model 4 clinical risk factors + CCTA + CT-FFR. The discrimination ability of the predictive model was represented using Harrell’s c-index and time-dependent ROC analysis at 730 days, and reclassification performance was compared using the relative integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and category-free net reclassification index (NRI). Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (5000 replications) to estimate optimism-corrected Harrell’s c-index for the prediction models; model calibration was assessed by comparing predicted with observed (Kaplan–Meier) risk at 730 days. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistically significant difference. For the exploratory procedural end point, event rates were compared between CT-FFR categories (≤ 0.80 vs. > 0.80) using Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Study population

After application of the exclusion criteria, the study included 190 severe AS patients receiving TAVR and 139 severe AS patients receiving SAVR between July 2023 and January 2025 (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the cohort and AVR were displayed in Table 1. The median time of follow-up was 17 months. The mean age was 61.36 ± 13.14 years, and 40.7% were female. The median Euro SCORE II was 1.41 (Q1-Q3 0.90–2.25) and STS-PROM score was 1.39 (Q1-Q3 0.85–2.27). Of the patients, 93 (28.3%) exhibited low CT-FFR values (≤ 0.80). A detailed, per-vessel report of CT-FFR results can be found in Supplemental Tables 1 and baseline characteristics stratified by CT-FFR status (≤ 0.80 vs. > 0.80) are presented in Supplemental Table 3.

Clinical outcomes

The composite endpoint occurred in 14.3% (47/329) of the overall population. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 3) demonstrated that patients with preoperative CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 had a significantly lower rate of survival free from the composite endpoint compared with those with CT-FFR > 0.80 (Log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 was associated with the composite endpoint in univariable analysis (HR 4.34; 95% CI 2.42–7.79; P < 0.001) and remained independently associated after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR 3.83; 95% CI 2.04–7.20; P < 0.001).

Stratified by procedure, the rate of composite events in the TAVR cohort was significantly higher in patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 than in those with CT-FFR > 0.80 (32.2% vs. 10.7%; P = 0.001). Similarly, in the SAVR cohort, patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 had a significantly higher incidence of composite events compared with the high CT-FFR group (26.5% vs. 4.8%; P < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier curves showed significant risk stratification for the composite endpoint in both the TAVR subgroup (Fig. 3B, Log-rank P < 0.001) and the SAVR subgroup (Fig. 3C, Log-rank P < 0.001). No significant difference in composite event rates was observed between the TAVR and SAVR groups (P = 0.087).

MACE occurred in 11.9% (39/329; Table 2). Reporting the components individually, these comprised heart-failure or cardiovascular-driven admission in 24 patients (7.3%), unstable angina in 8 (2.4%), cardiac death in 5 (1.5%), and nonfatal myocardial infarction in 2 (0.6%). Heart-failure or cardiovascular-driven admission was the most frequent component and was significantly more common in the CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 group in both the TAVR and SAVR cohorts (P = 0.037 and P = 0.014, respectively); notably, both nonfatal myocardial infarctions occurred in the SAVR cohort. All-cause mortality occurred in 4.0% (13/329).

In exploratory procedural end point, concomitant coronary revascularization at the time of AVR was more frequently observed in patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80. In the TAVR cohort, concomitant PCI was performed in 23 of 190 patients (12.1%) and occurred more often in those with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 than in those with CT-FFR > 0.80 (16.9% vs. 9.9%; P = 0.228). In the SAVR cohort, all patients who underwent concomitant CABG belonged to the CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 group (11.8% vs. 0%; P = 0.003); however, not all patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 underwent CABG. In a sensitivity analysis excluding the 27 patients who underwent peri-procedural revascularization (the exploratory revascularization endpoint), CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 remained independently associated with the composite endpoint (adjusted HR 3.82; 95% CI 2.04–7.16; P < 0.001) and MACE (adjusted HR 4.56; 95% CI 2.25–9.22; P < 0.001).

Prognostic value of CT-FFR

In univariable Cox regression analysis, CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 was a strong predictor of MACE (HR 5.248; 95% CI 2.725–10.107; P < 0.001) (Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders (diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, NT-proBNP), CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 remained independently associated with MACE (Adjusted HR 4.842; 95% CI 2.397–9.779; P < 0.001).

For all-cause mortality, although CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 showed an association in univariable analysis, it did not remain an independent predictor in the multivariable model (Adjusted HR 2.741; P = 0.071). Instead, age was identified as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality (HR 1.069 per year; P = 0.030).

Discussion

Patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) have a high prevalence of concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD), which adversely affects clinical outcomes after aortic valve replacement (AVR) [4, 22, 23]. In our study, we found that preoperative CT-FFR was a predictor of MACE in patients with severe AS undergoing AVR, including both TAVR and SAVR approaches. CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 was associated with a markedly increased risk of MACE in the overall cohort (multivariable HR, 4.84; 95% CI 2.40–9.78; P < 0.001), and this association remained consistent in both TAVR and SAVR subgroups. Incorporation of CT-FFR into the current noninvasive pre-AVR evaluation significantly improved risk stratification beyond clinical variables and anatomic coronary assessment on CCTA (NRI,0.346; P < 0.001), as reflected by meaningful gains in model discrimination and reclassification. By contrast, stenosis-based anatomic severity assessed by CAD-RADS was not independently associated with MACE after adjustment for clinical confounders. However, the limited incremental value of anatomical CCTA in this cohort should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect the modest number of events, the low prevalence of obstructive CAD, interobserver variability, and the limits of categorical anatomical phenotyping, rather than an intrinsic inferiority of anatomical assessment to CT-FFR. Although CT-FFR showed an association with all-cause mortality in univariable analysis, this relationship was attenuated after multivariable adjustment, indicating that the prognostic value of CT-FFR in this population is primarily driven by its ability to identify patients at increased risk of post-AVR ischemic and heart failure–related events rather than overall mortality. In the exploratory analyses, concomitant coronary revascularization at the time of AVR was more frequently observed in patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80, with concomitant PCI in TAVR occurring numerically more often in the low CT-FFR group (16.9% vs. 9.9%; P = 0.228) and concomitant CABG in SAVR occurring exclusively in patients with CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 (11.8% vs. 0%; P = 0.003).

Previous studies have primarily focused on patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR, suggesting that impaired coronary hemodynamics captured by CT-FFR is associated with adverse post-TAVR outcomes [15, 16, 24]. However, systematic evidence for its role in an “overall AVR population (including both TAVR and SAVR)” remains limited. Meanwhile, because guidelines and clinical pathways differ between SAVR and TAVR with respect to perioperative coronary assessment and management, earlier studies confined to a single procedure or mixing procedures without stratification may have restricted generalizability.

Against this background, we enrolled a real-world cohort comprising patients with severe AS and observed in the overall cohort that CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 was a stable and independent predictor of MACE (Adjusted HR 4.842; 95% CI 2.397–9.779; P < 0.001), whereas CAD-RADS ≥ 3 showed no independent association after adjustment. This suggests that purely anatomic stenosis grading may be insufficient for risk characterization in this population; by contrast, the “functional ischemia/flow-limitation” information provided by CT-FFR is more closely aligned with mechanisms underlying subsequent ischemia- and heart-failure–related events. More precisely, in this population a low CT-FFR likely reflects not only epicardial flow limitation but also diffuse atherosclerotic burden, microvascular dysfunction with increased downstream resistance, and the greater heart-failure severity and comorbidity that accompany it [25–27]. CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 is therefore best interpreted as an integrated physiology-anatomy-burden risk marker rather than a pure measure of focal epicardial ischemia [28]. Although AVR lowers afterload and may partially reverse microvascular dysfunction and the blunted hyperemic response over time, preoperative CT-FFR captures fixed epicardial disease and the baseline atherosclerotic and hemodynamic burden that may persist after valve replacement [5]. This may explain why preoperative CT-FFR continued to predict post-AVR adverse events despite the expected physiological changes after AVR. This functional advantage may be particularly relevant in the pre-AVR population, in whom coronary calcification is common and may limit CCTA-based stenosis assessment through blooming and beam-hardening artifacts. Although heavy calcification can also affect CT-FFR estimation by impairing lumen segmentation, a recent study using the same fully automated skCT-FFR algorithm reported better diagnostic performance of CT-FFR than CCTA in vessels with an Agatston score > 400 [29]. This supports the feasibility of applying this AI-based CT-FFR approach in calcified pre-AVR populations, while remaining supportive rather than definitive evidence because coronary calcium burden was not systematically quantified in our prognostic cohort.

Consistent with previous TAVR studies [15], we also found that the independent prognostic value of CT-FFR for all-cause mortality attenuated after multivariable adjustment (overall HR 2.741; P = 0.071), while age emerged as a more important determinant of mortality (multivariable HR 1.069 per year; P = 0.030). These findings indicate that, in our cohort, mortality risk is driven more by overall baseline risk and age, whereas the prognostic contribution of CT-FFR is primarily reflected in identifying individuals at high risk of post-procedural MACE. Notably, our stratified analyses further address the evidence gap in SAVR CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 independently predicted MACE in both the SAVR and TAVR subgroups (SAVR HR 8.286; TAVR HR 3.942; both P < 0.001) but did not independently predict all-cause mortality in either subgroup (SAVR P = 0.318; TAVR P = 0.083) (Table 3).

In addition, in terms of model performance, adding CCTA to clinical variables provided limited incremental benefit, whereas incorporating CT-FFR substantially improved discrimination and significantly enhanced individual risk reclassification (C-index increased to 0.742; NRI 0.346; P < 0.001). In clinical terms, this means that adding CT-FFR improved the model’s ability to assign higher predicted risk to patients who subsequently experienced events and lower risk to those who remained event-free, thereby reclassifying a meaningful net proportion of patients into more appropriate risk categories than clinical factors and anatomical CCTA alone—information directly relevant to preoperative risk counseling. This supports the added value of CT-FFR as a preoperative, noninvasive, physiology-based risk stratification tool, with potential relevance in the SAVR pathway, where additional invasive evaluation and contrast exposure might otherwise be considered. Prospective studies are warranted to further define its clinical role.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective, observational study, which limits generalizability beyond our institution and imaging platform and supports association rather than causation, with residual confounding that could bias the estimates in both TAVR and SAVR cohorts. The exclusion of patients with prior PCI or CABG, although necessary for reliable CT-FFR computation, selected a lower-complexity CAD population and restricted the disease range; this would most plausibly bias the CT-FFR–outcome association toward the null and underestimate its effect in unselected populations. The procedure-specific subgroup analyses, particularly in the SAVR cohort, were limited by small event numbers and wide confidence intervals; these results are exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger cohorts. The composite endpoint incorporated softer, which are more susceptible to detection and adjudication variability than hard endpoints. Second, intraoperative coronary revascularization is a potential source of confounding. Because clinicians were blinded to CT-FFR, revascularization is unlikely to mediate the CT-FFR–outcome relationship; however, by treating more severe coronary disease it would tend to reduce subsequent events preferentially in the low-CT-FFR group, biasing the association toward the null. Reassuringly, a sensitivity analysis excluding revascularized patients yielded essentially unchanged estimates, indicating that the magnitude of this bias is small. We also pre-specified an exploratory revascularization endpoint to capture this clinical pathway. Third, we did not incorporate the full spectrum of CCTA-derived markers(e.g., perivascular fat attenuation index, detailed plaque composition and high-risk plaque features, or microcirculatory indices). This omission does not bias the observed CT-FFR–outcome association in a specific direction but may underestimate the total achievable discrimination; multimodal integration warrants study in larger, multicenter cohorts. Relatedly, classification by a single CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 threshold does not distinguish anatomical subsets (e.g., left main or complex multivessel disease) that differ in prognostic and therapeutic implications; CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 should therefore be interpreted as a global, vessel-integrated risk marker rather than a substitute for lesion-level anatomical characterization, and these anatomical subsets were not captured as discrete variables in this cohort. Fourth, as a computational physiology approach derived from CCTA, CT-FFR is constrained by image quality and modeling assumptions; heavy calcification, motion, arrhythmia, suboptimal opacification, and limited spatial resolution may introduce measurement error. In addition, coronary artery calcium burden was not routinely quantified using the Agatston score in our cohort and therefore could not be incorporated as a covariate or used for subgroup analysis. Accordingly, although heavy calcification was considered during image-quality assessment, the independent impact of coronary calcium burden on CT-FFR estimation and prognostic performance could not be fully evaluated. A further limitation is the lack of invasive physiological assessment or perfusion imaging as a reference standard before and after AVR. Although CT-FFR values have been reported to remain relatively stable before and after AVR, the CT-FFR ≤ 0.80 threshold has not been directly validated in patients with severe AS undergoing pre-AVR evaluation [16]. Finally, the median follow-up was relatively short for evaluating long-term prognosis after AVR, so the present findings principally reflect intermediate-term risk and require confirmation over longer follow-up. Further refinement through standardized imaging, internal and external validation, and comparison against invasive physiologic references is warranted.

Conclusions

In patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR or SAVR, preoperative CT-FFR–derived coronary hemodynamic impairment was associated with an increased risk of post-AVR adverse cardiac events. The prognostic value of CT-FFR was consistent across both TAVR and SAVR populations and may improve individual-level risk reclassification.
