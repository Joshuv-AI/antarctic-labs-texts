Targeted AAV6 gene therapy restores corneal endothelial function in three hereditary corneal dystrophies
Authors: Bi Ning Zhang, Benxiang Qi, Shijiu Chen, Jing Su, Guoyun Li, Xinping Wang, Zhongmei Ren, Hengrui Zhang, Jun Cheng, Jingyu Qu, Yang Yang, Qingjun Zhou, Lin Cong, Lixin Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe corneal endothelium maintains corneal transparency and vision. Hereditary corneal dystrophies, including macular corneal dystrophy (MCD), Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD), and congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED), cause progressive endothelial dysfunction, for which corneal transplantation is currently the main treatment. We evaluate an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy approach in preclinical models of MCD, FECD, and CHED. A refined intracameral injection method enables uniform endothelial transduction without corneal puncture. A single AAV6 administration supports sustained transgene expression in the corneal endothelium for over 18 months without detectable adverse immune responses. In MCD mice, AAV6-Chst5 reduces corneal opacification and restores keratan sulfate levels. In FECD mice, AAV6-Col8a2 prevents corneal opacity in 87.5% of treated eyes. In the CHED model, AAV6-Slc4a11 resolves corneal edema within 7 days. Single-cell RNA sequencing identifies Wnt5a as a downstream factor associated with MCD pathogenesis. These findings support the therapeutic potential of endothelial-targeted gene delivery for corneal endothelial disorders.

Introduction

Corneal endothelial cells (CEC) are a single layer of cells in the innermost region of the cornea. In healthy adults aged 20–39 years, CEC density is approximately 3,000 cells/mm2 and declines by 0.3% annually.1 CECs serve as a barrier limiting the diffusion of aqueous humor and macromolecules into the corneal stroma,2 while their pump function maintains corneal dehydration and transparency by actively transporting ions and water into the anterior chamber. This process also supports nutrient delivery and metabolic waste removal in the avascular cornea.3 Aging, genetic mutations, trauma, or surgery can reduce CEC density or function, leading to corneal edema and vision loss. Currently, corneal transplantation remains the primary treatment for endothelial disorders. However, the scarcity of donor corneas leaves only one cornea available for every 70 patients in need.4,5 Therefore, there is an urgent need for development and advancement in corneal endothelial gene therapy.

Inherited diseases affecting CECs are a major indication for corneal endothelial transplantation. Our previous work revealed that macular corneal dystrophy (MCD), traditionally classified as a stromal dystrophy, is closely associated with endothelial dysfunction.6 In addition, Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD), caused by mutations in genes such as TCF4 and COL8A2, affects around 4%–5% of individuals over 40 years old.7 Congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED), a severe autosomal recessive disorder caused by SLC4A11 mutations, presents with corneal edema at birth and leads to profound visual impairment.8 In this study, we applied adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy to treat MCD, FECD, and CHED mouse models by targeting CECs.

Intracameral injection has been developed to deliver particles or drugs to corneal endothelium,9,10 but its targeting specificity for CECs and post-injection safety remains insufficiently evaluated. The corneal endothelium shares a neural crest origin with adjacent tissues, including the corneal stroma, iris, and trabecular meshwork,11,12 and is continuously exposed to aqueous humor flow, resulting in low transduction efficiency and increased off-target risk. In practice, intracameral injection has been more commonly employed in studies targeting the trabecular meshwork,13,14 rather than corneal endothelial therapy.15 These challenges highlight the urgent need to improve targeting specificity for improved CEC-specific targeting and rigorous safety and efficacy assessment of gene therapy strategies.

In this study, we developed an optimized intracameral AAV delivery strategy to achieve efficient, specific, and safe gene transfer to CECs in vivo. By refining the injection approach and systematically screening AAV serotypes, we established a corneal endothelium-targeted gene therapy platform. Using this strategy, we evaluated gene supplementation in multiple inherited corneal endothelial dystrophy models. This work provides a unified therapeutic framework for corneal endothelial gene therapy and highlights the critical role of endothelial dysfunction in corneal opacity and edema, offering translational potential to reduce reliance on corneal transplantation.

Results

Optimization of intracameral injection and observation methods for corneal endothelial gene therapy

The commonly used intracameral injection (traditional anterior chamber [AC] injection) in previous mouse studies requires needle entry at the limbus and the formation of a relatively long tunnel along the corneal stroma to minimize post-injection leakage (Figures 1A and 1B ).13,15,16 The limited anterior chamber volume restricts the total injection amount, and transient intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation is inevitable during the procedure. Traditional intracameral injection can lead to corneal edema, manifested as increased corneal thickness and opacity on the anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging (Figure 1B), decreased anterior chamber depth (ACD) (Figure 1D), corneal opacity (Figure 1E), increased central corneal thickness (CCT) (Figure 1F), and IOP (Figure 1G). To avoid these limitations, we explored an improved intracameral injection route (improved AC injection) (Figure 1A), in which the needle enters behind the iris and reaches the center of the anterior chamber without touching the cornea,17,18 which effectively avoided corneal edema (Figure 1C). The average ACD without intracameral injection is 422.3 μm, while after traditional injection, the ACD was 298.5 μm and after improved injection, 521.4 μm (Figure 1D), which increases the injected AAV volume for better contact with CECs and reduces the dilution effect of aqueous humor on viral concentration. Slit lamp photography indicated there was no visible corneal opacity after the improved injection (Figure 1E). Furthermore, the improved injection method effectively reduced post-injection CCT (Figure 1F) and successfully maintained IOP at normal baseline levels (Figure 1G), therefore avoiding potential intraocular tissue damage caused by IOP elevation.

Most importantly, during the traditional intracameral injection, there is a high risk of AAV leakage into the corneal stroma along the puncture channel. As was shown in Figure 1H, 2 weeks after the injection of 2 × 109 vector genomes (vg) AAV9-GFP, almost all visible GFP signals were distributed in the corneal stromal region, while the improved injection dramatically inhibited the stromal spreading of AAV9-GFP in the central cornea (Figure 1I). However, there were still GFP signals in the peripheral cornea (Figure 1I), indicating that besides injection, other factors also attributed to the leakage of AAVs from the anterior chamber into the corneal stroma. The circulating aqueous humor is continuously produced by the ciliary body and drains through the trabecular meshwork adjacent to the cornea, playing a role in corneal nourishing. We hypothesized that AAVs leaked to the corneal stroma from the anterior chamber along with the aqueous humor through the trabecular meshwork. To test the hypothesis, we applied timolol, a glaucoma medication that can reduce the outflow of aqueous humor. As expected, timolol dramatically decreased AAV9-GFP leakage to the peripheral corneal stroma (Figures S1A and S1B). We further injected sodium hyaluronate (Healon), a gel formula used during cataract surgery, into the anterior chamber to block the trabecular meshwork thoroughly. The physical blockage profoundly diminished AAV9-GFP signal in the peripheral cornea (Figures S1A and S1B). These results suggest that the off-target effects of intracameral injection of AAVs are caused by both corneal puncture and aqueous humor outflow. Therefore, to develop corneal endothelial-specific gene therapy, improvements in both the injection method and the AAV tropism are essential.

As for observation methods, corneal sections were commonly used for AAV transduction efficiency observation in previous studies. However, we identified that false-positive autoimmune fluorescence was frequently encountered. When the fluorescence intensity is too strong, green fluorescence could be observed in wild-type (WT) mouse corneal sections even without AAV-GFP transduction (Figure S2A), which was avoidable in the whole-mount corneal sample with 3D confocal scanning (Figure S2B). Therefore, we applied 3D corneal whole-mount scanning during our study for signal detection.

AAV6 specifically transduces corneal endothelial cells with resistance to aqueous humor flow

AAV tropism mainly depends on the target cell-surface receptors. Corneal endothelium is specifically abundant in α2,3 N-linked sialic acid.19 Sialic acid has been shown to influence the binding affinity of AAVs, especially for AAV6, which has a high tropism for sialylated receptors.20,21 Therefore, we examined the transduction efficiency of AAV6 together with three commonly used ocular AAV serotypes (AAV2, AAV8, and AAV9) (Figure 2A). All vectors carry a GFP signal for visualization. Two weeks after improved intracameral injection of 2 × 109 vg AAVs of different serotypes, AAV6 showed the highest transduction efficiency (Figure 2A) and the GFP was only restricted in the endothelial layer (red ZO-1 positive) (Figure 2A). No visible AAV2-GFP signal was observed in the cornea, consistent with its neuronal tropism in treating retinal diseases (Figure 2A). AAV8 and AAV9 displayed sparse transduction to CECs, but their presence was also spotted in the corneal stroma, especially in the peripheral corneal area (Figure 2A). Quantitative analysis of GFP-positive area and fluorescence intensity showed that AAV6 covered approximately 91.3% of the total corneal area, whereas AAV8 and AAV9 covered only 27.7% and 19.9%, respectively (Figure 2B). The GFP fluorescence intensity of AAV6 (1,265.1 × 106 a.u.) was also significantly higher than that of AAV8 (659.8 × 106 a.u.) and AAV9 (301.8 × 106 a.u.) We further examined whether there were off-target effects after improved intracameral injection of AAV6-GFP. The whole eyeball section was scanned for GFP signals. GFP was enriched in the corneal endothelial layer and visually absent in the other major tissues (Figure 2C), indicating a good endothelial specificity of our gene therapy system. Examination of the peripheral cornea showed that AAV6-GFP was strictly confined to the endothelial cell layer, with no transduction observed in stromal cells (Figure S3), indicating that AAV6 affinity to the endothelium has resistance to the aqueous humor flow. Thus, we selected AAV6 as our delivery vector for endothelium-targeting gene therapy.

Dose optimization for AAV6 particle injection

To determine the optimal dose for AAV6-mediated corneal endothelial gene therapy, we systemically examined the safety and target gene expression after intracameral injection of AAV6 particles. A gradient of AAV6-GFP ranging from 107 to 109 vg was injected, and the corneal status was observed by slit lamp and OCT after 1 month. There was no difference among different dosage groups in corneal transparency (Figure 3A). Significant corneal edema was observed in the 109 group in OCT (Figure 3A), with the CCT increasing from 107.9 to 117.0 μm (Figure 3B). Both the 108 and 109 groups displayed homogeneous GFP distribution throughout the whole endothelium (Figures 3C and 3D). The GFP-positive area was only 36.6% in the 107 group, whereas it reached 88.7% and 90.0% in the 108 and 109 groups, respectively (Figure 3D). Corneal endothelium is a single layer of hexagonal cells that are vulnerable to anterior chamber disturbance.22,23 To check whether intracameral injection of virus can cause damage to endothelial cells, we used alizarin red S staining of the endothelium and analyzed the deformation rate with Voronoi matrix using FIJI software with the BioVoxxel Toolbox.24 Surprisingly, 109 vg AAV6 carrying Chst5 (AAV-Chst5) caused severe corneal endothelial deformation, resembling the clinical manifestations of viral corneal endotheliitis (Figure 3E). In contrast, corneal endothelium receiving 107 and 108 vg AAV-Chst5 still kept regular morphology, similar to the WT corneal endothelium (Figure 3E). Quantitative analysis of CEC morphology revealed that hexagonal cells were the most abundant in all groups and the distribution of other polygonal cell types followed a normal pattern (Figure 3F). A cross-group comparison showed that in the 109 vg AAV group, the proportion of hexagonal cells was significantly reduced (Figure 3G). Further quantification of total cell counts under high-magnification fields also demonstrated a marked decrease in the 109 vg AAV group compared to the other groups, while no significant differences were observed among the WT, 107, and 108 vg AAV groups (Figure 3H).

We further examined the target gene expression using this gene therapy system. Gene expression of Chst5, the mouse version of MCD disease-causing gene, was quantified after intracameral injection of 107 to 109 vg AAV-Chst5. Chst5 increased by 14.5-fold in the 109 group and 13.7-fold in the 108 group 1 month after injection, and there was no significant difference between these two groups (Figure 3I). Similarly, microscale western blot analysis of the mouse corneal endothelium displayed more intense protein bands in the 108 and 109 vg groups (Figure 3J). Quantitative analysis of band intensity showed that CHST5 protein increased by 2.7-fold in the 109 group and 2.5-fold in the 108 group, with no significant difference between the two groups (Figure 3K). Therefore, we selected 108 vg AAV particles as the intracameral injection dose for subsequent experiments.

Long-lasting AAV6 transduction of corneal endothelium for at least 18 months

To determine the expression duration of the exogenous gene mediated by a single intracameral injection of 2 × 108 vg AAV6, we conducted an 18-month follow-up after AAV6-GFP administration. In the first week, weak but evenly distributed GFP signals were detected across the corneal endothelium. By the second week, GFP expression significantly increased, reaching a stable and high expression level around the first month and peaking at the sixth month. Although the GFP intensity began to decline slightly after the ninth month, the decrease was gradual, and a relatively high expression level was still maintained at the fifteenth month, with detectable signals persisting at the eighteenth month. Local 3D side and oblique views consistently showed that GFP expression remained confined to the corneal endothelium, with no GFP signals observed in the corneal stroma, further confirming the long-term specificity of intracamerally delivered AAV6 for CECs (Figure 3L).

Additionally, we performed quantitative analysis of GFP-positive area and fluorescence intensity across the whole cornea (whole mount) and in more amplified regions (local) at different time points post-injection. By the second week, the GFP-positive area of the whole cornea reached 88.1%, peaked at 97.0% by the sixth month, and remained 50.1% at the fifteenth month, decreasing to 25.7% by the eighteenth month (Figure 3M). In magnified local regions, the GFP-positive area reached 71.6% as early as the first week, peaked at 95.4% by the sixth month, and was still maintained at a relatively high level of 54.3% at the fifteenth month, with a noticeable drop to 31% by the eighteenth month (Figure 3N). The trend in GFP fluorescence intensity mirrored the changes in GFP-positive area.

For the target gene expression duration, Chst5 remained markedly elevated in the corneal endothelium compared with the control group (2.9-fold) 10 months after intracameral injection of AAV-Chst5 (Figure S4A). To decide whether the reduction in GFP and target gene expression is due to the neutralizing antibody, we collected aqueous humor, cornea, and serum of mice that received a high dose (2 × 109 vg) of AAV6-GFP to measure the antibody. No apparent increase in AAV6-neutralizing antibodies was detected in any of these tissues, comparable to those of the negative controls and substantially lower than those of the positive controls (Figure S4B). In addition, we performed a second intracameral injection 1 month after the initial administration and assessed endothelial GFP 1 month later. GFP expression in the corneal endothelium was markedly stronger after the second injection than after the single injection (Figure S4C), demonstrating that repeated intracameral administration is well tolerated and remains effective.

Safety profiling of AAV6 intracameral injection

To evaluate the safety of intracameral AAV6 delivery, we further examined corneal immune responses following AAV injection in WT mice with multiple methods (Figure S5). The detailed gating strategy for flow cytometry is provided in Figure S6A, along with results from blank controls, two isotype controls, and a positive control (Figure S6B). The positive control was set as WT mice receiving corneal tissue 1 week after intracameral injection of undiluted 2 × 1010 vg AAV-Chst5, which showed a clear CD45+ clustering (Figure S6B). In our previous study, we performed flow cytometry following corneal stromal injection of AAV particles in mice and assessed CD45+ leukocytes, CD11b+ myeloid cells, F4/80+ macrophages, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD19+ B cells. Only mild increase in CD45+ and CD11b+ populations but no other immune cells was observed.17 Therefore, we evaluated changes in CD45+ leukocytes, CD4+ T cells, and Ly6g+ granulocytes in this study. One week after a single intracameral injection of 2 × 108 vg AAV-Chst5, there were no increases in total CD45+ leukocytes, CD4+ T cells, or Ly6g+ granulocytes in the cornea compared to untreated WT controls (Figures 4A and 4B ). However, at 1-month post-injection, mild increases in CD45+ leukocytes (1.6-fold), CD4+ T cells (1.9-fold), and Ly6g+ granulocytes (2.1-fold) relative to WT controls were observed (Figures 4C and 4D). To obtain more comprehensive immune cell profiling after gene therapy, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with corneas 3 months after injection. A total of 210,661 cells were obtained, with 49,352, 50,908, 56,111, and 54,290 cells originating from the WT, R50H, R50H receiving AAV-Con, and R50H receiving AAV-Chst5 groups, respectively. Twenty cell clusters were detected and defined to eight cell types, Cluster_18 was defined as immune cells, including T cells and myeloid cells, according to the expression of H2-Ab1, C1qa, Cd74, Cd3d, Cd3e, and Cd3g (Figures S7 and S8). The extracted immune cell subsets were further distinctly categorized into myeloid and T cell subgroups (Figure 4G). Their top 10 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) clearly defined their distinct cellular identities (Figure 4H). At 3 months after injection, T cells were still enriched in R50H mice receiving injections, with 0.39% in AAV-Con group and 0.21% in AAV-Chst5 group, both higher than the WT (0.1%) and R50H mice (0.09%) receiving no injection (Figure 4I). In contrast, myeloid cells were resolved in AAV-Chst5 mice, with the cell proportion (0.2%) the same as WT mice (Figure 4I). Interestingly, R50H mice receiving AAV-Chst5 injection had lower corneal immune cells than those receiving AAV-Con injection for both immune cells (Figure 4I), suggesting that Chst5 supplementation refined corneal environment.

Furthermore, we validated the immune cells with immunostaining in corneal sections. CD45+ leukocyte staining on corneal cryosections from the WT, R50H, R50H mice receiving AAV-Con injection and R50H mice receiving AAV-Chst5 also showed no significant differences (Figure 4J). Quantitative analysis of CD45+ cell counts per high-power field across the four groups confirmed the absence of statistically significant differences (Figure 4K).

To determine whether intracameral AAV administration leads to systemic adverse effects, we collected major organs from WT mice 1 month after receiving 2 × 109 vg intracameral injection of AAV-GFP. Heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain were collected for off-target GFP screening. No visible GFP signal was detected (Figure S9A), indicating there was no off-target transduction in major organs. We further conducted histological staining of these organs to assess possible in vivo toxicity. No structural abnormalities were observed compared with WT mice (Figure S9B), suggesting that doses up to 2 × 109 vg of AAV particles are well tolerated. For the circulatory system, we performed PCR analysis to detect AAV genomes in the blood. No AAV genomes were detected in the blood and liver samples by PCR (Figures S9C and S9D), suggesting no detectable off-target AAV entry into the bloodstream or liver.

Therapeutic effects of AAV-Chst5 in CHST5 R50H mice at different ages

The R50H mutant mice develop subepithelial abnormal deposits in the central-to-paracentral cornea during early postnatal development, typically beginning at 6–7 weeks of age, which mimics the disease onset of MCD in humans, where clinical symptoms often manifest around the age of 10.6,25 The mice also present typical clinical manifestations of MCD, including diffuse and uneven corneal haze and deposits (Figure 5A; Figure S10A). The range and density of corneal opacity progressively worsen with age without treatment (Figure 5B). Since adult MCD mice already exhibit corneal opacification, we further tested whether AAV-Chst5 gene therapy could prevent disease onset. MCD symptoms in human patients usually appear in their teenage; we then administered 2 × 108 vg AAV-Chst5 via intracameral injection to 3- to 4-week-old R50H mice before visible corneal clouding and monitored disease progression for over 12 months. In the AAV-Con group, 95% of eyes developed progressive subepithelial opacities, while approximately 90% of eyes in the AAV-Chst5 group maintained transparent corneas with no abnormal deposits (Figures 5F and 5G). Alcian blue showed no obvious staining in the AAV-Chst5 group, in contrast to pan-stromal staining in the AAV-Con group, with statistically significant differences (Figures 5H and 5I). Moreover, KS content in the AAV-Chst5 group (4,245.1 ng/mg) was significantly higher than in the control group (113.9 ng/mg), and comparable to WT levels (4,565.0 ng/mg) (Figure 5J).

Since the IC3D classification system categorizes MCD as a stromal dystrophy,29 we also tested AAV-Chst5 gene therapy targeting the corneal stroma. In adult R50H mice with existing opacities, we performed intrastromal injection of AAV-Chst5. However, over a 4-week observation period, both treated and control groups exhibited progression of stromal clouding (Figure S11A), with no significant difference in corneal opacity areas between groups (Figure S11B). These results further support our previous findings that CECs play a key role in the onset and progression of MCD.

AAV-Chst5 improves corneal endothelial cell function by upregulating Wnt5a expression

To further investigate the reason that upregulating Chst5 in corneal endothelium can rescue MCD phenotypes, we analyzed scRNA-seq for endothelial genes that were decreased in R50H receiving no treatment or AAV-Con treatment but whose expression was restored to WT levels after receiving AAV-Chst5. After extracting the endothelial cell subsets, the expression distribution of endothelial marker genes (Col8a1, Slc4a1, and Aqp1) confirmed their cellular identities (Figures 6A and 6B). Intracameral injection of AAV-Chst5 successfully restored endothelial Chst5 protein expression levels in R50H mice (Figure S12A). The gene Wnt5a was identified follows the pattern of our selection, that it was lower in R50H and AAV-Con groups but higher in WT and AAV-Chst5 groups in top AAV-Chst5 DEGs (Figures 6C and 6D). Consistently, immunostaining showed upregulated WNT5A following AAV-Chst5 treatment (Figure S12B) without detectable alterations in endothelial cell morphology (Figure S12C). To examine the correlation between Chst5 and Wnt5a at the single-cell level, we further observed their co-expression heatmap, which revealed a significant co-expression between Chst5 and Wnt5a (Figure 6E).

To investigate the role of Wnt5a in CECs, we performed intracameral injection of AAV6 carrying Wnt5a short hairpin RNA in WT mice (Wnt5a knockdown [KD]), which resulted in an 84.1% KD of Wnt5a mRNA in CECs (Figure 6F). KD endothelial Wnt5a did not cause obvious corneal opacity within 1 month (Figure 6G) but increased the deformation rate of CECs (Figure 6H). Quantitative analysis of CEC morphology revealed that hexagonal cells were the dominant cell types in both controls and Wnt5a KD endothelium (Figure 6I). However, the proportion of hexagonal cells was significantly reduced in the Wnt5a KD group (Figure 6J) and quantification of total cell count under high-magnification fields also demonstrated a marked decrease compared to the controls (Figure 6K). We further developed a mouse model that mimics clinical corneal endothelial decompensation by introducing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress to corneal endothelium. ER stress has been widely applied to assess the function of CECs.30 Three days after ER stress induction, both the control and Wnt5a KD mice developed significant corneal stromal edema (Figure 6L), indicating substantial impairment of endothelial cell function. While edema in the control group resolved by day 5, the Wnt5a KD mice continued to exhibit pronounced stromal edema (Figure 6L), with the central cornea significantly thickened compared to the controls (Figure 6M). Loss of Wnt5a led to severe endothelial cell damage after ER stress, characterized by disorganized cell arrangement, enlarged cell size, and guttae-like deposits (Figure 6N). In contrast, the control group exhibited only mild damage after ER stress (Figure 6N). We further overexpressed Wnt5a using AAV6 in the neonatal R50H mice. Over a 3-month observation period, AAV6-Wnt5a prevented the development of corneal opacity in 85.7% of treated eyes, whereas only 7.1% of eyes in the AAV-Con group kept transparency (Figure 6O and 6P). These results indicate that Wnt5a acts as a downstream effector of Chst5 and plays an important role in maintaining corneal endothelium physiological function.

The CHED mouse model was established by knockout of the gene Slc4a11. Slc4a11 is a gene essential for maintaining intracellular and extracellular ion balance and osmotic homeostasis. A single intracameral injection of AAV-Slc4a11 significantly alleviated corneal edema from 162.4 ± 12.9 to 120.8 ± 12.7 μm in CHED mice within 1 week, and the corneal thickness remained close to the normal level throughout the 8-week observation period (114.8 ± 9.6 μm) (Figures 7K and 7L).

In addition, we validated the therapeutic efficacy of AAV6-Slc4a11 in an ultraviolet (UV)-induced corneal endothelial injury model in WT mice. In this model, corneal UV irradiation (500 J/cm2 for 20 min) induces pronounced corneal edema, which in control mice resolves slowly and typically requires approximately 7 days to return to normal corneal thickness. By contrast, mice pretreated with a single intracameral injection of AAV-Slc4a11 one week prior to UV exposure exhibited markedly accelerated recovery, with corneal thickness returning to near-normal levels by day 3 post-irradiation and remaining stable thereafter (Figure S13). These results suggest that our system can be applicable to a broad range of corneal endothelial disorders and holds great potential for clinical translation.

Discussion

CECs are central to corneal transparency and metabolic homeostasis, and endothelial dysfunction remains a major challenge in clinical diagnosis and treatment.34,35,36 Although penetrating keratoplasty and endothelial keratoplasty are the preferred therapeutic strategies, their clinical impact is constrained by donor shortages, surgical complexity, immune rejection, and limited graft longevity.37 Alternative approaches have shown promise, including endothelial cell regeneration,38,39 cell transplantation,40,41 and tissue-engineered endothelium.42,43 However, these attempts still face limitations, such as difficulties in achieving functional integration, the limited proliferative capacity of CECs, and challenges in large-scale clinical translation. The extensive application of AAV-based and non-AAV-based gene therapy in ocular diseases has already provided strong evidence for its safety and long-term efficacy,44,45,46,47 while gene therapy specifically targeting CECs remains largely unexplored.

The lack of an effective anterior chamber delivery method has been a major barrier to endothelial gene therapy. Traditional intracameral injection in mice requires creating a long puncture tunnel from the limbus toward the central cornea to reduce aqueous humor leakage.48,49,50 Given the limited volume of the mouse anterior chamber, injection of therapeutic agent often causes elevated IOP, corneal edema, aqueous humor and drug leakage, and uneven distribution of the drug, thereby compromising experimental reproducibility. Our improved intracameral injection strategy overcomes these limitations by puncturing posterior to the limbus with an 11-0 suture needle, avoiding stromal tunneling and enabling controlled aqueous humor release to accommodate drug delivery without inducing IOP surges. Optimized needle orientation and multi-point administration further promote homogeneous AAV distribution and efficient endothelial transduction, while conjunctival coverage after withdrawal minimizes leakage and supports wound healing. Collectively, this refined approach provides a more reliable and broadly applicable method for anterior chamber gene delivery.

We found that AAV6 has high specificity for CECs, displaying strong resistance to the aqueous humor flow, with no visible off-target in whole-eye sections. As AAV vectors persist as episomes, degradation and metabolic clearance of the viral genomes occur over time. Depending on the cell type, transgene expression typically lasts from several months to several years. Although studies on AAV targeting CECs are limited, AAV-mediated transduction targeting retinal cells has been shown to support exogenous GFP expression for approximately 1 year in rat.51 Clinical trials of AAV-based gene therapy targeting the retina have also demonstrated that therapeutic effects can remain stable over observation periods lasting several months to several years.52 In addition, CECs exhibit relatively high autophagic activity,53,54 which may contribute to AAV degradation through autophagy-related pathways. Moreover, mouse CECs are capable of regeneration,55,56 which may also contribute to the gradual reduction of GFP signal.

CECs are limited in number and exhibit high metabolic activity. Our study found that excessive AAV concentrations not only fail to enhance the expression of the exogenous target gene but also may impair the morphology and function of the corneal endothelium. This adverse effect is likely related to the excessive consumption of cellular energy and materials for the synthesis of the exogenous protein, ultimately compromising the endothelial physiological function. Therefore, optimization of AAV dosage is particularly critical.

We evaluated the safety of intracameral AAV injection using flow cytometry, whole-corneal scRNA-seq, and immunostaining. A mild and transient immune response was detected shortly after AAV-Chst5 injection and largely subsided by 3 months, consistent with our previous findings following stromal AAV injection.17 Notably, scRNA-seq at 3 months revealed a sustained low-level immune response in the AAV-Con group, likely due to continuous GFP expression, whereas this response was attenuated in the AAV-Chst5 group, possibly reflecting an improved corneal microenvironment, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Our previous research identified the critical role of CECs in the pathogenesis of MCD.6 Indeed, targeting endothelium yielded promising therapeutic outcomes, while the same AAV-Chst5 particles targeting the corneal stroma appeared to accelerate the accumulation of corneal opacities. Furthermore, we observed that the treatment effect was more pronounced in neonatal mice before corneal opacity onset, compared to adult mice that had already developed opacity. In practice, a large proportion of patients with corneal dystrophy seek medical attention only when the disease significantly affects visual acuity. As a result, early preventive gene therapy is challenging to implement in clinical practice, highlighting the importance of regular ophthalmic examinations and standardized genetic evaluations.

scRNA-seq revealed that the Wnt5a signaling pathway downstream of the Chst5 gene may be a critical target involved in pathological corneal deposition. Previous studies have shown that Wnt5a plays a role in promoting corneal epithelial wound healing and restoring normal stem cell function in the corneal epithelium.57 In the corneal endothelium, interleukin-1β-induced activation of the Wnt5a signaling pathway has been reported to promote endothelial cell migration.58 In other tissues, Wnt5a has been shown to regulate AQP2 expression via the calcineurin pathway in renal cortical collecting duct cells to support kidney pump function,59 and to stabilize endothelial tight junctions in brain microvascular endothelial cells through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.60 In our study, Wnt5a KD in WT mice resulted in marked corneal edema under stress, suggesting it may be involved in the pump function of CECs. Overexpression of Wnt5a avoided early corneal opacity in neonatal MCD mice. Both observations are consistent with the reported mechanism in other tissues.

In this study we identified that AAV6 could effectively transduce corneal endothelium with resistance to flowing aqueous humor and displayed exciting therapeutic effects in MCD, FECD, CHED, and UV-induced endothelial dysfunction models. Our study paved the way for the development of gene therapy for corneal endothelial diseases.

Limitations of the study

Although we identified AAV serotypes with strong tropism for CECs and observed no obvious off-target transduction following intracameral delivery, this study did not incorporate a corneal endothelial-specific promoter. This limitation reflects the current lack of well-characterized promoters that are both highly specific and broadly active in CECs based on available transcriptomic datasets. Future work will focus on systematic identification and validation of endothelial-specific promoters to further enhance the precision and safety of AAV-mediated gene delivery.

In addition, we observed sustained transgene expression in mouse corneal endothelium for up to 18 weeks following a single administration. While prolonged expression is likely appropriate for hereditary corneal disorders, such durability may not be optimal for certain non-genetic or reversible endothelial pathologies. In these contexts, gene delivery systems with temporal control of transgene expression may be more suitable and warrant further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were procured from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). CHST5 R50H mice were generated and bred by Viewsolid Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) as previously described.6 COL8A2 Q455K mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock #029749, Bar Harbor, USA).61 The point-mutant mice were backcrossed for at least 10 generations with C57BL/6J mice. Slc4a11 knockout mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). The genomic status of animals was identified with genotyping and littermates were used as controls.

Animal ethics approval for this study was granted by the Animal Investigation Committee of the Eye Institute, Shandong First Medical University, under the approval number SDSYKYJS No.202201209. All animals were reared in the animal facility under controlled environmental conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 1°C, 50 ± 10% humidity) with ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and water. The procedures were performed in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO).

Human samples

MCD corneas and healthy donor corneas were obtained from the Eye Bank of Qingdao Eye Hospital. Five corneas were collected for each group. One cornea per group was used for Alcian blue staining and the remaining four corneas were used for keratan sulfate quantification. This study was conducted under the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Eye Hospital (2022-61) with informed consent obtained from the participants.

Method details

AAV construction

The AAVs were generated by OBiO Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). AAVs of different serotypes carrying EGFP (pAAV-CMV-EGFP-3×FLAG-tWPA) were employed to screen for specific transduction of corneal endothelial cells. The AAV6-Chst5 (pAAV6-CMV-Chst5-3×FLAG-P2A-EGFP-tWPA), AAV6-Col8a2 (pAAV6-CMV-Col8a2-3×FLAG-tWPA), AAV6-Slc4a11 (pAAV6-CMV-Slc4a11-3×FLAG-tWPA) and AAV6-Wnt5a (pAAV6-CMV-Wnt5a-3×FLAG-P2A-EGFP-WPRE) contained Chst5 (Mouse, GenBank: NM_019950), Col8a2 (Mouse, GenBank: NM_199473), Slc4a11 (Mouse, GenBank: NM_001081162) and Wnt5a (Mouse, GenBank: NM_009524) under the control of a CMV promoter. The AAV6-shWnt5a (pAAV6-U6-shWnt5a-CMV-EGFP-WPRE) targeted Wnt5a (Mouse, GenBank: NM_001256224) under the control of a U6 promoter.

Traditional intracameral injection and improved intracameral injection of AAV

Mice were anesthetized and received topical 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops. Traditional intracameral injection was preformed according to the previous protocol.49 Using a Hamilton 36-gauge needle connected to a 10 μL syringe, with the bevel of the needle facing downward. The needle was inserted at the limbus and advanced through a tunnel approximately 2 mm long within the corneal stroma until reaching the central corneal area, then punctured downward into the anterior chamber. 2 μL of PBS containing sodium fluorescein or 2×109 vg of AAV was injected without aqueous humor release. After withdrawing the needle, the central cornea was gently compressed with a cotton swab for 1 min to prevent leakage.

For improved intracameral injection,17 the 11-0 suture needle was first used to create a puncture port 1 mm posterior to the corneal limbus. The posterior edge of the puncture port was gently pressed to release a small amount of aqueous humor (about 1.5–2 μL) to lower the intraocular pressure. A 36-gauge Hamilton microinjection needle attached to a 10 μL syringe was used to enter the incision, pass behind the iris, reach the center of the anterior chamber and 2 μL PBS containing sodium fluorescein, AAV of 2×107, 2×108 or 2×109 vg solution was injected. After withdrawing the needle, the local conjunctiva retracted to cover the puncture area, and no obvious aqueous humor leakage was observed. Tobramycin and dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment was applied to prevent infection.

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Mouse corneal OCT was performed with an RTVue XR OCT Imaging System (Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). The mouse cornea was placed toward the camera lens and scanned under the Pachymetry pattern. CCT was measured with inherent program of the machine.

Detection of GFP expression in cornea

Mouse corneas were harvested at different timepoint (1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month, 2 months, 3 months, 4 months, 6 months, 9 months, and 12 months) post AC injection. Eyeballs were punctured at the limbus using a 1 mL syringe and fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde Fixative Solution (BL539A, Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 20 min at 4°C. Corneas were circumferentially dissected at the scleral-limbal junction, washed three times with PBS, and divided into 4 radial segments. GFP signals were visualized using a Zeiss LSM 880 laser confocal microscope (Zeiss, Rossdorf, Germany) at different magnifications.

GFP-positive area and fluorescence intensity were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Two independent investigators, blinded to treatment groups, performed measurements to reduce inter-operator variability.

Corneal immunofluorescent staining

For the immunostaining of ZO-1 of corneal endothelium, eyeballs were fixed in Zamboni Fixative for 10 min at 4°C and corneas were circumferentially dissected. After washed 3 times with PBS, Then the corneas were incubated with ZO-1 antibody (1:200, ab221547, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 10% donkey serum and 0.1% Triton X-100 overnight at 4°C. After 3× PBS washes, corneas were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, ab150076, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 10% donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature. Following 3× PBS rinses, corneas were stained with DAPI solution (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 min. Corneal flat mounts were visualized with the confocal microscope.

For the immunostaining of CHST5 and WNT5A, corneas were fixed with Zamboni fixative for 10 min at 4°C and washed 3 times with PBS, followed by blocking in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 2% goat serum, and 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 day and subsequently incubated with Goat Anti-Chst5 Antibody (EB09182, Absolute Biotech, Boston, USA) or Rabbit WNT5A/B Polyclonal Antibody (55184-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Rosemont, USA) overnight at 4°C. After 3× PBS washes, corneas were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (1:400, ab150132, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, ab150076, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 10% donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature. After washing six times, the cornea was cut into 4 petaLs and imaged with the confocal microscope.

For the immunostaining of corneal frozen sections, the sections were fixed with Zamboni fixative for 15 min and washed 3 times with PBS, then incubated in 10% donkey serum to block for 1 h. Then the corneas were incubated with CD45 antibody (1:200, ab10558, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 10% donkey serum overnight at 4°C separately. After washing 3 times with PBS, the Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:400, ab150076, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 10% donkey serum was incubated with the corneas at room temperature for 2 h. After washing 3 times, the corneas were stained with DAPI solution (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 s. The corneal sections were examined with the confocal microscope.

Neutralizing antibody assay

One month after intracameral injection of a high dose (2 × 109 vg) AAV6-GFP, aqueous humor (20 eyes), corneal tissues (20 eyes), and serum (10 mice) were collected as test samples. Corresponding tissues from WT mice were set as controls. Serum collected from mice one month after intravenous injection of AAV6 capsid protein was used as the positive control, and serum from mice that had never been exposed to AAV was used as the negative control.

Neutralizing antibody levels against AAV6 were measured using a cell infection inhibition assay. Samples were serially diluted in 3.16-fold steps (3.16, 10, 31.6, 100, 316, and 1000). Each dilution was tested in triplicate wells. Test samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min in a water bath. Diluted samples were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with AAV6 virus solution (MOI = 5000) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of the virus-sample mixture was added to each well containing attached 293T cells with 180 μL medium. Luminescence was measured using the Bright-Lite Luciferase Assay (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and relative light units (RLU) were detected using an MD-iD3 multimode microplate reader. Inhibition rates of viral transduction were calculated by comparison with virus-only positive control wells. The transduction inhibition (TI) rate was determined at each dilution, and the dilution range corresponding to 50% inhibition was defined as the NT50.

Alizarin red S staining for corneal endothelium and analysis

The isolated murine cornea was positioned with the endothelial surface uppermost and following three successive PBS washes. Then the tissue was counterstained with 1% alizarin red S solution (G1083, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 90 s at room temperature. After thorough PBS rinsing, the corneal specimen was carefully flattened through four radial equidistant incisions along the limbal margin. The prepared tissue was then mounted endothelial side upward on a glass slide and immediately examined under a light microscope (DM4B, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with digital imaging capabilities.

The quantitative analysis of corneal endothelial cells was conducted in accordance with standardized protocols established in peer-reviewed ophthalmic research.24 Image Processing and Quantitative Analysis Digital images were processed using FIJI software (v2.3.0/1.53q; NIH) with the BioVoxxel Toolbox extension (v3.0.1; http://imagej.net/BioVoxxel_Toolbox). Cellular neighbors were systematically identified using the “Neighbor Analysis” module (BioVoxxel Toolbox), which automatically detected contiguous cell boundaries and assigned adjacency values. This algorithm generated a comprehensive dataset including total cell density and precise neighbor counts per endothelial cell. A comparative analysis was carried out for both the quantity of polygonal cells and the proportion of hexagonal cells per high-power field (HPF) among different groups.

RNA extraction, qPCR and genomic DNA detection

Chst5 mRNA expression levels were analyzed with mouse corneal endothelium at 1 month after intracameral injection of 107, 108 and 109 vg AAV-Chst5. Wnt5a mRNA expression levels were analyzed with mouse corneal endothelium at 1 month after intracameral injection of 108 vg AAV-shWnt5a. The endothelial cell layer was carefully dissected from murine corneas using microforceps, RNAs were extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (ER501, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The expression of mouse Chst5 and Wnt5a was determined by qPCR. Gapdh was used as the internal control. qPCR was performed on Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR Systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA).

Genomic DNA from blood and liver tissues was isolated using the DNeasyr Blood & Tissue Kit (No. 69504, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), and the target fragments were amplified with GoldenStar T6 Super PCR Mix (No. TSE102, Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The amplification products were subsequently analyzed by electrophoresis to detect the AAV genomes. The primer sequences were as follows:

Gapdh-F: 5′-GGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTGG-3’;

Gapdh-R: 5′-CCGTGAGTGGAGTCATACTGGAA-3’;

Chst5-F: 5′-GCCAGCTCTTCAGCCAACAC-3’;

Chst5-R: 5′-ATACGTCCATGTCGCATAGGAA-3’;

Wnt5a-F: 5′-GCCATGTCTTCCAAGTTCTTCCT-3’;

Wnt5a-R: 5′-GCAGAGAGGCTGTGCACCTAT-3’;

AAV6 genome-F: 5′-TTTATGAGGAGTTGTGGCCCGT-3’;

AAV6 genome-R: 5′-CCATGGAAAGGACGTCAGCTT-3’.

Corneal endothelium simple western assay (WES)

Two endothelial cell layers were carefully dissected from mouse corneas using forceps and lysed by incubation in 10 μL RIPA lysis buffer (P0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (P0100, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The samples were vortexed briefly and then incubated on ice for 30 min and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Capillary western blot analysis was performed using the ProteinSimple WES automatic system and in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (WS-3098, Biotechne, Santa Clara, USA). Endothelium protein samples (3.6 μL) were mixed with the 5× Fluorescent Master Mix (0.9 μL) and heated at 95°C for 5min. The ladder, blocking reagent, Chst5 antibody (EB09182, Absolute Biotech, Boston, USA), streptavidin-HPR, HPR-conjugated anti-goat secondary antibodies, Lumino-S, and Peroxide were added to designated wells in an assay plate. The prepared assay plate was placed into the WES instrument, followed by the insertion of capillaries. The bands were quantified detected for three times. The western blot images were generated and quantified using Compass software after normalization by β-actin antibody (ab179467, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Alcian blue staining

The Alcian blue staining was conducted with Standard Alcian Blue staining solution (G1560, Solarbio, Beijing, China) prepared according to the standard operating procedure. Paraffin sections (4 μm thickness) were fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde Fixative Solution for 5 min, followed by rinsing in 3% Acidified solution for 3 min. The sections were subsequently stained with 1% Alcian blue solution for 30 min, washed with 3% acetic acid to remove excess dye, and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red for 5 min. After dehydration in 95% ethanol, the slides were mounted for microscopic observation.

Keratan sulfate (KS) quantification

We employed a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method based on multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to quantitatively analyze KS in biological samples, targeting three disaccharides with different sulfation degrees.28 First, LC separated KS disaccharides by sulfation level. Then, MRM mode MS/MS selectively monitored specific precursor-product ion pairs for each disaccharide, with precursor ions fragmented in the collision cell to generate characteristic product ions for quantification, ensuring high selectivity and sensitivity. To reduce matrix interference, samples were pretreated by delipidation, deproteinization, glycan enrichment, and enzymatic digestion. The extracted KS disaccharides were derivatized with 2-aminoacridone (2-AMAC) to enhance ionization efficiency and chromatographic resolution.

For corneal tissue samples, they were homogenized and treated with acetone (10 volumes, 30 min sonication, followed by 5 additional volumes and another 30 min sonication). After centrifugation, the pellet was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in water, and digested with Pronase at 50°C overnight. The reaction was terminated by heating at 100°C for 10 min. The supernatant was precipitated with ethanol at −20°C overnight. The resulting pellet was reconstituted in a buffer with Keratanase II and an internal standard, digested at 37°C overnight, inactivated by heat, centrifuged, and the supernatant was lyophilized. The lyophilized sample was derivatized with 20 μL of 0.05 M 2-AMAC (DMSO-acetic acid, 17:3, v/v) for 30 min at room temperature, then reacted with an equal volume of 0.5 M sodium cyanoborohydride at 45°C in the dark for 4.5 h. After high-speed centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to an LC vial for analysis.

Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Thermo UltiMate 3000 LC system using a Phenomenex Luna Omega Polar C18 column. The mobile phases were 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate with 0.03% acetic acid (pH 6.0, MPA) and methanol (MPB). The gradient elution program was set as described in the original text with a constant flow rate of 120 μL/min. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a TSQ Quantiva triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in negative ion mode. Ionization parameters were optimized using standard solutions, and Xcalibur 4.2 software was used for instrument control and data acquisition.

Flow cytometry

Corneas were harvested from mice at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post intracameral injection of AAV-Chst5 (2 μL, 1×108 vg/μL). Eyeballs were incubated overnight at 4°C in Dispase II (15 mg/mL, 44390400, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in a 96-well plate. Corneal epithelium and stroma were dissected and subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion: the epithelium was treated with Trypsin (200 μL, 25200-056, Gibco, USA) and the stroma with Collagenase A (2 mg/mL, 48655301, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) through three 10 min incubations at 37°C, with mechanical dissociation via pipetting. Reactions were neutralized with DMEM/F12, and cells were pelleted by centrifugation (500 g × 5 min). After resuspension in PBS, single-cell suspensions were stained with APC anti-mouse CD45 antibody (1:500, 103111, BioLegend, San Diego, USA), APC anti-mouse CD4 antibody (1:500, 100411, Biolegend, San Diego, USA), and APC anti-mouse Ly6g antibody (1:500, 164505, BioLegend, San Diego, USA) for 30 min at 4°C to identify leukocytes, T cells and neutrophils respectively. APC Rat IgG2a, κ Isotype Ctrl Antibody (1:500, 400511, Biolegend, California, USA) and APC Rat IgG2b, κ Isotype Ctrl Antibody (1:500, 400611, Biolegend, California, USA) were used as respective isotype controls. Flow cytometry was performed using a CytoFLEX system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA).

Single-cell RNA transcriptome

Single-cell suspensions of wild-type mice, CHST5 R50H mutant mice, mutant mice treated with AAV-Con and AAV-Chst5 groups were adjusted to 700–1200 cell/μL according to the 10× Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 (1000268, Pleasanton, USA) Operation manual for computer and library construction. The constructed library was sequenced using MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform with a PE150 sequencing strategy. The entire single-cell quality control and analysis pipeline followed the standard procedure of the Seurat package. The quality control criteria were set as follows: nCount_RNA >1000; nFeature_RNA <8000; percent.mt < 20; and percent.rp < 40. The resolution for FindClusters was set to 0.5. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. Data visualization was performed using the ggplot2 and scRNAtoolVis R packages.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-induced corneal endothelial dysfunction

To investigate the role of Wnt5a in ER stress-induced corneal endothelial dysfunction, Wnt5a expression in wild-type mice corneal endothelial cells was knocked down via intracameral injection of AAV6-shWnt5a. 1 month later, ER stress was induced by intracameral injection of 2 μL 0.1 mmol/L ER stress agonist thapsigargin (T9033, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA).30 Mice were subsequently monitored for 10 days, during which corneal morphology was evaluated using slit lamp and OCT. After the observation period, corneal tissues were harvested for alizarin red staining to assess endothelial cell density, morphology, and junctional integrity.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics and GraphPad Prism 9.2.0. All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One way ANOVA was used to evaluate the significance of differences among groups. Tukey’s multiple comparison test was utilized for pairwise intergroup comparisons. Prior to these analyses, it was confirmed that the data approximated a normal distribution and exhibited homogeneity of variance. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
