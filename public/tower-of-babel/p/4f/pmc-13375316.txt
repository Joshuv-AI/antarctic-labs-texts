Urban mushroom farming for future foods: safety considerations and nutritional benefits
Authors: Malsha Samarasiri, Wei Ning Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing global population and rapid urbanization, urban agriculture has gained increasing attention as a sustainable pathway to achieving food security. This review discusses recent advances in urban mushroom farming, its integration with computer-based technologies, safety considerations, and the potential of mushrooms and mycelium-based foods as future foods. Urban mushroom farming systems include indoor/vertical farms, container/modular units, and rooftop/community-based setups. Technologies such as the Internet of Things, machine learning, and artificial intelligence can be incorporated to improve monitoring, control, and overall efficiency in urban mushroom farms. Mushrooms and mycelium-based products, including fruiting bodies, mycelial biomass, mycelium-fermented foods, hybrid or 3D-printed products, and mushroom extracts or functional ingredients, can be produced within urban environments while utilizing various food waste and agro-industrial residues as cultivation media. These products are rich sources of protein, essential amino acids, dietary fibre, antioxidants, ergosterols, and several essential minerals. Key safety considerations for mushrooms and mycelium-based foods in urban production systems, including biological hazards, mycotoxins, heavy metal accumulation, and potential allergenicity, are also discussed. Although mushrooms have a long history of consumption, macrofungal mycelium-based foods may be regarded as novel foods and evaluated under novel food safety regulations.

Sustainability Statement

This review primarily relates to UN Sustainable Development Goal SDG 2 (Zero Hunger). Moreover, it touches upon other SDGs, including SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), SDG 13 (Climate Action), and SDG 15 (Life on Land). Globally, there is growing interest in sustainable food production to meet rising demands. Mushrooms and their mycelium, rich in nutritional and therapeutic value, exhibit significant potential as future foods. They are compatible with urban farming, with features including minimal space and light requirements, low maintenance, resilience to adverse conditions, and the ability to degrade organic waste.

Introduction

According to the United Nations, by 2050, there will be an increase in global urbanization by 2.4 billion people, and 70% of the total population will live in cities (United Nations 2018, United Nations Development Programme 2024). However, it is not realistic to supply the future demand only with conventional food production. Despite the food security concerns, increasing food production has become constrained by the shrinkage of agricultural land, particularly in cities with land scarcity. As a result, food production within urban and peri-urban areas is emerging as a feasible approach to satisfy part of the rising food demand in cities, while also lowering environmental impacts and enhancing climate resilience (Aggarwal et al. 2024).

Urban agriculture uses soil, aquaponic, aeroponic, or hydroponic farming methods while enhancing the productivity of crops and utilizing 95% less water, fewer nutrients and fertilizers, and no pesticides (Clercq et al. 2018). It also provides several advantages such as reducing heat island impacts, improving urban infrastructure, protecting biodiversity, community development, promoting economic growth, educational opportunities, climate change mitigation, lowering food transport emissions, reducing food waste in farm to fork process, and safe conditions in extreme weather (Karaca and Ince 2023, Pradhan et al. 2023, Aggarwal et al. 2024). However, the challenges regarding urban agriculture are cost and complexity in scaling up, difficulties in training and building up a skilled workforce, incompatibility for certain crops, undesirable warmer temperatures, food safety concerns, and effects of polluted air (Buscaroli et al. 2021, Romanova and Lovell 2021, Karaca and Ince 2023, Aggarwal et al. 2024).

On the other hand, various protein alternatives are being studied and developed to satisfy increasing dietary requirements in the world. Fungi have been identified as rich sources of nutrients and have been consumed for thousands of years. Fungi that produce reproductive structures (sporocarps; also known as fruiting bodies) visible to the naked eye are classified as macrofungi (e.g. mushrooms), which are the focus of this review, whereas all other fungi (e.g. yeasts and molds) are categorized as microfungi (Winterhoff 1992). Mycoprotein, which has received increasing attention as a meat alternative, is produced by the fermentation of the filamentous fungus Fusarium venenatum, a soil-borne microfungus, while macrofungal mycelium is generally associated with mushroom production and lignocellulosic substrate degradation (Kumla et al. 2020, Pintado and Delgado-Pando 2020). In fact, the fruiting bodies (mushrooms) made by mycelium (vegetative structure of mushrooms) are favourable and well-known owing to the nutritional, therapeutic, and sensory values.

Mushrooms are identified as a rich source of dietary fibre and antioxidants, and a low-calorie food with low content of fat and sodium. Global mushroom and truffle production has increased by 403.44% between 2000 and 2021. In 2021, China was the leading country in mushroom and truffle production (93.03%), while the EU region contributed 3.40%, Japan 1.06%, the USA 0.78%, South Asia 0.73%, and Canada 0.31% (Food and Agriculture Organisation 2024). Mycelium of macrofungal species is a natural decomposer that can degrade a vast range of lignocellulosic biomass. By considering the degradation capacity and the nutritional, physiological, and sensory properties of mushrooms and mycelium, mycelium has been applied for the valorization of agro-industrial residues and food waste (Ahlborn et al. 2019). In urban settings, upcycling such organic waste streams can be conducted through macrofungi while gaining nutrient-enriched meat analogues, mushroom fruiting bodies, nutrient recycling and recovery, and biocomposite materials (Ahlborn et al. 2019, Raman et al. 2021, Sun et al. 2022). This review examines the nutritional and safety aspects of mushroom- and mycelium-based (macrofungal) foods, highlighting their potential as future food sources in urban production systems. The integration of mushroom farming with various technologies to improve efficiency and effectiveness within urban settings is also briefly discussed. Mushroom and mycelium as future food

Compared to plant-based meat alternatives, fungal proteins are generally considered a better option with respect to their nutritional value (higher quality in protein, rich in fibre), sensory values (meaty flavour, fibrous structure), consumer acceptability, greater production efficiency, and survival from climate and some crucial agricultural conditions (Wang et al. 2023a). Especially the mushrooms, fruiting bodies made by mycelium are favoured due to the existence of vitamin D2, all essential amino acids, and vitamin B, the higher levels of dietary fibre, and lower contents of fat and energy (Ahlborn et al. 2019).

Fungi-based food is not limited only to mushrooms but also covers the fungal biomass and fungal fermented food, which provide various functional properties, nutritional, and sensory benefits. Therefore, considering the literature, mushroom- and mycelium-based foods are grouped into four main categories in this review: mushroom fruiting bodies, mycelium-based foods, hybrid or 3D-printed products, and mushroom extracts or functional ingredients. There is extensive research on mushroom fruiting bodies, focusing on the use of various organic waste materials for cultivation and the effects of substrate type and composition, postharvest processing methods (including drying, preservation, and storage), and cooking conditions on mushroom yield and key attributes such as nutrient contents, flavour, physical dimensions, and antioxidant properties (Selli et al. 2021, Feng et al. 2022, Hamed et al. 2022; Samarasiri and Chen 2022, 2025a). Many types of waste materials such as sawdust, straw (rice, cereal, legume), bran (wheat, rice), stalk (sesame, sorghum), cottonseed hull, soybean residue, sugarcane bagasse, corncob, and animal and fruit waste have been utilized for the cultivation of mushrooms, especially the common varieties such as button, oyster, portobella, shiitake, cremini, and enoki mushrooms (Supplementary Table S1).

Fungi degrade/convert a wide range of lignocellulosic biomass waste while promoting a circular economy. Thus, fungi-based food is not only beneficial for escalating dietary requirements but also directs to a sustainable path of producing proteins and bioactive compounds. In that scenario, without separating biomass from a cultivation medium, the fermented edible substrate (composite: fungi + medium) can be used as a potential meat alternative or a nutrient-enriched product. For example, successful fermentation of sugar beet pulp by lactic acid bacteria and Pleurotus ostreatus mycelium resulted in a composite with an increase in protein digestibility and some key volatile compounds (Todorov et al. 2024). When value addition of maize stalk was performed by using Saccharomyces cerevisiae and P. ostreatus under solid-state fermentation, it supported the improvement of protein content and in vitro digestibility for creating a high-quality feed product (Darwish et al. 2012). Moreover, fermented soybean residue (okara) using Pleurotus spp. exhibited a potential as an appealing nutrient-enriched food through the enhancements of protein level and concentrations of umami taste substances (Samarasiri and Chen 2025b). Additionally, reduced off-flavours, improved techno-functional properties, increased antioxidant and enzyme activities, enhanced immune-activating bioactivity, and improved amino acid profiles have been reported in mycelium-based foods produced through the fermentation of various lignocellulosic biomasses (Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, the production of mycelial biomass, particularly under submerged fermentation, has been gaining attention as a promising source of high-quality protein (Melanouri et al. 2022, Bakratsas et al. 2023).

Owing to the desirable nutritional and flavour properties of mushrooms and the concerns associated with traditional meat industry such as resource scarcity, rising demand, health issues, and animal welfare, the substitution of meat with mushrooms or mycelium for the development of meat analogues has been explored (Supplementary Table S1). Z. Zhang et al. (2024) studied how different extractions of mycelium of oyster mushroom species can alter sensory properties in a soy-based meat analogue with a focus on taste, aroma, colour, and texture. That study revealed that mycelium composition not exceeding 8% could decrease the beany flavour, astringent and bitter tastes, while improving meat-like aroma, brightness, whiteness, and springiness of the meat analogue. The use of white button mushrooms as a meat extender in beef patties led to reduced sodium levels and achieved satisfactory liking scores from Hedonic sensory analysis compared to patties with textured soy (Wong et al. 2019). Moreover, improvements of fibre level and fibrous structure in meat analogues through application of mushrooms were shown in past work (Cerón-Guevara et al. 2019, Mazlan et al. 2020, Gao et al. 2025). In addition to such hybrid food, recently there has also been an attention towards the application of 3D printing technology to develop mushroom-based food while considering the favourable aspects of mushrooms such as high fibre level and sensory attributes (Keerthana et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2021, Demircan et al. 2023).

Mushrooms are a rich source of bioactive compounds with promising therapeutic properties. Past studies have evaluated a range of bioactive compounds in mushroom and mycelium extracts, including polysaccharides, triterpenoids, ergothioneine, and phenolic compounds, which exhibit antioxidant, anti-hypoxia, gut health-promoting, and antitumour activities, revealing the potential of mushrooms as functional ingredients in the diet (Liu et al. 2019, Hsu et al. 2021, Kerezoudi et al. 2021, Zhu et al. 2022). For instance, Jiao et al. (2018) found that supplementation with water-soluble polysaccharides of Agaricus bitorquis derived from fruiting body, mycelium, or fermentation broth prolonged survival in mice under hypoxic conditions, denoting the anti-hypoxia activity of A. bitorquis. In addition to that, the mycelium of Morchella esculenta, which is known as a delicious and expensive mushroom species, was found to contain triterpenoid compounds exhibiting anti-cancer and antioxidant activity, highlighting its potential as a functional food in future applications (Wang et al. 2020). Additional studies assessing bioactive compounds in mushroom and mycelium extracts are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Urban mushroom farming

Mushroom growing in urban agriculture has become a popular option on a domestic, medium, and large scale. Cultivation methods in urban mushroom farming can be broadly classified into indoor/vertical systems, container/modular systems, and rooftop/community-based systems, and they can operate as controlled environment agriculture (Table 1). Bags, buckets, monotubs, trays, and bottles are generally used and selected considering the factors such as cultivation method and scale, mushroom species, substrate type, experience level of cultivator, space, compatibility for sterilization and reusability, and expected yield. Urban mushroom farmers acquire information from peer interactions, training workshops, farmer associations, and forums, which support improved practices in harvesting, storage, crop protection, and marketing (Kavi et al. 2018). In fact, mushrooms can grow with minimal light and relatively little care compared to certain conventional crops. Another flexibility is that they can be cultivated on urban waste such as cardboard, coffee grounds, sawdust, and food waste, facilitating a circular economy. The principles of the circular economy have been developed with previous concepts such as reduce–reuse–recycle, green economy, closing loops, cleaner production, waste minimization, and sustainable consumption (Tóth 2019). Especially in urban mushroom farming, this can be achieved with the food waste and byproduct usage, nutrient recycling, surplus avoidance, and a diet shift towards foods manufactured with limited resources (Jurgilevich et al. 2016, Dorr et al. 2021).

The utilization of organic waste to grow mushrooms in urban settings can result in valuable outputs (Fig. 2) such as edible food products and soil amendment (Dorr et al. 2021). After yielding the required flushes of fruiting bodies, the remaining cultivation medium (spent mushroom substrate) can be beneficial as a packaging material, soil amendment rich in organic carbon, animal feed, and energy feedstock, or for enzyme recovery and wastewater treatment (Phan and Sabaratnam 2012). In an urban farm in France, oyster mushrooms were grown on spent coffee grounds (30 tonnes annual usage in 2018), and spent mushroom substrate was sold as a soil amendment to farmers. The life cycle assessment of that farm revealed similar climate change impacts in comparison to conventional farms but suggested a potential for lowering environmental impacts and energy inputs (Dorr et al. 2021). Another study demonstrated the potential for local self-sufficiency in oyster mushroom cultivation by using urban-based substrates, while also highlighting the value addition by converting residual waste streams in Stockholm Stad, such as wood, garden, cardboard, and paper waste, and spent coffee grounds into productive resources (Fagerström 2023).

Urban mushroom farming and its major outcomes.

In nature, various partnerships exist between plants and fungi, such as the symbiotic relationship (mycorrhiza), nutrient recycling, and preventing invasion by parasites (Stamets 2005). Thus, mushroom/mycelium growing can be complementary to the cultivation of certain plants to obtain sustainable food production in urban settings. Walsh (2015) developed a whole system approach for urban farming, considering three food production systems (mushrooms, agroforestry, and aquaponics), one waste management system (vermiculture), and two distribution methods (whole food store and delivery service). Moreover, when two systems of vertical farming of lettuce and urban farming of oyster mushrooms in Stockholm were viewed as a single system, potential synergies were recognized, such as light transfer, utilization of organic waste/spent mushroom substrate, and carbon dioxide cycling (Christiansen 2024).

Both commercial and domestic mushrooms can be cultivated within relatively confined spaces such as old warehouses, derelict buildings, and garages. Mushrooms can be grown on stackable trays, resulting in high-density food production. Frenila et al. (2015) presented solar-powered automated mushroom cultivation in the urban environment by using a plastic box (limited space), requiring less labour effort, and using renewable energy. According to the estimations of that study, a maximum of 250 g of mushrooms can be obtained on alternating days from a single box (50.8 × 33.0 × 35.6 cm), and several boxes can be stacked together. Two recent studies on mushroom vertical farming denoted the importance of evaluating the effects of the layer spacing and cultivation shelves orientation on the daylight intensity and distribution, and the influence of layers and lighting conditions on mushroom yield (Al Mamun et al. 2021, Yao et al. 2025).

Research on urban agriculture methods for mushroom cultivation, including vertical, container, and rooftop farming, is still emerging, despite the availability of literature on lab- or pilot-scale cultivation. A 12.2 m (40 ft) long shipping container was utilized to model a medium-scale oyster mushroom farm equipped for substrate preparation, storage, and incubation, with a production capacity ranging from 113.4 to 453.6 kg of mushrooms per day (Risal et al. 2023). Since mushroom cultivation requires relatively little space and low capital investment, it has also emerged as a feasible and attractive activity for community-based gardening, promoting community empowerment, knowledge exchange, and social network development (Phannan 2020).

Integration with technologies

Modern agricultural techniques, such as the Internet of Things (IoT), can be included in urban farming. IoT was used in oyster mushroom urban farming in Malaysia to build an automatic climate control system (temperature and humidity) at minimum cost, resulting in less burden for urban dwellers and improved yield under optimum cultivation conditions (Ariffin et al. 2020). As given in Table 2, recent research also demonstrates the integration of IoT, artificial intelligence (AI), edge computing, and machine learning with mushroom farming to enable smart monitoring, automated environmental regulation, and intelligent data processing, including mushroom species classification. Such smart systems have been shown to improve yield, product quality, and operational efficiency while promoting sustainable and cost-effective agricultural practices (Rahman et al. 2022, Rukhiran et al. 2023, Nguyen et al. 2024). However, access to or implementation of these controlled-environment technologies may be limited by financial, technical, and infrastructural barriers in urban systems in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Low- and middle-income countries

Mushroom cultivation has been recognized as a promising means of addressing poverty and food insecurity (Fig. 3) in land-scarce countries and LMICs. Given its reliance on low-cost substrates, limited land requirements, suitability for small- or domestic-scale production, low dependence on atmospheric conditions, low investment costs, resilience to climate variability, and short growth cycle, urban mushroom farming can contribute to local economies by enhancing livelihood opportunities, nutrient supply, and income generation (Khan et al. 2024, Ndayisaba et al. 2025, Poly et al. 2026). However, the challenges such as limited knowledge and training among farmers, inadequate infrastructure and technology, low consumer awareness, lack of spawn, market access constraints, and insufficient quality control and standards can be mitigated through government and organizational support via policy incentives, subsidies, research funding, and training programmes (Khan et al. 2024, Ndayisaba et al. 2025). For example, as of early 2025, nearly 900 urban farmers in Myanmar have gained skills in sustainable agriculture, including mushroom cultivation, with many receiving small business grants and over 4000 people benefiting from the United Nations Development Programme—Urban Resilience Project, which works to strengthen livelihoods and enhance food security in vulnerable communities (United Nations Development Programme 2025).

Consumer perception and acceptance

Consumer perception and acceptance represent critical determinants of the successful adoption of urban-grown mushrooms as future foods. For urban-grown foods in general, positive perceptions of sustainability, naturalness, health benefits, and practicality have been identified as important drivers of consumer acceptance, whereas unfamiliar production technologies (food technology neophobia), perceived risks such as safety concerns and pollution, and barriers related to access or price tend to limit acceptance (Grebitus et al. 2020, Gan et al. 2022, Califano et al. 2024, Food Standards Agency et al. 2025). For example, Jürkenbeck et al. (2019) noted that consumer perceptions of the sustainability of vertical farming systems strongly influence acceptance. Specifically in the context of mushrooms and mycelium-based foods, factors such as nutritional and therapeutic benefits, price, sensory quality, perceived safety of urban production environments, trust in waste-derived substrates, personal beliefs, cultural familiarity, and contributions to the circular economy can influence consumer willingness to consume and purchase these products (Dorr et al. 2021, Moxley et al. 2022, Ndayisaba et al. 2025).

Nutritional benefits

A total of 2000 edible mushroom species exist in the world, and mushroom consumption offers positive impacts on cognition, oral health, breast cancer risk, obesity, immune function, and cardiometabolic health (Derbyshire 2020, Pintado and Delgado-Pando 2020). Many species of mushrooms have been recognized as a rich source of various nutrients, including protein, essential amino acids, carbohydrates, dietary fibre, niacin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid, antioxidants, ergosterols, and some essential minerals such as potassium, selenium, copper, phosphorus, iron, and calcium (Stamets 2005, Pérez-Chávez et al. 2019, Pintado and Delgado-Pando 2020). As an abundant source of dietary fibre, mushrooms can supply 25% of the daily dietary requirement of dietary fibre, and the major contributors are chitin and β-glucan (Cheung 2013). In general, mushrooms consist of 50%–65% total carbohydrates, 19%–35% proteins, and 2%–6% fat, in which unsaturated fatty acids are dominant over saturated fatty acids (Derbyshire 2020). Mushrooms are also recognized as the best non-animal-based food for vitamin D2 due to their higher levels of ergosterols, which are precursors for vitamin D2 production under UV irradiation (Salemi et al. 2021). Especially, mushrooms are desirable owing to their low caloric value and low content of fat and sodium. These favourable aspects of mushrooms have increased the popularity of mycelium/mushroom-based proteins.

The mushrooms secrete digestive enzymes to degrade lignocellulose compounds present in the cultivation medium, and then the resulting nutrients are utilized for the mushroom life cycle from vegetative to reproductive phases. There are past studies covering the nutritional analysis of mushrooms and mycelium, which were subjected to changes in species, substrate ingredients, substrate composition, cultivation, post-harvest processing, and cooking conditions. For example, Ma et al. (2020) showed that the medium containing diaper core, coffee waste, sugarcane bagasse, banana skin, and eggshell with a weight ratio of 5:2:1:1:1 consisted of greater nutrient content compared to the commercial substrate, and it provided a higher yield of oyster mushrooms. Higher nutrient content in the substrate can stimulate mycelial development by providing more available energy, ultimately increasing mushroom yield. Furthermore, in another study, the substitution of sawdust with rice straw decreased the protein level in shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes) but had no remarkable effects on carbohydrate and lipid levels (Gao et al. 2020).

Apart from fruiting bodies, the nutritional values of mycelium and mycelium-based food were also assessed in past studies. Fermentation of organic matter using macrofungal mycelium can result in elevated protein levels due to the hydrolysis of complex nutrients by fungal enzymes and also the conversion of certain carbohydrates to protein, free amino acids, or peptides (Wang et al. 2023b, Samarasiri and Chen 2025b). Shiitake-fermented pea-rice protein concentrate blend gained improved protein quality and solubility, and decreased levels of the antinutrient compounds, including protease inhibitor and phytates, and thus it indicated that fermentation with mycelium can be an effective method to enhance nutritional value, digestibility, functionality, and flavour of plant proteins (Clark et al. 2022). Even though consumer interest in meat alternatives is increasing, mycelium-based proteins remain relatively overlooked compared to plant proteins, despite their higher protein content and superior essential amino acid composition (Derbyshire 2020). In fact, mycelium-based proteins require further evaluations, especially regarding their safety, applicability, and consumer acceptance. When submerged fermentation of aspen wood chips hydrolysate using P. ostreatus was conducted, biomass with higher protein yield and higher concentrations of the essential amino acids were obtained, indicating the suitability of biomass as a promising meat analogue (Bakratsas et al. 2023). In addition, the polysaccharide content of mycelium, particularly its dietary fibre fraction, represents another important source of nutritional and therapeutic value. β-glucans, chitin, α-mannan, polysaccharide-protein complexes, and α-glucans are some key polysaccharides of mycelium/mushrooms, which were reported (Chen et al. 2016, Hassainia et al. 2018, Jiao et al. 2018, Kerezoudi et al. 2021).

In today’s fast-paced world, the occurrence and prevalence of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, obesity, cancer, neurodegenerative, inflammatory, and cardiovascular diseases are widely observed owing to urbanization, lifestyle, and nutritional factors (Meulmeester et al. 2022). Mushrooms serve as a valuable dietary source of bioactive compounds that promote health through their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, anti-tumour, neuroprotective, and anti-microbial activities (Sánchez 2017a). Oxidative stress, which is known as the imbalance between the generation of oxidants and the defensive mechanism by antioxidants, contributes to these diseases. Some key antioxidants detected in mushroom mycelium, culture broth, and fruiting bodies are phenolic compounds, flavonoids, polysaccharides, tocopherols, carotenoids, ergothioneine, ascorbic acid, and glycosides (Sánchez 2017b). In addition, bioactive proteins and peptides (lectins, protease inhibitors), sterols (ergosterol), glutathione, polysaccharide–protein complexes are some other bioactive compounds reported in mushrooms and mycelium (Sánchez 2017a, Kumar et al. 2021). The concentrations of these bioactive compounds may vary depending on the product form (mycelium, fruiting body, and fermentation broth), culture medium, species, and post-harvest processing (Souilem et al. 2017, Piskov et al. 2020). Given their rich nutritional profile and diverse therapeutic properties, mushroom- and mycelium-based foods hold substantial potential for developing future food solutions within urban production systems.

Safety considerations

Mycelium and various fermented foods via mycelium, which could have a huge potential as a safe and reliable food with consumer acceptance, are yet to be explored widely (Derbyshire 2020, Strong et al. 2022, Fischer and Hilboesen 2025). Although mushrooms of a given species have a long history of consumption, foods and ingredients derived from the mycelium of the same species may be classified as novel foods and may be subject to the relevant regulatory requirements of novel food. In addition, the utilization of various food wastes and agro-industrial residues with macrofungal mycelium offers a sustainable pathway to produce alternative foods for the future, yet their safety and regulatory concerns must be addressed (Molitorisová et al. 2021). According to the literature, safety considerations for mushroom- and mycelium-based foods include biological hazards, mycotoxins, heavy metal accumulation, radioactivity, the presence of toxic or poisonous mushroom species, and potential allergenicity.

Biological hazards

Mushrooms have the potential to contain various foodborne pathogens, which can be introduced through cultivation medium, growers, growing environment, and irrigation water. Compared to cultivated mushrooms, wild mushrooms are more susceptible to microbial contamination since they are exposed to wild animals and livestock as well as handpicked from forests, since pathogens can enter mushrooms directly or via mycophagous species such as snails, insects, rodents, birds, and reptiles (Venturini et al. 2011, Niksic et al. 2016). Listeria monocytogenes, which is one of the major pathogens in mushrooms, has a survival ability in an extensive range of osmotic conditions and pH, and low temperatures, and a higher lethal rate in humans. In 2020, the first known Listeria outbreak relevant to enoki mushrooms occurred in the USA, leading to the hospitalization of 31 people and 4 deaths (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2020). Moreover, 15.8% of 184 samples from a small-scale mushroom farm in the USA (Viswanath et al. 2013) and 21.20% of mushrooms obtained from the Chinese market were evident in the presence of L. monocytogenes (Chen et al. 2018). Likewise, Escherichia coli and Salmonella were identified in 4 and 5 samples, respectively, from 100 samples of mushrooms purchased from Seattle (Samadpour et al. 2006). Improper handling, postharvest storage conditions, and packaging methods can also cause the presence of other pathogens such as Campylobacter, Clostridium botulinum, and Staphylococcus aureus (Niksic et al. 2016). However, microbial contamination can be prevented or reduced with the appropriate food preservation and handling techniques while relying on Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) and good hygienic and sanitary practices. The reduction of pathogens in mushrooms can also be achieved using different post-harvesting treatments with ultraviolet-C radiation, organic acids, nisin, caprylic acid, thymol ozone treatment, ultrasound, hydrogen peroxide, electrolyzed water, and other sanitizers (Yuk et al. 2007, Ding et al. 2011, Guan et al. 2012, 2013, Yoon et al. 2021, Chung et al. 2023).

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have directly assessed pathogenic microorganisms in foods produced from mushroom mycelium. Hashempour-Baltork et al. (2020) stated that the risk of zoonotic and infectious diseases is likely low in mycoproteins compared to livestock production. However, mycelium is susceptible to microbial contamination, such as by Trichoderma spp., which should be considered in urban cultivation systems, as it can inhibit and reduce mycelial growth (Allaga et al. 2021).

Mycotoxins

Despite the lack of studies, the presence of mycotoxins can be assumed if the mushroom/mycelium cultivation is contaminated with fungal organisms responsible for mycotoxin formation, including Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium. In the study by Van Dam et al. (2024), P. ostreatus mycelium has not been found to produce detectable levels of mycotoxins. On the other hand, Haidukowski et al. (2019) showed that Pleurotus eryngii mycelium was able to detoxify Aflatoxin B1 from contaminated solutions and had a maximum removal efficiency of 85%. In recent years, oyster (Pleurotus spp.) and shiitake (L. edodes) mushrooms have been more explored for their abilities to effectively degrade mycotoxins through their ligninolytic enzymes, such as laccase and manganese peroxidase (Loncar et al. 2023, Zapaśnik et al. 2025).

Heavy metals

Mushrooms can accumulate heavy metals in higher concentrations (Supplementary Table S2), especially via their growing mediums, leading to potential health concerns such as skin lesions, nerve damage, gastrointestinal and kidney dysfunction, cancers, and immune system dysfunctions (Balali-Mood et al. 2021, Ab Rhaman et al. 2022). The heavy metal uptake relies on microbial cell wall compositions, the chemistry of ion metals, cell physiology, ionic strength, metal concentration, pH, exposure time, and temperature (Ab Rhaman et al. 2022).

Heavy metals released into soil, air, and water through anthropogenic activities can enter mushrooms at any stage of their life cycle, from spawning to fruiting. Since different agri-industrial wastes, which may contain higher concentrations of heavy metals transferred through soil or industrial processes, are used as substrates for mushrooms, medium compositions should be properly evaluated and controlled to avoid detrimental levels of heavy metals in mushrooms. Higher heavy metal contamination may often be seen in mushrooms grown in a polluted environment (industrialized cities, roads, and mining areas) or uncontrolled growth conditions (forests) (Demková et al. 2021). Schlecht and Säumel (2015) suggested that cultivated mushrooms can be safe for consumption and suitable for urban food production, even in high-traffic areas, while trace metal levels in some wild-growing mushrooms exceeded EU limits. Makkar et al. (2025) showed that heavy metal levels of L. edodes mushrooms and their spent mushroom substrate from standardized cultivation were within the WHO/FAO regulatory safety limits. A similar trend was reported for the same mushroom species cultivated on substrates supplemented with agro-industrial wastewater from a dairy plant or a sugar mill (Kumar et al. 2022). However, oyster mushrooms grown on soil from mining sites accumulated Cd, Zn, and Cr at concentrations above the permissible levels (Sithole et al. 2022).

Past studies have shown the ability of mycelium to do remediation of various media. Y. Wang et al. (2021) showed that through surface adsorption and fixation, Morchella mycelium decreased the Pb content by 34.83% in crops grown in the soil along with mycelium. Another study revealed that P. ostreatus mycelium contributed to the biosorption of Pb, Ni, and Cr in wastewater by 25%, 89%, and 55%, respectively (Bhatnagar et al. 2021). Despite such findings relevant to mycoremediation, the heavy metal contents of mushroom mycelium-based food remain largely underexplored. Agaricus subrufescens accumulated Cd from contaminated substrates such as straw and livestock manure, while higher Cd levels inhibited mycelial growth and caused morphological abnormalities and oxidative stress, highlighting both safety risks and challenges for the sustainable development of mycelium-based foods (Ma et al. 2025).

Radioactivity

With the nuclear accidents at Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011, it was identified that mushrooms were contaminated by radioisotopes (Niksic et al. 2016). This safety concern is generally associated with wild mushrooms as radionuclides (e.g. radiocaesium) can remain in forest soils for long periods after nuclear accidents (Falandysz et al. 2021). After the aforementioned nuclear disasters, to mitigate and reduce the potential health effects through dose reduction, mushroom consumption has been mainly focused on local markets, but the expansion of the global mushroom market poses a concern of uncertainty of origin and inaccessible radioactivity data to the consumer (Guillén and Baeza 2014). After about 30 years of the Chernobyl disaster, the radiocesium-specific activity of analysed samples was very low and within international permissible limits (Caridi et al. 2021). Similarly, the effects of radioactivity on mushrooms in China from 2014 to 2016 following the Fukushima accident were significantly low or negligible, resulting in no considerable health concern (Saniewski et al. 2022). Thus, the radioactivity of mushrooms should be analysed based on the occurrence of such nuclear accidents, location, time, exposure, and relevant food safety legislations, whereas this issue is generally not a safety concern for mushrooms cultivated in urban settings.

Poisonous species

The major reason for mushroom poisoning is the misidentification of toxic species as edible species by consumers. However, this safety concern is primarily for wild-grown mushrooms, not for mushrooms cultivated in controlled urban farming systems where only known, safe mushroom strains are intentionally inoculated. Currently, more than 100 toxins have been detected in mushroom species (Yin et al. 2019). Amanita phalloides (death cap), Cortinarius orellanus (Fool’s webcap), and Clitocybe phyllophila (Frosty funnel) are some common poisonous species (Wennig et al. 2020). There is a need to evaluate the toxicity of under-explored species and to study toxic substances for their structural properties, toxicology, de novo synthesis, treatments, rapid detection through urine and blood tests, and usage as pharmaceutical drugs (Yin et al. 2019). These toxins can result in hepatic and renal failure, nerve damage, and can also cause death. For instance, Inocybe poisonous mushrooms are often misidentified as an edible species, and several cases of hospitalizations due to Inocybe poisoning were reported (Xu et al. 2020, Patocka et al. 2021).

Allergenicity

Fungal allergens are known as contributors to Type I allergic responses (Ferreira et al. 2017). Mycoproteins can lead to gastrointestinal reactions and even some life-threatening allergies (Hashempour-Baltork et al. 2020). Ingestion of edible mushrooms was reported to cause food allergic reactions (Supplementary Table S2). For example, IgE-mediated allergic reactions caused oral pruritus in a non-atopic adult following ingestion of shiitake mushrooms (Romero-Sanchez et al. 2023). Past studies have also revealed the possibility of IgE cross-reactivity between allergens from mushrooms and moulds (Gabriel et al. 2015, Ferreira et al. 2017, Gauld et al. 2024). Considering known cases of mushroom allergy, mycelial biomass and mycelium-based foods may also pose potential allergenicity concerns. The allergic cross-reactivity between fungi and mycoprotein/fermented food, and the possible allergens, were evaluated in past studies, particularly with a focus on microfungal species (Xing et al. 2022). However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have directly evaluated the allergenic potential of macrofungal mycelium-based food products. Systematic allergic contact dermatitis, contact dermatitis, and photosensitive skin lesions occurred upon consuming raw Shiitake mushrooms, and it was considered by EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens for their safety assessment of novel food consisting of pea and rice protein which was fermented by Shiitake (L. edodes) mycelia (Nguyen et al. 2017, Turck et al. 2022). Moreover, mycelium spores, specifically P. ostreatus spores, are regarded as greatly potent allergens leading to respiratory allergy. In indoor mushroom farms, fungal spores are commonly discovered to be floating in the air. Therefore, there is a potential risk of developing respiratory allergy for farmers involved with oyster mushrooms (Branicka et al. 2021). Necessary engineering and administrative controls, personal protective equipment, and health monitoring should be required to address the risk of respiratory allergies.

Key knowledge gaps and research priorities

This review synthesizes current knowledge on different forms of mushroom and mycelium-based foods, various types of urban mushroom farms, nutritional benefits, safety considerations, and substrate utilization, while also highlighting research gaps and identifying future priorities. Despite growing interest in urban mushroom farming as a sustainable source of future food, several knowledge gaps remain. Although mushroom cultivation has been widely studied, the development of mycelium-based foods as potential novel foods presents new opportunities. Nutritional data are limited, particularly regarding the effects of substrate composition and growing/post-harvest conditions on nutrient bioavailability and bioactive compounds in urban-grown mushrooms, as well as the nutritional and medicinal properties of macrofungal mycelium-based foods. Safety concerns, including microbial contamination, heavy metal accumulation, allergenicity, and mycotoxin presence, require systematic investigation under real urban conditions. Data from urban farms is scarce, and while some local food waste streams have yet to be evaluated as substrates. Socio-economic aspects, such as consumer acceptance, market potential, and community engagement, also require thorough assessment. Additionally, sustainability evaluations, including life cycle analyses and studies on resource efficiency, are not widely conducted in urban mushroom farming contexts. Future research may further differentiate between high-technology urban mushroom farming systems in high-income countries and low-cost, adaptable systems in LMICs, assessing their respective sustainability, scalability, and socio-economic impacts.

Conclusion

This review examined recent advances in urban mushroom farming, highlighting its potential as a sustainable food source with a focus on nutritional and safety aspects. Even though there are many studies on lab-scale and pilot-scale mushroom/mycelium cultivation, investigations on urban mushroom farming are still emerging, and warrant further research on the growth of mushroom/mycelium in indoor/vertical systems, container/modular systems, and rooftop/community-based systems. In addition, the effective integration of urban mushroom farming with computer-based technologies is being explored to enable optimized production, streamline operations, enhance resource utilization, improve product quality, and promote sustainable production systems. Mushrooms and mycelium-based food are not only recognized for their higher contents of protein, dietary fibre, essential amino acids, vitamin D2, and minerals, but also for the presence of different bioactive compounds. In urban mushroom farming, safety concerns are primarily related to pathogenic microorganisms, mycotoxins, heavy metal accumulation, and potential allergenicity. However, particularly mycelium-derived foods require further evaluations on their nutritional attributes and safety aspects as a potential future food. Overall, considering their nutritional benefits and suitability for urban farming, mushrooms and mycelium-based foods denote significant potential as future foods.
