Simultaneous surgical treatment of multiple abdominal echinococcosis and benign pelvic organ tumours: a case report
Authors: Anton Goncharov, Yury Zharikov, Cyrill Gorlenko, Yana Kiseleva, Yulia Stepanova, Ivan Gruzdev, Dmitry Kalinin, Beslan Gurmikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchinococcosis of the liver is a parasitic infection that poses a significant healthcare challenge in endemic regions. The majority of cases present with either no clinical signs or non-specific ones, making differential diagnosis difficult until after the operation. Preoperative diagnostics rely mainly on computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and ultrasound. The usual treatment for liver echinococcosis involves an operation, such as percutaneous intervention, surgical resection of the cyst as well as surrounding liver tissue, or major liver resection, followed by prolonged conservative treatment with Albendazole.Case summaryEven though cases of simultaneous treatment of the echinococcosis and neoplastic lesions are present in the literature, they are scarce and the new data on the subject is of importance. A 61-year-old female patient presented to our clinic with chief complaint of abdominal pain. Upon physical examination, a palpable mass was discovered in the right ileac region along with hepatomegaly. Laboratory studies indicated the presence of an unspecified infection. An MRI of the abdomen revealed masses in both ovaries and cystic lesions in the liver and pelvis. The patient underwent surgical resection of cysts located both in the abdominal cavity and liver, as well as resection of surrounding liver parenchyma in S2 through S7 for echinococcosis and both-sided ovariectomy, hysterectomy, and omentum resection for lesions of the ovaries. The hepatic and abdominal cystic lesions were found to be of echinococcal origin, and the right ovary lesion was a mature teratoma even though preoperatively it was considered to be cancer. The patient is currently alive and well with no signs of recurrence after seven years.ConclusionAs such cases are rare, the medical documentation should be well described and analyzed for future reference. It is now considered to be possible to treat multiple cystic echinococcosis of the liver and free abdominal cavity along with potentially malignant lesions of the abdomen and pelvis – in our opinion survival rates should be similar to the rates of malignancy alone as radical surgical treatment of cystic echinococcosis remains effective and well accepted route of treatment. This rare case demonstrates and emphasizes an importance of simultaneous surgical interventions as well as correct preoperative suggestion of diagnosis.Keywords: Case report, Simultaneous Surgical Treatment, Echinococcosis, Teratoma, Albendazole

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis is a zoonosis caused by the parasitic tapeworm Echinococcus granulosis. In most cases infection transmitted by accidental ingestion of worm eggs. In the human body, the parasite can neither develop into an adult form nor enter the organism of the terminal host [1, 2]. Cystic echinococcosis is an endemic disease with primary prevalence in Mediterranean countries, Asia, Africa and North America [3, 4].

In the human intestine, a larval oncosphere develops and enters the portal circulation, in most cases lodging in the liver, rarely in the lungs, kidneys or brain. After attachment the oncosphere forms a gradually enlarging cystic mass with two layers: outer cuticular and inner germinal, consisting of brood capsules with newly formed larvae called protoscolices. By transferring protoscolices or forming from the germinal membrane, echinococcus can form daughter cysts in the primary organ affected or disseminate to other organs [5, 6].

The clinical presentation is usually asymptomatic or limited to heaviness or pain in the right upper abdomen. Noticeable symptoms occur only in the presence of complications such as cyst rupture, infection or fistula formation [6]. Physical examination findings are also absent in most cases [7].

Furthermore, in non-endemic regions, suspecting echinococcosis as the diagnosis of choice in most cases appears to be complex. Thus, the correct diagnosis is often late, when radical surgical treatment is not possible. In these cases, chemotherapy remains the only option, but is usually associated with severe side effects [8].

Laboratory studies are also inconclusive in most cases, although serological studies may provide a clue. However, serological studies can be false negative in both early (i.e. CE1 and CE2) and late (CE4 and CE5) stages [9]. All of the above makes imaging the most reliable source for the diagnosis of hepatic echinococcosis. Computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging and ultrasound are preferred [10].

Still, in the setting where cystic echinococcus occurs along with other lesions, i.e. in the free abdominal cavity it could pose a complex problem for correct preoperative diagnosis which in turn affects a decision regarding optimal treatment route. As of now, there is no understanding as to what should be treated first: echinococcosis or another found lesion, or should it be treated simultaneously. Published case reports on the matter exist, though they are scarce and data is limited. In this case report we provide an example of successful simultaneous surgical treatment of cystic echinococcosis of the liver and abdominal cavity and mature teratoma of the ovary (Fig. 1).

In view of the above, cystic echinococcosis should always be considered when dealing with cystic lesions of the liver. Radical treatment of cystic echinococcosis of the liver usually involves surgery. To date, there is no consensus on the optimal extent of treatment. In general, there are three options: percutaneous interventions, complete radical surgical resection (including resection of the affected liver segments) or parenchyma-sparing interventions, which include echinococcectomy with pericystectomy and atypical liver resection [11, 12]. Surgical treatment is usually followed by adjuvant administration of albendazole, which has been shown to reduce recurrence rates. It may also be beneficial to consider giving albendazole before surgery [13].

Patient

Our patient, a 61-year-old white female, presented to the clinic with the chief complaint of mild pain in the right upper quadrant and left ileac region of the abdomen. Pain syndrome significantly reduces the patient’s quality of life (Visual Analogue Scale = 7–8 points). The patient presented with a previous MRI showing multiple cystic lesions in the liver and pelvis.

The patient stated that she first noticed abdominal discomfort three years prior to current visit and a month before she started experiencing mild pain in the right upper quadrant and underwent MRI on her own accord. Clinically significant morbidities included type 2 diabetes, arterial hypertension stage 2, grade 2, high risk and chronic kidney disease (CKD-EPI GFR 55.7 mL/min/1.73mBI, KDIGO 3a). Patient has not been taking any medication for these conditions and had no allergies.

The patient's general condition was satisfactory. Physical examination revealed a new palpable, non-tender, 8 × 10 cm lesion in the left iliac region. The edge of the liver protruded 5–7 cm below the costal arch, indicating hepatomegaly, which explained the mild pain in the right upper quadrant.

The patient had no relevant surgical history, had a body mass index of 28 kg/cm2. She was menopausal for 7 years at the moment of operation and had no significant gynaecological history.

Laboratory examinations

The patient underwent routine laboratory investigations, including a blood count, biochemical blood tests, coagulation studies, blood grouping, acid–base balance, serological study. The blood count revealed mild anaemia (Hb 11.4 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (168 × 109/L) and unspecific signs of the infection, such as leukocytosis (13.9 × 109/L), neutrophilia (11.6 × 109/L) and monocytosis (1.19 × 109/L).

The biochemical profile showed hypoproteinemia and hypoalbuminemia (52 and 28 g/L respectively), as well as elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) (79 mg/L), ALT and AST (145 and 143 U/L) reflecting liver damage which considered to be connected to infection. Levels of tumor markers such as CA-125, AFP, β-hCG all lied within reference.

Repeated blood glucose monitoring revealed elevated blood glucose (6.5 mmol/L) and glycated haemoglobin (6.6%), so short-acting insulin therapy was initiated resulting in maintenance of normal level of blood glucose.

Serological study (ELISA) has also been performed indicating presence of IgG for Echinococcosus granulosis.

According to coagulation study, our patient had hypercoagulability grade 3 (activated partial thromboplastin time 26 s, fibrinogen 5.7 g/L) and after correction with standard prophylactic dose of low molecular weight heparin, hypercoagulability was downgraded to grade 2. Patent achieved normal APTT.

Abdominal MRI (Fig. 2) was repeated to characterise the liver lesions. Numerous cystic lesions in the liver presented with thick walls and septa, daughter cysts and heterogenous content on T2WI and hyperintensive on T1WI. There was no contrast enhancement in the cystic walls or septa.

A cystic-solid lesion was seen in the left iliac region with the presence of fat in the structure. Due to its proximity to the left ovary and the presence of fat, ovarian teratoma was considered to be the diagnosis. A lesion in the uterus considered to be a leiomyoma. Due to presence of the possibly malignant lesion of the left ovary and presence of leiomyoma if the uterus precaution has been taken and surgical treatment chosen included both-sided ovariectomy and hysterectomy.

A cystic lesion with a thick wall and septa was observed in the right iliac region. The lesion did not show any contrast enhancement or fat component. The differential diagnosis was between a right ovarian teratoma and an extra-organ hydatid cyst.

Even though abdomen MRI provides significant data beneficial for correct diagnosis, US remains golden standard for staging of echinococcal cysts. Our patient underwent abdomen ultrasound which revealed liver lesion to most likely being early and active CE1 echinococcal cyst (Fig. 3).

Diagnosis

Based on laboratory and imaging studies, the preoperative diagnosis included bilobar echinococcosis of the liver (CE1) and echinococcosis of the free abdominal cavity (pelvis); solitary lesions of the right and left ovaries; uterine leiomyoma; and controlled glycaemia in type 2 diabetes. It is important to note that this diagnosis was based solely on the available data (staging for pelvic lesions has not been provided due to poor visualization during abdomen US).

Treatment

Due to previously suggested echinococcosis infection patient received 12 months of chemotherapy with 400 mg of Albendazole orally twice a day.

Following a thorough examination and multidisciplinary board meeting, it was decided to proceed with planned surgical resection of the cystic lesions of the liver and abdominal cavity along with resection of the surrounding liver tissue in S2 through S7 complemented with hysterectomy, both-sided ovariectomy and partial resection of the omentum. Notably, no spillage or cystic rupture occurred during the surgery. As usual, the surgery was concluded with abdominal cavity drainage. Intraoperative pictures are presented in Fig. 4. The operative time was 335 min and blood loss was 350 ml.

Morphological study of the specimen has also been performed confirming echinococcosis of the liver and revealing right ovarian lesion to also be an echinococcal cyst. Lesion in the uterus and left ovary have been confirmed to be a leiomyoma and a mature teratoma respectively (Fig. 5).

Outcome and follow-up

The patient's postoperative period was uneventful. Patient received systemic broad spectrum antibiotic treatment. On the seventh postoperative day ultrasonography revealed fluid accumulation in the subphrenic space resolve with US-guided punction. There was no signs of local complication to the wound or systemic infection. As the teratoma was mature, no further treatment was necessary. The patient was discharged from the hospital on the twelfth postoperative day after the operation with signs of clinical improvement. Seven years later a patient is alive and well with no clinical or instrumental signs of the recurrence. An MRI of the abdomen is planned for December of 2026.

Discussion

Echinococcosis of the liver remains a significant healthcare issue in endemic regions. Despite considerable awareness, correct diagnosis poses a challenge, especially in small local healthcare centres. The diagnostic approach should include a number of comprehensive yet broadly available laboratory tests, as well as imaging modalities such as such MRI and ultrasound, enhanced computed tomography is also frequently used though in this case it has not been performed due to conclusiveness of the already performed studies. All of the above constitutes a complete diagnostic approach for cystic echinococcosis [14].

The ultrasound remains a major tool for staging of the hydatid cysts as an unofficial working group on echinococcosis (WHO-IWGE) has developed a classification of echinococcosis based on ultrasound findings. Stages include CE1 and CE2 representing active cysts, transitional CE3a and CE3b forms and inactive CE4 and CE5 stages (Fig. 1). When a cyst is an incidental finding and cannot be described in detail, the term CL (cystic lesion) is used, which requires further investigation and is not included in this classification. This classification is a modified version of an ultrasound classification developed by H. A. Gharbi, W. Hassine, M. W. Brauner and K. Dupuch [15].

This paper reports a clinical case of a woman who was admitted to our hospital with unspecific complaint. Blood studies did not reveal any specific changes except for positive IgG for Echinocococcus granulosis. The features of the liver lesion correlated with echinococcosis, while the right ovary lesion shared indistinguishable patterns due to poor visualization. An enhanced computed tomography could have been complimentary to the ultrasound. Yet, possible results of the CT wouldn’t have changed an already chosen comprehensive surgical approach.

Based on the laboratory studies and imaging where were no doubt to the diagnosis of cystic echinococcosis. Though the nature of the left ovarian lesion remained doubted.

Surgical intervention was necessary for a number of reasons: the size of hydatid cysts (more than 3.5 cm), early US stage of CE1, the number of cysts and the possibility of their complete, relapse-free removal, severe abdominal pain syndrome, which did not allow us to adhere to the "Watch-and-wait approach" principle [16].

Postoperative morphological study revealed a classical pattern of echinococcosis for liver and abdominal cavity lesions, and mature teratoma and leiomyoma for the right ovary and uterus, respectively. The operation was deemed radical due to the benign nature of the mature teratoma. No specific complications arose in the early postoperative period, so the patient was discharged on POD 12. Seven years later, the patient is alive and well, with no imaging or laboratory signs of recurrence.

Main limitations of this study is dictated by its nature.

This case report adds to existing data on simultaneous surgical treatment of cystic echinococcosis and abdominal neoplasms and emphasizes on the possibility of correct preoperative diagnosis and successful relapse-free surgical treatment. Due to efficacy of the surgical treatment of cystic echinococcosis (when possible) survival rates of the simultaneous treatment of echinococcosis and neoplasms considered to be equal to that of neoplasm treatment alone.

Conclusion

The clinical case demonstrates that multiple echinococcosis of the liver and abdominal cavity can be successfully treated when surgical resection is possible even in the presence of other presumably benign (leaving possibility of malignancy) diseases, such as mature ovarian teratoma and uterine leiomyoma.
