Exploring effects of greenness and extreme heat exposure on hypertension incidence in elderly Chinese: a national cohort study
Authors: Yifan Tang, Sitong Chen, Weijia Wang, Yifan Chen, Kai Wang, Xu Wang, Long Bai, Chengxiu Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension poses a significant health challenge, particularly within aging populations, yet the health effects of modifiable environmental factors remain insufficiently understood. This study aims to examine the association between greenness and hypertension among older adults in China, and to determine whether the association is modified by extreme heat exposure.MethodsWe selected 7,726 participants aged ≥ 65 without hypertension at baseline from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), recruited during the 2000, 2002, and 2005 waves, and followed up through the 2018 survey. Greenness and extreme heat exposure during the inter-hypertension interval in each participant’s city of residence were quantified as the average Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the total number of days with daily mean temperature exceeding the T-year return level, based on Extreme Value Theory (EVT), respectively. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the association between greenness and hypertension, incorporating interaction terms to evaluate extreme heat exposure modifications.ResultsEach 0.1-unit increase in NDVI was associated with a 9.6% lower risk of developing hypertension (HR = 0.904; 95% CI: 0.859–0.952), after adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and physical health variables, as well as extreme heat exposure at the 20-year return level and its interaction with NDVI. The exposure-response curve revealed an inverted V-shaped pattern, with risk decreasing up to an NDVI of around 0.65 before plateauing or reversing. Consistent significant negative interaction was found between NDVI and extreme heat exposure across various T-year return levels, with the most pronounced one at the largest 20-year return level, suggesting that greenness may mitigate the incidence of hypertension during intense heat periods. Stratified analyses showed stronger protective associations in rural or town residents, single or divorced individuals, and those with limited gardening activity (all p-interaction < 0.05), with consistent protective effects observed across both low and high NDVI exposure levels.ConclusionGreen space mitigated the incidence of hypertension in older adults, particularly during extreme heat events. Urban greening could serve as a dual adaptation strategy against climate change and population aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06467-7.Keywords: Hypertension, Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Cox regression model, Extreme Value Theory, Population aging

Introduction

Population aging poses a significant global health and sociodemographic challenge, as highlighted in the 2020 World Population Aging Report by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. The report projects that by 2030, more than 700 million people will be 65 years old or above, underscoring the worsening of the global aging problem [1, 2]. China’s rapidly aging population faces significant challenges, such as increased incidence of chronic diseases, cognitive decline, and reduced social participation among the elderly [3, 4]. These issues pressure the country’s public health and increase the burden of diseases, calling for higher standards for the elderly service system [5]. Hypertension is a prevalent condition among the elderly, closely related to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, cognitive decline, and loss of autonomy, with its prevalence increasing with population aging, especially in low- and middle-income countries where it is notably higher [6–8]. In China, the prevalence of hypertension reached 27.9% between 2012 and 2018, affecting more than 250 million people [9]. Moreover, hypertension imposes substantial economic burdens through direct medical costs and lost productivity resulting from premature death and disability [10]. Consequently, it is imperative to identify readily modifiable determinants of hypertension for informing effective prevention and control strategies [11, 12].

Green space refers to open, undeveloped land with natural vegetation such as urban parks, gardens, and forests, and is an essential environmental factor in health sciences [13]. Recent studies have shown that green spaces in residential areas positively impact cardiovascular health, and greenness is considered an emerging modifiable environmental factor for hypertension [14, 15]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the health benefits of greenness, including promotion of physical activity [16, 17], stress reduction [18], and mitigation of air pollution [19, 20], all of which may contribute to reducing hypertension risk. This protective effect of greenness against hypertension is complex due to potential confounders, including demographic and lifestyle factors [20, 21]. Nevertheless, key health-related indicators in older adults, such as cognitive function, activities of daily living (ADL), and mental health [22], are often overlooked in such associations. In this study, we address this gap by incorporating these health dimensions alongside conventional demographic and lifestyle covariates in the greenness–hypertension analysis.

With the warming climate and an aging population, exposure to heat poses an increasing threat to public health, particularly among older people [23, 24]. Older individuals have reduced physiological capacity to adapt to heat stress, including diminished ability to regulate body temperature, altered sweating responses, impaired heat-induced increases in skin blood flow, and a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease [25, 26]. Given that greenness may buffer heat-related stress through pathways such as counteracting near-surface daytime shortwave radiation, increasing atmospheric humidity, and providing cooling effects [27, 28], examining its interaction with extreme heat exposure is critical for identifying synergies in preventing hypertension. To the best of our knowledge, the interaction between green spaces, heat exposure, and hypertension has been examined in few epidemiological studies [29], which used traditional percentile-based thresholds to define heat exposure and focused solely on the frequency of temperatures exceeding a high threshold. This approach neglects the magnitude of exceedance and the intensity of events, thus failing to accurately capture the statistical properties of extreme temperature events [30, 31]. To address this limitation and better capture the modifiable effect of extreme heat events, our study will utilize Extreme Value Theory (EVT), a well-established method widely used in climate science [32], disaster risk assessment [33, 34], and environmental health [35]. By employing EVT, we aim to identify extreme heat events across multiple return periods, thereby improving the accuracy of exposure assessment in examining its interaction with green spaces.

We utilized data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) spanning 2000 to 2018 to explore the combined impacts of green and heat exposures on the incidence of hypertension in older adults. The findings are expected to provide evidence for integrating environmental considerations into hypertension prevention strategies for the aging population, such as prioritizing green infrastructure in heat-vulnerable communities and incorporating heat-health risk assessments into geriatric care guidelines.

Methods

Study design and population

The study population was collected from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), an ongoing population-based prospective cohort designed to investigate determinants of healthy aging among older adults in China [36]. The CLHLS uses a multistage, stratified cluster sampling strategy, randomly selecting counties and cities from 23 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities, together covering more than 85% of the national population. Approximately half of the counties or districts in each province were chosen, yielding 501 counties representing both urban and rural areas [37]. The baseline survey began in 1998, with follow-up waves in 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011/2012, 2014, 2018, and 2022. At each wave, trained interviewers collected health-related information through structured questionnaires and anthropometric measurements. Detailed descriptions of the study design and data collection have been published previously [38, 39].

In this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed data for the participants who were recruited at baseline during the second (2000), third (2002), and fourth (2005) waves of the CLHLS, followed up through to the most recent wave in 2018, as data from the 2022 wave have not yet been released on the official data repository. The final study population comprised 7, 726 out of 14, 473 participants. Exclusions were applied sequentially, beginning with 197 individuals aged < 65 years, 4, 438 with a baseline diagnosis of hypertension, and 866 lacking baseline blood pressure measurements. Subsequently, 178 participants with incomplete demographic data, 872 whose city codes could not be matched to external environmental data, and 196 without follow-up blood pressure records were removed. The CLHLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee, Peking University (IRB00001052-13074), and the Institutional Review Board, Duke University (Pro00062871). All participants provided written informed consent.

Hypertension assessment and covariates

As part of the CLHLS, participants’ systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured by a trained healthcare provider using a traditional mercury sphygmomanometer during the baseline phase and at each subsequent visit. In this study, participants were considered to have hypertension if their SBP exceeded 140 mm Hg and/or their DBP exceeded 90 mm Hg or if they self-reported a history of hypertension [40].

The inter-hypertension interval was defined as the time elapsed from the most distant survey wave in which the participant was free of hypertension (start point) to the first subsequent survey wave at which hypertension was diagnosed (end point) [41]. Each hypertension period of an individual was treated as an independent episode, assuming independence between all observed event times regardless of whether they corresponded to the same or different participants [42]. For example, a participant (Appendix Figure A.1) was diagnosed with hypertension in 2002 and 2011, with normal blood pressure between 2005 and 2008. We defined this case as two time-to-event processes with time intervals of 2 and 6 years, respectively. Such an approach could accommodate better time-varying covariates and discontinuous intervals of risks [43]. During the study period, a total of 35, 995 person-years of follow-up were accumulated among 7, 726 participants, with an average follow-up duration of 4.66 years per participant.

We collated 14 key variables from the CLHLS database, representing the most frequently influential factors of the health and well-being of older adults, namely demographic characteristics, behavioral lifestyles, and health status [37, 40]. Demographic characteristics include age groups (65–79, 80+), gender (male or female), residence (urban or rural/town), marital status (married or single/divorced), ethnicity (Han or minority), and living arrangements (with family or in nursing homes). Behavioral lifestyle factors cover smoking status (current smoker as “yes” and former or non-smoker as “no”), drinking status (current drinker as “yes” and former or non-drinker as “no”), physical activity (currently active as “yes” and former or inactive as “no”), and the frequency of outdoor and gardening activities (almost every day or sometimes/never). Three health status variables are included in this study to assess participants’ ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL), cognitive function, and mental health: ADL is considered “healthy” if a participant demonstrates full capability in completing daily activities without assistance; cognitive function is classified as “healthy” if a participant correctly answers at least four out of five questions related to time, month, holiday date, season, and location; mental health is defined as “healthy” if a participant correctly answers at least five out of seven questions related to mood, anxiety, and irritability.

Exposures

NDVI

We estimated green space exposure using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), a widely used satellite-based index in remote sensing, to assess vegetation conditions and quantify the greening level by capturing chlorophyll content in vegetation canopies [44]. In general, NDVI values range from to 1, with positive values indicating green vegetation, higher values reflecting denser vegetation, and negative values representing non-vegetative surfaces like snow, ice, or water; 0 is associated with rocks or bare soil [45].

NDVI values were obtained from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Vegetation Index product on NASA’s Terra satellite, which provides 250 m spatial resolution and 16-day composite cloud-filtered observations, and are publicly available from the MODIS data repository [46]. First, for each 16-day period, we calculated the average NDVI values from all pixels within the administrative boundaries of each city. Then, we further averaged these city-level values across all composite periods within a calendar year to derive the annual mean NDVI for each city. Finally, we calculated the NDVI exposure for each participant by averaging the annual mean NDVI values during the inter-hypertension interval specific to the participant’s city of residence. To facilitate the interpretation of the results, we multiplied the NDVI values by 10 and used a 0.1-unit change in NDVI as the exposure metric in our models.

Extreme heat exposure

We utilized temperature data from the ERA5-Land reanalysis of historical observations for our primary analyses [47]. The ERA5-Land dataset provides hourly temperature data, based on satellite observations and ground-based measurements assimilated into a numerical weather prediction model, at a 9 km 9 km spatial resolution. We first averaged the 24 hourly temperature to obtain the daily mean temperatures for each grid. Subsequently, we calculated city-level temperature averages by aggregating these daily mean temperatures across all grids that fall within the administrative boundaries of each city.

To estimate the return levels of heat events using EVT, we analyzed daily temperature data for each city from 1981 to 2023. Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses included frequency and percentage of categorical variables. Chi-square test was employed to show the association of categorical variables with the risk of hypertension. We constructed five Cox proportional hazards regression models to explore the association between green exposure, extreme heat exposure, and the incidence of hypertension: Model 1 was a reference model with NDVI only; Models 2 to 4 added sequentially sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, and physical health factors, which all maintain VIF < 5 to avoid multicollinearity. Model 5, the final model, additionally adjusted for extreme heat exposure and its interaction with NDVI. This stepwise adjustment approach allowed us to assess the consistency and robustness of the association between NDVI and the incidence of hypertension. We also developed exposure-response curves to assess the nonlinear association between NDVI exposure and hypertension, using natural cubic splines with two interior knots located at the 25th and 75th percentiles [50]. In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis to explore the effect modification of NDVI on the incidence of hypertension across various demographic and lifestyle subgroups. To this, we divided the NDVI data at the median (50th percentile) into two groups: low-level (0.498–0.756) and high-level (0.757–0.877) NDVI exposure environments.

Effects of NDVI were estimated as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using statistical software R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Descriptive analysis

Among the 7, 726 elderly participants, 3, 723 individuals (approximately 48.19%) were aged over 80 years (see Table 1). Most were of Han ethnicity (93.46%), resided in rural or town areas (74.26%), and lived with family members (86.21%). Significant differences were observed between the hypertensive and non-hypertensive groups across most variables, including age, gender, residence, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, exercise status, outdoor activity, gardening activity, cognitive competence, ADL, and mental health, except ethnicity and living arrangements. Hypertension prevalence was highest among those aged 65–79 (74.4%) and in married individuals (72.3%). It was also higher in the elderly living in rural areas (65.0%), smokers (65.9%), alcohol drinkers (66.0%), and those with poor mental health (63.8%). Chi-square tests indicate that these differences are statistically significant, with all p-values being less than 0.001, except for ethnicity and living arrangements.

Greenness, heat exposure, and hypertension risk

In this study, we employed Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine the association between greenness and the incidence of hypertension, as well as its interaction with extreme heat exposure. To ensure robust estimates, we fitted five progressively adjusted models, each adding covariates to account for potential confounders.

Greenness and hypertension

Figure 3a illustrates a significant negative association between NDVI and the incidence of hypertension, which was consistent across Models 1 to 5, becoming more pronounced as additional variables were adjusted. We presented the results of Model 5, which included extreme heat exposure at the 20-year return level, selected for its stronger interaction effect compared to the 2-, 5-, 10-, and 15-year return levels (see Appendix Table A.1). In this fully adjusted model, accounting for sociodemographic, lifestyle, physical health variables, and green and heat exposure, the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.904 (95% CI: 0.859 to 0.952), indicating a 9.6% lower risk of hypertension for each 0.1-unit increase in NDVI. To validate the linearity assumption, we performed a diagnostic analysis using the martingale residual plot against NDVI (Appendix Figure A.3). The plot showed no clear trend, with residuals randomly scattered around zero, suggesting that the linear assumption holds. Additionally, the Loess smooth curve was fairly flat without noticeable curvature, further supporting the linear relationship.

Given the potentially complex association, we applied nonlinear modeling approaches motivated by previous studies reporting possible threshold or saturation effects of greenness on health [19]. Figure 3(b) presents the exposure-response curves modeled using natural cubic splines, illustrating the relationship between NDVI and the incidence of hypertension across the exposure range. The curve exhibited an inverted V-shaped pattern, characterized by an initial increase in hypertension risk with rising NDVI values up to approximately 0.65, followed by a subsequent decline in risk. Notably, the protective effect of NDVI became more pronounced beyond a value of 0.75. This suggests a potential threshold effect or optimal NDVI range for minimizing the incidence of hypertension.

Subgroup analysis

Table 2 reveals that the association between NDVI levels and hypertension varies across subgroups, comparing low-level (0.498–0.756) and high-level (0.757–0.877) NDVI exposure environments. Specifically, in both low and high NDVI environments, several subgroups showed a significant protective effect against hypertension, as indicated by HR values below 1 with p-values < 0.001. For example, individuals aged 65–79 years, those residing in rural or town areas, and people from the Han ethnic group all exhibited a reduced risk of hypertension as NDVI exposure increased, regardless of whether the exposure level was low or high. Furthermore, among individuals with ADL and mental health, the protective effect of greenness appeared particularly prominent. Notably, for those with impaired cognitive function, the inverse association between high NDVI and the incidence of hypertension was also pronounced, although this association was only statistically significant with p-values < 0.01.

Discussion

China’s rapidly aging population posed a significant challenge to public health due to the high incidence of chronic diseases, particularly hypertension, among individuals aged 65 years and above. This study utilized longitudinal data from the CLHLS project (2000–2018) and satellite-based remote sensing data to examine the relationship between green exposure, heat exposure, and hypertension in the elderly.

Overall, green exposure was associated with a reduced risk of developing hypertension, consistent with previous research findings [20, 40, 51]. Several studies had explored the mechanisms through which green spaces influenced the incidence of hypertension. A study in India found that higher green space density was linked to reduced vascular aging, leading to less arterial and carotid expansion, reduced arterial stiffness, and decreased intimal thickening, all contributing to a lower risk of hypertension [52]. Notably, the relationship between NDVI and hypertension risk followed an inverted V-shaped pattern, with the HR increasing steadily as NDVI rose up to approximately 0.65, after which it began to decline. These findings are consistent with previous studies, highlighting the non-linear impact of greenness exposure on hypertension [19, 40]. Furthermore, at NDVI levels around 0.75, the HR dropped below 1, reinforcing our conclusion that higher urban green exposure may have a protective effect against hypertension risk. Therefore, urban planning should consider not only the quantity of green spaces but also their quality and accessibility to maximize health benefits [53, 54].

Our study highlighted the complex relationship between cognitive competence, ADL, and mental health in the context of green space exposure and the incidence of hypertension. We found that individuals with normal ADL and good mental health were more likely to actively utilize green spaces for outdoor activities such as walking and exercising [55]. The enhanced social cohesion facilitated by green spaces may further increase the frequency of social interactions among these individuals [56, 57]. These activities not only help reduce psychological stress and strengthen social ties but also promote physical health, collectively contributing to a lower risk of hypertension [58, 59]. In contrast, for individuals with impaired cognitive function, although their ability to manage their health was relatively weaker, high green exposure may indirectly have reduced their incidence of hypertension by alleviating environmental stress, improving sleep quality, reducing excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system, and encouraging caregiver-guided outdoor activities [60].

The interaction between greenness and heat exposure, particularly under extreme heat conditions, provided essential insights into the potential health benefits of urban green areas [61]. Specifically, the strongest and most significant interaction effect was observed under extreme heat exposure at the 20-year return level, where higher NDVI was associated with a markedly lower risk of hypertension. This suggests that green spaces may have mitigated the adverse health impacts of extreme heat [62], potentially acting as a buffer against heat-induced cardiovascular strain [29, 60]. The observed buffering effect can be attributed to the capacity of vegetation to lower ambient temperatures via shading and evapotranspiration [63, 64], thereby reducing thermal stress. Additionally, vegetation plays a crucial role in diminishing air pollution by capturing particulates and contributing to reduced emissions [65]. The findings corroborate the hypothesis that, although green spaces provide certain benefits in regulating temperature during less severe heat events, their efficacy in mitigating health risks, such as hypertension, becomes significantly more pronounced when heat exposure reaches elevated levels [66].

Significant effects were not observed at lower heat exposure thresholds. On one hand, the lack of significant effects at lower heat exposure thresholds may be partially attributable to limited statistical power at these levels (all power < 0.2) [67]. On the other hand, the observed variation in interaction coefficients across return periods suggests a potential nonlinear modification of the heat-hypertension association by greenness. This finding may necessitate further investigation in future studies to elucidate the underlying mechanism and interactions. While the protective effects of green space may have varied with heat exposure levels, the overall trend suggested that well-designed green spaces could have significantly reduced the health risks associated with extreme heat, including hypertension. Notably, our method based on EVT identified heat exposure thresholds that were higher than those derived from traditional 92.5%, 95%, or 97.5% percentile-based definitions [29, 68], resulting in a more stringent and reliable characterization of extreme heat events relevant to health risks. Future research should further investigate the underlying mechanisms of this interaction and explore how urban green spaces could have been optimized to protect vulnerable populations from the escalating effects of climate change [51].

Despite the utilization of the CLHLS database, which offers a large and representative sample, our study is subject to several limitations. First, some variables, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and exercise, were self-reported, possibly subject to recall bias [69]. Second, the inherent constraints of the CLHLS limited the range of covariates available for adjustment. Consequently, potentially influential confounders such as neighborhood-level socioeconomic status, overall dietary patterns, environmental noise exposure, and familial predisposition to hypertension were either unmeasured or only partially controlled. This omission may limit both the internal validity of the effect estimates and the external validity (generalizability) of the findings [70, 71]. Third, due to privacy protections inherent in the CLHLS, participants’ residential addresses were not accessible. Consequently, city-level exposure data were utilized to estimate individual exposure levels [40]. However, this may introduce non-differential misclassification and measurement errors, which typically induce attenuation bias and thus likely lead to underestimation of the true magnitude of the observed association.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the protective effect of green exposure against hypertension in the elderly, with a non-linear relationship observed between NDVI levels and the incidence of hypertension. Additionally, green spaces were found to have mitigated the health impacts of extreme heat, especially during high-intensity heat events. These findings highlighted the importance of green infrastructure in reducing the incidence of hypertension and enhancing resilience to climate change, especially in aging populations.
