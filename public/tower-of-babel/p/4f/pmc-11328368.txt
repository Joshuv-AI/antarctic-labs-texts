Competency level and determinants among infection prevention and control staff in the Middle East and North Africa region
Authors: Majid M. Alshamrani, Elias Tannous, Fatmah Othman, Mohammed Al Zunitan, Mohammed Abalkhail, Aiman El-Saed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompetency of the staff working in infection prevention and control (IPC) is the cornerstone of successful IPC programs. The objective was to assess competency level and associated factors among IPC staff working in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out in 2019 among active IPC staff who were members of the Arab Countries Infection Control Network. The questionnaire covered demographic and professional characteristics of IPC staff and characteristics of work facilities and IPC programs. A competency score was created from self-perceived responses to 8 competency domains. The competency score was then transformed into 100-scale and categorized into two groups (≥ median and < median).ResultsA total of 176 participants completed the survey. Participants were mostly female (65.7%), and the mean age was 40.2 ± 8.3 years. The mean competency score was 61.4%. It was slightly variable between domains, being highest with preventing transmission of infection (65%) and lowest with sterilization and disinfection (59%). Higher (≥ median) competency score was associated with having CBIC certification (p < 0.001). It was also associated with facilities having IPC plan (p = 0.005), IPC committee (p = 0.049), regular meetings of IPC committee (p < 0.001), and IPC plan included communications with healthcare workers (p < 0.001). Domain-specific competency scores were significantly associated with receiving same domain-specific training (p < 0.05 for all).ConclusionsThe competency levels of IPC staff in the MENA region is still suboptimal. The current finding calls for more regional and national investment in IPC staff by providing up-to-date customized educational and training opportunities.

Introduction

Competency of the staff working in infection prevention and control (IPC) is the cornerstone of successful IPC programs and patient safety initiatives [1]. IPC staff are frequently recruited from a wide range of professional backgrounds, with variable educational level, training, and certification [2, 3]. Additionally, IPC structure and function continue to evolve, adding a pressure on IPC staff to meet the growing demands for complex healthcare services and accountability in healthcare setting [4, 5]. Therefore, defining and assessing the professional competency of IPC staff is extremely critical for the delivery of quality services and maintaining professional development [6].

Competency has been defined as “the proven ability to use knowledge, skills and personal, social and/or methodological abilities in work or study situations and in professional and personal development” [6, 7]. Several competency models have been suggested to define competency domains, subdomains, and assessment indicators. The most widely accepted model is the one suggested by the American Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology (APIC) in 2012 [8] and modified in 2018 [9, 10]. Additionally, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and World Health Organization (WHO) introduced somewhat different competency models [6, 11]. These models list the knowledge and skills required to run basic IPC program including evidence-based preventive practices, surveillance, outbreak investigation, hygiene, leadership, quality improvement, education, and research. [12, 13].

Assessing the current level of competency is an essential step in the process of advancing the competency of IPC staff and maintaining their professional development [10]. The competency of IPC staff has been assessed in few reports in USA and Korea [14–17]. In these studies, between 25% and 56% of IPC staff self-assessed their competency as competent or as proficient [14–17]. Lack of similar competency data in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region is well-recognized [18]. Additionally, the countries in the MENA region vary in healthcare resources, healthcare settings, and prevalence of infectious diseases [19]. This variability makes regional assessment of competency even more important, particularly with lack of standardized framework and apparently context-sensitive nature of IPC competencies [20]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess self-perceived competency levels in IPC staff working in MENA healthcare systems and to determine the factors associated with that competency levels.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional online survey was carried out between January and April 2019. The study obtained all required ethical approvals from the Arab Countries Infection Control Network (AcicN) and King Abdullah International Medical Research Center.

Setting and population

The study targeted IPC staff actively working in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region and who were members of the AcicN [21]. The later is a non-profit collaborative network that creates a platform for IPC personnel to connect and share experiences from different MENA countries. IPC staff actively working in IPC were included, irrespective of their nationality, working country, or competency level. The details of the study population and methods have been published elsewhere [22]. Of 269 ICP staff who responded to the survey, 185 completed the competency section. Subject confidentiality were maintained throughout the process of this research as the name of the participants and their IDs had not been collected.

Data collection tool

The survey was developed based on the published APIC MegaSurvey questions [14, 23] and additional questions suggested by a board of subject matter experts. The questionnaire was composed of three sections, demographic and professional characteristics of IPC staff, characteristics of the facilities and infection control program involved, and competency-related characteristics. The competency was self-assessed for eight IPC domains, including identification of infectious disease processes, surveillance and epidemiologic investigation, prevention/control of the transmission of infectious agents, management and communication, education and research, employee/occupational health, environment al cleaning, and sterilization and disinfection. Content and face validity of the survey were assessed by subject matter experts in IPC and epidemiology. Additionally, a pilot study was conducted among 15 participants currently practicing IPC in MENA countries with very positive feedback. Cronbach’s alpha for competency questions was 0.90, which indicate a strong reliability.

Definitions

The competency level was defined based on the APIC conceptual model of IPC competency [8, 10]. The novice level indicates limited knowledge, skills, and experience related to IPC activities. The approaching proficiency level indicates that IPC staff continues to build on their knowledge/skills in the core competencies while developing into an independent practitioner. Fully proficient level indicates a mastery in fundamental knowledge required for competent performance of current IPC practice standards, frequently verified by certification. Approaching advanced level indicates that IPC staff continue to build on their knowledge/skills towards an expert level. Expert level indicates a mastery of IPC domains, while sharing their knowledge and skills through mentoring, education, research, publication, collaboration, leadership, and role model.

Competency score

The score was created from the responses to 8 competency questions shown in Table 1. The answers were scored 1 for “novice knowledge/skills,” 2 for “approaching proficiency,” 3 for “fully proficient,” 4 for “approaching advanced,” and 5 for “advanced or expert level.” All competency questions were given equal weight. The competency score was then transformed into 100-scale for easy interpretation. This was done by dividing the actual score by the maximum possible score (40 points). The competency score was then categorized into two groups (high competency ≥ median and low competency < median score).

Data analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD). Demographic and professional characteristics of IPC staff as well as characteristics of the facilities and IPC programs were compared between those with high competency (≥ median) and low competency (< median score). Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, were used to compare categorical variables. T-test or Mann Whitney test, as appropriate, were used to compare continuous variables. Correlation between continuous variables was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. All P-values were two-tailed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) were utilized for statistical analysis.

Results

Self-perceived competency level

A total of 176 participants completed self-perceived competency levels. As shown in Table 1, the most frequent level of competency was approaching proficiency (24.2%), followed by fully proficient (24.0%), approaching advanced (22.3%), advanced/expert (16.9%), and finally novice (12.7%). The mean and median competency score was 61% and 60%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1, the average domain-specific competency scores were slightly different in different IPC domains. The score was highest with preventing/controlling the transmission of infectious agents (65%), followed by management and communication (63%), surveillance and epidemiologic investigation (62%), environmental cleaning (62%), identification of infectious disease processes (60%), employee/occupational health (60%), education and research (59%), and finally sterilization, disinfection, asepsis (59%).

Demographic and professional characteristics

As shown in Table 2, the participants were mostly female (65.7%) and the mean age was 40.2 ± 8.3 years. More than half (57.2%) had a Middle Eastern nationality while one third (34.7%) had Asian nationality. More than 80% of the participants were working in Saudi Arabia (44.3%), United Arab Emirates (29.0%), and Egypt (8.0%). Approximately two-thirds (67.6%) had a nursing background while 22.2% had a medical background. Participants had on average 17.1 ± 8.4 years of healthcare experience, including 8.6 ± 5.7 years of IPC experience. The most common IPC qualifications/certifications was bachelor degree in nursing (58.0%), followed by certificate in infection control (52.3%), certification board of infection control and epidemiology (CBIC, 36.9%), diploma in infection control (31.3%), post-graduate degree (19.3%), and doctor of medicine (14.2%). More than 80% of those who were not CBIC-certified had a plan to get it. The majority (96.0%) of the participants received some infection control training while only half (49.4%) were ever assessed in current position based on APIC competency model. The most time consuming activities were surveillance (26.7 ± 16.7% of average weekly working time), prevention/control transmission and isolation (12.5 ± 5.8%), and investigation of outbreaks and other incidents (12.2 ± 6.6%). Almost 40% of the participants were not satisfied with current compensation. Out of all the above characteristics, higher (≥ median) competency score was associated with having CBIC (p < 0.001) but not diploma in infection control (p = 0.048). Additionally, it was associated with spending more time on identification of infectious disease (p = 0.010) and education/research (p = 0.041).

Characteristics of the facilities

As shown in Table 3, more than 60% of the facilities were under the governmental sector. The most frequent IPC settings were medical and surgical wards (83.5%) and intensive care units (83.0%). The facilities had on average 404 beds, with 18% of them had more than 500 beds. The most frequent ratios of IPC staff to the number of beds was 1/100 (37.4%) and 1/150 (28.7%). More than 90% of the facilities had IPC plan (96.0%), which was based on a risk assessment (92.0%), IPC committee (94.3%), and regular meetings of IPC committee (90.2%). Less than 20% of the facilities exert pressure on their IPC staff not to report healthcare-associated infection (HAI). Higher (≥ median) competency score was associated with having IPC plan (p = 0.005), IPC committee (p = 0.049), regular meetings of IPC committee (p < 0.001), and IPC plan included communications with healthcare workers (HCWs, p < 0.001).

Training and perceived competency

The most frequent training received by IPC staff was training in surveillance (90.3%), followed by training in education (81.0%), quality improvement (77.6%), outbreak investigation (74.1%), employee/occupational health (72.3%), cleaning/sterilization (71.7%), management/ communication (70.8%), and finally research (68.4%). As shown in Fig. 2, domain-specific competency scores were significantly higher among participants who received compared with those who did not receive same domain-specific training (p < 0.05 for all). The difference was highest (training-dependent competency) in cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization domain (17%) and lowest in surveillance and epidemiologic investigation domain (9%).

Working tasks and perceived competency

As shown in Table 4, there were weak correlations between domain-specific competency scores and average percentage of weekly time spent on activities of the same domain (correlation coefficient was < 0.2 in all). The only significant positive correlation between same-domain competency score and working time (competency-dependent tasks) was observed in education and research (r = 0.176, p = 0.029).

Discussion

The current study evaluated the competency level of different IPC tasks among IPC staff working in healthcare facilities in the MENA countries. The average competency of all different tasks was approximately 60%, with 13% perceived themselves as novice and 17% as experts. Comparing the current level of competency is a little challenging because of the limited number of relevant reports and the use of variable definitions/groups of competency [14–16]. For example, three-level competency was used in the APIC USA study, four-level in the Australian study, and five-level in the Korean study [14–16]. Nevertheless, if extremes would only be considered; novice level was 13% in the current study compared with an average 6–19% in previous studies [14–16]. Additionally, expert level was 17% in the current study compared with an average 11–28% in previous studies [14–16]. Domain-specific competency levels were highest in prevention/control of the transmission of infectious agents and lowest in education and research as well as sterilization and disinfection. This was very close to what have been reported in the USA and Korean studies [14, 15]. Nevertheless, the difference between domain-specific competency levels was only 6% in the current study compared with 10% in the Korean study and > 15% in the USA study.

Competency level in the current study was significantly associated with CBIC certification but not professional background. Previous studies confirmed the importance of IPC certification that positively impacts the competency [15, 17]. On the other hand, they were inconsistent with professional background/qualifications with some praised public health nurse or experienced nurses [14, 16] and others praised post-graduate degrees [15]. It should be noted that the professional background in the current study is different from other studies where IPC staff is totally [15, 17] or predominantly nurses [14]. The positive impact of CBIC on competency is expected given its close alignment with the APIC domains that define the competency in the current study [24, 25]. CBIC in recent years is increasingly recognized as a preferred/required job certification/recognition for IPC professionals [26]. Interestingly, the lower frequency of expert in the current study compared with the USA study (17% versus 28%) was consistent with the lower frequency of CBIC-certified staff (37% versus 56%) [2, 14]. The lack of the same finding with non-CBIC certifications (Diploma/ Master in infection control) may underscore the importance of restructuring regional certifications to be better aligned with APIC domains and regional challenges.

Despite the known differences in IPC challenges in the MENA region including programs and staffing [18], the current study utilized the APIC competency model which was mainly designed for the USA IPC professionals. It should be noted that competency levels or domains described by APIC are very generic and are shared by almost all other programs. The difference is probably not about the importance of these domains but the relative weight and quality of different domains [20]. For example, education, certification, and training are equally important in both APIC model [14, 23] and all other models implemented in MENA region [6, 11] but their current status and available resources are different. Therefore, these differences were highlighted in this study to identify areas of potential competency improvement in the MENA region. This may potentially help in building a globally recognized IPC competency model that can be customized to address specific regional epidemiological and infrastructural challenges [18].

Competency level in the current study was closely matching receiving domain-specific training. Additionally, training in some IPC domains was associated with a bigger yield in competency such as in cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization domains. Previous studies have shown that training programs that are tailored to the local staff needs can significantly improve their knowledge and critical thinking [14, 27, 28]. A number of studies suggested a framework for developing career of IPC staff through different methods, including training [10, 29]. Given the variable background, any training provided to new IPC staff should be tailored to the gaps in IPC knowledge and skills [10, 29]. This can be achieved under the mentorship of more expert IPC staff [10]. Additionally, APIC provides detailed customized roadmaps for professional development for IPC staff with different levels of competency [30]. The current finding underscores the critical need for more regional and national support for IPC staff in getting more up-to-date educational and customized training opportunities. This may take different formats; in-person training workshops, in-service mentorship, scholarships in elite hospitals, conferences, online courses, training videos, and simulation laboratory [31]. Attending courses and training programs as well as on-job-training are the main resources for gaining competency in USA [14].

Competency level in the current study was associated with specific organizational characteristics, such as having IPC plan, IPC committee, and regular IPC meetings. This may imply that hospitals that have organized and structured IPC programs attract more competent IPC staff and vice versa. On the other hand, domain-specific competency level in the current study had no or weak correlation with the average weekly time spent on activities of the same domain. The only exception in this study was education and research which obviously cannot be conducted without prior education/training. It seems that IPC managers in the current study allocated IPC tasks based on availability/convenience rather than competency. This is a little scary specially when critical patient-safety tasks are performed. This adds more pressure on the need to improve competency.

Although this study is the first in the region to comprehensively address IPC competency and its associated factors, a number of limitations should be acknowledged. The current study used a cross-sectional design that can confirm associations but not causation. The self-assessment of competency is largely subjective and may introduce bias. Nevertheless, self-assessment of competency was used in almost all relevant studies [14–17] due to lack of standard assessment tool that cover all IPC domains. Additionally, previous attempts to produce an objective assessment tool should be improved before its use in future studies [12] Finally, recruitment of the study participants using AcicN database which obviously does not represent all IPC staff in the region may have introduced some degree of selection bias. However, the utilization of this database was the best available option in the light of lack of comprehensive regional networks/database [18].

In conclusion, the current study is reporting suboptimal competency levels in different IPC domains among IPC staff working in healthcare facilities in the MENA countries. Competency levels were significantly associated with CBIC certification and closely matching receiving domain-specific training. The current finding calls for more regional and national investment in IPC staff by providing up-to-date and customized educational and training opportunities as well as recognized certification. Additionally, the finding should advocate the development of globally recognized IPC competency training framework and evaluation methods that can be used in both low-resource and high-resource IPC setting.
