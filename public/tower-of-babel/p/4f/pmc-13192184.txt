Evaluation of occurrence, antimicrobial resistance rates, and antibiotic resistance determinants in Klebsiella pneumoniae from hospitalised patients in Lagos, Nigeria
Authors: Rebecca O. Abegunrin, Kehinde O. Amisu, Sundus Nsaif Al-Huchaimi, Samuel O. Ajoseh, Wasiu O. Salami, Aminat O. Lawal-Sanni, Morteza Saki, Kabiru O. Akinyemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe study investigated the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and resistance determinants of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from hospitalized patients in Lagos, Nigeria.MethodsOne thousand four hundred and forty clinical samples (400 urine, 700 blood, and 340 stool) were collected from inpatients admitted to four public hospitals in Lagos between May 2021 and July 2023. The samples were analysed microbiologically. The isolates were identified using API 20E. and were subjected to in vitro antibiotic susceptibility testing, extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESβLs), β-lactamase AmpC, and carbapenemases. The real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time PCR) was used to determine antibiotic resistance genes, including ESBLs and carbapenemases, using specific primers.ResultsThree Klebsiella species were reported in this study, with an overall prevalence of 5.97% (86/1440). Of this, 4.5% (65/1440) K. pneumoniae, 1.04% (15/1440) K. oxytoca, and the least prevalent 0.42% (6/1440) K. aerogenes were documented. K. pneumoniae was isolated in all the age groups, but was highest in groups 1–10 years. There was no statistically significant association (P-value > 0.05) between the occurrence of K. pneumoniae and age groups or sex. Over 80% of K. pneumoniae isolates were MDR and were 100% resistant to cephalosporins, carbapenems, and polymyxin antibiotics. Six resistant patterns were recorded, with CAZ-CTX-CPM-CIP-CRO-AUG-GM-CO-IMP-MEM-ETP-DOR being the most frequently encountered pattern, at 46.2%. Specifically, 16 of the 20 isolates (80.0%) harboured one or two ESBL genes such as blaSHV, blaTEM and blaCTX−M, and carbapenemase genes including blaIMP, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA−48.ConclusionThis study revealed the presence of MDR K.pneumoniae and the emerging isolates with the co-existence of ESBL, AmpC, and carbapenemase genes. Surveillance, early detection, and adherence to antibiotic policies are essential to mitigate the spread.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative, encapsulated, non-motile, facultatively anaerobic bacterium [1], widely distributed in various environments and often seen in the digestive system of both healthy people and animals [2, 3]. This pathogen is associated with a range of infections, including surgical site infections, pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), and bloodstream infections (BSIs) [4, 5]. The spread of K. pneumoniae-associated nosocomial infections has dramatically increased using medical devices such as respiratory support equipment, venous catheters, and urinary catheters [6].

In recent years, K. pneumoniae has been recognised by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the critical priority “1” pathogens, requiring urgent development of next-generation antibiotics and the implementation of new control strategies [7]. Antimicrobial resistance is a major worldwide topic that presents a severe risk to public health. In 2019, more than one million deaths were caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates of bacteria [8, 9]. The ease of transmission of MDR bacteria, especially in healthcare settings, raises concerns, and it is projected that by 2030, the number of deaths attributable to MDR bacteria will exceed five million [10]. Interestingly, K. pneumoniae has developed resistance to carbapenems as a result of the acquisition of specific carbapenemase genes such as blaKPC, blaNDM, blaVIM, blaOXA−48, and blaIMP−Like [11].

Among carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates, K. pneumoniae is the most common bacterial species [12]. Since the isolation and characterisation of the first carbapenem-resistant strain of K. pneumoniae (KPC) in 1996, the prevalence of carbapenem-resistant isolates has rapidly increased, attracting significant public attention due to the limited treatment options available [13, 14]. The ineffectiveness of novel beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, such as ceftazidime-avibactam and meropenem/vaborbactam, against New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM)-producing pathogens, extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), and Ambler β-lactamase-producing K. pneumoniae further exacerbates the limited therapeutic choices against K. pneumoniae-associated diseases [15, 16].

In Nigeria, several studies have documented MDR K. pneumoniae isolated from hospitalised patients resisting different antibiotics, including third and fourth-generation cephalosporin, carbapenem, fluoroquinolones, and penicillin [17–20]. However, data on the resistance mechanisms, such as ESBL and carbapenemase production and their encoding genes, are sparsely available. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence and resistance genes of MDR K. pneumoniae isolated from hospitalized patients in Lagos, Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study population, case definition, sample collection, and bacteriology

This research was conducted for 2 years between May 2021 and July 2023, involving 1440 inpatients with various infections admitted in Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LASUTH), Alimosho General Hospital (AGH), Randle General Hospital (RGH), and Badagry General Hospital (BGH), which contributed 540, 640, 100, and 160 samples, respectively. The research received ethical clearance from the Lagos State University Teaching Hospital ethics board, with the approval number Ref. No. LREC 06/10/1613. In addition, the consent of the patients was obtained before their participation. Patient data, including age and sex, were recorded.

Aseptic sample collection was performed, including 700 blood samples, 400 urine samples, and 340 stool samples. The specimens were collected from individuals diagnosed with urinary tract infections, gastroenteritis, bacteremia, and soft tissue abscesses. The bacterial culture was performed using brain heart infusion (BHI) and MacConkey agar plates (Oxoid, UK), which were incubated overnight at 37 °C in the presence of oxygen. The lactose-fermenting colonies were subjected to biochemical identification following the method outlined by Crown and Steel in 1993 [21]. Based on the Cowan and Steel criteria, colonies that showed positive reaction in Christensen’s citrate or Simmons’ citrate, urease, ONPG, lysine decarboxylase, gas from glucose, methyl red (MR), and negative reaction in Voges–Proskauer test, phenylalanine, ornithine decarboxylase, arginine dihydrolase, and H2S from triple suger iron (TSI) were considred Klebsiella species. They were further identified through biochemical tests using the MICROBACT 24E identification system (Oxoid, UK). Also, non-fermenting Gram negative bacilli were identified by MICROBACT 24E identification system (Oxoid, UK). Gram-positive bacteria were identified by API Gram Positive Identification kit (bioMérieux, France) according to company instructions.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

AST was performed on all verified isolates of K. pneumoniae using both disk diffusion and microdilution techniques and Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) protocols [22]. The following antibiotic disks were utilized: amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20 µg/10 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), ceftrixone (30 µg), erythromycin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), doripenem (10 µg), ertapenem (10 µg), and meropenem (10 µg). (Oxoid, UK). Zone interpretation charts were compared with the diameters of the inhibition zones formed around the antibiotic for interpretation [22]. For consistency, the microdilution test was performed using the same set of antibiotics plus tigecycline and colistin (Sigma, Deisenhofen, Germany), following the instructions provided by the CLSI [22]. This study defined resistance for isolates that showed intermediate or complete resistance to the tested antibiotics. MDR phenotype was used to describe isolates that exhibited non-susceptibility to at least one drug in three or more antimicrobial categories [23]. Multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARI) was measured using the formula a/b. In this formula, the letter “a” represents the number of antibiotics to which a bacterial isolate is resistant, and the letter “b” represents the total number of antibiotics to which that particular isolate has been exposed [24]. Escherichia coli ATCC® 25,922 was used as quality control strain.

Phenotypic screening and confirmation for ESBL, AmpC, and carbapenemase

The phenotypic detection of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), AmpC β-lactamases, and carbapenemases in K. pneumoniae isolates was investigated using the MASTDISCS® Combi ESBL, AmpC, and carbapenemase detection Set-D72C (Mast Diagnostics, Merseyside, UK) [25, 26]. A sterile cotton swab immersed in the standardized inoculum was employed to uniformly streak the entire surface of a Mueller-Hinton agar plate, which was then allowed to dry for 3 to 5 min prior to the equidistant placement of the six Set-D72C Carbi discs (designated A through F) using a sterile dispenser to ensure secure agar contact. The plates were subsequently incubated at 35 ± 2 °C in ambient air for 16 to 18 h in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines (Mast Diagnostics, Merseyside, UK) [25, 26]. Subsequently, the six discs from the D72C set—comprising disc A (cefpodoxime 10 µg, CPD), disc B (CPD + ESBL inhibitor, clavulanic acid), disc C (CPD + AmpC inhibitor), disc D (CPD + ESBL inhibitor + AmpC inhibitor), disc E (CPD + ESBL inhibitor + AmpC inducer), and disc F (a penem antibiotic such as meropenem or imipenem)—were placed on the inoculated MHA plate, ensuring a center-to-center separation of at least 20–25 mm to avoid zone overlap, followed by inverted incubation at 35 ± 2 °C in ambient air for 16–20 h. Upon incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters for all discs, with interpretations derived from manufacturer guidelines to delineate β-lactamase types based on differential zone sizes relative to disc F [25, 26]. Specifically, a zone diameter of ≤ 19 mm around disc F for carbapenemase production. ESBL positivity was indicated by a ≥ 5 mm increase in zone B over zone A and zone D over zone C [25, 26]. AmpC positivity activity was determined when zone C exceeded aones A and B by at least 5 mm, or as inducible activity through distorted or flattened zones adjacent to disc E on discs A or D; and co-production of ESBL and AmpC was inferred from comparisons among discs A through D [25, 26].

DNA extraction and purification

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Tris-EDTA (TE) boiling extraction method followed by purification and concentration spectrophotometrically (BIO-RAD Smart Spec 3000; Hercules, CA, USA) [24]. In this method, fresh colonies of 24-hour culture were suspended in 200 µL of TE buffer and placed in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 5 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min. A 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a sterile microtube and stored at -20 °C for real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time PCR) [24].

Genotypic detection of ESBL and carbapenemase genes of K. pneumoniae

Real-time PCR reactions were performed at defined volumes and concentrations, and the target specificity and sensitivity were evaluated using published primer/probe sequences, including ESBL genes (blaCTX−M, blaSHV, and blaTEM) and genes encoding carbapenemases such as blaVIM, blaNDM−1, blaIMP, blaKPC, and blaOXA−48 (Table 1) [24, 27] verified by BLAST on the National Center for Biotechnology Information website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Quantitative PCR was run on a Qiagen Rotor-Gene Q 2plex using 36-well tubes sealed with four-cap strips, with a total of 45 cycles [28]. The program consisted of an initial 95 °C denaturation for 60 s (1 cycle), followed by 44 cycles of 95 °C for 60 s, 63 °C for 30 s, and 60 °C for 30 s; a post-amplification melt-curve step was performed at 72 °C for 15 s as previously described by Akinyemi et al. [28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS version 20 to facilitate a structured examination of the collected data. Frequency distributions were used to describe patterns within the dataset, and associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05, reducing the probability that observed effects reflected random variation.

Results

Prevalence of bacterial isolates from clinical samples collected from different hospitals in Lagos, Nigeria

A total of 512 bacterial isolates were recovered from 1440 clinical samples, representing a prevalence of 35.56%. The majority of isolates (235) originated from urine samples (400), followed by blood (220 from 700 samples), and stool (57 from 340 samples). Among the urine isolates, Escherichia coli was the most prevalent (n = 84, 35.74%), followed by K. pneumoniae (n = 29, 12.34%), Salmonella spp. (n = 22, 9.36%), and Proteus spp. (n = 20, 8.51%). Salmonella species were the most common (n = 32, 14.54%) from blood samples, followed by Proteus spp., (n = 26, 11.81%), E. coli (n = 24, 10.91%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 24, 10.91%). K. pneumoniae (n = 20, 9.09%) and K. aerogenes (n = 5, 2.27%) recorded the lowest prevalence. Furthermore, Salmonella spp. were isolated from 52.63% (n = 30) of the stool samples, followed by K. pneumoniae (n = 16, 2.07%) and E. coli ( n =, 7.08%). Other species, including K. oxytoca, Serratia spp., and Proteus spp., were also isolated, while K. aerogenes was the least isolated (n = 1, 1.75%) (Table 2).

Distribution of Klebsiella species from clinical sources based on demographic factors

In this investigation, three Klebsiella species including K. pneumoniae (n = 65, 4.51%), K. oxytoca (n = 15, 1.04%), and K. aerogenes (n = 6, 0.42%), with an overall prevalence of 5.97% (86/1440) across various clinical settings and patient demographics, were identified (Table 3). K. pneumoniae was detected in all clinical prognoses, with the highest prevalence in urinary tract infections (n = 29, 7.25%). K. aerogenes was only detected in bacteraemia (n = 5, 0.71%) and gastroenteritis (n = 1, 1.11%). A significant correlation (P-value = 0.021) was observed between the presence of Klebsiella species and the hospital of origin. Prevalence varied by age, with the highest incidence of K. pneumoniae in the 1–10 years age group at 6.43% (9/140). Gender-wise, K. pneumoniae was slightly more prevalent in females at 4.55% (38/835) compared to males at 4.46% (27/605). No significant association was found between Klebsiella species occurrence and age groups or sex, suggesting that these factors may not significantly influence the occurrence of these bacteria.

Antibiotic susceptibility and resistance patterns of K. pneumoniaeisolated from clinical sources in Lagos

Six different resistance patterns are observed in the K. pneumoniae isolates, showing high levels of multidrug resistance. For instance, all three Klebsiella species exhibited high antibiotic resistance, with 100% resistance to cephalosporins, carbapenems, and polymyxin (Table 4). Notably, Klebsiella species resisted 10–12 out of 15 commonly used antibiotics and MARI indexes between 0.67 and 0.8 (Table 5). For instance, the most common resistance pattern is resistance to 12 antibiotics (CAZ, CTX, CPM, CIP, CRO, AUG, GM, CO, IMP, MEM, ETP, DOR) and is seen in 30 isolates with a MAR index of 0.8. The second most common pattern is resistance to 12 antibiotics (CAZ, CTX, CPM, CIP, CRO, AUG, CO, TGC, IMP, MEM, ETP, DOR) seen in 3 isolates with a MARI index of 0.8. Resistance to 11 antibiotics (CAZ, CTX, CPM, CIP, CRO, AUG, CO, IMP, MEM, ETP, DOR) is seen in 12 isolates for the third pattern and six isolates for the 4th pattern, both with a MAR index of 0.73. The 5th and 6th patterns show resistance to 10 and 11 antibiotics in 6 isolates, with a MAR index of 0.67 and 0.73 (Table 5).

Phenotypic occurrence of ESBL, AmpC, and carbapenemase production in K. pneumoniae isolated from clinical cases

In this study, 20 out of 65 K. pneumoniae isolates obtained from three clinical sources (stool, blood, and urine) tested positive for any of the investigated β-lactamases, including ESBL and carbapenemases, representing an overall prevalence of 31%. Among these 20 positive isolates, stool samples contributed the highest proportion at 50% (10/20), followed by urine at 30% (6/20) and blood at 20% (4/20), with these proportions derived from the distribution of resistant isolates across sources (as total isolates per source were not specified). Regarding the frequency of specific resistant types, ESBL producers accounted for 30% (6/20) of the positives, distributed as 67% (4/6) from stool, 33% (2/6) from blood, and 0% (0/6) from urine; in contrast, carbapenemase producers comprised 40% (8/20) of the positives and were observed across all sample types, with 50% (4/8) from urine, 25% (2/8) from blood, and 25% (2/8) from stool. This study recorded six isolates (30%) of AmpC-producing K. pneumoniae from patients with urinary tract infections.

Occurrence of ESBL and carbapenemase genes in K. pneumoniae

In this study, 65% (13/20) of the phenotypically ESBL and or carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae isolates harboured genes encoding any of these mechanisms of resistance. Specifically, one or two of the four investigated carbapenemase genes [blaIMP, blaVIM, blaOXA−48, blaKPC, and blaNDM−1] and ESBL genes [blaSHV, blaTEM, and blaCTX−M] were harboured by 85% (11/13) K. pneumoniae isolates (Table 6). Among the 13 K. pneumoniae isolates, carbapenemase-encoding genes showed distinct prevalence trends: blaVIM was most common in stool isolates (3/5) and urine isolates (1/2). However, blaKPC was frequent in K. pneumoniae from blood (2/6) and stool (1/5). Also, blaOXA−48 appeared in (2/5) K. pneumoniae recovered from stool, and (1/6) from blood. On the other hand, of the three ESBL encoding genes investigated, blaSHV was detected in (7/13) K. pneumoniae isolates, particularly in (4/5) K. pneumoniae from stool, and 1/2 from urine samples. However, blaTEM was common in 3/5 K. pneumoniae isolates from stool and 2/6 blood-sourced K. pneumoniae isolates. Also, blaCTX−M was detected in 4 of the 13 K. pneumoniae isolates, including 2/5 from stool and 2/6 from blood samples. Specifically, blaVIM frequently coexists with blaSHV in strains (S10, S18, B50, and U200) (Table 7). Interestingly, blaKPC pairs with blaSHV in S15 K. pneumoniae isolate. Specifically, bla IMP shows two distinct coexistence patterns: combined with blaOXA−48 and blaCTX−M in isolate B58, and paired with blaSHV in B100 isolate (Table 7). Additionally, blaNDM−1 co-existed with blaVIM alongside blaSHV in S18 and blaTEM in U195 isolate (Table 7).

Discussion

Drug resistance in K. pneumoniae is a global challenge [29], and it is usually a common pathogen causing hospital- and community-acquired infections [30]. In this study, 14 bacterial genera were identified, with an overall prevalence of 35.83%. The prevalence of each organism identified as Salmonella spp. 16.41%, E. coli 21.88%, Serratia 7.03%, Proteus spp. 9.38%, Citrobacter 1.56%, Staphylococcus aureus 3.52%, P. aeruginosa 6.64%, Enterobacter spp. 1.17%, Shigella spp. 2.34%, Providencia 2.73%, E. faecalis 1.95%, Edwardsiella 3.13%, and A. baumannii 5.47%. Also, Klebsiella species accounts for a total prevalence of 5.97% (86/516), comprising 65 (75.58%) K. pneumoniae, 15 (17.44%) K. oxytoca and 6 (6.97%) K. aerogenes. The isolation of bacteria in the Enterobacterales family and other bacterial families has been reported in blood, urine and stool samples of hospitalised patients and is known to play roles in the disease aetiology [19, 31]. Interestingly, K. pneumoniae was the most predominant of all the Klebsiella species, with 7.25% (7/400) K. pneumoniae recovered from urine, 2.87% (20/700) from blood, and 4.71% (16/340) from stool. Several studies have reported varying prevalences of K. pneumoniae from different sources, including urine [32–34], blood [35, 36], and stool [37], and have been implicated in many diseases, such as urinary tract infections [38], septicemia [39], and gastrointestinal tract diseases [40].

In this study, K. pneumoniae was identified across all clinical prognoses investigated. For instance, 10.0% of K. pneumoniae were reported from patients with abdominal pain without gastroenteritis and diarrhoea. This finding is similar to 20.1% of K. pneumoniae reported in patients with Chinese intra-abdominal infections [41] and 12.9% of K. pneumoniae reported in patients with intra-abdominal infections by Hawser et al. [42]. Furthermore, this study observed that 7.25% of K. pneumoniae was isolated from patients with urinary tract infections. This result is consistent with the studies elsewhere in which K. pneumoniae-associated urinary tract infections, as well as K. oxytoca, contributed to the high prevalence recorded [43–45]. Moreover, 5.56% K. pneumoniae and 2.0% K. oxytoca were recorded in patients with gastroenteritis, and 3.0% in diarrheogenic patients. Similar studies have also documented K. oxytoca with K. pneumoniae and K. aerogenes in patients with gastroenteritis and diarrhoea in other regions of the globe [46–48]. Also, this study recorded 2.6% K. pneumoniae, 0.71% K. aerogenes, and 0.43% K. oxytoca from patients with bacteremia. Higher prevalences of K. pneumoniae and K. oxytoca have been reported in patients with septicemia by several studies [49–51]. However, few studies have reported the occurrence of K. aerogenes, formerly Enterobacter aerogenes, from clinical samples of patients with different clinical manifestations [52–54] .

The demographic analysis of the Klebsiella species recovered in this study showed K. pneumoniae occurred in all the age groups studied, with age group 1–10 years having been at the highest risk in 6.43% of the subjects studied. There was a higher occurrence of K. pneumoniae in females at a 4.55% (38/835) prevalent rate than 4.46% (27/605) recovered from males. Moreover, 0.59% (5/835) K. aerogenes was detected in females, while 0.17% (1/605) K. aerogenes was detected in males. Also, 1.08% (9/835) of K. oxytoca was detected in female and 0.99% (6/605) in male patients. Similarly, Namratha et al. [55] The highest isolation rate among patients aged 60 years and above was reported, with a prevalence of 28.57% in males and 32.43% in females. Chakraborty et al. [50]. A high prevalence rate of Klebsiella species in males over 50 years was also reported. The high occurrence documented by these studies might be associated with the weaker immune status of older people. However, the reason for the high occurrence of Klebsiella species in lower ages (1–10 years), as recorded in this study compared to other studies, might be connected to the immature immune system of the patient’s age group, increased exposure rate, and hygiene practices.

Furthermore, in this study, all K. pneumoniae exhibited complete (100%) susceptibility to tigecycline and resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics, including cephalosporins, carbapenems, and colistin. Additionally, the isolates displayed varying (50–67%) resistance levels to other commonly used antibiotics such as gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, and erythromycin. This study shared some similarities with that of Sahu et al. [47]. who reported high susceptibility of K. pneumoniae to tigecycline (97.29%). But, conversely, to this study, high susceptibility to colistin (96.95%), levofloxacin (93%), gentamycin (96.5%), amikacin (94.5%), imipenem (95.79%), and ampicillin (94.41%) were also reported in their study. Furthermore, Zhao et al. [56] reported high (92–100%) K. pneumoniae resistance to third-generation cephalosporin and carbapenem, with complete susceptibility to tigecycline. However, the elevated resistance of K. pneumoniae to colistin observed in this investigation aligns with findings from various global studies that have documented similar patterns of antimicrobial resistance [57–59]. The disparity between this study and other investigations could be associated with the misuse of antibiotics in different regions, resistance gene transfer among bacterial pathogens, mutations, and hygiene practices.

Over 80% of K. pneumoniae were MDR with a MAR index of 0.7–0.8. Among these isolates, eight distinct resistance profiles were identified. This study revealed that K. pneumoniae exhibited a high resistance profile CAZ-CTX-CPM-CIP-CRO-AUG-CO-IMP-MEM-ETP-DOR. Six resistant patterns were recorded in K. pneumoniae, with CAZ-CTX-CPM-CIP-CRO-AUG-GM-CO-IMP-MEM-ETP-DOR being the most frequently encountered pattern in 46.2% of the isolates. This high resistance profile and the index could be attributed to antibiotic abuse, veterinary usage of antibiotics, mutation, and resistance gene transfer. The high resistance of K. pneumoniae to cephalosporin, colistin, and carbapenem is a threat to public health, particularly in developing countries such as Nigeria, where antibiotics are seldom abused because they are expensive, and the cost of treatment of a patient with K. pneumoniae-associated disease is around 150 dollars. This development is worrisome and calls for urgent attention. Nevertheless, we reiterate the recent suggestion by Akinyemi et al. [19]. for the need to constantly review the antibiotics prescription process and strict antimicrobial stewardship implementation.

In the present study, 20 of the 65 clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae obtained from diverse infection sites produce at least one of the β-lactamases examined. Specifically, six isolates (30%) exhibited ESBL production. A remarkably high prevalence (84%) of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae was observed elsewhere [60]. Several other investigations have also reported elevated rates of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae. For instance, Feizabadi et al. [61] documented 72.1%, Sharma et al. [61]. 67.04%, Hoda Hassan et al. [62] 66.7%, and Mohammed et al. [63]. 30%. The variations in prevalence could be attributed to differences in geographical locations, sample sources, sample sizes, and the acquisition of resistance genes. This study recorded six isolates (30%) of AmpC-producing K. pneumoniae from patients with chronic urinary tract infections such as nephritis and pyelonephritis, among others. Similarly, Bhaskar et al. [60] observed 14 (7.8%) cases of AmpC-producing K. pneumoniae, while Hemalatha et al. [64]. identified 7 (9.2%) K. pneumoniae isolates that tested positive for AmpC. However, two separate Indian studies reported 8% and 43% prevalence rates of AmpC producers [65, 66]. Furthermore, in this investigation, eight isolates (40%) of K. pneumoniae with MBL activity. Over the years, K. pneumoniae has acquired carbapenemases, enzymes capable of breaking down most β-lactams, including carbapenems [16, 60]. Recent data suggest a rise in the prevalence of carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria in the last few years [67]. In a study by Bhaskar et al. [60]., 66% of the isolates were carbapenemase producers. Similarly, Gupta et al. [36]. reported a high prevalence of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae in New Delhi. Therefore, the detection of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae with the production of these enzymes from inpatients calls for urgent attention and further investigation to avert the possible persistence and transmission of these pathogens in the Lagos ecosystem.

In this study, 65% (13/20) of the phenotypically ESBL and/or carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae isolates carried genes associated with these resistance mechanisms. Intriguingly, 35% (7/20) isolates expressing ESBL or carbapenemase phenotypes alone or in combination were found to lack detectable genes encoding these enzymatic functions. This might be due to the occurrence of other ESBL or carbapenemase genes un-investigated in our study or the occurence of mutations in chromosomal genes encoding porins. Specifically, among the four investigated carbapenemase genes (blaIMP, blaVIM, blaOXA−48, and blaNDM−1) and the three ESBL genes (blaSHV, blaTEM, and blaCTX−M), 85% (11/13) of K. pneumoniae isolates harboured one or two of these resistance genes. Furthermore, 11/13 K. pneumoniae isolates harboured any of the ESBL encoding genes. Of this, 6/11 K. pneumoniae carried blaSHV, and 5/11 K. pneumoniae isolates harboured each blaTEM and blaCTX−M. Surprisingly, 5/11 K. pneumoniae carried at least two of the ESBL encoding genes. Similar to this finding, Jassim and Hassan [68] also reported the occurrence of blaSHV, blaTEM, and blaCTX−M in 30/30 (100%) K. pneumoniae isolates. Furthermore, this study recorded that 11/13 of the carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae isolates harboured any of the four carbapenemase-encoding genes. Of this, 3/11 of the carbapenemase-producing-K. pneumoniae harboured each of blaIMP and blaKPC, 6/11 blaVIM, and 3/11 of the carbapenemase-producing-K. pneumoniae isolates carried blaOXA−48. Notably, 4/11 of the carbapenemase-producing-K. pneumoniae isolates harboured at least two of the four carbapenemase-encoding genes. Similarly, other studies have also reported the occurrence of these genes: blaIMP, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA−48 in co-existence or singly in clinical K. pneumoniae isolates from different regions [28, 69, 70]. Furthermore, Patil et al. [71]. recorded carbapenemase genes in 61.0% of K. pneumoniae isolates recovered from hospitalised patients. Specifically, 21% of the isolates carried the blaKPC gene, 16% blaNDM gene, 10% blaVIM gene, 8% blaOXA−48 gene, and 5% harboured the blaIMP gene [71]. Similarly, Laolerd et al. [72]. recovered a total of 287 Enterobacterales isolates that exhibited resistance to at least one of the carbapenemase genes: blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA−48, blaVIM, and blaIMP, with K. pneumoniae being the predominant species among the carbapenem-resistant bacterial isolates. Furthermore, Al-Hasso [73] detected carbapenemase genes in 49 K. pneumoniae isolates, most of which harboured blaKPC and blaNDM gene markers, and 20 (40.8%) of the isolates carried double or combined genes such as blaKPC + blaVIM, blaKPC + blaNDM, blaKPC + blaOXA−48. Amer et al. [74] and Kazi et al. [75]. reported carbapenemase gene detection rates of 97.7% and 98.2% in K. pneumoniae in Egypt and India, respectively. Notably, in this investigation, a significant proportion, 69% (9/13) of these isolates exhibited co-occurrence of ESBL genes and one or more carbapenemase genes. Studies elsewhere have reported the co-existence of carbapenemase and ESBL encoding genes in K. pneumoniae isolated from clinical sources [24, 76]. However, the differences observed in this present finding and some other reported studies elsewhere, as highlighted in this work, may arise from variations in the number and source of the isolates, as well as study locations. Therefore, considering that carbapenems are currently the most potent and effective agents for treating bacterial infections, conserving their efficacy against resistant microorganisms is crucial. These findings highlight a high distribution of Klebsiella species that exhibit different mechanisms with several encoding genes for resisting carbapenems and other beta-lactam antibiotics in Nigeria. Undoubtedly, this trend is exacerbated by the continued use of these antibiotics in hospitals and other healthcare institutions. Hence, there is a need to suspend these antibiotics within the healthcare settings in Lagos, Nigeria, and possibly the neighbouring countries.

Conclusion

This study confirmed a high prevalence and distribution of MDR K. pneumoniae across the age groups, with peak occurrence within March and April in Lagos Hospitals. Over 80% of the isolates recorded in this study exhibited resistance to first-line antibiotics, including 3rd and 4th generation cephalosporin, carbapenem, and colistin. Tigecycline was the only antibiotic investigated with a high susceptibility rate. This study recorded emerging K. pneumoniae isolates harbouring co-existing ESBL and carbapenemase genes as a public health threat in our environment. Early diagnosis and adherence to antibiotic policies that restrict antibiotic use may effectively control the co-existence of these resistance genes produced by K. pneumoniae.
