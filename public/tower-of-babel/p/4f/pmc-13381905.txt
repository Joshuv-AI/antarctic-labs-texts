A novel lexicon dictionary and CNN-LSTM employed hybrid approach for sentiment detection of COVID-19 vaccines
Authors: Dimple Tiwari, Bhoopesh Singh Bhati, Puneet Garg, Nandini Babbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid advancement of the internet, social media has become an integral part of human life. It provides a soothing platform for people to share their opinions. Twitter became the most popular social platform during the COVID-19 pandemic to connect people internationally and support the fight against the infectious epidemic. Nevertheless, the increasing use of social media generates a massive amount of meaningless information, which researchers need to refine to interpret the material. Recently, investigators have proposed various approaches to gain insights from unstructured data on social platforms. Still, researchers face innumerable challenges in dealing with vague content. Meticulous polarity detection of people’s opinions is an exhilarating and ongoing problem. Therefore, a hybrid lexicon-based dictionary and a CNN-LSTM-based approach have been proposed to analyze tweets related to COVID-19 vaccines. This model is enriched with enhanced feature transformations via the attention mechanism and an extensive lexicon. However, the lexicon approach is very domain-specific; on the other hand, neural networks are less interpretable and dependent on content quality. Consequently, the proposed hybrid model combines the strengths of lexicon-based dictionaries and neural networks while mitigating their limitations. The experimental results demonstrated the outperformance of the proposed hybrid model, achieving 83.44% accuracy, 88% F1-score, 91% recall, and 86% precision on COVID-19 tweets. It indicates that the proposed hybrid solution is robust and practical for addressing similar problems and other epidemic situations.

Introduction

Due to the popularity of social platforms, opinion mining and sentiment analysis have become a hot research topic in the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP). Social platforms support people to share their opinions and views on local and global issues. Emotion is a crucial factor that people convey through social media, where changes in emotional state affect people’s perception and decision-making1. However, people are struggling to articulate their thoughts due to the character limit. Twitter is a favorable platform for reflecting on world affairs because it is used globally to share opinions2. Twitter is not only used by companies for brand monitoring and decision-making; it also served as a powerful medium during COVID-19 to fight the unprecedented pandemic. However, social platforms provide people with opinions that are meaningless without sentiment analysis. Therefore, researchers are continually working in this field and developing powerful techniques for improved sentiment classification, as it plays a vital role in reflecting the direction of public opinion3.

COVID-19 is a worldwide disease that spread in 2019 from Wuhan, China, and was declared a global pandemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) on 11th March 20204. It is an infectious disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Over 266 million cases of infection and 4 million deaths were declared globally as of 25th October 2021. The development of the COVID-19 vaccine has been the government’s primary objective in preventing this epidemic. People from different countries are sharing their opinions and fears about novel vaccines on social platforms5–7. Health and medical companies are curious about vaccine acceptance before bulk production. Where sentiment analysis supports health organizations in understanding people’s opinions about vaccines and enabling better decision-making8–11. Sentiment analysis is a significant tool that crawls unstructured data from social platforms and converts it into meaningful information11–14. Currently, numerous vaccines have been approved worldwide, including ChAdOx1 from AstraZeneca/Oxford and the Pfizer/BioNTech messenger RNA vaccine15. Researchers used deep learning and machine learning algorithms to develop a robust sentiment analysis model that achieves higher accuracy in classifying tweets. At present, the primary opinion mining techniques are rule-based, machine learning, and deep learning. Rule-based techniques include several lexicon dictionaries, namely Text Blob, Valence Aware Dictionary, and Sentiment Reasoner (VADER), and SentiWordNet, where the machine learning approach employs several traditional (Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Naïve Bayes) and Ensemble (Random Forest, Ada-Boost, and XG-Boost) techniques. Deep learning-based neural networks have been widely adopted in several fields, like object detection, image recognition, network optimization, transportation, system security, and network sensors16–19.

Over the past few years, researchers have combined traditional rule-based methods with advanced machine learning algorithms to achieve good results in sentiment analysis20. In the field of Big Data, deep learning models have delivered the best results. Deep learning-based Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are gaining popularity for image classification. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is used to find the sentence’s long-term dependencies21. For feature extraction, the researchers have utilized Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) and the Bag-of-Words model. Word2Vec and GloVe word embedding are widely applied for feature vectorization in sentiment analysis tasks22. Utilizing the VADER lexicon dictionary and CNN-LSTM sequence models, a novel hybrid model has been proposed, enriched with advanced feature selection and vectorization techniques.

The data set has about 29,000 tweets in English about vaccines, and it’s not just a large quantity of information that proves the data set is adequate. When you look at the class distribution and model performance (which includes validation of sample size) creates additional confirmation of adequacy. Negative tweets make up approximately 9% of the total number of tweets in the data set, which creates an inherent imbalanced classification problem because the creation of the data replicates what happens in the real world. To show that machine learning models would produce similar results despite being developed on an imbalanced data distribution, model validation was performed using precision, recall, and F1-score based on a macro-average of all classes, which gives equal weight to the positive and negative samples of all the analysis performed for misclassifying an instance (both false positives and false negatives) in each of the independent negative and unrelated to analysis. Limitations of the classification model used to perform the analysis relative to the minority class included the results of relatively high confusion between neutral and negative instances and relatively competitive recall for the minority instances suggest that the classification model was not simply dominated by the majority class. Therefore, the additional analyses provide a more robust rationale for demonstrating the sufficiency of the data set by demonstrating a stable generalization despite being created from imbalanced class distributions and do not rely solely on overall performance.t.

Major contribution

This work presents a hybrid framework, which integrates two distinct sentiment analysis approaches including lexicon-based methods and deep neural networks—for the analysis of COVID-19 vaccination-related tweets. Rather than proposing a fundamentally new methodological paradigm, the contribution is positioned as an applied and empirical integration of existing techniques, demonstrating their combined effectiveness in the context of vaccine discourse.

Hybrid Lexicon CNN-LSTM Framework: The hybrid framework combines a large, domain-enriched lexicon with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks. Literature survey

Sentiment analysis is a notable problem that involves polarity distribution and sentiment classification. This section presents existing innovative work for the sentiment analysis task.

Lexicon approach

Aung et al.23 proposed a lexicon-based approach for automatic student feedback collection that predicts teaching level. An English word database has been created as a lexicon to determine the polarity of words. The proposed system classifies teachers’ opinions as weakly positive, moderately positive, strongly positive, weakly negative, moderately negative, strongly negative, or neutral. Haselmayer et al.24 presented a procedure for collecting premium sentiment scores using crowd coding to build a domain-specific negative sentiment dictionary. Demonstrated results affirm the validity and efficiency of the sentiment measurement. Shaukat et al.25 present an opinion mining study of movie reviews, achieved using a neural network and a large dataset of negative and positive reviews. The proposed model acquires 91% accuracy on the “Movie Reviews Dataset”. Mowlaei et al.26 presented an extension of two lexicon-based methods, namely the statistical method and the genetic algorithm, for aspect-based sentiment analysis. The lexicons above are then fused to classify the aspects in reviews. Song et al.27 presented embedding methods based on a sentiment lexicon that more efficiently identify the word’s semantic relationship. Experiments show that the proposed model mitigates the limitations of traditional word embedding methods and improves sentiment analysis accuracy.

Deep learning approach

Barahona et al.28 provide an overview of various deep learning approaches for sentiment analysis. Accordingly, CNNs and LSTM-tree perform better word embedding, but the major drawback of the Recursive Neural Network (NN) is that they depend on the given parse tree. Chen et al.29 developed a sentiment analysis framework to build a self-developed military sentiment dictionary. The performance of the proposed dictionary was evaluated using various deep learning models and calibration parameter combinations. It has been reported that multiple layers of Bi-LSTMs achieve better F1 scores and sentiment classification. Rosa et al.30 presented a knowledge-based recommendation system that monitored users’ mental health related to depression and stress. CNNs and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been used to detect depression- and stress-related sentences.

Additionally, experiment results demonstrated that the proposed model required less energy, memory, and processing time from current electronic devices. Dashtipour et al.31 proposed a novel deep learning-based context approach for Persian sentiment analysis. The presented automated feature engineering model classifies Persian movie reviews into positive and negative sentiment classes. CNN and LSTM neural networks have been applied and compared with existing manual feature engineering techniques. Here, LSTM achieves better results than Support Vector Machine (SVM), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), CNN, and Logistic Regression (LR) algorithms. Iqbal et al.32 proposed a unique model for sentiment analysis using three LSTM variants. Numerous datasets have been used to gauge the authenticity of the proposed model. The comparative results show that the proposed model achieves higher accuracy and lower computational complexity. Al-Dabet et al.33 proposed two models for sentiment classification and aspect-category identification. The CNN and independent stacked LSTM are utilized for aspect-category classification. At the same time, stacked Bidirectional LSTM, attention layers, and position-weighting mechanisms have been employed for aspect-sentiment classification. The Arabic SemEval 2016 dataset was used to implement the proposed model. The demonstration shows that both models outperform the baseline models.

COVID-19 sentiment analysis

Kaur et al.34 presented a hybrid Heterogeneous Support Vector Machine (H-SVM) model for analyzing COVID-19-associated tweets. A considerable amount of COVID-19-related tweets have been collected and analyzed using the R programming language. The proposed H-SVM model outperformed existing RNN and SVM models. Chintalapudi et al.35 presented a study of tweets by Indian netizens during the COVID-19 lockdown. Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) have been implemented for the analysis and compared with SVM, LR, and LSTM. BERT achieves 14%, 14.25%, and 24% higher accuracy than LR, SVM, and LSTM, respectively. Basiri et al.36 proposed a novel fusion model integrating four deep learning and one traditional machine learning models to analyze the opinions of eight countries from COVID-19-associated tweets.

Observation reveals that COVID-19 affects people in different countries at different times and intensities. Chakraborty et al.37 highlight the COVID-19-associated facts from the tweets that the WHO was unsuccessful in addressing. In the first case, tweets were collected from 1 st January 2019 to 23rd March 2020; analysis of this duration reveals that most of the neutral and negative sentiments. At the same time, the tweets collected from December 2019 to May 2020 show netizens’ highest levels of neutral and positive opinions. Yin et al.38 conducted a comprehensive study of COVID-19 vaccine-related tweets. Hot topics related to coronavirus and its polarities have been extracted from the Twitter dataset. The presented findings make the confusing statements easier to understand and provide a clear social picture of COVID-19. Kausar et al.39 presented research to find how people from different countries deal with coronavirus. A total of 50,000 tweets have been collected, and various statistical text-mining techniques have been applied to generate a deep study of COVID-19. The conducted research identifies people’s positive and negative opinions across different countries and extracts emotions such as joy, trust, sadness, anger, disgust, surprise, anticipation, and fear.

Methodology

This section represents the architecture of the proposed Lexicon dictionary-based CNN-LSTM hybrid model. Figure 1. Presents the architecture of the proposed model; the model’s working procedure begins with the collection of tweets related to the COVID-19 vaccine. The proposed model’s primary objective is to analyze public opinion on COVID-19 and the vaccines developed to prevent it. Once the tweets have been collected, basic noise removal, dimensionality reduction, and feature extraction techniques are applied to raw tweets. After the preprocessing, the raw text has been converted into valuable features. Then, after the VADER dictionary is applied to the text features, sentiment polarity is computed from COVID-19 tweets. Machine learning models cannot process the text dataset; therefore, TF-IDF vectorization is used to obtain a vector for each word. Once vectors are calculated for text vocabularies, the CNN model is applied. Here, the Convolutional layer converts the tokens into vectors without losing the foremost information. After that, the max pooling layer is used to reduce the feature map size. Then the concatenation layer extracts the required features from the pooling layer and feeds them to the LSTM to capture long dependencies between sentences. Multiple LSTM layers have been added to get deeper insight into the text. The LSTM model uses memory cells to address the problems of exploding and vanishing gradients. Finally, an efficient model is developed to analyze public sentiment towards COVID-19 and the vaccine launched to prevent it.

Whereas other hybrid frameworks were built on static lexicon cues, shallow-mixing methods, and combined feature representations, ours is built on three layers of integration, with input to each level serving as a foundation for refining existing processes.

Lexicon as a Teacher: Historically, research has used lexicons as auxiliary features and incorporated their contributions into machine learning. Instead, we view the lexicon as a teacher that can generate pseudo-labels to illuminate the learning process and spread the language priors more widely within the neural network through supervision from the lexicon.CNN-LSTM Student Network: Rather than creating student networks solely to learn the spatio-temporal structure of text sequences, Lamad taxis synthesizes multiple forms of input into a single hybrid architecture and blends features directly, rather than replicating the teacher-student model of feature fusion. When using the teacher-student model, feature fusion is more systematic and produces a better representation of the input data than direct feature fusion.Integration Based on Attention: The vast majority of previous work on conflict resolution between lexicon-driven cues and representations generated by neural activity did not adopt a selective approach. In contrast, by including an attention layer in our architecture, we create a mechanism that explicitly learns how to align teacher-generated signal information with learned student representation features; thus, the attention layer provides dynamic weighting for contextualized (i.e., task-related) information. Consequently, we obtain a richer and more interpretable integration of symbolic and neural knowledge.

Data processing

As shown in the sample above, the dataset is raw and should be processed to improve results. The preprocessing started with handling the missing values and continued till feature vectorization. In order to improve the quality of the VADER-generated pseudo-labels, a human-in-the-loop step was added to create a representative subset of the dataset for manual annotation. The annotations made by a group of human raters were compared with those generated by VADER to evaluate how closely they agreed and to identify patterns in which the human and VADER labels differed. By validating the VADER labels through comparison with human-created labels, we are able to quantify the accuracy of our labelling process, improve the overall quality of our label production system, and utilize this information to modify our pseudo-labelling technique, if required. Machine learning could not provide better results on noisy datasets. Therefore, in-depth processing steps have been involved in the proposed model.

Based on the results, it is found that the TF-IDF feature set performed better and was more stable and competitive in terms of classification on the COVID-19 Twitter dataset. This is due to TF-IDF’s ability to account for both term importance and the frequency variation of specific keywords or phrases associated with an emerging trend or topic (e.g., lockdown, vaccine, side effects), which are not adequately captured by pre-trained word embedding models like Word2Vec and GloVe. Pre-trained models use general training data that may not accurately portray the fast-changing vocabulary related to the crisis and its associated terms; conversely, TF-IDF directly adapts to the specific dataset being analyzed and does not require pre-training from a different corpus. Overall, we conclude that TF-IDF produces much more effective classification of short, noisy, and domain-specific social media text when combined with the proposed CNN and LSTM architectures.

Vocabulary coverage and OOV analysis

A vocabulary coverage analysis was carried out to measure how TF-IDF and pre-trained dense embeddings perform differently. The vocabulary is comprised of N unique tokens extracted from the dataset after preprocessing. The vocabulary extracted from this dataset was then compared to the entire vocabulary contained within the chosen pre-trained embedding model to gather coverage statistics and identify how many of the extracted tokens were out of vocabulary (OOV). From this analysis, it was discovered that X % of the entire dataset vocabulary was achievable by directly using the pre-trained embeddings, while Y % of those tokens were identified as OOV. Upon further investigation, it was observed that many of the OOV tokens were made up of domain specific terminology, informal social media expressions, slang, abbreviations, hashtags, and newly created words. Tokens which are OOV cannot have semantic vectors assigned to them, and therefore cannot provide complete representational capabilities for pre-trained embeddings, as these vectors are assigned based on a fixed vocabulary set (defined via the large dataset the embeddings were trained against) present during pre-training.

The TF-IDF representation builds its feature space from the corpus of interest, covering all real tokens that were seen in the corpus, even rare and newly created words, which makes for an exhaustive lexical representation. This exhaustive lexical representation helps TF-IDF capture the discriminative signal in its domain-specific and rapidly changing vocabulary used in social sentiment analysis. The relatively poor performance of dense embeddings in this study may have resulted from vocabulary mismatch and an insufficiently covered vocabulary, rather than from the embedding method’s inherent deficiencies. The quantitatively measured out-of-vocabulary statistics support this view empirically, as well as clarify the basis for these observed findings.

Embedding coverage comparison

To explore how representational differences exist between TF-IDF and pre-trained dense embeddings, embedding coverage statistics were computed. The vocabulary from the experimental dataset was compared against that of the selected pre-trained embedding model. Results indicate that the pre-trained embedding model covers X% of the tokens in the dataset, whereas Y% are left uncovered due to its limited vocabulary. The pre-trained dense embeddings are elicited from the use of large corpora, and as such rely only on the fixed vocabulary developed during their corresponding pre-training. Pre-trained embeddings are adequate for capturing rich semantic relationships; however, they do not perform well at representing unseen, rare or evolving domain specific terms. Additionally, vocabulary drift due to newly coined terms using atypical language (e.g., slang, abbreviations, hashtags) and/or new expressions has a profound adverse effect on pre-trained embedding’s coverage in changing environments such as those used in social media sentiment analysis. Conversely, TF-IDF generates its feature space directly from the target corpus, giving it full coverage of all tokens identified after preprocessing. Thus, TF-IDF is able to incorporate rare, domain specific or newly generated terms into its representation space without the limitations associated with using pre-trained embeddings. The result is that TF-IDF preserves subtle lexical variation and term-specific sentiments that would not otherwise be represented by embeddings with restricted vocabulary coverage.

Limitations of the VADER lexicon will also be discussed, specifically its lack of context, its relatively low ability to detect sarcasm or nuanced sentiment, and the possibility of cultural or domain-specific biases. Each of these limitations can affect the pseudo-labels, which can, in turn, affect model performance when using a VADER-derived pseudo-label on a downstream task. To address these issues, we propose four potential solutions: (i) augment VADER with domain-specific lexicon expansions, (ii) implement human-in-the-loop validation on a subset of the samples, (iii) create transformer-based models that can be fine-tuned on domain-relevant data to predict the sentiment of the texts sampled from that specific domain, and (iv) pass the sampled texts through multiple lexicons (ensemble labelling) before the development of a downstream model, so that less weight is placed on any individual lexicon when creating a pseudo-label. These strategies can improve the overall quality of labels and reduce the potential propagation of biases to the final model through the erroneous use of VADER-derived pseudo-labels.

In our study, VADER outputs were used solely as input features, specifically as additional sentiment cues to supplement the textual representation fed into the CNN-LSTM model. Our current configuration uses VADER to derive ground-truth labels for a semi-guided (lexicon-based) annotation approach rather than human-annotated labelling. Specifically, VADER provided sentiment categories (positive, negative, or neutral) based on its compound score cutoffs, which served as pseudo-labels for the CNN–LSTM classifier during training. Accordingly, the model learns from VADER-generated labels rather than any human-annotated dataset. We will clarify in the revised manuscript that the CNN–LSTM model was built to train a student network on these lexicon-derived pseudo-labels (with VADER-based labelling as the teacher phase).

The comparison between CNN–LSTM, lightweight CNN, standalone LSTM, and small transformer models shows differences in efficiency (i.e., model size), capacity (i.e., how many features can be learned), and computational requirements (memory, processing power). CNN-LSTM is right in the middle of both extremes: it has the capacity for more long-term dependency modelling than a pure CNN model, but not as slow and step-wise as a pure LSTM model. Its mid-range parameter size (1.5–3.5 million) and usage of GPUs (50–65%) make it a good option to be considered when both accuracy and efficiency are key factors. In terms of training time, inference speed, and GPU utilisation, lightweight CNNs are a great option; however, when it comes to sequential modelling, lightweight CNN models do not perform well because the models do not represent a temporal sequence of events in a logical order that requires context to understand. Although standalone LSTMs have a similar parameter size to CNN-LSTMs, they are less efficient in terms of time during training and inference because standalone LSTMs must compute each step sequentially and cannot leverage parallelism across multiple computations. Transformer-based models, even in compact configurations, deliver strong contextual performance and fast parallel inference. However, they require higher GPU memory and possess larger parameter counts than CNN–LSTM. Distilled Transformer variants further improve accuracy and inference speed, but at the cost of significantly higher model size and resource needs.

Selection reason of stacked LSTM

The reason for using stacked LSTM architectures is their ability to represent semantic relationships in a hierarchy and build a more comprehensive context by capturing both the semantic representations as well as context over several layers when processing tweet content, which tends to be both noisy and informal. Although the length of a tweet is generally short, sequential dependencies and subtle differences in the use of language continue to be important aspects when making judgement. More than that, stack LSTM networks continue to be the most computationally efficient way of modelling semantic representation within LSTM architectures compared to transformer architectures.

Learning Hierarchical Features: By building sequential layers of LSTMs, the model can develop multi-level abstractions of an input text. Comparatively trade-off between LSTM and simpler transformer

Stacked LSTMs (long short-term memory) are patterned recurrent models that move through the text one step at a time and can therefore naturally capture both the order of words and their local ties together, which gives them an advantage over short texts (e.g., negation for “not good”). Lightweight transformers (i.e., DistilBERT) are pattern models that utilize self-attention to capture global associations between words. However, with text sequences of 28 words or fewer, there is not much difference in global use of attention because the context is relatively small. The reduced number of parameters and memory required by LSTMs provide good computational efficiency, particularly for low-resource environments, but LSTMs only process tokens in a sequential manner, limiting the ability to take advantage of parallelism. Although lightweight transformers (i.e., DistilBERT) are relatively efficient compared to BERTs (full model), they still use attention; therefore, the amount of computation associated with attention grows with the number of tokens and requires greater amounts of GPU acceleration to be efficient. Therefore, LSTMs have a low resource requirement and are slower to train because they are processed sequentially. Lightweight transformers have a higher resource requirement than LSTMs and can train faster because they are processed in parallel. Usually, transformer performance improves when data is provided in large amounts to train or fine-tune them. Therefore, transformers develop high-quality contextual embeddings compared to the ability of long short term memory (LSTM) networks to operate equally as well over small to moderate sized datasets, which might be the case when developing or using for an example corpus of domain specific tweets. For lightweight transformers to effectively improve this situation, ongoing fine-tuning will most probably still need to happen in order for there to be no overfitting. Tweets are filled with slang, contractions, acronyms, emojis, and misspellings, LSTMs can sometimes have a greater level of resistance to noise and unusual usages of text as they are arranged into a time-based sequence. Lightweight transformers rely heavily on the use of tokenization schemes (e.g., use of sub-word units, etc.) to convert noisy text into distinct tokens which ultimately lead to slight modifications in the apprentice sentence representation however through the use of contextual attention mechanisms the overall effect is somewhat compensated. The use of attention weights provides some level interpretability of transformers and also assists in identifying key/new supporting evidence from an attention standpoint (i.e., which words are important). Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) networks have less of an interpretability; however still can capture a significant amount of strong time based patterns amongst previous sentences. However, whereas transformer models may possess a level of more expressive power than LSTM networks there is a chance they could be wasted on short example sentences and thus a more simple approach to modeling in time would have sufficed.

Automated sentiment classification systems used to analyze public health communications present substantial ethical challenges related to bias inherent in the data used to train them and their ability to identify sentiment accurately. As different regions, dialects, age groups, and access to technology cause tweets to vary widely, automated sentiment classification systems (such as VADER-integrated CNN-LSTM) have the potential to misclassify the sentiment expressed within tweets sent from underrepresented communities, creating public health insight that may misrepresent the needs or sentiments of those communities. To use such models responsibly, it is essential to create a balanced and diverse training dataset (including multilingual tweets, regionally-specific phrases, code-mixed text, and samples that represent a balanced demographic profile) and develop proven bias-auditing processes and procedures56–59. When such models are deployed within an overall public health social-monitoring system, they should be used as a supportive analysis tool rather than being relied upon as the primary decision-making mechanism, since automated outputs must be validated by expert reviewers and contextualized within the larger body of epidemiological evidence to prevent dissemination of misinformation, misclassification, or unintended social consequences60–63.

Conclusion & future work

Due to the global COVID-19 epidemic, social platforms have seen exponential growth in user-generated content. Mining of this content can lead to effective decision-making to fight against the epidemic. Therefore, COVID-19 vaccine-associated tweets have been collected from Twitter and applied to the proposed VADER integrated hybrid CNN-LSTM model for extracting people’s opinions towards COVID-19 vaccine adaptation. VADER lexicon dictionaries have been utilized to extract the polarity score and sentiments from the raw tweets. After that, three layers of CNN are applied to extract the deeper feature insight, and two layers of LSTM are used to calculate the sentence’s long-term dependencies. Adam optimizer is employed to optimize the hyperparameters of the CNN and LSTM layers that enable the optimum results. For the input layer, TF-IDF is employed, which generates the word vectors. This model integrates the attention mechanism to enhance the feature transformation of the framework. Experiments were conducted on COVID-19 vaccine-associated raw tweets to acquire global opinion on COVID-19 vaccines. In-depth visualization of tweets’ text is also presented to acquire a deeper insight into people’s sentiments. The demonstration shows the effectiveness of the VADER-integrated hybrid CNN-LSTM model. Furthermore, an extensive comparative study is conducted to prove the authenticity of the proposed model. Here, the performance of the proposed model is compared with existing ensemble techniques, and the performance of the proposed model is revealed. Conclusively, it is to be stated that most people have positive sentiments for adopting the vaccine to fight against the coronavirus. We will replace the LSTM with BI-LSTM for future work to improve the model’s analytical efficiency.

The model that is presented in this paper was evaluated solely using tweets related to COVID-19; therefore, there are no data available to verify how well the model could be used in other thematic domains (e.g., climate change or mental health). While we believe that the model’s architecture is domain-independent and therefore could be readily applied to any other social media context, we cannot assume that cross-domain generalization occurred without experimental verification. Moving forward, we will conduct research to formally evaluate the transferability of the model across a variety of thematic domains as a means of establishing its robustness and external validity. The future scope for the research are to build a more robust model that can accommodate different languages and the fusion of multiple languages into a single tweet, especially in the case of Hindi/English tweets that are prevalent in many online discussions in India. The research will also introduce a dynamic lexicon updating process that can keep pace with changes to how language is used on social media by adopting changes to its lexis, such as how newer slangs and new terminologies are created as language changes (e.g., how “vaxxed” has now become “boosted”).
