Implant‐related orofacial pain managed with implant apicoectomy: A case report
Authors: Oscar Durán‐Garnica, Karen Villarreal‐Arizpe, Hong Chen, Hsun‐Liang Chan, Guo‐Liang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, the use of implants has emerged as a widely accepted method for the replacement of missing teeth. Although rare, chronic pain associated with implant placement has been reported in the literature. Conversely, apicoectomy, the removal of the apical portion has been integrated into the implantology field in cases with retrograde peri‐implantitis, allowing for cleaning and reconstructing the affected area without losing the implant. This case report aims to introduce an alternative treatment for managing orofacial pain associated with a buccally positioned implant with its apex outside the bone contour through the technique of apicoectomy.MethodsA 63‐year‐old Asian female presents with chronic pain localized to the left side of her face, accompanied by nasal discharge and tenderness upon palpation of the surrounding soft tissues of implant #9. The cross‐sectional view from cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) demonstrated that implant #9 was positioned too buccally, leading to insufficient facial bone coverage, with the apex of the implant situated outside of the bone contour. Implant apicoectomy and soft tissue augmentation with connective tissue graft (CTG) was performed.ResultsTwo weeks following the surgical procedure, the patient reported a complete resolution of her chronic orofacial pain and nasal discharge.ConclusionsAn apicoectomy approach may be a viable method for addressing an implant without retrograde periimplantitis yet having the apex out of the bone housing. This approach allows for the retention of an implant that is fully integrated within the oral cavity.Key points Implant apicoectomy may relieve chronic pain when the implant apex extends beyond the bone, offering a conservative alternative to full implant removal.Minimally invasive microsurgery and ultrasound imaging support precise diagnosis, flap design, and soft tissue preservation. This enables targeted access, implant apex removal, and enhanced healing.Tunneling with connective tissue grafting effectively augments peri‐implant soft tissue without disrupting the mucosal margin or interdental papilla. Plain language summaryDental implants are a popular solution for replacing missing teeth. While it is uncommon, some patients do experience persistent pain after the placement of an implant. An apicoectomy, which removes the implant's tip, can effectively treat infections localized in the implant's apex, allowing cleaning, and reconstructing the affected area while preserving the implant itself. This case report presents an alternative treatment for orofacial pain caused by a misaligned implant with its apex extending beyond the bone contour, utilizing the apicoectomy technique. A 63‐year‐old Asian female experienced persistent left‐side facial pain, along with nasal discharge and tenderness around her upper left central implant. A 3D X‐ray (cone beam computed tomography [CBCT]) revealed improper implant positioning, leading to inadequate bone coverage, with its tip extending outside the bone contour. Implant apicoectomy and soft tissue augmentation with a palate graft was performed. Two weeks post‐surgery, the patient reported complete relief from her orofacial pain and nasal discharge. This technique offers a practical option for addressing an implant that is not infected but has its tip outside the bone contour, allowing for the retention of a fully integrated implant in the oral cavity.

INTRODUCTION

Implant therapy is now a well‐established solution for replacing missing teeth, with mean survival rates exceeding 90% over 20 years. 1 , 2 , 3 Nevertheless, complications may occur and are classified in three categories: esthetic, biomechanical, and biological. 3 , 4 Esthetic complications relate to peri‐implant tissue quality/quantity, and the visual integration of the prosthesis. 3 Biomechanical complications typically result from damage from functional overload. 4 Biological complications involve pathological changes in peri‐implant hard and soft tissues, such as mucositis and peri‐implantitis. 4

Pain, a common symptom of complications, is particularly concerning when it persists beyond the expected postoperative period, as it may indicate more severe conditions. Common etiological factors of tenderness around dental implant include inflammation, infection, and nerve damage. 5 Typical postoperative pain after implant surgery is nociceptive in nature—transient 6 and associated with irritation of nearby nerves and tissue damage. 7 , 8 Trigeminal neuropathic pain has also been reported in post‐implant placement cases presenting with persistent pain and signs or symptoms of neurosensory disturbances, 7 particularly in the mandible 8 and patients aged 60 years and older. 9 Nociplastic pain is a distinct category of pain arising from altered nociception without clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage or somatosensory system disease. 10 This newer classification may also apply to implant patients, particularly those with chronic or overlapping bodily pain, as reported in a case series. 11 Differentiating persistent post‐implant pain according to underlying pain mechanisms is essential for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment.

Surgical treatment, particularly implant apicoectomy, has been reported as an effective approach for preserving osseointegrated implants while treating retrograde peri‐implantitis. 12 , 13 Beyond this primary indication, implant apicoectomy has also been applied to address other postoperative complications. Levitt reported significant improvement in paresthesia following mandibular implant apicoectomy. 14 Similarly, Dawood et al. documented a case in which implant apex resection was performed to manage a postoperative infection resulting from apical perforation by a zygomatic implant. 15 Collectively, these case studies may imply that implant apicoectomy may be considered for the management of complications associated with implant apical perforation, including pain, numbness, and foreign body sensation. 16

This case report aims to present the clinical features and management of persistent orofacial pain (OFP) in a patient with soft‐ and hard‐tissue deficiencies following implant placement, in accordance with the CARE guidelines. 17 The condition was managed through implant apicoectomy and soft‐tissue augmentation. The clinical decision‐making process, treatment approach, and the potential contribution of an apically extruded implant to chronic OFP were discussed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This case report documents a case of implant apicoectomy and peri‐implant soft tissue augmentation with an autogenous connective tissue graft (CTG). Clinical study registration was not required for this case report. The procedure was conducted by an experienced periodontist (HC) at the Dental Faculty Practice (DFP) of The Ohio State University College of Dentistry (OSUCOD), in Columbus, OH, USA.

Clinical presentation

A 63‐year‐old healthy Asian female, non‐smoker classified as ASA I, presented to the DFP at OSUCOD in May 2024. She was referred for evaluation and treatment of localized periodontitis on #2 and #15. Upon comprehensive periodontal evaluation, implant #9 could be seen through thin mucosa. The implant displayed a thin peri‐implant tissue phenotype and exhibited grey facial discoloration due to the translucency of the thin mucosa (Figure 1). Probing depth (PD) around implant #9 ranged from 2 to 3 mm with 1 mm mucosal recession. Signs of bleeding on probing (BOP) and implant mobility were negative. Mild tenderness was noted upon palpation on the surrounding peri‐implant soft tissues, especially the more apical part. She commented on “tenderness of the same area while stretching her lip”. According to her, esthetics was not a primary concern because of a low smile line and the age. A review of her dental history revealed that tooth #9 was extracted in January 2011, followed by the placement of implants #7 and #9 four months later. The final restoration, a metal‐porcelain implant‐supported bridge, was delivered in March 2012. A periapical radiograph was taken, showing normal interproximal bone remodeling. During the evaluation visit, the patient recollected “frequent but mild headache, and nasal drainage” of the left side after the implant was placed (Table 1). She did not relate the symptoms with the implant until now. She consulted with her primary care physician before without any definitive diagnosis. The patient indicated a desire to decipher the role of the implant on her headache and nasal drainage. Occlusal analysis was not contributory. To examine peri‐implant tissues in more detail, especially soft and facial bone thickness, non‐ionizing and chairside ultrasound (US) imaging (Zonare Z One Ultrasound System, Mindray) was taken following the documented scanning protocol. 18 B (brightness)‐mode US images showed the complete length (13 mm) of the implant surface exposed, characterized as a hyperechoic dash line with continuous bright and dark short lines corresponding to the peaks and valleys of the threads. It also revealed that soft tissue thickness of 0.58mm and 0.61mm at 1mm and 3mm below the gingival margin, respectively (Figure 2). To rule out that the implant is placed in the nasal cavity, a cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was ordered. Fortunately, the implant was not in the nasal cavity. However, no facial bone was found on implant #9, with the apex protruding outside of the facial alveolar ridge. Based on the 2017 AAP/EFP World Workshop on Periodontal and Implant Diseases Classification, 19 implant #9 was diagnosed as peri‐implant soft and hard tissue deficiencies. The prognosis varies depending on the signs/symptoms to be addressed and the patient's expectations. The overall prognosis of the current implant/prosthesis is questionable due to implant positioning and presence of signs/symptoms. To eliminate or at least alleviate mucosal tenderness, the prognosis would be favorable by removing the protruding implant apex and increasing soft tissue thickness. However, the prognosis of headache and nasal drainage was uncertain because of the unknown etiology.

The patient was suggested with treatment options for implant #9, including (1) explantation of the malpositioned implant, ridge augmentation, and subsequent new implant and prosthesis restoration (a fixed implant retained prosthesis #7–#9), (2) implant apicoectomy and soft tissue augmentation, or (3) monitoring without treatment. After a thorough discussion regarding the advantages and disadvantages, benefits and risks of each option, the patient decided to proceed with the second option, knowing the primary aim is to reduce soft tissue tenderness at the implant apex, and a preference for a conservative approach.

Case management

An apicoectomy procedure, soft tissue tunneling, and a connective tissue graft for implant #9 was performed using an operating microscope (Extaro 300, Zeiss) for enhanced visualization with the minimally invasive approach. The patient reviewed and signed a surgical consent form before the procedure. Following administration of local anesthesia (Lidocaine HCl 2% Epinephrine 1:100,000, Henry Schein), a vertical incision of approximately 4 mm was made on the mucosa mesial to implant #9. Neither intrasulcular incision nor traditional vertical incisions starting at the mucosal margin were chosen to avoid a risk of trauma‐induced mucosal recession and loss of interdental papilla. This decision was made because the primary aim was to access the apex, instead of the coronal part of the implant. A full‐thickness flap was elevated from the vertical incision to expose the implant apex, with tunnel preparation extending coronally to reach the mucosal margin. Fibrous encapsulation was carefully dissected around the apex to avoid flap perforation (Figure 3). Approximately 3 mm of the exposed implant apex was excised using a high‐speed rotary handpiece with a diamond bur. The sharp cutting edge of the apicoectomy section was rounded and polished (Figure 4). 20 The surgical site was then thoroughly irrigated with copious normal saline to remove as much titanium debris as possible. After the completion of apicoectomy and implantoplasty, a CTG with diameters of 21 (length) × 5 (width) × 1 (thickness) mm) was harvested from the left palate using the intraoral de‐epithelialization technique (Figure 5). 21 A pre‐made surgical stent was placed immediately after the harvest to facilitate hemostasis of the donor site. The long side of the graft was oriented apicocoronally and placed into the tunnel and secured with 7‐0 polypropylene sutures to adjacent fixed tissue to cover the facial surface of the implant (Figure 6). Finally, multiple interrupted sutures were placed along the vertical incision using 7‐0 polypropylene to achieve hemostasis (Figure 7). Postoperative medication included the prescription of analgesics (Ibuprofen 600 mg q6h for 2 days and PRN pain) and antibiotics (Amoxicillin 500 mg q8h tid until completion).

RESULTS

Postoperative appointments

Two weeks following the surgical procedure, the patient surprisingly reported a complete resolution of her chronic OFP and nasal discharge. The wound healing was within normal limits (Figures 8 and 9). No signs or symptoms of infection were noted. No early exposure of CTG or necrosis was found. The sutures were removed after the wound evaluation. However, mild recession of peri‐implant mucosa was noted, exposing approximately 1 mm of the implant abutment. Minor occlusal adjustment was executed to exclude possible occlusal trauma. Notably, 3 months after the surgery, the peri‐implant mucosa exhibited a significant tissue augmentation, especially at the implant apex (Figures 8 and 9) and free of the pre‐existing symptoms, such as mucosal tenderness and OFP. Upon the 6‐month follow‐up, intraoral examination revealed that PDs were within the normal range (2–4 mm) without a significant change of the mucosal margin nor BOP. No significant bone changes were noted on the periapical radiograph compared to the initial condition (Figure 10). Moreover, the patient tolerated the treatment well and was satisfied to be continuously free of symptoms.

DISCUSSION

This case report documents the management of post‐implant placement OFP by implant apicoectomy and soft tissue augmentation. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case suggesting a potential association between apical alveolar bone perforation by a standard implant and chronic OFP. The patient presented with localized post‐implant pain characterized by tenderness to palpation, pain triggered by tissue displacement, and accompanying symptoms such as headache and nasal drainage. All symptoms resolved promptly following the removal of the apically protruding portion of the implant.

The decision to save or remove an ailing dental implant is complex and must be guided based on a combination of clinical, esthetic, functional, and patient‐related factors. Saving an implant may be appropriate when (1) the peri‐implantitis is manageable, (2) the prosthetic components remain functional, (3) esthetic concerns are correctable through soft‐tissue grafting or prosthetic modification, or (4) when patient‐specific factors, such as financial constraints or emotional investment, support the preservation. 22 In this case study, apicoectomy was performed primarily based on the patient's expectation on saving the implant. Other supportive factors include the stable interproximal bone level of this implant, minimal impact on osseointegration after apicoectomy, the long survival period of this implant over years, and the well‐conditioned implant #7 sharing occlusal loading.

Postoperative pain after implant placement is associated with various risk factors, including age over 40 years, smoking, anxiety, and depression. 7 , 11 The prevalence rate of altered sensation following implant placement ranges from 8.5% to 36%. 7 This occurs more often on mandible due to drilling‐induced nerve damage 23 or implant placement overt proximate to the nerve. 8 Conversely, chronic postoperative pain after implant surgery is relatively uncommon on maxilla. Patients may experience immediate postoperative neuropathic pain, unilateral headache or dull facial pain on the side of implant placement on maxilla. 11 In this case study, based on the patient's pain characteristics, treatment response, and limited dental and medical history, the symptoms are most consistent with nociceptive pain, which arises from actual or potential tissue damage. The implant apical perforation, along with hard and soft tissue deficiency, may irritate surrounding tissues during functional loading or stretching of adjacent tissues. Maxillary central incisor, upper lip, and parts of the ipsilateral nasal mucosa are innervated by the maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve, particularly via infraorbital nerve and its branch, anterior superior alveolar nerve. 24 Given the shared maxillary innervation, it is plausible that postoperative conditions, such as altered occlusal loading, periapical soft and hard tissue deficiencies, and upper‐lip movement, may have contributed to irritation of these neural branches. Although direct evidence is lacking, this anatomically grounded mechanism may explain this patient's persistent OFP accompanied by rhinorrhea. Nasal symptoms, such as congestion and rhinorrhea, are not uncommon in patients with trigeminal nerve‐mediated facial pain and headaches, indicating a possible neurogenic origin or referred pain mechanism underlying these co‐occurring orofacial and nasal symptoms. 25 , 26 , 27 Additionally, local inflammation can also contribute to increased tissue sensitivity to pain, such as the pain to palpation. Neuropathic and nociplastic pain are common in chronic pain patients, particularly following injury or invasive procedures. However, these types of pain are less likely in this case since the patient did not provide any descriptors that represent neuropathic‐related pain, and after the apicoectomy, the resolution of symptoms occurred within 2 weeks. The improvement occurred within the regular healing period and made long‐lasting nociplastic changes in the central or peripheral nervous system unlikely. Nevertheless, without additional neurosensory testing and a comprehensive medical history, other pain mechanisms cannot be entirely excluded.

This case was managed with the assistance of modern technology. The use of surgical microscope enhanced the quality of minimally invasive technique, with which a limited vertical incision was made to minimize the disruption of vascularity to the facial mucosa and papilla. 28 The surgical microscope allows minimal flap extension, preservation of soft tissues, 29 facilitated revascularization, 30 higher patient satisfaction, 31 and enhances the results of apicosurgical procedures. 32 US served as a real‐time, non‐ionizing diagnostic tool to assess peri‐implant tissue conditions. 18 Soft tissue thickness measured as <1 mm indicated a high risk for postsurgical recession. 33 , 34 The implant surface was visualized in a sagittal view and its malposition confirmed transversely. 35 , 36 Doppler US further identified areas of increased perfusion suggestive of inflammation. 37 This case indicates the potential of US as an effective tool for peri‐implant tissues evaluation prior to CBCT imaging.

Admittedly, the limitations of this study include: (1) limited retrieval of the patient's medical and dental history, (2) short follow‐up period (6 months), and (3) partial resolution of the esthetic complication. The patient's psychological history and the details of implant therapy were hard to fully explored before the surgery, which may provide some clues to further understand the source of the OFP. As this is a single case report, definitive causation cannot be established. The observed symptom resolution may be coincidental or influenced by multifactorial processes. While the resolution of symptoms and patient satisfaction are encouraging, the presence of buccal dehiscence still poses a risk of esthetic complications. Longer‐term follow‐up is necessary to determine the durability of symptom resolution and tissue stability.

CONCLUSION

A conservative implant apicoectomy with a connective tissue graft through the vestibular tunneling approach under microscope could resolve soft tissue tenderness, avoiding explantation and subsequent complex and lengthy reconstruction. The unintended outcome of “curing” OFP signifies the possible role of implant malposition on patient welfare. The practice of ruling out dental implants as an etiology of persistent OFP deserves further study and confirmation through interprofessional collaboration.
