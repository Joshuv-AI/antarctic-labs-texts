Surveillance for Coxiella burnetii animal reservoirs in Hong Kong suggests low zoonotic risk of Q-fever from local animals including ruminants, companion animals and rodents
Authors: Francesca Rizzo, Omid Nekouei, Wing Yan Jacqueline Tam, Theethawat Uea-Anuwong, Ioannis Magouras, Pawel Bęczkowski, Jason C.C. Chan, Simon Y.H. Wong, Tania A. Perroux, Kate J. Flay, Thomas H.C. Sit, Julia A. Beatty, Vanessa R. Barrs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Hong Kong, the average annual incidence of Q-fever in humans was one case per year from 2008 to 2023. The prevalence of C. burnetii infection in animals in Hong Kong is not reported. This study aimed to identify potential domestic and wild animal reservoirs of C. burnetii to inform human exposure risks from local animals.A cross-sectional study was conducted to detect infection and/or exposure using biological samples from 1118 animals including 377 ruminants (200 feral cattle, 35 water buffalo, 142 goats), 216 rats and 525 owned or unowned dogs and cats between 2020 and 2023. PCR to detect C. burnetii IS1111 DNA was performed on reproductive tissues, liver, faeces, blood, and/or vaginal swabs from 866 animals, and sera from 691 animals were tested for antibodies against C. burnetii by ELISA.C. burnetii DNA was not detected in any sample. While C. burnetii antibodies were not detected in any bovine or canine sera, 2/157 (1.3%) cats and 1/142 (0.7%) goats were seropositive. No evidence of active C. burnetii infection was detected in any of the 866 tested animals. Evidence of exposure was found only in cats and goats, albeit at low frequency. No evidence of a role for rodent reservoirs of C. burnetii in Hong Kong was identified.The sporadic serological evidence and the absence of molecular evidence of active infection in key reservoir species, along with the limited local livestock population, suggest that the risk of acquiring C. burnetii infection from exposure to local animals in Hong Kong is low.

Introduction

Coxiella burnetii is a zoonotic obligate intracellular Gram-negative bacterium that infects domestic and wild animals including mammals, birds, reptiles and arthropods. Ticks can acquire the bacterium by feeding on infected hosts or through transstadial and transovarial transmission. Ticks are both reservoirs and vectors of C. burnetii and have a critical role in maintaining the pathogen's sylvatic transmission cycle and in facilitating spillover of infection between wildlife and livestock [1], [2]. Domestic ruminants are considered to be primary reservoirs and represent the most common source of Q-fever infection for humans [1]. C. burnetii has a biphasic life-cycle, alternating between an obligate intracellular pathogen (large cell variant) in which bacterial replication occurs, and a highly infectious, extracellular, environmentally-resistant spore-like form (small-cell variant). The small-cell variant can survive for several months on dust and is dispersed by wind over long distances [3].

The cellular targets of C. burnetii in animal hosts are primarily circulating monocytes and macrophages, as well as trophoblasts in pregnant females. While most animals are subclinically infected, domestic ruminants including cattle, sheep and goats are more susceptible to clinical disease, especially reproductive disorders such as abortion, metritis and infertility [4]. Birth fluids and placental tissue of infected ruminants are a major source of environmental contamination. Low level intermittent shedding in urine, faeces, semen and milk also occurs [5]. Antibodies against C. burnetii persist for several months to years [4].

Q-fever is an occupational hazard for people working with animals, including abattoir workers, farmers, and veterinary professionals/paraprofessionals [6], [7]. Transmission occurs primarily via inhalation. In addition to ruminants, cats and dogs have also been implicated in human infections as they can become subclinically infected and harbor C. burnetii in their genital tracts [8], [9], [10], [11]. Since the 1980s, parturient cats and dogs have been traced as a source of Q- fever outbreaks in multiple geographic regions, highlighting the risk of parturition-associated zoonotic transmission [8], [12], [13], [14], [15].

Surveillance studies have confirmed the endemicity of C. burnetii in livestock and humans in the Chinese Mainland [16], [17]. A comprehensive, but somewhat dated, systematic review of 29 studies conducted from 1989 to 2013 reported overall C. burnetii seroprevalences of 10% in people, 15% in cattle and 12% in goats [18]. However, Q-fever is not a notifiable disease in the Chinese Mainland [19]. In the Hong Kong special administrative region (SAR) of China, an average of one human case has been reported annually since 2008, when Q-fever was declared a notifiable human disease [20]. However, no surveillance for C. burnetii infection in animals in Hong Kong has been reported.

Hong Kong has a population of 7.51 million people and a total land area of 1115 km2 in which 40% of the land is preserved as country parks or special nature areas. Local food animal production includes swine, poultry and aquaculture [21]. Cattle and water buffalo, once used for dairy farming and rice cultivation, were abandoned in the 1970s and established a free-roaming bovine population of approximately 900 cattle (Bos taurus) and 180 water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis), in multiple herds dispersed across habitats ranging from urbanized areas to rural country parks [22], [23]. Whilst sheep are not farmed in Hong Kong, there are 100 or more goat small holdings ranging in size from several to over 500 goats in the New Territories of Hong Kong. An estimated 240,000 households kept 304,000 dogs pet dogs and 280,000 cats in 2023, according to thematic survey data collated by Hong Kong's Legislative Council Commission [24].

The objective of this study was to identify potential domestic and wild animal reservoirs of C. burnetii in Hong Kong, to inform occupational exposure risks for people working with animals in the region.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study to detect active infection and/or previous exposure to C. burnetii was conducted in locally resident animal populations. Convenience samples were obtained from domestic and wild ruminants, and from non-ruminant species sampled between November 2020 and December 2023 in Hong Kong.

Ethical approval

The sampling of animals in this study was approved by the Animal Research Ethics Sub-Committee of the City University of Hong Kong, including ruminants (Internal Ref: AN-STA-00000007, A-0826), cats and dogs (A-0709, AN-STA-00000004), and rats (A-0380). After collection, all biological samples were stored at −80 °C until testing.

Samples from ruminants

Since capture and restraint of feral ruminants is restricted, non-invasive, freshly voided faeces were collected opportunistically from cattle being observed in eight districts in Hong Kong for other studies between November 2022 and October 2023 [23], [25] (Fig. 1). While feasible, this approach imposes a limitation on detection since faecal shedding is low and intermittent in non-pregnant animals [5]. Blood samples, rectal swabs and vaginal swabs were also collected by veterinary officers of the Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department (AFCD) of the Hong Kong SAR from a subset of the cattle population, and from water buffalo between February 2023 and October 2023 when animals were caught and restrained for veterinary care. Samples from non-ruminant animals

Opportunistic blood sampling of apparently healthy owned and unowned dogs and cats was performed during neutering surgeries at local animal shelters (Fig. 1). In addition, samples of uterus and testes were retained after surgical removal. Amniotic fluid and placental tissues collected from the extirpated uterus of gravid females were also retained. Cat and dog samples were collected between January 2022 and March 2023. Demographic and clinical data were collected, where available. Archived sera and liver samples stored at −80 °C were retrieved from peri-domestic rats of four different species (Rattus norvegicus n = 143, R. tanezumi n = 63, R. andamanensis n = 8, Niviventer huang n = 2) that were trapped in nine urban and farm sites in Hong Kong between November 2020 and August 2021 and humanely euthanized for another project (Fig. 1) [26].

Molecular testing for C. burnetii DNA

DNA was extracted from tissue samples, amniotic fluid and vaginal swabs using the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Before DNA extraction, 25 mg of each tissue sample (liver, testes, uterus) was homogenized in a Precellys Evolution homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) at 6000 rpm (20 s for three cycles). For blood, fluid and faeces, DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit and the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit, respectively (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

The presence of amplifiable DNA in each DNA template was confirmed by PCR targeting the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase gene (GAPDH), used as an endogenous internal control [27], [28]. C. burnetii DNA in non-rodent animals was detected using a PCR assay specific for a 687 bp fragment of the repetitive transposon region (IS1111) [29]. A synthetic customized oligonucleotide was used as a positive control (IDT, Coralville, Iowa, USA). To test for C. burnetii in rodent samples, a nested PCR assay targeting the IS1111 genetic region to enhance sensitivity in wildlife-derived tissues was used [30], and commercially sourced C. burnetii DNA was used as a positive control (Amplirun® Vircell, Granada, Spain).

To verify the presence and size of the amplification products, all tested samples tested were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Serological testing for anti-C. burnetii antibodies

Seroconversion against C. burnetii was assessed using two different commercial ELISA kits, according to the manufacturers' instructions. Ruminant sera were tested with the IDEXX Q- fever Ab test (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, Maine, USA), which is validated for ruminant species and utilizes an antigen derived from the Nine Mile reference strain of C. burnetii isolated from a tick and an anti-ruminant IgG conjugate and is validated for ruminant species [31]. For non-ruminant species (cats, dogs and rats), the IDVet ID Screen Q-Fever Indirect Multispecies kit (IDVet, Grabels, France) was used, in the absence of commercially available serological kits validated for use in these species [31]. The IDVet kit, coated with a C. burnetii antigen from a bovine isolate, uses a mixture of protein A and G HRP-conjugate to detect mammalian IgGs. Confirmation with alternative independent assays for ruminant sera, i.e., indirect Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA), was not performed.

In the molecular study, seven samples tested negative by GAPDH PCR (faecal samples from three cattle and three buffalo, and one blood sample from a buffalo). The absence of amplification, even after a 1:10 DNA dilution, suggested the presence of PCR inhibitors in these samples, which were subsequently excluded from testing. C. burnetii DNA was not detected in any of the 866 samples tested, regardless of animal species (Table 2). All 125 cats tested were unowned and 31 (24.8%) were pregnant, while one had recently given birth. Among 153 dogs tested by PCR, 79 (51.6%) were unowned and 74 (48.4%) dogs were owned. No dogs were known to be pregnant although these data were incomplete.

Three of 691 animals for which serology was performed, specifically, one goat and two cats (0.4%), tested positive for anti-C. burnetii antibodies (IgG) (Table 2). Discussion

This study, the first to test for C. burnetii infection in the local animal populations of Hong Kong, identified no evidence of an active enzootic cycle of C. burnetii. The target for molecular testing selected here, the transposon element IS1111 is a multicopy gene, facilitating the detection of low burdens of C. burnetii [32]. However, a negative PCR result does not exclude infection due to the intermittent nature of C. burnetii shedding by non-parturient infected animals [1], [5]. Hence, serological evidence can assist in detecting C. burnetii exposure. The 100 feral bovids tested serologically in this study, represent 9.3% of the bovid population and all tested seronegative. It is possible that the sterilization of a large proportion of the feral bovid population, through a capture-sterilise-release/relocate program managed by the Hong Kong AFCD, may have contributed to this result in this closed herd. Sterilization techniques have previously included castration, oviduct removal or use of an injectable immunocontraceptive vaccine that targets GnRH [33].

In other regions in Asia and Oceania, the prevalence of C. burnetii in bovids varies markedly from country to country. For example, a meta-analysis of 103 studies from South Asia identified a high herd level prevalence, with 77.3% of ruminant herds containing seropositive animals and an estimated pooled seroprevalence in cattle of 13.8% and 13.2% in India and Pakistan, respectively [34]. In North-Eastern Australia 61.2% of beef cattle herds tested contained seropositive animals and the overall seroprevalence was 6.4% [35]. By contrast, despite a burgeoning population of over 5 million dairy cattle in New Zealand, the country remains free of C. burnetii [36]. It has been postulated that this is because of an absence of a sylvatic animal-tick cycle. Haemaphysalis longicornis, the only livestock tick reported in New Zealand, has been reported to occasionally harbor Coxiella-like bacteria, but evidence of vector competence for C burnetii is currently lacking [37].

Whilst we found no evidence of bovid reservoirs of C. burnetii, we did not perform surveillance of cattle in Hong Kong's abattoirs, which slaughter, an average of 25 cattle daily, imported by road from the Chinese Mainland [20]. The proximity of Hong Kong to endemic foci of infection is demonstrated by an outbreak of Q-fever in 2018 and 2019 in Zhuhai City, Guangdong Province, which is less than 60 km from Hong Kong. C. burnetii infection rates as high as 32.4% in cattle and 31.0% in goats from a local slaughterhouse were identified using metagenomic next-generation sequencing. C. burnetii sequences identified from animals were closely related to those from humans, pinpointing the slaughterhouse as the source of the epidemic [38].

Of the 142 goats sampled in three herds in this study, a single goat was seropositive using two different commercial ELISA assays. Whilst these two assays utilise different antigens and conjugates, a false-positive result cannot be ruled out, since cross-reactivity to C. burnetii antigens might occur in both assays [31]. The positive goat was sampled in a recreational farm located in the New Territories, less than 10 km from the border with the Chinese Mainland, where Q-fever is endemic in a wide range of animal species [17]. Transboundary transmission of C. burnetii is theoretically possible since the environmental resilience of C. burnetii and its capacity for windborne spread has been correlated with interherd disease transmission and outbreaks among human populations even at considerable distances from the original source [39]. Mid- to long-range transmission events, particularly in settings characterized by high wind speed, open landscapes, and elevated temperatures have also been reported [40]. It is hypothesized that the absence of significant mountainous barriers between Hong Kong's border and the Chinese Mainland, coupled with cold winter monsoon winds from northern and north-eastern China create environmental conditions that might facilitate transboundary airborne transmission of C. burnetii [41].

No evidence of active infection or exposure was found in the peri-domestic rats. The greater sensitivity of the nested PCR used for rat samples, than the conventional PCR used for the other species, supports the robustness of these results. C. burnetii has been identified, mostly by molecular detection, in a diverse range of rat species from urban and rural environments in many regions, including the Chinese Mainland, Korea, Africa, South America and Europe with prevalences ranging from 1.4 to 45% [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]. Whilst our findings suggest that rodents have a minimal role in the epidemiology of C. burnetii in Hong Kong, in other regions, there is evidence of a role for infected rodents in zoonotic transmission cycles, and as important reservoirs for the maintenance and spread of C. burnetii in endemic areas via bi-directional transmission with their tick ectoparasites [47], [48]. Thus, ticks should be included in future C. burnetii surveillance programs in Hong Kong.

In our study, C. burnetii was not found in any reproductive tract samples from companion animals, including from pregnant cats. While C. burnetii has occasionally been identified in subclinically infected cats by bacterial isolation from vaginal swabs or by DNA detection in uterine samples, shedding in infected cats is infrequent and primarily associated with parturition or abortion, as it is in other species [9], [49], [50]. However, we did detect low level seropositivity (1.27%) among cats, while no dogs were seropositive. Serological confirmation of positive feline samples was not possible since only one of the two ELISA assays used in this study is capable of detecting feline anti-C. burnetii antibodies. If Hong Kong is a generally low-prevalence geographic area, the validity of these positive results in cats may be questioned. Assuming 1% prevalence of C. burnetii infection in cats, with a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 96% [29], the positive predictive value of the serological test used is expected to be only around 18.5%, making false-positive results more likely in these conditions. Therefore, the absence of independent confirmation, combined with the low PPV expected with this test, suggests that these results should be interpreted cautiously, to indicate possible exposure only. These findings highlight the importance of confirmatory diagnostics in low-prevalence populations.

The sporadic serological evidence and the absence of active infection or shedding in key reservoir species highlighted in our study, along with the limited local livestock population, suggest that the risk of Q-fever from contact with local animals in Hong Kong is low. This study aimed to identify potential reservoir animal hosts of C. burnetii in Hong Kong and was not intended to provide an estimate of infection prevalence, nor of occupational risks for slaughterhouse workers who are also exposed to animals from the Chinese Mainland. Going forward, surveillance of workers and animals in Hong Kong's slaughterhouses would inform this risk. In addition, molecular surveillance of ticks would be useful to better understand the local epidemiology of C. burnetii infection due to the role of ticks in pathogen maintenance and ecological stability.
