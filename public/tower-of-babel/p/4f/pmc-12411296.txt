Association Between Small Vessel Disease and Migraine: A Study Based on Peak Width of Skeletonized Mean Diffusivity
Authors: Kang Min Park, Jin-Hong Wi, Dong Ah Lee, Ho-Joon Lee, Bong Soo Park, Jinseung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeMigraine is a common neurological disorder associated with structural and functional brain changes, while small vessel disease (SVD) is the leading cause of white matter damage in older adults. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is effective for assessing white matter, and the peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) is a novel marker for such damage. No study has used PSMD to assess SVD in migraine patients. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between migraine and SVD using PSMD.MethodsSeventy-three migraine patients and 73 healthy controls underwent brain DTI to calculate PSMD. Clinical data on migraine characteristics were collected. We compared PSMD values between groups and analyzed correlations with clinical factors in patients with migraine.ResultsPatients with migraine had a significantly higher PSMD than healthy controls (2.08×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.04×10-4 mm2/s, p=0.027), indicating increased white matter damage due to SVD. However, there was no difference in the PSMD between men and women with migraine. No significant differences in the PSMD were found between patients with migraine with and without aura (2.14×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.07×10-4 mm2/s, p=0.074). A positive correlation existed between the PSMD and disease duration in men with migraine (r=0.601, p=0.006). Sex differences were evident, with men having a higher prevalence of aura and with women having a greater headache intensity (47.3% vs. 12.9%, p=0.002; 6.0 vs. 8.0, p=0.025).ConclusionsOur findings indicate a potential association between migraine and SVD, with PSMD serving as a useful MRI marker for assessing white matter damage in headache disorders.Keywords: migraine, diffusion tensor imaging, cerebral small vessel diseases

INTRODUCTION

Migraine, the most common neurological disorder, affects approximately 15% of the population.1,2 It is characterized by vascular dysfunction, temporary nerve activation, and hormonal and neuromodulatory disruptions that can result in long-term structural and functional changes in the brain.3,4,5 The symptoms, intensity, duration, frequency, and related features of migraine attacks vary considerably among individuals, with sex being a contributing factor.1

Although not routinely used in clinical practice for migraine, neuroimaging has been instrumental in studying the underlying neurological mechanisms of this disorder.6 Studies have shown that patients with migraine exhibit greater white matter abnormalities (WMAs), with the risk increasing in those who experience more frequent and severe attacks.7,8 They are also more likely to have silent infarct-like lesions, particularly in the posterior circulation territory.9,10 Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) have revealed both functional and microstructural brain alterations in patients with migraine, including abnormal connectivity in pain-related networks, sensory hypersensitivity, and disruptions in axonal integrity, which changes often associated with cortical spreading depression (CSD) and altered cortical excitability.11,12,13

Small vessel disease (SVD) is a common cerebrovascular condition that primarily affects the brain’s small perforating arterioles, capillaries, and venules. It is largely driven by aging and vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus, as well as smoking, chronic kidney disease, and branch atheromatous disease.14 The most prevalent forms of SVD are arteriolosclerosis, also known as hypertensive or deep perforator arteriopathy, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy, with most cases occurring sporadically.15 Markers of cerebral SVD include lacunes, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), white matter hypointensities (WMhs), and cerebral microbleeds, are commonly detected on brain MRIs in older adults. Among DTI-based approaches, the peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) has emerged as a robust, fully automated marker for assessing white matter integrity. PSMD captures the variability in mean diffusivity (MD) across major white matter tracts, offering high sensitivity to subtle microstructural changes. The calculation of PSMD is fully automated and fast, and has shown stable reproducibility between different scanners and studies, making it well-suited for application to large samples in clinical settings.16 Originally developed to quantify the severity of cerebral SVD, it has also shown associations with cognitive impairment.16,17 PSMD has been shown to demonstrate superior performance than conventional MRI markers such as WMHs, lacunar volume, brain parenchymal fraction, and microbleed count in explaining the cognitive function and quantifying the disease burden of SVD.16

Emerging evidence suggests that migraine may involve microvascular dysfunction, as indicated by the release of vasoactive neuropeptides, CSD, and endothelial dysregulation.18,19 Despite growing interest in this area, no study has investigated this relationship using the PSMD, a sensitive DTI-derived marker of white matter integrity and SVD burden.16,17 Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate whether migraine is associated with increased PSMD, thereby contributing novel insights into the potential role of small vessel pathology in migraine pathophysiology. Additionally, we investigated the WMhs, which is another MRI marker for white matter damage based on T1-weighted imaging, in patients with migraine and compared them with healthy controls.

METHODS

Participants

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Haeundae Paik Hospital (HPIRB 2022-07-017). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects, and all medical procedures followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. From August 2018 to December 2023, patients with migraine with or without aura were recruited based on the following criteria: 1) newly diagnosed with migraine with or without aura according to the 3rd edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders20 and 2) underwent brain MRI, including three-dimensional T1-weighted imaging, axial T2-weighted imaging, and DTI, at the time of migraine diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) the presence of any structural lesions on their brain MRI, 2) poor-quality DTIs for quantitative analysis, or 3) any medical or neurological conditions other than migraine. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the patients’ medical records, including age, sex, age at headache onset, disease duration (time between headache onset and MRI), headache frequency per month, presence of aura, and headache intensity measured on a visual analog scale (VAS). An age- and sex-matched control group of healthy participants with no history of medical or neurological conditions, including migraine, was also enrolled. All participants had normal brain MRI scans revealing no structural abnormalities.

Diffusion tensor imaging

DTI was conducted using the same 3.0-T MRI scanner (AchievaTx, Phillips Healthcare) with a 32-channel head coil, following the same protocol for both the patients with migraine and healthy controls. DTI was performed using spin-echo single-shot echo-planar pulse sequences with a total of 32 different diffusion directions. The acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time/echo time, 8,620/85 ms; flip angle, 90°; slice thickness, 2.25 mm; acquisition matrix, 120×120; field of view, 240×240 mm2; and a b-value of 1,000 s/mm2. The three-dimensional T1-weighted images were scanned using the following parameters: inversion time=1,300 ms, repetition time/echo time=8.6/3.96 ms; flip angle=8°; and isotropic voxel size= 1 mm3.

Obtaining the PSMD

Fig. 1 illustrates the procedure for calculating the PSMD from the DTI data.16 The analysis was conducted on a Linux system using the FMRIB Software Library (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/#/). The process comprised four main steps. The first step involved preprocessing the DTI data, which included correcting for motion and eddy currents, brain extraction, and tensor fitting. The second step was skeletonization, which utilized tract-based spatial statistics by aligning a fractional anisotropy map to a common space and projecting it onto the skeleton. The same transformation matrices were applied to the MD data to create a skeletonized MD map. The third step comprised the application of a custom mask created using a template thresholded at a fractional anisotropy value of 0.3. The final step involved a histogram analysis, in which the width of the histogram (calculated as the difference between the 95th and 5th percentiles) was determined based on the MD values from all voxels in the skeleton.

Process for obtaining the PSMD. A: The first step involves preprocessing the DTI data, including motion and eddy current correction, brain extraction, and tensor fitting. B: The second step is skeletonization, which includes normalization, projection to the skeleton template, and application of a custom mask. C: The final step comprises a histogram analysis and calculation of the PSMD based on the difference between the 95th and 5th percentiles. DTI, diffusion tensor imaging; MD, mean diffusivity; PSMD, peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity.

White matter hypointensity segmentation

To segment WMh from T1-weighted images and acquire WMhs, we used WMH-Synthseg,21 which provides segmentation for WMHs or WMhs from scans of any resolution and contrast without retraining, available as module in the development version of Freesurfer.

Statistical analysis

An independent sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare continuous variables between the groups, while the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between the groups. The performance of the classification was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated to analyze the correlations between the PSMD and clinical characteristics in patients with migraine. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the R package (version 4.1.0, http://www.R-project.org).

Among the participants, 16 reported experiencing aura, all of which were visual. The median headache intensity on the VAS was 8.0, and the median migraine attack frequency was 4.0 per month.

Difference in the PSMD between groups

The PSMD was significantly higher in patients with migraine than in healthy controls (2.08×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.04× 10-4 mm2/s, p=0.027) (Fig. 2). However, the PSMD did not differ between patients with migraine with and without aura (2.14×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.07×10-4 mm2/s, p=0.074) or between men and women with migraine (2.14×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.06×10-4 mm2/s, p=0.138).

Difference in the PSMD between patients with migraine and healthy controls. The figure illustrates that the PSMD is higher in patients with migraine than in healthy controls (2.08×10-4 mm2/s vs. 2.04×10-4 mm2/s, p=0.027). PSMD, peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity.

Difference in the WMhs between groups

The WMhs was significantly higher in patients with migraine than in healthy controls (1310.9 mm3 vs. 1154.7 mm3, p=0.047). However, the WMhs did not differ between patients with migraine with and without aura (1290.1 mm3 vs. 1316.8 mm3, p=0.880) or between men and women with migraine (1189.8 mm3 vs. 1249.6 mm3, p=0.498).

Correlation between the PSMD and clinical characteristics

Table 3 shows the correlation between the PSMD and clinical characteristics in patients with migraine. The PSMD did not correlate with disease duration. However, in the sub-analysis by sex, the PSMD positively correlated with disease duration in men with migraine (r=0.601, p=0.006) (Fig. 3) but not in women with migraine. Furthermore, in all the patients with migraine, no significant association was found between the PSMD and other clinical characteristics, including age at onset, headache intensity, or headache attack frequency.

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis using PSMD showed an area under curve (AUC) of 0.569 in distinguishing the patients with migraine and healthy controls. Additionally, ROC curve analysis using WMhs revealed an AUC of 0.552 in distinguishing the groups. Although the AUC using PSMD was higher than that of WMhs, there were no significant difference in the comparison of AUC between PSMD and WMhs in distinguishing the groups (p=0.763) (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the differences in PSMD between patients with migraine and healthy controls, and explored the correlation between the PSMD and various clinical characteristics. We found that 1) patients with migraine exhibited significantly higher PSMD values than healthy controls; 2) disease duration positively correlated with PSMD in men with migraine; and 3) no significant PSMD differences were observed between patients with and without aura. These findings suggest a potential association between migraine and white matter microstructural damage related to cerebral SVD, and highlight the utility of PSMD as a sensitive imaging biomarker in migraine. Furthermore, men with migraine reported experiencing aura more frequently, whereas women with migraine reported higher VAS scores for headache intensity.

We used the novel MRI marker, the PSMD, which automatically captures the variability of MD values across major white matter tracts and is a highly promising tool for examining white matter damage related to SVD.16 The PSMD has consistent cognitive correlations across diverse populations, especially in individuals with cerebral SVD.16,22,23 Typically, the PSMD is higher in patients with cerebral SVD than in those with primary neurodegenerative disorders.24

WMHs are four times more likely to occur in patients with migraine than in the general population.25 Factors such as age, sex, attack frequency, and headache duration can influence the risk of WMHs in patients with migraine.26 However, the relationship between WMHs and certain migraine characteristics is unclear, or relevant study findings are conflicting.9 A meta-analysis of WMAs revealed a significant association with migraine with aura, but not with migraine without aura. The association of lacunar infarct-like lesions was reportedly stronger with migraine with aura than with migraine without aura, although no significant associations were observed in terms of migraine with and without aura when patients with migraine were compared with controls.9 In the present study, patients with migraine had a higher PSMD than healthy controls did, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. However, unlike earlier studies, no differences in PSMD were observed according to the presence or absence of aura. The progression of WMAs in patients with migraine is not linked to the frequency, duration, or severity of migraine attacks or to the use of antimigraine treatment.27 While previous research has revealed an association between the severity of WMHs in the frontal lobe and an increasing age and the chronicity of migraine,28 we found no correlation between the PSMD and factors such as age at onset, duration of the disease, headache intensity, attack frequency, or the presence of aura.

Interestingly, a correlation between disease duration and the PSMD was observed only in men with migraine. Estrogen replacement therapy has been shown to enhance cerebral blood flow through endothelial NOS- and cyclooxygenase-dependent mechanisms, offering protection against cerebrovascular diseases.29 It also alters regional cerebral metabolism and brain activation patterns in postmenopausal women, particularly in areas such as the inferior frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices, which may reflect changes in small vessel function.30 Menopause and the resulting estrogen deficiency have been strongly associated with leukoaraiosis, and early menopause appears to increase the risk of silent brain infarction in the elderly.31 These findings suggest that female hormones may help reduce the risk or delay the progression of cerebral SVD. The positive association between disease duration and PSMD observed only in males may reflect the relatively young age of the study participants and the mitigating effects of female hormones on PSMD changes in females. In our study, the significantly higher prevalence of migraine with aura in men with migraine might account for this sex-specific relationship. It is in a line with a meta-analysis, which revealed that patients with migraine with aura had a substantially higher risk of WMHs with odds ratio 1.68 than controls, while those without aura showed a less pronounced and statistically nonsignificant increase in risk, with an odds ratio of 1.34.9 Aura is a significant risk factor for WMHs. CSD, an electrophysiological phenomenon unique to aura, differentiates migraine with aura from those without aura.32 This may partially explain the observed sex-specific relationship between PSMD and disease duration.

The exact pathophysiological mechanisms that result in WMAs in individuals with migraine are not completely understood. These lesions may arise from ischemic microvascular issues that lead to a localized reduction in blood flow to the brain tissue.33 During migraine attacks, a decrease in cerebral perfusion pressure hinders the removal of embolic particles, and occlusive thrombi further diminish blood flow.34 Several factors support the idea that episodic ischemia plays a role in the development of WMAs in individuals with migraine. These individuals appear to be more susceptible to the formation of cerebrovascular clots, with evidence indicating endothelial dysfunction.35,36 Additionally, a higher occurrence of persistent foramen ovale has been noted in individuals with migraine, which may facilitate the development of cerebral microembolism.37 The severity of WMAs in these individuals is associated with decreased cerebrovascular reactivity to carbon dioxide.38 During migraine attacks, hypoperfusion can delay vascular recovery, which increases the risk of subclinical or clinical ischemia and contributes to the formation of WMAs due to a compromised cerebrovascular response. Another mechanism underlying migraine is neuroinflammation. In fact, there is evidence of a proinflammatory baseline state in individuals with migraine, with elevated peripheral levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Furthermore, during a migraine attack, serum concentrations of C-reactive protein, von Willebrand factor, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α increase further.39 There is also evidence of intracranial plasma extravasation on the side of the headache, with technetium-99m human serum albumin tracer extravasation observed in the area of the reported pain.40 In addition, gadolinium enhancement near the middle meningeal artery has been reported after a series of migraine attacks.41 Given the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying migraine, including the release of vasoactive neuropeptides, propagation of CSD, and endothelial dysfunction, it is plausible that prolonged attacks can lead to more severe microcirculatory damage. This increased damage could explain the observed positive correlation between disease duration and PSMD in men with migraine.18,19 Similar to our findings, a previous study observed an association between WMHs and the age and duration of migraine in patients, but not with attack frequency.42 The positive correlation between disease duration and PSMD in male patients further supports the notion of progressive microvascular involvement.

We analyzed the clinical characteristics of our study population based on the presence or absence of aura. Only the VAS score for headache intensity showed a significant difference, with patients with migraine with aura having lower scores than did those without aura. The PSMD did not show any differences based on the presence of aura. According to the findings of previous studies, some patients with migraine with aura have shown a high headache severity, while migraine without aura has also been associated with a moderate-to-severe headache intensity.43,44 Therefore, it cannot be concluded that aura consistently reflects headache intensity.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to provide evidence of SVD in patients with migraine by utilizing PSMD analysis. To enhance the homogeneity of our participant pool, we restricted enrollment to individuals exclusively diagnosed with migraine, that is, without any other concurrent diseases. Despite these strengths, our study also has certain limitations. First, while we enrolled a large number of patients with migraine from a single center and used the same MRI scanner, which could enhance the homogeneity of the participants, the single-center design may have introduced a selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to a broader migraine population. Second, the retrospective nature of our study precluded a definitive determination of the temporal relationship between migraine and white matter damage due to SVD. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether the observed disparities in the PSMD are a direct consequence of migraine or a contributing factor. Third, our investigation did not account for potential lifestyle factors, such as smoking or obesity, that could influence migraine outcomes. Nonetheless, our study represents a significant advancement in this field, as it is the first to directly examine the differential presence of the PSMD in patients with migraine and healthy individuals. Lastly, we could not analyze WMHs, since some datasets lacked fluid attenuated inversion recovery images. Instead of WMHs, we analyzed WMhs, which is another MRI marker for white matter damage based on T1-weighted imaging. Although WMHs are more accurate and widely used to assess white matter damage, they have strong correlation with WMhs.45

In conclusion, we found that patients with migraine had a higher PSMD than healthy controls, suggesting a potential association between migraine and SVD. Our findings emphasize the importance of diagnosing and treating migraine. Additionally, the PSMD can be used as a feasible MRI marker to study white matter damage caused by SVD in headache disorders.
