Amino acid balancing and microalgal proteins (Spirulina) improve production performance and milk quality in dairy cows under low-protein diets
Authors: Tianle He, Wenyi Ren, Shuangming Yang, Jinni Bai, Yajun Guo, Yongjiang Wu, Jinping Shi, Caijuan Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroalgal protein (MP, Spirulina) is a highly promising novel sustainable protein resource, yet the mechanisms by which it regulates the production performance and milk quality of lactating dairy cows remain unclear. This study elucidates the regulatory mechanisms by which amino acid balancing and MP supplementation modulate production performance and milk quality in mid-lactation dairy cows fed low-protein diet (LPD). Results demonstrated that dietary interventions significantly altered milk composition. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, proteomic and metabolomic analyses were employed, leading to the identification of 1186 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and 1167 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs). Key signaling molecules, such as GNB1, GNG5, GNG10, and GRB2, interacted with metabolic hubs like α-ketoglutaric acid/Glutamic acid within a regulatory network, enhancing amino acid sensing and signal transduction. The observed coordinated changes in key signaling molecules and metabolic hubs suggest that amino acid sensing and signal transduction pathways may be activated, potentially promoting energy metabolism and nitrogen conversion efficiency. The identified key molecular markers of milk quality lay a scientific basis for the production of high-quality dairy raw materials and the development of value-added dairy products.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Physiology

Introduction

Dairy products are one of the most important food categories in the human diet, providing high-quality protein, calcium, and various bioactive compounds essential for human health. The nutritional quality, functional properties, and processing suitability of raw milk directly determine the quality, market competitiveness and nutritional value of final dairy products, which is a core interdisciplinary concern spanning animal nutrition and food science fields1. Holstein cattle, as the leading dairy breed globally, play a vital role in both the economy and human nutrition due to their superior production performance and milk quality2. Under intensive farming systems, optimizing nutritional strategies to enhance milk composition and increase the nutritional value of raw milk remains a central focus within ruminant nutrition research3,4. With growing consumer demand for functional dairy products, quality indicators such as milk fat content, protein levels, and bioactive constituents have become crucial parameters reflecting the core competitiveness of dairy farms5. Meanwhile, the development of sustainable livestock farming has received increasing attention, prompting researchers to not only improve production efficiency but also consider milk quality and the reduction of environmental emissions6. This multiple set of demands is driving innovations in dairy feed formulations toward a comprehensive direction that integrates efficiency, quality, and environmental friendliness.

Traditional dairy cow nutrition research has mostly focused on increasing milk yield and reducing farming costs, while neglecting the systematic effects of nutritional interventions on the food quality of raw milk7. In recent years, low-protein diet (LPD) have been widely used as a green farming technology, but there are still controversies regarding their effects on core food quality indicators. Recent studies demonstrate that supplementing Rumen-protected amino acids (RP-AA) enables a reduction of dietary crude protein levels by 1.5%–2% without compromising milk production performance8. Furthermore, research indicates that LPD combined with limiting amino acids can significantly improve nitrogen utilization efficiency while maintaining milk protein synthesis9,10. The metabolism of mammary epithelial cells heavily depends on the pattern of amino acid supply in the bloodstream11. Methionine (Met) and lysine (Lys), as the primary limiting amino acids in ruminants, not only regulate milk protein synthesis but also influence lipid metabolism via methyl donor and signal transduction pathways12. Supplementing rumen-protected methionine (RP-Met) has been shown to significantly increase conjugated linoleic acid in milk fat12, while precise RP-Lys supplementation optimizes the ratio of casein to whey proteins13. Notably, RP-AA have been confirmed to promote mammary epithelial proliferation and the expression of proteins involved in fatty acid and milk protein synthesis14. This also marks a paradigm shift in dairy nutrition from crude protein maximization to precision amino acid balancing. However, this precision supplementation approach may mean that dairy cows lose the opportunity to obtain other unknown bioactive substances from complex, intact protein matrices, which may have subtle but important effects on the functional properties and final quality of dairy products. Therefore, it is imperative to seek high-quality proteins that can match traditional proteins and also provide multiple health benefits.

In the ongoing search for novel sustainable protein sources, microalgal proteins (MP), recommended by the FAO as a future high-quality protein source, has attracted attention15,16. Spirulina, as an important member of MP, has been widely noted because its protein content reaches 50%-70% of dry weight, its amino acid profile highly matches the requirements for milk protein synthesis in the mammary gland of dairy cows17,18, its lysine and methionine content is 35% and 28% higher than that of soybean meal, respectively, and it can precisely supplement the limiting amino acid gaps in LPD17,19. Related research has suggested its application potential and complexity. Dietary supplementation with Spirulina helps improve feed conversion efficiency, immunity, production performance, reproductive parameters, and alleviate oxidative stress during the transition period in dairy cows, and it can directly affect milk composition, sensory perception, and microbial community17,20. However, in the context of LPD, whether there is an essential difference in the molecular regulatory networks between the holistic bio-nutrition model provided by Spirulina and the chemical precision nutrition model provided by RP-AA in improving dairy cow lactation performance and milk composition remains unclear. From the perspective of food science, most existing studies on LPD have focused on animal production performance and nitrogen utilization efficiency, while neglecting the systematic evaluation of raw milk food quality attributes, especially the changes in functional components and molecular characteristics that affect dairy processing and nutritional value. Moreover, whether there are essential differences in the effects of RP-AA and MP on the food quality of milk remains unclear, and the molecular mechanisms linking dietary nutrition regulation to milk food quality have not been fully elucidated. Clarifying these issues is of great significance for guiding the production of high-quality raw milk and developing functional dairy products.

Therefore, this study was designed to address the dual challenges of sustainable livestock production and high-quality dairy raw material supply from the integrated perspective of animal nutrition and food science. Using mid-lactation Holstein cows as the research model, we systematically evaluated the effects of amino acid-balanced LPD and MP supplementation on raw milk production performance and key food quality indicators. Furthermore, we employed integrated proteomic and metabolomic approaches to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which nutritional regulation modulates milk food quality. The overarching goals are to provide a comprehensive theoretical framework for precision nutrition in lactating dairy cows and to lay the groundwork for the development of novel functional dairy products.

Results

Amino acid balancing and MP maintain DMI and production performance in dairy cows under LPD conditions

The entire experimental protocol of the present study was precisely conducted and completed following the standardized procedure depicted in Fig. 1A. Figure 1B and 1C shows the changes in DMI of dairy cows across different treatment groups over time. Results indicated that there were no significant differences in weekly DMI among the groups, and no obvious changes in DMI were observed for any group over the course of the study (P > 0.05). Figure 1D and 1E illustrate the temporal changes in milk yield of dairy cows in different treatment groups. Specifically, at the same time points, there were no significant differences in milk yield between groups (P > 0.05). A gradual decrease in milk yield was observed in all groups over time, which is a well-documented normal physiological phenomenon for dairy cows in mid-lactation21. This indicates that, under the context of a LPD, both amino acid balance and MP can compensate to a certain extent for the impact of insufficient dietary protein on the productive performance of dairy cows. Notably, the milk yield of dairy cows in the MP group is very close to that in the NP group, which demonstrates that the MP group’s diet is more effective than the LP group’s in maintaining the productive performance of dairy cows.

Amino acid balancing and MP improve milk composition in dairy cows under LPD conditions

Figure 2A–F depict the changes in milk composition of cows fed experimental diets for four weeks. There were no significant differences in lactose content among the groups at this time point (P > 0.05); however, the LP and MP groups exhibited higher milk lactose levels numerically compared to the NP group (Fig. 2A). Additionally, both milk fat (Fig. 2B) and milk protein (Fig. 2C) contents in the MP group were significantly higher than those in the NP group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed between the LP group and other groups (P > 0.05). Compared with the NP group, the milk urea nitrogen content in both the LP and MP groups was significantly decreased (P < 0.05); however, there was no significant difference in urea nitrogen content between the LP and MP groups (Fig. 2D). The total solids content in both the LP and MP groups was significantly higher than that in the NP group (P < 0.05), with no significant difference observed between the LP and MP groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2E). Additionally, although the somatic cell count in the LP and MP groups was lower than that in the NP group, the difference among all groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2F).

Effects of amino acid balancing and MP on milk quality in mid-lactation dairy cows fed a LPD (n = 25).

A–F Changes in milk composition after 4 weeks of experimental dietary feeding. (G–L) Changes in milk composition after 8 weeks of experimental dietary feeding. (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01).

Figure 2G-L illustrate the milk composition after eight weeks of dietary treatment. Similar to the earlier time point, no significant differences in lactose content were observed among groups (P > 0.05), but the LP and MP groups showed higher lactose levels numerically than the NP group (Fig. 2G). Notably, both the milk fat content (Fig. 2H) and milk protein content (Fig. 2I) in the MP group were significantly higher than those in the NP group (P < 0.05). The LP group also showed significantly higher milk fat and protein content compared to the NP group (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in these components between the MP and LP groups (P > 0.05), although numerical increases were observed in the MP group. Compared with the NP group, the milk urea nitrogen content in both the LP and MP groups was significantly decreased (P < 0.05); however, there was no significant difference in urea nitrogen content between the LP and MP groups (Fig. 2J). The total solids content in both the LP and MP groups was significantly higher than that in the NP group (P < 0.05), and the total solids content in the MP group was significantly higher than that in the LP group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2K). Additionally, although the somatic cell count in the LP and MP groups was lower than that in the NP group, the difference among all groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2L). It can thus be concluded that, in the context of LPD, amino acid balance and MP can effectively improve milk quality. This improvement is specifically evidenced by increased organic matter content, reduced urea nitrogen and somatic cell content, with these beneficial effects becoming increasingly pronounced over time.

Identification and functional annotation of DEPs in milk

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms by which amino acid balance and MP affect dairy quality under the background of a LPD, we conducted proteomic sequencing analysis on milk samples. As shown in Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 1, protein expression levels varied across samples from different groups, with high reproducibility within groups and clear separation between groups. Subsequent analysis identified DEPs among the groups: 72 DEPs (32 upregulated, 42 downregulated) between NP and LP groups; 77 DEPs (29 upregulated, 48 downregulated) between NP and MP groups; and 92 DEPs (29 upregulated, 63 downregulated) between LP and MP groups (Fig. 3B). A Venn diagram further illustrated the shared and unique DEPs among the groups (Fig. 3C). Detailed information on the DEPs is provided in Fig. 3D.

Pairwise comparisons of DEPs across groups (Fig. 3E–G) revealed distinct proteomic profiles. Functional annotation of these proteins via KEGG pathway analysis showed that the most enriched categories fell under organismal Systems and metabolism, notably including immune system, endocrine system, digestive system, global and overview maps, and signal transduction, among others. Although pathways such as amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism involved fewer DEPs, their biological significance cannot be overlooked (Fig. 3H). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that metabolic pathways was the most prominently enriched pathway across different nutrient treatments (Fig. 3I-K). Additionally, DEPs in NP and LP groups were significantly associated with fatty acid metabolism, amino acid synthesis, and energy metabolism pathways (Fig. 3I), while DEPs between NP and MP groups were linked to fatty acid synthesis, protein synthesis, and digestive and absorptive processes (Fig. 3J). The DEPs between LP and MP groups were primarily related to amino acid metabolism, protein synthesis, and digestion/absorption processes (Fig. 3K). These findings suggest that DEPs are closely related to systemic functions and metabolic processes, chiefly involving immune response, endocrine regulation, fatty acid synthesis, protein synthesis, digestion, and absorption, along with core functions such as regulating milk production performance. It can thus be concluded that, in the context of a LPD, amino acid balance and MP can ultimately enhance dairy cows’ milk production performance by regulating physiological processes such as digestive and absorptive functions, overall health status, and metabolic homeostasis.

Construction and validation of PPI network for milk DEPs and their correlation with milk components

Proteins play irreplaceable roles in biological processes, and many perform their functions not independently but through interactions with other proteins, forming complexes. Based on the DEPs, we constructed PPI networks for the milk samples under various nutritional regimes (Fig. 4A–C). In these networks, nodes represented by darker rectangles are considered core functional proteins. Additionally, we developed an integrated PPI network encompassing all DEPs across the three groups, from which key hub proteins, including RPS19, RPL27, RPL10L, RPL10, RPL29, RPS15A, HMGN2, and RPL6, were identified (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, the network centered on BTN1A1, ACSL3, ADIPOQ, PLIN2, LSS, and ACSL1 exhibited limited connectivity with the main network, indicating discrete functional modules (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To investigate how nutritional interventions affect milk nutritional factors, we first validated selected candidates in vitro. The results demonstrated that CD177 concentrations in milk from the LP and MP groups were significantly higher than in the NP group (P < 0.01), with the MP group showing even higher levels (P < 0.01). Similarly, CARD9 levels were significantly elevated in the LP group compared to NP and MP groups (P < 0.01), and MP levels were higher than NP (P < 0.05). Both CD38 and HNRNPAB concentrations were significantly higher in the NP and MP groups relative to LP (P < 0.01), with NP also surpassing MP for CD38 (P < 0.05) and HNRNPAB (P < 0.01). METTL7A levels in LP and MP groups exceeded those in NP (P < 0.01), with LP also higher than MP (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). Correlation analysis between proteomic sequencing and measured protein concentrations indicated a high concordance (R² = 0.89), confirming the validity of our sequencing data (Fig. 4E). Finally, correlation analysis revealed a strong correlation between DEPs and milk composition parameters under the treatment of amino acid balance and MP in a LPD conditions. This indicates that dietary interventions in dairy cows can regulate milk composition by altering the proteomic characteristics of milk (Fig. 4F–H).

Identification and functional annotation of DEMs in milk

Building upon the proteomic findings which revealed significant alterations in milk proteins related to metabolic pathways, we conducted metabolomic analysis to further investigate the regulatory mechanisms of different nutritional treatments on milk metabolism. This aimed to decipher changes in milk metabolites and their intrinsic correlations with DEPs. As depicted in Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 4, milk samples across groups exhibited high metabolite expression abundances, robust intra-group reproducibility, and distinct inter-group clustering. Subsequently, we identified DEMs in milk samples under different treatments. A total of 63 DEMs (43 upregulated, 20 downregulated) were identified between the NP and LP groups, 90 DEMs (69 upregulated, 21 downregulated) between the NP and MP groups, and 60 DEMs (51 upregulated, 9 downregulated) between the LP and MP groups (Fig. 5B). A Venn diagram of DEMs across groups further illustrated the shared and unique metabolites among comparisons (Fig. 5C). Detailed information on all DEMs in each group is presented in Fig. 5D. Pairwise comparisons of DEMs across the three groups revealed abundance profiles shown in Fig. 5E–G.

Functional annotation of DEMs demonstrated that within the KEGG global classification, the metabolism category harbored the highest number of enriched DEMs, with particularly prominent enrichment in pathways such as global and overview maps, amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and chemical structure transformation maps. While other pathways also showed enrichment, they contained fewer associated metabolites (Fig. 5H). These findings indicate that nutritional treatments induced pronounced global metabolic reprogramming, primarily concentrated in core metabolic pathways such as amino acid, carbohydrate, and nucleotide metabolism. Notably, metabolic pathways emerged as the most enriched term across all comparisons (Fig. 5I–K). In addition, DEPs between NP and LP groups were enriched in pathways related to amino acid and alkaloid biosynthesis (Fig. 5I), while DEPs between NP and MP groups were enriched in protein digestion and absorption pathways (Fig. 5J). DEPs between LP and MP groups were associated with trace element and carbon metabolism pathways (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these results suggest that DEPs are intricately linked to bovine systemic functions and metabolic processes, particularly in biological pathways related to immunity, endocrine regulation, fatty acid synthesis, protein anabolism, digestion/absorption, as well as core functions like signal transduction and metabolic regulation. Finally, correlation analysis confirmed strong associations between DEMs and milk components under different nutritional treatments (Fig. 5L–N). Fewer DEMs related to milk components were identified in NP-vs-LP and LP-vs-MP comparisons than in NP-vs-MP, indicating that nutritional interventions altered milk quality by reprogramming metabolomics profiles, with the most pronounced differences observed between NP and MP groups. This indicates that, in the context of a LPD, amino acid balance and MP can alter the metabolomic profiles of milk, thereby participating in the process of regulating milk composition. The results supports a functional link between nutritional interventions, milk metabolome changes, and the modulation of milk components.

Combined analysis of proteomics and metabolomics

Proteomic sequencing yields numerous DEPs, yet direct correlation between proteins and phenotypes remains challenging, complicating the identification of key regulatory proteins from large datasets. Single-omics analyses often struggle to interpret complex biological network regulations. Metabolomics bridges the gap between proteins and phenotypes, enabling rapid identification of candidate proteins and metabolic pathways through integrative proteomics-metabolomics analyses, which provide comprehensive insights into molecular functions and regulatory mechanisms. To investigate the correlations between DEPs and DEMs under the treatment of amino acid balance and MP in a LPD conditions, and to further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms by which dietary interventions affect dairy cow productive performance and milk composition, we constructed correlation heatmaps of all DEPs and DEMs under different nutritional treatments (Fig. 6A). At the same time, we integrated functional enrichment pathways of DEPs and DEMs from each group to generate a Venn diagram (Fig. 6B), revealing two centrally overlapping pathways: metabolic pathways and protein digestion and absorption. The NP-vs-LP and LP-vs-MP comparisons shared three common pathways: nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, and phenylalanine metabolism. The NP-vs-MP and LP-vs-MP comparisons highlighted three overlapping pathways: glutathione metabolism, carbon metabolism, and Arginine and Proline Metabolism. Five shared pathways were identified in NP-vs-LP and NP-vs-MP comparisons: fatty acid biosynthesis, oxytocin signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis. Subsequently, using Spearman correlation analysis, we selected DEPs and DEMs with FC > 2 or <0.5 and P < 0.05 to construct treatment-specific correlation heatmaps (Fig. 6C–E). Further, a multi-omics integration strategy was employed to generate a potential integrated regulatory network (Fig. 6F) based on pathway co-enrichment and correlation analysis. This network revealed that signaling molecules (GNB1, GNG5, GNG10, GRB2) and metabolic hubs (α-ketoglutaric acid, glutamic acid) were central nodes connecting nutritional interventions to milk component synthesis. Specifically, the upregulation of G protein subunits and GRB2 was significantly correlated with the accumulation of α-ketoglutaric acid and glutamic acid, which in turn were positively correlated with milk fat and protein content. These consistent changes across multiple omics levels suggest that these molecules may be involved in the regulation of mammary nutrient utilization, but direct causal relationships remain to be verified.

Discussion

The improvement of raw milk quality is the core link connecting upstream animal nutrition regulation and downstream food science research, as it directly affects the processing characteristics, nutritional value, functional properties and sensory quality of dairy products. This study not only confirmed that LPD supplemented with amino acids or Spirulina can maintain stable dairy cow production performance, but more importantly, demonstrated that these nutritional strategies can significantly improve the key food quality indicators of raw milk, which has important guiding significance for the dairy industry1. Driven by the dual carbon goals and food safety strategies, LPD have emerged as a crucial technological pathway for the green transformation of dairy farming10. While reducing dietary crude protein levels can decrease urinary nitrogen excretion and lower feed costs, the widespread adoption of this technology has been consistently constrained by the challenge of maintaining production performance. Consequently, within the LPD framework, achieving synergistic improvements in environmental benefits, production performance, and food quality through precise regulation of amino acid balance and identification of high-quality protein sources that provide both nutritional supplementation and bioactive regulatory functions has become a key scientific question spanning the fields of nutrition and food science.

This study systematically evaluated how amino acid balance and MP supplementation in LPD affect the production performance and milk composition of mid-lactation dairy cows. The results demonstrate the potential of these nutritional strategies to significantly improve milk quality while maintaining stable milk yield. Regarding production performance, neither DMI nor milk yield was significantly affected by dietary treatments across groups, consistent with previous reports8,22,23. This indicates that moderate reduction of dietary protein levels, when complemented with limiting amino acids or novel protein sources, can effectively maintain nitrogen metabolic homeostasis without compromising production performance. Notably, the milk yield in the MP group was comparable to the normal-protein group and significantly superior to the LP group, suggesting that MP may compensate for amino acid deficiencies in LPD through its balanced amino acid profile or unique bioactive compounds. This finding aligns with Lamminen et al17. report on the high rumen-bypass protein value of microalgae in ruminants, where its efficient intestinal absorption may contribute to improved overall nitrogen utilization efficiency. In terms of milk composition improvement, the MP group demonstrated significant advantages in both milk fat and protein content, with total solid content at week 8 also being significantly higher than in the LP group. This improvement likely stems from multiple mechanisms. Research indicates that specific long-chain fatty acids abundant in microalgae can modulate mammary lipid metabolism and stimulate milk fat synthesis24. Concurrently, the superior amino acid balance of MP, particularly its high lysine and methionine content, may more precisely meet the requirements for milk protein synthesis in mammary tissue25. Notably, the significant increase in milk fat, milk protein and total solids content in the MP group is of great practical value for the dairy industry. Higher total solids content can increase the yield of dairy products such as cheese and yogurt by 5%-8% per ton of raw milk, while the balanced amino acid profile and increased bioactive components in milk can enhance the nutritional value of final dairy products26. In addition, the significant reduction in milk urea nitrogen not only indicates improved nitrogen utilization efficiency in cows, but also helps to reduce protein denaturation during heat processing, improve the texture of fermented dairy products, and reduce the formation of harmful substances such as biogenic amines. The significant reduction in MUN provides further evidence for the efficacy of LPD and amino acid balancing strategies, consistent with the theoretical framework proposed by Zeleke et al27. regarding reduced dietary nitrogen excess and improved nitrogen utilization efficiency. The decrease in MUN directly reflects reduced nitrogen metabolic burden and enhanced nitrogen deposition efficiency, serving as a sensitive indicator for assessing dietary protein utilization. The sustained improvements in milk composition achieved through RP-AA and MP align with findings from in early-lactation cows, though our study demonstrates potentially superior magnitude and persistence of effects, possibly attributable to differences in lactation stage, microalgae source, and inclusion rates. Furthermore, under LPD conditions, while amino acid balancing alone improved certain milk composition parameters, its effects on milk fat and total solid content were inferior to those in the MP group receiving microalgae supplementation. This suggests that MP may exert effects beyond mere amino acid supplementation, potentially through its complex protein composition and distinct digestion, absorption, and utilization pathways28–30. Correspondingly, the lower numbers of DEPs and DEMs between NP and LP groups compared to NP and MP groups, further strengthens the consistency between experimental treatments and observed outcomes, indicating that amino acid balancing and MP in LPD primarily regulate milk composition through alterations in milk DEPs and DEMs.

The core proteins identified in the PPI network are ribosomal proteins, essential structural components of the ribosome that directly participate in the translation and synthesis of all intracellular proteins. In the bovine mammary gland, elevated expression or enhanced activity of these ribosomal proteins indicates robust protein synthesis capacity within mammary epithelial cells, directly contributing to increased milk protein content31–33. Furthermore, this heightened protein synthesis supports the growth, maintenance, and renewal of mammary epithelial cells, which is critical for sustaining high milk yield and healthy lactational function14,34,35. HMGN2 is a chromatin-binding protein that can promote the transcription of various genes, including those encoding milk proteins, thereby enriching the mRNA pool and indirectly but effectively supporting high-level protein synthesis36,37. GRB2, a key adapter protein in cellular signal transduction, mediates hormonal (prolactin) and nutritional stimulation of mammary cells, promoting proliferation and differentiation, thereby enhancing overall lactational performance and positively regulating milk yield and composition38,39. CFL2 likely facilitates efficient secretion of milk components such as lipids and proteins by regulating cytoskeletal dynamics. VAMP8, a critical mediator of vesicle-membrane fusion, acts as a direct executor of milk component secretion40,41. Both lipid droplet-enclosed vesicles and casein-containing secretory vesicles rely on SNARE protein complexes such as VAMP8 to release their contents into the alveolar lumen41. Efficient VAMP8 function is thus essential for the successful secretion of milk fat, protein, and lactose into milk. Collectively, under LPD conditions, amino acid balancing and MP supplementation activate specific anabolic pathways in the mammary gland, significantly elevating milk protein and fat content without compromising milk yield, thereby improving overall milk quality.

The long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase (ACS) family plays a pivotal role in fatty acid metabolism, mediating fatty acid activation essential for milk fat synthesis and overall milk composition42. Prior research indicates that ACS family members influence not only fatty acid utilization but also the type and amount of milk fat, thereby affecting nutritional quality and flavor43. Recent studies in ruminant biology have further elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which ACS family members govern milk lipid synthesis and dairy quality regulation. Based on whole-genome characterization of the goat mammary gland, researchers identified members of the ACSS, ACSL, and ACSF gene families as key participants in fatty acid activation. Among these, ACSS2 and ACSF3 were found to exhibit specialized roles in milk lipid metabolism and flavor formation, providing a molecular foundation for understanding the regulatory mechanisms of milk component biosynthesis44. At the regulatory level, miR-181a downregulates ACSL1 by targeting its mRNA, thereby negatively affecting lipid synthesis in mammary cells, indicating that the expression of ACS genes is finely controlled by microRNAs (miRNAs), which may serve as potential nutritional targets to modulate milk fat synthesis45. Furthermore, proteomic profiling of dairy cows across lactation stages revealed that activation of the PPAR signaling pathway upregulates ACSL4 expression, thereby promoting de novo milk fat synthesis. This finding underscores the dynamic involvement of ACS family members in lipid biosynthesis under pathway-specific regulation46. At the interface of immunity and metabolism, C4BPA coordinates inflammatory responses and lipid anabolism by modulating PPAR pathway activity and ACSL1 expression, highlighting the multifaceted roles of ACS proteins in orchestrating mammary epithelial cell metabolism47. Concurrently, SREBP1 serving as a master regulator of lipid synthesis was shown to significantly upregulate key enzymes such as ACSL1 and ACSS2 upon overexpression, thereby enhancing fatty acid activation and milk fat biosynthesis. This finding further solidifies the ACS family’s central role as regulatory hubs in milk lipid metabolism48. Collectively, these results establish the ACS family as core modulators of mammary fatty acid metabolism, nutritional intervention responses, and dairy quality improvement. In the present study, the significant upregulation of rate-limiting enzymes like ACSL1 and ACSL3 under LPD plus nutrient fortifier interventions, coupled with marked improvements in milk fat and protein content, strongly indicates that activating mammary fatty acid activation enzymes can effectively promote milk component synthesis and accumulation. This not only aligns with prior research on the ACS family’s central role in fatty acid metabolism regulation but also provides further validation that enhancing key rate-limiting enzymes in mammary fatty acid activation can improve milk composition.

Multi-omics integration analyses identified signaling-associated proteins GNB1, GNG5, GNG10, GRB2, and metabolic intermediates AKG and Glu as critical nodes in the molecular network governing efficient nutrient utilization in the mammary gland. As β and γ subunits of G proteins, GNB1, GNG5, and GNG10 are extensively involved in G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-mediated signal transduction49 and play central regulatory roles in activating pathways such as mTOR and PI3K-AKT50–54. GRB2, a key adaptor protein in receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) pathways55, bridges upstream growth factor signaling with downstream metabolic and anabolic pathways56–59, with functional regulation of these signaling networks validated in prior studies. Wu et al60. demonstrated that transcription factor KLF4 is significantly upregulated in mammary tissues of high milk fat cows, promoting milk fat synthesis by activating the PI3K-AKT/mTOR pathway, enhancing FASN transcription, and accelerating fatty acid synthesis and lipid deposition. Similarly, Zhang et al61. showed that activating PI3K-Akt, Wnt, and mTOR pathways enhances milk protein synthesis, where upstream factor S100A9 indirectly strengthens these pathways’ activity by regulating amino acid transporter genes, promoting protein accumulation. Further research confirms that activating PI3K-AKT/mTOR signaling enhances both milk fat and protein synthesis. Additionally, regulatory molecules like circRNA8220 synchronously increase β-casein and triglyceride levels in mammary cells by modulating these pathways62. Collectively, these findings establish the PI3K-AKT/mTOR pathway as essential for regulating mammary cell proliferation and nutrient biosynthesis. As intracellular nutrient-sensing hubs, mTOR and PI3K-AKT pathways exert multifaceted regulatory roles in substrate absorption, transport, and metabolism. On one hand, they upregulate amino acid transporters, enhancing mammary cells’ uptake of RP-AA to supply substrates for protein synthesis61,63. On the other hand, the PI3K-AKT pathway promotes membrane localization and expression of glucose transporters (GLUT1/4), improving glucose uptake efficiency64,65. Moreover, mTOR activation regulates key enzymes like fatty acid synthase and enhances fatty acid uptake/synthesis, providing carbon sources for lipid deposition. These mechanisms collectively enable mammary cells to dynamically adjust substrate absorption and metabolic flux in response to nutritional inputs, ensuring efficient synthesis of milk fat and protein. As central substrates in the TCA cycle and amino acid metabolism, AKG and Glu not only participate in energy production and nitrogen metabolism but also serve as crossroads for multiple metabolic pathways. For instance, AKG links the TCA cycle with Glu transamination, while Glu acts as both a protein-building amino acid and a key substrate for nitrogen transport and redistribution66. Their high abundance and coordinated presence indicate that enhanced exogenous substrate supply and signaling regulate mammary cells to efficiently integrate carbon-nitrogen metabolism and boost milk component accumulation. Critically, the synergistic network of signaling pathways and metabolic intermediates provides structural advantages: upon sensing nutritional status, mTOR and PI3K-AKT pathways not only activate transporters but also directly regulate TCA cycle and amino acid metabolic enzymes, enhancing intracellular production and turnover of AKG and Glu67. Specifically, these pathways upregulate glutamine synthase and Glu dehydrogenase, enhancing substrate flux in the TCA cycle and improving carbon-nitrogen metabolic efficiency68. As a key node in protein synthesis and branched-chain amino acid metabolism, Glu also acts as an amino donor in signal transduction, accelerating milk protein accumulation by balancing amino acid availability and synthesis rates. Through signaling-mediated nutrient sensing, enhanced substrate flux, and metabolic network activation, mammary cells sustain high-level milk component biosynthesis. Therefore, the consistent changes in milk proteomic and metabolomic profiles indicate that key signaling pathways and metabolic hubs may be coordinately regulated in the mammary gland, potentially enhancing its capacity for nutrient sensing, uptake, and utilization, thereby establishing the molecular basis for the synthesis and accrual of milk constituents. This study demonstrates that signaling molecules such as GNB1, GNG5, GNG10, and GRB2 synergize with metabolic hubs like AKG and Glu through interconnected networks: upstream signaling amplifies the perception of critical substrates and accelerates signal transduction, while downstream metabolites enhance energy and nitrogen source conversion efficiency. This bidirectional interplay forms the molecular basis for efficient nutrient utilization in the mammary gland.

It should be clearly noted that all omics analyses in this study were performed based on milk samples, which are products secreted by the mammary gland. Although the vast majority of proteins and metabolites in milk are directly synthesized, processed, and secreted by mammary epithelial cells, and their coordinated changes can provide strong indirect evidence for changes in mammary gland metabolism, they cannot directly reflect the metabolic status of the mammary gland tissue itself. Therefore, future studies can adopt in vitro experiments to directly verify the activation of signaling pathways and the regulatory patterns of metabolic processes in mammary gland tissue.

In summary, under LPD conditions, both amino acid balancing and MP (Spirulina) supplementation effectively enhanced dairy cow production performance and improved milk quality, which is likely associated with enhanced mammary gland nutrient sensing and efficient substrate utilization, as evidenced by the coordinated changes in milk proteomic and metabolomic profiles. At the mechanistic level, integrated omics analyses revealed that amino acid balancing and Spirulina protein supplementation were associated with the upregulation of PPAR pathway-related proteins and ACS gene family members in milk, which may contribute to enhanced fatty acid metabolism and improved milk fat synthesis efficiency in the mammary gland. Concurrently, the activation of Wnt, mTOR, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways played critical roles in regulating mammary cell proliferation and nutrient biosynthesis. The synergistic operation of these processes provided robust support for the synthesis and accumulation of milk components (Fig. 7). Furthermore, MP (Spirulina) supplementation yielded superior effects compared to amino acid balancing alone, demonstrating its significant potential and application value in advancing LPD strategies. From the perspective of food science, this study clarifies the effects and molecular mechanisms of different nutritional regulation strategies on raw milk food quality under LPD conditions. The identified key molecular markers and regulatory networks provide important targets for the precise regulation of milk quality. These findings not only promote the green transformation of dairy farming but also provide a theoretical basis for the production of high-quality raw milk and the development of functional dairy products, which has important application value for the dairy industry.

Methods

Ethics statement

All operational procedures in this study were strictly conducted in compliance with the approvals granted by the Committee of the Ministry of Agriculture of China (Beijing, China) and the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Ningxia Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (NKYLLSC-2026032).

Experimental farms and animal selection

From an initial pool of 600 healthy mid-lactation Holstein cows, 75 individuals with similar body condition scores were selected based on body weight (635.34 ± 54.16 kg), parity (2.56 ± 0.14), days in milk (92.35 ± 5.05 d), and milk yield (37.75 ± 2.12 kg). Using a completely randomized block design, they were randomly allocated to three groups: a control group fed a normal-protein diet (NP group, CP = 17.80%), and two treatment groups fed a LPD (LPD, CP = 16.00%): the amino acid-balanced group (LP group, LPD + RP-Lys + RP-Met) and the microalgal proteins-supplemented group (MP group, LPD + MP), with 25 cows in each group. The LP group was supplemented with 20 g/head/day RP-Lys and 30 g/head/day RP-Met (KDQ Technology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China). The RP-Lys product contained 39% effective lysine with a rumen-bypass efficiency of 72%, and the RP-Met product contained 44% effective methionine with a rumen-bypass efficiency of 84%, as verified by the manufacturer and confirmed in our previous in situ experiments. These supplementation levels were calculated based on the NRC (2001) model to achieve the recommended metabolizable protein amino acid profile for mid-lactation dairy cows (Lys accounting for 6.8% of metabolizable protein and methionine accounting for 2.4% of metabolizable protein); For the MP group, the LPD was supplemented with Spirulina (Guangyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) at a level of 5% of dietary dry matter (DM), substituting an equivalent portion of protein sources in the basal diet18. Supplementary table 1 provides the ingredients and chemical composition of the experimental diets, and Supplementary table 2 provides the main nutritional composition of the Spirulina. The dietary mixing process followed a standardized protocol: RP-AA and Spirulina were first homogenized with a premix, then mixed thoroughly with concentrate feed, and finally incorporated into the total mixed ration (TMR) using a TMR mixer. To maintain clear separation between treatment groups, a 160 m long pen (with a capacity of 100 cows) was partitioned into three equal-length segments (each 53–54 m). Manual movable steel pipes were installed between adjacent segments to prevent inter-group mixing. Each cow was allocated an individual feed trough (90 cm × 90 cm × 90 cm in size) to facilitate the measurement of daily feed intake and leftover amounts (The feed trough is equipped with an automatic feeding door system (American Calan Inc., Northwood, NH), and correspondingly, each cow wears a collar-type sensor). The feed refusal rate was monitored and maintained below 10% to minimize feed waste and ensure consistent nutrient intake. The experimental duration was ten weeks, including a two weeks pre-feeding adaptation period followed by an eight-week feeding trial. Cows were provided with continuous access to fresh drinking water. Throughout the experimental period, all cows were subjected to a consistent daily schedule: feeding at 07:00, 14:00, and 22:30 h, and milking at 06:00, 13:00, and 22:00 h.

Feed sample collection and analysis

Throughout the experiment, the daily intake of TMR was precisely recorded for each cow using individual feed bins. Fresh TMR samples were collected weekly at fixed times using a multi-point sampling method from newly filled feed bunks. After thorough mixing, a final composite sample of approximately 250 g was obtained via orthogonal sampling. Concurrently, samples of the silage and concentrate used to prepare the TMR were also collected. All feed samples were temporarily stored at −20 °C and subsequently oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h to determine DM content. The dried TMR samples were ground through a 1 mm screen using a Cyclotec 1093 mill (Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden) for subsequent analysis. To ascertain the absolute DM content, feed samples were further dried at 10 5°C for 4 h. The composition of the TMR was analyzed using standard methods: DM, crude protein (CP; calculated as Kjeldahl nitrogen × 6.25), ether extract (EE), calcium (Ca), and phosphorus (P) were determined according to AOAC69 methods 930.15, 981.10, 920.39, 927.02, and 965.17, respectively69. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was analyzed using heat-stable α-amylase and sodium sulfite according to Van Soest et al. 70 and acid detergent fiber (ADF) was determined by AOAC69 method 973.18. Both NDF and ADF were expressed on an ash-inclusive basis71. Starch content was measured using a total starch assay kit (K-TSTA, Megazyme Ltd., Bray, Ireland) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Milk samples collected

Milk yield for each cow was automatically recorded using a calibrated milking device connected to a rotary teat cup system (ALPROTM by DeLaval©, Tumba, Sweden). The total daily milk production was calculated by summing the milk volume obtained from three milking sessions conducted throughout the day. By the conclusion of the 4th week during the formal feeding phase, a total of 50 mL milk samples were collected following the proportion of 4:3:3 (morning: noon: evening). Potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was incorporated as a preservative, and the samples were stored at −4 °C before being promptly sent to the laboratory for milk composition analysis and somatic cell counting. At the end of the 8th week, alongside the milk samples collected for composition analysis, an extra 50 mL milk aliquot was obtained at the same ratio (4:3:3, morning: noon: evening). After rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen, the sample was preserved at −80 °C to facilitate subsequent sequencing procedures.

Detection of milk components

Milk composition analyses were conducted using the MilkoScan FT120 (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark) to determine the contents of milk Lactose, milk fat, milk protein, urea nitrogen, and total solids. The procedure involved first powering on and preheating the FOSS MilkoScan™ device (FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark). Concurrently, refrigerated milk samples were heated to 20°C in a water bath. Following the selection of the appropriate detection mode on the milk analyzer, the preheated samples were placed into the sample inlet, allowing the instrument to draw the sample automatically. Each sample was analyzed with a minimum of three technical replicates to ensure accuracy. Additionally, somatic cell counts in the milk samples were measured using the LACTOSCAN SCC (Milkotronic, Bulgaria). The process involved mixing milk samples with a SOFIA stain, then pipetting 10 μL onto the detection plate. The fluorescence-based detection, coupled with photographic counting, was used to quantify somatic cells, with each sample tested in at least ten replicates.

Proteomic mass spectrometry and protein identification

Prior to sequencing, milk samples underwent a series of preparatory steps, including protein extraction, denaturation, reduction, alkylation, enzymatic digestion, and desalting of peptide fragments. The labeled peptides were then marked using TMT reagents. Following labeling, samples were separated via reversed-phase chromatography, lyophilized, and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis using an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with a Thermo Fisher Scientific EASY-nLC 1200 system (MA, USA). Data-independent acquisition was performed in diaPASEF mode, and the resulting DIA data were analyzed using Spectronaut 18 with default parameters (BGS factory settings). Raw proteomic data were further processed through Spectronaut X (Biognosys AG) for database searching against the UniProt database, including a contaminant database to exclude false-positive identifications. Annotated data were subsequently uploaded to the Omicshare platform (https://www.omicshare.com) for comprehensive analysis. In the identification of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), LFQ intensities and P values were used to calculate fold changes (FC), with criteria set at |FC | > 1.2 and P < 0.05 results visualized via volcano plots. Hierarchical clustering analyses were performed using R (v 4.3.1), and the outputs were displayed as heatmaps. Protein annotations were primarily retrieved from UniProt, KEGG, and GO databases. DEPs were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on Omicshare to elucidate their biological functions.

Proteome enrichment analysis and interaction network analysis

To investigate the interactions among DEPs, the identified proteins were inputted into the STRING database (http://string-db.org), with the species set to “Bos taurus”. The resulting protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape (v 3.10.1). Additionally, to elucidate the complex relationships within this network, a detailed topology analysis of the entire protein interaction network was performed.

Metabolomics LC-MS analysis

Prior to metabolomic sequencing, milk samples were thawed at 4 °C. An appropriate aliquot was then mixed with precooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:1:1, v/v), vortexed, subjected to low-temperature ultrasound, and incubated before centrifugation at 14,000 × g at 4°C for 20 min. The supernatant was vacuum-dried, and prior to mass spectrometry, residues were reconstituted in 100 μL of acetonitrile-water (1:1, v/v), vortex-mixed, and centrifuged again at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The clear supernatant was used for analysis, with six biological replicates per group. The sequencing was commissioned to Guangzhou Jidi Ao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., using an AB TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer to acquire primary and secondary spectra, coupled with an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC UHPLC system and HILIC chromatography. To enhance molecular feature detection, we integrated public databases such as MassBank, Metlin, and MoNA, along with a self-constructed secondary spectral database for metabolite annotation.

Raw data were converted into the “.MzML” format using ProteoWizard. Peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed with the XCMS software. Data quality was assessed, metabolites with more than 50% missing values within groups were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using KNN methods. Extreme values were removed, and the data were normalized based on total peak area for comparability across samples and metabolites. Subsequent multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA, were conducted in R.

Metabolites with VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and FC > 2 or <0.5 were considered significant differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs); FC > 2 indicating upregulation and FC < 0.5 indicating downregulation, with results visualized in volcano plots. Further, hierarchical clustering of significant metabolites and samples was performed using the ComplexHeatmap package (v 1.2.1) in R, with results displayed as heatmaps. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses for these DEMs were carried out using the Omicshare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/), with bubble plots illustrating pathway distributions. By integrating KEGG pathway insights and metabolite profiles, we comprehensively assessed the impact of nutritional treatments on milk metabolic composition.

Integration of proteomics and metabolomics data

The DEPs, metabolites, and significantly enriched KEGG pathways identified from the proteomic and metabolomic sequencing analyses were subjected to correlation analysis. These associations were visualized using Cytoscape (v 3.10.1), providing an integrated network view of their potential interactions and functional relationships.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationships between DEPs, DEMs, and key milk quality indicators. Spearman rank correlation coefficients (r) and corresponding P values were calculated between all pairs of variables using the cor.test function in R software (v 4.3.1). To control for false discovery rate (FDR) resulting from multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Correlation pairs with an absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.6 and an adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. These thresholds were selected based on previous multi-omics studies in dairy science to ensure both biological relevance and statistical confidence.

Validation of proteomic sequencing results with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Total protein was extracted from the same milk samples used for proteomic sequencing using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland, Cat# 04693132001). Samples were incubated on ice for 30 min with gentle vortexing every 5 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The upper lipid layer was carefully removed, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 23227). All samples were diluted to the optimal concentration range of each ELISA kit with the provided sample diluent. The ELISA experiments were performed strictly following the manufacturers’ protocols (Relevant assay kits were custom-ordered from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Jiangsu, China)). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and a standard curve was included on each plate. All standard curves had a correlation coefficient (R2) > 0.98. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for all samples was < 6%, and the inter-assay CV was <9%, which meets the international standards for ELISA experiments. The protein concentrations of the samples were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves. The correlation between ELISA results and proteomic sequencing data was analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis.

Statistical analysis of data

Nutritional and milk compositional data were initially processed using Microsoft Office Excel 2020. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For repeated measures including dry matter intake and milk yield, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used with treatment as the between-subject factor and week as the within-subject factor. For milk composition, proteomics, and metabolomics data at a single time point, one-way ANOVA was applied. Normality and homogeneity of variance were verified before analysis; non-normal data were log-transformed. When a significant treatment effect was detected, Tukey’s HSD test was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. For proteomic and metabolomic data with multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. In this study, data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference between the two sets of results and is marked with an “*” in the inset; P < 0.01 was considered a highly significant difference between the two sets of results and is marked with an “**” in the inset.
