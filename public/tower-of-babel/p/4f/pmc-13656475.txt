Bilibili and Douyin (Chinese TikTok) videos as sources of health information about ventricular septal defect: a cross-sectional content and quality analysis of top-ranked patient-facing videos
Authors: Xuping Zhang, Yimamujiang Aximu, Peipei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid growth of short-form social media and video-sharing platforms has greatly expanded public access to health information; however, the characteristics and quality of videos concerning ventricular septal defect (VSD) have not yet been systematically evaluated. Although VSD is a well-established congenital heart disease, patients and caregivers may still rely on social media to understand diagnosis, prognosis, follow-up, and treatment options. In this study, the intended audience was defined as patient-facing general users, particularly patients, parents, and caregivers who may have encountered the clinical diagnosis term “ventricular septal defect” in medical consultations, ultrasound reports, or discharge summaries and subsequently searched for related explanations online. Therefore, evaluating the quality of highly visible VSD-related videos is relevant to patient education and digital health communication. This study aimed to assess the quality and reliability of short-form videos related to VSD on the Douyin (Chinese TikTok) and Bilibili platforms.MethodsThe Chinese keyword “室间隔缺损” was used to retrieve relevant videos from Douyin and Bilibili. This clinical term was selected because the study focused on users who search after exposure to a formal diagnosis; colloquial expressions such as “heart hole” were not included because they may retrieve heterogeneous videos on congenital heart disease in general rather than VSD-specific content. This choice is acknowledged as a limitation.To capture the content most likely to be encountered by general users, the first 100 videos returned by each platform’s default search order were screened, yielding 129 videos for final analysis. The Global Quality Score was defined as the primary outcome because it provides an overall assessment of educational quality and usefulness for patients. The Video Information and Quality Index, Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool, JAMA Benchmark criteria, and modified DISCERN were used as secondary complementary measures assessing audiovisual presentation, understandability/actionability, transparency, and reliability, respectively.Video eligibility was assessed independently by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Because reviewer-level screening decisions were not retained, Cohen’s κ for eligibility screening could not be calculated.ResultsWithin the sampled top-ranked videos, Douyin videos demonstrated significantly higher overall quality than those on Bilibili, particularly with respect to mDISCERN, GQS, and VIQI scores. Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between video quality and audience engagement on Bilibili (p > 0.05). In contrast, on Douyin, PEMAT and VIQI scores showed moderate positive correlations with likes, collections, and shares (r = 0.48–0.64, p < 0.001). Across the whole sample, engagement indicators such as likes, collections, comments, and shares were highly correlated with one another, suggesting that these variables mainly reflected a shared popularity construct rather than independent dimensions of educational quality. However, these findings should be interpreted as platform- and sample-specific observations rather than evidence of inherent superiority of one platform over another.ConclusionsAmong the top-ranked Chinese-language VSD-related videos included in this cross-sectional analysis, Douyin showed higher scores on several quality and reliability measures than Bilibili. Nevertheless, the overall quality of videos on both platforms remained low to moderate, and high-quality, transparent, evidence-based videos were not consistently available. These findings suggest that clinicians, educators, professional organizations, and platforms should collaborate to improve the reliability, transparency, and patient-centeredness of short-form VSD educational videos.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04509-8.

Introduction

Ventricular septal defect (VSD) is one of the most prevalent congenital heart diseases, accounting for approximately 20% of all cases [1]. It is characterized by an abnormal opening in the interventricular septum that permits blood to flow from the left ventricle to the right ventricle [2]. Depending on its size and location, VSD may result in left-to-right shunting, pulmonary hypertension, cardiac volume overload, and eventually congestive heart failure if untreated.

Although VSD is clinically well defined, its diagnostic and therapeutic principles are well established, patients and caregivers frequently face practical questions regarding spontaneous closure, follow-up intervals, indications for intervention, prognosis, exercise, pregnancy, and daily-life precautions. These questions are often raised outside formal clinical encounters, and many individuals increasingly seek explanations from short-form social media videos. This is particularly relevant in the Chinese context, where parents of children with congenital heart disease have been reported to seek health information from both online and offline sources and to combine information obtained from professionals and peers. Therefore, the relevance of this study lies not in redefining the clinical management of VSD, but in evaluating whether the information most visible to the public is reliable, understandable, transparent, and useful for patient education.

The rationale for focusing on Chinese-language platforms is threefold. First, the study was conducted in mainland China, where Chinese-language short-form video platforms are widely used for health-information seeking. Second, national internet data indicate that short-video use is highly prevalent in China, and internet-based health services are increasingly used by the public. Third, YouTube and Instagram are not directly accessible in mainland China, making systematic evaluation of these platforms infeasible in this research setting.

Douyin and Bilibili are better characterized as short-form social media or video-sharing platforms rather than “educational platforms.” In this manuscript, we therefore avoid describing them as educational platforms. Instead, we define them as social media platforms that may function as informal sources of patient-facing health information. Douyin and Bilibili were launched in 2016 and 2009, respectively [3, 4]. Bilibili reported 117.3 million average daily active users and 376 million monthly active users in the third quarter of 2025. Comparable publicly disclosed official user metrics for Douyin are limited, and therefore we did not calculate a direct market-share comparison between Douyin and Bilibili. However, the credibility of videos on both platforms has not been systematically evaluated, leading to the dissemination of inaccurate or unprofessional information in a subset of videos [5–7]. For congenital heart diseases such as VSD, incomplete or poorly contextualized online information may increase anxiety, create unrealistic expectations, or lead to misunderstandings of follow-up and treatment decisions.

Previous studies have assessed the reliability and quality of short videos or online videos on various medical topics, including inflammatory bowel disease, thyroid nodules, coronary heart disease, liver cancer, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, hypertrophic scarring, and other health conditions. These studies generally suggest that short-form video platforms can improve access to health information, but that popularity and visibility do not necessarily correspond to reliability, transparency, or clinical usefulness. However, to our knowledge, no previous study has specifically evaluated VSD-related Chinese-language videos on Douyin and Bilibili.

Accordingly, this study aimed to conduct a cross-sectional content and quality analysis of the top-ranked VSD-related videos on Douyin and Bilibili. The primary objective was to evaluate overall educational quality using the Global Quality Score. Secondary objectives were to assess reliability, transparency, understandability, actionability, audiovisual presentation, and the relationship between quality indicators and audience engagement. We further aimed to identify practical implications for clinicians, educators, and platform governance in improving public-facing VSD education.

Method

Ethical considerations

This study involved the analysis of publicly available short-video data from Douyin and Bilibili, which are accessible to the general public. No personal identifying information was collected, and no interaction with individuals occurred; all privacy concerning participants has been preserved. According to the standards proposed by Eysenbach and Till [8], researchers may conduct research in public places or use publicly available information about individuals without obtaining consent. According to the Declaration of Helsinki, this type of research, which uses open-source, publicly available data, does not require ethical approval. Therefore, ethical review was not deemed necessary.

Eligibility criteria and information sources

Videos included in the study must be relevant to ventricular septal defect. A video was considered relevant if its primary content addressed ventricular septal defect, including its definition, etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, follow-up, or patient experience. Videos were classified as irrelevant if they appeared in the search results but did not substantially discuss VSD, mentioned VSD only briefly without meaningful educational content, focused mainly on other cardiac or non-cardiac conditions, or were unrelated to medical or health information.

Platform verification status was recorded as a descriptive variable for uploader classification, but it was not used as an eligibility criterion for selecting videos. On Bilibili, both individual/professional verification and institutional verification badges were recorded when visible; on Douyin, medical professional verification and institutional or enterprise verification were recorded when visible. Videos were not excluded solely because an uploader lacked verification.

The content was sourced from two prominent Chinese short-form video platforms: Douyin and Bilibili were selected because they are widely used Chinese-language video-sharing platforms and represent two distinct social video environments encountered by Chinese-speaking users.These platforms were selected because they represent widely used Chinese-language social video environments rather than because they are formal educational platforms.

Search strategy

A search was conducted on Douyin and Bilibili using the keyword “室间隔缺损” (the Chinese term for ventricular septal defect) on November 11, 2025. The English term is “ventricular septal defect.” Because users typically engage first with the highest-ranked search results, and because these platforms provide a large and dynamically changing body of content, we limited screening to the first 100 videos returned by the default search interface on each platform. This approach was designed to simulate the information environment most visible to ordinary users at the time of retrieval. It should therefore be understood as a visibility-based cross-sectional sampling strategy rather than a random sample or a complete census of all VSD-related videos on the platforms.

The search term was selected to target patients, parents, and caregivers who had already encountered the clinical disease name through a medical consultation, echocardiography report, a discharge summary, or other clinical documents. We did not include broader colloquial expressions such as “心脏有洞” (“a hole in the heart”) or broader disease labels such as “先天性心脏病” (“congenital heart disease”), because these terms may retrieve a large number of videos addressing atrial septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, cyanotic congenital heart disease, or congenital heart disease in general rather than VSD-specific information. Nevertheless, this search strategy may have missed videos designed for users unfamiliar with the formal disease name, and this limitation is acknowledged.

The order in which videos appeared was determined by each platform’s proprietary search-ranking algorithm. Although the exact weighting of ranking factors is not publicly disclosed, the returned order likely reflected a combination of keyword relevance, user engagement, recency, and other platform-specific recommendation factors. Duplicate videos, irrelevant videos, and videos published within seven days were excluded. Videos published within seven days were excluded to reduce instability in early engagement metrics, such as likes, comments, collections, and shares, which may change rapidly immediately after upload. Irrelevant videos were defined as those returned by the search algorithm that did not primarily concern VSD; these included videos focused on other diseases, videos in which VSD was mentioned only in passing, and videos lacking substantive disease-related educational information.

After applying the exclusion criteria, the remaining videos were independently screened and evaluated by two reviewers. Disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer. Because the original reviewer-level inclusion/exclusion decisions for all screened videos were not retained in the analytic dataset, Cohen’s κ for screening agreement could not be calculated. For quality assessment, the scoring instruments were applied according to predefined criteria. The final scores were interpreted as structured evaluations of educational quality rather than definitive judgments of clinical accuracy.

Video characteristics

In the content analysis, the number of views, days since upload, comments, likes, collections, shares, video length, source of upload, and style of video shooting were extracted. “Days since upload” was calculated as the number of days between the upload date and the search date, whereas “video length” referred to the duration of the video in seconds. The source of upload was categorized into professional individuals, medical examination training institutions, professional institutions, non-professional individuals, and non-professional institutions. The style of video shooting was categorized into Animation/Action, Medical Scenarios, slide-based lecture/classroom presentation, Question & Answer, and Solo Narration. Video content also varied, including disease introductions, medical examinations, surgical procedures, prognostic discussions, and patient self-reports.

Assessment of content and quality of videos

Two aspects of ventricular septal defect videos from Douyin and Bilibili were assessed: content and the quality of the information. To facilitate interpretation for readers unfamiliar with these instruments, brief definitions, principal domains, and scoring ranges of all five metrics are summarized in Appendix Table S1.

The Global Quality Score was predefined as the primary outcome measure because it provides an overall evaluation of educational quality, comprehensiveness, flow, and usefulness for patients. The remaining four instruments were used as secondary complementary measures rather than independent primary endpoints. Specifically, mDISCERN was used to assess reliability, balance, and reference to uncertainty; JAMA Benchmark criteria were used to evaluate transparency, authorship, attribution, currency, and disclosure; PEMAT was used to evaluate understandability and actionability; and VIQI was used to assess audiovisual presentation, information flow, accuracy, and consistency between title and content. Thus, the five instruments were intended to capture related but distinct dimensions of patient-facing video quality.

The Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) was used to evaluate video reliability [9, 10], which included four criteria: (1) providing authorship; (2) listing copyright information and references, as well as sources of content; (3) providing initial date and subsequent updates; and (4) disclosing conflicts of interest, funding, sponsorship, advertising support, and video ownership. The total score is 4 points, with each criterion receiving 1 point [11]. In addition, the Global Quality Scale (GQS) was used to assess educational content quality [12–15], focusing on the quality, comprehensiveness of the listed information, and its usefulness for patients. Scores on the scale ranged from 1 (poor quality) to 5 (excellent flow and quality) [16]. A modified DISCERN score (Quality Criteria for Consumer Health Information) was used to analyze the reliability and quality of the videos [17, 18], consisting of five questions, with 1 point awarded for every “Yes” and 0 points for every “No.” Specifically, five criteria were used: (1) the video was clear, concise, and understandable; (2) the sources of information used were reliable; (3) the presented information was balanced and unbiased; (4) additional sources of information were listed for patient reference; and (5) areas of uncertainty/controversy were mentioned [19]. The PEMAT [20] consists of 25 questions, with 21 assessing the understandability of health information and 4 evaluating the actionability of recommendations in videos. Each item is scored as “agree = 1, disagree = 0, N/A.” The total score (PEMAT-T) and the scores for understandability (PEMAT-U) and actionability (PEMAT-A) are calculated as “Total Points/Total Possible Points × 100.” Higher scores indicate better performance. The VIQI tool [21] encompasses four dimensions: information flow, information accuracy, quality (with one point for each image, animation, interview, video caption, and summary), and precision (the level of coherence between video title and content). Thus, The five instruments were intended to capture related but distinct dimensions of patient-facing video quality.

All included videos were scored independently by two reviewers. Before formal scoring, the reviewers discussed the scoring criteria and performed pilot scoring on a small subset of videos to ensure consistent interpretation of the instruments. For each video, the final score used in analysis was the mean of the two independent reviewer scores. Major discrepancies were discussed to ensure consistent interpretation of the scoring criteria, and unresolved cases were adjudicated by a third reviewer. Inter-rater reliability for continuous or ordinal quality scores was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients, and the results are reported in the Results section.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 20 was used to analyze the data. In this study, the independent t-test was employed to compare quantitative variables with a normal distribution. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two groups of quantitative data that did not follow a normal distribution. Additionally, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare multiple sets of data with non-normally distributed quantitative variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The adjusted p-value, using the Bonferroni method, was employed for post-hoc binary comparisons to evaluate the significance among multiple sets of data. The strength of the correlation was classified as follows: |r| ≤ 0.2 represented no relationship; 0.2 < |r| ≤ 0.4 implied a weak relationship; 0.4 < |r| ≤ 0.6 indicated a moderate relationship; 0.6 <|r|≤ 0.8 suggested a strong relationship; and |r|> 0.8 denoted a very strong relationship. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because reviewer-level inclusion/exclusion decisions were not retained, Cohen’s κ for video screening could not be calculated. Inter-rater reliability was assessed only for the GQS using intraclass correlation coefficients, as reviewer-level raw scores were available only for this scale. Correlation analyses were interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal influence.

Results

Video characteristics

A total of 200 top-ranked videos (100 from Douyin and 100 from Bilibili) were screened. Among the excluded irrelevant videos were those unrelated to VSD, those mainly addressing other cardiovascular or non-cardiovascular topics, and those containing only passing mention of VSD without substantial educational content. For Douyin, 20 irrelevant videos were removed; for Bilibili, 4 duplicates, 44 irrelevant videos, and 3 videos published within seven days were removed. As a result, 129 eligible videos were included for further analysis (Fig. 1). Thus, the final analytic sample represented the top-ranked and most visible search results at the time of data collection, rather than a complete census of all VSD-related videos on either platform.

The analyzed videos were published between June 6, 2020, and October 10, 2025. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of content providers for VSD-related videos on Bilibili and Douyin. On Bilibili, videos were uploaded by various sources, including professional individuals (59.18%), medical training institutions (14.29%), professional institutions (12.24%), non-professional individuals (8.16%), and non-professional institutions (6.12%). In contrast, Douyin was predominantly populated by professional individuals, who contributed 95.00% of all videos, with non-professional individuals and professional institutions contributing only 3.75% and 1.25%, respectively. These findings suggest that Bilibili features a more diverse mix of institutional and individual contributors, whereas Douyin content is overwhelmingly created by individual professionals.

Because the original reviewer-level inclusion/exclusion decisions for all screened videos were not retained, Cohen’s κ for video inclusion could not be calculated. Reviewer-level raw scores were available only for GQS. For GQS, the exact agreement between the two reviewers was 59.7% (77/129). The two-way random-effects absolute-agreement single-measure ICC was 0.495, and the average-measure ICC was 0.662. The single-measure ICC indicated borderline poor-to-moderate reliability, whereas the average-measure ICC indicated moderate reliability. Because reviewer-level raw scores were not available for mDISCERN, VIQI, PEMAT, or JAMA benchmark criteria, ICCs for these measures were not calculated.

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of 129 videos related to ventricular septal defects (VSD) on Bilibili (n = 49) and Douyin (n = 80). Significant differences were observed between the two platforms in terms of video engagement, days since upload, quality scores, and content characteristics. Douyin videos received significantly more shares (median 248.5 vs. 10.5, p = 0.002) and comments (median 188.5 vs. 1, p = 0.003) than Bilibili videos, although differences in likes and collections were not statistically significant. The median days since upload since upload was longer for Bilibili videos (840 vs. 412.5 days, p < 0.001), and the video length was significantly greater (242 s vs. 66.5 s, p < 0.001). In terms of quality evaluation, Douyin videos exhibited higher scores across most metrics, including the mDISCERN score (3.175 ± 0.725 vs. 2.796 ± 0.889, p = 0.009), Global Quality Score (3.237 ± 0.484 vs. 2.857 ± 0.540, p < 0.001), Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI, 15.000 ± 1.750 vs. 13.694 ± 1.735, p < 0.001), and JAMA benchmark score (1.950 ± 0.219 vs. 1.755 ± 0.434, p = 0.001). No significant difference was observed in PEMAT scores (p = 0.570). Regarding video styles, Bilibili predominantly featured educational and academic formats such as slide-based lecture/classroom presentations (48.98%) and animation-based explanations (16.33%), whereas Douyin mainly employed solo narration (51.25%) and question-and-answer (Q&A) formats (37.50%), focusing on concise and interactive delivery (p < 0.001). Video content also varied significantly between the two platforms (p < 0.001). Bilibili mainly focused on disease introductions (40.82%) and medical examinations (16.33%), while Douyin featured more surgical procedures (21.25%), prognostic discussions (11.25%), and patient self-reports (3.75%), reflecting a greater emphasis on practical and experiential information.

The distribution of quality scores for Douyin and Bilibili videos was examined in greater detail using kernel density plots (Fig. 3), which provide insight into the variability of video quality across the two platforms. It presents kernel density plots of quality scores for VSD-related videos on Bilibili and Douyin. For PEMAT scores, the distributions for the two platforms largely overlapped, indicating similar levels of understandability and actionability. In contrast, Douyin videos demonstrated a right-shifted distribution for GQS, VIQI, mDISCERN, and JAMA benchmark scores, with a higher proportion of videos in the upper range of scores. These patterns suggest that, while the comprehensibility of videos was similar between platforms, Douyin contained videos with generally higher overall quality, informational quality, reliability, and transparency than Bilibili. However, given that the average scores remained in the low-to-moderate range rather than at the top end of the scales, truly excellent videos appeared to be uncommon overall, even on Douyin.

On Bilibili, no significant correlations were observed between video quality scores (PEMAT, VIQI, GQS, mDISCERN) and engagement indicators (likes, collections, shares, and comments; all p > 0.05). These results indicate that, in this sample, engagement metrics on Bilibili were not significantly associated with the measured video quality scores. In contrast, Douyin showed multiple significant positive correlations between video quality scores and engagement metrics. These findings suggest that, in the Douyin sample, videos with better understandability, actionability, and audiovisual information quality tended to receive higher engagement. However, because this was a cross-sectional correlation analysis, the results do not establish that quality caused greater public interaction.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study systematically reviewed VSD-related videos on two of the most popular short-form video platforms in China, Douyin and Bilibili. The purpose of this study was not to advance clinical knowledge of VSD itself, which is already a well-defined congenital heart disease, but to evaluate the quality of patient-facing information that general users are likely to encounter when searching for VSD-related content.

Overall, the quality of VSD-related short videos on both platforms was generally unsatisfactory or only moderate. Within the visibility-based sample analyzed in this study. Douyin videos demonstrated significantly higher overall quality than those on Bilibili, especially in terms of mDISCERN, GQS, and VIQI scores. Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between video quality and audience engagement on Bilibili (p > 0.05). In contrast, on Douyin, PEMAT and VIQI scores showed moderate positive correlations with likes, collections, and shares (r = 0.48–0.64, p < 0.001).

A further key finding was that engagement metrics were not consistently aligned with quality scores. On Bilibili, engagement metrics were not significantly associated with video quality scores. On Douyin, understandability/actionability and audiovisual information quality were moderately associated with likes, collections, and shares. These results indicate association rather than causation and should not be interpreted as evidence that quality directly influenced user engagement.

Platform differences

The observed differences between Douyin and Bilibili should be interpreted cautiously. Douyin and Bilibili differ in user demographics, platform culture, content-production norms, video length, interaction style, and proprietary recommendation algorithms [22–24]. Therefore, the observed differences do not establish that one platform is inherently superior to the other. Rather, they indicate that, among the top-ranked Chinese-language VSD-related videos retrieved at the time of the search, the Douyin sample contained more videos with better structured, more visually engaging, and more reliable patient-facing educational features [25, 26].

Possible explanations include differences in creator composition and video format. In the present sample, Douyin content was predominantly produced by professional individuals and was often delivered through concise solo narration or question-and-answer formats. These formats may make complex topics such as spontaneous closure, surgical indications, and follow-up easier for patients and caregivers to understand [27, 28]. In contrast, Bilibili included a broader mix of uploaders and more slide-based or classroom-style presentations, which may provide longer explanations but not necessarily greater patient-centeredness or transparency. Similar patterns have been documented in studies on other medical topics—such as liver cancer, thyroid disease, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors [29–31]—where Douyin tended to outperform Bilibili in delivering concise and medically accurate content.

Because this study did not directly examine recommendation algorithms, user profiles, or platform-level exposure mechanisms, algorithmic effects should be interpreted only as possible explanations rather than demonstrated mechanisms.

Quality versus engagement

A notable finding is that popular videos were not necessarily reliable or educationally complete. Likes, comments, shares, and collections may reflect visibility, emotional resonance, presentation style, entertainment value, or the uploader’s popularity, rather than accuracy, balance, or clinical usefulness. This distinction is particularly important for VSD because oversimplified videos may lead patients or caregivers to misunderstand prognosis, spontaneous closure, the timing of intervention, or the need for individualized follow-up.

On Douyin, better understandability/actionability and audiovisual presentation were moderately associated with higher engagement. This suggests that viewers may be more likely to interact with videos that are easy to understand and visually well structured [32]. However, such associations do not prove causality. It is also possible that platform ranking, creator reputation, editing style, topic selection, or unmeasured confounders contributed to both higher quality scores and higher engagement.

User comments may represent a valuable but underexplored dimension of audience engagement. Beyond reflecting popularity, comments may provide insight into viewers’ understanding, unresolved concerns, requests for clarification, and potential misconceptions related to VSD. In the present study, comments were analyzed quantitatively as an engagement indicator rather than qualitatively as textual data. Future research should incorporate thematic or qualitative analysis of user comments to better identify common questions, informational needs, and possible misinformation in audience responses to VSD-related videos.

Practical implications for clinicians, educators, and platforms

The present findings have several practical implications. First, clinicians caring for patients with VSD should recognize that patients and caregivers may obtain information from short-form videos before or after clinical consultations [32]. Clinicians may therefore consider asking families what online information they have viewed, correcting misunderstandings, and directing them to reliable educational resources.

Second, medical educators, hospitals, and professional societies should consider producing short, visually clear, patient-centered VSD videos that address common concerns such as spontaneous closure, indications for follow-up, symptoms requiring medical attention, timing of intervention, prognosis, and daily-life precautions. To improve transparency and reliability, such videos should clearly identify the creator’s professional credentials, cite evidence-based sources where possible, disclose the date of production or update, and acknowledge areas where individualized clinical assessment is necessary.

Third, platforms could improve health-information governance in concrete ways. For VSD-related content, platforms could add optional or mandatory health-information labels encouraging creators to disclose professional credentials, cite clinical guidelines or authoritative sources, provide update dates, and include reminders that management decisions depend on individual echocardiographic and clinical findings. Platforms could also develop expert-reviewed health-content tags for common congenital heart disease topics and avoid ranking medical videos solely by engagement metrics. These measures would not require platforms to make clinical judgments for every video, but they could improve transparency and help users identify more trustworthy sources.

Future work

Future studies should combine quantitative scoring with qualitative content analysis. Specifically, future research should evaluate the accuracy of individual clinical claims, analyze user comments to identify common misconceptions and information needs, and assess whether exposure to short-form videos affects patient understanding, anxiety, treatment expectations, or healthcare-seeking behavior. Longitudinal studies are also needed because search results and engagement metrics change over time. Cross-platform and cross-language comparisons may further clarify how platform affordances, creator identity, and audience characteristics shape public exposure to congenital heart disease information.

Conclusion

In summary, professional individuals remain the primary contributors of high-quality medical content on both Douyin and Bilibili. Among the top-ranked Chinese-language videos included in this cross-sectional study, Douyin showed higher scores than Bilibili on several measures of overall quality, reliability, and audiovisual presentation. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Douyin is universally superior to Bilibili or that all Douyin videos provide high-quality medical education. The overall quality of VSD-related videos on both platforms remained low to moderate, and important elements such as source attribution, transparency, balance, and clinical context were often insufficient.

These results support the need for clinicians, medical educators, professional organizations, and platforms to collaborate in producing and promoting accurate, transparent, and patient-centered VSD educational videos. Improving the quality of short-form health information may help reduce misunderstanding, support informed communication between clinicians and families, and enhance public health literacy regarding congenital heart disease.

Limitations

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the sample was restricted to the first 100 videos returned by each platform’s default search interface. Therefore, the final dataset should be interpreted as representing the most visible VSD-related content at the time of retrieval rather than the full body of VSD-related videos available on Douyin and Bilibili. In addition, because both platforms use dynamic and proprietary ranking systems and do not provide a stable, transparent count of all searchable videos, the representativeness of the 129 included videos cannot be fully guaranteed. Thus, our results reflect a visibility-based cross-sectional snapshot rather than a random or exhaustive evaluation.

Second, the search strategy used only the formal Chinese clinical term “室间隔缺损.” This approach was appropriate for diagnosed patients and caregivers who may search using terminology from medical records or clinical consultations. However, it may have missed videos using colloquial expressions such as “心脏有洞” or broader labels such as “先天性心脏病.” Future studies should compare clinical and lay search terms to better capture the information environment encountered by users with different levels of disease awareness.

Moreover, Douyin and Bilibili differ in platform culture, user demographics, creator communities, video length, interaction mechanisms, and recommendation algorithms. These factors may confound direct comparisons between platforms, and the findings should not be generalized to other linguistic, cultural, or platform contexts.

Fourth, engagement metrics do not necessarily reflect viewers’ comprehension or behavioral change. In particular, although the number of comments was included as an engagement metric, the textual content of comments was not subjected to formal qualitative or thematic analysis. As a result, this study could not directly assess audience understanding, specific concerns, or the presence of misinformation within user discussions.

Fifth, although standardized instruments were used to assess reliability, transparency, understandability, and overall quality, the study did not include independent clinical fact-checking of every statement in each video. Therefore, we cannot definitively classify specific videos as medically inaccurate or harmful. Rather, low scores on instruments such as mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA should be interpreted as indicating a greater potential risk of incomplete, misleading, or insufficiently supported health information.

Sixth, although five instruments were used to provide a multidimensional assessment, some overlap exists among these tools. To address this issue, GQS was defined as the primary outcome in the revised manuscript, while mDISCERN, JAMA, PEMAT, and VIQI were interpreted as secondary complementary measures. Future research should further refine standardized assessment frameworks for short-form medical videos and incorporate interrater reliability, clinical fact-checking, qualitative comment analysis, and longitudinal follow-up.
