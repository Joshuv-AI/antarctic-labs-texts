iRGD-functionalized PLGA nanocomplex co-loaded with paclitaxel and Trametes robiniophila Murr for targeted inhibition of chemoresistant and metastatic signaling gene PDCD4 in colon cancer
Authors: Li Li, Ying Liu, Xiao Wei, Rui Li, Wang Shuan, Mei Yan, Qun Zhang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Colon cancer represents one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Current therapies like FOLFOX (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin) and FOLFIRI (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and irinotecan) give only temporary benefits in advanced colon cancer [2]. Relapses are common as cancer cells trigger epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), boost drug-efflux activity and strengthen survival signaling that supports resistance and metastasis [3]. Overcoming these adaptive programs remains a central challenge in the treatment of metastatic colon cancer.

Paclitaxel (PTX), a diterpenoid microtubule-stabilizing agent, shows broad antitumor activity by inducing mitotic arrest and apoptosis in rapidly proliferating cells [4]. However, its clinical use in colon cancer has been limited by poor solubility, non-specific distribution and dose-limiting toxicities caused by the Cremophor EL vehicle [5, 6]. These pharmacokinetic limitations highlight the need for advanced delivery systems capable of enhancing intratumoral drug concentration while reducing systemic toxicity. Biodegradable polymeric nanocarriers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) have emerged as versatile solutions which offer controlled release and biocompatibility [7]. Recent studies further support their efficacy in gastrointestinal malignancies demonstrating that PLGA-based nanoparticles can encapsulate hydrophobic compounds like PTX, extend circulation time and improve tumor-specific accumulation [8].

The tumor enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect is heterogeneous across patients, tumor types, and even within individual lesions, which limits uniform intratumoral drug exposure [9]. Active targeting strategies using tumor-homing peptides have shown promise in improving delivery specificity. Among these, iRGD (internalizing arginine–glycine–aspartic peptide; sequence CRGDKGPDC) has been widely validated for its dual-receptor mechanism [10]. The RGD motif binds αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins on tumor vasculature and subsequent proteolytic cleavage exposes a C-terminal CendR motif that engages neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) [11, 12]. Functionalizing PLGA nanoparticles with iRGD is therefore a rational route to increase receptor-mediated uptake and deeper tissue penetration, with the aim of raising local drug concentration while limiting systemic exposure [13].

Parallel to advances in nanocarrier engineering, renewed interest in natural-product–based adjuvants has identified several bioactive compounds capable of reversing chemoresistance [14]. Trametes robiniophila Murr (Huaier), a medicinal fungus long used in Chinese oncology practice, exhibits anti-proliferative, anti-migratory and pro-apoptotic effects across diverse malignancies [15, 16]. Studies show that Huaier extracts down-regulate PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling, suppress EMT and inhibit metastasis through modulation of TGF-β and β-catenin pathways [17]. Moreover, Huaier demonstrates favorable safety and has been clinically applied as an adjuvant to conventional chemotherapy [18]. These properties make it an attractive complementary agent for PTX that mitigate resistance and improve efficacy through distinct yet convergent mechanisms.

The integration of PTX and Huaier into a dual compound nanocomplex offers a mechanistically synergistic platform. Within such a construct, PTX exerts direct cytotoxic pressure on proliferating tumor cells and Huaier-derived molecules modulate signaling networks governing migration and drug resistance. Functionalization with iRGD further enhances tumor targeting by exploiting integrin/NRP-1 expression on colon-cancer vasculature [19]. Co-encapsulation in a PLGA matrix ensures synchronized drug release, improved pharmacokinetics and efficient cellular uptake [20].

In this study, publicly available colon-cancer transcriptomic datasets (GSE44076 and GSE253699) were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with metastatic and chemoresistant phenotypes. Intersecting these DEGs resulted in an 18-gene core signature linked to tumor progression. Subsequent network and survival analyses prioritized PDCD4 and CCND1 as potential therapeutic targets. A multifunctional PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was computationally designed by co-assembling paclitaxel with Trametes robiniophila Murr-derived bioactive metabolites within a PLGA scaffold functionalized with the tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD. Target prediction, docking and molecular-dynamics simulations were performed to characterize compound–protein interactions and assess structural stability and ADMET profiling evaluated pharmacokinetic and safety parameters. This approach combines transcriptomic prioritization with structure-based validation to identify PDCD4 as the preferential intracellular target of the designed nanocomplex in colon tumors.

Methodology

DEG analysis and target prioritization

Colon cancer transcriptomic datasets, GSE253699 (n = 6) and GSE44076 (n = 98) [21] were retrieved from NCBI GEO preprocessed using GEOquery and Biobase R packages. Differential expression was computed with limma with empirical Bayes moderation and thresholds of adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg) and |log₂ fold-change| > 1 [22]. Metastasis-associated genes were compiled from the [23] (286 genes). Chemoresistance-associated genes specific to colon cancer were extracted from [24] (49 genes). Significant DEGs from GSE44076 and GSE253699 were intersected with the curated metastasis and chemoresistance sets and were retained as candidate targets.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction

Functional annotation of the 18-gene set was done via STRING database with a minimum interaction confidence score set at 0.7 (high confidence) [25]. Biological Process terms were obtained through the BINGO plugin (Biological Networks Gene Ontology) in Cytoscape with a hypergeometric test (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR < 0.05) [26]. Enriched terms (GO BP and Reactome) from STRING were also visualized as a network in R using ggplot2 [27].

Survival analysis of candidate genes

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of the 18 overlapping genes identified, survival analysis was conducted using KMplotter plotter [28]. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied and only genes with FDR ≤ 0.01 were considered statistically significant [29].

Designing of PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex

The chemical structures of the active constituents of Trametes robiniophila Murr (TP-MUR) and paclitaxel (PTX) were obtained from the PubChem database in 2D SDF format [30]. The selected TP-derived bioactive molecules included adenosine (PubChem CID: 60961), ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol (PubChem CID: 125947), 3β-hydroxystigmast-5-en-7-one (PubChem CID: 91753723), Daucosterol (sitogluside) (PubChem CID: 5742590) and ergosterol (PubChem CID: 444679). The standard chemotherapeutic agent Paclitaxel (PTX) (PubChem CID: 36314) was used as the co-loaded cytotoxic compound. The targeting peptide iRGD (CRGDKGPDC) (PubChem CID: 163358733) and PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid) was constructed using polymer constituents’ lactic acid (PubChem CID: 612) and glycolic acid (PubChem CID: 757). All compounds were downloaded as SDF files from PubChem [31] and visualized in UCSF Chimera (v1.17.3) for spatial orientation and energy minimization [32]. The finalized 3D construct of the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD system was assembled in UCSF Chimera.

Targets predictions

For target prediction, PDB structure of finalized PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD was converted into SMILES (O1[C@@H](n2c3ncnc(N)c3nc2)[C@H](O)[C@H](O)[C@H]1CO) and was submitted to SwissTargetPrediction (species: Homo sapiens) [33]. Top 100 predicted targets with probability scores were exported. The merged SwissTargetPrediction list was intersected with the 10 genes and two of the colon cancer DEGs were shortlisted as the potential targets of PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD. These verified targets were CDK4–Cyclin D1 complex (ChEMBL1907601) and Programmed cell death protein 4, PDCD4 (ChEMBL1781868). Target-class summaries and ligand-efficiency plots were referenced from ChEMBL for descriptive reporting [34].

Expression and clinical correlation analysis

The transcriptional expression profiles of CCND1 and PDCD4 were analyzed in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA2) tool [35]. Expression levels were compared across molecular subtypes of microsatellite instability (MSI-H, MSI-L, and MSS) and among pathologic stages (I–IV). Data were normalized as log₂(TPM + 1) and analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test; significance was set at p < 0.05 [36].

Mutational profiling and genomic alterations

Genetic alterations of CCND1 and PDCD4 in COAD were explored using the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas dataset) [37]. Mutation frequency, alteration type (missense, splice-site, truncating or amplification).

Immune infiltration and drug-sensitivity correlation

Immune infiltration correlations were examined through the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) portal using TCGA-COAD RNA-seq data integrating deconvolution-based immune cell abundance scores from TIMER2.0 [38]. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between gene expression (CCND1 and PDCD4) and 22 immune cell subsets with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment (q < 0.05). Drug-sensitivity correlations were evaluated by integrating CCLE gene-expression profiles with Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) data [39]. Spearman correlation analyses were performed between expression of CCND1 and PDCD4 and compound area-under-curve (AUC) values across COAD-derived cell lines. FDR < 0.05 and |r| > 0.4 were considered significant.

ADMET profiling

Drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties of the designed compound were evaluated. Physicochemical descriptors, Lipinski’s rule-of-five compliance, gastrointestinal absorption, lipophilicity, solubility and bioavailability parameters were initially predicted using the SwissADME web server [40]. To complement primary ADMET screening, toxicity and pharmacokinetic endpoints were also computed using the DeepPK (DPK) platform [41], which incorporates deep-learning-based prediction algorithms trained on large-scale experimental ADMET datasets [42]. The resulting DPK output files were processed and visualized in R (v4.3.1).

Docking analysis

The crystallographic structures of the shortlisted targets PDCD4 (PDB ID: 3EIJ) and CDK4 (PDB ID: 2W9Z) were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) [43]. The cyclic tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD (sequence: CRGDKGPDC) was constructed de novo in UCSF ChimeraX using the Build Structure tool [44]. For receptor selection of iRGD, the crystal structure of integrin αvβ3 (PDB ID: 1L5G) was docked with iRGD peptide using Cluspro server [45]. To explore the intracellular targeting of the co-loaded drug components, molecular docking of the drug constituents (PTX and TP compounds) with CCNT1 and PDCD4 was performed separatelty using the CB-Dock2 web server [46].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To assess structural stability and conformational dynamics of the best-performing ligand–protein complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS (v2023) with the CHARMM36 all-atom force field [47]. Protein topologies were generated via the pdb2gmx utility, and ligand parameters were prepared using the CGenFF server [48]. Each complex was solvated in a cubic box with TIP3P water molecules and neutralized with Na⁺/Cl⁻ counterions. Energy minimization was followed by 100 ps of NVT and NPT equilibration at 300 K and 1 bar using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively [49]. Production MD was executed for 100 ns and trajectories were analyzed for root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and hydrogen-bond stability.

Nanocomplex preparation

The PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was synthesized using a single-emulsion solvent-evaporation method. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA; 50:50, MW 30–60 kDa; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the polymeric carrier. Briefly, PLGA (100 mg) was dissolved in 5 mL of dichloromethane (DCM) to form the organic phase. Paclitaxel (10 mg) and Trametes robiniophila Murr-derived bioactive metabolites (total 10 mg; equimolar mixture of ergosterol, ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol, adenosine, daucosterol and 3β-hydroxystigmast-5-en-7-one) were co-dissolved with PLGA, resulting in a PLGA: drug ratio of 10:1 (w/w) and a PTX: TP ratio of 1:1 (w/w). The organic phase was emulsified into 20 mL of an aqueous phase containing 2% (w/v) poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA; MW 30–70 kDa) under probe sonication (Sonics Vibra-Cell) at 40% amplitude for 2 min in an ice bath to prevent thermal degradation. The resulting oil-in-water emulsion was stirred magnetically at room temperature for 3 h to allow complete solvent evaporation. Nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and washed three times with ultrapure water to remove residual PVA and unencapsulated compounds. The final pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4).

Surface functionalization with iRGD peptide

For surface functionalization, the carboxyl groups of PLGA nanoparticles were activated using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC; 0.2 mM) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; 0.5 mM) in 0.1 M MES buffer (pH 6.0) for 30 min at room temperature under gentle stirring. Subsequently, iRGD peptide (CRGDKGPDC; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added at a nanoparticle-to-peptide ratio of 5:1 (w/w) and allowed to react for 2 h at room temperature. Unbound peptide and reaction by-products were removed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min, followed by washing with PBS. The final PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was stored at 4 °C and used within two weeks for physicochemical and biological characterization.

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and drug loading (DL%) of paclitaxel in the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex were quantified by indirect measurement. Briefly, nanoparticles were separated by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min, and the amount of unencapsulated paclitaxel in the supernatant was quantified using HPLC (Thermo Scientific Vanquish Core system) at 227 nm. Morphological and size characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

A drop of the nanocomplex suspension was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid. After 5 min, excess liquid was blotted, and the sample was stained with a 1% uranyl acetate solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 min. The grid was then air-dried and imaged using a Thermo Scientific Talos F200C TEM operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. TEM images were analyzed to confirm the spherical shape and discrete nature of the nanoparticles.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic diameter and Polydispersity Index (PDI) of the nanocomplex were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, now part of Spectris, utilizing Thermo Fisher reagents/standards for calibration). Samples were appropriately diluted with DI water and analyzed at 25 °C with a scattering angle of 173°. The measurements were performed in triplicate.

In vitro drug release kinetics

The in vitro release profile of paclitaxel from the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was evaluated using a dialysis bag diffusion method under sink conditions. Briefly, an aliquot of nanoparticle suspension equivalent to 1 mg paclitaxel was placed into a dialysis membrane (molecular weight cut-off 3.5 kDa; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sealed at both ends. The dialysis bag was immersed in 50 mL of release medium consisting of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.1% (v/v) Tween-80 to maintain sink conditions and ensure paclitaxel solubility. The system was incubated at 37 ± 0.5 °C in a shaking water bath maintained at 100 rpm to simulate physiological conditions. At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 165 h), 1 mL of release medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed buffer to maintain constant volume and sink conditions. Paclitaxel concentration in the collected samples was quantified using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Core HPLC system equipped with a UV detector set at 227 nm. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a C18 reverse-phase column using an acetonitrile: water (60:40, v/v) mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The cumulative percentage of drug released was calculated based on a previously established calibration curve (R² > 0.999). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell culture

Human colon cancer cell lines HCT116 (Homo sapiens, male, colorectal carcinoma; ATCC® CCL-247™, RRID: CVCL_0291) and HT-29 (Homo sapiens, female, colorectal adenocarcinoma; ATCC® HTB-38™, RRID: CVCL_0320) and the normal colon epithelial cell line CCD 841 CoN (Homo sapiens, male, normal colon epithelium; ATCC® CRL-1790™, RRID: CVCL_2189) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA), an accredited and internationally recognized cell bank. HCT116 (ATCC CCL-247), HT-29 (ATCC HTB-38) and CCD 841 CoN (ATCC CRL-1790) were obtained directly from ATCC in March 2024, and each arrived with a certificate of analysis confirming cell line identity (short tandem repeat profile), species verification and absence of contamination. All lines originate from human colorectal tissue, with HCT116 and HT-29 representing malignant colorectal epithelial cells and CCD 841 CoN representing non-transformed colonic epithelial cells. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were used between passages 5 and 15 to maintain consistency and minimize phenotypic drift. Authentication was confirmed through STR profiling conducted by ATCC prior to purchase, and all three lines were verified to match their reference profiles in the Cellosaurus database with no reports of misidentification or cross-contamination. All cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection kit and were confirmed negative before and during the experiments conducted in this study.

Cytotoxicity analysis

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated for 72 h with free paclitaxel, TP metabolites, PTX–TP@PLGA without iRGD, or PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD. After incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan Sky microplate reader (Thermo Fisher). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of untreated control cells.

Cellular uptake study

For qualitative and quantitative evaluation of nanoparticle uptake, PLGA was co-formulated with Rhodamine B (Thermo Fisher) during nanoparticle preparation. HCT116 and HT-29 cells were incubated with the fluorescently labeled nanocomplex for 4 h at 37 °C, washed twice with PBS, and detached using enzyme-free cell dissociation buffer (Thermo Fisher). Mean fluorescence intensity was analyzed on an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher). To verify receptor-mediated uptake, parallel samples were pre-incubated with anti-integrin αvβ3 antibody for 30 min before treatment.

Control considerations and fluorescence quantification

Cellular uptake experiments were designed to evaluate receptor-mediated internalization by comparing iRGD-functionalized nanoparticles with non-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles and by employing αvβ3 integrin blocking assays. Free iRGD peptide alone and scrambled-sequence peptide–functionalized nanoparticles were not included as separate experimental arms in this study. For fluorescence-based uptake analysis, rhodamine B was incorporated during nanoparticle preparation under identical conditions for all formulations, and uptake was quantified by flow cytometry using mean fluorescence intensity under uniform acquisition settings to minimize inter-sample variability.

Colony formation assay

Long-term proliferative capacity following nanocomplex exposure was assessed using a colony formation assay. HCT116 and HT-29 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated with sub-cytotoxic concentrations of free paclitaxel, TP metabolites, PTX–TP@PLGA, or PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD for 24 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh drug-free medium, and colonies were allowed to grow for 10–14 days, with medium changes every three days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 20 min, washed gently with distilled water, and air-dried. Visible colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted manually, and the plating efficiency and surviving fraction were calculated relative to untreated controls. For colony formation experiments, sub-cytotoxic concentrations of free paclitaxel were defined as doses not exceeding 25% of the corresponding IC₅₀ values determined from 72 h viability assays. Based on these criteria, free paclitaxel was used at concentrations ≤ 5 nM for HCT116 cells and ≤ 7 nM for HT-29 cells to minimize acute cytotoxicity while allowing assessment of long-term clonogenic potential.

Western blot analysis

For target validation, total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on Bolt 4–12% Bis-Tris gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk prepared in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PDCD4 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. PA5-17386) and β-actin (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. MA5-15739). After washing, membranes were incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 31460) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 32106) and visualized on a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, considering p*-value < 0.05, p**-value < 0.01, and p***-value < 0.001, as significant.

Prognostic significance

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of the 18 shortlisted DEGs identified ten genes significantly associated with overall survival in colon cancer patients at a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 1% (Fig. 3). Elevated expression of TGM2, DKFZp686P1128, CBP2, SERINC2, MYL9, IER5L and ANTXR1 correlated with reduced survival probability showing hazard ratios (HRs) between 1.53 and 2.60. Conversely, higher expression of HSBP1, CCND1 and PDCD4 was associated with improved survival outcomes (HR < 1). Among the adverse prognostic markers, MYL9 (HR = 2.25, p = 1.7 × 10⁻¹²) and DKFZ (HR = 2.6, p = 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁶) emerged as the most strongly associated with poor outcomes. In contrast, PDCD4 (HR = 0.61, p = 5.2 × 10⁻⁶) and HSBP1 (HR = 0.66, p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴) displayed significant protective associations consistent with their recognized roles in apoptosis regulation and cellular stress adaptation.

Target prediction identifies CCND1 and PDCD4 as convergent nodes

SwissTargetPrediction analysis of the constituent ligands within the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex identified multiple human protein targets with high prediction confidence (Fig. 5A). Cross-referencing these predictions with the 10-gene prognostic panel refined the candidate list to two convergence nodes i.e., Cyclin-dependent kinase 4/Cyclin D1 (CDK4–CCND1; ChEMBL1907601) and Programmed cell death protein 4 (PDCD4; ChEMBL1781868). ChEMBL bioactivity summaries for these targets revealed that CDK4–Cyclin D1 primarily characterized by kinase inhibition parameters (IC₅₀, Ki) while PDCD4 datasets encompassed translational control and apoptosis-related functional readouts (Fig. 5B–C).

(B) ChEMBL summary for CDK4–Cyclin D1, including assay-type distribution and ligand-efficiency scatter. (C) ChEMBL summary for PDCD4 highlighting assay-type distribution and ligand-efficiency scatter

The prioritization of PDCD4 and CCND1 over the other candidates within the 18-gene core signature was based on a multi-layered selection strategy. First, survival analysis reduced the candidate list to ten genes with significant prognostic impact (FDR ≤ 0.01), among which PDCD4 and CCND1 were notable for their protective associations. Second, both PDCD4 and CCND1 occupy functionally central positions in pathways governing translation control, apoptosis, and cell-cycle progression. Third, SwissTargetPrediction analysis of the PTX–TP ligand ensemble identified only PDCD4 and the CDK4–CCND1 complex as overlapping targets within the prognostically filtered gene set.

Characterization of CCND1 and PDCD4 in COAD

Analysis of TCGA-COAD data demonstrated that PDCD4 expression progressively decreased with advancing tumor stage (Stage I–IV, p = 0.014) whereas CCND1 showed a mild, non-significant upward trend (p = 0.27) (Fig. 6A–B). Correlation profiling revealed that PDCD4 expression positively associated with macrophages, dendritic cells and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (r ≈ 0.3–0.45, FDR < 0.05), whereas CCND1 displayed inverse correlations with cytotoxic and antigen-presenting immune subsets (Fig. 6C). Integration with CTRP pharmacogenomic data showed that CCND1-high cell lines exhibited reduced sensitivity to several kinase inhibitors while PDCD4-high lines demonstrated enhanced responsiveness to apoptotic and cytotoxic compounds (|r| > 0.4, FDR < 0.01) (Fig. 6D). cBioPortal-based mutational profiling revealed low overall alteration frequencies (< 2%) for both genes (Fig. 6E). The absence of recurrent truncating mutations suggests that their dysregulation in colon cancer is primarily transcriptional rather than mutational.

Pharmacokinetic and ADMET characterization of the nanocomplex

Pharmacokinetic assessment of the designed compound revealed favorable physicochemical and drug-likeness profiles (Fig. 7A–D). The molecule exhibited moderate polarity (TPSA = 133.5 Å²) and low lipophilicity (consensus Log P = − 1.49). SwissADME analysis confirmed compliance with Lipinski’s and Veber’s criteria. The compound demonstrated predicted gastrointestinal absorption within the low-to-moderate range and was not a substrate or inhibitor of P-glycoprotein (Fig. 7B). Metabolic profiling indicated no inhibitory activity toward key cytochrome P450 isoenzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6 or CYP3A4) (Fig. 7C). DeepPK-based toxicity predictions revealed a low probability of hepatotoxicity, AMES mutagenicity and hERG inhibition (Fig. 7D).

Docking analysis of PDCD4 and CCND1 with PTX and TP metabolites

In peptide–receptor docking, iRGD bound integrin αvβ3 (PDB: 1L5G) with a single dominant cluster (n = 996) and a lowest-energy weighted score of − 636.7 (cluster center − 555.2). For PDCD4, all ligands localized within the conserved catalytic cavity (C1; volume ≈ 2200 Å³), with binding energies ranging from − 6.0 to − 9.6 kcal/mol. The strongest interactions were observed for PTX (–9.6 kcal/mol), daucosterol (–9.5 kcal/mol) and ergosterol (–9.2 kcal/mol), stabilized through multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts with residues E217, R216, S214, Q263, E330, E337, K326 and F359. Adenosine formed additional hydrogen bonds with K22 and E217. These ligands uniformly occupied the same binding pocket as shown in Fig. 8A. Docking to CCND1 yielded relatively weaker binding scores, ranging from − 5.5 to − 8.2 kcal/mol, with PTX (–7.3 kcal/mol) and ergosterol (–7.9 kcal/mol). Interactions primarily involved residues E144, N145, D158, H95, K35, G15, V14 and L147 within a shallower surface pocket (C1–C4 volumes ≈ 470–2734 Å³). The smaller number of hydrogen bonds and less negative Vina scores suggested limited binding stability compared with PDCD4 (Fig. 8B). Consequently, PDCD4 was shortlisted as the preferential molecular target for the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex.

Molecular dynamics simulation of PDCD4–ligand complexes

50ns GROMACS simulations of PDCD4 bound to Paclitaxel, daucosterol, ergosta-7,22-dien-3β-ol, 3β-hydroxystigmast-5-en-7-one, ergosterol and adenosine showed rapid relaxation within the first ~ 5–10 ns, followed by stable trajectories (Fig. 9A–C). Backbone RMSD values converged to ligand-dependent plateaus of ~ 4–6.5 Å, with the adenosine complex tracking the lowest displacement across the window and the PTX/daucosterol complexes occupying the upper range. Radius-of-gyration profiles were largely steady at ~ 25–26.5 Å. Per-residue RMSF analysis revealed uniformly low flexibility across the structured core, with expected peaks at the termini and a localized mobility increase in the ~ 300–330 region (Fig. 9B).

Physicochemical characterization of PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex

The PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was successfully synthesized and characterized for morphology, size distribution, and in vitro drug release kinetics. The morphology and size of the synthesized nanocomplex were determined using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). The TEM image (Fig. 10A) revealed that the nanoparticles were discrete and highly spherical with a uniform shape, confirming the successful formation of the polymer matrix structure (Fig. 10). DLS analysis (Fig. 10B) demonstrated a tight and monodisperse size distribution, yielding an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 165 nm. The narrow peak and low Polydispersity Index (PDI ≈ 0.12) confirm the homogeneous dispersion and excellent colloidal stability of the formulation (Fig. 10B). The in vitro drug release profile was determined over a 165 h period (Fig. 10C) The nanocomplex exhibited a characteristic biphasic release pattern: an initial burst release occurred within the first 24 h (releasing 35%-40% of PTX), which ensures rapid local therapeutic concentration (Fig. 10C). This was followed by a sustained release phase, where the drug slowly diffused from the polymer matrix. The cumulative drug release reached approximately 90% after 7 days (Fig. 10C). To further elucidate the release mechanism, cumulative release data were fitted to standard kinetic models including zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas equations. Among these models, the Higuchi model demonstrated the best fit (R² ≈ 0.97), indicating that paclitaxel release from the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex was predominantly governed by diffusion through the polymeric matrix. Consistently, Korsmeyer–Peppas analysis yielded a release exponent value (n ≈ 0.43), which is characteristic of Fickian diffusion-controlled release behavior. These findings confirm that the nanocomplex provides predictable and sustained paclitaxel release, supporting its suitability for prolonged intratumoral drug delivery.

Encapsulation efficiency, drug loading and surface functionalization

The PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex demonstrated high formulation quality and reproducibility. The encapsulation efficiency of paclitaxel was 78.6 ± 3.4%, with a corresponding drug loading of 7.2 ± 0.6% (Table 1). These values are consistent with previously reported PLGA-based paclitaxel formulations and indicate efficient incorporation of the hydrophobic drug into the polymeric matrix. Surface functionalization with the tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD was achieved with a conjugation efficiency of 64.3 ± 4.1% (Table 1), confirming effective peptide coupling via EDC/NHS chemistry. In vitro cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, and target engagement of the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex

In-vitro cytotoxicity assay revealed that PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD produced the greatest growth inhibition with IC₅₀ ≈ 4.5 nM (HCT116) and ≈ 6.5 nM (HT-29), outperforming PTX–TP@PLGA (≈ 9–13 nM) and free paclitaxel (≈ 18–28 nM). TP metabolites alone were minimally cytotoxic (IC₅₀ ≥ 1.2–1.5 µM). These trends indicate enhanced potency from nanoencapsulation and further improvement with iRGD (Fig. 11A). Cellular uptake assay showed that mean fluorescence intensity increased ~ 2.9× in HCT116 and ~ 2.8× in HT-29 for NP + iRGD versus NP (no iRGD), and was partially reduced by αvβ3 blockade, supporting receptor-mediated internalization (Fig. 11B). Although peptide-only and scrambled-peptide controls were not included, the observed reduction in uptake following αvβ3 integrin blockade supports a receptor-associated contribution to iRGD-mediated nanoparticle internalization. Clonogenic survival assay shown that short exposures at sub-cytotoxic doses led to the lowest surviving fractions with PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD (~ 22% HCT116; ~28% HT-29), lower than PTX–TP@PLGA (~ 38–45%) and free PTX (~ 62–70%), while TP alone had minimal effect (~ 92–95%) (Fig. 11C-D). Further target engagement analysis PDCD4 levels increased relative to control with nanoformulations, peaking with PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD (~ 2.2-fold in HCT116; ~1.9-fold in HT-29) (Fig. 11E).

Discussion

This work integrates transcriptomic prioritization, systems-level network analysis, and structure-based modeling to rationalize PDCD4 as the preferential intracellular target for a PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex in colon adenocarcinoma. Our DEG discovery strategy leveraged established microarray/RNA-seq pipelines (GEOquery/limma) and conservative thresholds yielding robust signal in two orthogonal cohorts (GSE44076; GSE253699). The intersection with literature-curated metastasis and chemoresistance panels distilled an 18 DEGs that recapitulates central hallmarks including cytoskeletal remodeling, vesicle trafficking, and stress/apoptotic control. Within the 18 DEGs, survival analysis identified PDCD4 as protective and CCND1 as non-deleterious to mildly favorable in some contexts, while several cytoskeletal and membrane-trafficking genes were associated with poor outcome. The PDCD4 finding aligns with extensive evidence that PDCD4 is a tumor-suppressor frequently downregulated in CRC, where loss relates to invasive behavior and adverse prognosis [50, 51]. Mechanistically, PDCD4 represses cap-dependent translation and interacts with AP-1 signaling; its expression is further curtailed by specific microRNAs in gastrointestinal cancers [52, 53]. TCGA analysis showing stage-wise decline of PDCD4 and positive correlations with CD8⁺ T-cell/macrophage infiltration are therefore coherent with PDCD4’s role in constraining tumor growth and shaping an immune-active microenvironment.

By contrast, CCND1 shows heterogeneous prognostic associations in CRC. Meta-analytic and cohort studies report both unfavorable and favorable signals, likely reflecting subtype, MSI status, and therapy context [54–57]. The low alteration frequencies for PDCD4 and CCND1 in TCGA further support transcriptional/epigenetic control as primary drivers, consistent with prior reports of miRNA-mediated PDCD4 silencing and variable CCND1 expression regulation. Target nomination with SwissTargetPrediction provided an orthogonal cheminformatic view that converged on PDCD4 and the CDK4–CCND1 axis [58]. While target-prediction carries known false-positive risks, we mitigated this by intersecting predictions with a prospectively filtered 10-gene prognostic panel and by validating downstream with structure-based docking and MD. Docking was executed on curated crystal structures (PDCD4 3EIJ; CCND1 2W9Z) using CB-Dock2 which couples curvature-based pocket discovery (CurPocket) to Vina for reproducible blind docking [46]. Across six ligands (five TP metabolites plus paclitaxel), PDCD4 showed consistently stronger binding (≈ − 6.0 to − 9.6 kcal/mol) than CCND1 (≈ − 5.5 to − 8.2 kcal/mol) with denser hydrogen-bond/hydrophobic networks centered on PDCD4 residues E217/R216/S214/Q263/E330/E337/K326/F359. These site-convergent poses within PDCD4’s main cavity argue for coherent pharmacophore complementarity. The iRGD–integrin αvβ3 result provides additional face validity for the delivery layer which aligns with the integrin–NRP-1 transport and tumor penetration mechanism of iRGD [59, 60]. Importantly, PLGA + iRGD strategies have improved intratumoral accumulation and antitumor efficacy in preclinical CRC models [13, 61, 62]. In silico ADMET analyses using SwissADME and DeepPK predicted favorable drug-likeness and a low probability of major toxicity liabilities. The designed construct also showed no predicted inhibition of key cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and was not flagged as a P-glycoprotein substrate. From a formulation perspective, PLGA is a clinically validated, biodegradable polymer with an established safety record in approved drug products and iRGD has been widely used in preclinical models without overt systemic toxicity. Moreover, nanoencapsulation of paclitaxel is expected to mitigate Cremophor EL–associated toxicities and reduce off-target exposure by improving tumor-selective delivery.

50ns GROMACS simulations of PDCD4–ligand complexes showed post-equilibration RMSD plateaus and stable Rg across all ligands, with adenosine producing the lowest global deviation and sterol-rich ligands inducing modestly higher fluctuations without structural loss [63]. Functionally, the synthesized nanocomplex demonstrated markedly enhanced cytotoxicity in colon cancer cells (IC₅₀ ≈ 4.5 nM in HCT116; 6.5 nM in HT-29) compared with PTX–TP@PLGA or free PTX consistent with improved intracellular delivery via the iRGD–integrin αvβ3 pathway [10, 64]. Uptake assays indicated ~ 3-fold higher internalization, while clonogenic survival and immunoblot analysis confirmed reduced colony formation and a ~ 2-fold increase in PDCD4 protein levels, validating target engagement at the cellular level. Together, these data align with prior reports that iRGD-modified PLGA nanoparticles enhance tumor penetration and therapeutic efficacy in colorectal models [13, 65].

Although PDCD4 has been previously described as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer and linked to invasion and microRNA-mediated regulation [66]. Its exploitation as a rational, therapy-guiding molecular target remains limited. In the present work, we introduce a systematic, data-driven framework that elevates PDCD4 from a descriptive biomarker to a functionally engaged therapeutic target. By integrating differential expression analysis across independent cohorts, metastasis- and chemoresistance-focused gene filtering, network topology, and survival stratification, PDCD4 emerged as a convergent, clinically favorable node. This prioritization was further supported by target prediction, structure-based docking and MD simulations, which collectively indicated preferential binding of the PTX–TP ligand ensemble to PDCD4. Importantly, in vitro validation demonstrated increased PDCD4 protein levels following treatment with PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD.

Future investigations should include quantitative analyses of PDCD4 modulation and downstream translational repression in COAD xenograft or orthotopic models. Additionally, studies assessing biodistribution, release kinetics, and iRGD-mediated delivery efficiency in αvβ3/NRP-1 positive tumors will be essential to establish therapeutic relevance and translational feasibility.

Conclusion

This study integrates differential expression, phenotype-anchored filtering, network topology, and survival stratification to identify an 18-gene metastasis and chemoresistance signature in colon tumors and to guide rational nanotherapeutic design. Structure-based modeling revealed preferential affinity of PTX and TP-derived metabolites for PDCD4 over CCND1, supported by stable hydrogen-bond and hydrophobic interactions during 50 ns molecular dynamics simulations. ADMET profiling confirmed drug-likeness, low toxicity, and favorable pharmacokinetics. Mutation analyses showed low alteration rates for PDCD4/CCND1, consistent with stage-associated expression trends. Physicochemical and in vitro evaluations demonstrated that the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanocomplex possessed optimal size and stability, enhanced cytotoxicity and cellular uptake, reduced clonogenic survival, and upregulated PDCD4, confirming effective intracellular targeting. Collectively, these findings identify PDCD4 as the principal molecular target of the nanoplatform and provide a mechanistic foundation for developing PDCD4-directed nanotherapeutics against metastatic and chemoresistant colon cancer. Future studies should focus on in vivo biodistribution, safety and efficacy evaluation in orthotopic and metastatic colon cancer models to define the therapeutic window and assess the clinical translation potential of the PTX–TP@PLGA–iRGD nanoplatform.
