A Case of Intralobar Pulmonary Sequestration in Situs Inversus Totalis
Authors: Panpan Li, Jinfeng Zhao, Xintao Chen, Xiaolong Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary sequestration is a rare congenital malformation of the lower respiratory tract, while situs inversus totalis—characterized by complete transposition of the thoracic and abdominal viscera—represents an equally uncommon developmental anomaly. We report a case of multiple intralobar pulmonary sequestration coexisting with situs inversus totalis in a patient who presented to the emergency department with acute onset haemoptysis. The perioperative management was notably challenging due to the presence of an aberrant feeding artery and the atypical anatomical orientation of the lesions. The patient was successfully managed through a combined approach of percutaneous embolization followed by video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery.

Introduction

Pulmonary sequestration is a rare congenital abnormality of the respiratory tract, characterized by non‐functional lung tissue that lacks normal communication with the tracheobronchial tree and receives its arterial supply from an anomalous systemic vessel [1]. Intralobar sequestration, which is enclosed within the visceral pleura of the affected native lung, accounts for approximately 75% of all pulmonary sequestration cases [2]. Situs inversus totalis, defined as complete mirror‐image transposition of the thoracic and abdominal viscera, represents another uncommon congenital anomaly, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 10,000 and a higher prevalence in males [3]. Although each of these entities has been well documented independently, the coexistence of multiple intralobar sequestrations with situs inversus totalis is exceedingly rare, and the optimal therapeutic strategy for such concomitant anomalies remains poorly defined. Here, we report the case of an 18‐year‐old male patient with concurrent multiple intralobar pulmonary sequestrations and situs inversus totalis, who was successfully managed through a combined approach of percutaneous arterial embolization followed by video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). This case underscores the diagnostic and perioperative challenges imposed by aberrant vascular anatomy and visceral malposition, and offers practical insights into the multidisciplinary management of similar rare presentations.

Case Report

An 18‐year‐old male patient was admitted to the hospital with a chief complaint of recurrent haemoptysis accompanied by chest tightness lasting for 1 h, without associated symptoms such as palpitations, cough, or fever. On admission, his blood pressure was 130/82 mmHg, and peripheral oxygen saturation was 99%. Laboratory findings revealed no evidence of anaemia. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated the following findings: (1) situs inversus of the thoracic and abdominal viscera; (2) hypoplasia and bronchiectasis involving the lower lobe of the right lung and the middle lobe of the left lung; (3) alveolar haemorrhage in the lower lobe of the right lung; and (4) right pleural thickening (Figure 1).

CT pulmonary angiography and bronchial angiography revealed two well‐circumscribed, mass‐like opacities located within the parenchyma of the left middle lobe and the right lower lobe. Tortuous arterial feeders originating from the proximal descending thoracic aorta and the celiac axis were observed (Figure 2).

CT pulmonary angiography and bronchial angiography.

Based on these radiological findings, the patient was diagnosed with multiple intralobar pulmonary sequestrations coexisting with situs inversus totalis.

In view of the severe haemoptysis associated with the intralobar sequestration, an interventional radiology consultation was obtained, and the patient was deemed a suitable candidate for endovascular intervention. Under local anaesthesia, femoral arterial access was established, and selective angiography of the right bronchial, phrenic and left gastric arteries was performed, which demonstrated that these vessels supplied the sequestration in the right lower lobe via tortuous feeding arteries. Embolization was subsequently performed using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles measuring 350–560 and 560–710 μm in diameter. Post‐embolization control angiography confirmed complete devascularization of the sequestration. Following the procedure, the patient's haemoptysis showed marked clinical improvement.

On the following day, the patient underwent VATS for resection of the right lower lobe intralobar sequestration under general anaesthesia, with the patient placed in the left lateral decubitus position using a two‐port approach. The resected specimen was sent for histopathological examination, which confirmed the diagnosis of pulmonary sequestration. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the chest drains were removed on the second postoperative day. Of note, the sequestration in the left middle lobe was not resected during the same session, in accordance with the patient's preference after full disclosure of the associated surgical risks.

Discussion

Pulmonary sequestration is a congenital lung anomaly with a reported incidence ranging from 0.15% to 1.80% [4]. The concurrent occurrence of multiple pulmonary sequestrations and situs inversus totalis is exceedingly rare, with only three cases, including the present one, documented in the literature to date [5, 6]. Preoperative diagnosis of pulmonary sequestration remains challenging in many instances. In our patient, given the presenting symptom of haemoptysis, computed tomography pulmonary angiography was performed promptly and facilitated an accurate diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of clinicians remaining vigilant to the possibility of coexisting congenital anomalies—particularly pulmonary sequestration—in patients with situs inversus totalis who present with respiratory manifestations. Furthermore, the coexistence of these two rare conditions may suggest a shared histogenetic basis, as previously proposed in the literature [7].

Intraoperatively, the anatomical configuration of the right lung corresponded to that of the morphologically left lung, exhibiting two lobes, while the remaining thoracic structures presented as mirror‐image counterparts of the left side. The sequestration lesion was observed to occupy the lower lobe of the hypoplastic right lung; consequently, a lower lobectomy was performed, and the aberrant feeding arteries were meticulously divided using a vessel‐sealing system following clipping. Intraoperative blood loss was minimal, attributable to the prior embolization procedure.

Surgical resection, in the form of single or multiple lobectomies, remains the mainstay of treatment for pulmonary sequestration [8]. In recent years, however, arterial embolization has emerged as a promising alternative for managing symptomatic pulmonary sequestration [9, 10]. As an endovascular technique, embolization offers a less invasive option for selected patients, and occlusion of the feeding artery—resulting in progressive infarction and fibrotic involution of the sequestered tissue—appears to achieve outcomes comparable to those of surgical resection. Nevertheless, in the present case, the patient underwent arterial embolization followed by VATS. This combined approach was primarily driven by two considerations. First, massive haemoptysis in such patients typically results from erosion of the aberrant systemic artery supplying the sequestration [11], and while the risk of bleeding is substantially reduced following embolization, complete occlusion cannot always be guaranteed; residual or collateral perfusion may lead to persistent vascularization and even revascularization over time. Second, in the context of situs inversus totalis, the distorted mirror‐image anatomy and the unpredictable course of aberrant vessels pose considerable challenges for surgical orientation and vessel identification. Supporting this strategy, Porez et al. reported successful management of a similar case using thoracic endovascular aortic repair followed by VATS lobectomy [12]. Therefore, in patients with pulmonary sequestration coexisting with situs inversus totalis, we advocate that VATS lobectomy remains an indispensable component of definitive treatment, as reliance on embolization alone may be insufficient to address the long‐term risks associated with this complex anatomical anomaly.

The surgical management of asymptomatic pulmonary sequestration remains a subject of debate13. In the present case, the sequestration located in the left middle lobe was not resected during the same session, in accordance with the patient's preference after thorough discussion of the potential surgical risks. The patient has since been maintained under regular follow‐up with periodic CT surveillance.

In clinical practice, clinicians should remain vigilant to the possibility of coexisting congenital anomalies‐particularly pulmonary sequestration‐in patients with situs inversus totalis who present with respiratory symptoms. The concurrent occurrence of these two rare entities may point to a shared histogenetic basis, as previously suggested in the literature. For definitive management, VATS lobectomy remains an indispensable component, while preoperative arterial embolization serves as a valuable adjunctive strategy, especially in the setting of situs inversus totalis where aberrant vascular anatomy and visceral malposition pose additional surgical challenges.
