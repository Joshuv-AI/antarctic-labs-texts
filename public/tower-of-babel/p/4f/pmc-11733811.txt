Craniectomy and Cranioplasty Effects on Balance and Gait in Rehabilitation: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Leandra A. Stuckey, Elizabeth E. Holland, Miranda G. Gurra, David Aaby, Jennifer H. Kahn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Cranioplasty alone did not improve balance and gait after brain injury.•Rehabilitation in conjunction with cranioplasty improved balance and gait.•Balance and gait improved with rehabilitation postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty.•Balance and gait improvements postcraniectomy were greater than postcranioplasty.•There was a biphasic pattern of recovery postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty.

Decompressive craniectomy is a neurosurgical procedure that can be used to alleviate intracranial pressure or remove an intracranial mass after traumatic brain injury, nontraumatic brain injury (surgical or oncological mechanism of injury), or stroke.1 Cranioplasty is a neurosurgical procedure where a part of the skull that has been removed or is missing is replaced with the patient's bone or a synthetic material to restore cosmesis and protect the cerebrum.1,2

Cranioplasty after decompressive craniectomy has the potential to enhance neurologic recovery by reinstating regular cerebral spinal fluid dynamics.1,3,4 Motor and cognitive outcomes improve from 0 to 6 months after cranioplasty.4, 5, 6, 7 Previous research in this area has described a biphasic pattern of cognitive and motor performance improvement after brain injury, craniectomy, and cranioplasty. This biphasic pattern consists of an initial functional improvement after craniectomy and subsequent functional improvement after cranioplasty.8 Although other research studies have investigated postcranioplasty functional outcomes in relation to bed mobility,9,10 transfers,9,10 quality of life, and cognition,5,7,9,11 to our knowledge, no studies have looked at the effects of cranioplasty on gait and balance.

An individual's recovery postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty can be affected by several factors, including geographic access to health care,5,9,11 available resources,12 and the knowledge and specialization of staff on the multidisciplinary team.13 Investigating the effects of decompressive craniectomy and subsequent cranioplasty on gait and balance could enhance recovery and quality of life through targeted interventions.14,15 It is impossible to predict every potential factor that could affect an individual's recovery. However, identifying the optimal therapeutic windows can assist in developing an evidence-based plan of care to maximize patient outcomes.

The objective of this study was to analyze changes in balance and gait in patients undergoing rehabilitation postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty, including comparison of outcomes across time periods and rate of change and comparison in outcomes among diagnoses. We hypothesized cranioplasty improves balance and gait in patients recovering from brain injury.

Methods

Study design and participant inclusion

We reviewed electronic health records from an inpatient rehabilitation hospital between January 2017 and January 2022 with the University's Institutional Review Board approval to obtain retrospective deidentified data. Informed consent was not applicable due to the nature of the study. Patients who were 18 and older and completed inpatient rehabilitation postcraniectomy or postcranioplasty were included. Fifty-three participants met the inclusion criteria (20 female [38%], 33 male [62%]), participants’ mean age (SD) was 52.3 years (16.9). All participants were coded in the rehabilitation hospital's electronic medical record with the following diagnoses: stroke (n=19, mean age ± SD: 52.2±18.2y), traumatic brain injury (n=28, mean age ± SD: 45.4±14.4y), and nontraumatic brain injury (surgical or oncological mechanism of injury; n=7, mean age ± SD): 57.1±16.9y) (table 1). In our institution, individuals with craniectomy are frequently admitted to acute inpatient rehabilitation while awaiting cranioplasty, as opposed to a long-term care facility. Outcome measures were collected at initial admission to rehabilitation postcraniectomy (baseline) and 3 follow-up visits: first discharge from postcraniectomy rehabilitation admission, readmission to rehabilitation postcranioplasty, and final discharge from postcranioplasty rehabilitation admission. Hereafter, each time point will be referred to as baseline, first discharge, readmission, and final discharge (fig 1). The clinicians administering outcome measures were trained by the facility and provided the opportunity to discuss challenging administration and scoring situations to standardize each test's execution.Fig 1Timing of data collection and rehabilitation admissions defined.Fig 1

Measures

Balance and gait were assessed using the following measures: Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Functional Gait Assessment (FGA), 10-meter walk test (10MWT), and 6-minute walk test (6MWT). Based on their strong psychometric properties and clinical utility, these tests are recommended in a clinical practice guideline as a core set of outcome measures for adults recovering from neurologic conditions.16 The BBS measures static and some aspects of dynamic sitting and standing balance and consists of 14 items scored on an ordinal scale of 0-4 with a maximum score of 56 points.17 The FGA assesses walking balance and consists of 10 items scored on an ordinal scale of 0-3 with a maximum score of 30 points.18,19 For both the BBS and FGA, lower scores indicate more impaired balance and higher risk for the patient to have a future fall. The 10MWT measures how quickly a person can walk 10 m (measured in meters per second) and has established reference values for healthy individuals and individuals postneurologic injury.18,20, 21, 22 The 6MWT measures endurance and distance walked (in meters) over a 6-minute interval.18

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.2.1. For each outcome measure, linear mixed models were used to examine the following: the overall change over time, the change over time by diagnostic group, and whether the change over time differed by diagnostic group. FGA did not have enough data to include diagnoses. All models included random intercepts to account for repeated measurements within a participant and used all available data at all 4 time points. Differing changes over time by diagnostic group were tested by including an interaction term in the model. If interactions were not significant, only the main effects from each model are reported.

Length of stay was calculated as the number of days between the following time points: (1) baseline to first discharge and (2) readmission to final discharge. Linear regression was used to evaluate the change in each outcome between final discharge and readmission after jointly adjusting for both lengths of stay. Leave of absence duration was calculated as the number of days the participant was at the acute care hospital for the cranioplasty procedure (days between craniectomy admission and cranioplasty admission to rehabilitation). β estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all models. A 2-tailed P value ≤.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Fifty-three participants met the study inclusion criteria. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, there were variable numbers of participants that completed outcome measures during ≥1 time point: BBS, n=46 (17 stroke, 23 traumatic brain injury, 6 nontraumatic brain injury); FGA, n=9 (8 traumatic brain injury, 1 nontraumatic brain injury); 10 MWT, n=33 (9 stroke, 18 traumatic brain injury, and 6 nontraumatic brain injury); and 6MWT, n= 48 (16 stroke, 25 traumatic brain injury, 7 nontraumatic brain injury). Change in outcome measures during leave of absence (first discharge to readmission)

From first discharge to readmission, BBS decreased 6.3 points (95% CI, −11.8 to −1.5), FGA decreased 6.6 points (95% CI, −13.8 to 0.6), 6MWT decreased 19.2 m (95% CI, −73.5 to 35.2), and 10MWT increased 0.089 m/s (95% CI, −0.097 to 0.276) (table 2).

Change in outcome measures during postcranioplasty admission (readmission to final discharge)

From readmission to final discharge, BBS increased 11.3 points (95% CI, 6.6-16.0), FGA increased 10.4 points (95% CI, 4.8-16.1), 6MWT increased 59.4 m (95% CI, 14.1-105.0), and 10MWT increased 0.132 m/s (95% CI, −0.039 to 0.303) (table 2).

Change in outcome measures across rehabilitation course (baseline to final discharge)

All outcome measures showed improvement from baseline to final discharge: BBS 17.9 points (95% CI: 12.7-23.2), FGA 7.8 points (95% CI, 0.6-15.0), 6MWT 141.0 m (95% CI, 89.0-192.0), and 10MWT 0.381 m/s (95% CI, 0.188-0.575) (table 2).

Comparisons between baseline to first discharge (postcraniectomy admission) and readmission to final discharge (postcranioplasty admission)

On average, the length of stay from baseline to first discharge was 23.2 days (range, 0-72d), leave of absence at the acute hospital for cranioplasty procedure was 6.3 days (range, 1-26d), and length of stay from readmission to final discharge was 29.0 days (range, 2-70d). From the postcraniectomy admission (baseline to first discharge) to the postcranioplasty admission (readmission to final discharge), BBS increased by 0.8 points (95% CI, −5.0 to 6.7), 6MWT decreased 38.0 m (95% CI, −92.9 to 16.9), and 10MWT decreased 0.072 m/s (95% CI, −0.292 to 0.147). There was no significant change in any of the outcomes between the postcraniectomy admission and postcranioplasty admission while adjusting for the length of stay.

Difference in diagnoses

For each outcome, rates of change between rehabilitation phases did not significantly differ by diagnosis (P values for interaction terms: BBS P=.3, 6MWT P=.2, and 10MWT P=.2).

Discussion

The hypothesis that a cranioplasty procedure improves balance and gait in patients recovering from brain injury was not supported. Patients did not demonstrate improvements in balance and gait solely because of the cranioplasty procedure; in fact, there was a decline in both balance and gait outcome measures immediately postcranioplasty. Given the clinically meaningful changes and statistically significant results, this study supports rehabilitation as a critical factor in recovery and improved balance and gait outcome measures after acquired brain injury, both in the initial phase of rehabilitation postcraniectomy and during further rehabilitation postcranioplasty. The findings of this study demonstrate a biphasic pattern of recovery for gait and balance outcome measures, consistent with the pattern of motor and neuropsychological recovery mentioned in previous research.8 The BBS, FGA, and 6MWT demonstrated the following trend: improvement during the postcraniectomy admission followed by a decline immediately postcranioplasty, then improvement during the postcranioplasty admission. The 10MWT demonstrated improvement through both rehabilitation admissions without a decline immediately postcranioplasty. The sustained improvement in 10MWT speed immediately after cranioplasty was not statistically or clinically significant. The reasons for improvement in gait speed between rehabilitation admissions are unknown and warrant further investigation.

Advocating for patients to receive rehabilitation postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty can improve overall outcomes, especially when working with knowledgeable and specialized multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams. From baseline to final discharge, all outcome measures improved beyond the minimal detectable change criteria, indicating each reached clinically meaningful change during rehabilitation.23,24 Across the full rehabilitation time frame, the BBS, 6MWT, and 10MWT each improved >2 times the minimal detectable change threshold, and the FGA improved >1.86 times the minimal detectable change threshold.18,23, 24, 25 A comprehensive rehabilitation program should be routine health care following decompressive craniectomies and cranioplasties.

During postcraniectomy admission (baseline to first discharge), BBS, 6MWT, and 10MWT improvements were clinically meaningful, meeting the threshold for minimal detectable change, and were statistically significant.18,24,25 FGA improvements were clinically meaningful but not statistically significant.23 During postcranioplasty admission (readmission to final discharge), BBS and FGA improvements were clinically meaningful and statistically significant. The 6MWT and 10MWT, however, did not reach clinical or statistical significance during postcranioplasty admission.18,23, 24, 25

The data indicate that participants experienced more substantial and statistically significant improvements in their rehabilitation postcraniectomy than in the subsequent cranioplasty, as measured by BBS, 6MWT, and 10MWT. The data trend for FGA showed the opposite: there were greater clinically and statistically significant improvements during postcranioplasty rehabilitation. The data for the FGA may be impacted by the smaller sample size of individuals who completed the FGA (n=9). Length of stay postcraniectomy or postcranioplasty did not influence functional improvements for the BBS, 6MWT, or 10MWT. There was not enough data to compare the postcraniectomy admission and postcranioplasty admission for the FGA.

Finally, differences in balance and gait outcomes by diagnosis postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty were analyzed in this study. For each outcome measure, all 3 diagnosis groups showed similar improvement patterns; there was no statistical or clinical difference among the results of each diagnosis group. Prior research indicates that patients recovering from traumatic brain injury had better functional recovery following cranioplasty than patients recovering from hemorrhagic stroke postcranioplasty.10 These improvements could be linked to stabilizing intracranial pressure, allowing the brain to return to a closed system once the cranioplasty procedure has occurred, increased cerebral blood flow, or cerebrospinal fluid hydrodynamic change.1,3,26,27

Study limitations

This study provides preliminary data on how balance and gait are affected by craniectomy and cranioplasty when combined with rehabilitation. This study did not include individuals with traumatic or nontraumatic brain injury without craniectomy or cranioplasty. It is important to note that the data collected for this study is limited to what was gathered during the regular course of patient care for individuals with craniectomy or cranioplasty. Not all individuals could complete all 4 outcome measures at each time point. Some patients could not safely participate in the measures, whereas others were discharged before retesting could occur. To further validate the results mentioned above, a prospective study that focuses on one specific diagnosis during each phase of rehabilitation (baseline, first discharge, readmission, and final discharge) is necessary. Additionally, the available data did not include any demographic information. In future studies, examining demographic data to determine if it affects the recovery process could be beneficial.

In this study, the sample size was small for the FGA, and only patients diagnosed with traumatic and nontraumatic brain injury completed this measure. There is not an established minimal detectable change for the study outcome measures for patients recovering from traumatic or oncologic brain injuries. With no specific measures for each of these populations, we used the data for patients recovering from stroke to establish if a clinical difference was made. Not having specific minimal detectable changes for these patient populations may make the results less generalizable. Future research examining traumatic brain injury recovery and establishing minimal detectable changes can help identify clinical significance. Finally, future research should continue to investigate the effects of cranioplasty on outcomes, including the length of time between craniectomy and cranioplasty, to maximize the therapeutic window.

Conclusions

In summary, the cranioplasty procedure alone did not improve performance on 3 outcome measures. However, cranioplasty in conjunction with therapy was when the most gains occurred. This contributes to the growing body of evidence suggesting that patients who undergo cranioplasty surgery following a stroke, traumatic brain injury, or nontraumatic brain injury would benefit from receiving therapy to enhance their functional outcomes. Rehabilitation professionals can use these results to estimate expected improvements in gait and balance postcraniectomy and postcranioplasty to tailor their treatments based on where their patient is in the recovery process. This will enable clinicians to customize their treatments according to the specific stage of recovery. Moreover, these findings can further contribute to clinical reasoning and rehabilitation interventions by categorizing the recovery process into 2 distinct phases, phase 1: rehabilitation postcraniectomy and phase 2: rehabilitation postcranioplasty, to optimize the overall recovery outcome.
