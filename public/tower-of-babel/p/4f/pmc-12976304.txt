In-situ formation of oriented perovskite nanosheets with tailored optical dipoles enabling >30% EQE in pure-red LEDs
Authors: Shaowei Liu, Dezhong Zhang, Lei Wang, Binhe Li, Wei Yuan, Ziheng Xiong, Kai Chen, Helong Zhu, Wenping Wu, Shuang Li, Liu Yang, Yunzhuo Liu, Hongmei Zhan, Chuanli Qin, Jiaqi Zhang, Jun Liu, Lixiang Wang, Chuanjiang Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integration of crystallographic control into solution-processed perovskite films remains a challenge for efficient light emission, as disordered optical dipoles fundamentally limit photon extraction, a bottleneck constraining both classical and quantum planar optoelectronic devices. Here, we address this by developing an in situ formation strategy for oriented quasi-2D perovskite nanosheets within films via ligand-engineered crystallization. By designing and orchestrating steric hindrance and π–π interactions of ligands, we direct the crystallization kinetics to yield regular face-on nanosheets exhibiting enhanced horizontal transition dipole moment orientation compared to conventional isotropic films. The in situ architectural control also elevates both the photoluminescence quantum yield beyond 90% and carrier mobility comparable to 3D perovskite levels. These synergies enable perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) with an external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 31.2% for pure-red emission at 635 nm, comparing favorably to other pure-red PeLEDs. Concurrently, the peak luminance and operational stability of the in situ nanosheet PeLEDs exhibit significant improvements.Subject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Optical materials and structures

Introduction

Metal halide perovskite semiconductors have emerged as promising candidates for next-generation light-emitting sources, offering exceptional color purity, solution processability, and high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs)1–7. Pure-red perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs), critical for ultrahigh-definition displays, have recently demonstrated color purity conforming to the Rec. 2020 standard and external quantum efficiencies (EQE) exceeding 25%8,9, yet they still trail behind state-of-the-art organic LEDs (OLEDs)3,10. Notably, planar OLEDs leverage molecular orientation engineering to enhance the alignment of transition dipole moment (TDM), theoretically enabling peak EQEs exceeding 40% through optimized light outcoupling11,12. In contrast, solution-processed perovskites exhibit inferior performance due to unresolved crystallographic challenges posed by randomized TDMs, which limit efficient photon outcoupling and theoretically restrict peak EQEs to about 30%.

Low-dimensional perovskites, leveraging quantum confinement effects and anisotropic optical properties13–15, offer a promising route to address these limitations. Recent advancements highlight the efficacy of ligand-assisted presynthetic approaches in producing anisotropic perovskite nanostructures, such as nanosheets, with horizontal TDM ratios rivaling those of top-performing organic emitters11,16. Colloidal perovskite nanosheets synthesized via solution-phase techniques (e.g., thermal injection and ligand-assisted reprecipitation) have exhibited near-unity PLQYs in suspension17. However, integrating these colloidal systems into functional films confronts interrelated challenges. Achieving precise control over crystallographic orientation during film formation remains technologically elusive15, while ligand desorption frequently occurs, resulting in defect proliferation and PLQY decrease18,19.

A transformative yet underexplored strategy involves the in situ construction of structurally intact, size-uniform, and orientation-controlled perovskite nanosheets directly within films. Theoretically, quasi-2D perovskites with their inherent layered crystallization dynamics can serve as an ideal template for constructing such architectures7,18,19. However, experimental observations predominantly reported amorphous nanocrystals rather than well-ordered nanostructures18. The scarcity of in situ nanosheet formation stems from stringent thermodynamic and kinetic requirements. Ligand-induced lattice distortions and interlayer mismatches impose prohibitive energy barriers for nanosheet formation20, highlighting the critical need for advanced crystallization control strategies.

In this work, we addressed these challenges by in situ constructing perovskite nanosheets within films through ligand engineering. By designing the perovskite and employing 2-naphthalene methylamine (2-NMA) as a sterically optimized ligand, we controlled the crystallization kinetics by the simultaneous orchestration of steric hindrance effects and π–π stacking interactions, thereby minimizing the formation energy barrier of nanosheets. This strategy yielded face-on aligned nanosheets with a strong TDM orientation and suppressed defect density. The resulting films achieved a high PLQY exceeding 90% while maintaining carrier mobility comparable to that of 3D perovskites. These synergistic advantages enabled pure-red PeLEDs with a record EQE of 31.2%, along with markedly improved luminance of 13,400 cd m−2 and operational lifetime of 105 h, thus establishing a paradigm for high-performance solution-processed optoelectronics.

Results

We proposed that ligand engineering offers a promising strategy for modulating crystal morphology in perovskites by influencing crystallization kinetics and reducing the energy barrier for nanosheet formation. Based on considerations of steric hindrance and interlayer interaction, we designed the perovskite structure and used 2-NMA as the primary ligand, as shown in the molecular structure and electrostatic potential in Fig. S1. Although both 2-NMA and the commonly used 1-naphthalene methylamine (1-NMA) share similar chemical compositions21–24, their distinct substitution patterns lead to notable differences in the resulting perovskite structures and properties.

In comparison, 2-NMA exhibited improved dispersibility (Fig. S2 and Table S1) and higher solubility than 1-NMA (Fig. S3), which could contribute to a more uniform perovskite precursor solution25,26. Importantly, the terminal ammonium group of 2-NMA encountered reduced steric hindrance during crystallization, while the naphthalene moiety promoted enhanced π–π stacking between adjacent layers. These critical distinctions will be elaborated upon in the following discussion. Computational analyses, based on these structural distinctions, revealed that the energy barrier for the formation of the perovskite nanosheet structure mediated by 2-NMA was much lower than that of 1-NMA (Fig. 1a), with an energy barrier of <0 eV. Theoretical results predicted that 2-NMA possessed the potential to induce nanosheet formation in films, in contrast to the isotropic crystal films typically formed under the influence of 1-NMA (Fig. 1b, c).

Experimental characterization of the prepared perovskite films confirmed substantial disparities in crystallization behavior between the two ligands. In 1-NMA-based perovskite films, cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Fig. 1d and Fig. S4a) revealed the presence of isotropic crystals with an irregular morphology, along with abundant dark particles (~5 nm) concentrated at the bottom of the film. Lattice spacing analysis identified two distinct patterns: d = 3.0 Å for the irregular crystals, which corresponded to quasi-2D perovskites9,27, and d = 2.3 Å for the dark particles, consistent with PbI2 characteristics28,29, suggesting incomplete crystallization with unreacted PbI2 residues. In contrast, 2-NMA-based films exhibited complete phase purity without residual PbI2. Well-oriented multilayer structures with a uniform d = 3.2 Å lattice spacing (characteristic of layered quasi-2D perovskites) were observed (Fig. 1e and Fig. S4b).

To further investigate the morphology of the crystals, the films were carefully detached from substrates and dispersed using an antisolvent (Fig. S5)8. TEM characterization on the detached crystals revealed that 1-NMA yielded irregularly shaped perovskite crystals (Fig. 1f), whereas 2-NMA facilitated the formation of well-defined nanosheets (Fig. 1g) with a cubic-phase inorganic framework aligned along the [110] zone axis, as confirmed by the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Fig. S6)27. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed structural improvements (Fig. S7), with the characteristic diffraction peak at ~28° showing an increase in peak intensity. These observations confirmed that the use of 2-NMA promoted the in situ formation of ordered perovskite nanosheets within films.

The analysis of 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Fig. 1h, j) revealed distinct interactions between ligands and the inorganic framework. Specifically, upon interaction with CsPbI3, the -NH3+ proton in 2-NMA exhibited a more pronounced downfield chemical shift compared to that in 1-NMA. Similarly, the methylene protons displayed a consistent trend (Fig. S8a, b), indicating stronger interactions between 2-NMA and the inorganic lattice. The observations were corroborated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. S8c, d), which revealed shifts in the binding energies of Pb 4f and I 3d in 2-NMA-based perovskite films relative to those in 1-NMA-based films. These results indicate enhanced electron density redistribution, attributable to stronger hydrogen bonding interactions.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further substantiated these experimental observations. The ammonium group of 1-NMA exhibited a shallower binding depth within the perovskite inorganic framework compared to that of 2-NMA (Fig. S9a, b), which can be primarily attributed to the increased steric hindrance resulting from its naphthyl substitution pattern. This spatial constraint further disrupted the ordered packing of ligands at the framework surface (Fig. S9c). In contrast, 2-NMA achieved an orderly alignment through an anchoring geometry that was optimized by moderate steric hindrance (Fig. S9d). Moreover, the ordered arrangement of 2-NMA enabled enhanced interlayer coupling (Fig. S10), as indicated by nearly doubled π–π interaction energies (Fig. S10e) and delocalized charge density distributions across adjacent ligand layers (Fig. 1i, k). This conjugated network not only templated the growth of quasi-2D layered crystals but also promoted charge transport along the stacking direction.

To elucidate the crystallization kinetics of nanosheets, we investigated the precursor solution-to-film evolution pathways mediated by 1-NMA or 2-NMA ligands, as schematically illustrated in Fig. S11. Firstly, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. S2a, b) revealed larger colloidal assemblies in the precursor solution containing 2-NMA ligands, attributable to reduced steric hindrance enabling solution-phase pre-nucleation cluster assembly. Subsequently, in situ photoluminescence (PL) tracking during the preparation of perovskite films, including spin-coating and annealing (Fig. 2c, d), revealed distinct crystallization pathways. Films with 2-NMA exhibited immediate in situ seed-induced perovskite growth during spin-coating (PL emergence at t = 12 s) at room temperature, whereas those with 1-NMA required thermal activation (annealing at 70 °C, at t = 25 s). To further investigate the crystallization dynamics, in situ absorption spectroscopy was conducted during the thermal annealing process (Fig. S12), which revealed faster crystallization kinetics in the 2-NMA-based films. These results aligned with the calculated lower formation energy barrier presented in Fig. 1a, confirming the thermodynamic advantage of 2-NMA in nanosheet crystallization.

2D grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements were conducted on the perovskite films (Fig. 2e, f). The 1-NMA-based films exhibited diffraction rings along certain extended arc segments, indicating considerable random crystal orientation within the films. In contrast, the 2-NMA-based films displayed discrete Bragg spots, suggesting enhanced crystalline orientation of the perovskite. This observation was consistent with the cross-sectional TEM results of the films, corroborating the in situ formation of highly face-on oriented nanosheets within the 2-NMA-based perovskite films.

The highly oriented face-on perovskite nanosheets within the films enhanced the orientation of TDMs. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the nanosheet films achieved a horizontal TDM ratio of 86%, exceeding the 68% observed in conventional isotropic-emitting films (Fig. 3a). This improvement is anticipated to potentially increase the output coupling efficiency of planar PeLEDs by ~20%. In addition, the carrier transport properties of these face-on oriented nanosheets merited particular attention, as carriers traversed the ligand spacing layers during device operation. The strong conjugation of ligands enabled efficient interlayer carrier transport, whereas conventional films suffered from limitations due to weak inter-ligand stacking effects26. Hall effect measurements confirmed that nanosheet films exhibited carrier mobility comparable to that of 3D perovskite films (Fig. 3b), surpassing conventional counterparts. The morphology of ligand-free 3D perovskite crystals is shown in Fig. S13. The superior charge carrier transport capability was further corroborated by the enhanced conductivity in the longitudinal direction of nanosheet films (Fig. S14 and Fig. 3b).

Distinct absorption spectral characteristics and subtle PL peak shifts (Fig. S15) indicated variations in phase composition between conventional and nanosheet films. Temperature-dependent PL studies conducted over the range of 80 to 290 K (Fig. S16) revealed a slightly reduced exciton binding energy (Eb) in nanosheet films (Fig. 3c), which should be correlated with their structural and electronic modifications accompanied by changes in phase distribution. Transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy (Fig. 3d, e) provided additional evidence of quasi-2D phase differentiation21,22,25. The n = 2 phase in nanosheet films was suppressed, thereby potentially reducing energy dissipation and carrier loss due to its associated high defect density30,31. Time-resolved TA spectral analysis (Fig. S17 and Table S2) revealed the energy funneling effect characteristic of quasi-2D perovskites31–33, further confirming the quasi-2D phase composition within the films.

The nanosheet films achieved a PLQY exceeding 90%, surpassing that of conventional films at ~60% (Fig. S18a), accompanied by an increase in the average PL lifetime (τave) from 7.36 to 12.76 ns (Fig. S18b). These improvements were related to the reduced trap-induced recombination. Notably, the trap density in nanosheet films was lower than that of conventional counterparts, decreasing from 3.1 × 1016 cm−3 to 1.2 × 1016 cm−3 (Fig. S19). Carrier recombination analysis further demonstrated substantially suppressed nonradiative recombination in nanosheet films (Fig. 3f), which could be ascribed to their superior crystallization with low trap density. The marginally reduced radiative recombination rate correlated well with the decreased exciton Eb of nanosheet films. Consequently, the nanosheet films exhibited remarkable light emission performance, while also demonstrating excellent charge transport properties. These synergistic advancements collectively established a critical foundation for the development of high-performance optoelectronic devices.

The pure-red PeLEDs were fabricated with the device structure depicted in Fig. S20. The UPS measurement and energy levels alignment for the devices were presented in Fig. S21. The optimized nanosheet devices achieved an EQE of 31.2% (Fig. 4a), representing an improvement compared to the 16.8% EQE of devices using conventional perovskite films, while effectively reducing efficiency roll-off. The current efficiency (Fig. S22) of both devices exhibited consistent trends with the EQE performance. Furthermore, the nanosheet devices demonstrated enhanced peak luminance from 1100 to 13,400 cd m−2 and reduced turn-on voltage from 2.4 to 1.8 V (Fig. 4b). The angle-dependent electroluminescence (EL) intensity was in accord with a Lambertian profile (Fig. S23). A faster response was observed for the nanosheet devices (Fig. S24), attributed to their superior carrier transport properties.

Statistical comparisons of peak EQE and luminance between conventional and nanosheet devices (Fig. 4c) confirmed the exceptional repeatability and reliability of high-performance nanosheet devices. An optical simulation of the devices was conducted (Fig. 4d), and the results demonstrated that increasing the horizontal TDM ratio leads to a notable improvement in outcoupling efficiency, thereby enhancing the EQE of devices. Taking into account the PLQYs and horizontal TDM ratios of the perovskite films, simulations predicted a maximum EQE of ~31% for nanosheet devices (Fig. 4e), which was in agreement with the experimental results. The EQE of conventional devices is also closely aligned with the simulation predictions. The nanosheet devices compared favorably to other previously reported pure-red PeLEDs (Fig. 4f and Table S3).

The nanosheet PeLEDs demonstrated pure-red EL emission, with exceptional spectral stability under bias stress (Fig. 4g). Specifically, the EL peak position remained consistently at 635 nm across the bias range of 3 to 6 V. In contrast, conventional devices exhibited a progressive blue-shifting of EL emission from 641 to 636 nm as the bias voltage increased, which was attributed to severe ion migration and phase segregation34–37. The operational stability of nanosheet devices was improved, with the half-lifetime (T50) extended from about 10 h for conventional devices to over 105 h at an initial luminance of 100 cd m−2 (Fig. S25).

Additionally, two other wavelength-specific PeLEDs (610 and 660 nm) were fabricated by tuning the halogen composition, achieving high EQEs of 22.6 and 30.5%, respectively (Fig. S26). The uniform growth of high-quality films was realized over a large area, as illustrated by the EL emission of large-area devices (12 cm2, Fig. 4g). The CIE coordinates of the EL emissions were presented in Fig. S27, with the pure-red emission closely matches the Rec. 2020 standard. These findings highlighted the promising potential of nanosheet-based PeLEDs for future applications.

Discussion

This work achieved a breakthrough in light-emissive materials by demonstrating the in situ formation of oriented perovskite nanosheets through ligand engineering. The use of 2-NMA optimized steric hindrance and enhanced interlayer π–π coupling, thereby promoting ordered in situ nanosheet crystallization. Consequently, the nanosheet film showed an 86% horizontal TDM ratio and excellent optoelectronic performance. The structural advantages contributed to reduced nonradiative recombination and enabled carrier mobility comparable to 3D perovskites. The research achieved a high EQE of 31.2% for pure-red PeLEDs at 635 nm, along with enhanced brightness and operational stability. This work redefined the design principles of solution-processed semiconductors, offering a universal paradigm for the development of next-generation optoelectronics, including high-efficiency light emission, lasing, and scalable integrated photonics.

Materials and methods

Materials

Naphthyl methyl ammonium iodide (1-NMAI, 99%), Phenylpropyl ammonium iodide (PPAI, 99%), Cesium Iodide (CsI, 99.999%), Poly(9,9-dioctylfluorene-alt-N-(4-sec-butylphenyl)-diphenylamine) (TFB, 99%), Poly(2,5-bis(2-ethylhexyloxy)-1,4-phenylenevinylene) (POT2T, 99%), and Guanidinium Iodide (GAI, 99.5%) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.5%), and chlorobenzene (CB, 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Lead iodide (PbI2, 99.999%), lead bromide (PbBr2, 99.999%), Ethanol (99.9%), Ethyl acetate (EA, 99.99%), NiOx nanoparticles, and 4-Phenylamino-1,2,3-triazole (4PACz) were purchased from Liaoning Youxuan New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. LiF was purchased from Jilin Oled Material Technology Corp. All the chemical materials were directly used without any further purifications.

Synthesis of materials

2-NMAI and trifluoromethyl-phenylethylammonium iodide (CF3PEAI) were synthesized in our group. 2-NMAI was prepared by reacting 2-NMA with HI at a molar ratio of 1:1.1 in methanol under a temperature-controlled condition of 0 °C for 5 h. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C to facilitate crystallization of 2-NMAI. The resulting crystals were thoroughly washed with diethyl ether and collected via suction filtration, which was repeated multiple times to ensure purity. Similarly, CF3PEAI was synthesized following an analogous procedure.

Perovskite precursor solutions preparation

For the preparation of the perovskite nanosheet (EL emission at 635 nm) precursor solution, PbI2, PbBr2, CsI, PPAI, 2-NMAI, and CF3PEAI were dissolved in anhydrous DMF/DMSO (4:1, v/v) at a molar ratio of 2-NMAI:PPAI:CF3PEAI:CsI:PbI2:PbBr2 = 0.56:0.36:0.24:1.1:1:0.36, with the molar concentration of Pb²⁺ being 0.12 M. In the case of the conventional perovskite precursor solution, 2-NMAI was replaced by 1-NMAI. For the samples with EL emission at 610 nm, the precursors were dissolved in anhydrous DMF/DMSO (4:1, v/v) at a molar ratio of 2-NMAI:PPAI:CF3PEAI:CsI:PbI2:PbBr2 = 0.56:0.36:0.24:1.1:0.44:0.92. For the samples with EL emission at 660 nm, the precursors were dissolved at a molar ratio of 2-NMAI:PPAI:CF3PEAI:CsI:PbI2:PbBr2 = 0.56:0.36:0.24:1.1:1.2:0.2. For the preparation of 3D perovskites, PbI2, PbBr2, CsI, were dissolved in anhydrous DMF/DMSO (4:1, v/v) at a molar ratio of CsI:PbI2:PbBr2 = 1.6:1:0.36, with the molar concentration of Pb²⁺ being 0.12 M. The mixture was subsequently stirred at 60 °C overnight to ensure complete dissolution. Finally, the solution was filtered through a 0.45 µm polytetrafluoroethylene membrane filter before use.

Device fabrication

The indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates were sequentially cleaned in detergent, distilled water, acetone and isopropanol by an ultrasonic cleaner. The pre-cleaned substrates were treated by ultraviolet ozone for 20 min to make the surface hydrophilic. The NiOx (20 mg mL−1) aqueous solution was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 50 s onto the ITO substrates, followed by drying at 150 °C for 15 min in ambient air. Then the samples were transferred into a nitrogen-filled glovebox. 4PACz (0.5 mg ml−1 in ethanol) was spin-coated on the NiOx film at 2000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. TFB (4 mg mL−1 in CB) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by drying at 130 °C for 20 min. LiF (1.5 nm) was then deposited at a pressure <3.0 × 10−4 Pa by thermal evaporation. After that, perovskite films were fabricated by spin-coating the precursor solutions at 4000 rpm for 50 s, 120 µL of EA was poured onto the film as an antisolvent at 10 s. Then the films were annealed at 70 °C for 15 min. Ultimately, POT2T (35 nm), LiF (1 nm), and Al (100 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation, respectively. The active device area is 0.08 cm2.

Cross-sectional microstructure characterization

Perovskite films were prepared on the quartz substrates in a nitrogen glovebox. TEM specimens were prepared using a Helios 5 CX FIB-SEM. Protected with 1.5-μm Pt/C via GIS, regions were milled at 30 kV/5-10 pA to expose features. A U-shaped trench (10 × 2 μm) enabled lift-out via the EasyLift manipulator. Tungsten (W) deposition was applied to weld the lamella to the probe tip at non-irradiated edges. The transferred lamella was further thinned to <50 nm thickness on a Cu TEM grid (Quantifoil R2/2), with final electron transparency confirmed under 5 kV electron beam illumination. The focused ion beam (FIB)-prepared lamellae were promptly transferred to a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200S TEM system equipped with a high-brightness field emission gun and a Gatan K3 IS camera. High-resolution imaging was subsequently performed. All observations were conducted at an acceleration voltage of 40 kV.

Preparation and characterization of samples for top-view TEM

The perovskite film was carefully delaminated from the substrate and immersed in ethyl acetate (antisolvent) under ultrasonication for 10 min to achieve homogeneous dispersion. The resultant colloidal suspension was then deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh) using micropipette deposition and annealing-dried (40 °C) prior to TEM characterization. HRTEM images were recorded using a JEM-F200 (200 kV). TEM data processing was carried out using Gatan™ Digital Micrograph. The organic components are highly sensitive to the electron beam. Therefore, exposure time and beam current should be carefully controlled to prevent structural degradation.

NMR measurements

NMR 1H spectra were acquired using a Bruker Avance III HD 500 MHz spectrometer. Initial measurements were conducted on individual solutions of 1-NMAI and 2-NMAI in deuterated DMSO. Subsequent analyses were performed after the introduction of CsPbI3 into the solutions, with complete dissolution confirmed by visual inspection of clarity.

XPS measurements

XPS spectra were measured by a spectrometer system (Nexsa G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A monochromatic Al Kα line (1486.6 eV) was used for XPS.

DLS characterizations

The colloidal size data were recorded using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 equipment. The sample cell utilized a colorimetric cuvette made of methacrylate, with a light path of 10 mm.

Optical characterization

UV-vis absorption spectra of perovskite films were obtained by a PerkinElmer Lambda 35 UV-vis spectrometer. The PLQYs of the perovskite samples deposited on quartz substrates were determined using the integrating sphere. A continuous-wave laser with a wavelength of 390 nm and power density ranging from 0.1 to 10 mW cm−2 was employed as the excitation source under controlled conditions (temperature: 20 °C, relative humidity: 40%). 2D GIWAXS and XRD measurements

The 2D GIWAXS results were collected at beamline BL02U2 with PILATUS 2 M of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), China. A monochromatic beam of λ = 1.240 Å was used, and the incident angle was 0.4°. The distance between the sample and the detector was calibrated with a lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6) sample. The XRD curves of perovskite films were obtained using a SmartLab (9 kW) X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα1,2 radiation (λ = 1.541 Å).

Longitudinal conductivity

The perovskite films were deposited onto the ITO surface by spin-coating and annealing processes, followed by the thermal evaporation of Au electrodes with a thickness of 30 nm. The fabricated devices were subsequently characterized using current-voltage (I-V) measurements conducted with a Quantaurus-QY (C9920-02) and a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter. A voltage sweep ranging from 0 to 5 V was applied to evaluate the longitudinal conductivity.

Hall measurements

The Hall effects were measured by an AccentHL5500 Hall System (Lake Shore). The magnetic field was calibrated at room temperature using a known Hall probe to determine its strength. Metal electrodes were fabricated at four distinct positions on the sample, which was then mounted on the probe station. The approximate resistance of the sample was measured with a multimeter, and an appropriate test current was selected accordingly. The sample current was adjusted based on specific conditions, and the Hall voltage was subsequently measured. Typically, four measurements were conducted by reversing the directions of the current and magnetic field among different electrodes to eliminate potential side effects. Carrier mobility was calculated based on the acquired data.

PeLED characterization

The active area of the device was 8 mm2. The current density-voltage-luminance characteristics of the PeLEDs were recorded using a computer-controlled Keithley 2400 source meter unit. The measurement parameters were configured as follows: the bias was swept from zero to forward bias with a voltage scanning interval of 0.2 V and a dwell time of 1 s at each step. The EL intensity and spectra were measured using an integrating sphere-spectrometer system (QE65 Pro, FOIS-1-FL integration sphere). The system was calibrated using an HL-3P-INT-CAL calibration light source (Ocean Optics), which provides known reference values for intensity and wavelength. The EQE was verified using a Hamamatsu Absolute EQE Measurement System (C9920-12) to ensure measurement reliability. The system configuration includes a 3.3-inch integrating sphere with Spectralon reflective coating and a back-thinned CCD spectrometer (Model C10028). All photometric parameters were calculated from spectral data within the 380–780 nm range, providing accurate determination of both luminous flux and EQE values. This verification process confirms the reliability of our reported device performance. The operational stability tests were conducted using the calibrated integrating sphere system (Ocean Optics) within a nitrogen-filled glovebox at a constant ambient temperature of 20 °C. The unencapsulated devices were driven under a constant current density to achieve an initial luminance of 100 cd m−2. The luminance was monitored in real-time until it decayed to 50% of its initial value (T50). Time-resolved EL dynamics were measured using an avalanche photodiode (APD) detector coupled with a pulsed voltage source. The devices, encapsulated with UV-curable adhesive, were driven by short electrical pulses (200 μs width, 4 V square voltage pulse) generated by a high-speed waveform generator. These measurements were conducted under precisely controlled ambient conditions (20 °C temperature, 30% relative humidity). ESI was performed by using the electrochemical workstation (CHI660C, Chen Hua, China) with a frequency range from 1 Hz to 0.1 MHz in the dark.

Optical simulation

The optical simulation of the PeLED was performed using the COMSOL Multiphysics software package. To examine the EQE of the nanodevice under various orientations of the TDM, a 2D model was developed utilizing the Wave Optics Module within COMSOL. The light emission efficiency of the nanodevice is influenced by both the PLQY of the luminescent material and the light extraction efficiency of the nanodevice. By solving the wave equation via the finite element method, the light extraction efficiency of the device for different orientations of the TDM was evaluated, thereby enabling the determination of the EQE of the nanodevice.

DFT calculations

The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional with Grimme D3 dispersion correction, employing the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method, was utilized in this study. The projected augmented wave (PAW) method was utilized to describe core-valence interactions in all DFT calculations. The energy cutoff for plane wave expansions was set to 520 eV, and the 1 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack grid k-points were used to sample the Brillouin zone integration for structural optimization. To depict the interactions between molecules and CsPbI3, four molecules were individually positioned on the upper and lower surfaces, and molecular arrangements with varying interactions were constructed by adjusting their relative positions. Structural optimization was carried out with energy and force convergence criteria set at 1.0 × 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively. Molecular electrostatic potentials were visualized through filled contour plots generated in VMD 1.9.4.
