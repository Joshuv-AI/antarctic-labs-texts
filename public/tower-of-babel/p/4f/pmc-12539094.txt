Blue light treatment of psychiatric disorders: relationships with systemic inflammation, lipid metabolism, and clinical symptoms
Authors: Lina Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychiatric disorders impose a substantial burden on individuals and society, and current treatment exhibit limited efficacy. Emerging evidence indicates that blue light exposure can influence mood and psychiatric conditions, yet its underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Since that systemic inflammation is regarded as an important factor in mental health, this study explores the potential relationships between blue light therapy, immune-related pathways, and psychiatric symptoms.MethodIn this single-center retrospective study, medical records from 270 hospitalized psychiatric patients were analyzed. Patients received either routine treatment alone or in combination with blue light therapy, and were further stratified by season, treatment duration, and primary diagnosis.ResultsBlue light therapy was related to significant changes in key inflammation markers and psychiatric symptoms. Notably, we observed seasonal variations in the relationship between immune markers and specific psychiatric symptoms following blue light therapy.ConclusionBlue light therapy may offer a promising adjunctive approach for psychiatric disorders potentially through its associations with systemic inflammation and related symptoms. More studies are needed to explore its pathology and potential applications in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07247-8.Keywords: Blue light treatment, Psychiatry, Inflammatory, Lipid

Background

Psychiatric diseases have been substantial burden and distress on affected individuals and society [1]. Despite advances in pharmacology, treatment efficiency for psychiatric diseases remains limited [2]. This has led to a growing interest in non-drug therapies due to their practical applicability and less side effect profile compared to the pharmacological agents [3]. Given the brain’s sensitivity to external stimuli such as light, more studies has explored the potential of light-based interventions to modulate neural activity and improve mental health outcomes [4–6].

Initially, light therapy was primarily utilized for the treatment of seasonal affective disorder(SAD) [7]. Research indicated that exposure to light can generate high levels of reactive oxygen, subsequently influencing human physiological functions [8]. Over time, the scope of light therapy has expanded. Some studies have explored its efficacy in a broader range of psychiatric conditions [7]. However, the precise mechanisms through which light modulates mood, or other psychiatric problems are remain under investigation.

Blue light is becoming a promising treatment for patients with psychiatric diseases [9], largely due to its high sensitivity to retinal ganglion cells [10], which play a crucial role in regulating sleep and circadian when administered at appropriate doses [11]. A possible explain for efficacy of blue light is that it can affect the central circadian clock located within the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) [12]. By influencing the SCN, it can alter internal metabolite levels [13] and lipid homeostasis [14, 15]. Emerging evidence points to a link between psychiatric disorders and inflammatory markers [16, 17], such as leukocytes, haptoglobins or C-reactive protein (CRP). Higher concentrations of these indicators have been linked to a higher likelihood of psychiatric conditions, suggesting that neuroinflammation may affect the pathophysiology of these disorders [18]. Notably, neuroinflammation showed a circadian regulation [19] which mainly involve lipid, immune function and glymphatic system [20], which usually is impaired in psychiatric disorders [21]. Furthermore, Barlattani et al.’s study showed that individual differences in light sensitivity are modulated by PER3 VNTR polymorphisms in bipolar disorder, highlighting how circadian‑clock genetics may modulate individual sensitivity to blue light [22].

Although numerous studies have established links between inflammation and psychiatric disorders, most investigations have focused on cytokines, chemokines, and indices of oxidative stress. Yet, a large number of these biological markers are often challenging to obtain routinely due to invasiveness, cost, and technically demands [23]. In contrast, parameters derived from a blood cell count including neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets—is inexpensive, readily accessible, and reliably reflect inflammatory status [24].

Among these, NLR (neutrophil/lymphocyte), PLR (platelet/lymphocyte), SII (platelet×neutrophil/lymphocyte), and LMR (lymphocyte/monocyte) ratios have gained prominence as clinically useful inflammatory biomarkers in psychiatric research. NLR, in particular, mediates crosstalk between innate and adaptive immune responses [25] through cytokine-mediated signaling and HPA axis modulation and is consistently elevated across multiple psychiatric disorders. The practical advantage of NLR is its calculation from routine complete blood count (CBC) data, making it more accessible than markers like CRP, which require specialized assays [26]. Similarly, PLR is associated with mood dysregulation and schizophrenia [27]. Both NLR and PLR are elevated in psychiatric conditions across diagnostic categories: affective disorders (bipolar and unipolar depression) and schizophrenic psychosis (schizophrenia) [28], underscoring immune dysregulation’s role in psychiatric pathophysiology [29]. LMR reflects immune regulation through monocyte-lymphocyte interactions [30], while derived from the product of platelet and neutrophil counts divided by lymphocytes, SII provides a multidimensional evaluation of inflammatory activity [31]. Putrada Ninla-aesong et.al. found that SII, NLR, and PLR are significantly higher in patients with major depressive disorder relative to controls, with NLR and SII demonstrating strong diagnostic potential (AUC > 0.7) [32]. Peripheral blood routine testing offers substantial clinical utility due to their convenience, cost-effectiveness, and accessibility [33]. However, no studies have directly compared the prognostic value of these inflammatory indices with the clinical mechanisms of blue light therapy in psychiatric populations [32].

Despite evidence linking lipid and inflammatory markers to psychiatric illness, blue light remains primarily applied in the treatment of depression, with limited exploration in other psychiatric disorders [34]. Furthermore, no studies have examined the relationships of blue light, lipid, and immune parameters across different psychiatric diagnoses and time points. Therefore, more studies are warranted to elucidate the correlation between the blue light treatment and biological changes.

We examined the relationship between peripheral inflammation indices (LMR, SII, NLR, PLR)) [35] and the psychiatric symptoms following blue light therapy of different duration and time points. This study may help to shed light on that how blue light correlates with psychiatric symptom changes and inform future clinical applications.

Method

Participants

This retrospective study utilized data from Shenzhen Kangning Hospital China. We sequentially screened electronic medical records of patients admitted between January 2021 to January 2023. Inclusion criteria were age above 18 years, hospitalization longer than one week, and ability to cooperate with light therapy. Exclusion criteria included: serious somatic disorders, severe impulsivity or self-harm risk, recent use of lipid-lowering, antibiotic, or immunomodulatory drugs within 6 months, or active infection during hospitalization.

As a non-pharmacological intervention, blue light therapy was available throughout the entire study period, with no concentrated implementation phase. Participation was entirely voluntary, and clinicians needed to explain the procedure, potential benefits, and risks to the patients to obtain their informed consent. Assignment to the blue light group was therefore non-random and primarily patient-driven regardless of diagnosis, season, or physician preference. Patients who discontinued therapy within one week were excluded from analysis. We finally included 132 patients who received blue light therapy. For the control group, we consecutively enrolled 138 eligible patients who declined blue light therapy during the same admission period. This parallel enrollment strategy controlled seasonal variations while maintaining comparable hospitalization timelines between groups. This non-random allocation process may introduce selection bias, limiting causal inferences. The Shenzhen Kangning hospital ethical committee reviewed and approved the study.

Based on established diagnostic criteria, patients were categorized into four groups: Psychotic disorders group (schizophrenia, delusional disorder, acute and transient psychotic disorder; paranoid disorder and schizoaffective psychosis); Affective disorders group (depression, bipolar disorder, manic episode); Organic mental disorders (dementia, other mental disorders caused by brain damage and functional disorders), Neurotic disorders group (anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder). Given the neurotic group small sample (n = 8 patients) and frequent comorbidity with depression, we combined neurotic group and affective disorders group to analysis [36].

Considering the seasons influence for phototherapy, patients were further divided into four groups based on the season during which they received treatment: spring, summer, autumn and winter. Additionally, patients in the blue light group were categorized into three subgroups based on the duration of therapy received: within 2 weeks (n = 21,15.9%), 2–3 weeks (n = 51,38.6%), and 3–4 weeks (n = 60, 45.5%). The overall mean of the blue light therapy duration was 20.62 ± 5.82 days. (Table 1)

Treatments

Routine treatment

All patients received standard oral pharmacotherapy for mental disorders, as prescribed by their attending physicians. The oral pharmacotherapy includes antidepressants, antianxiety drugs, antipsychotic drugs, mood stabilizer, benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine sedatives.

Blue light therapy

Blue light therapy was administered each morning using a table-mounted device (Model: YR-580 A, Wuhan Yourui Technology Co., LTD). Each device comprises a polymer-encased mainframe containing a grid of LED units. During operation, the Blue Energy Light device was set to deliver its maximum optical power density (> 6.0 mW/cm²), with a frequency of 40 Hz (± 1 Hz) and an input voltage of AC 220 V (± 22 V). For safety and standardization, each session was automatically limited to 30 min of continuous use, after which the device would deactivate.

Participants in the blue light group were required to use the emitting device daily during morning hours (within 2 h post-awakening, before 11:00 am. Participants positioned themselves at the prescribed 40-inch working distance, maintaining 20–40 degrees of angular displacement from the device, so that the light was positioned to evenly illuminate both sides of the face, while participants were advised to avoid staring directly at the diodes to prevent discomfort. This procedure is consistent with clinical recommendations regarding the timing and positioning for light therapy, which recommend morning exposure and indirect viewing to maximize efficacy and minimize adverse effects. In our study, the exposure time was fixed at 30 min per session [7, 37]. During every session, at least one nurse was present in the therapy room to supervise. Participants were directed to preserve an alert state and eyes uncovered and to keep their faces oriented toward the light device. Leaving the therapy room was not permitted during the session except in cases of emergency, in which case a physician would assess the situation and determine whether the session should be discontinued. The treatment adherence was recorded by nursing staff, with records filed in each patient’s medical record. All patients included in the final analysis fully adherence to the prescribed 30-minute therapy session as documented by nursing staff. No treatment withdrawals occurred due to adverse effects or medical emergencies. In cases where patients voluntarily requested early termination of therapy, the discontinuation date was recorded as the end of treatment duration for that individual. The total treatment duration for each participant was defined as the continuous interval from initial to final blue light session.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the overall treatment response, assessed using the Clinical Global Impression Efficacy Index scale (CGI-EI). The CGI-EI is a widely used tool in psychiatry research and clinical practice for evaluating overall therapeutic efficacy. This clinician-reported measure holistically evaluates illness severity by integrating historical, symptomatic, behavioral, and functional parameters [38]. The therapeutic response is classified into four levels: Level 4: Markedly effective – Symptoms are completely or almost completely relieved. Level 3: Effective – Symptoms show significant improvement or are partially relieved. Level 2: Slightly effective – Symptoms are minimally relieved. Level 1: No change or worsened – Symptoms remain unchanged or have deteriorated. The study applied the CGI assessed after finishing the therapy.

To further evaluate the severity of specific symptom domains, the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) [39]was administered at baseline and after treatment. The BPRS assesses five syndrome categories: anxiety/depressive, excited/agitation, aggression, abnormal thinking, apathetic. Higher BPRS scores indicate greater symptom severity. All scales were administered by trained raters to ensure consistency and reliability of the assessments.

The second outcome was 13 serum biochemical markers (cell counts, blood lipids and immunological indicators) in peripheral biological samples. Peripheral blood cell counts (neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, platelets) were quantified at baseline to derive four inflammatory indices through standardized calculations: Inflammatory biomarkers were computed as: NLR = N/L, PLR = P/L, LMR = L/M, SII = (N×P)/L, where N = neutrophils, L = lymphocytes, P = platelets, M = monocytes [40, 41]. Lipid metabolism index (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein [HDL], low-density lipoprotein [LDL]). All the laboratory tests were performed on fasting blood drawings in the early morning of the first or the second day of admission.

Diagnosis

The initial diagnoses were derived from the electronic medical records. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), was used for diagnostic coding (ICD-10 codes, F00-F09, F10-F19, F20-F29, F30-F39 and F40-F48) were used to identify the diagnosis. A systemic psychiatric examination was made by two psychiatrists with attending titles or above.

Other measurements and definitions

The data includes age, education level, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes. Hypertension: either antihypertensive medication use or persistent elevation (SBP > 140 mmHg/DBP > 90 mmHg) on repeated measurements; Diabetes: Documented use of glucose-lowering drug regimen or verification of hyperglycemia (fasting glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L); Education: Categorized by duration of formal schooling into: <9 years, 9-12year and > 12years.

Statistics

All outcome assessments adhered to the intention-to-treat (ITT) analytical approach. Group comparisons were conducted using appropriate statistical methods: independent t-tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for non-parametric distributions. Group differences in proportions were tested by χ² statistics. Missing values were filled by means. Sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of primary findings. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Multiple comparison adjustments were implemented with Bonferroni method for primary outcomes and Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for interaction analyses. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (v.26; Armonk, NY).) and the figures were completed using R version 4.4.3.

Results

Baseline metrics

In total, the cohort (n = 270, Fig. 1) comprised 72 patients with schizophrenia, 4 patients with schizoaffective psychosis, 5 patients with acute and transient psychotic disorder, 8 patients with delusional disorder, 49 patients with bipolar disorder, 14 patients with depression, 35 patients with dementia, 74 patients with other mental disorders caused by brain damage and functional disorders, 9 patients with anxiety related disorder(7 anxiety disorder, one obsessive-compulsive disorder and one somatization disorder).

Comparation of the diagnosis categories between the blue light group and control group

As shown in Table 1, the mean age of the 270 patients (122 men and 148 women) enrolled in this study was about 57 years old (56.77 ± 15.32). Among them, 132 (48.9%) patients who underwent blue light therapy were defined as the blue light group, while the remaining 138 patients (51.1%) were in the control group. No significant differences existed in the age, sex, and education level between the two groups. Diagnosis categories did not differ significantly (p = 0.172). Psychotic disorders (n = 89) accounted for the major percentage, which was 33%. In the control group, the psychotic disorder group had 49 patients (35.5%), the affective disorder group had 30 patients (21.7%) and the organic mental disorder group was 59 patients (42.8%). The blue light group had 40 patients (30.3%) in the psychotic disorder sub-category, 42 patients (31.8%) in affective disorder and 50 patients (37.9%) in organic mental disorder.

The two patient groups demonstrated clinically meaningful differences in CGI severity (P = 0.015). In the control group, 32 participants (23.2%) were rated as Level 1 (no change or worsened), 19 (13.8%) as Level 2 (slightly effective), 49 (35.5%) as Level 3 (effective), and 38 (27.5%) as Level 4 (markedly effective). In contrast, in the blue light therapy group, 16 participants (33.3%) were rated as Level 1, 30 (22.7%) as Level 2, 59 (44.7%) as Level 3, and 27 (20.5%) as Level 4. Overall, CGI-EI scores improved more significantly in the blue light group. (Table 1)

Sensitivity analysis (supplementary Table S1) adjusting for demographic and metabolic factors showed: BMI was consistently associated with treatment response (Model 3: β = −0.180, 95% CI [−0.035, −0.007], p = 0.004). Diabetes (Model 3: β = 0.160, 95% CI [0.053, 0.370], p = 0.009) and hypertension (Model 3: β = 0.159, 95% CI [0.042, 0.314], p = 0.010) were positively associated with better outcomes. Psychiatric diagnosis classification (psychotic/affective/organic mental disorders) had no significant effect (Model 3: p = 0.439). No multicollinearity was detected (VIF range: 1.04–1.19).

Table 2 presents a comparative analysis of symptom improvement between blue light group and control group. After rigorous multiplicity adjustment (Bonferroni α = 0.01), the blue light group showed significantly greater improvement in depressive/anxiety symptoms (adjusted p = 0.010) compared to controls. Contrary to expectations, the control group showed better improvement in abnormal thinking (adjusted p = 0.008) and apathetic symptoms (adjusted p = 0.008). No significant differences between-groups were observed for excited and aggression symptoms (both p > 0.112).

Changes in different seasons

Tables 4 and 5 show that blue light therapy group showed different changes in psychiatric symptoms across all seasons, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 0.5) for excited/agitation, aggression, and abnormal thinking. The most pronounced improvements in excited/agitation and aggression were observed during summer and autumn (p ≤ 0.001, d<−0.8). Abnormal thinking also improved significantly across all seasons (p < 0.05; d ∈ [−0.97, −0.78]). Depressive/anxiety symptoms showed moderate improvement across all seasons (d ∈ [−0.51, −0.43]). Apathetic symptom exhibited minimal changes (d ∈ [−0.20, 0.00]), with no change detected in winter (p = 1.00). Summer and autumn showed the largest symptom improvements, particularly in agitation and aggression (p < 0.001, d<−0.8). while apathetic symptoms remained largely unchanged in winter (p = 1, d = 0.00). Depressive symptoms then improved moderately during spring (p < 0.001, d = −0.47). Regarding inflammatory and lipid markers, NLR showed significant reductions in spring (p = 0.020, d = −0.41) and autumn (p = 0.002, d = −0.61). SII decreased in summer (p = 0.001, d = −0.31) and autumn (p = 0.035, d = −0.43). HDL decreased most in summer (p = 0.001, d = −0.53) and LDL decreased in autumn (p = 0.014, d = −0.44,). In contrast, TG in winter showed a slight increase from 1.29 ± 0.70 to 1.57 ± 0.72 (p = 0.012, d = 0.39).

The differences in treatment durations groups

Significant time-dependent differences emerged across symptom measures (p < 0.001) except for apathetic symptoms at 3–4 weeks (p = 0.058). The largest effect sizes were observed in abnormal thinking (Cohen’s d = −0.95 to −0.82), Excited/agitation (d = −0.89 to −0.81), and aggression (d = −0.76 to −0.75). Depressive/anxiety symptoms showed smaller effect sizes ranging from d = −0.48 at 4 weeks to d = −0.01 at 2 weeks. Apathetic symptoms showed the smallest effect sizes (d = −0.19 to −0.05). Among inflammatory markers, NLR showed significant reduction at 2–3 weeks (p = 0.013, d = −0.38) but not at other intervals. PLR showed negligible changes (p > 0.05, d = −0.2 to 0.03). SII showed small positive effects (d = −0.30 to −0.23). LMR showed a small negative change at 3–4 weeks (d = −0.27, p = 0.034). Figure 2 showed the mixed-effects model analysis examining the relationships among diagnostic subgroups, treatment duration, seasons and depressive/anxiety scores. After Bonferroni correction, no main effects were found for diagnosis, treatment duration or season (all p > 0.0.5). For interaction effects (FDR-corrected), psychotic disorder patients showed significantly changes in depressive/anxiety symptoms in spring compared to winter (b = 1.714, SE = 0.730, p = 0.021, adjusted p = 0.042, 95% CI [0.267, 3.161]). Affective disorder patients exhibited different progression patterns at 2–3 weeks compared to 3–4 weeks (b = 1.407, SE = 0.484, p = 0.004, adjusted p = 0.012, 95% CI [0.447, 2.366]). Additionally, treatment duration of within 2 weeks showed a significantly different response in summer compared to winter (b = 1.79, SE = 0.89, p = 0.046, adjusted p = 0.046, 95% CI [0.323, 3.550]).

Mixed-effects model: depression/anxiety symptoms by diagnosis, seasons, and duration

Figure 3 reported the mixed-effects model analysis for aggression symptoms. No significant differences were found between diagnostic groups after Bonferroni correction (psychotic diseases vs. organic mental disorders: p = 0.329, adjusted p = 0.658; affective diseases vs. organic mental disorders, p = 0.826, adjusted p = 1.000). Aggression symptoms were significantly higher in summer compared to winter (b = 1.66, p < 0.001, adjusted p = 0.003, 95% CI [0.72, 2.61]) with no significant seasonal changes were observed for spring (p = 0.132) or autumn (p = 0.624). A treatment duration of 2–3 weeks was associated with significantly lower aggression symptoms compared to 3–4 weeks (b = −1.41, p = 0.002, adjusted p = 0.004, 95% CI [−2.30, −0.53]). No significant association was found in the duration of within 2 weeks (p = 0.798). After FDR correction, two significant diagnosis-by-season interactions were identified. Patients with psychotic disorders and those with affective disorders both showed attenuated summer-associated aggression compared to those with organic mental disorders (psychotic: b = − 1.48, p = 0.018, adjusted p = 0.036, 95% CI [− 2.69, − 0.26]; affective: b = −1.53, p = 0.010, adjusted p = 0.025, 95% CI [−2.69, −0.37]). The interaction between affective disorders and spring was marginally non-significant (b = − 1.11, p = 0.062, adjusted p = 0.083). Finally, the changes in 2–3 weeks’ treatment duration differed in autumn compared to winter remained non-significant after correction. (b = 1.11, p = 0.056, adjusted p = 0.075). Thus, this study further explored the correlation of different diagnostic subgroups, treatment duration and season with symptoms through a mixed effects model and adopted a strict multiple comparison correction method to enhance data reliability.

Mixed-effects model: aggression symptoms by diagnosis, seasons, and duration. Bonferroni correction was applied separately within each factor group (diagnosis, season, duration) to adjust for multiple comparisons, with the significance threshold (α) divided by the number of comparisons within each factor (e.g., α = 0.05/2 for diagnosis). For interaction effects, FDR (False Discovery Rate) correction was used across all tested interaction terms, which controls for the expected proportion of false discoveries. Analysis of excited/agitation and abnormal thinking symptoms revealed no significant interaction effects between diagnostic groups and either treatment duration or seasonal factors (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Similarly, apathetic symptoms showed no statistically significant interactions with diagnosis, treatment duration, or seasons (Supplementary Table S5).

Safety and tolerability

Safety monitoring was conducted via retrospective review of electronic medical records. No severe adverse events were reported during blue light therapy. Three individuals required treatment modifications: Two patients reduced their treatment duration from four weeks to two weeks due to self-reported drowsiness. One participant discontinued therapy after one week due to fever onset (unrelated causality could not be ruled out). Additionally, five patients reported increased dream frequency during treatment but completed the full treatment course. No other treatment-emergent adverse effects were documented. Overall, blue light therapy was well tolerated in this patient cohort.

Discussion

The present study provides novel evidence linking that blue light treatment to therapeutic outcomes, systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism in psychiatric disorders. This research provides the first evidence of changes in systemic inflammation (NLR, PLR, LMR and SII) and lipid metabolism following the blue light treatment in this population. Future studies should investigate the mechanistic pathways underlying blue light’s therapeutic benefits.

Notably, the blue light group exhibited better CGI outcomes despite a higher baseline burden of metabolic comorbidities (diabetes and hypertension). This finding may be attributable to the modulation of inflammatory and lipid pathways, as both diabetes and hypertension are known to disrupt these systems [42]. Additionally, sensitivity analysis ruled out diagnosis group as a major confounder, strengthening the robustness of our primary findings. This study showed that in blue light therapy group, significantly systemic inflammatory markers (NLR, SII) were reduced while LMR was increased, may indicate anti-inflammatory properties. These aligned with established evidence that NLR, PLR, and SII reflect heightened innate immunity, whereas elevated LMR is associated with adaptive immune function [35]. Blue light’s anti-inflammatory effects could involve HPA axis modulation [43], through its stimulation of ipRGCs that innervate the SCN, thereby influencing circadian timing system [44]. This pathway is known to synchronize systemic inflammation and diurnal neuroimmune dynamics [12]. Neonatal studies demonstrated blue light’s circadian influence through peripheral Bmal1/Cry1 gene expression in PBMCs and concomitant melatonin suppression [45]. Both vivo and vitro experiments demonstrate melatonin treatment significantly reduced dim blue light -induced hippocampal microglial activation and neuroinflammation [46]. The SCN orchestrates circadian-controlled immunity through molecular oscillations of clock components (CLOCK/BMAL1 heterodimers and PER/CRY repressors). Such temporal regulation prevents inflammatory dysregulation, as circadian misalignment elevates key mediators (IL-6, TNF-α) via NF-κB pathway activation [47]. Circadian rhythm disorders are closely related to elevated inflammatory markers and increased 24-hour IL-6 levels by 15% [48]. Our results are further supported by evidence that blue light suppresses dendritic cell (DC) activation, reducing secretion of IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α [49].

This study identified significant correlations between NLR and abnormal thinking, as well as PLR and apathetic symptoms, consistent with prior studies linking neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes to neuroinflammation in mood disorders [26, 50]. Mazza et al. also reported elevated NLR values in MDD patients relative to healthy controls [51]. Peripheral immune cells (lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils) may contribute to mood regulation by releasing pro-inflammatory factors and activating astrocytes and microglia [52]. Elevated SII values indicate systemic inflammatory states by simultaneously quantifying: (1) thrombocytic activity (platelets), (2) granulocytic response (neutrophils), and (3) lymphocytic regulation - offering superior stability over single-parameter indices [33] [53]. The degree of SII reduction from admission to discharge may predict the alleviation of depression [54]. Neutrophils contribute to inflammatory persistence and participate in adaptive immune regulation [55]., the anti-inflammatory effects of blue light may involve multiple mechanisms, including hormonal modulation (e.g., leptin, melatonin) and antioxidant upregulation [56].

After correction, SII was related with abnormal thinking and PLR was related with apathetic symptoms. Confirming established evidence, our data reinforce SII’s clinical utility in anticipating TBI-related affective disturbances through its integration of thrombotic-inflammatory pathways, s, further highlighting the connection between inflammation with mental disorders [57]. Elevated levels of circulating immune cells and their composite ratios (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte, monocyte-to-lymphocyte, platelet-to-lymphocyte, and systemic immune-inflammation indiceshave been observed in traumatic brain injury patients experiencing mood disorders [58]. The neutrophil-lymphocyte-monocyte-platelet axis promotes tumorigenesis through proliferative, angiogenic, immunomodulatory and metabolic mechanisms [59]. Consistent with Barlattani et al.’s seminal work, our findings support the inflammation-glymphatic-psychosis axis, wherein AQP4-mediated CSF clearance disturbances potentially contribute to: schizophrenia pathophysiology, major depressive episodes, anxiety disorder manifestations [21]. The glymphatic network may bridge the gap between circadian disruption, neuroinflammation, and symptom persistence by clearing brain waste during sleep [60]. Thus, blue light can act as a powerful mediator of clock gene expression, glymphatic flow, and inflammatory signaling, which is associated with psychiatric symptoms.

Our findings suggest that elevated triglyceride (TG) levels showed significant correlations with apathy severity scores, suggesting a link to lipid dysregulation and neuropsychiatric manifestations. However, this contrasts with previous reports by Yan Li et al. [61], potentially reflecting populations, methodologies, or circadian differences, as TG levels fluctuate under endogenous circadian control [62] and are influenced by blue light [63]. Additionally, Cheng-Hsieh Huang et al. reported that blue LED lighting dysregulated metabolism in mice by inducing gut dysbiosis and reduction in microbial diversity and commensal-pathogen imbalance [64].

Given the human brain contains the second highest concentration of lipids [57], lipids play essential roles in neuronal differentiation and synaptogenesis [58, 59], structural integrity of cell membranes and myelin sheaths, and regulation of neuronal excitability and signal transduction [60]. Moreover, growing evidence implicates lipid dysregulation in the pathogenesis of various neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia and dementia [65]. The observed association between TG and apathetic symptoms in our study supports this, suggesting lipid metabolism may represent a shared pathway between metabolic and psychiatric disorders.

Different length of the blue light treatment might correlate with clinical efficacy variably, yet optimal blue light treatment duration remains unclear [37]. While current research focused on acute circadian effects within 24-hour periods, our study represents the first comprehensive analysis of seasonal variations in immune and metabolic responses to blue light therapy among psychiatric patients across a full annual cycle. Supporting evidence comes from studies demonstrating that blue light exposure alters TG and TG/HDL ratios seasonally, coinciding with shifts in melatonin secretion [66]. Morning light therapy advanced sleep phases, reduced somnolence, and showed volumetric correlations with posterior thalamic regions [67, 68]. and cortisol levels [69]. Our findings showed that the relationship of blue light treatment with depression and anxiety after one-week was enhanced in summer, contrasting with the general finding of increased baseline aggression in summer months. Seasonal SCN activity fluctuations may drive circannual HPA axis variations, characterized by elevated glucocorticoid secretion and enhanced pro-inflammatory signaling during winter months [70]. Circadian timing system though the microglial, can affect these physiological function [71]. This seasonal change were supported by a study from Denis Gubin et al., demonstrating that proxies of metabolic health including TG, HDL were associated with delayed phrases of melatonin during winter/summer solstices [66], suggesting treatment protocols may require seasonal adjustment.

The findings suggest that blue light therapy has clinically meaningful and population-specific links across multiple domains. These therapeutic outcomes likely stem from blue light’s ability to stabilize circadian rhythms, which are often disrupted in psychiatric populations [72]. Patients with affective disorders showed particularly robust improvements in abnormal thinking, potentially reflecting blue light’s mood-stabilizing properties [73]. The significant interaction between psychotic disorders and spring season for depressive/anxiety symptoms suggests that circadian-sensitive interventions may be particularly effective during seasonal transitions for this population. This doesn’t align with previous reports of seasonal and non-seasonal depression [37], indicating that circadian interventions may have unique value for non-seasonal depression. The spring transition period may represent a critical window for treating psychotic disorders.

Conclusion

This study suggests that blue light treatment may represent a promising therapy for patients with psychiatric disorders, demonstrating association with improved outcomes and inflammatory pathways. Importantly, personalizing blue light interventions based on specific diagnoses and seasonal patterns could be linked to better clinical efficacy and optimize patient care Further studies should elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to develop individualized protocols that maximize the therapeutic benefits of blue light therapy in diverse clinical settings.

Limitation

First, causality cannot be inferred from these retrospective findings linking phototherapy with psychiatric, inflammatory and metabolic changes. Without randomization or blinding, the study is susceptible to selection bias and potential confounding factors that were not fully controlled.

Second, although the sample size was relatively large and included a range of psychiatric diagnoses, the heterogeneity of the patient population may have influenced the results. While subgroup analyses by diagnosis were conducted, more detailed stratification and larger samples within each diagnostic category would strengthen the conclusions and allow for more precise assessment of differential treatment effects.

Third, the study lacked detailed information on participant’s circadian rhythms, sleep patterns, and genetic factors such as clock gene polymorphisms, like PER3 genotypes, which are known to influence both psychiatric symptoms and biological markers. These unmeasured variables may have contributed to variability in treatment response and biomarker changes.

Fourth, the absence of standardized assessment of treatment adherence and tolerability limits understanding of the feasibility and safety profile of blue light therapy in this clinical population. Potential adverse effects or discomfort related to blue light exposure were not systematically recorded. Future prospective, randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols, comprehensive circadian and genetic assessments, and systematic safety monitoring are warranted to validate and extend these findings.
