GC–MS profiling and antivirulence potential of limonene-rich Citrus medica cv. ‘Rugosa’ essential oil: in vitro and in silico evaluations
Authors: Emira Noumi, Khulood Fahad Alabbosh, Qusai Alsenani, Najah Alshammari, Mamdouh Alshammari, Talal K. S. Alshaya, Ozgur Ceylan, Karim Hosni, Mejdi Snoussi, Adel Kadri, Vincenzo De Feo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial therapy is a primary approach for treating microbial infections, yet increasing antibiotic resistance continues to hinder effective disease management, highlighting natural products as promising alternative sources of novel anti-infective agents. In this context, Citrus medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO was chemically profiled by GC-MS, and its antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum sensing inhibitory action was firstly tested using both in vitro and computational studies. C. medica cv ‘‘rugosa ‘’ EO revealed a predominance of monoterpene hydrocarbons (79.12%) with limonene (76.15%) as the major component, along with minor compounds such as limonene oxide (3.32%), trans-carvyl acetate (1.93%), nerol (1.75%), cis-carveol (1.37%), myrcene (1.31%), carvacrol (1.14%), and β-bisabolene (1.10%). The EO exhibited potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, with inhibition zones up to 30.00 ± 1.73 mm, surpassing limonene (6.00–25.00 mm) and ampicillin (6.00–8.67 mm), and antifungal effects (12.33–14.33 mm) comparable to amphotericin B (11.67–15.33 mm). MICs values ranged from 0.097–1.562 mg/mL with MBC/MFC ratios mostly 2–8, indicating strong bactericidal and fungicidal activity. The EO also demonstrated dose-dependent antibiofilm inhibition (B. subtilis 84.57%; P. aeruginosa 43.19%), quorum-sensing suppression in C. violaceum (100% at MIC; 62.70% at MIC/2; 34.78% at MIC/4) and reduced swarming motility (77.17–46.74%), highlighting its anti-virulence potential. Computational analyses of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO phytocompounds, particularly valencene (− 9.3 kcal/mol) and aromadendrene (− 8.3 kcal/mol), showed strong binding affinities and stable 200 ns MD interactions, along with favorable ADMET profiles, confirming their drug-like potential. These results indicate that phytochemicals from these medicinal plants possess promising anti-infective and anti-virulence properties and merit further investigation for therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47009-4.

Introduction

Uncontrolled consumption of antibiotics has paved the way for the rapid rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens worldwide, and this escalating issue has become a critical global health emergency, with microbial resistance contributing to nearly 16 million infection-related deaths globally and more than 65% of these cases involving biofilm-forming communities1–5. These organisms are major contributors to a wide spectrum of human infections, including urinary tract infections (Escherichia coli), respiratory infections (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), gastrointestinal illnesses (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter), and sexually transmitted infections (Neisseria gonorrhoeae), in addition to their significant involvement in healthcare-associated infections caused by E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter baumannii marked by high resistance to multiple class of antibiotics6–8. These infections are associated with severe morbidity and mortality, with P. aeruginosais acknowledged as a key opportunistic pathogen for humans having the ability to cause acute and chronic infections representing a serious risk to vulnerable patients9,10. A major determinant of pathogenicity in bacteria is quorum sensing (QS), a density-dependent communication system mediated by autoinducers that regulate virulence, motility, antibiotic resistance, and biofilm formation11–13. Biofilms, complex microbial communities encased in a self-synthesized extracellular matrix, provide substantial protection against host immunity and antibiotics, contributing to chronic and nosocomial infections. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), approximately 65% of microbial infections and up to 80% of chronic infections are associated with biofilm formation14. Biofilm development depends on bacterial species and environmental conditions such as pH, nutrient availability, fluid dynamics, surfactants, and temperature, while increasing cell density enhances QS signaling and biofilm maturation15,16. Because QS plays a central role in coordinating these pathogenic behaviors, and in order to mitigate the development of resistance by suppressing the production of virulence factors, quorum quenching (QQ) has emerged as a promising strategy that interferes with QS pathways via QS inhibitors or QQ enzymes17–19. Quorum sensing, as a regulator of bacterial virulence, remains a promising drug target for these infections.

In this context, plant-derived secondary metabolites offer an environmentally friendly and sustainable approach to inhibit QS and biofilm development, provide therapeutic attributes for next-generation antimicrobial therapies aimed at combating MDR pathogens20–23. In light of these challenges, EOs compounds with antibacterial, anti-QS, and anti-biofilm properties offer a promising alternative Research and hat attracted a great interest as safe and effective to combat microbial infections24–28.Cuminum cyminum L. EO and its active compound, cuminaldehyde, exhibited broad-spectrum anti-QS activity by suppressing QS-regulated flagellar motility in P. aeruginosa27. Similarly, Carum carviEO and its major component, carvone, demonstrated strong dose-dependent inhibition, highlighting their dual ability to disrupt both pigment production and bacterial motility28. It has been reported that EOs interfere with biofilm development by breaking down extracellular matrices and disrupting quorum sensing. In addition, P. barbatus EO suppressed P. aeruginosaPAO1 biofilm formation and remodeled its structure by inhibiting swarming motility and N-acyl homoserine lactones-mediated signaling29.

Accordingly, this study provides, for the first time, a comprehensive assessment of the chemical composition and bioactivities (antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and anti-QS) of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ essential oil, supported by molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, DFT calculations, and ADMET assessment.

Materials and methods

Plant material and essential oil preparation

Fruits from C. medica L. cv. ‘rugosa’ tree (Fig. 1) was collected in January 2025 from local gardens in Hail region (Saudi Arabia). Taxonomical identification of plant species was done by Prof. Vincenzo De Feo, and a voucher specimen (AN 007) was stored in the herbarium of the Biology Department (College of Science, University of Ha’il, Hail, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia). The essential oil was extracted using hydro distillation technique for 3 h, following the procedure described in the European Pharmacopoeia30. For the experiments, freshly collected fruits were cleaned with tap water to remove dust, then peeled using sterile knife. Fruit peels were ground in a Waring lab blender and subjected to hydro distillation using Clevenger apparatus for 3 h. The calculated yield of extraction was about 1.2% calculated on the fresh weight. The obtained volatile oil was solubilized in n-hexane, filtered over anhydrous sodium sulphate and stored under N2 at 4 °C in the dark using 5 mL-dark bottles, until tested and analyzed.

GC-MS profiling of EO

The obtained C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO composition was analyzed using a Varian CP-3800 gas chromatograph coupled with a Saturn 2000 ion trap mass spectrometer. Separation was performed on an HP-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm). Injector and transfer line temperatures were set at 220 °C and 240 °C, respectively. Oven temperature was programmed from 60 °C to 240 °C at 3 °C/min, with helium at 1 mL/min as carrier gas. Samples (0.2 µL, split ratio 1:30) were injected, and compounds were identified via retention times, linear retention indices (LRIs) relative to n-alkanes, and mass spectra comparison with NIST 98, ADAMS 95, and an in-house library31. Molecular weights were confirmed using GC-CIMS with methanol as the ionization reagent.

Biological assays

Antimicrobial activities of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO

The ability of the obtained essential oil from C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ peels was tested using disc diffusion assay for the estimation of growth inhibition zone (expressed in mm) on agar media, and microdilution assays on 96 well microtiter plates for the determination of MICs values (minimal inhibitory concentration, expressed in mg/mL), and MBCs/MFCs values (Minimal bactericidal/fungicidal concentrations, expressed in mg/mL). These protocols are previously published by our team31,33.

Briefly, pure colonies of 24 bacteria isolated from fish and shellfish products were cultivated using Mueller-Hinton agar medium, 5 yeasts (Candida spp.) on Sabouraud chloramphenicol agar, and 2 molds (Aspergillus niger, A. fumigatus ATCC 204305) on potato dextrose agar were used to prepare a homogenous suspension. A cotton swab was used to inoculate fresh Petri dishes. Sterile filter paper discs (6 mm in diameter, Biolife, Italy) were impregnated with ten microliters of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO, its major compound (limonene) and two reference drugs used: Ampicillin (10 mg/mL, 10 µL/disc) and amphotericin B (10 mg/mL, 10 µL/disc). Then, the discs were placed in the cultured plates. The treated Petri dishes were kept for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for bacteria, 48 h for yeasts, and for 3 days for molds. All tests were performed in triplicate and the mean diameter of the inhibition zone was calculated (mGIZ ± SD in mm).

MICs and MBCs/MFCs values were determined using the microdilution assay in 96-well microtiter plates. For the experiment, a stock solution of the obtained essential oil and its main component (200 mg/mL) was prepared in diluted dimethyl sulfoxide solution (DMSO-1%). Then, twofold serial dilutions were prepared in 96-well plates. A volume of 5 µl of the microbial inoculum was added to each well of the microtiter plate containing 0.1 mL of the serially diluted volatile oil, limonene, and reference drugs. Incubation was performed overnight at 37 °C. Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs) were defined as the lowest concentration of compound able to inhibit the growth of the microorganisms. To determine the MBCs/MFCs values, 3 µl from the wells medium with no visible growth were removed and point-inoculated in appropriate agar plates. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the growth of the microorganism was observed. The concentration at which the microorganism was killed (no growth) is recorded as the minimum concentration to kill bacterial and fungal strains tested in this study.

Evaluation of biofilm inhibition

Antibiofilm activity of rugosa EO was tested against several microbial strains including S. aureus, E. faecalis, L. monocytogenes, P. aeruginosa, S. typhimurium, E. coli, C. albicans, and C. tropicalis. MICs were assessed via the broth microdilution method following CLSI guidelines. Bacterial suspensions (5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL) were incubated with serial EO dilutions (5–0.0781 mg/mL) in 96-well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration preventing visible growth. Biofilm formation was assessed in TSB with 0.25% glucose for 48 h at 37 °C under MIC and sub-MIC EO concentrations (1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 MIC). Crystal violet staining and solubilization (33% acetic acid for Gram-positive, 70% ethanol for Gram-negative/fungi) were used to quantify biofilms at 550 nm.

Quorum sensing inhibition (QSI) assay

QSI was evaluated in C. violaceum CV026 on soft top agar with AHL. Wells containing MIC or sub-MIC EO were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days. QS inhibition was indicated by colorless halos against the purple bacterial lawn.

Swarming motility assay

Swarming motility of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was assessed on 0.5% agar plates containing EO at MIC, MIC/2, or MIC/4. Controls lacked C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO. After 18 h at 37 °C, swarming distances were measured to evaluate inhibition.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking studies were performed in order to understand the binding behavior of selected compounds to various protein targets. Six protein structures with PDB IDs 7C7R, 3QP5, 2UV0, 4JVC, 7PTF, and 5TZ1 were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank34–40. Each individual protein was prepared before docking by removing co-crystallized ligands, water molecules and other heteroatoms that are not essential for its function, followed by structural optimization for good docking performance. A total of 39 phytocompounds as well as reference compounds were chosen and prepared for docking analyses. Docking simulations were carried out by using AutoDock Vina software, for this purpose, a suitable grid box was determined around the active site of each target protein for proper ligand sampling. Docking calculations were performed independently for each of the protein targets and the binding affinities were recorded. The top-ranked docked poses were further analyzed in order to detect key protein-ligand interactions and binding patterns41,42.

Molecular docking simulations

In order to assess the dynamic stability and conformational dynamics of the most promising docked complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted with the best performing compounds in complex with the major protein targets. MD simulations were performed with Desmond, one of the modules of the Schrodinger LLC software suite. Protein-ligand complexes were prepared and minimized using the Maestro Protein Preparation Wizard and simulation systems were built using the System Builder tool. Each system was solvated in an orthorhombic simulation box using the TIP3P water model and the appropriate counterions were introduced, in order to neutralize the system. The OPLS 2005 force field was used for all the simulations. Production MD runs were performed, lasting for 200 ns for NPT ensemble conditions and at a temperature of 303.3 K and a pressure of 1 atm. The resulting trajectories were analyzed to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and protein-ligand contact profiles, which can give information on the structural stability and flexibility and the persistence of the protein-ligand interactions34,43.

ADMET and DFT analysis

In addition, ADMET analysis was carried out to predict the pharmacokinetic and toxicity-related properties of selected compounds by ADMET-AI platform. Key parameters involved in absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity were evaluated to calculate the drug-likeness and safety potential of the compounds45. Finally, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed in order to explore the electronic properties and reactivity of the major compounds. All the DFT calculation has been performed with B3LYP functional & 6-31G basis set and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) has been calculated by the calculation of HOMO & LUMO energies41. These quantum chemical descriptors were applied to measure molecular stability, electron distribution and reactivity in order to complement the results obtained from the docking and molecular dynamics methods.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate. GraphPad Prism v10.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, USA) was used to statistically analyze each experiment followed by One way and two-ways comparison of means using Tukey comparison test, at the significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

GC-MS analysis of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO

The results of the GC-MS analysis of C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO composition (Retention Index (RI) and %) were summarized in Table 1; Fig. 2. The overall annotated content reached 99.79%, in which thirty-nine compounds were identified, establishing a limonene-type chemotype (76.15%), followed by limonene oxide (3.32%), trans-carvyl acetate (1.93%), nerol (1.75%), cis-carveol (1.37%), myrcene (1.31%), carvacrol (1.14%), and β-bisabolene (1.10%), with other compounds are present in traces. The oil was classified as monoterpene hydrocarbons (79.12%) followed by oxygenated monoterpenes (17.31%), sesquiterpene hydrocarbons (2.23%), oxygenated sesquiterpenes (0.20%) and others (0.93%) (Table 1, Supplementary material S1). Evaluation of antimicrobial effects

The antimicrobial assay revealed that C. medica cv. ‘rugosa‘ EO exhibits a strong and broad-spectrum inhibitory activity that consistently surpasses that of limonene and the reference antimicrobials (Table 2). The EO produced large mean growth inhibition zones (mGIZ) against major fish and shellfish pathogens, particularly Photobacterium damselae (30.00 ± 1.73 mm), V. fluvialis (29.67 ± 1.53 mm), Aeromonas hydrophila (29.00 ± 1.73 mm), and V. alginolyticus (26.67 ± 1.53 mm). In comparison, limonene generated lower mGIZ values, ranging from 19.00 ± 1.00 to 24.67 ± 0.58 mm. Although limonene exhibited moderate and reproducible antimicrobial activity overall (mGIZ = 6.00–25.00 mm), with pronounced effects against Gram-negative aquatic bacteria such as P. damselae (24.67 ± 0.58 mm) and V. fluvialis (23.67 ± 0.58 mm), its efficacy was limited against resistant strains, including P. aeruginosa (7.00 ± 0 mm). By contrast, ampicillin (AMP) showed weak antibacterial activity against most isolates, with mGIZ values largely restricted to 6.00–8.67 mm, indicating a high level of resistance. Regarding antifungal activity, the EO inhibited Candida spp. with mGIZ values of 12.33–14.33 mm, exceeding those of limonene (10.33–11.67 mm) and approaching the activity of amphotericin B (AMB; 11.67–15.33 mm). Similarly, against filamentous fungi (Aspergillus niger and A. fumigatus), the C. medica cv. ‘rugosa’ EO (11.67–12.33 mm) displayed inhibitory effects comparable to AMB (12.67–13.67 mm) and higher than those observed for limonene (10.67–11.00 mm). Overall, the consistent increase in mGIZ values observed for the EO relative to limonene supports synergistic interactions between limonene and minor EO constituents, highlighting C. medica cv. ‘rugosa‘ EO as a promising natural antimicrobial agent against antibiotic-resistant aquatic pathogens.

Assessment of biofilm inhibition

The ability of pathogens to form robust biofilms underlies their reduced responsiveness to antibiotics, making infections difficult to eradicate. In light of the promising MIC results, we proceeded to assess the capacity of the EO to suppress biofilm development in the selected strains examined in this study. As depicted in Fig. 3, a dose-dependent antibiofilm activity against all tested microorganisms. The concentrations used in this analysis for MIC values were 1.25 (S. aureus), 2.5 (B. subtilis), 10 (E. coli), 0.625 (P. aeruginosa), and 1.25 (C. albicans) µg/mL. Sub-MICs were tested for biofilm inhibition against the tested strains. At the MIC level, EO exhibited its strongest antibiofilm effect against B. subtilis (84.57 ± 1.56%), followed by C. albicans (74.63 ± 0.37%) and S. aureus (69.38 ± 0.53%), whereas lower inhibition was observed for E. coli (59.75 ± 2.25%) and P. aeruginosa (43.19 ± 1.49%). When the concentration was reduced to MIC/2, substantial antibiofilm activity was still maintained, particularly against C. albicans (59.40 ± 1.41%) and B. subtilis (55.64 ± 0.93%). A marked decline in inhibition occurred at MIC/4, with P. aeruginosa (13.80 ± 0.27%) and E. coli (21.17 ± 0.59%) showing the weakest responses. At the lowest tested concentration (MIC/8), only limited inhibition was detected for B. subtilis (15.58 ± 0.74%), S. aureus (10.14 ± 0.29%), and C. albicans (5.07 ± 1.18%), while E. coli and P. aeruginosa exhibited no detectable antibiofilm activity, indicating their comparatively higher resistance.

Insilico prediction of mechanistic pathways

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking enables to simulate and analyze the interactions between ligands and target proteins to elucidate their binding modes and interactions, facilitating the identification of promising therapeutic candidates46–48. Molecular docking analysis was carried out to assess the binding affinity and interaction of selected phytocompounds to the target selected protein. The obtained docking results showed that several compounds have a good binding potential with binding energies from moderate to high affinity for the range of studied targets (Table 4).

Docking analysis of β-eudesmol with the 7C7R protein showed a favorable binding affinity of −6.8 kcal/mol with good interaction network in the binding pocket. The ligand formed hydrogen bonds with Pro379 and Lys381 residues and these interactions were significant in anchoring the molecule in the active site. Additional stabilization was achieved by alkyl interactions with the residues Leu528 and Leu720 and several van der Waals contacts involving the residues Asp378, Glu539, Gly380, Phe527, Tyr526, and Thr722. The closest interaction was noted with Pro379 at a distance of 2.4 Å, suggesting strong accommodation of the ligand. The 3D binding poses clearly show the stable orientation of β-eudesmol in binding cavity of 7C7R (Figs. 7A, 8A). Figures 7 and 8 were generated by using the protein-ligand complex file in Discovery Studio 2025 (https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio). The 2D interaction module was utilized for the interaction diagram.

For the aromadendrene-3QP5 complex, the docking analysis demonstrated a high affinity with the target protein with a strong binding energy value of −8.3 kcal/mol. Aromadendrene interacted with twelve residues and predominantly with alkyl interactions with Ile153, Leu100, Tyr80, Leu57, Trp84, Leu85 and Tyr88 creating a hydrophobic interaction core. Additional stabilization was given by van der Waals contacts to Ser155, Leu72 and Met89, Val75 and Met135. The nearest interacting residue was Tyr88 (3.9 Å) which supports compact and stable binding mode. These interactions are well visualized in the 3D profile illustrated in Figs. 7B, 8B.

ADMET predictions

Natural compounds must meet standard pharmacokinetic requirements, and in silico methods enable rapid, cost-effective evaluation of ADMET properties to identify promising candidates early in drug development49–51. The ADMET profiling of the identified compounds revealed the overall favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics supporting the potential of the compounds, as bioactive agents (Supplementary material S2). As summarized in the ADMET table, most compounds met the Lipinski’s Rule of five, as well as acceptable molecular weights, and lipophilicity (Log P values are mostly within the acceptable range). High predicted human intestinal absorption was observed for the majority of molecules, which is indicative of good oral bioavailability. In addition, aqueous solubility and hydration free energy values indicated an appropriate hydrophilic-lipophilic profile as vital for effective distribution in the system. Toxicological and metabolic predictions were also consistent with the drug-likeness of compounds. The majority had low clinical toxicity and acceptable acute toxicity (LD50) values and with no significant carcinogenic risk predicted. Cytochrome P450 interaction analysis showed low levels of inhibition with most compounds for key cytochrome P450 isoforms (CYP2C9 and CYP2C19) indicating low risk for metabolic interference. Collectively, these results in ADMET studies indicate the selected phytocompounds have favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profiles that add strength to the compounds for further experimental validation.

DFT calculations

DFT calculations were done to examine the electronic properties and reactivity of 4 selected compounds, trans-carvyl acetate, valencene, β-bisabolene, and β-eudesmol (Fig. 12). Figure 12 was generated by using the chk files to evaluate the HOMO and LUMO sites in Gauss View 6 (https://gaussian.com/gaussview6/). The FMO analysis gave an insight to the stability and chemical reactivity of these compounds by assessing their HOMO and LUMO energy levels. Trans-carvyl acetate showed the HOMO energy of −6.04 eV and the LUMO energy of −0.13 eV with 5.9 eV energy gap indicating moderate electronic stability and reactivity. Valencene displayed a relatively lower energy gap of 5.3 eV that resulted from the HOMO and LUMO energies − 6.13 eV and − 0.80 eV, respectively, suggesting high chemical reactivity among the analyzed compounds.

In contrast, β-bisabolene showed greater energy gap of 6.7 eV with the HOMO and LUMO energy of −6.12 and 0.62 eV reflecting increased molecular stability. Similarly, β-eudesmol showed the highest energy gap 7.1 eV with HOMO and LUMO values (−6.30 eV and 0.86 eV respectively) and represented high electronic stability and lower reactivity. The spatial distribution HOMO and LUMO orbitals showed distinct regions of electrons rich and electron deficient which support the difference in reactivity and stability. These results complement the results obtained by the docking and molecular dynamics methods by introducing a quantum chemical perspective on the behavior of the selected compounds.

Discussion

The increasing incidence of multidrug-resistant bacterial outbreaks in healthcare settings highlights the need for alternative antimicrobial agents from natural products that target biofilm formation (critical factor for food safety) and quorum sensing52,53. This study rigorously emphasizes the antivirulence efficacy of citrus EOs and its principal bioactive constituent, limonene, against P. aeruginosa strains. The first main compound found in this oil was limonene, which represents more than three quarters of the EO composition.

P. aeruginosa is a highly pathogenic, biofilm-forming opportunistic bacterium responsible for nearly 65–80% of nosocomial infections, primarily due to its regulation of quorum-QS, motility, adhesion, and virulence-associated genes. In P. aeruginosa, the QS system, mediated by signaling molecules, controls biofilm development and the production of various virulence factors54. Many citrus (most popular world fruit crops) cultivars have been widely demonstrated for their antivirulence effect. In fact, the evaluation of the antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties of Citruschangshan-huyou Y.B. EO and its mode of action have been reported55.Citrus reticulata EOs inhibit has been shown to inhibit P. aeruginosa biofilm formation through the decrease of QS and this inhibition may be due to total of EOs compounds more than the limonene56. The antibacterial, antibiofilm activity of EO from C. medica L. var. sarcodactylis and its nanoemulsion have been also demonstrated57. Pompia and grapefruit citrus EOs have been shown to effectively inhibited bacterial and fungal biofilms, including mixed-species biofilms, enhanced the activity of conventional antimicrobials, disrupted quorum sensing in P. aeruginosa, and increased membrane permeability in C. albicans, highlighting their potential for controlling polymicrobial infections58. Gao et al.59 studied the anti-biofilm activity of EO from C. medica L. var. sarcodactylis Swingle and suggested a significant inhibition towards the biofilm formation of L. monocytogenes, C. paradisi, and C. reticulataEOs at 0.125% (v/v) have been shown to inhibit significantly the in vitro biofilm formation of PAO1 and PA14 strains between 40 and 50%60. Abbad et al.61 stated that a mixture of mixtures of C. sinensis with thyme EOs induced a significant inhibition of biofilm formation reaching 74.96% at sub-MIC. The efficacy of the mandarin peel EO containing 75.16% limonene was evaluated for antibiofilm activity against (A) nigerwith the effect on the development of black mold in onion bulbs in both curative and preventive applications62. Castillo et al.63 have been reported the ability of citrus extracts to inhibit the activity of AI-2 molecules that mediate QS in Campylobacter jejuni by decreased autoinducer production by about 90%. Meanwhile, an alternative lemon EO did not affect violacein production in C. violaceum, implying that the QS pathway remains unaffected64. The C. medica L. var. sarcodactylishas been also demonstrated for its antibacterial and antibiofilm activity65. A notable inhibitory effect of C. bergemia flower EO was observed against (B) paramycoides and B. chungangensis. In addition, pronounced antibiofilm (52.1–69.5%) and QS inhibitory effects were detected, increasing in a dose-dependent manner66. Moreover, Khanem and co-workers reported that lemon, lemon balm, and lavender essential oils disrupt E. colibiofilms through cellular damage and are effective against resistant strains67. The same study demonstrates that these oils remove mature biofilms at concentrations ranging from 3% to 82.25% and exhibit antibacterial activity at low concentrations (MIC 3.13–50 µg/mL)67.

The computational analyses show that phytocompounds from Rosa rugosa, especially valencene (−9.3 kcal/mol to 2UV0 and 5TZ1), aromadendrene (−8.3 kcal/mol to 3QP5) and the β-bisabolene, have high binding affinities and stable dynamics over 200 ns of MD simulations, via hydrophobic, alkyl and pi-interactions with critical active-site residues, illustrating their medicinal potential against the target proteins (PDB: 7C7R, 3QP5, 2UV0, 4JVC, 7PT These results are consistent with favorable ADMET profiles such as high intestinal absorption, Lipinski compliance and low toxicity risks along with positive results from DFT-derived stability making them promising drug-like candidates for experimental validation. This work extends previous evidence of the bioactives in rugosa against Nrf2 activation and antioxidant defense through a docking-validated Keap1 inhibition and the proven antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory sesquiterpenes to imply broader use in disorders linked to oxidative stress68,69. Future in vitro/in vivo assays and lead optimization is warranted for the translation of these in silico insights into the clinical.

Conclusions

Antimicrobial therapy remains a cornerstone for infection management, yet rising antibiotic resistance highlights the need for alternative natural agents. In this context, C. medica var. ‘rugosa’ EO phytocompounds have shown significant therapeutic potential. The EO, dominated by monoterpene hydrocarbons with limonene as the major constituent, robust efficacy of the essential oil in suppressing bacterial communication, underscoring its promise as a natural quorum-quenching and anti-virulence agent, surpassing limonene alone. Similarly, rugosa compounds such as valencene, aromadendrene, and β-bisabolene demonstrated strong binding affinities and stable interactions with key target proteins, supported by molecular dynamics simulations and hydrophobic, alkyl, and pi interactions. These compounds also displayed favorable ADMET properties, including good absorption, drug-likeness, low toxicity, and DFT-confirmed stability. Together, these findings reinforce previous evidence of Nrf2 activation, antioxidant defense via Keap1 inhibition, and antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities, suggesting broad therapeutic applications for oxidative stress-related and infectious disorders, demonstrating strong interference with cell-to-cell signaling and supporting its potential as a promising anti-virulence agent and warranting further in vitro, in vivo, and lead-optimization studies to translate these natural biomolecules into clinical use.
