Trends in Enterobacteriaceae Resistance Patterns: A Nine‐Year Retrospective Study From North Lebanon
Authors: Mazen Zaylaa, Maya El Bitar, Ali Jaafar, Majd Hammoud, Hiba El Chakra, Abdul Rahman Maaliki, Farah El Dali, Dina Awad, Yasmine Hijazi, Omar El Gharbawy, Ricardo Sarraf, Ibtihal Kassam, Diala Wehbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Enterobacteriaceae poses a critical threat to global health, particularly in regions where antibiotic misuse and limited surveillance prevail. Intensive care units (ICUs) act as focal points for resistance emergence and dissemination. This study aimed to evaluate long‐term resistance trends among clinically significant Enterobacteriaceae isolated from two hospitals in North Lebanon (2016–2024).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 14,013 Enterobacteriaceae isolates obtained from Al Monla and Dar Al Shifa hospitals between 2016 and 2024. Identification was achieved using biochemical testing or MALDI‐TOF mass spectrometry, and susceptibility testing followed EUCAST standards. No molecular characterization of resistance genes was performed. Data were analyzed using SPSS v23.0, applying Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong 14,013 isolates, Escherichia coli (E. coli) was predominant (66.4%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) (12.4%), Proteus mirabilis (P. mirabilis) (5.5%), and Enterobacter cloacae (E. cloacae) (3.7%). Overall, β‐lactam resistance remained widespread across species. In E. coli, carbapenem non‐susceptibility increased from 2.7% to 4.8% (p < 0.001). P. mirabilis and E. cloacae exhibited significant increases in ESBL production (p = 0.006 and p = 0.007, respectively). K. pneumoniae showed fluctuating resistance trends with a mild decline in carbapenem resistance (p = 0.029). ICU isolates demonstrated higher ESBL and MDR rates than non‐ICU isolates (p < 0.01), and hospital‐acquired infections showed significantly higher resistance across most drug classes compared to community‐acquired cases (p < 0.05).ConclusionOver the 9‐year study period, Enterobacteriaceae isolates in North Lebanon demonstrated persistently high β‐lactam resistance and increasing carbapenem non‐susceptibility, particularly in E. coli. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing AMR surveillance and provide regional data that may support future evidence‐based clinical and public health decision‐making.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a fundamental evolutionary response that enables microorganisms to survive under selective pressure imposed by antimicrobial agents [1]. AMR has emerged as a major global health threat, hindering the effectiveness of treatment when it comes to severe infection in the whole world. Global surveillance of AMR has shown that it was associated with 4.94 million deaths in 2019 and it is expected to rise to 8.22 million by 2050 [2].

The primary driver of AMR remains the widespread and often inappropriate use of antibiotics in both community and healthcare settings. Factors such as limited public awareness, unrestricted access to over‐the‐counter antibiotics, and poor adherence to prescribed regimens significantly contribute to resistance development [3]. In addition, inadequate prescription of antibiotics by healthcare professionals contribute to the rise in AMR [4]. Moreover, the misuse of antibiotics in treating viral infections, which has no beneficial clinical outcome. This was especially evident during COVID‐19 pandemic that exacerbated the irrational use of antibiotics due to the lack of clear guidelines regarding the management of patients and even more so the ones with severe cases [5].

Intensive care units (ICUs) have frequently been described as epicenters for the development and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. This is especially problematic considering the vulnerable patient population of ICUs but also impacts on other hospital wards and even the community, as patients are frequently transferred between wards and eventually discharged [6]. Among the most concerning pathogens in this setting are members of the Enterobacteriaceae family—Gram‐negative, rod‐shaped bacteria that are major causes of both hospital‐ and community‐acquired infections. Enterobacteriaceae exhibit diverse resistance mechanisms, notably the production of β‐lactamases, including extended‐spectrum β‐lactamases (ESBLs), AmpC‐type cephalosporinases, and carbapenemases [7]. Bacteria acquire resistance through two main genetic events, one of which is mutation in chromosomal genes which increases the intrinsic resistance mechanisms, or by horizontal transfer of mobile genetic elements which is the main culprit in the development of Enterobacteriaceae carrying plasmid‐encoding for beta lactamase [8].

Clinically, Enterobacteriaceae are responsible for a broad spectrum of infections, such as bacteremia, urinary tract infections, pneumonia, and gastrointestinal diseases [9]. The emergence of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) strains in hospital environments poses a critical challenge for patient management, resulting in prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality [10].

Despite growing global attention to antimicrobial resistance, Lebanon still lacks robust longitudinal, multicenter surveillance data on Enterobacteriaceae resistance trends, particularly outside Beirut and Mount Lebanon. Most available local studies are single‐center, short‐term, organism‐specific, or focused on selected resistance phenotypes, which limits their ability to describe temporal trends, compare care settings, or inform regional stewardship policies. In addition, data that stratify resistance patterns by ICU versus non‐ICU settings and hospital‐acquired versus community‐acquired infections remain limited in the Lebanese context. North Lebanon represents a distinct epidemiological setting because it serves a heterogeneous population with varying healthcare access, high community antibiotic exposure, and referral of critically ill patients to regional hospitals, all of which may influence local resistance patterns. However, AMR surveillance data from this region remain underrepresented in the literature. In this context, the present study provides a 9‐year, multicenter retrospective analysis (2016–2024) of clinically significant Enterobacteriaceae isolates from two hospitals in North Lebanon. By combining long‐term trend analysis with stratification by ICU versus non‐ICU and hospital‐ versus community‐acquired infections, this study adds regional epidemiological data on phenotypic resistance patterns in an underrepresented area of Lebanon. These findings may contribute to a better understanding of local resistance dynamics and help guide future surveillance efforts and empirical treatment strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective study analyzed antimicrobial susceptibility data from bacterial isolates obtained in the microbiology laboratories of two hospitals in North Lebanon, Al Monla and Dar Al Shifa, between January 2016 and December 2024.

Data Collection and Sample Size

An electronic search of the laboratory information systems of Al Monla and Dar Al Shifa hospitals was conducted to retrieve all culture specimens collected between January 2016 and December 2024. Specimens included urine, blood, respiratory secretions, body fluids, wound/pus, and other clinical samples. These isolates originated from diverse patient populations, including pediatric and adult patients, critically ill individuals, and represented both community‐acquired and hospital‐acquired infections.

Deduplication and unit of analysis: The statistical unit of analysis was the deduplicated bacterial isolate. Deduplication was performed using patient identifiers. For non‐bloodstream specimens, duplicate isolates were defined as isolates of the same organism recovered from the same patient within a 3‐month period and showing an identical antimicrobial susceptibility pattern; only the first isolate was retained for analysis [11]. For bloodstream isolates, the same criteria were applied, but using a 14‐day interval to better distinguish repeated sampling from potentially distinct bloodstream infection episodes. No formal adjustment for within‐patient clustering was performed. Basic specimen data included demographics (age, sex), culture date, specimen site, and patient setting. Infection classification was recorded as community‐acquired, hospital‐acquired, or ICU‐acquired. Inpatient isolates were defined as specimens obtained from patients admitted at the time of collection, whereas outpatient isolates were those collected from patients seen in outpatient clinics or without hospitalization. After deduplication, a total of 14,013 isolates were included: 7,264 from Al Monla Hospital and 6,752 from Dar Al Shifa Hospital. Data from all positive cultures were included regardless of patient characteristics, including age, underlying medical conditions, or hospitalization status.

Enterobacteriaceae Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The isolates included in the study were identified using biochemical gallery tests (API 20E; BioMérieux, Marcy‐l’Étoile, France) or by MALDI‐TOF mass spectrometry (BioMérieux). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby‐Bauer disk diffusion method. All laboratory procedures followed the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST 2024) guidelines for microbiological methods and interpretive criteria (EUCAST: Breakpoint Tables, Version 14.0, 2024). The antibiotics tested included penicillins (amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin, and piperacillin/tazobactam), cephalosporins (cefalotin, cefuroxime, cefoxitin, cefixime, ceftazidime, and cefepime), carbapenems (ertapenem and imipenem), quinolones (nalidixic acid and ciprofloxacin), sulfonamides (trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole), and aminoglycosides (amikacin and gentamicin). All isolates were tested against these antibiotics to determine susceptibility patterns and guide the classification of resistance phenotypes.

Phenotypic Detection of ESBL, CRE, and MDR Enterobacteriaceae

Resistance to third‐generation cephalosporins (3GC), specifically Cefixime (10 µg) and/or Ceftazidime (30 µg), was used to screen for potential Extended‐Spectrum Beta‐Lactamase (ESBL) production. Confirmatory ESBL detection was performed using either the Double‐Disk Synergy Test (DDST) or the Combination Disk Test (CDT), with Clavulanic acid enhancement indicating ESBL activity.

Isolates exhibiting resistance to third‐generation cephalosporins but negative for ESBL production were further evaluated as cephalosporinase‐producing strains, based on characteristic resistance patterns (e.g., high‐level resistance to cephalosporins, preserved susceptibility to carbapenems).

Resistance to Ertapenem (10 µg) and/or Imipenem (10 µg) was used as a phenotypic marker for CRE, following CDC definitions. Resistance to Ertapenem alone was considered sufficient to classify an isolate as CRE in settings where confirmatory carbapenemase detection was not performed. MDR classification was based on CDC criteria, defining isolates as MDR if they exhibited acquired non‐susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes.

Statistical Analysis

Data were coded, validated, and analyzed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi‐square (χ 2) test, with a p‐value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. When expected cell counts were less than 5, the two‐sided Fisher's exact test was applied.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Beirut Arab University under approval number (2025‐H‐0155‐M‐0709). All data were collected retrospectively from medical records with the approval of both hospitals with full anonymization, without the inclusion of any personal identifiers, to maintain patient confidentiality and adhere to ethical standards.

Results

Prevalence and Age Distribution

Of the 14,013 isolates, E. coli was the predominant organism (66.4%, n = 9,300), followed by K. pneumoniae (12.4%, n = 1,744), P. mirabilis (5.5%, n = 777), E. cloacae (3.7%, n = 513), and other organisms (12.0%, n = 1,679). Therefore, the present study focused on the 12,334 isolates corresponding to the four most common organisms (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. mirabilis, and E. cloacae). Regarding specimen type, the most common source was urine (n = 9,480; 67.65%), followed by wound and pus (n = 2,921; 20.84%), blood (n = 488; 3.48%), respiratory specimens (n = 461; 3.29%), others (n = 515; 3.67%), body fluids (n = 134; 0.97%), and not provided (n = 14; 0.1%) (Table 1). In urine specimens (n = 9,480), E. coli was the most frequently isolated organism (79.8%, n = 7,562), followed by K. pneumoniae (10.4%, n = 982), P. mirabilis (3.8%, n = 358), and E. cloacae (1.4%, n = 132). In respiratory specimens (n = 461), K. pneumoniae was the most common isolate (32.3%, n = 149), followed by E. coli (28.0%, n = 129), E. cloacae (8.2%, n = 38), and P. mirabilis (5.4%, n = 25). In blood specimens (n = 488), E. coli was the most frequently identified organism (50.4%, n = 246), followed by K. pneumoniae (16.0%, n = 78), E. cloacae (10.0%, n = 49), and P. mirabilis (3.5%, n = 17). In wound and pus specimens (n = 2,921), E. coli remained the most common isolate (39.0%, n = 1,139), followed by K. pneumoniae (16.2%, n = 474), P. mirabilis (11.6%, n = 340), and E. cloacae (8.6%, n = 252). Table 2 presents the distribution of bacterial isolates across different age groups. Overall, E. coli was the predominant pathogen, accounting for 66.4% of all isolates (n = 9,300), followed by K. pneumoniae (12.4%), P. mirabilis (5.5%), E. cloacae (3.7%), and other Gram‐negative bacteria (12.0%). The highest proportion of isolates was obtained from patients aged 65 years and older (41.5%), followed by those aged 20–64 years (39.6%). In all age groups, E. coli remained the leading organism, with the highest frequency observed in the 5–19 years category (74.8%). K. pneumoniae and P. mirabilis were more common in adult and elderly patients, while E. cloacae and other species showed lower but consistent distributions across all ages (Table 2).

Antimicrobial Resistance Results

Figure 1 and Table 3 illustrate the temporal trends in antimicrobial resistance among major Gram‐negative bacteria isolated between 2016 and 2024. In E. coli, ESBL production remained relatively stable (mean = 38.4%, p = 0.183). Carbapenem‐non‐susceptible also rose (2.7% to 4.8%, p < 0.001), whereas MDR isolates remained stable around 62% (p = 0.060). For K. pneumoniae, ESBL rates fluctuated (means = 31.0%) without significant trends, while carbapenem‐non‐susceptible showed a mild decline (p = 0.029). MDR prevalence remained moderate at 51.1%. In P. mirabilis, ESBL production increased significantly from 13.7% to 28.1% (p = 0.006), with MDR rates remaining high (mean = 65.2%, p = 0.005). For E. cloacae, ESBL production rose significantly (p = 0.007), while carbapenem‐non‐susceptible rates were relatively stable. Similarly, in Proteus spp., ESBL and MDR rates were consistently elevated throughout the study period, reflecting ongoing resistance challenges in this genus.

Table 4 presents the yearly resistance trends of E. coli from 2016 to 2024. Resistance was highest to amoxicillin (mean = 74.0%) and piperacillin (73.9%), followed by amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (56.7%) and cephalosporins (mean range: 43.4%–58.7%). Carbapenem‐non‐susceptible remained low (ertapenem 3.5%, imipenem 2.2%) but showed a rising trend (p < 0.001). Fluoroquinolone resistance averaged 42.7%–60.6%, while trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole resistance was 47.9%. Among aminoglycosides, resistance to amikacin was lowest (5.9%), and gentamicin showed moderate resistance (21.5%), both declining significantly over time (p < 0.001). Table 5 summarizes the resistance profile of K. pneumoniae from 2016 to 2024. Moderate resistance occurred to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (47.4%) and piperacillin/tazobactam (42.7%), both showing decreasing trends. Among cephalosporins, resistance ranged between 38.5% and 45.4%, while Carbapenem‐non‐susceptible remained low (< 2%). Fluoroquinolone resistance declined steadily (nalidixic acid 29.7%, ciprofloxacin 27.9%), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance averaged 40.9%. Aminoglycoside resistance remained relatively low (amikacin 5.7%, gentamicin 20.4%), both showing downward trends over time. Between 2016 and 2024, P. mirabilis isolates showed moderate to high resistance to most β‐lactams and quinolones, with notable increases in amoxicillin, cefuroxime, and ciprofloxacin resistance (p < 0.01). Carbapenem‐non‐susceptible remained rare, while trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole and gentamicin resistance persisted at moderate levels throughout the study period (Table 6). Between 2016 and 2024, E. cloacae isolates showed consistently high resistance to most penicillins and first‐generation cephalosporins, with 100% resistance to amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, and cefoxitin. Moderate resistance was observed to cefuroxime, showing a decreasing trend over time (p < 0.01). Carbapenem‐non‐susceptible remained low (< 10%), while quinolone and sulfonamide resistance showed mild fluctuations without significant change. Aminoglycoside resistance was generally low and stable throughout the study period (Table 7).

Discussion

Our study represents the first large scale surveillance of Enterobacteriaceae in North Lebanon, providing valuable insights into regional antimicrobial resistance (AMR) trends. Among Enterobacteriaceae, E. coli and K. pneumoniae especially stand out as the leading causes of infections worldwide, which is primarily due to their increased resistance to multiple antibiotics [12]. This trend was consistent with our findings in North Lebanon. Our data showed that infections were most frequent in individuals over 65 years, likely due to age‐related declines in immune cell function rather than a reduced white blood cell count [13]. Older adults are also more susceptible to infections with multidrug‐resistant organisms such as Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Vancomycin‐Resistant Enterococci, and carbapenem‐resistant Enterobacteriaceae, owing to increased antibiotic use, comorbidities, frequent healthcare exposure, and immunosenescence [14].

Our dataset identified the urinary tract as the primary site of infection (67.65%), predominantly caused by E. coli (79.8%), consistent with previous findings [15, 16]. Coming in second is wound and pus (20.84%) with a hierarchy consistent with regional wound‐site surveillance, E. coli (39%), other (24.5%), and K. pneumoniae (16.2%) [17], E. coli (39%). However, in respiratory infections, K. pneumoniae was the preceding cause (32.3%) similar to another study in China with 40% [18]. To note that P. mirabilis and E. cloacae were mostly found in wound and pus (11.6%) and body fluids (11.9%) respectively contrasting the findings in Beirut as they were mostly found [16]. Also, 41.5% of cases occurred in patients older than 65% and 39.6% in those aged 20–64, with E. coli remaining the leading pathogen in both groups, consistent with the results found in a retrospective study done in a hospital in China [19].

Concerning antimicrobial resistance, a major finding was E. coli showing an increase in ESBL production from 35.8% in 2016% to 40% in 2021 which was similar to the results of a study done in Geitaoui hospital which included data from 2017 to 2020 [16]. Regarding ESBL production in K. pneumoniae, the mean production over the years is 30.98%, consistent with findings from other studies [15, 16]. A study from the UAE reported that among E. coli and ESKAPE pathogens, E. coli and K. pneumoniae showed the highest resistance rates, with E. coli increasing significantly from 36.2% to 75% between 2000 and 2020 [20]. Furthermore, it is important to note the presence of a significantly higher number of ESBL producing P. mirabilis in north Lebanon (25.38%) compared to Beirut (6.8%) [16]. In E. cloacae, only a small proportion of ESBL producers was detected; however, the global rise of ESBL‐encoding E. cloacae, particularly in hospital settings and among patients with prior antibiotic use, remains a growing concern [21].

Another globally observed threat is the presence of Carbapenem resistant strains of Enterobacteriaceae [12]. Overall, we are seeing an increase in resistance rates, as the average percentages for P. mirabilis, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, E. cloacae were 1.7%, 3.6%, 3.6% and 7.4%, respectively. These results align with findings across the region of the Middle East as well as multiple countries in Europe notably Belarus, Ukraine, Greece, and Russia [22, 23, 24]. The relatively low baseline seen in North Lebanon provides a valuable chance to limit the spread of resistance through strong infection control before rates rise to match regional levels. Of note, E. cloacae showed the highest proportion of carbapenem‐resistant isolates, a pattern also described in studies from the United States, pointing to a potentially emerging global health concern [21, 25]. A study conducted in Tabriz, Iran from 2018 to 2019 showed that the isolates mainly K. pneumonia and E. coli were resistant to at least three of the used carbapenems that were imipenem, meropenem and ertapenem [26]. Another study performed in California hospitals from 2014 to 2017 demonstrated that the proportion of E. coli expressing carbapenem resistance has increased by 24% per year [27].

Another point worth discussing is the overall increase in resistance of E. coli to all cephalosporins generations. Especially 3rd Generation Cephalosporins are showing the highest resistance in 2024 as it is progressively increasing from 47% in 2016% to 56% in 2024, which parallels the reduction in susceptibility noted in studies between 2011 and 2016 highlighting the consistent pattern of rising antibiotic resistance [15, 28]. Similar increase in resistance was also observed in P. mirabilis and K. pneumoniae. A study conducted in Australia showed that 97% and 24.5% of E. coli and K. pneumoniae respectively were resistant to Ceftriaxone and Cefoxitin [29]. No significant change in E. cloacae resistance was observed. Resistance to third‐generation cephalosporins greatly limits treatment options for gram‐negative infections, leaving carbapenems as the main therapy—yet their efficacy continues to decline [30].

Focusing on ICU and non‐ICU infections, our study found that ESBL‐producing E. coli were more frequent in ICU patients (46.7%) than in non‐ICU patients (41.2%). Although other studies considered ICU patients only, the overall ESBL rates for E. coli in the ICU ranged from 43.2% in Turkey to 61.1%–64% in India and Ethiopia [31, 32, 33]. Meanwhile ESBL‐producing K. pneumoniae accounted for 33.6%, which lies at the lower end compared with other countries 38.5% in Turkey and 82.8% in Ethiopia [31, 32]. Carbapenem resistance remained relatively low across species: E. coli (4.5%), K. pneumoniae (3.6%), P. mirabilis (3.9%), and E. cloacae (8.6%). These findings are comparable to a study from New Jersey, which reported rates of 0.3%, 2.8%, 0.5%, and 5.4%, respectively [34]. In contrast to other studies which reported higher rates of K. pneumoniae up to 68.4% in Turkey [35]. Also, MDR exceeded 50% across all four species. These findings align with international reports, including data from Nepal showing resistance rates of 87.16% in E. coli and 84.21% in K. pneumoniae [36]. Similarly, P. mirabilis showed a resistance rate of 82.5% in Iran [37]. Regional differences were also noted, with 37.6% of E. cloacae isolates identified as MDR in Saudi Arabia [38], while in Ethiopia, all P. mirabilis isolates were reported as non‐MDR [31]. The lack of a significant difference in resistance rates between ICU and non‐ICU isolates is concerning, as it may reflect horizontal transmission or emerging community spread. On the other hand, the rise compared with previous regional studies may be linked to a higher burden of comorbidities in ICU patients, use of immunosuppressive therapy, overuse or prolonged use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, and inadequate adherence to infection control measures, including poor compliance with hand hygiene and isolation precautions, especially during pandemics.

Hospital‐acquired infections showed notably higher antimicrobial resistance than community‐acquired ones, particularly in E. coli. ESBL‐producing E. coli were more frequent in hospital settings (41.8%) compared to the community (32.3%). Similarly, hospital isolates displayed higher multidrug resistance rates (66.3% vs. 54.3%). These findings are consistent with the Chinese SMART study (2016–2017), where ESBL‐producing E. coli were more prevalent in hospital IAIs (51.7%) compared to community cases (42.4%) [39, 40]. Similarly, K. pneumoniae showed a significant difference in ESBL production between hospital‐acquired (32.2%) and community‐acquired (27.5%) isolates, paralleling international trends observed in SMART Europe [41].

Multiple factors play a role in the increase of AMR in Lebanon. According to the literature, antibiotic abuse is the main culprit, and an association between ESBL production as well as carbapenem resistance was reported in multiple studies [42, 43]. This is further evidence of the need to implement new systems and guidelines to combat this threat [44]. This was demonstrated in a prospective study done in Saudi Arabia, where they implemented an AMS program in a 1000‐bed hospital, and their results showed a significant decrease in infections related to resistant bacteria [45].

The findings of this study provide regional epidemiological data on resistance trends and priority Enterobacteriaceae pathogens in North Lebanon. These results add to the current understanding of local AMR patterns and may help inform future surveillance efforts and empirical treatment strategies. Addressing the limited AMS infrastructure in Lebanon is essential to curb rising resistance and align with global containment goals. This study has some limitations, including the absence of genetic analysis of resistant strains, missing data due to its retrospective design, and lack of information on antibiotic use.

Although international comparisons provide useful epidemiological context, they should be interpreted with caution because differences in susceptibility breakpoint systems (e.g., EUCAST vs. CLSI), resistance definitions, study design, and laboratory methods may limit direct comparability across studies. Regional variability also limits the generalization of findings across Lebanon, and causality cannot be inferred. Nevertheless, the large number of isolates offers valuable insight into the current AMR situation in North Lebanon. The absence of national surveillance data for Enterobacteriaceae between 2021 and 2024 further underscores the need for ongoing monitoring and research to better understand and manage antibiotic resistance locally and regionally.

Conclusion

This study provides important regional insight into the evolving patterns of antimicrobial resistance in North Lebanon. The observed resistance trends underscore the importance of ongoing surveillance and provide epidemiological data that may support empirical treatment decisions and future healthcare planning. The longitudinal nature of the data also contributes to a better understanding of local resistance dynamics over time.
