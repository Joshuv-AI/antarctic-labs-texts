Cryptosporidium parvum multidrug resistance protein confers resistance to toxic gut microbial metabolite
Authors: Wanyi Huang, Rui Xu, Abigail Kimball, William H Witola, Megan T Baldridge, Yaoyu Feng, Lihua Xiao, L David Sibley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCryptosporidium parvum subtypes differ in pathogenicity, but the underlying factors are largely unknown. We show that two genetically similar C. parvum isolates grow equally well in vitro but differ in pathogenicity in immunocompromised mice. Reduced oocyst shedding of the avirulent strain was restored by antibiotic treatment, suggesting susceptibility to colonization resistance imparted by the microbiota. This resistance was associated with a gene encoding a parasite ABC transporter and enhanced infectivity. Molecular analyses indicate that the ABC transporter belongs to a multidrug resistance protein (MRP) family. CpMRP1 binds bacterial metabolites, notably deoxycholic acid (DCA) that inhibits C. parvum growth. CpMRP1 is exported from small granules to the parasite-host interface, potentially mediating the export of xenobiotics. Loss of CpMRP1 reduces infectivity and DCA resistance in mice, and CpMRP1 polymorphisms across isolates determine susceptibility to DCA. These results define CpMRP1 as a determinant of C. parvum sensitivity to microbiome-mediated inhibition, thereby influencing infectivity.

INTRODUCTION

Cryptosporidium is a wide-spread enteric pathogen that infects animals and humans and causes severe diarrheal disease in children, especially in developing countries.1,2 More than 45 species of Cryptosporidium have been described, some of which have a narrow host range, while others have much broader host ranges.3 Most human infections are caused by C. hominis, which is spread primarily human to human, or C. parvum, which infects a variety of farm animals and causes zoonotic infections in humans.3–5 Within C. parvum, more than 20 subtype families have been recognized based on sequence analysis of the 60-kDa glycoprotein encoding gene (GP60).6 Among them, IIa is a highly abundant type, which circulates in farm animals and commonly infects humans in North America and Europe.3,7

Although the mouse is not a natural host for C. parvum, immunocompromised mice are susceptible, thus allowing studies to dissect the importance of immune factors that control infection. Interferon (IFN) gamma (Ifng−/−, GKO),8,9 IFN gamma receptor 1 (Ifngr1−/−),10 and interleukin-12 (IL-12) (Il-12−/−)11,12 knockout (KO) mice are all highly susceptible to infection with C. parvum. Additionally, mice lacking conventional dendritic cells (cDC1) are susceptible to infection with C. parvum, likely because of the role of cDCs in producing IL-12.13 Such murine models have shown variation in infectivity and lethality between C. parvum isolates with different subtypes,14–16 and between different isolates of the same subtype.14,17 Comparative genomic analyses suggest that copy-number variation and sequence polymorphism in subtelemeric genes may be responsible for differences in infectivity between C. parvum isolates, including genes encoding SKSR, insulinase-like proteases, MEDLE proteins, mucin glycoproteins, ATP binding casette (ABC) transporters, and secretory proteins.14,17,18 However, these genes are largely untested by experimental techniques, and thus their functions remain conjectural.

In addition to the immune system, changes in the composition and diversity of the microbiome also significantly influence the incidence and severity of the infection.19–23 Although adult wild-type mice are resistant to C. parvum, antibiotic treatment substantially increases susceptibility,24 suggesting that the normal microbiota provides colonization resistance. Consistent with the importance of the endogenous flora, neonatal mice,19,25,26 calves,27,28 and human infants1,2 are highly susceptible to C. parvum infection. There are two contributing mechanisms underlying this susceptibility: (1) the microbiota primes the immune system to respond to enteric pathogens,29 (2) metabolites produced by the microbiota affect Cryptosporidium growth, including the indole and secondary bile acids.22,30 Regardless of the relative contribution of these two host resistance factors, the contribution of C. parvum genetic variability to survival in the hostile intestinal environment is poorly understood.

Conventional genetic crosses with linkage mapping have identified candidate genes associated with external environments in other apicomplexans. Studies in Toxoplasma gondii and Plasmodium falciparum (P. falciparum) have largely relied on isolation of recombinant progeny clones, genotyping and phenotyping them individually.31,32 However, bulk segregant analysis (BSA) provides a powerful alternative, as it relies on composite analysis of pools of recombinant phenotypes and genotypes, typically by whole-genome sequencing (WGS).33,34 In BSA, progeny from a cross between phenotypically different parents are subjected to a selective pressure. Alleles that favor survival under the selective pressure show an increased frequency of inheritance, while those that have decreased fitness are reduced. Polymorphisms in the genome serve as markers to follow the inheritance of regions that mediate enhanced fitness, which are used to define candidate genes using quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping. BSA has been used in P. falciparum to study drug resistance35,36 and parasite growth.37 Population genetics studies provide evidence for recombination of Cryptosporidium isolates in the wild and under experimental conditions.38–40 However, it is currently not possible to generate single-cell clones of C. parvum, despite in vitro propagation systems that allow completion of the life cycle.41 Hence, BSA studies could be highly informative to explore the genetic basis for phenotypic variation in C. parvum.

In this work, we analyzed phenotypic differences of two isolates that share the IIa subtype at GP60 for infectivity in vitro and in immunocompromised mouse models. We utilized genetic tagging, genetic crosses, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to generate recombinant progeny of the isolates with infectivity differences. We further performed BSA with permissive and restrictive conditions to map significant associations between parasite genes and enhanced infectivity of the virulent strain. We then used CRISPR-Cas9, structural and functional analyses, and in vivo and in vitro assays to investigate candidate genes. The results define the role of an ABC transporter in controlling susceptibility of C. parvum to microbial metabolites, thereby influencing infectivity in mice.

RESULTS

Exploring strain variation in C. parvum

Most laboratory studies have been conducted with the IOWA isolate of C. parvum,42 which has been passaged in various laboratories since its original isolation from a calf in Iowa State University. However, our laboratory has routinely used another isolate of C. parvum, called the AUCB-1 line that was first isolated from a calf at Auburn University and referred to here as AUB.43 Our early work with transgenic AUB lines showed that infections were lethal in GKO mice, even in the absence of antibiotic treatment,41,44,45 while reports from other laboratories indicated much less severe outcomes performing similar procedures with the IOWA isolate obtained from the Bunchgrass farm.46,47 During our previous studies on the influence of metabolites on C. parvum growth in vivo, we also made use of an isolate of the IOWA isolate that was passaged serially in calves by our colleagues at University of Arizona, referred to here as IOWA-AZ.19 Our anecdotal experience with the IOWA-AZ isolate was that it was less virulent in mice, although its passage history in a different laboratory made it difficult to directly compare. Hence, we decided to serially passage both isolates in neonatal male Holstein bovine calves and purify them under similar conditions at the University of Illinois using previously published protocols.48 Both AUB and IOWA-AZ C. parvum isolates led to high oocyst shedding and severe infection in calves, characterized by fever, persistent watery mucoid diarrhea, dehydration, weight loss, and lethargy.

The IOWA-AZ isolate is attenuated in mice compared with AUB

To investigate differences in the infectivity of C. parvum isolates systematically, we first compared AUB and IOWA-AZ isolates using a standard in vitro growth assay in HCT-8 cells, as well as a continuous culture method in stem-cell-derived epithelial cell monolayers grown under air-liquid interface (ALI) conditions.41,49 Both isolates grew at similar levels in these systems, indicating that they do not have intrinsic differences in host cell invasion and/or replication (Figures 1A and S1A). Wild-type (C57BL/6) adult mice were highly resistant to both isolates of C. parvum (Figure S1B). Consequently, we infected various immunocompromised mice to compare growth in vivo. In Ifngr1−/− and Stat1−/− mice, AUB induced high oocyst shedding and mortality, while IOWA-AZ caused only mild oocyst shedding and no mortality (Figures S1C and S1D). In Il-12−/− and NOD/SCID/gamma-C chain (NSG) mice, although there was no mortality for either AUB or IOWA-AZ isolates, the oocyst shedding was significantly higher for the former than for the latter isolate (Figures S1E and S1F). In highly susceptible GKO mice, the AUB isolate resulted in high oocyst shedding and 70% mortality, whereas IOWA-AZ resulted in low shedding and no mortality (Figures 1B and 1C). In contrast, Ifnar1−/− and Ifnlr1−/− mice developed only transient infections with either isolate (Figures S1G and S1H).

To determine whether the lower infectivity of the IOWA-AZ isolate in immunocompromised mice could be increased to levels comparable to that of the AUB isolate, we tested: (1) increasing the infection dose, (2) repeated passage in mice, and (3) pretreatment with antibiotics to suppress endogenous flora. We observed that IOWA-AZ infectivity was not restored in Ifngr1−/− mice by either increasing the dose or repeated passaging (Figures S1I and S1J). However, the defect in oocyst shedding of IOWA-AZ was significantly restored by treating the mice with antibiotics for 5 days before infection, resulting in 50% mortality in treated GKO mice (Figures 1D and 1E). These data indicate that the IOWA-AZ isolate is attenuated in immunocompromised mice compared with AUB but regains infectivity and lethality following suppression of the endogenous flora. In addition, these findings define an experimental system to analyze phenotypic differences in C. parvum virulence. The genetic backgrounds and key features of the mouse models are listed in the Table S1.

AUB and IOWA-AZ have high genomic similarity

GP60 sequence analysis revealed that AUB and IOWA-AZ are IIaA17G2R1 subtypes, differing by three non-synonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (Figure 1F). We also investigated the extent of genetic differences by WGS (Table S2). We found no structural variation or copy-number variation between AUB and IOWA-AZ genomes. We extended the analysis to include other IIa subtype isolates18,42,50–53 and maximum likelihood (ML) trees showed clades were segregated by geographical origin, with AUB, IOWA-AZ, and IOWA-CDC clustered together (Figure 1G). Phylogenetic network analysis revealed gene flow between AUB and IOWA-AZ isolates, suggesting that they have recently exchanged regions by recombination (Figure 1H). Collectively, these data indicate that AUB and IOWA-AZ isolates are genetically related, despite their infectivity differences in mice.

We identified 215 high-quality SNPs between AUB and IOWA-AZ, consistent with high overall genome similarity. Among the SNPs, 150 fell within coding regions, including 94 non-synonymous changes potentially affecting gene function. Genes containing ≥2 SNPs are summarized in Table S3. Genome-wide SNP mapping showed even distribution across chromosomes (Figure 1I), providing an ideal marker set for downstream genetic linkage analyses.

Genetic crosses between AUB and IOWA-AZ provide forward genetic analyses to identify genes that favor infectivity in mice

To identify the molecular basis underlying differences in mouse infectivity between C. parvum isolates, we conducted genetic crosses between the AUB and IOWA-AZ isolates (Figure 2). We have previously generated tk-GFP replacing the thymidine kinase (TK) locus and uprt-mCherry replacing the uracil phosphoribosyl transferase (UPRT) locus reporter lines of the AUB isolate and crossed them in ALI cultures and in GKO mice.40,41 These prior studies established that C. parvum is (1) capable of self-fertilizing and outcrossing at approximately equal rates, (2) chromosomes segregate independently, and (3) chromosomes undergo frequent intramolecular recombination.40,41 These features are ideal for performing genetic crosses and linkage analysis to map genes responsible for phenotypic differences.

To achieve genetic crosses between AUB and IOWA-AZ, we introduced the GFP or mCherry sequences into the TK and UPRT genetic loci to generate fluorescently tagged lines of the IOWA-AZ isolate using CRISPR-Cas9 (Figures S2A and S2B). PCR analysis and immunofluorescence staining showed that both transgenic lines had the correct insertion into the respective genomic loci (Figures S2C–S2F).

Initially, we utilized the ALI culture platform to examine recombination between Cp-IOWA-AZ-UPRT-mCherry and Cp-AUB-TK-GFP parents (Figure 2A). On 3 days post infection (dpi), immunofluorescence assay (IFA) revealed red, green, and yellow progeny representing the outcomes of self- and outcross-fertilization (Figure 2B). These findings mirror prior studies using intra-strain crosses in AUB and confirm that there is no incompatibility in performing crosses with C. parvum isolates of different genetic backgrounds. However, the number of outcrossed F1 oocysts produced in the ALI was limited (100 oocysts per well), which might have insufficient complexity to allow for multiple recombination events needed for unbiased population analyses.

As an alternative, we performed crosses in antibiotic-treated GKO mice, which restores the growth of IOWA-AZ, such that each isolate is expected to grow with similar efficiency during crossing and expansion (Figure 2C). Following a single round of meiosis (3 dpi), we isolated recombinant progeny using FACS (Figure 2D). We harvested more than 104 double positive (yellow) oocysts per mouse (>2 × 105 in total by pooling multiple mice) (Table S4). We confirmed the infectivity of the sorted yellow oocysts by infection of HCT-8 cells and detecting the GFP and mCherry markers. Double positive and singly positive asexual stages were detected on HCT-8 cells after infection, indicating segregation of the markers after meiosis. This finding was consistent with the previous study of C. parvum outcrossing in vitro and confirmed the F1 nature of the harvested yellow oocysts40 (Figure 2E). To generate pools for BSA, recombinant progeny were serially passaged in antibiotic-treated GKO mice (permissive conditions × three times) or untreated GKO mice (restrictive conditions × three times), allowing expansion and selection, respectively (Figure 2F).

Recombinant progeny from F1 generation, first and third expansions under permissive condition, and first and third selections under restrictive condition were simultaneously used for WGS. We generated 17 sets of high-quality sequence data with 100% genome coverage and >100× sequencing depth (Table S5). Samples from the permissive and restrictive mouse pools contained sufficient oocysts (i.e., >106) to allow direct sequencing using WGS. However, due to the relatively low yield of recombinant F1 oocysts, we performed whole-genome amplification (WGA) on these samples prior to WGS. To confirm that this process did not introduce bias, we compared the read depth and mapping efficiency of pool #3 from the GKO mice infected under restrictive conditions (−antibiotics) using WGA followed by WGS vs. direct sequencing by WGS. We found that the WGS data from 105 oocysts with WGA showed similar genome coverage and sequencing depth as direct sequencing of 106 oocysts (Table S5), indicating no bias after WGA.

To control for the possibility that the tagged regions might influence the outcome of the crosses, we swapped the markers to generate Cp-AUB-UPRT-mCherry and Cp-IOWA-AZ-TK-GFP lines and crossed them in vivo using the same procedure (referred to as cross 2). We obtained similar results from cross 2, including the number of F1 yellow oocysts and the quality of WGS data (Tables S4 and S5).

BSA identifies a major determinant of C. parvum pathogenicity in mice

To identify genomic regions that promote fitness in vivo, we performed BSA to compare inheritance frequencies of alleles during expansion under permissive conditions (+antibiotics) vs. restrictive conditions (−antibiotics) in GKO mice with the initial outcrossed F1 oocysts (Figure 2H). Inheritance frequencies of alleles differed across eight chromosomes during permissive expansion (Figure 3A). In both independent crosses, the allele frequencies on chromosome 4 were stable around 0.5, while those on chromosomes 1, 2, 5, and 7 were variant and in some regions were skewed toward one parental line, although these regions differed between the two crosses (Figure 3A). Not surprisingly, the strongest shift in allele frequencies corresponded to the fluorescent protein makers used to select the F1 recombinant oocysts, including the middle regions of chromosomes 1 and 5, where the UPRT and TK loci lie, respectively (Figure 3A). To counteract the variation in the allele frequencies of outcrossed F1 oocysts, we calculated the Δ(eSNP index) with a 100 kb sliding window, which showed allele frequency differences between pools under permissive or restrictive condition and the pool of yellow F1 recombinant oocysts. Regions with Δ(SNP index) gradually shifted to one of the parental lines during passaging in mice were defined as QTLs (blue boxes in Figure 3B). The tagged loci also showed shifts in Δ(SNP index), and read mapping confirmed absence of signal at TK and UPRT loci in the parental lines, with ~50% read depth in yellow F1 oocysts (Figures 3B, S3A, and S3B). Surprisingly, the read depths of the permissive expansion pools increased to 100% wild type for both UPRT and TK loci after a few rounds of mouse infection (Figures S3A and S3B). The rapid replacement of the fluorescent protein markers with the wild-type gene indicates that these loci are not selectively neutral as originally thought, even though the genes themselves are non-essential. Collectively, these results indicate that despite initial bias on chromosomes 1 and 5, rapid recombination quickly reverts these chromosomes to neutral ratios and thus should not obscure identification of genes important in infectivity under restrictive conditions.

During expansion under restrictive conditions, some allele frequencies gradually shifted to the virulent AUB parental line, including the beginning of chromosome 1, the end of chromosome 6, and the 1,010–1,193 kb region of chromosome 7, which were identified as possible QTLs (Figures 3A and 3B). In addition, there were some regions that showed enhanced allele frequencies from the AUB parent in only one of the crosses, such as the 264–286 kb region on chromosome 6 in cross 1 and the 1,218–1,283 kb region on chromosome 8 in cross 2 (Figures 3A and 3B). To assess the statistical significance of QTLs, we compared bulk sequences from pools obtained from permissive expansion to pools from restricted expansion using the G′ statistic. These analyses revealed a significant QTL peak on chromosome 7 that was identified in both crosses (Figure 3C). This locus, within the sliding window, contained four genes which had SNPs between AUB and IOWA-AZ (Table S6). Among them, cgd7_4520 contains two non-synonymous SNPs (Table S6). Two of the other candidate genes only contained a single synonymous SNP and the third candidate contained a single non-synonymous SNP (Table S6). As expected, the allele frequency in recombinant yellow oocysts was ~50:50 but progressively shifted toward the AUB allele during selection (Figure S3C).

The smaller peaks selected from expansion under strictive condition, such as those on chromosomes 6 and 1, contained other genes that potentially influence infectivity (Figure 3C). The former contained the GP60 gene, which encodes proteins that mediate host infectivity and pathogenicity54 and is also a polymorphic gene between AUB and IOWA-AZ. The region on chromosome 1 includes cgd_1g30 and cgd_1g750 genes that each contains one SNP between AUB and IOWA-AZ. This region also contains the cgd1_140 (SKSR1) gene that was identified as a virulence enhancing gene based on a separate cross between C. parvum IId isolates.55

The cgd7_4520 gene encodes a protein located in SGs and exported at the host/parasite interface during invasion

The candidate gene cgd7_4520 encodes a protein that is annotated as an ABC transporter and has not been previously characterized. Due to its potential role in transport and the presence of two non-synonymous SNPs that might suggest strain-specific differences in function, we chose this candidate among the four genes in the QTL for further study. To investigate its cellular localization throughout the parasite life cycle, we tagged the C terminus of this protein with a triple hemagglutinin (3HA) epitope tag. In sporozoites, cgd7_4520–3HA was localized in the middle of the parasites but apical to the nucleus (Figure 4A). After several failed attempts to localize the cgd7_4520–3HA using ultrastructure expansion microscopy (U-ExM), an alternative approach was taken to tag the protein with the spaghetti monster HA tag (referred to as cgd7_4520-smHA). U-ExM revealed cgd7_4520-smHA localized to numerous small vesicles around the nucleus that were distinct from dense granules (Figure 4B). This localization pattern is consistent with the compartment small granules (SGs), which are known to contain other secreted proteins.56 Comparison with 1B5, a marker outlining the feeder organelle,57 and host actin, which forms a pedestal at the invasion site,58,59 showed that cgd7_4520–3HA was translocated to the host/parasite interface and localized within the feeder organelle and above the actin pedestal, during parasite invasion (Figures 4C and 4D). It was also expressed at the base of macrogamonts, recognized by mouse antibody 4D857 and in microgametes recognized by their unique nuclear morphology (Figure 4D). Collectively, these findings indicate that the ABC transporter encoded by cgd7_4520 is exported from sporozoites and occupies the interface defined by the feeder organelle at the host parasite interface (Figure 4E).

Deletion and polymorphism of the cgd7_4520 affect C. parvum infectivity in mice

To assess the biological significance of the cgd7_4520, we attempted to replace the gene in the AUB genome with a neomycin resistance selection cassette that also contained a nanoluciferase reporter using CRISPR-Cas9. Surprisingly, the gene was dispensable, and we readily generated a KO mutant lacking the entire cgd7_4520 coding region (referred to as Cp-AUB-cgd7_4520-KO or KO for short) (Figure 5A). We also created a line in which the AUB allele was replaced with the IOWA-AZ variant (Cp-AUB-cgd7_4520-SWAP, SWAP), and a line in which the endogenous AUB allele was tagged (Cp-AUB-cgd7_4520-TAG, TAG) (Figure 5A). All transgenic lines were validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing following amplification in mice (Figures S2G–S2K).

We compared the KO, SWAP, and TAG lines for growth in vitro and in vivo (Figure 5B). All three lines grew to similar levels in HCT-8 cells, indicating similar capacities for host cell invasion and/or replication (Figure 5C). In contrast, when used to infect GKO mice without the antibiotics or paromomycin treatment, the KO and SWAP lines resulted in significantly lower infection intensity than the TAG line, which served as a control for wild type (Figure 5D). Correspondingly, infection of mice with the KO and SWAP lines resulted in significantly less weight loss and decreased morality than infection of mice with the TAG line (Figures 5E and 5F). Interestingly, compared with the SWAP line that was intermediate, the KO line showed reduced growth and resulted in no mortality in GKO mice (Figures 5D and 5F).

Orthologs of cgd7_4520 are polymorphic among other Cryptosporidium isolates with different infectivity

To assess the potential relevance of cgd7_4520-associated differences in infectivity of a broader range of isolates, we performed comparative genomics on Cryptosporidium isolates with different infectivity, which have been described in previous studies.14,15,18,38,60,61 The two non-synonymous mutations we identified here were unique to AUB and IOWA-AZ (Figure S4A). We then evaluated the polymorphism of the orthologs of cgd7_4520 between isolates, by calculating the SNP distribution with a 10-kb window. We found that the orthologs of cgd7_4520 were polymorphic among other C. parvum IIa and IId isolates, as well as between C. tyzzeri isolates, and among C. hominis isolates (Figures S4B–S4E).

The cgd7_4520 belongs to the MRP family

To elucidate the molecular function of cgd7_4520, we performed the functional annotation and structural prediction for the encoded protein. cgd7_4520 is predicted to have two ABC membrane domains and two AAA domains (Figure 6A). The two non-synonymous mutations between IOWA-AZ and AUB were localized in the first ABC membrane domain (Figure 6A). cgd7_4520 showed the highest sequence identity (30.4%) to human multidrug resistance protein 4 (MRP4), which has a broad tissue distribution and efflux activity for multiple substrates.62 In addition, the AlphaFold predicted that cgd7_4520 also displayed a canonical exporter architecture featuring two globular nucleotide-binding domains (NBDs) and transmembrane regions (TMRs) with long intracellular loops (ICLs) (Figure 6B). Therefore, we named the protein encoded by the cgd7_4520 as C. parvum MRP (CpMRP1).

Deletion or mutation of CpMRP1 increases parasite susceptibility to the microbial metabolite DCA

Previous studies have shown that metabolites that are abundant in the microbiota of adult mice are toxic to C. parvum growth.19,22 Consequently, the potential function of CpMRP1 to efflux xenobiotics prompted us to test its role in resistance to metabolites. We selected seven microbial metabolites that inhibit C. parvum growth in HCT-8 cells to evaluate their potential as substrates of the CpMRP1 by molecular docking. Deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) have similar structures, although they differ in a hydroxyl at position 12 (Figure S6A). DCA and LCA showed the strongest predicted binding affinity to both AUB and IOWA-AZ CpMRP1 as compared with other tested metabolites, indicating possible interactions between these two metabolites and CpMRP1 (Figure 6D). To explore whether other ABC transporters might also transport these toxic metabolites, we examined all 21 annotated ABC transporters in C. parvum. Sequence comparisons revealed low similarity among them, with a maximum identity of 40% (Figure S4F). While CpMRP1 showed strong binding affinity to DCA and LCA, other transporters, particularly cgd2_3870, also exhibited potential interactions with these metabolites based on similarly low binding affinity values (Figure S4G). These findings suggest that C. parvum ABC transporters may have distinct substrate preferences, and some may function synergistically to mediate resistance. Further analysis across three dominant Cryptosporidium spp. (C. parvum, C. hominis, and C. tyzzeri), which differ in host range, infectivity, and transmission, revealed that some of the transporters had higher dN/dS ratios compared with the average of all orthologous genes (Figure S4G). This supports prior observations that ABC transporters in Cryptosporidium are highly polymorphic, under selective pressure, and associated with host adaptation.63 Notably, while 20 of the 21 transporters were identical between AUB and IOWA-AZ, the mutations on CpMRP1 altered binding affinity to DCA and LCA. DCA showed the highest difference between the two isolates with more than 1 unit difference, indicating that AUB has an ~10-fold higher binding affinity to DCA than IOWA-AZ (Figure 6D). Consistent with this prediction, the mutations of T310K and S299N in CpMRP1 shifted the binding position of DCA so that the AlphaFold model of CpMRP1 from AUB had two predicted H-bonds while the corresponding CpMRP1 allele from IOWA-AZ had only one (Figure 6C).

In host intestinal system, the DCA and LCA are produced by genera Eubacterium and Clostridium.64 To assess environmental differences between the two BSA conditions highlighting the CpMRP1, we compared the microbiota shifts of four uninfected GKO mice hosted in two cages at three time points: 1 day before antibiotic treatment (before), 5 days after treatment (after), and 5 days post-treatment cessation (recovery) (Figure S5A). Analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing revealed a significant reduction in microbial diversity and a marked shift in taxonomic composition following antibiotic administration, with partial recovery post-treatment (Figures 6E and S5B). While the microbial communities in the two cages were distinct prior to treatment, they became more similar after treatment and during recovery (Figures S5C and S5D). Importantly, Eubacterium and Clostridium were depleted following antibiotic treatment and started re-emerging during the recovery period (Figure 6F). The findings indicate that the major taxa responsible production of DCA and LCA are reduced under antibiotic-treated conditions.

To evaluate the role of CpMRP1 in mediating resistance to DCA or LCA, we first compared the dose-response curves between the KO, SWAP, and TAG lines. Interestingly, the DCA dose-response curves of the KO and SWAP lines were left-shifted compared with the TAG line, with significant decreases in EC50, while the LCA dose-response curves of the lines were similar with no difference in EC50 (Figures 6G and S6B). The data indicate that deletion of CpMRP1, or expression of the IOWA-AZ allele of CpMRP1, increases parasite susceptibility to DCA. This finding was supported by comparing the growth inhibition of these lines under 50% effective concentration (EC50) and EC90 treatment, which showed that the parasite growth was significantly inhibited by DCA when the CpMRP1 was deleted or swapped to IOWA-AZ (Figure 6H), while there was no change in susceptibility to LCA (Figure S6C). Moreover, DCA and LCA combined treatment results in an additive reduction in parasite growth, suggesting that these bile acids may act through distinct mechanisms (Figure S6D). To validate role of CpMRP1 in vivo, we employed a neonatal mouse model to assess parasite growth under DCA treatment (Figure 6I). Neonatal mice are highly susceptible to Cryptosporidium infection but gradually develop resistance as they grow.65 Importantly, the DCA-producing bacterial genera Eubacterium and Clostridium emerge around 2 weeks of age in C57BL/6 mice,19 resulting in a DCA-free window during early life (Figure 6I). When 5-day-old neonatal mice were infected and treated with DCA, the KO and SWAP lines exhibited significantly lower infection intensities during a DCA-free window (6–9 dpi, 11–14 days old) compared with untreated controls, while no difference was observed in the TAG line, as measured by fecal luciferase activity (Figure 6J). Notably, while the SWAP and TAG lines showed comparable growth in the absence of DCA, the KO line showed reduced growth without DCA treatment.

To examine the role of DCA in growth inhibition across the life cycle, we quantified parasite growth in cultures treated with EC50 or EC90 of DCA at different time points.22 We found that the total number of C. parvum was not significantly different between DMSO- and DCA-treated cultures until 24 h post infection (hpi), indicating that DCA did not affect C. parvum excystation and invasion, or parasitophorous vacuole formation (Figure S6E). Instead, DCA delayed progression through sequential life cycle stages, rather than directly killing the parasite (Figure S6F). This finding was further supported by observations at a later point (72 hpi), where parasites under EC50 DCA treatment were still capable of progressing through asexual stages (Figures 6K and S6G). In contrast, deletion of CpMRP1, or expression of the IOWA-AZ allele of CpMRP1, significantly delayed the development of sexual stages even at 72 hpi, which supports the role of CpMRP1 in mediating resistance to DCA (Figures 6K and S6G).

DISCUSSION

Decreased severity of infection of IOWA-AZ was observed in several immunocompromised mice, including Ifngr1−/−, Stat1−/−, Il-12−/−, NSG, and GKO mice. Both Ifnar1−/− and Ifnlr1−/− mice showed slightly increased oocyst shedding in the early phase of infection, keeping with previous reports that IFN-λ provides partial protection against infection in neonatal mice, which are more susceptible to infection,66,67 although this trait did not differ between parasite isolates. Importantly, the greatly reduced oocyst shedding and lower mortality of the avirulent isolate IOWA-AZ in GKO mice was restored by antibiotic pretreatment. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating increased susceptibility of C. parvum in antibiotic-treated mice,20 and studies showing that the endogenous flora provides colonization resistance to pathogens.68–70 Moreover, the data suggest that the reduced infectivity of IOWA-AZ is due to greater sensitivity to colonization resistance imparted by the microbiota, rather than differences in susceptibility to immune mechanisms of control.

Despite sharing highly similar genomes that differ by only 215 SNPs, IOWA-AZ is highly attenuated compared with AUB in multiple immunocompromised mouse strains. Because the avirulent nature of IOWA-AZ was not influenced by increasing the dose or repeated passage, we reasoned that the infectivity differences between AUB and IOWA-AZ was due to functional variants in specific genes. BSA of permissive vs. restrictive pools of cross progeny were used to define QTL regions containing genes that contribute to C. parvum infectivity in vivo. Using this approach, we identified genomic loci on chromosomes 1, 6, and 7 that were positively associated with the more infectious AUB parent. Among them, there is a significant QTL on chromosome 7, containing 4 genes with polymorphisms between AUB and IOWA-AZ. Based on the presence of two non-synonymous SNPs, we focused on cgd7_4520 as a candidate gene contributing to C. parvum infectivity. We have not examined the role of the remaining polymorphic genes, and potentially they could also contribute to the QTL difference between the isolates. We also found minor QTLs on other chromosomes, although their contribution varied between the genetic crosses. The QTL chromosome 6 contains the GP60 gene, which was found to contribute to differences in infectivity between subtypes of IId isolates as shown by allelic swapping.54 The QTL on chromosome 1 corresponds to a similar region identified in a recent cross of C. parvum IId isolates with different infectivity. BSA analyses of progeny selected by passage in mice for different days identified a gene called SKSR1 as an important pathogenesis factor of C. parvum in mice.55 However, this gene is identical between AUB and IOWA-AZ, suggesting a different determinant on chromosome 1 may contribute to infectivity differences in the present study. The observation that different genetic regions have been found to contribute to pathogenesis in different genetic crosses between different C. parvum isolates indicates that virulence is multi-genic and depends on the genomic context of the relevant isolates.

In addition to QTLs that contribute to differences between AUB and IOWA-AZ, we observed dramatic changes in allele frequencies at the TK and UPRT loci. The deleted copies of these genes that had been replaced by florescent protein markers were rapidly reverted to wild-type alleles during passage in mice under nonselective conditions (+antibiotics). Although, TK and URPT genes are not essential for the parasite,41,47 they clearly contribute to fitness. The rapid reversion of these regions also illustrates the frequent recombination of C. parvum chromosomes during meiosis, consistent with previous reports.40 Taken together, these findings indicate that genetic properties of C. parvum are highly amenable to forward genetic analysis of complex traits, including infectivity.

The gene encoding cgd7_4520 contains two non-synonymous SNPs between AUB and IOWA-AZ, suggesting that it may contribute functionally to the differences in infectivity between these two parental isolates. Surprisingly, we found that cgd7_4520 was dispensable and its deletion did not affect C. parvum AUB growth in vitro. However, deletion of cgd7_4520 significantly reduced growth in GKO mice and prevented body weight loss in infected animals, thus rescuing the mice from death. Additionally, allelic swap of the IOWA-AZ allele into the AUB background reduced infectivity in GKO mice, although this alteration did not affect growth in vitro. The following findings support designation of cgd7_4520 as a virulence factor based on principles established by Molecular Koch’s Postulates71 as a virulence factor: (1) specific alleles show association with pathogenesis, (2) loss of the gene leads to attenuation in vivo but not in vitro, and (3) the difference in infectivity is reversed by molecular exchange of alleles.

Several lines of evidence suggested a putative function for the gene encoded by cgd7_4520. First, it is annotated as an ABC transporter. Interestingly, C. parvum contains 21 members of the ABC transporter family,72 which has been implicated in transport a variety of substrates including amino acids, lipids, ions, and sugars.73 Second, cgd7_4520 encodes a protein with closest homology to the MRP4 that functions in transporting multiple xenobiotics,62 leading us to name it CpMRP1. Third, CpMRP1 is found in SGs, which are secreted during invasion55,56 and following invasion, CpMRP1 localizes to the feeder organelle, a membranous interface rich in transport activities including the glucose transporters CpGT1 and CpGT2.74 Fourth, homology modeling and docking studies indicate a propensity for CpMRP1 to bind to secondary bile salts such as DCA and LCA. Collectively, these features led us to test the model that CpMRP1 mediates differential susceptibility to secondary bile salts.

Previous studies have shown that secondary bile salts inhibit C. parvum growth in vitro,22 although the mechanism by which they enter the parasite is unknown. Mammalian cells import DCA and related metabolites using the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter, Takada G-protein-coupled receptor 5 and other receptors at the surface of epithelial cells.75,76 Subsequent accumulation of DCA in the parasite may occur by diffusion or inadvertent carriage across the feeder organelle by transporters that are designed for nutrient update. Regardless of exactly how DCA gains entry to the parasite cytosol, our data suggest that CpMRP1 serves to export DCA back into the host cell cytosol across the feeder organelle. Interestingly, deletion of cgd7_4520 also reduced infectivity of AUB in neonatal mice, where DCA would not be expected to be a substrate. Hence, CpMRP1 may fulfill another function in vivo, perhaps exporting alternative inhibitory metabolites. Considering this alternative role, it is possible CpMRP1’s ability to export DCA arose secondarily to its primary function and thus it could be thought of as an evolved pathogenesis determinant.

By comparing the susceptibility of the wild-type AUB allele of CpMRP1 to the allelic swap bearing the IOWA-AZ allele of CpMRP1, we were able to confirm that it mediates differential susceptibly to DCA-mediated growth inhibition, although not to LCA. We found that the DCA delays the progression through sequential life cycle stages of C. parvum, including asexual and sexual stages. Our findings implicate the microbiota in colonization resistance, as the reduced infectivity of IOWA-AZ in GKO mice was restored following reduction in the abundance of DCA-producing taxa after antibiotic treatment. DCA is one of the most abundant secondary bile acids produced by the microbiota in humans and animals.64,77,78 Secondary and tertiary bile salts, including derivatives of DCA, are important in mediating colonization resistance to bacterial pathogens including Clostridium difficile,79,80 and the protozoan parasite C. parvum.22 The production of DCA and related secondary bile salts is much higher in the colon relative to small intestine, reflecting the greater abundance of microbial taxa that act on bile salts.81 Although the small intestine is the primary site of C. parvum infection in adult animals, the infection also reaches very high burdens in the colon in neonatal mice.65 Hence, production of secondary bile salts may be particularly important during the transition from susceptible neonatal animals to more resistant adults.19,22 Consistent with this prediction, treatment of neonatal mice with exogenous DCA reduced the growth of C. parvum in a CpMRP1-dependent manner.

The specific alleles of cgd7_4250 defined here are unique to the AUB and IOWA-AZ isolates, and hence, they are unlikely to mediate virulence differences on a broader level. However, orthologs of cgd7_4520 are also polymorphic in other isolates of C. parvum that differ in infectivity,17 as well as in C. hominis, and C. tyzzeri, suggesting that polymorphisms in this gene may play a broader role in infectivity of Cryptosporidium. Moreover, computational analyses identified several other C. parvum ABC transporters that are both polymorphic and that are predicted to bind DCA and related metabolites. Hence, our findings may foretell a more general role for ABC transporters in mediating export of toxic metabolites and in contributing to differences in pathogenesis. Our study provides a platform for forward genetic analysis of complex traits in C. parvum and sheds light on the evolution of C. parvum virulence by demonstrating a molecular mechanism for tolerance to microbial metabolites that influences infectivity in vivo.

Although our model suggests that CpMRP1 exports toxic metabolites, we have not directly monitored binding or efflux activity of the parasite protein. Additional studies are also needed to examine the roles of related ABC transporters that are predicted to bind DCA, and which are polymorphic, to determine whether they also contribute virulence by mediating resistance to toxic metabolites. Importantly, while we did not observe a difference in infectivity of IOWA-AZ or AUB during propagation in calves, these animals lack a mature microbiota and hence would be expected to have low levels of secondary bile salts. Hence, additional studies would be necessary to determine whether CpMRP1, or other related ABC transporters, play a similar role in natural hosts such as cattle or humans, as was identified in mice.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Cryptosporidium isolates and lines

The C. parvum “AUB” isolate (IIaA17G2R1 subtype) was obtained from a calf at the University of Auburn, initially named AUCP-1.86 The C. parvum “IOWA-AZ” isolate (IIaA17G2R1 subtype) was obtained from Dr. Michael Riggs at the University of Arizona, where they have been passaging it in calves for several decades.87–89 These two isolates have been passaged at 6-month intervals in calves using previously described procedures.48 Briefly, 1–2 days old neonatal male Holstein bovine calves maintained under Biosafety level 2 conditions were orally inoculated with 5 × 107 oocysts suspended in normal saline. Fecal samples containing shed oocysts were collected from the calves between days 4–12 post-infection and stored at 4 °C until use for extraction of oocysts. Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. C. parvum oocysts were extracted and purified from calf feces by sequential sieve filtration, Sheather’s sugar flotation, and discontinuous sucrose density gradient centrifugation as previously described.48 The AUB-TK-GFP and AUB-UPRT-mCherry lines were prepared previously and have been passaged in our lab by serial inoculation of oocysts into immunocompromised mice at approximately 6-month intervals.22 Purified oocysts were stored at 4 °C in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) plus 50 mM Tris and 10 mM EDTA (pH 7.2) for up to six months. The species and subtype identity of the isolates were determined by PCR and sequence analysis of the 18S rRNA and GP60 genes.90,91

Mice

Animal studies in mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Studies Committee (School of Medicine, Washington University in St. Louis). Ifnar1−/− and Ifnlr1−/− mice were provided by the laboratory of Megan T. Baldridge in the Department of Molecular Microbiology, Washington University in St. Louis. C57BL/6j mice (strain #000664), Ifngr1−/− mice (strain #025394), Ifng−/− mice (referred to as GKO) (strain #002287), Stat1−/− mice (strain #012606), IL-12−/− mice (strain #002693), and Nod scid gamma mice (referred to as NSG) (strain #005557) were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Table S1). All mice were bred in-house in a specific pathogen-free animal facility on a 12:12 light/dark cycle. All infection studies were performed on mice between 8 and 12 weeks of age, and both male and female mice were used equally, unless otherwise specified. Mice were housed with same-sex siblings throughout the experiments. Mice that became non-ambulatory or lost 20% of their body weight during the infection were humanely euthanized according to the protocol.

Cells

Human ileocecal adenocarcinoma cells (HCT-8; ATCC CCL-244) were cultured in RPMI 1640 ATCC Modification Medium (Thermo Fisher A14091–01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free with the e-Myco plus Mycoplasma PCR detection kit (Boca Scientific).

METHOD DETAILS

Preparation of C. parvum oocysts

Prior to infection of cell cultures or animal models, C. parvum oocysts were treated in a 40% bleach solution (containing 8.25% sodium hypochlorite) diluted in PBS for 10 min on ice, then washed three times in PBS. Oocysts of each isolate or line were produced in the same laboratory, purified using the same procedures, and age-matched to ensure equal viability. The purified oocysts were suspended in PBS and stored at 4 °C until use.

C. parvum growth assay

HCT-8 cells were plated in 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) with black side and clear bottom and grown to confluence. Cells were infected with 1 × 105 C. parvum AUB or IOWA-AZ oocysts per well. Cells and parasites were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min, and then permeabilized in TSS buffer (PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% BSA) for 20 min. C. parvum was labeled with a polyclonal rabbit serum generated against C. parvum sporozoites referred to as PanCp57 diluted 1:2,000 in TSS for 60 min, followed by Alexa Fluor goat anti-rabbit 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted 1:1,000 in TSS for 60 min. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 diluted 1:2,000 in TSS for 10 min. Plates were imaged using a 10× objective on a BioTek Cytation 3 cell imager (9 images per well in a 3 × 3 grid). BioTek Gen5 software v3.08 (Agilent) was used to quantify the total number of parasites (puncta in the GFP channel) and host cells (nuclei in the DAPI channel) per well.

For long-term C. parvum growth, the air-liquid interface (ALI) culture was generated as previously described.41,49 C. parvum AUB or IOWA-AZ oocysts were added to the top of the ALI culture after three days of top medium removal, at a challenge dose of 1 × 105 oocysts per transwell. At 4 hpi, the top of transwells was washed twice with DPBS to remove invaded sporozoites and oocysts. On specified dpi, cells were scraped from the transwells and DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN). The C. parvum and host cell genome equivalents were quantified on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) with primers for their GAPDH genes, as previously described.41

Experimental infection of mice

To analyze phenotypic differences in C. parvum infectivity and virulence, different immunocompromised mice (GKO, Ifngr1−/−, Ifnar1−/−, Ifnlr1−/−, Stat1−/−, IL12−/− and NSG mice) and C57BL/6 mice were used to monitor AUB or IOWA-AZ isolate infection. Mice were equally separated in two groups (AUB and IOWA-AZ infection groups, n=3 for each group), and each mouse was infected with 2 × 104 AUB or IOWA-AZ oocysts by oral gavage. Except for C57BL/6 mice, infections in immunocompromised mice were repeated in a second similarly sized group of mice. Fecal pellets were collected from each mouse and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

To assess the correlation between infection dose and the IOWA-AZ infectivity, nine Ifngr1−/− mice were divided into three groups, which were infected with 2 × 104, 5 × 104, and 1 × 105 IOWA-AZ oocysts, respectively. Fecal pellets were collected every three days for further processing. To assess the influence of repeated passages in mice and infectivity, Ifngr1−/− mice were divided into two groups. One group of mice (n = 5) were infected with 5 × 104 IOWA-AZ oocysts. Oocysts were harvested from fecal pellets of those mice on 9 dpi, pooled and used to inoculate the other group of Ifngr1−/− mice (n = 3). Fecal pellets were collected every three days for further processing. To assess the correlation between the endogenous flora and the IOWA-AZ infectivity, six GKO mice were divided into two groups, one group of mice were given antibiotic water containing 1 g/L ampicillin, 1 g/L neomycin and 0.5 g/L vancomycin (Sigma) for five days before infection. All mice were infected with 2 × 104 IOWA-AZ oocysts on 0 dpi by oral gavage. The infections were repeated in a second similarly sized group of mice. Fecal pellets were collected every three days for further processing.

Fecal DNA was extracted from feces using the QIAamp Powerfecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The number of C. parvum oocysts per fecal pellet was calculated using the quantitative qPCR assay as described.22

Whole-genome sequencing of AUB and IOWA-AZ

Purified oocysts (106) of each C. parvum AUB and IOWA were subjected to five freeze-thaw cycles, and the DNA was extracted from them using the QIAamp DNA minikit (Qiagen, United States). Libraries were constructed using automated protocols on the SciClone G3 NGSx workstation (Perkin Elmer) by the Genome Technology Access Center (GTAC) at Washington University. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was acquired using Illumina protocols on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) to generate 150 bp reads. The WGS reads were used for genome assembly using CLC Genomics Workbench v19.0.1. The published C. parvum IOWA-CDC genome was used as a reference to map the assemblies and identify structural variants using Mauve v2.3.1.18,92

A total of 291 WGS datasets of C. parvum, C. hominis, and C. tyzzeri were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) using prefetch v2.11.3 (https://github.com/ncbi/sra-tools). Genome datasets that were generated in published studies are summarized in Table S2.

Variant analysis

Sequence reads obtained from Illumina were trimmed for adapter sequences and poor sequence quality (Phred score < 30 and trimmed length < 60) using fastp v0.23.4. The trimmed reads were mapped to the reference C. parvum IOWA-CDC genome using the BWA-MEM v0.7.17. Duplicate reads were marked and removed in the bam files using the MarkDuplicates algorithm of Picard v3.1.1 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) with REMOVE_DUPLICATES=true, CREATE_INDEX=true, and ASSUME_SORTED=true. Genome coverage and sequence depth were estimated using the mpileup algorithm of SAMtools v1.7 (http://samtools.sourceforge.net/). The mpileup and call algorithms of BCFtools v1.12 (https://samtools.github.io/bcftools/) were used to generate a VCF file of sequence variants, with parameters -c 50, -d 500, and -ploidy 1. Low quality SNPs (QUAL < 30, FORMAT/DP < 3 and AVG FORMAT/DP < 25) were filtered out using the BCFtools filter algorithm. The distribution of each SNP between isolates were visualized using TBtools-II (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools-II), while the number of SNPs present in 10 kb along the genome was calculated using VCFtools v0.1.16 (https://vcftools.github.io/man_latest.html) and plotted by a R package ‘ggplot2’ (https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/ggplot2).

Relationships among C. parvum isolates were examined using phylogenetic analysis. A maximum likelihood (ML) tree was generated from the wgSNPs using RAxM-NG with a substitution model and 1,000 replicates for bootstrapping. The substitution model was estimated using jModelTest v2.1.10 based on values from Akaike Information Criterion.82 Gene flow between isolates was assessed using phylogenetic network analysis using the neighbor-net algorithm of SplitsTree684 (https://github.com/husonlab/splitstree6).

Generation of parasites with fluorescent markers for genetic crosses

Transgenic parasites were generated to express the fluorescent marker GFP in the thymidine kinase locus (TK) on chromosome 5 (TK-GFP) or the mCherry protein in the uracil phosphoribosyl transferase locus (URPT) on chromosome 1 (UPRT-mCherry) using CRISPR/Cas9. The Cas9 and tagging plasmids were previously generated in our lab.22

Bleached IOWA-AZ oocysts were treated with sodium taurodeoxycholate at 37 °C for 60 min for excystation. The excysted sporozoites (5 × 107) were electroporated with TK-GFP-Nluc-P2A-neo or UPRT-mCherry-Nluc-P2A-neo plasmids (50 ug) together with the Cas9 plasmids (25 ug) containing the corresponding gRNAs. The electroporated parasites were orally gavaged to two to three GKO mice (pre-treated with antibiotics for five days) that were 5 min previously treated orally with sodium bicarbonate (8%, 200 μL/mouse) to neutralize gastric acid. Transgenic parasites were selected by treating GKO mice with 16 g/L paromomycin in the drinking water starting 24 h after the oocyst inoculation. Fecal pellets from infected mice were collected daily starting on 6 dpi. DNA was extracted from fecal pellets on 9 dpi as described above, and oocysts were purified from fecal pellets by low-speed centrifugation (200 rpm for 10 min). The transgenic parasites were verified by measurement of nanoluciferase activity using Cytation 3, fluorescence microscopic analysis of purified oocysts, and PCR amplification of extracted DNA. Primers for PCR amplification are listed in the key resources table.

Genetic cross between C. parvum isolates

For in vitro crosses, C. parvum AUB-TK-GFP and IOWA-AZ-UPRT-mCherry oocysts (1 × 105 oocysts per isolate per transwell) were simultaneously added to the top of the ALI culture on day 3 after top medium removal. At 4 hpi, the top of transwells was washed twice with DPBS to remove invaded sporozoites and oocysts. Transwells were moved to a new 24-well plate with DPBS in the bottom chamber at 3 dpi. Monolayers were fixed with 100 mL 4% formaldehyde for 10 min. Cells were washed twice with PBS and then permeabilized and blocked with TSS for 20 min. Primary antibodies were diluted in TSS for staining: rabbit-GFP was used at 1:2000, rat-mCherry was used at 1:1000. Monolayers were incubated with primary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS, then incubated in secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes diluted 1:1,000 in TSS buffer for 60 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst diluted 1:2,000 in TSS for 10 mins at room temperature. The membrane was cut out of the transwells using a scalpel and mounted on a glass slide with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For in vivo crosses, four GKO mice were pre-treated with antibiotics for five days and simultaneously infected with 1 × 105 oocysts of pairs of AUB-TK-GFP and IOWA-AZ-UPRT-mCherry. On the 3 dpi, small intestines were collected, cut open, and placed in 15 mL tubes with cold PBS. The tubes were shook 100 times by hand to strip the epithelial cells and the oocysts. A 40 μm filter (FALCON) was placed on top of a 50 mL tube. The contents of the 15 mL tube were transferred to the filter, and then 40 mL PBS was added to wash the filter. The 50 mL tube was centrifuged at 4 °C, 2000g, for 10 min, and the supernatant was removed and discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of cold PBS, and incubated with 40% bleach solution for 10 min on ice, then washed three times with cold DPBS. The pellet was then resuspended in 500 μL of cold DPBS, filtered with 30 μm filters (Cysmex, Germany), and loaded into the sorting chamber of a SONY SH800 Cell Sorter. The recombinant progeny (as yellow, both GFP and mCherry positive) were sorted as described previously.40 The yellow oocysts were verified by live fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Microscope Axio Observer.Z1). The in vivo cross was repeated with AUB-UPRT-mCherry and IOWA-AZ-TK-GFP in four GKO mice with antibiotics.

Generation of bulk sequencing pools

For each cross experiment, eighteen GKO mice were divided into two groups (n = 9 for each group), including permissive condition groups (pre-treated with antibiotics five days prior infection) and restrictive condition groups. Each mouse was infected with 2 × 104 oocysts on 0 dpi. Fecal samples were collected from each mouse every two days after infection to monitor the oocyst shedding pattern by measuring nanoluciferase activity using Cytation 3. Mouse survival was monitored daily. Intestinal contents were collected after they were sacrificed.

The sorted yellow oocysts were orally gavaged to three antibiotic-treated GKO mice (referred to as 1st GKO + antibiotics). The intestinal contents of the mice were retained for purification of oocysts, which were used to infect three additional GKO mice in two sequential groups (referred to as 2nd GKO + antibiotics and 3rd GKO + antibiotics). The oocysts from the 3rd + antibiotics were used to infect three GKO mice in the absence of antibiotics and this process was repeated in three sequential pools of mice (referred to as 1st GKO, 2nd GKO, and 3rd GKO). C. parvum oocysts were purified separately from each mouse by sucrose and cesium chloride gradient centrifugation as described above.

WGS of bulk pools and bulk segregant analysis

The sorted yellow oocysts and purified C. parvum oocysts of florescent tagged lines and from each mouse of 1st GKO + antibiotics, 3rd GKO+ antibiotics, 1st GKO, and 3rd GKO were used for WGS. To increase the DNA extraction, the phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction method was used to extract whole genome DNA (wgDNA) from 106 oocysts. Whole-genome amplification (WGA) was performed on the extracted wgDNA from 105 oocysts using a REPLI-g single cell kit (Qiagen, Cat. 150043) following the manufacturer-recommended. DNA products were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using the 150-bp paired-end approach.

All sequence reads were processed together. They were trimmed and mapped to the reference C. parvum IOWA-CDC genome, while duplicate reads were marked and removed as described above. Genome coverage and sequence depth were estimated using SAMtools v1.7. The mpileup and call algorithms of BCFtools v1.12 were used to generate a VCF file of sequence variants, with parameters -d 500 and -ploidy 1. Low quality SNPs (QUAL < 30, FORMAT/DP < 3 and AVG FORMAT/DP < 25) were filtered out using the BCFtools filter algorithm. To obtain the list of SNPs that differed between the parents in the core genome, only homozygous SNPs were retained, based on >90% reference allele frequency in AUB and IOWA-AZ genomes with a read depth > 25.

The VCF file was formatted as a TABLE file for BSA using VariantsToTable algorithm of GATK. Allele frequencies for SNPs were compared between pools, including F1 generations of AUB and IOWA-AZ, recombinant lines passaged under permissive conditions (antibiotic-treated GKO mice), and recombinant lines passaged in mice after selective pressure (untreated GKO mice) using a QTLseqr package in R.83 Identification of QTLs in bulk pools was performed using quantitative trait locus (QTL)-seq and G’ approaches in the QTLseqr package. A sliding window of 100 kb was used to calculate the tricube-smoothed Δ(SNP index) and G′ values across all eight chromosomes. Significant QTLs defining regions responsible for enhanced fitness were estimated based on 95% confidence intervals with a false discovery rate of 0.01. In addition, G′ values between the permissive and restrictive conditions were represented as mean of G′ values between individual pools. SNPs that differ between the two parental isolates within the 100 kb window of QTL boundaries were used to identify candidate genes.

Construction of CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids and generation of transgenic parasites

The CRISPR/Cas9 technology was used to generate transgenic C. parvum lines with fluorescent and nanoluciferase reporters as described.47 Cas9 plasmids were generated by adding sgRNAs targeting the gene of interest to the linear Cas9 plasmid using the Gibson assembly kit. Two sgRNAs targeting the 3′ and 5′ end of the cgd7_4520 CDS regions were used to knock out (KO) the gene, while two gRNAs targeting the 5′ and 3′ UTR were used to tag or swap the gene. sgRNA sequences are listed in the key resources table.

To generate the repair plasmid for the KO line, the homology repair fragments were PCR amplified from the AUB genome, consisting of 220 bp of the 5′ UTR and 277 bp of the 3′ UTR of cgd7_4520. The fragments were assembled with a reporter-drug selection cassette (-GFP-Nluc-P2A-neo-) amplified from TK-GFP-Nluc-P2A-neo using a Gibson assembly kit. To generate the repair plasmids for tag and swap lines, a 539 bp of the 5′ UTR and the entire CDS of the cgd7_4520 (4179 bp) were amplified from the AUB and IOWA-AZ genomes, respectively, as the 5′ homology repair fragments, while a 650 bp of the 3′ UTR was amplified from the AUB genome as the 3′ homology repair fragments. The fragments were assembled with a reporter-drug selection cassette (−3HA-Nluc-P2A-neo-) amplified from TK-GFP-Nluc-P2A-neo using the Gibson assembly kit. The repair plasmids were linearized using Pst 1 restriction endonuclease (New England Biolabs, lot: 10227210) before transfection.

To provide greater sensitivity for detecting the cgd7_4520 protein, the AUB isolate was tagged with a spaghetti monster HA tag (smHA).74 A gRNA targeting the 5′ UTR of the cgd7_4520 was used, and 213 bp of the 3′ UTR was amplified from the AUB genome as 5′ homology repair fragments. The 3′ homology repair fragment was taken from the tag/swap plasmids. The fragments were assembled with a reporter-drug selection cassette (-smHA-Nluc-P2A-neo-) amplified from CpGT2-smHA-Nluc-P2A-neo74 using a Gibson assembly kit.

The AUB sporozoites were generated as above and electroporated with linear repair plasmids (25 ug) together with the corresponding Cas9 plasmids (25 ug). The electroporated parasites were orally gavaged to three GKO mice. Transgenic parasites were selected and verified as described above. Purified oocysts (2 × 104) were orally gavaged to two to three NSG mice to amplify the transgenic lines. The GKO and NSG mice for KO and SWAP lines were pre-treated with antibiotics five days prior infection. C. parvum oocysts were purified from fecal samples as described above. The transgenic lines were checked by nanoluciferase activity and PCR as described above, and their related primers show in the key resources table.

IFA localization

Excysted sporozoites (1 × 106 per coverslip) were added to poly-D-lysine coated coverslips for 1 h at 37 °C and air dried. HCT-8 cells were plated at 3 × 105 cells per well in 24-well tissue culture plates with 12-mm-diameter glass coverslips (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 24 h. Monolayers were infected with 1 × 105 bleached oocysts. The coverslips were fixed and permeabilized/blocked as described above. Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in TSS and stained the coverslips for 60 min each. Primary antibodies included Rat antibody HA diluted 1:500, mouse antibody 1B5 diluted 1:500, mouse antibody 4D8 diluted 1:20, rabbit PanCp diluted 1:5000, while secondary antibodies included anti-rat 488, anti-mouse 568 (igM), anti-rat 647, anti-mouse 568, anti-rabbit 568, and anti-mouse 647 all diluted 1:1000. Hoechst 33342 diluted 1:2000 and phalloidin-488 stain diluted 1:200 were used to stain nuclides and actin proteins. Coverslips were mounted on glass slides using ProLong Glass antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sealed with nail polish.

U-ExM was applied as described previously.93 Excysted sporozoites were plated at 1 × 106 for each coverslip as described above. Samples were embedded overnight in a mixture of 1% acrylamide and 0.7% formaldehyde for protein anchor and crosslinking prevention. Polymerization of expansion gel was performed on ice containing monomer solution (19% sodium acrylate/10% acrylamide/0.1% (1,2-dihydroxyethylene), 0.5% ammonium persulfate (APS) and 0.5% tetramethyl ethylenediamine (TEMED). Polymerized gels were denatured at 95°C for 90 min in the denaturation buffer (200 mM SDS, 200 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-Base, pH= 9.0) and expanded in H2O overnight. Well expanded gels were shrunk in PBS and stained with rat anti-HA at 1:200 overnight under 37°C, followed by anti-rat 488 at 1:500 for 5 h under 37°C, and Hoechst at 1:500 overnight under room temperature. All antibodies were diluted in PBS with 2% BSA (freshly made). After each staining, the gels were washed three times with PBS/0.1% Tween for 10 min. Stained gels were re-expanded in water overnight for imaging.

All images were captured on a Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63x, 1.4 N.A. Zeiss Plan Apochromat oil objective lens and acquired using ZEN v2.5 software.

Transgenic C. parvum growth assay

HCT-8 cells were plated in the black-sided, clear-bottomed 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) and grown to confluence. Cells were infected with 5 × 103 bleached oocysts for each well, cells were fixed and labeled as described above (PanCp as primary antibody, anti-rabbit 488 as secondary antibody, and Hoechst 33342 for DNA). Plates were imaged using a 10× objective on a BioTek Cytation 3 cell imager (20 images per well in a 5 × 4 grid). BioTek Gen5 v3.08 was used to quantify the total number of parasites (GFP channel) and host cells (DAPI channel) per well.

Experimental infection of mice with transgenic C. parvum

To evaluate the in vivo infectivity and virulence of transgenic C. parvum, GKO mice were divided into groups (n > 3 for each group), and each mouse was infected with 2 × 104 purified oocysts by oral gavage. Fecal samples were collected per mouse every three days after infection to monitor the oocyst shedding patterns by measuring nanoluciferase activity as described above. Mouse survival was monitored daily. The experiment was terminated when all mice in a group died or lost 20% of their body weight.

Structural prediction and functional annotation of target genes

The target genes were annotated by searching the NCBI non-redundant protein database using BLASTp v2.10.1 with an e-value of 1 × 10−6. In addition, the amino acid sequence of cgd7_4520 was uploaded to SMART (https://smart.embl.de), which provided the identification and extensive annotation of protein domains. The protein structure of cgd7_4520 was predicted using AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com).

16S sequencing and analysis

To evaluate the effect of antibiotic treatment in mice, four GKO mice were given the antibiotic water for five days. Fecal samples were collected on 1 day before treatment, 5 days after, and 5 days after stopping treatment. DNA from cecal contents was isolated as described above. Each sample was amplified in triplicate with Golay-barcoded primers specific for the V4 region (F515/R806) and checked by gel electrophoresis. The final pooled samples, along with aliquots of the three sequencing primers, were sequenced using a paired-end 250bp Illumina protocol. Reads were filtered and processed using QIIME 2 pipeline.85 Taxonomy and functional groups were determined using the release 138 of the SILVA database (https://www.arb-silva.de).

Molecular docking

A collection of 7 metabolites that are associated with the adult murine gut microbiota and inhibit C. parvum growth in HCT-8 cells22 were selected for molecular docking. The AlphaFold structures of AUB and IOWA-AZ cgd7_4520 proteins, with the highest scores, were docked to each metabolite using Vina v1.2.5 following the manufacturer’s protocol. The search space including the binding site was defined based on the binding pocket of human MRP4 with a grid size of 20.

C. parvum growth assay with DCA and LCA

To compare the EC50 values of the metabolites for inhibiting transgenic C. parvum, DCA and LCA were tested in a 9-point serial dilution series starting at 140 μM. C. parvum growth assays were performed as described above, and relative parasite numbers were calculated as a ratio of the number of C. parvum in treated wells divided by the mean number of parasites in 0.2% DMSO negative control wells. EC50 and EC90 values were calculated in GraphPad Prism 9 using a non-linear regression curve. In addition, the C. parvum growth assay was also performed under EC50 and EC90 concentrations (based on wild type AUB) of DCA and LCA using the transgenic C. parvum lines.

To examine the role of DCA in growth inhibition of C. parvum, HCT-8 cells were plated at 3 × 105 cells per in 24-well tissue culture plates with 12-mm-diameter glass coverslips and incubated for 24 h. Monolayers were infected with 1 × 105 oocysts of C. parvum WT AUB or transgenic lines and treated at EC50 or EC90 concentrations of DCA in 0.2% DMSO medium. The coverslips were fixed and stained as described above. The number of parasites at each life stage was counted from 10 fields using a 100× oil immersion objective on a Zeiss Axioskop Mot Plus fluorescence microscope. The sum of parasites at each life stage at each time point was divided by the total number of C. parvum for that time point. Ratios were averaged over three independent experiments.

Neonatal mouse model

To assess infection levels of the transgenic C. parvum with DCA in vivo, DCA solutions were prepared at 40 mg/mL in vehicle (100%-DMSO), aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Five-day-old C57BL/6 mice were divided into six groups (n ≥ 6 for each group). Three of them were treatment groups with 5 mg/kg daily DCA, and the other three were control groups with vehicle daily. On the first day of the experiment (0 dpi), each mouse was infected with 2 × 104 purified oocysts by oral gavage in 50 μL PBS. Mice received 50 μL gavage of their treatment solution every 24 h since 1 dpi. Fecal samples were collected per mouse every day from 6 to 9 dpi to monitor the oocyst shedding patterns by measuring nanoluciferase activity as described above.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

All statistical analyses were performed using the Prism v10 unless otherwise specified. A Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test was used to compare the survival curves of two groups. A Mann Whitney U test was used to compare the means of two groups. A two-way ANOVA test was used to examine differences in experimental groups across multiple experiments or time points corrected for multiple comparisons using the Sidak method was used to compare the means of three or more groups at a same time point.
