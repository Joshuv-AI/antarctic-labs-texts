Mechanical hypertrophy versus biological optimization: rethinking the role of ALPPS in the era of systemic therapy
Authors: Yu-Han Cui, Guo-Qing Zhang, Xin Liang, Zu-Chao Du, Jia-Hao Xu, Li-Hui Gu, Ming-Da Wang, Chao Li, Tian Yang
Published: 2026

In “Assessing Surgical Innovation: ALPPS: An IDEAL Example of Disruptive Innovation” published in Annals of Surgery, Linecker et al. recently characterized the evolution of associating liver partition and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy (ALPPS) as a successful model of disruptive surgical innovation using the IDEAL paradigm (1). They also emphasized that colorectal liver metastases (CRLMs) represent the premier indication with clear survival benefits, while applying ALPPS in other hepatobiliary malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) necessitates rigorous patient selection. However, the historical significance of this mechanical approach must be weighed against the biological optimization offered by contemporary oncological strategies.

ALPPS was initially heralded as a transformative innovation in hepatobiliary surgery. By increasing future liver remnant (FLR) volume by 50% to 80% within just 7 to 11 days, ALPPS enabled curative resection for patients with advanced hepatobiliary malignancies previously deemed unresectable due to insufficient FLR volume (2). However, because ALPPS combines portal vein ligation with extensive in situ parenchymal splitting, the extremely high incidence of perioperative morbidity and dismal long-term oncological survival observed in its early clinical applications necessitated urgent refinement of the original procedure (3). Technical modifications including minimally invasive robotic approaches have subsequently improved perioperative outcomes (4). Data from the ALPPS Italian Registry shows that the utilization of this procedure has remained relatively stable over the past decade, while the adoption of minimally invasive approaches has significantly increased, acting as a protective factor against short-term mortality (5). ALPPS has evolved from initial enthusiasm into a specialized tool whose safety is highly dependent on patient selection and functional assessment.

Analyses of interstage complications demonstrate that adverse events following the first stage, particularly biliary leakage and subsequent septic shock, severely impair interstage hepatic regeneration. These infective complications are independent predictors of post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) and mortality following the second stage (3,6). Therefore, rigorous interstage infection control and precise functional assessment must be conducted to ensure the safety of completion hepatectomy. Furthermore, it is demonstrated that while rapid hypertrophy occurs, the actual functional recovery of the FLR is overestimated by the morphological volume increase during the early interstage phase (7). This critical dissociation between morphological volume and actual hepatic functional capacity explains why PHLF occurs even when FLR volume appears sufficient. Consequently, dynamic functional assessments utilizing hepatobiliary scintigraphy appear critical to prevent premature second-stage interventions and to mitigate the risk of mortality.

However, ensuring functional safety does not change the fact that ALPPS addresses only the anatomical constraint of insufficient liver volume while neglecting the underlying tumor biology. Advanced hepatobiliary malignancies frequently demonstrate occult intrahepatic micrometastases and circulating tumor cells at presentation. ALPPS facilitates macroscopic tumor clearance but fails to address microscopic systemic dissemination. Consequently, the high incidence of early recurrence remains a formidable barrier to achieving long-term survival (8). For instance, in CRLM, despite achieving R0 resection in over 90% of cases, overall survival (OS) remains comparable to two-stage hepatectomy (TSH), suggesting that surgical radicality, limited by physiological and biological factors, does not translate into proportional survival benefits (9).

The LIGRO randomized controlled trial published in 2021 reported higher resectability and longer median OS with ALPPS than with conventional TSH in patients with CRLM (10). Its intention-to-treat (ITT) design and the dropout observed in the TSH group are clinically relevant, because failure to achieve sufficient hypertrophy after portal vein embolization or TSH is an inherent limitation of conventional TSH. At the same time, survival was not the primary endpoint of the original trial. The cross-over design permitting rescue ALPPS in the TSH group complicates attribution, and ALPPS candidates may differ from TSH candidates in baseline clinical and oncological characteristics (11-13). Therefore, the LIGRO trial should not be simply interpreted as proving routine superiority of ALPPS for all patients. Rather, it supports a cautious comparison between ALPPS and TSH, heavily dependent on patient-specific surgical risks and oncological factors. It also advocates individualized comparison among TSH, portal vein embolization or ligation, rescue ALPPS, systemic therapy, and, in emerging selected CRLM pathways, liver transplantation (14).

While technical refinements and rigorous patient selection have optimized the application of ALPPS, the profound impact of evolving systemic therapies must not be overlooked. In HCC, combination immunotherapy regimens, particularly atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and durvalumab plus tremelimumab, have established new standards of care, achieving objective response rates of approximately 20% to 30% and extending median overall survival to 16 to 19 months. The combination of lenvatinib with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors has also shown meaningful tumor shrinkage and has facilitated conversion of initially unresectable tumors to resectability in selected patients (15). For CRLM, modern chemotherapy backbones combined with targeted agents against EGFR or VEGF, and immunotherapy for subsets with high microsatellite instability or deficient mismatch repair, have improved disease control and help identify favorable tumor biology (16).

Collectively, these findings advocate for transitioning from immediate surgical intervention toward prioritizing initial systemic control. This should not be interpreted as viewing surgery and systemic therapy as mutually exclusive. Rather, systemic therapy functions not merely as palliation but as a stratification mechanism, identifying patients with biologically favorable disease who will benefit from subsequent resection while sparing those with aggressive systemic dissemination from futile major surgery. Therefore, rather than replacing ALPPS, modern systemic therapies can achieve risk stratification and select ideal candidates, maximizing the procedure's therapeutic efficacy.

The future of advanced hepatobiliary malignancy management lies not in the advancements of radical resection alone, but also in biological optimization, functional verification, and precision timing of surgical intervention. Routine upfront ALPPS lacks robust clinical rationale in the era of systemic control and functional assessment. However, ALPPS preserves its utility as a highly selective, specialist, curative-intent rescue strategy for carefully selected patients, particularly those with CRLM who demonstrate biologically controlled disease, preserved hepatic function, and inadequate FLR volume. The central debate is not whether ALPPS should be universally adopted or discarded, but how to integrate it into modern multimodal liver oncology.
