Peripheral odontogenic keratocyst - A case report
Authors: Hung-Pin Lin, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

An odontogenic keratocyst (OKC) is well known for its distinctive histopathological features, relatively aggressive behavior, and its association with Gorlin syndrome.1,2 The extraosseous or peripheral odontogenic keratocyst (POKC) represents a rare variant of odontogenic cysts that arises in soft tissue.1–3 In this report, we described a case of POKC occurring in the cheek of a male patient. This case was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of MacKay Memorial Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (25MMHIS421e).

A 47-year-old male patient was referred to our hospital for evaluation and management of a swelling in the left cheek that had persisted for several weeks. Intraoral examination revealed a rubbery mass with an intact mucosal surface located in the left buccal mucosa. An initial clinical impression of infection led to the prescription of antibiotics for one week to the patient; however, no significant improvement was observed. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a hypodense lesion measuring 2.9 × 2.1 cm in the left masticator space, without evident contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A and B). With a provisional diagnosis of a cystic lesion, complete surgical excision was performed under general anesthesia, and the specimen was submitted for histopathological examination. The postoperative course was uneventful.

Computed tomography (CT) images and histopathological photomicrographs of the peripheral odontogenic keratocyst (POKC) in the present case. (A and B) Axial (A) and cropped coronal (B) CT images demonstrate a hypodense lesion (circled) measuring 2.9 × 2.1 cm in the left masticator space, without appreciable contrast enhancement. (C and D) Low-power photomicrographs show a cystic lesion lined by a thin layer of parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, exhibiting a relatively flat interface with the underlying connective tissue. Focal separation of the epithelial lining from the connective tissue is observed (curved arrow in D). (E and F) Medium-power photomicrographs reveal a cyst lined by parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium (arrowheads), characterized by hyperchromatic, palisaded columnar basal cells. A moderate chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate is present in the subepithelial connective tissue (asterisk in F). (Hematoxylin and eosin staining; scale bars: C and D = 2 mm; E and F = 200 μm).

Microscopic examination revealed a cystic lesion lined by a thin layer of the parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium with a relatively flat interface between the epithelium and the underlying connective tissue (Fig. 1C and D). Partial detachment of the epithelial lining from the connective tissue was noted (Fig. 1D). The lining epithelium exhibited characteristic features, including a basal layer of hyperchromatic, palisaded columnar cells and a parakeratinized surface (Fig. 1E and F). Additionally, a moderate chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate was present within the fibrous connective tissue wall (Fig. 1F). Correlation of the clinical findings with these characteristic histopathological features established the diagnosis of a POKC.

Although considerably less common than intraosseous OKCs, lesions with similar characteristics have been reported in the gingiva and other oral soft tissues.1–3 The pathogenesis of POKC may involve ectopic odontogenic epithelium or, in subcutaneous cases, dermal appendages.2 A recent systematic review by Huang et al. summarized the clinical features and treatment outcomes of POKCs.3 These lesions show a slight male predominance and a mean patient age of 54 years.3 The most commonly affected sites include the gingiva (56.9%), followed by the buccal mucosa (27.5%), and other locations.3 One case reported in that review involved the same anatomical site as the present case.3,4 However, it is important to consider the possibility that some lesions may represent recurrence of a previously treated intraosseous OKC extending into the adjacent soft tissue, rather than the true primary peripheral lesions, particularly based on the patient history.2,3 Among 34 reported cases with follow-up data, a recurrence rate of 20.6% was observed.3 Huang et al. recommended complete excision under magnification, such as with a surgical microscope, along with long-term follow-up as an appropriate management strategy for POKCs.

Cheek swelling is a clinical presentation that may be encountered in dental practice. The differential diagnosis of the cheek swelling includes infectious and inflammatory conditions, lymphadenopathy, mesenchymal neoplasms, and cystic lesions.5 This case highlights POKC as a rare cause of cheek swelling and underscores the critical role of histopathological examination in establishing a definitive diagnosis, which is essential for guiding appropriate management.
