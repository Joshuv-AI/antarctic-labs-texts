“Addressing the urgent need for external cephalic version training and promotion: a survey on theoretical course and practical workshops participants” – a pilot study
Authors: Kuba Ptaszkiewicz, Magdalena Kołak, Lena Wójcik, Bartosz Wróbel, Zuzanna Gałuszka, Patryk Mickiewicz, Hubert Huras, Andrzej Jaworowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesExternal cephalic version (ECV) is a valuable technique for reducing the incidence of planned cesarean deliveries due to fetal malpresentation. The objective of the study was to evaluate the impact of a course conducted by specialists from a top-tier reference hospital on participants and their willingness to incorporate the acquired knowledge of performing external cephalic version (ECV) into their daily medical practice.MethodsA survey-based study was conducted among 20 participants, including gynecology and obstetrics residents and specialists, who attended an ECV training course. The course combined theoretical instruction with hands-on practice. Knowledge and confidence levels were assessed before and after the course using standardized questionnaires and a 14-question test. The survey consists of a substantive part, testing the participant’s knowledge before and after the course, and a survey part, asking about the expectations, level of satisfaction, and sense of confidence in performing the procedure after the course. The normality of the data was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test, Student’s t-test was applied for variables with normal distribution whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for variables with non-normal distribution.ResultsOut of the 20 respondents, only 15 successfully completed the surveys, as some responses were incomplete or unclear, some participants completed only the pre-course survey, and others provided multiple selections. In the initial knowledge test, participants achieved an average score of 7.8 out of 14 points (± 1.86), which increased to 12.33 points (± 1.84) in the post-course test. This reflects an average improvement of 4.53 points (± 2.55) out of 14, corresponding to a group average 68.15% (± 49%) increase in knowledge (p < 0,001).ConclusionThe study demonstrates that structured ECV training significantly enhances both knowledge and hopefully confidence among gynecologists and obstetricians. Participants showed a notable improvement in theoretical understanding and readiness to perform ECV. These findings highlight the need to integrate ECV training into specialist education to promote its wider adoption and potentially reduce cesarean section rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08061-2.Keywords: External cephalic version, ECV training, Obstetric education, Cesarean section reduction, Fetal malpresentation, Gynecology, Obstetrics, Medical training, Hands-on simulation

Introduction

One strategy to decrease the incidence of planned cesarean sections for breech presentation is the external cephalic version (ECV), which has an overall success rate of around 50% [1]. ECV is a non-invasive obstetric procedure performed in late pregnancy to manually rotate the fetus from a breech or transverse position to a cephalic (head-down) presentation by applying pressure to the maternal abdomen under ultrasound guidance. The goal of ECV is to increase the likelihood of vaginal delivery and reduce the need for cesarean section, which is commonly required in cases of persistent breech presentation. When performed by experienced clinicians and in appropriate candidates, ECV offers a safe and effective alternative to planned cesarean delivery. ECV is generally considered a safe procedure that does not increase the risk of perinatal complications [1–3]. Despite its safety, maternal anxiety and fear, often stemming from a lack of widespread awareness and patient education about the procedure, can lead to reluctance to undergo the procedure. Approximately 25% of women opt out of ECV, and over 40% express concerns about pain and potential risks to the fetus [4, 5]. Consequently, efforts are being made to identify patients who may be more (e.g., AFI >10 cm) or less (e.g., obese) suitable candidates for the procedure. Several factors that could influence the success rate of ECV remain the subject of ongoing debate. Although various prediction models and scoring systems have been developed to enhance the success rate of ECV, they show inconsistent results. Positive predictors of successful ECV often include higher parity, a posterior placenta, amniotic fluid volume, and fetal position [6–14]. Other influential factors, such as estimated fetal weight, palpation of the fetal head, fundal height, and uterine tone, are still uncertain and remain a topic of discussion. Overweight and obesity present significant obstetric challenges, particularly given the rapid increase in the prevalence of overweight and pre-pregnancy obesity in recent years [15]. Being overweight alone is associated with a higher risk of comorbidities during pregnancy, and cesarean sections in overweight women are linked to an increased risk of complications, such as Premature Rupture of Membranes or Umbilical Cord Prolapse [16, 17].

Various international guidelines recommend that external cephalic version (ECV) should be offered to eligible women with breech presentation at term, and its success is influenced not only by patient-related factors but also by the provider’s experience and technical skill. In response to this, several countries have developed structured training programs to improve clinicians’ competence and confidence in performing ECV. These initiatives typically include a combination of theoretical modules, simulation-based training using mannequins, and supervised hands-on clinical practice. Although several simulation-based training programs for ECV have been introduced to improve provider confidence and procedural uptake, existing evidence on their effectiveness remains inconclusive. Some studies show increased institutional adoption following training, but without significant improvement in success rates or clinical outcomes [18]. Despite these efforts, uptake and standardization of ECV training remain inconsistent across institutions, and evidence on the long-term clinical impact of such programs is still limited. These gaps highlight the need for further research into the effectiveness of provider training.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured ECV in-person training session by administering pre- and post-intervention surveys, with the aim of assessing changes in participants’ procedural competence and their intent to integrate ECV into clinical practice, as a potential strategy to reduce cesarean delivery rates over time.

Materials and methods

Participants

The sample group for this study consists of 20 participants, all actively practicing OB doctors, including 7 residents specializing in gynecology and obstetrics and 13 specialists. The majority of the respondents (60%) were male, while 40% were female. Four individuals held the title of MD/PhD, while the remaining participants were medical doctors. In terms of work location, 11 participants work in large cities with a population of over 200,000 inhabitants, 5 work in mid-sized cities with a population of 100,000–200,000 people, and 4 work in small towns with under 100,000 people. Additionally, the participants came to the in-person training from different regions of Poland, as they are all members of various regional medical councils.

Survey and test creation

The study is based on administered questionnaires completed before and after the course, along with a 14-question test assessing theoretical knowledge. The questionnaire was developed by a team of five, including 3 experienced lecturers from Jagiellonian University, tutors and course creators (MD, specialist Ob&Gyn and specialist of perinatology), and 2 students who helped during the course. Alpha Cronbach 0.816. The questionnaires consist of standardized questions. The pre-course questionnaire inquires about prior experience with the ECV procedure, the respondent’s self-assessment of their theoretical and practical knowledge, and their expectations for the course. The post-course questionnaire evaluates participant satisfaction, perceived confidence in performing the procedure after completing the course, and self-assessed improvement in the form of Likert scale questions.

The test comprises 14 single- or multiple-choice questions jointly developed by the course instructors [see Supplementary files 1 & 2]. It was reviewed and refined by a total of five individuals: experienced lecturers from Jagiellonian University with extensive expertise in designing examination questions, as well as students. The questions primarily addressed key fundamental aspects of the ECV procedure essential for its safe execution. The difficulty of the questions varied, with some focusing on specific scenarios or highly detailed topics. In addition to the 14 core survey questions described above, an additional set of 6 pre-course and 8 post-course questions using a 10-point Likert-type scale was included to evaluate participants’ subjective assessment of their theoretical and practical knowledge, procedural confidence, and expectations regarding ECV. These questions were designed to measure self-perceived proficiency, the likelihood of future ECV implementation in clinical practice, and the perceived value of the training session. The items were included both before and after the training to allow comparative analysis of knowledge and attitude changes (see Supplementary Files 1 and 2).

During the course, all necessary content for correctly answering the test was covered and explained. Participants completed the initial questionnaire along with the test upon entering the room without access to the correct answers. The second test was administered after both the theoretical and practical components of the course, at which point participants received their results, the correct answers, and a discussion of the questions. The questionnaires were entirely anonymous.

Course structure

The course was led by experienced specialists at a top-tier reference center and was designed for doctors undergoing specialist training, as well as for obstetrics and gynecology specialists who wished to enhance their practical skills in this area. The course consisted of two parts. The first part was a short lecture given by an expert specialist in fetal-maternal medicine, who covered various aspects of the ECV procedure, including the technique for performing the procedure, its indications, potential complications, and the necessary conditions for carrying out the procedure. Additionally, the doctors explained how to monitor the fetus during the ECV procedure. In the second part, the participants were divided into two groups. One group focused on learning and practicing the correct technique for performing ECV on the realistic model (Model LM-080, Koken, Tokyo, Japan) under the guidance of an experienced doctor. Model practice involved participants performing predefined movement sequences [Table 1; Fig. 1] derived from techniques used in other centers (excluding closed-eye exercises. The second group learned communication skills with a simulated patient (SP). The groups were then switched so that each group underwent both types of training.

Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using RStudio version 2023.09.1 + 494. All survey responses were treated as continuous variables in all tests. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Student’s t-test was applied for variables with normal distribution, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for variables with non-normal distribution. Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The Linear Model was created for assessing the correlation between average expectations before to average ratings after. We also calculated the average percentage increase to quantify the change in observed outcomes before and after intervention. This calculation allowed for a standardized comparison of the magnitude of change, expressed as a percentage of the initial (pre-intervention) value.

All outcomes were analyzed and discussed together for self-improvement purposes.

Results

Out of the 20 respondents, only 15 successfully completed the surveys, as some responses were incomplete or unclear, some participants completed only the pre-course survey, and others exceeded the number of allowed answers.

In the initial knowledge test, participants achieved an average score of 7.8 out of 14 points (± 1.86), which increased to 12.33 points (± 1.84) in the post-course test [Table 2]. This reflects an average improvement of 4.53 points (± 2.55) out of 14, corresponding to a group average 58.08% (± 35.50%) increase in knowledge (p < 0,001) [Table 2].

Survey and test results, on entry and after training

Two of the course participants had prior experience performing the external cephalic version (ECV) procedure, while 13 had previously observed or assisted in it at least once. Having completed the course, all the participants felt confident implementing the ECV procedure in their practice.

A significant increase in self-confidence was observed, rising from an initial mean score of 3.13 (± 2.27) to 7.06 (± 1.8) out of 10, reflecting a 2.25-fold improvement [Table 2]. When comparing the subjective assessment of theoretical knowledge before and after the course with the corresponding test scores, a statistically significant trend (Adjusted R² = 0.53, p < 0,01) was noted, suggesting a potential correlation between increased confidence and improved test performance.

The course received an overall rating of 9.2/10 in the post-course survey. Comparing average expectations before (7.8 ± 0.9 out of 10) to average ratings after (9.2 ± 0.8 out of 10), we observed a statistically significant average increase of 1.4 (p < 0.01) [Table 2]. In a scale of 1 to 10, the question of ECV being a procedure of choice was granted 8.6 points (± 1,4) [Table 2], with 8 participants stating ECV was always their procedure of choice. Additionally, 12 participants emphasized that the ECV course was highly important, even essential, in the training process of obstetricians.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates the positive impact of an ECV procedure course, combining both theoretical and practical training, on increasing knowledge and self-confidence in performing the procedure. The results of the study are promising, indicating that such a course can significantly enhance the competence of gynecologists and obstetricians, potentially enabling them to incorporate ECV into their daily clinical practice.

The knowledge gain observed in this study is substantial. The average score in the initial knowledge test was 7.8 out of 14 points (± 1.86), which increased to 12.33 points (± 1.84) in the post-course test, reflecting an average improvement of 4.53 points (± 2.55, p < 0.01), corresponding to, on average, a 58.08% (± 35.50%) increase in knowledge. This finding highlights the course’s effectiveness in improving the participants’ theoretical knowledge regarding the ECV procedure. However, it is important to note that the subjective assessment of theoretical knowledge before and after the course showed a statistically significant trend (R² = 0.56, p < 0.01), suggesting that the self-assessment might reflect the actual improvement. This may indicate that further research involving a larger sample size is necessary to draw definitive conclusions about the correlation between the course and knowledge enhancement. The establishment of a stronger correlation would be valuable for determining the true impact of such training programs and whether they should be a standard component of gynecological education.

A particularly noteworthy finding is the significant improvement in participants’ self-confidence. The average confidence score increased from 3.13 (± 2.27) to 7.06 (± 1.8) out of 10, representing a 2.25-fold improvement. Confidence plays a crucial role in the willingness of healthcare professionals to perform medical procedures, and this increase suggests that the training course had a direct positive effect on participants’ perceived ability to perform the ECV. The other factor causing the reluctance to perform ECV is, as noted in a study by Harendarczyk [19], prioritizing the perceived safety over the potential benefits of vaginal delivery by health professionals. This concern might be resolved by attending the theoretical part of the course and learning the true statistics of the incidence of any complications of ECV, oscillating around 6%. While the incidence of serious complications such as fetal death or placental abruption, according to meta-analytic data, occurs in only 0.24% of procedures [20]. The rise in self-confidence is of particular importance in light of the high rates of elective cesarean sections in Poland [21], as increased confidence in performing ECV may help reduce reliance on this surgical option, thereby promoting more vaginal deliveries when appropriate.

To further understand the attitude towards ECV in Poland, it is important to mention the findings from the survey research by Manasar-Dyrbus [22], which stresses that most Polish obstetricians prefer elective CS over ECV for term pregnancies with non-cephalic presentation. The relatively low level of knowledge about ECV highlights the need for improved education. Enhanced training and better anesthesia support could increase acceptance of ECV and reduce CS rates. It is also worth mentioning that experienced practicing physicians, not familiar with the method, expressed greater concerns about ECV complications and emergency CS risks than eager, newly trained, and more open-minded physicians. This indicates that ECV training may benefit every gynecologist and obstetrician regardless of their experience.

Additionally, 12 participants highlighted the ECV course’s essential role in the obstetricians’ training process, underlining its value in changing attitudes towards the procedure. The course increased participants’ theoretical knowledge and practical skills and hopefully altered their perspective on the importance of ECV in obstetric practice. This shift in perspective is crucial, as it suggests that well-structured training programs can effectively foster a more positive and proactive approach to the procedure, ultimately improving clinical outcomes. Research papers by Coltart [23], Hutton [24], and Baumgart [25] have also shown that hands-on training, along with standardized training programs, can have a positive impact on ECV outcomes as well as reduce the number of unnecessary cesarean sections.

On the other hand, there is evidence [18] suggesting a lack of correlation between participation in courses and workshops and subsequent success in external cephalic version (ECV). While inexperience is known to be a risk factor for ECV failure [24], it remains unclear whether this experience pertains to practice on models or on patients. It appears to us that participation in introductory courses is necessary for complete novices to familiarize themselves with the procedure. Subsequent courses may serve a primarily educational or refresher function; however, the fact remains that courses and practice on phantoms cannot substitute for clinical experience.

Our analysis demonstrated that Polish practitioners recognize the necessity of incorporating ECV training into specialist education. However, the absence of such courses, coupled with a general lack of knowledge about ECV [22], fosters apprehension regarding the implementation of this procedure in hospitals and clinical settings. Our survey revealed a significant increase in self-confidence following the course, which comprised both theoretical and practical components, as well as a heightened readiness to perform ECV independently. Given the alarming and continuously rising rate of cesarean sections in Poland, we strongly believe that expanding the availability of such training programs, disseminating knowledge, and addressing fears surrounding ECV are critical steps toward reversing this troubling trend.

Strengths and limitations

While our study’s results are encouraging, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. The sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Many of the participants were already familiar with the method. They knew literature, assisted in or performed the procedure under supervision. It might have impacted the results from the entrance test, inflating the results, and lowering % of improvement and improvement/self-assessment ratio in comparison between pre- and post-surveys. Furthermore, the lack of long-term follow-up data means that we cannot assess the lasting impact of the course on participants’ clinical practice or their sustained confidence in performing ECV. Future studies involving larger cohorts and long-term assessments are necessary to confirm the benefits of such training programs.

Moreover, we cannot overstate the significance of ECV as a method of choice. As practitioners performing ECV and focusing our attention on research into that matter, we are biased towards preferring ECV as a solution. As the rate of vaginal deliveries has been declining [25, 26], and cesarean section rates approach 50% in Poland, against the indications of WHO and European standards [27].

Our course has garnered the interest of a wide range of medical professionals, including not only residents but also seasoned specialists, doctors of medical sciences, and professors. Consequently, despite its relatively small size, the surveyed group is highly diverse, enabling the collection of insights from representatives of various professional backgrounds—not only from major academic centres but also from private practitioners and physicians working in smaller towns and district medical centers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study supports the importance of incorporating ECV training courses into the education of gynecologists and obstetricians. The significant increase in both knowledge and self-confidence demonstrates that such training can effectively prepare healthcare professionals to perform the procedure with greater competence. Nevertheless, further research is needed to establish definitive evidence regarding the course’s long-term effects and its potential to reduce the reliance on cesarean sections.
