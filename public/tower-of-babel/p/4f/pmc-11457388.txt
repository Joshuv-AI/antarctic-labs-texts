Artificial intelligence in predicting recurrence after first-line treatment of liver cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Linyong Wu, Qingfeng Lai, Songhua Li, Shaofeng Wu, Yizhong Li, Ju Huang, Qiuli Zeng, Dayou Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the performance and methodological quality of artificial intelligence (AI) in predicting recurrence after single first-line treatment for liver cancer.MethodsA rigorous and systematic evaluation was conducted on the AI studies related to recurrence after single first-line treatment for liver cancer, retrieved from the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and CNKI databases. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity (SENC), and specificity (SPEC) of each study were extracted for meta-analysis.ResultsSix percutaneous ablation (PA) studies, 16 surgical resection (SR) studies, and 5 transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) studies were included in the meta-analysis for predicting recurrence after hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment, respectively. Four SR studies and 2 PA studies were included in the meta-analysis for recurrence after intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) and colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM) treatment. The pooled SENC, SEPC, and AUC of AI in predicting recurrence after primary HCC treatment via PA, SR, and TACE were 0.78, 0.90, and 0.92; 0.81, 0.77, and 0.86; and 0.73, 0.79, and 0.79, respectively. The values for ICC treated with SR and CRLM treated with PA were 0.85, 0.71, 0.86 and 0.69, 0.63,0.74, respectively.ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates the comprehensive application value of AI in predicting recurrence after a single first-line treatment of liver cancer, with satisfactory results, indicating the clinical translation potential of AI in predicting recurrence after liver cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-024-01440-z.Keywords: Liver cancer, Artificial intelligence, Recurrence, Percutaneous ablation, Surgical resection, Transarterial chemoembolization

Introduction

Liver cancer poses a significant global public health burden, especially in China, which accounts for nearly half of liver cancer burden. Liver cancer is classified into primary and secondary liver cancer. Primary liver cancer originates in the liver itself, with pathological types including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and mixed hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma. HCC comprises 80% of the global liver cancer burden, followed by ICC at 15% [1]. Secondary liver cancer, on the other hand, arises from the spread of cancer originating from non-hepatic tissues to the liver. According to research, among 2.4 million cancer patients, 5.14% experienced liver metastasis, and the most common primary site for young women was breast cancer, while for young men, it was colorectal cancer [2]. The first-line treatment options for liver cancer include liver surgical resection (SR), percutaneous ablation (PA), and transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). For HCC, the risks and benefits of first-line treatment options must be considered, and personalized treatment should be provided based on tumor factors and the patient's functional status. SR remained the preferred first-line treatment for HCC, particularly in Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stages 0-A. Although PA was characterized by minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery, meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials had shown that SR offered longer disease-free survival and a lower recurrence rate compared to PA [3]. However, PA was considered the optimal treatment option for early-stage HCC that was not amenable to resection [4]. TACE was often used as a bridging therapy, in combination with SR or PA, to maximize patient benefits [5]. SR was considered the only viable and potentially curable first-line treatment for ICC, but resectability and curability remained low [6]. Lymph node metastasis was the primary cause of poor long-term survival after SR in ICC patients, and portal lymph node dissection was a common surgical approach for staging. However, for unresectable ICC, PA as an alternative therapy had shown to improve survival rates in patients with ICC tumors smaller than 3 cm, similar to SR [7]. TACE was used exclusively for palliative treatment of ICC to improve patients' quality of life. As for colorectal liver metastases (CLRM), regardless of the systemic therapy administered, SR remained the gold standard for curing rectal cancer with CRLM, but only 20% of patients with solitary CRLM had the opportunity to undergo SR [8]. While surgical resection was considered the preferred option, based on current literature, there was no evidence to suggest that non-surgical therapies were inferior to SR. For instance, a retrospective study found that PA was suitable for elderly males with CRLM, particularly those with tumors smaller than 2 cm, compared to SR [9]. Despite the widespread application of these three first-line treatment options in liver cancer, the high recurrence rate remains a significant challenge. For example, a single-center study revealed that among 1235 HCC patients who underwent SR and 517 HCC patients who received PA, the early recurrence (ER) rates were 19% and 37%, respectively [10]. In a prospective study, 259 ICC patients treated with SR had an ER rate of 50% during follow-up, with a late recurrence rate of 29% [11]. Additionally, a multicenter study showed that among 207 patients with CRLM who underwent PA, the ER rate was 48% [12]. Given these findings, effectively predicting the recurrence of liver cancer after first-line treatment has become a widely explored area of studies.

Accurately predicting the recurrence of liver cancer after treatment has profound implications for treatment planning and long-term management strategies. To achieve this, various predictive models and biomarkers have been extensively explored as they offer insights into recurrence risk. The advancement of such predictive methods has the potential to ultimately lead to more personalized treatment plans and improved outcomes for cancer patients. For instance, a multicenter prospective study constructed a predictive model utilizing factors such as tumor diameter, tumor number, large vessel invasion, satellite nodules, alpha-fetoprotein, HBV-DNA level, and prothrombin time. This model was thoroughly validated through internal, external, and prospective cohorts, accurately predicting ER after HCC-SR [13]. Furthermore, a machine learning model developed based on 14 clinicopathological features of 536 ICC patients had been used to predict ER after ICC-SR, aiming to provide personalized consultation, treatment, and advice [14]. Additionally, a nomogram for predicting ER after CRLM-SR was formulated using factors like the number of liver metastases, suspected lymph node involvement, neurovascular invasion, colon/rectum location, albumin levels, post-operative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) levels. This nomogram achieved area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.87 and 0.79 in internal and external validation, assisting in decision-making for selective follow-up strategies [15]. Moreover, several meta-analyses have shed light on biomarkers associated with ER after first-line treatment for liver cancer from a large sample perspective. For example, a meta-analysis encompassing nine ICC-ER-related studies revealed that preoperative factors such as cirrhosis, gender, and HBV status were not significantly associated with ER. However, among postoperative pathological factors, multiple tumors, microvascular invasion, large vessel invasion, lymph node metastasis, and R1 resection were identified as risk factors for increased ER [16]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of 24 CRLM-ER-related studies indicated that elevated levels of CEA and CA199, larger and bilobar liver metastases, extensive liver resection, positive surgical margins, and postoperative complications were associated with an increased risk of ER [17]. It is crucial to note, however, that these analyses are primarily based on observational studies investigating clinical and pathological factors related to ER following first-line treatment. Furthermore, some of them are analyzed from a postoperative perspective. Observational studies, due to the interference of numerous confounding factors and the lack of balanced baseline characteristics between cohorts, may not always yield reliable conclusions. Therefore, future research with rigorous experimental designs and comprehensive analysis is warranted to enhance the accuracy and reliability of predictive models for liver cancer recurrence.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been elucidated as an alternative biomarker for predicting cancer recurrence after treatment, demonstrating satisfactory results compared to clinical pathological features. AI utilizes computer technology to extract deeper-level information from medical images such as computed tomography (CT) [18], magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [19], or ultrasound (US) [20], encompassing radiomics and deep learning [21]. For instance, a machine learning radiomics model developed based on contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) images of 318 HCC patients had been validated in a test cohort to predict ER after PA, which radiomics model outperformed the clinical model [22]. Similarly, a radiomics model developed from CT images of 138 ICC patients also showed superior predictive capabilities for ER after SR compared to clinical models [23]. However, upon further investigation, it had been observed that only AI meta-analyses focusing on HCC treatment recurrence were available, and these had not conducted independent analyses for various first-line treatment protocols. Moreover, there was a lack of comprehensive meta-analyses evaluating the recurrence after first-line treatment in ICC and CRLM. The potential of AI in predicting cancer recurrence is immense, yet its full application and understanding remain to be explored.

Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to analyze the application of AI in predicting recurrence after first-line treatment for liver cancer, including HCC, ICC and CLRM. The goal was to clarify the current study status of AI in recurrence prediction, as well as to evaluate the methodological quality of the studies. By systematically reviewing and synthesizing the available evidence, we hope to provide a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness and limitations of AI in predicting cancer recurrence, thereby guiding future research and clinical applications. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (Supplementary materials 1-PRISMA_2020_checklist).

Methods

Protocol and registration

The review protocol was registered on the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk; registration number CRD42024558716).

Study search strategy

A comprehensive search was undertaken in the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and CNKI databases before May 10, 2024, utilizing the following keywords: “radiomics”, “deep learning”, “liver”, “ablation”, “resection”, “TACE”, “transarterial chemoembolization”, “HCC”, “hepatocellular carcinoma”, “ICC”, “cholangiocarcinoma”, “metastasis”, and “metastases”. The objective was to retrieve studies pertaining to the utilization of AI in forecasting recurrence post-primary treatment of focal liver lesions. Following the initial search, all relevant literature concerning recurrence was thoroughly scrutinized, primarily through the examination of abstracts and the full text. The inclusion process was independently evaluated by two reviewers, each with over three years of expertise in abdominal imaging diagnosis. Any inconsistencies were resolved by a senior reviewer, boasting over five years of experience in abdominal imaging diagnosis.

Study selection criteria

The studies must adhere to the following criteria: (1) study type: original study; (2) population: patients with liver cancer, including HCC, ICC, and CRLM; (3) treatment strategy: receiving only a single first-line treatment, including SR, PA, and TACE; (4) the pre-treatment imaging examinations include US, CT, or MRI; (5) objective: quantitative analysis of pre-treatment images using radiomics and deep learning AI methods; (6) results: sufficient data to reconstruct a 2 × 2 sensitivity (SENC) and specificity (SPEC) table for predicting post-treatment recurrence.

The studies were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) any comments, meta-analyses, case reports, guidelines, errata, or duplicate cohort studies; (2) the use of combined treatment strategies; (3) AI quantitative analysis using post-treatment images or images of non liver cancer; (4) the presence of anti-tumor treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy before first-line treatment; (5) controversial predictive purposes; (6) insufficient data extraction.

Data extraction

The extraction of study data was independently conducted by a reviewer with 3 years of study experience and independently verified by another reviewer with the same experience. The extraction encompassed information such as the first author, publication year, country of publication, AI methods (radiomics or deep learning), pathological types of liver cancer, design type (prospective or retrospective), number of centers (single or multi-center), image type (US, CT, or MRI), follow-up strategy (within 2 years or longer), and a 2 × 2 SENC and SEPC table comprising true positives (TP), false negatives (FN), false positives (FP), and true negatives (TN). In cases where two or more AI models were proposed based on the same cohort, the model with higher diagnostic accuracy was prioritized for analysis.

Quality assessment

The Radiomics Quality Score (RQS), Checklist for AI in Medical Imaging (CLAIM), and Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) were applied to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies [24]. RQS included 16 evaluation indexes, including image acquisition, image preprocessing, validation, performance evaluation, practicality, open science. CLAIM included 42 evaluation indexes, compared to RQS, a further subdivision and evaluation of all steps related to AI was conducted. QUADAS-2 was used to evaluate the risk of bias at the study level, including bias in fields such as: (1) patient selection, (2) index test, (3) reference standard, and (4) flow and timing. A modified version of QUADAS-2 proposed by Sollini et al. and verified by Bedrikovetski S et al. was used [25].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis of TP, TN, FP, and FN was performed using the “midas” module in Stata (version 12.0) and Meta-DiSc (version 1.4), and the pooled SENC and SEPC were determined based on the fixed-effects model. The summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curve assessed the value of AI in predicting recurrence after first-line treatment. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the Cochrane Q-test (p-value < 0.05) and I2 statistic (> 50%). The Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test was employed to assess publication bias. All statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Results of search strategy

The results of the search strategy were shown in Fig. 1. A total of 98 AI studies related to PA, 327 studies related to SR, and 75 studies related to TACE were identified and rigorously evaluated. After initial screening, 69 PA-related studies, 240 SR-related studies, and 52 TACE-related studies were excluded due to their irrelevance to recurrence, leaving 29 PA-related studies, 89 SR-related studies, and 23 TACE-related studies for further analysis. Among the 123 studies, 80 studies were excluded because they were unable to extract the data of SENC and SPEC or the number of recurrent cases was unknown, 8 studies adopted two kinds of first-line treatments [26–33], and 5 studies did not conduct model validation [34–38]. In the SR-related studies, two came from the same institution, and the one with lower performance [39] was excluded.

Search strategy for study inclusion

After these exclusions, in the application of AI in predicting HCC recurrence, a final total of 6 PA-related studies [22, 40–44], 16 SR-related studies [45–60], and 5 TACE-related studies [61–65] were included in this study.

In the application of AI in predicting ICC recurrence, no studies had been found on the application after PA or TACE for ICC, and only 11 studies related to SR had been identified [23, 66–75], with 4 of them finally included in the meta-analysis [23, 67, 73, 75].

In the application of AI for predicting the recurrence of metastases, most of them were of the CLRM pathological type, therefore, only AI for CLRM were analyzed. In the application of AI in predicting CLRM recurrence, only 8 were related to PA [12, 33, 36, 76–80], 9 were related to SR [81–89], and 1 was related to TACE [90]. After exclusion, only 2 PA-related studies [12, 80], 1 SR-related study [81], and 0 TACE-related studies met the criteria for the meta-analysis.

Characteristics of the included studies

Except for one study that adopted a prospective design, the remaining studies included in the meta-analysis all employed a retrospective design. Only 8 studies utilized multi-center data, and 6 studies performed external data validation. According to the affiliation of the first author, only one study originated from the United States, while the remaining studies were all from China. All studies defined recurrence, including progression of the primary lesion, intrahepatic recurrence, and extrahepatic metastasis. Regarding the imaging protocols, with 17 using a single scanner, 8 using multiple scanners, and 8 with unclear scanner details. 4 studies were based on US images, 14 on CT images, and 15 on MRI images. 29 studies relied on manual segmentation by multiple individuals, 19 used ITK-SNAP software, but only 25 performed an intraclass correlation coefficient analysis, and 2 did not report segmentation details. The segmentation methods were manual in 30 studies and semi-automatic in only 3 studies. In terms of image preprocessing, only 16 studies performed resampling. Over half of the studies extracted features based on the Pyradiomics software package. 31 studies employed a multi-step feature reduction strategy, while 2 study was unclear. 22 studies used cross-validation for performance assessment, and 20 studies compared their performance with other methods. 29 studies performed multivariate analysis, and 31 studies adopted ensemble modeling strategies. 23 studies conducted calibration and 22 studies conducted decision curve analyses (Table 1).

Quality assessment

Based on the analysis of the aforementioned characteristics, the RQS, QUADAS-2, and CLAIM methods were used to assess the methodological quality of the studies. The RQS results showed a median total score of 16, ranging from 10 to 22. In the application of HCC, among different first-line treatment strategies such as PA, SR, and TACE, the median RQS scores were 12, 16, and 18, with ranges of 10–16, 10–22, and 12–18, respectively. In the application of ICC, the median RQS scores of SR was 16, with ranges of 10–16. In the application of CLRM, the median RQS scores of PA was 14.5, with ranges of 14–15. None of the studies reported cost-effectiveness, phantom studies, the development of science (Supplementary materials 2-RQS).

The median CLAIM score was 71% (30/42). Some CLAIM items were typically reported, including abstract structure, study introduction, study goal, inclusion criteria for the study population, model performance metrics, performance metrics to distinguish the optimal model(s), and study limitations. However, there were also some items that were often not reported, such as the selection of data subsets, de-identification methods, handling of missing data, rationale for choosing the reference standard, anticipated sample size and how it was determined, failure analysis of incorrectly classified cases, and availability of the full study protocol (Supplementary materials 3-CLAIM).

QUADAS-2 was used to analyze the risk of bias and applicability questions. 29 studies were deemed to have a low risk of bias. However, bias risks and application issues related to the index test were frequently observed, primarily due to the lack of detailed reporting on image preprocessing. Regarding the study flow and timing, the risk of bias remained unclear across all 33 studies (Supplementary materials 4-QUADAS-2). The quality assessment was displayed in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The management of liver cancer, including HCC, ICC, and CRLM, typically involves first-line treatments such as SR, PA, and TACE. However, post-treatment recurrence remains a significant clinical challenge, necessitating the exploration of combined therapeutic approaches, such as targeted or immunotherapy, for patients experiencing relapse. Accurately identifying patients prone to liver cancer recurrence and subsequently delivering precision post-surgical interventions remains a critical area of research. AI methods have been extensively applied to predict recurrence following first-line treatments for liver cancer. The effectiveness of AI in predicting recurrence after treatment for primary liver cancer was satisfactory, with moderate to high efficacy. However, in CRLM, AI still need further exploration to improve the prediction of recurrence after treatment. Despite the use of multiple quality assessment methods, the meta-analysis still exhibited considerable heterogeneity, a finding consistent with many other AI meta-analyses.

The progress of artificial intelligence in primary malignant focal liver lesions

Through the survey of multiple databases, it had been revealed that over 300 original studies had been conducted on the application of AI in predicting recurrence after liver cancer treatment, primarily focusing on first-line therapy for HCC, including PA, SR, or TACE. However, regarding ICC, no AI studies on recurrence prediction after PA and TACE treatments had been identified, with only a dozen studies found on recurrence prediction after SR treatment. For CRLM, over a dozen studies had been discovered investigating AI in recurrence prediction after SR or PA treatment. This aligned with the global statistics on liver cancer burden. Additionally, the application of AI in predicting recurrence after liver transplantation had also been investigated, yet only one study meeting the criteria for meta-analysis was found. This study utilized CT images from a single-center cohort of 151 HCC patients to develop a machine learning model, achieving a SENC and SPEC of 0.86 and 0.85 for predicting recurrence after liver transplantation [94]. The AI methods employed include radiomics and deep learning, primarily utilizing images from CT, MRI, and US. Interestingly, pathomics based on quantitative analysis of postoperative pathological images was gradually being explored. For instance, a deep learning analysis of whole-slide images from multiple centers for HCC had achieved an AUC of 0.72 in predicting recurrence after SR treatment [95]. Furthermore, a recurrence-related histological score constructed using deep learning methods based on 576 whole-slice images predicted recurrence with an AUC of 0.74 [96]. The findings suggest that further exploration is still needed in applying AI for recurrence prediction after ICC and CLRM treatments. Additionally, pathomics, as a quantitative analysis based on pathological images, is still far from meeting the requirements of precision medicine.

Subsequently, the application of meta-analyses in liver cancer was investigated. In the PubMed database, three meta-analyses utilizing AI for HCC recurrence prediction were identified, while no AI meta-analysis on ICC or CRLM recurrence was found. These three meta-analyses encompassed 49, 15, and 10 studies, respectively, none of which provided detailed analysis on the treatment regimen, including single or combination therapy. However, one of the meta-analyses offered a detailed summary of AI's predictive capabilities for HCC recurrence based on cohort and image type. From the above, it was evident that published meta-analyses had not focused on the value of AI in predicting recurrence after a single first-line treatment. However, considering the variability in recurrence rates among different first-line treatment options, such as PA treatment leading to a higher recurrence rate compared to SR, overlooking the treatment regimen in recurrence assessment may overestimate the accuracy of AI in recurrence prediction. Therefore, this review, unlike other meta-analyses, would focus on the application of AI in predicting recurrence after first-line treatment for liver cancer, aiming to assess the value of AI in each first-line treatment regimen, and precisely identify high-risk populations for recurrence and facilitate timely addition of combination therapy.

Quality assessment

This review employed three quality assessment methods: RQS, QUADAS-2, and CLAIM. Among them, RQS and QUADAS-2 were commonly used in numerous meta-analyses. The average RQS score in this review was 16, ranging from 10 to 22, similar to the findings of other meta-analyses [91–93], indicating a moderate-to-low quality. The primary reasons for the relatively low RQS quality were associated with factors such as prospective, cost, open science, phantom study, multiple timepoints, and biological correlates, which had been disclosed in numerous meta-analyses, raising questions about their applicability. QUADAS-2 deemed 29 studies to have a low risk of bias. However, it was often observed that there were biases and application issues related to index testing, primarily due to the lack of detailed reports on image preprocessing. QUADAS-2 seems to overestimate methodological quality, which is also similar to other meta-analyses [97]. CLAIM, a quality assessment method introduced in recent years, encompasses 42 quality control items, overlapping with RQS to a great extent, but expanding to quality control of various sections of the research writing. CLAIM has gradually been adopted in the quality assessment of AI meta-analyses. For instance, in a meta-analysis evaluating COVID-19 pneumonia versus other pneumonias, 19 studies using deep learning methods had an average CLAIM score of 20, with the highest score being 29 and the lowest 14 [98]. In another meta-analysis evaluating the prediction of medulloblastoma subtypes, 5 radiomics studies achieved an average CLAIM score of 23 [99]. In this review, the median CLAIM score was 71% (30/42). Some CLAIM items were typically reported, including abstract structure, study introduction, study goal, inclusion criteria for the study population, model performance metrics, performance metrics to distinguish the optimal model(s), and study limitations. Few studies disclosed factors such as de-identification methods, how missing data were handled, intended sample size and its determination, initialization of model parameters, failure analysis of incorrectly classified cases, registration number and name of registry, and where the full study protocol could be accessed. RQS highlighted the specific issue of phantom studies in radiomics, while CLAIM pointed out deficiencies in missing data handling, sample size or power calculations, as well as shortcomings in data de-identification and failure analysis [24]. Both RQS and CLAIM emphasized the importance of data robustness, which was intimately tied to segmentation methods. These methods encompass manual, semi-automatic, and fully automatic approaches, leveraging techniques such as gray-level based methods, learning-based techniques, and graph-cut methods. Segmentation methods could exhibit variability depending on the application scenario and imaging modality. Manual segmentation, a commonly employed technique in radiomics, relied on the assessment of intra-observer and inter-observer variability to evaluate robustness. For instance, the reproducibility of features among observers could quantitatively assess the magnetic susceptibility of intracranial dissecting aneurysms, as evidenced by the study [100]. Employing learning-based techniques, such as deep learning, could lead to pseudo-segmentation if the training data selection was inadequate, as deep learning heavily relied on the quality and representativeness of the training data. While fully automatic segmentation eliminates human factors, it too could result in pseudo-segmentation, yet it remaind a promising avenue for further development in machine learning applications. This was particularly evident in the context of deep learning for ultrasound images, where manual segmentation of thousands of images was impractical, highlighting the advantages of fully automatic approaches [101, 102]. In addition to segmentation techniques, imaging artifacts, including noise, motion, and others, significantly impact the performance of segmentation methods, as exemplified in [103]. Currently, no single segmentation method can yield acceptable results for every medical image, making the selection of an appropriate method for a given segmentation task a challenging dilemma. Comprehensive predictive performance

This review incorporated six PA studies, 16 SR studies, and five TACE studies to predict the recurrence of HCC after treatment. In the evaluation of recurrence after PA and SR treatment, the comprehensive assessment performance was satisfactory, achieving moderate to high performance with AUCs of 0.92 and 0.86, respectively. However, in the assessment of recurrence after TACE treatment, the comprehensive SENC, SPEC, and AUC were 0.73, 0.79, and 0.79, respectively, indicating only moderate performance. These results were similar to the conclusions of published meta-analyses on HCC recurrence prediction [91–93]. However, the AI meta-analysis based on PA treatment recurrence showed a higher conclusion compared to other meta-analyses, which was likely related to the differences in recurrence among first-line treatment options mentioned in this review, and could also be attributed to the relatively small number of studies included. In the prediction of recurrence after ICC-SR and CLRM-PA treatment, the comprehensive AUCs were 0.86 and 0.74, respectively. It was evident that AI demonstrates more stable performance in predicting recurrence after SR treatment, which may be associated with the more extensive application of SR in liver cancer. Furthermore, by combining clinical pathological features, AI had improved the performance of predicting recurrence after first-line treatment to varying degrees. This review, as same as the previous meta-analysis, analyzed the total predictive performance of AI in all first-line treatment schemes, and AUC reached 0.86, with similar performance. However, it underestimated the high predictive performance of AI in the recurrence of PA treatment, and overestimated the low predictive performance of AI in the recurrence of TACE treatment.

Limitations

This review encountered several noteworthy limitations: (1) The number of studies included in the recurrence evaluation of ICC and CLRM were relatively small, which could not completely exclude the possibility of over-exclusion during the literature screening process. Therefore, the generalizability of the meta-analysis results remained a relevant topic for discussion. (2) The meta-analysis results revealed a high degree of heterogeneity in SENS and SPEC. This heterogeneity was primarily attributed to the lack of standardization in scientific rigor and clinical relevance. (3) AI studies had limitations, such as the robustness of manual segmentation and model overfitting, which still require more standardized quality control protocols to improve study quality [105]. (4) There were limitations in the evaluation of RQS, QUADAS-2, and CLAIM, and the evaluation criteria were interpreted based on relevant literature in this study, which was still needed to more effectively evaluate the quality of AI. (5) All the studies were retrospective, lacking validation of the effectiveness of AI in predicting HCC recurrence through high-quality, multi-center, prospective studies. (6) The studies included in this review were mainly from China, and the global applicability of the conclusions still required regional validation.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates the comprehensive application value of AI in predicting recurrence after a single first-line liver cancer treatment, with satisfactory results indicating the clinical translation potential of AI in predicting recurrence. However, it must be acknowledged that due to the complexity of the AI analysis process, there are variations in studies that lead to heterogeneity in the meta-analysis. Therefore, further development and improvement of quality assessment standards for AI methodologies are still needed to achieve the goal of precise evaluation through the generalized application of AI.
