Waste to wealth: dual-mode optical-electrochemical ultrasensitive sensing of H2O2 using N-doped graphene quantum dots derived from agricultural residues
Authors: Ummadisetti Bhanu Teja, M. V. R. Akshay Sampath, Uday Kumar Ghorui, Shamili Bandaru, Nilja George, Sambasivam Sangaraju, Siddhartha Ghosh, Mahesh Kumar Ravva, Sabyasachi Chakrabortty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For a sustainable circular economy, the processing of substantial agrarian waste into valuable materials would be an excellent strategy. This minimizes the cost of discarding waste while simultaneously reducing pollution and CO2 emissions. Herein, we synthesize cotton shell-derived nitrogen-doped graphene quantum dots (CS-N-GQDs) through hydrothermal synthesis. The structural, optical, morphological, and compositional properties of CS-N-GQDs are analysed using various analytical tools. The theoretical insights of CS-N-GQDs are corroborated through density functional theory (DFT) studies at the ωB97XD/6-31G(d,p) level of theory. Further, the nanomolar detection of H2O2 through optical and electrochemical sensing is performed for applications in environmental monitoring, food quality assessment, and biomedical analysis. Importantly, optical and electrochemical sensing show limits of detection of 5.34 ± 2 nM and 5.96 ± 1 nM, respectively, at a broad linear range of 10 nM to 1 mM. The possibility of detecting H2O2 using CS-N-GQDs in real-time analysis is also tested in commercial juice and beverage samples. This work reveals that cotton shells are useful in producing green and economic dual-mode optical and electrochemical sensor probes for the rapid, sensitive, and selective sensing of H2O2. Combined spectroscopic, electrochemical, and theoretical analyses provide mechanistic insights, revealing dynamic photoinduced electron-transfer quenching for optical sensing and diffusion-controlled electrocatalytic reduction for electrochemical sensing.

Introduction

Agrarian waste is an abundant, inexpensive and sustainable natural resource. Improper disposal techniques, such as stubble burning, microbial decomposition, and landfilling, have created serious ecological issues around the earth. These conventional practices often fail to leverage the potential of agricultural waste, highlighting the urgent need for approaches that promote its reuse and sustainability. Efficiently converting agrarian waste into value-added products is a useful strategy that can reduce CO2 emissions and play a vital role in resolving the energy and environmental issues. One such conversion is synthesizing value-added nanomaterials from biowaste.1–6

Graphene quantum dots (GQDs) are nanostructured carbon materials that belong to the class of zero-dimensional nanomaterials. They exhibit remarkable optoelectronic properties, including tunable fluorescence, outstanding conductivity, high quantum yield, high biocompatibility, and quantum confinement effects (QCE). These unique attributes have enabled their application in several fields, including sensing, bioimaging, drug delivery, catalysis and energy storage.4,7–10 Conventional top-down and bottom-up methods for GQD synthesis commonly use refined chemical precursors and multistep processing, which can increase cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability for large-scale production. The use of biowaste is one such great alternative for the fabrication of GQDs through an eco-friendly method, aligning with the principles of green chemistry.2,3 Agrarian residues, such as rice husks, fruit peels, and agricultural waste, have higher compositions of carbon, making them ideal precursors for producing GQDs.2,3,10–12 Among agricultural wastes, cotton shells are often discarded as insignificant waste after cotton harvesting; however, they hold immense potential as precursors for the synthesis of GQDs. So far, there are no reports on using cotton shell biowaste as a precursor to produce GQDs. By bridging this gap, cotton shells can have a substantial research value for the green, low-cost, and large-scale production of GQDs because they offer abundant carbon, oxygen and nitrogen sources and can reduce the consumption of harmful and expensive chemicals, gradually resulting in a decrease in environmental pollution to a certain extent. Despite these advantages, GQDs derived from biomass often face some challenges, such as low quantum yield (QY) and presence of impurities, which limit practical applications, and the need for raw materials in abundance, which restricts the scalability. Furthermore, current post-synthesis methods involve complex purification processes, such as high-speed centrifugation and prolonged dialysis, which might hinder their efficiency. Therefore, devising relatively simple and effective processes to attain GQDs with improved QY from profuse agricultural waste is crucial to reveal their potential and address environmental and material-production challenges.2–5,10 An additional advantage lies in the tunability of GQD properties through doping, where incorporating heteroatoms, such as nitrogen, boron, sulphur, and phosphorus, into the lattice structure of GQDs and functionalization can modify their electronic, optical, and chemical characteristics.13 Nitrogen doping, in particular, has been widely recognized for improving quantum yield, increasing electrical conductivity, boosting electron-transfer rates, and imparting surface activity, thereby enabling the versatile, effective, and broad application of GQDs.14–17 This strategy has the combined benefits of producing high-value functional nanomaterials for cutting-edge scientific applications.10–12,18 Their distinctive structural, optoelectronic properties, excellent biocompatibility, high water dispersibility, high current density, rapid electron mobility, abundant surface functional groups, and remarkable photochemical stability contribute to their efficacy in various sensing platforms.17,19

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is an inorganic molecule used in various industrial, food processing, environmental, and biological processes at determined safety-level concentrations.20 H2O2 is added to food products to increase deodorization, shelf life, and whitening, also as an oxidant. In environmental contexts, H2O2 is used as an indicator of oxidative pollutants. It also acts as a key signalling molecule in cellular pathways, a disinfectant in water treatment, and a bleaching agent in industries. H2O2 is a reactive oxygen species formed in aerobic metabolism, playing a key role in signal transduction and oxidative stress in organisms. Thus, the detection and quantification of H2O2 are critical because their imbalance or abnormal ranges can lead to oxidative stress, failure of the central nervous system, damage in biological systems, and diseases, such as Alzheimer's, diabetes, cancer, and Parkinson's.21–26 Various analytical detection techniques have been advanced for H2O2 detection, such as chromatography, colorimetry, fluorescence spectrophotometry, electrochemical methods, electron spin resonance spectroscopy, amperometric biosensing, chemiluminescence, fluorometry, and flow injection analysis. However, many of these methods have limitations, such as poor sensitivity, low selectivity, complex instrumentation, time-consuming procedures, and high operational costs.21,23–25 Among them, optical and electrochemical sensing methods have emerged as prominent, cost-effective, and efficient approaches for detecting H2O2 at low concentrations.27 In recent years, dual-mode sensors that integrate optical and electrochemical detection have gained increasing attention, as they combine the advantages of both techniques, enabling the cross-validation and high reliability of results.8,14,15

In this work, we report the design and synthesis of CS-N-GQDs, a dual-mode sensor, for H2O2 detection. Various analytical methods, such as HRTEM, XRD, XPS, Raman, FTIR, and zeta potential, were employed to investigate the morphology, structure, elemental composition, and photophysical properties of the synthesized CS-N-GQDs. By employing parallel optical and electrochemical detection modes, the sensing platform enables the complementary evaluation of responses, leading to improved sensitivity, selectivity, and analytical reliability. This sensing strategy, characterized by a low detection limit, rapid response, and cost-effective operation, shows strong potential for H2O2 monitoring in environmental, biomedical, and industrial applications. Additionally, we perform theoretical calculations to obtain the optimized structure and a deep understanding of fluorescence properties, governed by functionalization and defect induction. Post-sensing analysis was performed to correlate the theoretical findings with experimental observations. The study bridges the gap between waste valorization and sustainable resource utilization, offering significant implications for green chemistry, environmental science, and sensor technologies to address the growing demand for efficient and eco-friendly sensing platforms.

Results and discussion

Green synthesis of CS-N-GQDs

CS-N-GQDs were obtained from cotton shell extract by one-pot hydrothermal synthesis, as depicted in Fig. 1. This approach represents a green synthesis strategy because it utilizes biomass-derived waste extract as a renewable carbon precursor, employs water as a solvent, and avoids hazardous chemical reagents, in accordance with principles of green chemistry. The complete development for the green synthesis of CS-N-GQDs from cotton shell extraction is simple, economic, and non-hazardous. The extraction process was initiated by washing 5 g of cotton shells with DI water. The washed cotton shells were added to a 100 mL round-bottom flask with 35 mL of DI water and kept for reflux heating for 2 hours at 100 °C. The obtained cotton shell extract (CSE) was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the CSE supernatant was taken; the leftover cotton shells were preserved for further utilization in soil compost and compost bricks. Now, 20 mL of CSE was transferred into a 100 mL teflon-lined autoclave and processed through a hydrothermal process at 180 °C for 4 hours. After cooling it to room temperature, the product was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes and filtered with a 0.22 µm membrane to remove large and unreacted particles. Obtained CS-N-GQDs were stored at 4 °C for further use.

HRTEM analysis of CS-N-GQDs

As shown in Fig. 2a, nanostructured CS-N-GQDs were studied by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), which revealed that the synthesized CS-N-GQDs were fairly monodispersed and quasi-spherical in shape. Fig. 2b clearly revealed a distinct lattice fringe pattern with interplanar spacing (d) of 0.31 nm, which corresponded to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon. This observation confirmed the partial retention of the sp2-hybridized graphitic domains in CS-N-GQDs.28,29 The presence of graphitic stacking, as evidenced by the lattice spacing, was consistent with previous reports on nitrogen-doped GQDs derived from biomass sources, such as cellulose, lignin, and agricultural residues. Nitrogen incorporation into the carbon matrix is known to slightly alter the lattice parameters and enhance defect density, thereby improving electronic conductivity and modulating the photoluminescence behaviour of GQDs. The slight distortion in the graphitic layers observed in certain regions could be attributed to the substitutional nitrogen atoms or edge functional groups (pyridinic, pyrrolic, or graphitic N) introduced during the doping process. These structural modifications were beneficial for enhancing electron transfer and catalytic properties, as they create localized electronic states within the π-conjugated framework.30 The average particle size of CS-N-GQDs was calculated to be ∼4.09 nm, ranging from 2 to 6.5 nm, as depicted in Fig. 2c. This size distribution implied controlled nucleation and growth during the carbonization process. Overall, the HRTEM analysis substantiated the formation of nanosized, crystalline nitrogen-doped graphene quantum dots obtained from sustainable cotton shell biomass. The well-defined lattice fringes, narrow size distribution, and morphology confirmed the success of the synthesis route in producing GQDs. The close agreement between the d-spacing values of CS-N-GQDs from the HRTEM and XRD techniques strengthens the structural assignment.

XRD analysis of CS-NGQDs

The structural characteristics of as-synthesized CS-NGQDs were studied by X-ray diffraction. A diffraction peak at 2θ = 27.97° corresponded to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon, as illustrated in Fig. 2d, suggesting the existence of stacked sp2-hybridized carbon layers in the structure, which was in close agreement with the reference JCPDS file: 75-1621.31,32 The interlayer spacing (d) was calculated using Bragg's law.

The d-spacing value was 0.31 nm, which was slightly smaller than the value for bulk graphite (0.335 nm), indicating lattice compression due to nitrogen doping and surface functionalization. This value corresponded well to the 0.31 nm interlayer spacing seen in HRTEM. In addition, peaks at 30.67°, 35.34°, and 58.10° were observed, which were associated with graphitic oxide domains having disordered regions, higher-index planes or the diffraction of turbostratic carbon layers formed during the pyrolysis of the biomass.33

XPS analysis of CS-N-GQDs

The elemental, electronic, and surface chemistry characteristics of CS-N-GQDs were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The wide-scan spectrum presented in Fig. 2e shows the atomic composition of CS-N-GQDs, indicating 67.7% of carbon, 24.9% of oxygen, and 7.4% of nitrogen. As shown in Fig. 2f, the presence of three principal elements – carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) – was confirmed, with their characteristic peaks located at 284.0 eV (C 1s), 399.0 eV (N 1s), and 531.0 eV (O 1s) binding energies, respectively. The atomic N/C ratio of 0.11 confirmed the successful incorporation of nitrogen as dopants in the carbon framework. The O/C ratio was calculated as 0.37, indicating a significant existence of oxygen-containing functional groups on CS-N-GQDs.34 These heteroatoms would contribute to the modulation of the electronic structure and surface reactivity of CS-N-GQDs, which could be beneficial for sensing applications. Further, the deconvolution of the C 1s spectrum of CS-N-GQDs indicated the presence of three other peaks, 284.64, 285.63, and 287.63 eV, which were attributed to CC, C–C/C–O/C–N, and CO, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 2g.35 The fitting results indicated contributions from both sp2 and sp3 hybridized carbon atoms, consistent with graphitic (CC/C–C) and defect-related domains in CS-N-GQDs. The relative ratio of sp2/sp3 carbon content was 1.07, suggesting a partially graphitized structure with a considerable amount of functionalized carbon domains.29,36 The N 1s spectrum showed two deconvoluted peaks at 399.94 and 401.64 eV, as depicted in Fig. 2h, corresponding to pyrrolic/C3N4/amine and graphitic nitrogen, respectively. The great abundance of pyrrolic nitrogen over graphitic nitrogen, as shown in the spectra, can disrupt π-conjugation and reduce electron delocalization, resulting in a wide bandgap for CS-N-GQDs. The increase in the optical bandgap was due to the presence of more pyrrolic nitrogen atoms than graphitic nitrogen forms.37–39 The natural doping of nitrogen in specific forms in CS-N-GQDs is better for sensing and other applications because it intrinsically controls the electron donors.40 The improved catalytic activity of CS-N-GQDs relative to pristine GQDs was attributed to the increased number of active sites in the form of pyrrolic nitrogen and graphitic nitrogen, which enhances the electrocatalytic activity due to their high electronic conductivity.37 The O 1s peak at 532.24 eV, as shown in Fig. 2i, was assigned to (C–O/CO), possibly corresponding to carboxy, epoxy, and hydroxy functional groups, which was also confirmed with C 1s spectra. The oxygen-containing surface functionalities in GQDs assisted in modulating surface-state energetics and interfacial charge-transfer behaviour, enhancing aqueous dispersibility and providing adsorption sites that enable sensitive PL quenching and enhanced electrocatalytic response, especially towards H2O2.41,42

Raman spectroscopic analysis of N-GQDs

Raman spectroscopy supports the study of the structural characteristics and defect density of CS-N-GQDs. As depicted in Fig. 3a, the spectrum displayed two prominent peaks at 1379 cm−1 and 1521 cm−1, corresponding to the D and G bands, respectively. The D band originates from the defect-related vibrations and is usually associated with structural imperfections, edge effects, and disorder within the carbon framework. In contrast, the G band denotes the signals from the in-plane vibrations of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms and reflects the degree of crystallinity for the carbon framework. The ID/IG ratio of 1.1 suggested a considerable amount of disordered structure arising from nitrogen-induced defects.43 The peak shifts and changes in Raman intensity may depict lattice distortion caused by nitrogen doping and possible Stone–Wales defects, which are known to affect phonon behavior through CC bond rotation (90°) and strain without introducing further Raman-active modes.44–46 These defect-induced structural distortions and nitrogen incorporation are further supported by FT-IR analysis, which elucidates the surface functional groups and bonding environments of CS-N-GQDs.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis

The FT-IR spectrum of CS-N-GQDs, as shown in Fig. 3b, clearly revealed the presence of functional groups introduced during the carbonization of cotton shell biomass. The peak around 3202 cm−1 could be attributed to the stretching vibrations of O–H and N–H bonds, confirming the presence of hydroxyl moieties retained from the biomass precursor, as well as nitrogen-based amine functionalities incorporated during synthesis.47,48 The signal at 1615 cm−1 was mainly related to the CO stretching vibrations of carbonyl groups, with a possible contribution from CC stretching within aromatic domains, which indicated the coexistence of a graphitic framework and residual oxygenated groups.43 A well-defined band at 1346 cm−1 corresponded to C–N stretching, confirming nitrogen doping through functionalities, such as pyrrolic nitrogen, consistent with early vibrational spectroscopic studies.49 In addition, the peak at 1051 cm−1 was assigned to C–O–C linkages or secondary C–N vibrations, which supported the presence of epoxy groups or nitrogenous moieties embedded in CS-N-GQDs.50,51 These surface functional groups of CS-N-GQDs can play a crucial role in sensing by facilitating intermolecular interactions and charge-transfer processes.

Optical characterization of CS-N-GQDs

The UV-visible absorption spectrum of CS-N-GQDs exhibited a shoulder peak at approximately 292 nm, which corresponded to the π → π* transitions of sp2-hybridized aromatic carbon domains, as shown in Fig. 3c.52,53 This specifies the existence of conjugated graphitic structures within CS-N-GQDs. The Tauc plot obtained from the absorbance, as shown in Fig. S1, described the bandgap of CS-N-GQDs, which was 4.0 eV, further confirmed by theoretical DFT calculations.54–56 The photoluminescence emission spectrum of CS-N-GQDs, as depicted in Fig. 3c, showed a strong peak at 444 nm, with an excitation of 354 nm, which fell within the blue region of the visible spectrum. This emission was attributed to surface-localized emissive states introduced by heteroatoms, such as amine, pyrrolic, and carbonyl groups, which modulated the electronic energy levels at the CS-N-GQD edges. The Stokes shift (Δνst) and emission full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of CS-N-GQDs were 90 nm (0.71 eV) and 98 nm (0.62 eV). This showed that CS-N-GQDs had a low self-absorption effect and low energy loss.49,57,58 The photoluminescence emission of CS-N-GQDs, as shown in Fig. 3d, exhibited a clear excitation-dependent behaviour, where the emission spectra were red-shifted in response to different excitation wavelengths. Although the mechanism responsible for the excitation-dependent behaviour of GQDs remained uncertain, this phenomenon could be from the synergistic contribution of the quantum confinement effect, often referred to as the size effect, varied emissive states, heteroatom doping, and surface functionalization modifications.34,59 The emission characteristics of CS-N-GQDs were further evaluated using the Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) 1931 chromaticity diagram. As depicted in Fig. 3e, the calculated CIE chromaticity coordinates were (x, y) = (0.152, 0.144), which fell within the blue region of the chromaticity space, confirming the dominant blue emission of CS-N-GQDs. Based on the CIE analysis, the colour purity of CS-N-GQDs was calculated to be 72.17%, indicating a well-saturated and clearly defined blue emission.60–62 The visual representation of CS-N-GQDs in a normal and handheld UV lamp was shown in Fig. 3f. The PL stability was also assessed by monitoring the emission intensity every 10 days at room temperature, as shown in Fig. S2a. CS-N-GQDs showed their notable long-term stability, maintaining consistent fluorescence throughout the period of 50 days, which highlighted their robustness for practical applications, such as sensing and biomedical applications.

Blue-emitting CS-N-GQDs and the standard reference solutions were optically matched at an excitation of 354 nm to measure the fluorescence quantum yield (QY). Quinine sulfate was dissolved in 0.1 M H2SO4 and used as the standard (QY = 54%). The relative fluorescence quantum yield of CS-N-GQDs was calculated and determined to be 41.3%.63–65

Optical sensing of H2O2 with CS-N-GQDs

The emission spectra of CS-N-GQDs exhibited a strong emission peak centered around 444 nm under excitation at 354 nm. Upon the incremental addition of H2O2 solution, a gradual decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed, indicating an effective quenching, as depicted in Fig. 4a. This fluorescence quenching efficiency was increased proportionally with H2O2 concentration over the range from 10 nM to 1 mM, as shown in Fig. 4b. The limit of detection was 5.34 ± 2 nM, and the linear response was up to the nanomolar regime (R2 = 0.96), further demonstrating the suitability for quantitative H2O2 sensing. Deviations from linearity at high concentrations could be rationalized as resulting from the near-complete quenching of excitons, where the probability of collisional encounters saturates, a typical phenomenon in dynamic quenching-based sensors.66 Selectivity studies predominantly demonstrated a stronger response toward H2O2 compared to other biologically relevant molecules present in the real-time samples, such as glucose (Glu); ascorbic acid (AA); citric acid (CA); ethanol (EtOH); and common ions, including potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), sodium (Na+), magnesium (Mg2+), ferric (Fe3+) and ferrous (Fe2+) ions. One-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant effect of analyte identity on fluorescence response (p < 0.05). Tukey's post-hoc analysis demonstrated that the response towards H2O2 differed significantly from all tested interferents, confirming the high selectivity of the sensing platform. As illustrated in Fig. 4c, the enhanced response arises from the redox-active nature of H2O2, which promotes efficient electron transfer during interfacial collisions. This specificity derives from the chemistry between the oxidative potential of H2O2 and the surface functional groups, such as C–O–C, O–H, and nitrogen-doped surface states, which serve as efficient acceptors of excited electrons during quenching.67Fig. 4d shows the pictographic representation of CS-N-GQD solution under a UV handheld lamp of 365 nm wavelength, visually showing a steady decrease in the intensity of fluorescence with increasing concentration of H2O2, consistent with the spectroscopic data. The visual change of fluorescence intensity further provided a qualitative confirmation of quantitative quenching behaviour. The quenching could be attributed to electron or energy transfer between CS-N-GQDs and the reactive oxygen species generated from H2O2 decomposition.20 This showed the potential of CS-N-GQDs as fluorescent probes for H2O2 detection. Fig. 4e displays the absorption spectra of CS-N-GQDs, 1 mM H2O2, and the mixture of CS-N-GQDs and 1 mM H2O2, which confirms that there is no prominent shift in the absorbance spectra, and a dynamic quenching mechanism occurred. This dynamic quenching pathway was consistent with the Stern–Volmer equation observed experimentally, as depicted in Fig. 4f, showing a strong linear correlation between the fluorescence intensity ratio (I0/I) and H2O2 concentration, with a linear coefficient of R2 = 0.95. This indicated a quenching process governed by collisional encounters, which was further elucidated with lifetime measurements.

The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy measurements of CS-N-GQDs revealed a multi-exponential decay behaviour, indicative of heterogeneous emissive sites and surface states. The decay profile could be fitted with three lifetime components, such as τ1 = 4.05 ns (52.22%), τ2 = 1.67 ns (24.08%), and a long-lived τ3 = 13.40 ns (23.69%), resulting in an average lifetime (τavg) of 9.09 ns. The coexistence of long and short components suggested that the photoluminescence originated from a combination of core excitonic transitions and surface trap-assisted radiative recombination pathways. The significant contribution of τ3 highlighted the role of nitrogen-induced defect states in providing long-lived emissive channels. The decay profiles shown in Fig. 4g further confirm that the average fluorescence lifetime of CS-N-GQDs decreases from 9.09 ns to 6.43, 6.38, and 6.19 ns upon the addition of 10 nM, 500 nM, and 1 mM H2O2, respectively. This demonstrated efficient excited-state electron transfer between CS-N-GQDs and the reactive oxygen species generated by H2O2. Significantly, negligible lifetime changes were observed in Fig. S3 when the potential interferents (Glu, AA, EtOH) were added. The multi-exponential fitting data shown in Table S1 indicated that only H2O2 induced such dynamic interactions. These results collectively confirmed that the photoluminescence quenching of CS-N-GQDs by H2O2 proceeded predominantly through a dynamic electron-transfer mechanism.68

In dynamic quenching, the non-radiative deactivation of excited electrons occurs via transient collisions during the excited-state lifetime, rather than through a static complex formation prior to excitation. This is supported by the less substantial shifts in absorption spectra and changes in PL lifetime profiles, establishing that the quenching does not involve ground-state complexation but rather excited-state interactions.68 Mechanistically, the dynamic quenching involves photoinduced electron transfer (PET) between excited CS-N-GQDs and H2O2 molecules upon collision, resulting in nonradiative relaxation pathways and PL intensity decrease without stable complex formation. Heteroatom doping and functionalization enhance the charge density and electronic delocalization in GQDs, increasing the possibility and rate of these electron transfer collisions under aqueous conditions.69,70 This mechanistic understanding not only rationalized the observed high sensitivity and selectivity but also aligned with broad findings on the fluorescence behaviour and potential redox-based sensing application of CS-N-GQDs.

Electrochemical sensing of CS-N-GQDs

As shown in Fig. 5a, the bare glassy carbon electrode (GCE) exhibited a nearly featureless voltammogram, suggesting sluggish electron-transfer kinetics arising from the limited electroactive surface area and poor catalytic activity of the unmodified electrode in a phosphate buffer (pH = 7). In contrast, the CS-N-GQD-modified electrode showed a prominent reduction peak at −0.6 V, which was attributed to the electroactivity of CS-N-GQDs. Upon the addition of 1 mM H2O2, the reduction peak current increased significantly, indicating the electroactivity of the sensor towards H2O2 reduction. This enhanced response originated from the synergistic effect of heteroatom functionalization into GQDs, as it improved conductivity and facilitated fast electron transfer, providing an electroactive site for H2O2 reduction. Further, the sensitive response towards the analyte was studied by amperometric and cyclic voltammetry analyses.71,72 Furthermore, the cyclic voltammetry studies for the sensor were also carried out at 60 mV s−1 in PBS by adding various concentrations of H2O2 (as depicted in Fig. S4a), with a prominent increase in the reduction current with the increase in the concentration of H2O2. The linear calibration curve was obtained from the CV responses, with a variation of H2O2 concentrations, as depicted in Fig. S4b, to calculate LOD, which was found to be 6.06 ± 0.7 nM.

The chronoamperometric response of CS-N-GQDs to consecutive additions of H2O2 was studied at a fixed potential (−0.6 V), as depicted in Fig. 5b. By the addition of H2O2, a rapid increase in the reduction current was observed, reaching a steady current within seconds, thereby demonstrating fast electron transfer kinetics, as confirmed by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.73,74Fig. 5c showed a linear relationship between current response and H2O2 concentration in the broad dynamic range used, with a linear coefficient of R2 = 0.96. LOD was calculated using the 3.3σ/slope method (σ refers to the standard deviation of the responses), which was found to be 5.96 ± 1 nM, indicating a high sensitivity towards H2O2. Additionally, the sensitivity of the electrochemical sensor was determined from the slope of the calibration plot, representing the change in current with respect to analyte concentration. The consistency in analytical performance parameters, particularly the limit of detection, was obtained from both chronoamperometric and cyclic voltametric analyses, indicating that the developed sensor upheld excellent robustness and operational consistency across different electrochemical measurement modes. This enhanced sensitivity could be given by the high density of active sites and the improved conductivity from nitrogen and oxygen in the carbon matrix.75,76

The amperometric curve shown in Fig. 5d exhibited a fast and constant current response upon the addition of H2O2. The CS-N-GQD sensor reached steady-state current within 7.92 s, indicating a fast electron transfer process and the efficient diffusion of H2O2 molecules towards the electrode interface. The response rate was calculated to be 1.23 µA cm−2 s−1, signifying exceptional kinetic characteristics. Such rapid responsiveness makes the CS-N-GQD-based sensor suitable for excellent real-time H2O2 detection.

Further, to understand the electron transfer rate at the electrode–electrolyte interface, we elucidated the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) for bare GCE and CS-N-GQDs. The Nyquist plots were best fitted with equivalent Randles electronic circuits, as shown in Fig. S5. The EIS plot had a small semicircular curve, which indicated that there was low charge-transfer resistance. The solution resistance was calculated and found to be 78.83 Ω and 88.19 Ω, and the charge transfer resistance was 85.77 Ω and 93.55 Ω for CS-N-GQDs and bare GCE, respectively, indicating enhanced interfacial electron-transfer kinetics. Additionally, the reduced solution resistance suggested improved electrolyte–electrode contact and relatively low ohmic losses. These results demonstrated that the incorporation of CS-N-GQDs improved the electrochemical conductivity and facilitated fast electron transfer, which was advantageous for H2O2 sensing.77–79

Fig. S6a illustrates the current density versus potential profile of cyclic voltammetry (CV) of CS-N-GQDs at different scan rates after adding 1 µM H2O2 in the PBS buffer solution. The change in current response with increasing scan rate in the electrochemical sensing of H2O2 indicated a scan-rate-dependent electrochemical process. This dependence response is a characteristic property of a diffusion-controlled mechanism, where the peak current increases as the scan rate rises due to the enhanced mass transport of the analyte to the electrode surface.80–82 The linear fit shown in Fig. S6b represented the correlation between the peak current and the square root of the scan rate. The linearity (R2 = 0.98) specified that the electrochemical process was primarily diffusion-controlled. In diffusion-controlled processes, the mass transport of the analyte to the electrode surface primarily relies on diffusion rather than adsorption. This signified the reduction behaviour at the electrode surface, which was facilitated by CS-N-GQDs, by the diffusion of the H2O2 molecules from the bulk solution to the electrode–electrolyte interface. The contribution of diffusion and adsorption was 72.56% and 27.43%, respectively, from the deconvoluted CV plot (Dunn's plot), as shown in Fig. S6c, confirming that diffusion predominantly controlled the process.

Further, the diffusive nature of the electrode material could be justified from the slope of the linearity plot of log peak current vs. log scan rate, as shown in Fig. 5e. The slope value (b) was 0.40, as obtained from the log(IP) versus log(scan rate) plot, thereby supporting the diffusive nature of the electrochemical process. For electrochemical systems, a slope of ∼0.40 corresponds to a fully diffusion-controlled process. If the slope is ∼1, it indicates a kinetic-controlled process. From the power law equation (ip = aνb), where ip is the peak current, ν is the scan rate, and b is the power-law exponent, we can apply a logarithmic transformation to derive a linear relationship of the form:(log(ip) = b log(ν) + log(a)).

This equation corresponds to a straight line in the form of y = mx + c, where y = log(ip) (logarithm of peak current), x = log(ν) (logarithm of scan rate), m = b (the slope of the linear plot), and c = log(a) (the intercept). Here, the slope was 0.40, which was close to the theoretical value for a diffusion-controlled system. This variation could be ascribed to the unique electronic structure and surface functional groups of CS-N-GQDs, which enhanced the electron-transfer kinetics and surface catalytic behaviour. The overall data interpreted that the electrochemical sensing of H2O2 with CS-N-GQDs as a catalyst involved a predominantly diffusion-controlled process at the electrode interface. However, the slight contribution from surface phenomena could be due to the synergistic effect of sensor surface-active sites and their interaction with H2O2.71,78,83

The fabricated electrode exhibited excellent storage stability, retaining approximately 96% of its initial current response after 45 days of storage, as shown in Fig. S2b. Selectivity studies were performed using the chronoamperometric study by adding 1 mM concentration of the interferents (AA, Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Glu, EtOH, CA, Fe2+, and Fe3+) and H2O2 at 50 seconds time interval and recording the current response, as shown in Fig. 5f. There was no prominent change in response with other interferents, showing the selectivity of the sensor towards the analyte. One-way ANOVA showed a statistically significant effect of the analyte identity on electrochemical response (p < 0.05). Tukey's post-hoc analysis confirmed that the response towards H2O2 differed significantly from all tested interferents, confirming the high selectivity of the sensing platform. The analytical performance of the developed CS-N-GQD sensor was benchmarked against recently reported carbon-dot and graphene quantum dot-based H2O2 sensing platforms, as shown in Tables S4 and S5. The sensor exhibited low detection limits of 5.34 ± 2 nM (optical) and 5.96 ± 1 nM (electrochemical), which were comparable to or better than those of many reported biomass-derived carbon nanomaterial systems. Beyond sensitivity, the present platform offered the distinct advantage of integrating complementary optical and electrochemical detection within a single biomass-derived GQD system, enabling the cross-validation of analytical responses and improved sensing reliability. These results highlighted the potential selectivity of the sensor material as an efficient electrode towards H2O2 and provided a foundation for the future development of high-performance biosensors.

Plausible mechanistic insights for H2O2 sensing

The optical sensing mechanism proceeded predominantly through an excited-state dynamic quenching process, where the excited fluorophore interacted with the analyte molecules, leading to non-radiative deactivation pathways. This interaction resulted in a measurable decrease in fluorescence intensity without significant alteration in the ground-state absorption characteristics; the dynamic quenching mechanism of CS-N-GQDs for H2O2 is illustrated in Fig. 6a. Such a quenching process is typically ascribed to collisional interactions among the fluorophore and the analyte during excited-state dynamics, proving the dynamic quenching behaviour rather than a static complex formation. The electrochemical sensing of hydrogen peroxide proceeded via a diffusion-controlled reduction process at the electrode surface. Upon the addition of H2O2, the reduction current increased due to enhanced electron transfer, resulting from the high availability of H2O2 molecules diffusing toward the electrode. The presence of an isosbestic point, as shown in Fig. S6a, indicated a stable and well-defined reaction pathway without the accumulation of intermediates or side reactions. These results demonstrated that the electrode provided an efficient platform for the sensitive and reliable electrochemical sensing of H2O2. The probable electrochemical sensing mechanism is schematically represented in Fig. 6b.

Schematic of the dual-mode optical and electrochemical sensing mechanism of H2O2. (a) Optical sensing mechanism illustrating the fluorescence quenching of CS-N-GQDs in the presence of H2O2via a dynamic quenching process by the photoinduced electron transfer (PET) pathway. Upon excitation, electrons from the excited-state CS-N-GQDs are transferred to H2O2, resulting in nonradiative relaxation and a decrease in photoluminescence intensity. (b) Electrochemical sensing mechanism based on a diffusion-controlled process at the CS-N-GQD-modified glassy carbon electrode (GCE). H2O2 molecules diffuse from the bulk solution to the electrode surface, where they undergo catalytic reduction. The enhanced electron-transfer kinetics at the CS-N-GQD interface led to a rise in the reduction current relative to the H2O2 concentration.

To further understand the optoelectronic properties and proposed sensing mechanism, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. The optimized structure revealed a favourable interaction of H2O2 with the functional sites of CS-N-GQDs through intermolecular interactions, consistent with the experimental data.

DFT study and interpretation

Ground-state electronic structure

The molecular geometry was fully optimized using ωB97XD/6-31G(d,p) level of theory. The optimized geometry is shown in Fig. 7a, corresponding to a local minimum on the potential energy surface, as confirmed by the absence of imaginary frequencies, and is employed for further calculations. Experimentally, UV-vis/Tauc analysis gave a direct optical bandgap of ∼4.0 eV. The calculated HOMO and LUMO gap for our model system was 4.06 eV (Fig. 7b), and it was consistent with the experimentally derived optical gap, supporting a wide-gap quantum-confined sp2 domain picture, where heteroatom incorporation and surface termination modulated frontier levels without collapsing the gap.

Atom colours: C (gray), O (red), N (blue), and H (white). (b) HOMO/LUMO isosurfaces and energy diagram. (c) Representative NTO pairs for the key S6 state.

Excited states analysis

Using time-dependent DFT excited-state analysis, the calculated excited states contained several visible excitations with non-zero oscillator strengths in the 2.54–3.03 eV range (488–409 nm), as tabulated in Table S2. Among these, state 6 (S6) was a prominent bright transition at 450 nm (2.75 eV, f = 0.2514) and lay very close to the experimentally observed blue photoluminescence maximum (∼444 nm; 2.79 eV). This agreement supported using S6 as the most relevant computed reference for discussing the experimentally active emissive manifold. Our further analysis revealed that S6 was primarily governed by a HOMO → LUMO + 2 transition, which was consistent with the presence of closely spaced acceptor orbitals in a defect/functionalized sp2 sheet. Correspondingly, the NTO description was highly compact; the leading NTO pair accounted for 84% of the transition density, as shown in Fig. 7c. S6 NTOs further showed that the hole/particle amplitudes were spatially localized on chemically perturbed regions (defect/heteroatom/oxygenated motifs) within the single-sheet network, consistent with a π-framework (π → π*) excitation rather than a lone-pair-dominated n → π* process. Importantly, S5 and S7 (highest fosc) also occurred within the same VEE window; taken together, the S5–S7 set could be viewed as a mixed but optically allowed manifold that collectively supported blue emission, as depicted in Fig. S7a and b, with S6 serving as a convenient bright-state anchor for discussion.

The natural transition orbital analysis of the S6 state showed that the hole and particle densities were distributed over the chemically perturbed sp2 framework and nearby heteroatom/oxygenated regions. This suggested that the emissive state was not purely localized in an ideal graphitic core but was influenced by surface and defect-related electronic environments. Such localization was important for sensing, because H2O2 could interact with these surface-accessible regions and perturb the excited-state charge distribution. This DFT result supported the experimentally observed dynamic fluorescence quenching. Since the emissive orbitals were associated with surface/defect-rich regions, the interaction of H2O2 with these regions could introduce an electron-transfer-assisted nonradiative pathway. This interpretation was observed with the decrease in PL intensity, the reduction in fluorescence lifetime, and the absence of major ground-state absorption changes after H2O2 addition. Therefore, the TDDFT and NTO results provided a molecular-level basis for assigning the optical response to dynamic PET-assisted quenching at functionalized surface states.

Interaction between H2O2 and GQDs

To rationalize the preferential interaction of H2O2 over H2O at oxidized surface motifs relevant to electrochemical response, counterpoise (CP)-corrected binding energies were evaluated for two explicitly constructed complexes involving an epoxy oxygen site on the CS-N-GQD model. In one configuration, H2O2 was directly bound to the epoxy oxygen, with H2O hydrogen-bonded to H2O2, as shown in Fig. S8a; in the alternate configuration, H2O was directly bound to the epoxy oxygen, with H2O2 hydrogen-bonded to H2O, as shown in Fig. S8b. The configuration with direct epoxy⋯ H2O2 binding was found to be slightly more stable by ∼0.08 kcal mol−1 in CP-corrected binding energy, relative to the alternate arrangement, as tabulated in Table S3.

Overall, the DFT results connected the experimental optical and electrochemical observations in two ways. First, the TDDFT/NTO analysis showed that the emissive states were associated with functionalized surface/defect regions that could be perturbed by H2O2, supporting the fluorescence quenching mechanism. Second, the counterpoise calculation study confirmed that the oxygenated surface sites could support a favorable but weak H2O2 association, consistent with a surface-mediated electrochemical reduction process, without requiring the irreversible structural modification of CS-N-GQDs. Further, the post-sensing analysis of CS-N-GQDs with H2O2 provided a strong experimental and complementary theoretical validation of sensing mechanisms.

Post-sensing chemical analysis of CS-N-GQDs toward H2O2 detection

To elucidate the interactions and structural changes occurring in CS-N-GQDs during H2O2 sensing, post-analysis was carried out using XPS, FTIR, and zeta potential measurements. These techniques together provided an understanding of surface modification, functional group contribution, and colloidal stability after exposure to H2O2. Deconvoluted XPS spectra showed that for CS-N-GQDs, as shown in Fig. S9a–d, the C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s spectra remained unchanged, with no prominent shifts in binding energies or emergence of new peaks. These results indicated that H2O2 interaction with CS-N-GQDs did not involve any covalent reactions or catalytic decomposition but instead proceeded via non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions), consistent with the observed zeta potential variation and FTIR shifts. Fig. S9e shows the FTIR spectra of CS-N-GQDs after exposure to H2O2 and a noticeable change compared to the pristine sample, confirming the interaction between H2O2 and the surface functional groups. A shift of ∼30 cm−1 in the C–O–C stretching band indicated the slight modification of the oxygen-containing functional environment, suggesting the involvement of epoxy groups during the sensing process. Additionally, the O–H/N–H stretching band exhibited a change in intensity and shape after H2O2 treatment, which could be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between H2O2 and the surface functional groups of CS-N-GQDs. These spectral variations indicated surface-level interactions rather than structural degradation, supporting the proposed sensing mechanism involving intermolecular interactions and charge transfer. As shown in Fig. S9f, CS-N-GQDs exhibited a negative zeta potential of −16.1 mV, confirming a colloid stability of GQDs with abundant oxygenated functional groups. Upon exposure to various concentrations of H2O2, the zeta potential shifted noticeably to +2.1 mV, indicating surface charge neutralization. Such interfacial charge modulation was associated with the observed fluorescence quenching and electrochemical signal variations. These findings provided mechanistic insight into the dual-mode H2O2 detection, making it suitable for real-time, practical sensing applications.

Real-time analysis of H2O2

To validate the practical applicability of the as-synthesized CS-N-GQDs beyond ideal aqueous buffers, real-time H2O2 sensing was evaluated in commercial juice and beverage sample matrices using both optical (fluorescence quenching) and electrochemical (amperometric/CV) readouts. According to the FDA, the standard residual H2O2 should be approximately 14.7 µM (≤0.5 ppm). These beverage matrices contained sugars, citric/ascorbic acids, antioxidants, and salts, which could potentially interfere with H2O2 detection; therefore, recovery-based quantification was essential to establish robustness under realistic conditions.

The juice/beverage samples were filtered (0.22 µm) to remove suspended solids and were then analyzed using a standard addition (spike-recovery) protocol. First, the inherent H2O2 concentration present in each sample (found, µM) was determined from the corresponding calibration curve. Next, each sample was spiked with 1 µM H2O2, and the concentration was re-measured (found, 1 µM spiked). As shown in Fig. 8a, CS-N-GQDs provided consistent fluorescence-based quantification across all beverage types. The native H2O2 levels were in the sub-µM range, while the measured concentrations after 1 µM spiking increased accordingly, confirming that the fluorescence quenching response remained functional even in colored/chemically complex samples. Recoveries were near-quantitative, indicating negligible matrix effects and corroborating the sensor's interference tolerance toward typical beverage matrices. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test, demonstrated significant differences among the real beverage samples (p < 0.05), confirming the capability of the developed sensor to distinguish varying H2O2 levels in different sample matrices.

Fig. 8b shows the corresponding electrochemical analysis in the same juices/beverages. The CS-N-GQDs/GCE platform retained strong catalytic responsiveness toward H2O2 in real samples, consistent with the fast kinetics observed in the buffer (steady-state response within ∼7.92 s). Importantly, spike-recovery remained near quantitative across all beverages, demonstrating that the electrode response was largely resilient to matrix components and that electron-transfer/catalytic sites on CS-N-GQDs remained accessible under practical conditions. A key outcome, as shown in Fig. 8, is that both optical and electrochemical modes provided comparable recovery behaviour in the same beverage matrices. This dual-readout agreement strengthened analytical confidence by allowing internal cross-checking and reducing the likelihood of false positives/negatives from any single measurement mode. Overall, these results confirmed that CS-N-GQDs acted as a robust dual-mode probe for real-time H2O2 monitoring in food/beverage samples, supporting their applicability for rapid screening and quality-control settings.

Experimental and methods

Materials

Cotton shells were collected from the local field of plots in Shiva Rampuram (Prakasam district, Andhra Pradesh). Ortho phosphoric acid, quinine sulphate, hydrogen peroxide, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from SRL chemicals. Nafion solution and potassium phosphate dibasic were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Ultrapure water was used in the experiment.

Material characterization of samples

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) was performed to analyse morphology and d-spacing on a JEOL-JEM 2100 high-resolution transmission electron microscope operating at a voltage of 200 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using an Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical Ltd) with Cu Kα radiation. The Raman spectra of the sample were obtained using B&W Tek (USA; BWS415-532H-001). The oxidation state and binding energy of the materials were identified with an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) PHI Versaprobe III and fitted with XPSPEAK 41 software. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on Bruker ALPHA II. The zeta potential measurements were carried out in a double-layered electrical cell, using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument. The absorbance studies were performed using a Tecan Spark spectrophotometer. The PL spectra, lifetime measurements, quantum yield (QY%) calculations, optical sensing and real-time analysis of H2O2 were measured using a Horiba Fluorolog spectrofluorometer. Electrochemical measurements and real-time analysis for H2O2 sensing were carried out using Admiral Squidstat plus an electrochemical workstation.

Optical sensing of H2O2

Optical sensing was performed using CS-N-GQDs as fluorescent probes. Fluorescence measurements were done using a Horiba Fluorolog spectrofluorometer. For each measurement, 50 µL of CS-N-GQD stock solution was diluted with deionized water to a final volume of 3 mL. To this, 1 mL of H2O2 solution, prepared at varying concentrations in DI water, was added, yielding final H2O2 concentrations from 10 nM to 1 mM. Emission spectra were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 354 nm, with emission collected over the range of 375–600 nm. The change in emission intensity was observed by changing H2O2 concentration to determine the limit of detection, and the quenching mechanism was studied. The selectivity of CS-N-GQDs was also studied using different interfering samples in the detection of H2O2. Each measurement was repeated in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Electrochemical sensing of H2O2

Electrochemical measurements for H2O2 detection were conducted using the Admiral Squidstat Potentiostat instrument, using a standard three-electrode setup comprising a platinum wire as the counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference, and a modified 4 mm glassy carbon electrode (GCE) as the working electrode. The electrode surface was modified with CS-N-GQDs and Nafion solution by drop-casting and drying under ambient conditions. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was initially performed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (PBS) to study the electrochemical behaviour of the modified electrode. CV scans were carried out within a potential range from 0 to −1 V, varying the scan rates from 10 to 120 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted to analyse the interfacial charge-transfer resistance for the modified electrode. Various concentrations of H2O2, from 10 nM to 1 mM, were added to the buffer solution, and the CV response was recorded to evaluate the sensor's response and linearity. Chronoamperometry (CA) was employed to assess the real-time current response and response time upon the sequential additions of H2O2 at a fixed potential. Selectivity studies of H2O2 were also performed using CA with different interfering samples. All electrochemical experiments were performed at room temperature. Each measurement was repeated in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Computational details

The ground-state (S0) geometry and vertical excitation calculations were computed using density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT), respectively. All calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 software package. Geometry optimizations were performed at the ωB97XD/6-31G(d,p) level of theory. To account for the aqueous environment used in experiments, solvation effects were included through an implicit solvent treatment using the polarizable continuum model (PCM) with water as the solvent. TDDFT vertical excitations were then evaluated on the S0-optimized geometries at the same level to obtain excitation energies and oscillator strengths. The excited-state electron–hole character was analyzed using natural transition orbitals (NTOs) generated within the Gaussian TDDFT framework, enabling the assignment of the optically relevant transitions in terms of the dominant hole–electron pairs and their spatial distribution on the modelled sp2 framework. Frequency calculations were performed on the optimized structures to confirm that the stationary points corresponded to true minima (no imaginary frequencies). The structural model was constructed based on spectroscopic and microscopic evidence (XPS, FT-IR, XRD, and HRTEM) to represent a single-layer defect/functionalized graphene-like network that captured the chemically relevant bonding motifs of CS-N-GQDs. Accordingly, the computational model was intended to represent the experimentally inferred motifs (heteroatom/oxygenated functionalities and defective sp2 domains); the corresponding model stoichiometry and functional-group placement were summarized.

Real-time analysis of H2O2 in juice and beverage samples using CS-N-GQDs

To evaluate the practical applicability of CS-N-GQDs, the real-time detection of H2O2 was performed using commercially available juice and beverage samples. The samples were initially filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to remove suspended particulates. For the optical detection assay, 50 µL of the CS-N-GQD solution was first diluted to 2 mL with deionized water and further diluted by the addition of 1 mL of the filtered juice sample, making the final assay volume 3 mL of stock solution. The change in fluorescence intensity was correlated with H2O2 concentration using a calibration curve prepared from standard H2O2 solutions. Recovery studies were conducted by spiking known concentrations of H2O2 into the juice and drink samples to evaluate the method's accuracy. In a similar way, the electrochemical sensing was performed with the filtered juice samples by adding 50 µL of the mixture from the stock solution to the PBS buffer electrolyte in the electrochemical setup. The variation in current density was correlated with H2O2 concentration through a calibration curve constructed using standard H2O2 solutions. Method accuracy was assessed via recovery experiments by spiking known amounts (1 µM) of H2O2 into juice and beverage samples. Each measurement was repeated in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Statistical analysis and limit of detection calculations

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent trials (n = 3). For statistical analysis, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while p < 0.01 was considered highly significant. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using LOD = 3.3σ/S, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank response (n = 3), and S is the slope of the corresponding calibration curve obtained from linear regression analysis. The calculated LOD values were used to assess the sensitivity of the CS-N-GQD sensor for optical and electrochemical sensing toward H2O2 detection.

Conclusion

In summary, we converted cotton-shell agrarian waste into nitrogen-doped graphene quantum dots (CS-N-GQDs) via a facile hydrothermal process, enabling the integration of biomass valorization with high-performance sensing. Structural and compositional characterization revealed nitrogen-doped, partially graphitized CS-N-GQDs with abundant heteroatom groups and defect-rich domains, which collectively modulated the electronic structure and contributed to the sensing performance. The probe functioned as a dual-mode platform for hydrogen peroxide detection, achieving an ultrasensitive and selective response in both fluorescence and electrochemical techniques, with nanomolar limits of detection over a broad dynamic range (10 nM to 1 mM). Density functional theory further correlated the emissive states and analyte interactions with CS-N-GQDs. Post-sensing analyses confirmed non-covalent interactions between H2O2 and CS-N-GQDs, resulting in functional-group changes and surface charge modulation without structural alteration. Reliable real-time performance with juice and beverage samples highlighted the platform's practical robustness for real-time monitoring in environmental, food safety, and biomedical applications. This work establishes an effective waste-to-wealth approach by transforming low-value agrarian residues into high-value functional nanomaterials for advanced sensing applications. While CS-N-GQDs exhibited good sensing performance, some limitations still existed. The present study is primarily a laboratory-scale study, and large-scale production and long-term operational stability need to be further evaluated. Moreover, the sensing performance has been mostly validated in beverage samples, and extensive studies in complex biological and environmental matrices are yet to be explored. Although fluorescence and electrochemical sensing modes were successfully demonstrated, photoelectrochemical behaviour and device-level integration were not addressed in this work. Future research will focus on the fabrication of portable dual-mode and photoelectrochemical sensing devices, validation in clinically and environmentally relevant samples, and the large-scale sustainable production of biomass-derived N-GQDs. The developed platform may be extended toward wearable sensors and smartphone-assisted detection systems, as well as the monitoring of other reactive oxygen species and biologically important analytes, thereby advancing the practical implementation of waste-derived multifunctional nanomaterials.
