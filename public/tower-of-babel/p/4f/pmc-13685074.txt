Efficacy of phloroglucinol in pediatric acute intussusception and development of a predictive model for treatment failure: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qi Zhou, Xiangfen Ji, Chao Liu, Xiang Li, Lei Zhang, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute intussusception is a common abdominal emergency in children. Although air enema reduction is an established effective treatment, the use of intravenous phloroglucinol alone for this condition has rarely been reported. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of phloroglucinol compared with air enema for pediatric acute intussusception and to develop and validate a predictive model for phloroglucinol treatment failure, aiding clinicians in early identification of high-risk children for timely management adjustment and prevention of severe complications.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 1149 children with acute intussusception admitted to our hospital between January 2014 and December 2023. Baseline characteristics, clinical presentations, and ultrasound findings were collected. We used chi-square test to compare treatment outcomes between the phloroglucinol group (n = 757) and the air enema group (n = 392). Children in the phloroglucinol group were randomly divided into training and validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. Independent risk factors for phloroglucinol treatment failure were identified using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, and a predictive model was constructed accordingly. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA).Results(1) No statistically significant difference was observed in treatment success rates between the phloroglucinol and air enema groups (93.7% vs. 95.4%, P = 0.227). (2) Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified duration of symptoms, vomiting, and intussusception depth as independent risk factors for phloroglucinol treatment failure (all P < 0.05). A nomogram incorporating these factors achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.96 (95%CI: 0.94–0.99) in the training set and 0.93 (95%CI: 0.81-1.00) in the validation set. Calibration curves demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. DCA indicated a significant clinical net benefit of the model.ConclusionPhloroglucinol appears to be an effective treatment option for pediatric acute intussusception. The developed nomogram, based on duration of symptoms, vomiting, and intussusception depth, exhibits robust predictive performance and may serve as a convenient and reliable tool for the early identification of children at high risk for phloroglucinol treatment failure, facilitating timely clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Intussusception, the invagination of one intestinal segment into an adjacent lumen, leads to obstructed passage of intestinal contents, bowel wall edema, venous congestion, and potentially obstruction [1].It is a frequent cause of acute abdominal pain in infants and young children, predominantly affecting those aged 3 to 18 months, with a peak incidence between 4 and 6 months [2–4].Intussusception is categorized as primary or secondary, with the latter accounting for approximately 10% of cases. Common pathological lead points include Meckel’s diverticulum, polyps, and lymphoma, with rare etiologies such as Burkitt’s lymphoma co-existing with Meckel’s diverticulum also reported [2, 5].Non-pathological triggers may involve dietary changes, rotavirus vaccination, recent infections, enteritis, and dehydration [2, 6].

Clinical manifestations of pediatric acute intussusception are variable. Classic symptoms include paroxysmal abdominal pain or crying (occurring in approximately 84.1% of cases [7] ), vomiting (41.5%–70.8% [7, 8] ), and red currant jelly-like stools or positive fecal occult blood (6.0%–70.7% [7–9], with wide variation potentially attributable to differences in time to presentation). An abdominal mass (67.9% [7] ) and abdominal distension (76.0% [9] ) may also be present. In infants under 3 months, presentations are often more atypical, predominantly featuring vomiting, bloody stools, and intermittent crying [10]. Some children may present with non-specific symptoms such as lethargy [3].

Management strategies for pediatric intussusception include both surgical and non-surgical approaches. Non‑surgical treatments mainly include ultrasound‑ or fluoroscopy‑guided air enema and hydrostatic enema. These are widely used in clinical practice because of their high success rates and good safety profiles, and they are the first‑line options for most children with stable vital signs and no signs of peritonitis or bowel perforation. However, there remains debate worldwide as to which is superior. Some support air enema as the preferred non‑surgical method, arguing that it achieves higher reduction success rates, shorter procedure times, and comparable safety to hydrostatic enema [11]. Others advocate hydrostatic enema as the first choice, citing advantages such as lower cost, shorter duration, and no radiation exposure [12]. The use of sedatives is also controversial [13].

Based on the authors’ experience, children often struggle to cooperate with air enema reduction due to fear and pain. Phloroglucinol, a smooth muscle relaxant, can alleviate intestinal spasms associated with intussusception by relaxing the intestinal smooth muscle [14]. This action not only helps relieve spastic pain and abnormal peristalsis (which may exacerbate the invagination) but may also create conditions conducive to spontaneous reduction. Nevertheless, the value of phloroglucinol in treating pediatric acute intussusception remains inadequately investigated.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the clinical efficacy of phloroglucinol versus air enema in treating pediatric acute intussusception, and to construct and validate a nomogram for predicting phloroglucinol treatment failure.

Most existing predictive models focus on failure of pneumatic or hydrostatic enema (e.g., the CMUI score) [15]. There is no study specifically investigating the value of phloroglucinol in treating pediatric acute intussusception, nor a prediction tool for the response to phloroglucinol therapy.

If non‑surgical treatment failure is not recognized promptly in children with intussusception, disease progression may lead to serious complications such as intestinal perforation and necrosis [16]. From a clinical communication perspective, early identification of high‑risk cases allows clinicians to lower parental expectations for this “simple, less painful” therapy beforehand, preparing them mentally for the possibility that “my child may well need further intervention”. From a clinical practice perspective, it helps clinicians remain vigilant and make timely decisions about further intervention (air enema/surgery). Establishing a predictive model for phloroglucinol treatment failure enables risk stratification: low‑risk children can be appropriately managed with medication, while high‑risk children require heightened vigilance to avoid delayed surgery, thereby reducing adverse outcomes, improving prognosis, and providing individualized decision support.

Our study fills the gap in “drug‑only treatment of intussusception and individualized risk assessment”, providing a basis for reducing unnecessary enema attempts and optimizing treatment pathways.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included children diagnosed with acute intussusception at Qilu Hospital (Qingdao), Cheeloo College of Medicine, Shandong University, between January 2014 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No.: KYLL-KS-2025239). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Diagnosis was confirmed by abdominal ultrasound, demonstrating a “target” or “doughnut” sign in the transverse sections and a “sleeve sign” in the longitudinal sections. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 0–14 years; (2) ultrasound-confirmed intussusception. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of other surgical acute abdominal conditions; (2) history of abdominal surgery; (3) missing critical clinical data.

Methodological details

Treatment success: intussusception resolution confirmed by ultrasound.

Treatment failure: intussusception still visible on ultrasound.

In clinical practice, we observed that most children achieved resolution of intussusception on repeat ultrasound approximately 2 h after a single dose of phloroglucinol. For children who showed a “partial response” (defined as: (1) reduction in the depth of intussusception on ultrasound, and (2) improvement in clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain and vomiting) after the first dose, a second dose of phloroglucinol (2 mg/kg) was given, and ultrasound was repeated 1 h later. If intussusception resolved after the second dose, this was also considered treatment success.

Children who still had visible intussusception after two doses were classified as phloroglucinol treatment failures. In addition, children whose intussusception depth remained unchanged or increased on the first follow‑up ultrasound (after one dose) were also considered treatment failures.

For patients in the air enema group, we employed a self-made air enema device to deliver pulsatile inflation at a pressure of 80 to100 mmHg. No sedatives or antispasmodics were used before the procedure.

In the early phase of the study, we mainly used classical air enema for children presenting with intussusception. Gradually, we observed in small‑scale clinical practice that phloroglucinol was indeed quite effective. Based on preliminary studies, we gained sufficient confidence in using phloroglucinol alone for pediatric intussusception. From approximately 2019 onwards, for children presenting to our hospital with intussusception, we offered parents two treatment options (intravenous phloroglucinol or direct air enema) for them to choose. In clinical practice, the vast majority of parents chose phloroglucinol, while a few opted for direct air enema.

The specific treatment procedures – both the air enema protocol and the phloroglucinol administration regimen – did not change during the 10‑year period.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), season of onset, duration of symptoms, potential triggers, body temperature, clinical symptoms (abdominal pain/crying, vomiting, bloody stools), intussusception depth (measured by ultrasound), treatment modality, and treatment outcome.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages). Continuous variables, after normality testing, were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Group comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate.

The phloroglucinol group (n = 757) was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 530) and an internal validation set (30%, n = 227) using the createDataPartition function from the caret package in R software (version 4.3.3) with a fixed random seed (set.seed(731)). Univariate logistic regression analysis was initially performed in the training set. Variables with P < 0.1 were entered into a multivariate logistic regression analysis using forward stepwise selection to identify independent risk factors for phloroglucinol treatment failure. A nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariate model to visualize the predicted risk.

Model performance was evaluated by: (1) discrimination, using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC); (2) calibration, using calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test; and (3) clinical utility, using decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess net benefit across various threshold probabilities. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1149 children with intussusception were included. Of these, 757 (65.9%) received intravenous phloroglucinol (2 mg/kg) (phloroglucinol group), and 392 (34.1%) underwent direct air enema reduction (air enema group) (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the patients

Comparison of efficacy: phloroglucinol vs. air enema

In the phloroglucinol group, treatment was successful in 709 children (93.7%) and failed in 48 (6.3%). In the air enema group, success was achieved in 374 children (95.4%) and failure in 18 (4.6%). The difference in success rates was not statistically significant (χ² = 1.459, P = 0.227) (Table 2). For the 48 failures in the phloroglucinol group, 38 showed “partial response”: reduction in intussusception depth, and improvement in symptoms such as abdominal pain and vomiting.10 showed “no response”: no change or increase in intussusception depth, and no relief of symptoms.

Treatment Options and Outcomes

No serious adverse events (bowel perforation, necrosis, peritonitis, etc.) occurred.

For children who failed phloroglucinol treatment, the subsequent management was air enema. Notably, all children who failed phloroglucinol therapy in this study achieved successful reduction with subsequent air enema, and none required surgery. This suggests that phloroglucinol administration may facilitate subsequent intussusception reduction.

Recurrence occurred in both groups. Among the enrolled patients, 239 experienced recurrence(majority only once; a few had multiple recurrences).

Risk factors for phloroglucinol treatment failure

The 757 children in the phloroglucinol group were randomly divided into a training set (n = 530, 70%) and a validation set (n = 227, 30%). Clinical characteristics were well-balanced between the two sets (Table 3). Univariate logistic regression analysis in the training set identified duration of symptoms > 12 h, vomiting, intussusception depth, age, and season of onset as potential risk factors (P < 0.1). Subsequent multivariate analysis confirmed that duration of symptoms > 12 h (OR = 7.44, 95%CI = 2.76–20.08, P < 0.001), vomiting (OR = 7.90, 95%CI = 2.69–23.24, P < 0.001), and intussusception depth (OR = 7.11, 95%CI = 3.78–13.39, P < 0.001) were independent risk factors for phloroglucinol treatment failure (Table 4). There were 40 treatment failure events in the training set. The final multivariate model retained three independent risk factors, with EPV ≈ 13.3, which is within an acceptable range. Although “winter” (OR = 0.37, 95%CI: 0.14‑0.99, P = 0.049) and “age” (OR = 0.99, 95%CI: 0.98‑1.00, P = 0.094) met the entry criterion of P < 0.1 in univariate analysis, in forward stepwise regression, after introducing “duration of symptoms > 12 hours”, “vomiting” and “intussusception depth”, their P values rose to 0.12 and 0.19, respectively, failing to meet the retention criterion. Collinearity diagnostics showed VIF < 2 for all candidate variables, so exclusion was not due to multicollinearity. Thus, we selected a more parsimonious and robust model.

Development of the nomogram prediction model

A logistic regression model was constructed incorporating these three independent risk factors and was visualized as a nomogram (Fig. 2). The total score, derived by summing the points for each predictor, corresponds to the individual predicted probability of phloroglucinol treatment failure, providing an intuitive quantitative tool for clinical assessment.

Nomogram for predicting the risk of treatment failure with phloroglucinol in pediatric intussusception

Validation and evaluation of the nomogram model

The prediction model exhibited excellent discriminative ability, with AUCs of 0.96 (95%CI: 0.94–0.99) in the training set and 0.93 (95%CI: 0.81-1.00) in the validation set (Fig. 3). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between the model’s predicted probabilities and the observed failure rates, closely approximating the ideal diagonal (Fig. 4). DCA revealed that the model provided a positive clinical net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 5). Confusion matrix analysis showed high specificity (> 96%) and negative predictive value (NPV > 99%) in both sets. However, sensitivity was 75% in the validation set, and the positive predictive value (PPV) was 50% (Table 4), suggesting that while the model is highly reliable for excluding low-risk individuals, its ability to accurately identify high-risk individuals may be improved with further refinement.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that phloroglucinol achieves a success rate of 93.7% in treating pediatric acute intussusception, which is comparable to the 95.4% success rate observed with traditional air enema (P = 0.227). This finding suggests that phloroglucinol could be considered an effective treatment alternative, particularly for children who may have difficulty cooperating with air enema due to fear or pain.

Non-surgical management of pediatric intussusception primarily involves ultrasound- or fluoroscopy-guided air or hydrostatic enema, both widely used with high success rates and favorable safety profiles. For air enema, a single-center prospective study by Vakaki et al. [17] reported an overall success rate of 97.7% in 131 children undergoing ultrasound-guided air enema, with no complications such as perforation and low recurrence, supporting its use as a first-line therapy. A meta-analysis by Qafesha et al. [11] encompassing 29 studies (9,281 patients) further indicated that air enema is associated with a higher success rate (risk ratio 1.22) and shorter reduction time (mean difference − 2.64 min) compared to liquid enema, with no significant differences in perforation or recurrence rates, advocating for its preference as the primary non-surgical modality.

Hydrostatic enema is also an effective alternative. In a retrospective study by Guo et al. [18], ultrasound-guided warm saline enema was successful in 92.31% of 169 children, with a mean reduction time of approximately 20.84 min and minimal complications (1.78% nausea/vomiting). Similarly, Yadav et al. [19] reported a success rate of 92.85% in a cross-sectional study of 81 children undergoing saline hydrostatic enema. Furthermore, colonoscopic reduction may be feasible in select cases with experienced operators and surgical backup, as suggested by Shahramian et al. [20], who reported a success rate of 98.33%. Research by Shikha et al. [21] also hinted that hydrocortisone might increase the proportion of “easily reducible” cases.

Phloroglucinol is a musculotropic, non-atropine, non-papaverine antispasmodic agent that selectively relieves smooth muscle spasm without anticholinergic side effects and with minimal impact on normal smooth muscle activity [14, 22]. Its mechanisms in alleviating intussusception are likely threefold. First, phloroglucinol directly relieves gastrointestinal smooth muscle spasm. A randomised double-blind trial by Jung H et al. showed that it significantly inhibited gastric peristalsis (peristalsis inhibition rate: 22.5% vs. 9.9% during gastroscopy) and improved endoscopic visualisation, supporting the inference that it relieves intussusception symptoms by inhibiting smooth muscle spasm [14]. A case report also indicated that phloroglucinol may assist small‑bowel intussusception reduction by “relieving intestinal spasm and improving peristalsis” [23]. Second, phloroglucinol acts at the molecular level to regulate smooth muscle contraction. Animal studies have shown that it inhibits oxytocin‑induced contraction of gastric circular muscle, downregulates oxytocin receptor (OTR) protein and mRNA expression, and modulates oestradiol, prostaglandin E2 and F2α levels [24]. Although not directly studied in intussusception, this provides a molecular basis for its spasmolytic effect. Third, phloroglucinol has broad‑spectrum smooth muscle antispasmodic properties. As a non‑atropinic, non‑papaverinic antispasmodic, it is as effective as conventional agents (e.g., scopolamine) for renal/biliary colic, confirming its broad inhibitory effect on visceral smooth muscle spasm [25]. Additionally, in patients with uterine hyperperistalsis (≥ 3 waves/min), phloroglucinol significantly improved embryo transfer success, indirectly reflecting its ability to regulate abnormal peristalsis [26, 27]- a property that can be extrapolated to intestinal smooth muscle modulation.

Beyond spasmolysis, some studies have demonstrated anti‑inflammatory or antioxidant effects of phloroglucinol (e.g., through AhR, Nrf2/HO‑1 pathways) [28, 29]. While intussusception is often accompanied by local inflammation and spasm, this anti‑inflammatory effect may theoretically contribute to symptom relief, but it is not considered a direct causal factor for reduction.

Currently, phloroglucinol is primarily used in adults for acute spastic pain of the digestive and biliary tracts, urinary tract, and gynecological conditions [30]. It has a favorable safety profile with no significant toxicity, teratogenicity, or mutagenicity [14]. Our study suggests that its application in pediatric intussusception is straightforward, may reduce child resistance to subsequent air enema if needed, and is well-accepted by parents.

Previous research has identified various factors associated with non-surgical reduction failure. Regarding age, several studies have identified infancy (particularly < 1 year) as a risk factor for failure [31–34], although some have also found older age (≥ 4 years) to be associated with increased risk, potentially indicating a higher proportion of secondary intussusception [35]. Concerning duration of symptoms, Acer-Demir et al. [8] demonstrated that symptoms lasting > 12 h were associated with higher rates of fever, peritoneal fluid, radiological abnormalities, and recurrence. Although Williams et al. [36] noted that a short delay (< 8 h) from diagnosis to enema did not affect success rates, delayed presentation is generally linked to poorer outcomes, especially in resource-limited settings [9, 37, 38]. For intussusception depth, Acer-Demir et al. [8] found that a longer intussuscepted segment correlated with increased recurrence risk, particularly in children < 2 years old. Clinically, the presence of bloody stools has been consistently confirmed as a significant predictor of treatment failure [32–34, 39], while vomiting, suggestive of high-grade obstruction or more severe illness, can also elevate failure risk [33]. Carter et al. [26] identified tachycardia (HR ≥ 128 bpm) and elevated systolic blood pressure (≥ 115 mmHg) as markers of systemic stress or early shock associated with failure. Tseng et al. [40] reported a higher recurrence rate for adenovirus-associated intussusception (48.1% vs. 13.8%), which might indirectly increase the risk of treatment failure.

Our model incorporates three variables – symptom duration, vomiting, and intussusception depth – all of which are common predictors of failure in non‑surgical treatment in many studies. These findings are largely consistent with previous research on non‑surgical treatment of pediatric intussusception [8, 33, 36].

Vomiting is a classic clinical manifestation of intussusception. Persistent vomiting suggests obstruction of intestinal content passage, aggravated bowel wall oedema, or compression of mesenteric vessels, leading to increased tension and fragility of the intussuscepted segment, making the bowel more susceptible to injury or resistant to reduction [41].

Prolonged symptom duration reflects disease progression. Some studies have grouped patients by symptom duration (< 12 h vs. ≥12 h) [8]. In clinical practice, symptoms beyond 12 h are often accompanied by bowel wall oedema, local ischaemia, or adhesion formation, which “incarcerate” the intussuscepted segment more tightly and increase resistance during enema [42].

Greater intussusception depth indicates more firm entrapment of the bowel segment [41].

All these factors reduce bowel wall compliance, increase resistance to non‑surgical reduction, or pose a risk of irreversible injury, thereby significantly increasing the probability of failure.

Our model demonstrated good discrimination in both the training (AUC = 0.96) and validation (AUC = 0.93) cohorts, with calibration plots close to the ideal line and DCA indicating positive clinical net benefit. These results collectively suggest that the nomogram possesses satisfactory predictive performance for phloroglucinol treatment failure. Despite its limitations, our predictive model has a clear and actionable advantage: very high specificity and an extremely high negative predictive value (NPV > 99%). Clinically, this means that when the model classifies a child as “low risk”, the probability of phloroglucinol treatment failure is nearly zero (< 1%). Therefore, the model provides clinicians with a reliable “safety net”. For these low‑risk children, initial treatment with phloroglucinol can be confidently attempted, potentially avoiding the pain, radiation exposure (if fluoroscopically guided), and resource consumption associated with immediate air enema. This ability to safely rule out high‑risk cases is the model’s primary clinical value.

Conversely, the modest sensitivity (75%) and low positive predictive value (PPV, 50%) in the validation set are major limitations that need to be acknowledged.

75% sensitivity means the model will miss one‑quarter (25%) of children who will actually fail phloroglucinol treatment. These “false‑negative” cases represent missed opportunities for early intervention. Clinicians might unknowingly delay more definitive treatment (air enema or surgery) for these children, potentially allowing disease progression.

50% PPV means that among children labelled as “high risk” by the model, only half will actually fail phloroglucinol. If applied conservatively, this would lead to overtriage – unnecessarily denying half of these patients a potentially effective, non‑invasive treatment and subjecting them to a more invasive enema procedure.

Thus, the model in its current form should not be used as a strict “rule‑in” tool to deny phloroglucinol therapy. Its primary role remains adjunctive: to increase clinical vigilance and prompt closer monitoring of high‑risk children, rather than to mandate immediate treatment escalation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design introduces potential selection and information bias, and the generalizability of our findings requires validation through multi-center, prospective studies with larger, more diverse samples. This may first require a consensus among multi‑center clinicians regarding the therapeutic efficacy of phloroglucinol. Second, the retrospective nature precluded analysis of certain potential confounders, such as detailed pain scores and specifics of the reduction procedure. Third, the moderate sensitivity suggests that the current model lacks key determinants of failure. Future studies should prospectively collect and evaluate advanced sonographic features (e.g., bowel wall thickness, compromised wall vascularity on power Doppler, the presence of a pathological lead point such as Meckel’s diverticulum or polyp, or significant free fluid) as well as systemic inflammatory and injury biomarkers (e.g., serum C-reactive protein, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein levels). Advanced sonographic features directly reflect the degree of ischemic compromise and mechanical irreducibility, while the biomarkers may indicate a more advanced inflammatory or ischemic state not fully captured by symptoms alone. Finally, the application of phloroglucinol in pediatric intussusception lacks support from large-scale, multi-center randomized controlled trials and is not yet integrated into mainstream guidelines. Further investigation is warranted to clarify its mechanism of action, optimal dosage, appropriate patient selection, and potential synergy with other reduction methods.

Conclusion

In conclusion, phloroglucinol appears to be an effective treatment for pediatric acute intussusception, with success rates comparable to air enema. The predictive model based on duration of symptoms, vomiting, and intussusception depth can effectively identify children at high risk for phloroglucinol treatment failure. Its favorable discrimination and calibration provide a practical quantitative tool for individualized clinical decision-making. For low-risk children, phloroglucinol may be considered a first-line option; for high-risk children, closer monitoring and preparation for alternative interventions are advisable. Future high-quality studies are needed to validate and refine this model and to establish the optimal role of phloroglucinol in managing pediatric intussusception. The authors plan to conduct a prospective randomized controlled trial to further evaluate its efficacy.
