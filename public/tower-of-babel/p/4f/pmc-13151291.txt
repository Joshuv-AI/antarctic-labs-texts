Leveraging experience-based co-design to support an innovative health workforce: a graduate certificate in health services innovation case study

Background

The dual challenge of population growth and ageing is exerting considerable strain on Australia’s healthcare system, with implications for the complexity and responsiveness of healthcare delivery. The shifting demographics demand that healthcare delivery systems adapt, yet the capacity of the current workforce remains insufficient to meet this demand [1, 2]. Workforce shortages persist, and health professionals must rethink resource allocation and service delivery models while acquiring the advanced skill sets required to navigate a complex healthcare environment [3, 4].

While in theory, evolving healthcare demands should drive corresponding changes in workforce training, evidence suggests this is not happening at the necessary pace [5]. A key challenge lies in preparing current and future healthcare workers to engage with innovative solutions. Without sufficient training in problem-solving, collaboration, and innovation, healthcare workers struggle to implement and sustain transformative practices. Addressing this gap necessitates educational strategies that foster motivation, capability, innovation and translation skills and capabilities among healthcare professionals [6, 7].

Workforce education and healthcare complexity

The ability to adapt to healthcare complexities requires equipping staff and managers with skills in design frameworks, knowledge translation, and implementation science [8]. Educational programs must integrate these competencies, ensuring healthcare professionals can critically engage with reliable and current information for strategic decision-making [9]. This shift moves beyond academic exercises to real-world applications in complex health system management [10, 11].

Programs that adopt these strategies have demonstrated measurable benefits, including enhanced self-efficacy, confidence in intervention design, and research practices, ultimately contributing to health service innovation [12]. For example, The Leading Health Services Innovation program exemplifies this approach [13]. Designed to support the local health workforce, the program addresses educational deficiencies by incorporating interactive learning strategies to optimise knowledge translation and decision-making processes. Through these efforts, the LHSI program seeks to produce a workforce capable of driving transformation and innovation within the healthcare sector.

Experience-based co-design: a catalyst for innovation

Experience-based co-design (EBCD) is a participatory improvement approach that combines empirical evidence with stakeholder experiences to identify ‘touch points’ and co-create solutions through iterative design cycles. Internationally, co-design approaches have been applied in health service improvement and in tertiary education design. However systematic descriptions of how EBCD can be operationalized to co-design postgraduate workforce education for innovation capabilities remain limited. In particular, with explicit attention to aligning service priorities with academic requirements EBCD offers a transformative approach to addressing the gaps in workforce development. EBCD fosters a collaborative partnership between academia and industry, leveraging the unique strengths of each. Universities contribute theoretical expertise, advanced research methodologies, and access to emerging technologies, while industry offers insights into real-world challenges and emphasises practical application [14].

Collaborative partnerships in co-design

Co-design thrives on collaborative partnerships that leverage the complementary strengths of academia and industry [14]. The collaboration extends beyond idea generation to resource optimisation, where shared facilities, personnel, and expertise facilitate cost-effective initiatives [13]. Beyond technical and logistical advantages, EBCD fosters workforce development by immersing participants in real-world problem-solving scenarios, enhancing practical skills, employability, and innovation readiness. Integrated into initiatives like the LHSI program, EBCD helps healthcare systems address workforce deficits and equip professionals with the tools to navigate an evolving landscape. Simultaneously, a co-designed approach optimises resources—shared facilities, personnel, and expertise—to facilitate cost-effective initiatives while stimulating knowledge transfer. By translating academic insights into industry applications, such collaborations drive novel products, processes, and solutions. Early industry engagement further strengthens implementation by identifying potential challenges in the design phase, increasing the likelihood of success.

The terms “co-production” and “co-design” are often used interchangeably and ambiguously; however they possess distinct characteristics. Co-design refers to the collaborative process of designing services or interventions, emphasising stakeholder engagement during the planning and development phases [15]. In contrast, co-production extends beyond design to encompass the joint delivery and evaluation of services, involving stakeholders throughout the entire service lifecycle [16]. Despite these differences, both approaches share common principles of partnership, shared decision making, and valuing stakeholder contributions, which often leads to their conflated use in practice. Recent literature highlights the feasibility of EBCD as a valuable methodology for fostering meaningful stakeholder involvement and enhancing the quality and responsiveness of healthcare services [17]. Yet a distinguishing feature of EBCD is that it isn’t bound by a definition, rather it is a participatory approach that integrates empirical research evidence with the user experience and adopts a structured process for involvement [17, 18]. This method ensures that healthcare interventions are both scientifically grounded and tailored to the actual needs and preferences of end-users, enhancing the relevance and perceived alignment of care delivery [17].

EBCD frameworks are flexible, but are centred around two components, (1) User-centred orientation (experience-based) and (2) Co-design (a collaborative change process) [15]. The user-centered approach of EBCD ensures that innovations directly address the real challenges faced by individuals in the healthcare system, enhancing the quality of care and increasing the likelihood of successful adoption. The co-design process typically includes understanding user experiences which are analysed to identify critical touchpoints. Stakeholders are then engaged in co-design workshops to collaboratively develop solutions. The outcomes of these workshops are then reviewed and refined based on ongoing feedback and evaluation.

The implementation and evaluation of EBCD has been extensively explored in healthcare consumer groups, demonstrating its feasibility in enhancing patient-centered care and service delivery [19–21]. Similarly, co-design principles have been successfully applied in tertiary education, fostering collaborative learning environments that prepare students for real-world challenges [22–24].

While EBCD is increasingly recognised for its contributions to healthcare innovation, its application in workforce training remains underexplored. Existing literature highlights its success in enhancing patient-centered care models and service delivery [18, 25, 26], yet systematic frameworks for co-designing educational programs that prepare healthcare professionals for innovation are lacking. Moreover, while healthcare consumers and students are common beneficiaries of EBCD, there is limited research on its role in workforce development, where healthcare workers themselves are the end-users. Addressing this gap could unlock new strategies for equipping health professionals to lead innovation and transformation within the sector.

An important issue in feasible and acceptable co-design processes is the presence of power dynamics that may influence collaborations between academic, sector/industry and leadership stakeholders. Co-design processes frequently assume equal participation, yet in practice, organisational hierarchies, professional identities and different accountabilities shape whose voices are heard and prioritise [26]. Academic stakeholders may dominate because of experience and control of research design and reporting, while operational staff may defer due to time constraints or perceived lack of authority [27]. Industry stakeholders, particularly in healthcare governance roles, can exert disproportionate influence by controlling resources and operational requirements, limiting authentic collaboration [28]. Without mechanisms to negotiate power asymmetries, co-design may reinforce existing hierarchies rather than partnering for innovation. Co-design activities and strategies should incorporate reflexive practices and facilitation approaches to acknowledge and redistribute power equitably.

This case study examines the co-design process of a Graduate Certificate in Health Services Innovation with health service stakeholders using a structured EBCD-informed, multi-stage co-design stages, and to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of that co-design process in aligning sector needs with academic requirements. By providing a detailed case study of how EBCD can be operationalised in postgraduate health workforce education, this study contributes to medical education scholarship by offering a transparent, replicable approach to participatory curriculum design aligned with implementation-oriented capabilities.

Methods

Study design

This work was undertaken within a broader hybrid type II implementation-effectiveness evaluation of a workforce education program. This paper reports the co-design component as an embedded qualitative case study of the development of a Graduate Certificate in Health Services Innovation. The program was co-designed through a university-health serviced partnership using multi-staged EBCD- informed processes.

The Graduate Certificate consisted of four sequential courses (10 credit points each), with each course undergoing the same four-stage co-design process. While the co-design framework was consistent across courses, minor refinements were made following the pilot implementation of Course 1. The co-design component was undertaken using a qualitative case study methodology, appropriate for in-depth examination of a complex, context-dependent intervention within a real-world setting. Case study methods enable holistic exploration of processes, actors, decisions, and contextual influences over time, particularly where the boundaries between the phenomenon and its context are not clearly delineated [29, 30]. In this study, the Graduate Certificate in Health Service Innovation constituted a single, instrumental case, with the co-design process as the unit of analysis. Multiple sources of evidence informed the case study, including outputs from iterative co-design workshops, curriculum artefacts, stakeholder feedback, and refinement cycles, allowing for triangulation and analytic depth consistent with rigorous case study inquiry, as well learning assessment aligned to the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) Level 8 requirements [31].

Situated Learning Theory [32] supports this co-design study through an emphasis of learning as a social process embedded in authentic real-world contexts. The study design deliberately involves healthcare professionals, academics and health sector stakeholders in curriculum development which reflects the theory’s focus on communities of practice. Participants co-construct knowledge through shared problem-solving and reflection, aligning courses and actual workplace needs. This context-rich collaborative approach enhances relevance and encourages deeper engagement.

The co-design process was conducted over multiple phases and involved diverse stakeholder groups, including health industry partners, alumni, healthcare professionals, domain experts and academic staff (Table 1). Stakeholders were specifically selected based on their experience in health service delivery, education, or policy to ensure that the design process was grounded in real-world expertise and system needs.

Stakeholders involved in the co-design process

Using a multi-phase design, the research captured lived experiences of healthcare staff and identified critical touch points in professional development. Stakeholders were engaged in iterative co-design workshops to develop and refine educational strategies. Workshops were structured as communities-of-practice style dialogues in which stakeholders co-constructed curriculum elements grounded in workplace realities (e.g. authentic artefacts, implementation planning) aligning with Situated Learning Theory’s emphasis on authentic participation and social learning [32]. The approach was underpinned by collaborative partnership principles, with academic and industry stakeholders contributing to all stages of the design, implementation, and evaluation. Co-design workshops served as the primary mechanism for collaborative development. These sessions were structured using the EBCD framework, incorporating both user experience capture and participatory design cycles. Participants contributed to identifying priority challenges in workforce innovation development, proposed design elements, and engaged in iterative feedback and refinement of proposed solutions.

To evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the co-design process, a mixed-methods approach was chosen to provide a comprehensive understanding of stakeholder perceptions and process outcomes. Qualitative data included detailed notes, transcripts from workshop discussions, and open-ended survey responses, which were analysed thematically to identify key patterns and insights. Quantitative feedback was collected through structured surveys administered after each co-design phase, focusing on perceived relevance, clarity and feasibility of the outputs. This mixed methods evaluation enabled triangulation of outcomes and supported a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility, acceptability, and stakeholder perceptions of the co-design process.

While the broader program evaluation uses a hybrid type II implementation-effectiveness design, this paper reports only the co-design process and early implementation outcomes related to feasibility, acceptability, and curriculum alignment of the GCHSI. Effectiveness outcomes related to learner capability or service impact are outside the scope of this case study and will be reported separately.

Study setting and population

This study was conducted in partnership with one of the largest group public HHS organisations in the Southern Hemisphere. This HHS encompasses a comprehensive range of health services, including quaternary and tertiary hospitals, community-based care, and aged care facilities. Governed by a statutory board of directors, the organisation is recognised as a leader in delivering world-class healthcare and advancing health innovation through research, emerging technologies, and strategic partnerships.

The academic team involved in this case study project included experts in health services management and health economics, bringing a wealth of knowledge in integrating clinical and business decision-making. Their research priorities aligned with the objectives of this initiative, focusing on knowledge translation and health services innovation.

The target population for this co-design GCHSI included health professionals from the partnering HHS as well as from other hospitals and health services within the state. These individuals were invited to contribute to the newly co-designed Graduate Certificate in Health Services Innovation, developed to enhance workforce capabilities and drive transformation within the healthcare sector.

Participants and recruitment

Stakeholders were purposively invited based on role relevance to curriculum design, implementation, or end-user experience (clinical, managerial, executive, alumni, academic, curriculum design). Inclusion criteria were: (i) experience in health service delivery/management or workforce education; (ii) capacity to provide feedback on curriculum and assessment design; and (iii) consent to participate.

Sample size was pragmatic, determined by role coverage and saturation of actionable feedback across stages. Workshops used facilitated round-robin input, anonymous survey options, and explicit invitation of dissenting views to reduce hierarchy effects. Decisions were documented with rationale and fed back to stakeholders to validate interpretations.

The co-design process

A four-stage framework for the co-design of each of the courses was developed by the authors as the co-design framework applied in this case study. (Fig. 1) Each stage required completion before the next stage was able to commence.

Co-design framework

This involved gaining a clear understanding of customer expectations regarding engagement with the end-user group and collaboratively developing a process to identify critical touchpoints. To align with academic and structural requirements, university course profile templates were reviewed by the academic team to determine the information necessary for course development. This review also identified elements of the course profile that could benefit from stakeholder input through co-design activities.

The aim of this co-design framework was to ensure representation from diverse stakeholder groups, with clearly defined terms of reference, incorporate varied modes of communication to facilitate engagement, and offer multiple options for stakeholder participation to accommodate differing needs and availability (Table 2).

An interactive webpage (using Microsoft Sway) was developed for each course in the graduate certificate, providing comprehensive information on the course introduction, format, aims, Learning Outcomes (LO), teaching and learning activities, and assessment suite. To facilitate feedback collection, an anonymous online survey was embedded within the webpage. Additionally, to accommodate diverse preferences for providing feedback—such as the use of track changes—this information was made available in an editable Word document.

Feedback surveys were integrated into the aforementioned webpage to systematically gather insights on the course and co-design process. The survey assessed perceived relevance of learning outcomes, alignment of assessments, clarity of weekly structure, overall satisfaction, and open-ended improvement suggestions. The items were adapted from the Quality Indicators for Learning and Teaching (QILT), [33, 34] and university evaluation banks (see Supplementary S1 for full instrument). This review was conducted in consultation with learning and teaching curriculum experts, ensuring the creation of a tailored suite of feedback questions designed to elicit comprehensive responses.

A focus group for the health professional stakeholders was co facilitated by the course convenor and the HHS Business area stakeholder. The focus group was 90 min, allowing the course convenor to present the course profile information, gather feedback progressively, and provide time for further questions and discussion. Those who attended the focus group were provided with the option to complete the embedded online survey synchronously in the focus group, or complete following the focus group. Focus group sessions were recorded using Microsoft Teams with the transcript option. This provided another participation modality for all stakeholders, allowing them to listen to the recorded focus group and provide their feedback asynchronously.

Data analysis

Survey data were exported into a spreadsheet and analysed using descriptive statistics (frequencies and proportions) to characterise feasibility, acceptability and perceived alignment across co-design stages and courses. The study was not powered for inferential comparisons across stakeholder groups or courses. Qualitative data from focus groups, open-ended survey responses and written feedback were managed in NVivo©.

Qualitative data from focus groups, open-ended survey responses and written feedback were analysed using Framework Analysis as described by Ritchie and Spencer (1994), to support structured data management across predefined evaluation domains relevant to co-design feasibility, acceptability and alignment. Data were organised into matrices to enable systematic comparison across stakeholder groups and co-design stages [36].

In parallel, qualitative data familiarisation and interpretation were supported using Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase approach to thematic analysis, enabling systematic identification, coding, and interpretation of recurring patterns in participants’ narratives [37]. Initial observations were documented during data familiarisation to support analytic reflexivity.

Two researchers reviewed coding and resolved discrepancies through discussion to refine the codebook and confirm themes. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout the coding process, and deviant or negative cases (e.g. comments indicating misalignment or insufficient detail) were actively sought and used to refine themes.

This combined analytic approach enabled both structured evaluation of predefined co-design outcomes and inductive exploration of stakeholder experience.

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Griffith University (GU Ref No: 2023/566) and the Townsville Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2023/QTHS/100088), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Ethical conduct followed the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2023) [38] and relevant governance requirements under the National Health and Medical Research Council Act 1992 [39]. Ethical challenges in participation of curriculum and GCHSI program design were appropriately supported by agreements between the stakeholders and through program governance structures that dealt with issues such as intellectual property, data ownership and participant consent.

Results

Feasibility and acceptability of engagement modalities

Outcomes indicate that a structured, multi-stage EBCD-informed co-design process was feasible and acceptable for developing a GCHSI that was academically rigorous and professionally relevant. Feedback on the four stages of the co-design process is presented in Table 4. These outcomes reflect the evaluation indicators defined in the Methods section: participation levels across co-design stages (feasibility), stakeholder satisfaction and continued engagement (acceptability), and feedback relating to learning outcome alignment, assessment authenticity, and documented curriculum refinements (alignment). Following the co-design process for the first course, it was clear that there were further changes required to the original co- design framework to better capture stakeholder feedback.

The wide range of professional experience and stakeholder roles represented across the co-design stages contributed to the breadth of perspectives captured during the process. However, participation was voluntary and the outcomes may therefore reflect some degree of self-selection bias, as individuals more interested in curriculum development may have been more likely to participate.

While informal email feedback, and clarification with focus groups participants for course 1 indicated that Microsoft Sway and Forms were user-friendly and timely feedback mechanisms for both synchronous and asynchronous feedback, survey outcomes indicated participants did not feel that there was adequate detail presented in the web page summary for them to provide informed feedback:

Curriculum and assessment refinements from stakeholder touchpoints

The difficulty in achieving the ‘right balance’ while working within the policy requirements for university course profiles was also evidenced by the need to schedule a second focus group in stage 2 co-design for course 1, as participants required more information in order to provide support for course profile submission to the university.

Similar themes emerged from the focus group discussions for course 1 where end-users wanted to know if ‘specific topics/activities’ were going to be included. All of those topics/activities flagged by end-users were mapped for inclusion in the draft course profile for course 1, but the detail provided in the co-design materials did not reveal this.

Other emerging themes from the co-design pilot was that there was no value in separating stakeholder groups by having tailored webpages, and participants felt that one feedback survey at the end of the webpage would remove the duplicative nature of the feedback questions.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the practical value and feasibility of applying a structured, multi-stage EBCD co-design process to the development of a postgraduate Graduate Certificate in Health Service Innovation. By engaging health service stakeholders, end-users, and academic staff across iterative co-design stages, the process supported the development of curriculum and assessment structures that stakeholders perceived as relevant, authentic, and aligned with both service priorities and academic requirements.

Importantly, this work does not seek to advance new theory regarding workforce innovation or implementation, nor to evaluate downstream workforce or service outcomes. Rather, it provides an empirically grounded illustration of how co-design principles can be operationalised within a real-world higher education-health system partnership. In doing so, it offers practical insight into the mechanisms through which alignment between academic design standards and health system expectations can be negotiated and refined in practice. Reporting was informed by qualitative and mixed-methods reporting guidance including Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) and Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) [40, 41] principles to support transparency in sampling, data generation, and analytic processes.

One of the most salient contributions of this case study lies in demonstrating how EBCD can function as an enabling infrastructure for educational design. The co-design process created structured opportunities for surfacing and reconciling differences in language, priorities, and assumptions between academic and service stakeholders. These negotiations were particularly evident in the development of authentic assessment tasks, where service-derived artefacts and implementation planning tools were incorporated while maintaining alignment with AQF Level 8 requirements. This finding reinforces existing scholarship on authentic assessment and situated learning [39], while grounding these principles in an applied, sector-facing context.

The iterative refinement of the co-design framework following the pilot course further highlights the adaptive nature of participatory educational design [42]. Feedback related to information sufficiency, engagement modalities, and survey burden led to concrete adjustments in process and materials, highlighting the importance of reflexivity and responsiveness when applying co-design in complex institutional environments. These insights are especially relevant for universities and health services seeking to collaborate on workforce education under constraints of time, workload, and governance requirements.

The diverse modes of engagement offered through the co-design process, including asynchronous surveys, synchronous focus groups, editable documents, and recorded sessions, enabled inclusive participation and accommodated different stakeholder needs and availability. This approach resonates with the tenets of inclusive curriculum design and universal design for learning [43] and is reflective of EBCD’s core focus on capturing varied stakeholder experiences.

Despite the overall success of the co-design process, several challenges were noted. The university’s course profile requirements, including limitations on word count and format, restricted the extent to which stakeholder suggestions could be integrated directly into formal course documentation. Additionally, early separation of stakeholder feedback mechanisms created duplication and confusion, which was resolved in later stages by consolidating engagement via a single, comprehensive feedback pathway.

The co-design process also highlighted the iterative nature of achieving alignment between stakeholder expectations, academic standards, and institutional processes. These tensions are well-documented in the literature on participatory curriculum development, where the need to accommodate both rigour and responsiveness can create friction [18, 24]. However, by embedding mechanisms for feedback, refinement, and communication closure, this project demonstrated how co-design can be operationalised as a collaborative and transformative educational practice.

Overall, this study positions co-design not merely as a curriculum development tool, but as a structured approach for supporting trust-building, stakeholder relevance, and alignment in postgraduate health education. The resulting framework offers a replicable model for other health education post graduate programs seeking to balance institutional demands with meaningful industry collaboration.

The experience from this co-design process resulted in a viable GCHSI program of learning in health service innovation and demonstrated that the methods and processes used may be adaptable to other contexts. However, transferability should be tested in settings with different workforce profiles, resource constraints, and governance arrangements.

Limitations and future research

This study was conducted within a large HHS partnership and a single university context, which may limit transferability to other settings (e.g. primary care, private providers, or rural services). However, the partnership encompassed multiple clinical and managerial roles across diverse service contexts, providing exposure to a broad range of system perspectives. Stakeholder participation was voluntary and may reflect some degree of self-selection or positive response bias. This was partially mitigated through the inclusion of multiple stakeholder groups and the use of structured EBCD activities that prioritised shared reflection over individual evaluation. Feedback may also have been influenced by social desirability and existing organisational relationships, although data were collected across multiple points and roles to reduce reliance on single perspectives.

As GCHSI program designers evaluating the co-design process, the authors acknowledge potential confirmation bias. Triangulation across multiple data sources and the inclusion of anonymous feedback mechanisms were used to strengthen the credibility of the outcomes. Finally, the evaluation focused on feasibility, acceptability, and curriculum alignment rather than learner performance or downstream workforce and service outcomes. This reflects the study’s implementation-focused aims and positions future longitudinal research to examine learning transfer, workforce capability, and system-level outcomes.

Future research could test adaptations of this co-design approach for more practically oriented clinical training and continuing professional development contexts. As well as examining mechanisms for optimizing feedback loops under time and workload constraints, and evaluating learner-level outcomes (capability, behaviour change) and service-level outcomes (implementation, quality, safety) overtime.

Conclusion

Case study outcomes demonstrate the feasibility and acceptability of using a structured EBCD-informed co-design process to develop a postgraduate health workforce program aligned with academic standards and service context needs. Active involvement of healthcare professionals, academic staff and health system partners across all co-design stages supported development of an inclusive curriculum and applied assessment approach. Continuous feedback loops enabled iterative refinement of curriculum design and learning activities, strengthening alignment with both service and academic requirements.

This case study contributes to the emerging evidence supporting participatory curriculum development in health management and workforce education. It provides an operationalised, multi-stage co-design model applied to development of a four-course Graduate Certificate in Health Services Innovation, demonstrating feasibility and stakeholder acceptability across synchronous and asynchronous feedback modalities. These outcomes provide practical design considerations for aligning authentic assessment, curriculum structure and service priorities within regulated higher education environments and support structured partnership-based co-design approaches for workforce education in complex health system contexts.
