Estimating asian elephant abundance: a comparative analysis of dung counts and genetic SECR in a known population of Kodagu, Karnataka, India
Authors: Vishnupriya Kolipakam, Himanshu Matta, Sanath K Muliya, Mayur V Markad, Lallianpuii Kawlni, Ujjwal Kumar, Bhawana Pant, Souritra Sharma, Samrat Mondol, Ramesh K Pandey, Qamar Qureshi

ABSTRACT

Here, we compared conventional line-transect dung counts with a non-invasive genetic spatially explicit capture-recapture (genetic SECR) approach in Kodagu, Karnataka, using high-throughput microsatellite sequencing (SSR-Seq). Background

The Asian elephant (Elephas maximus), an ecological engineer and cultural icon, plays a pivotal role in maintaining forest structure and seed dispersal across South and Southeast Asia [3, 8]. Despite legal protection, elephant populations face escalating threats from habitat loss, fragmentation, and human–elephant conflict [16, 26]. India alone harbors over 50% of the global Asian elephant population (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change [MoEFCC], [32], making effective monitoring within its borders critical for the species’ long-term survival. Accurate population estimates are fundamental for guiding conservation policy, yet obtaining reliable numbers for this wide-ranging, cryptic megafauna remains challenging.

In addition to informing short-term conservation interventions, accurate population estimates also underpin our understanding of evolutionary processes in fragmented landscapes. As elephant populations become increasingly isolated [15, 40], demographic monitoring must account not only for abundance but also for the genetic and spatial structure that can affect evolutionary potential [18]. Conventional methods provide limited insight into gene flow, inbreeding, or substructure. Non-invasive genetic tools, particularly when integrated with spatial capture–recapture (SECR) frameworks, can address these limitations by simultaneously estimating abundance and identifying individuals—thereby enabling inferences about effective population size, mating systems, and genetic connectivity. This dual capacity to generate ecological and evolutionary information makes genetic SECR a uniquely powerful tool for monitoring long-lived species in human-dominated habitats.

Traditional survey methods for elephants each have significant limitations. Aerial and direct ground counts are feasible in open habitats but fail in dense tropical forests where elephant detectability can fall below 20% [27]. Line-transect dung surveys have therefore become a standard indirect method in forested regions [6, 39]. In this approach, dung density is estimated and converted to elephant numbers using assumed defecation and decay rates [1]. While cost-effective and applicable over large areas, dung counts are prone to substantial biases. Dung decay and defecation rates vary spatially and seasonally, and using non-local or averaged values can introduce >50% error in population estimates [2, 10]. Dung-based counts in tropical forests often overestimate true abundance when local decay rates are slower than assumed (leading to dung accumulation) or when defecation rates are higher than assumed [2, 22, 39]. These limitations highlight a critical gap: the lack of a standardized, spatially explicit framework that accounts for imperfect detection and habitat heterogeneity while identifying individuals.

Non-invasive genetic sampling has emerged as a powerful alternative for monitoring elusive wildlife. By genotyping DNA from fecal or other trace samples, individual elephants can be “captured” via their unique genetic profiles [10]. When combined with Spatially Explicit Capture–Recapture (SECR) models, genetic tagging explicitly incorporates detection probability and animal movement, addressing key shortcomings of traditional indices [14]. This approach does not rely on ancillary decay or defecation parameters,instead, population size is inferred directly from detection/re-detection of individual genotypes in space [14]. SECR models thereby account for individuals’ home range use and varying detection within the survey area, yielding density estimates that are less biased by environmental factors [14]. However, applying genetic SECR at large scales has been constrained by logistical challenges, especially in biodiverse regions like Asia. Genotyping many samples is expensive and labor-intensive, and high genetic diversity in Asian elephant populations complicates multilocus matching [43]. Fortunately, recent methodological advances such as high-throughput microsatellite sequencing (SSR-Seq) have started to ease these constraints [34], [45]. These techniques enable rapid genotyping of dozens of loci from hundreds of fecal samples in parallel, increasing efficiency and accuracy of genetic capture–recapture studies.

In this study, we bridge two critical gaps in Asian elephant population ecology: (1) the lack of empirical comparisons between dung-count and genetic SECR methods under field conditions, and (2) the need for a scalable framework that balances accuracy and field practicality in complex, human-modified landscapes. We conducted a comparative evaluation in Kodagu district, Karnataka, India – a fragmented forest landscape with a known population of 34 elephants. We hypothesized that genetic SECR would provide a population estimate with significantly lower bias and uncertainty than dung counts, while also yielding valuable information on genetic diversity and population structure. To test this, we carried out simultaneous line transect dung surveys and non-invasive genetic sampling in the study area. This work represents the first rigorous validation of genetic SECR against a known wild elephant population in Asia, answering calls by the IUCN Asian Elephant Specialist Group for standardized, reliable monitoring approaches [42]. By integrating spatial capture–recapture theory with molecular ecology, we demonstrate a replicable framework for minimizing the biases of traditional methods. We further discuss how this approach can be integrated into national monitoring programs and conservation strategies. Our findings underscore the importance of adopting evidence-based, innovative methods to safeguard Asia’s elephants amid accelerating environmental change [25, 41].

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Kodagu District, Karnataka, India (approx. 12°14′N, 76°03′E), a region within the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot. The landscape is a mosaic of tropical evergreen, semi-evergreen, and moist deciduous forests interspersed with coffee plantations and human settlements [37]. Our focal site was a 5-km radius around the Mathigodu Elephant Camp (12°14′19.6″N, 76°03′09.0″E), a key site for elephant management and conflict mitigation (Fig. 1). The camp houses 19 semi-captive elephants, of which 12 are regularly allowed to forage in the surrounding forest. In addition, two wild herds—referred to locally as “Ananya” and “NE4”—also use this area. The matriarchs of both herds are radio-collared, and their GPS tracking data were used to delineate home ranges (Fig. 1). Based on long-term Karnataka Forest Department records, which include direct counts of semi-captive elephants and GPS-collar monitoring of two matriarchs from resident wild herds, the population in this landscape is known to be 34 individuals.

Home ranges were estimated using 100% Minimum Convex Polygons (MCPs) based on GPS fixes pooled across years and seasons, calculated using the R package adehabitatHR [7]. The two herds had home ranges of 116.68 km2 (Ananya) and 116.87 km2 (NE4), with approximately 87.84% spatial overlap. Combined with a 5-km buffer around the Mathigodu camp, the total study area encompassed 165.63 km2, including shared use areas by wild and semi-captive elephants. Based on long-term field observations and forest department records, the known elephant population in this landscape is 34 individuals (Fig. 1).

Line transect dung count surveys

We conducted line transect dung surveys between June and July 2021 following established distance sampling protocols [6]. Nineteen transects (1.8–2.2 km,mean 2.0 km) were laid along existing forest paths and elephant trails for logistical feasibility. While this facilitated access, it may have biased counts upward by focusing on areas of regular elephant use. Transects were distributed across major habitat types (moist deciduous ~45%, semi-evergreen ~25%, evergreen ~10%, and plantations/settlements ~20%) in approximate proportion to their availability. Each transect was surveyed once by trained field teams who recorded all visible elephant dung piles along the transect and measured their perpendicular distances from the transect line using laser rangefinders. Observers were trained to maintain consistent detection effort and minimize bias in distance estimation and dung detection.

Data were analyzed using Distance 7.3 software [38], with a half-normal detection function and right truncation at 20 meters to account for declining detectability in dense vegetation. We truncated observations at 20 m, based on pilot surveys indicating that few dung piles were visible beyond this distance in dense undergrowth. This threshold is consistent with previous Indian elephant dung surveys [4, 30]. The effective strip width and detection probability were estimated from perpendicular distance data. Dung density (piles/km2) was calculated from encounter rates, detection probability, and effective strip width.

To estimate elephant density, we applied conversion factors based on published defecation and decay rates for elephants in southern India. Genetic sampling and genotyping

During the same survey period, fresh dung samples were opportunistically collected along the transects for genetic analysis. For each sample, approximately 10 g of the outer dung layer was collected using sterile gloves and spatulas to minimize contamination. Samples were stored in zip-lock bags with silica gel and transported to the Wildlife Institute of India for laboratory analysis.

DNA extraction was carried out using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were quantified, and samples with sufficient yield were selected for genotyping. We used 10 polymorphic microsatellite loci—LAT8, LAT13, EMU02, EMU03, EMU11, EMU12, EMX1, EMX2, EMX3, and EMX5—previously validated in elephants [9, 29]. PCR was conducted in 9.6 µL reactions using Qiagen Multiplex PCR kits. Thermocycling conditions followed established protocols, with locus-specific annealing temperatures and appropriate positive and negative controls.

Amplified products were visualized on 2% agarose gels. Libraries for sequencing were prepared using the QIAseq FX DNA Library Preparation Kit (QIAGEN). Adapter ligation, bead cleanup, and limited-cycle PCR enrichment followed manufacturer protocols. Final libraries were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and quality-checked using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Sequencing reads were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq [24], and paired-end reads were merged using PEAR [46]. Locus-level demultiplexing and length filtering were performed with SeqKit [36]. Consensus allele sizes were inferred using custom scripts, and alleles were binned using TANDEM [31]. Genetic diversity metrics (allele richness, observed and expected heterozygosity, PIC, PID, and PIDsib) were calculated using standard population genetics software, including CERVUS [28]. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium deviations were assessed at each locus. We used the R package allelematch [19] to cluster genotypes and identify unique individuals. To account for genotyping artefacts, we required ≥90% read agreement within each locus for allele calls. Genotypes with <70% locus coverage were treated as incomplete and flagged as unreliable. For individual matching, we applied a conservative 9/10 locus identity rule, i.e., two profiles differing at only one locus were considered the same individual. This follows best practice for NGS-based SSR-Seq genotyping, where each allele is read hundreds of times and error rates are substantially lower than in conventional fragment analysis [19, 34]. Allowing one mismatch across 10 loci ensures robustness by minimizing false splits while accommodating occasional dropout. Individuals were considered the same if they shared alleles at ≥9 of 10 loci. This conservative matching threshold minimized false recaptures due to genotyping error.

Spatial capture–recapture modeling

To estimate elephant density from genetic data, we used spatially explicit capture–recapture (SECR) modeling with area search polygon detectors, implemented in the R package secr [12, 14]. Dung samples were collected opportunistically along the 19 transects. For SECR analysis, the study area was divided into 4 km2 cells, which served as a conceptual detector array,each dung sample was assigned to the cell in which it was collected. A spatial capture matrix was constructed by linking individual elephant genotypes with the GPS-tagged locations of the dung samples. The state space was defined using a habitat mask with a 16 km buffer around the polygon array, as recommended via the suggest.buffer argument in secr. Non-suitable habitats, such as settlements and open lands, were excluded from the habitat mask to ensure only detections within habitable areas were included. The habitat mask excluded only human settlements and open agricultural fields. Coffee plantations, which elephants regularly use as corridors, were retained as suitable habitat. This step was taken to reduce edge effects and enhance model precision.

We used the hazard-half-normal detection function [33], which models cumulative detection probability for area search detectors. Its two key parameters are λ₀, the baseline encounter rate at the activity center, and σ, the spatial scale of detection (reflecting home range size). These govern the shape of the detection curve [11].

Bias estimation using Bayesian inference

To evaluate bias in population density estimates derived from genetic mark–recapture and line transect sampling, we used a Bayesian hypothesis testing approach based on Bayes factors, alongside distributional visualisation. For each method, we simulated 100 replicate estimates based on empirical means and standard deviations reported from field surveys for genetic mark–recapture, and for dung decay based estimation. Simulations assumed normally distributed estimation error based on the central limit theorem and empirical standard deviations from field data. While this simplifies real-world variance structure, it provides a practical approximation for comparing estimator bias under observed uncertainty levels. Simulated data were combined into a single dataset and analysed using ‘dplyr’ and ‘ggplot2’’ in R. We used Bayes factors (BF₁₀) to evaluate evidence for deviation from the known population value of 0.21 elephants/km2. Low BF₁₀ values support the null hypothesis (no bias), whereas values >10 indicate strong evidence against it.

We summarised the estimates by computing the sample mean and empirical 95% credible intervals (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles) for each method. A plot was generated to visualise the full distribution of simulated estimates. Point estimates and error bars were overlaid to represent the mean and 95% intervals, respectively with a comparison to the true mean. This graphical approach allowed direct visual comparison of both bias and uncertainty across estimation methods.

Results

Dung count estimates

From 19 transects (40.85 km total length), we recorded 174 dung piles. The fitted detection function yielded an effective strip width of ~5 m and an estimated dung density of 2138 piles/km2. Applying the defecation and decay parameters (16.33 piles/day and 0.0097/day, respectively), we derived an elephant density of 1.27 individuals/km2. This translates to ~127 elephants (95% CI: 64–190) in the 165.63 km2 area, overestimating by a significantly large margin from the known population.

Genetic capture–recapture estimates

Out of 144 dung samples, 124 were successfully genotyped. Thirty-six unique genotypes were initially identified; however, two of these profiles had ~45% missing data (amplified at only 5–6 loci), and one additional profile was a singleton detection. Recapture rates varied among individuals (Supplementary Table S1). Of 33–34 elephants identified, 30 were detected on two or more occasions, and several were observed across multiple transects, indicating strong spatial mixing of herds. Three individuals were singletons, one of which was a low-confidence genotype with high missing data. If these low-confidence cases are excluded, the dataset yields 33–34 unique individuals, which closely matches the known population of 34 elephants. Allelic richness (mean = 11.2 alleles/locus) reflects high genetic diversity. Expected heterozygosity (0.69) was higher than observed (0.35), suggesting possible substructure or inbreeding. The probability of identity (PID = 3.8×10⁻11) and sibling PID (2.2×10⁻4) confirm the high discriminating power of the marker panel (Table 1).

Bias estimation using Bayesian inference

Bayesian one-sample t-tests yielded divergent support for the hypothesis that the two estimation methods reflect the true population density of 0.21 elephants/km2. For the genetic mark–recapture method, the estimated mean density of 0.23 elephants/km2 y yielded a Bayes factor (BF₁₀) of 0.317, when compared with the true mean (0.21 elephants/km2) indicated that the data were more likely under the null hypothesis than under the alternative, providing moderate evidence that the estimates were not biased relative to the true population. In contrast, the line transect method returned a Bayes factor of 940,579, representing decisive evidence against the null hypothesis. This suggests that estimates from the line transect approach were substantially biased upward relative to the true density. These findings were consistent with the visualisation of simulated estimates (Fig. 2). Mark–recapture estimates were distributed around the true value, while line transect estimates were more dispersed and markedly higher. The true density fell within the credible interval of the mark–recapture estimates but well outside the interval for the line transect method.

Discussion

Our results clearly demonstrate that genetic SECR offers superior accuracy in estimating Asian elephant abundance compared to traditional dung-based surveys. The dung count method overestimated the known population largely due to uncertainty in decay and defecation rates—a limitation widely reported in prior studies [2, 39]. By contrast, the genetic SECR model produced an estimate within the 95% confidence interval of the actual number of elephants in the area, with confidence intervals encompassing the known population size. This level of accuracy, achieved without reliance on habitat- and climate-sensitive decay rates, underscores the robustness of genetic SECR as a monitoring tool.

Because transects followed existing trails, dung counts may be inflated compared to random or straight-line transects that also sample low-use areas. This limitation is common in forest surveys and reflects the trade-off between logistical feasibility and methodological rigor [27]. The dung-based estimate (127, 95% CI: 64–190) was highly imprecise, limiting its ability to detect population changes. In contrast, the genetic SECR estimate (39 ± 8,95% CI: 34–47) tightly encompassed the true population, highlighting the greater precision of genetic approaches. Our findings are consistent with earlier work on forest elephants in Ghana [10] and Cambodia [23], where DNA-based methods similarly outperformed traditional estimation techniques.

The apparent discrepancy between the known population size (34) and the 36 genotypes identified was due to two samples with ~45% missing data and one singleton profile. When considered conservatively, the genetic dataset corresponds to 33–34 unique individuals, in agreement with the known number. Such discrepancies are common in non-invasive genotyping studies and reflect the balance between strict and relaxed thresholds for identity matching [19, 43]. With NGS-based SSR-Seq, where each allele is read at high depth, the accepted practice is to allow one mismatch across 10–15 loci [34]. Our use of a 9/10 matching threshold is therefore consistent with current standards and provides a robust safeguard against both over-splitting and over-merging individuals.

The low PID and PID-sib values confirmed the efficacy of the microsatellite panel for individual identification—a prerequisite for reliable capture–recapture analysis. Beyond abundance estimation, genetic SECR also yielded valuable insights into population structure and genetic diversity. High allelic richness and expected heterozygosity suggest that the Kodagu elephant population retains genetic diversity. However, the consistently lower observed heterozygosity and deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at multiple loci may signal fine-scale substructure, recent isolation, or inbreeding—warranting caution in interpreting adaptive potential. These findings align with earlier studies from Peninsular Malaysia, where spatial genetic differentiation was detected even among neighboring elephant herds, reinforcing the importance of long-term, landscape-level genetic monitoring [17]. Deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at select loci, and low observed heterozygosity, are possibly due to Walhund effect, or inbreeding, in our sample, and highlight the importance of continued monitoring of genetic health [10, 44]. From an evolutionary standpoint, the observed genetic patterns may reflect restricted gene flow between semi-captive and wild herds, potentially signalling incipient substructure within the population. Deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at two loci and reduced observed heterozygosity could indicate either inbreeding or non-random mating among partially isolated groups. Such structure, even at fine spatial scales, has evolutionary consequences, including increased drift and reduced adaptive potential. Our findings echo those from Peninsular Malaysia, where semi-isolated elephant herds showed significant genetic differentiation [17]. While our sample size limits robust inference, the potential emergence of substructure within a relatively small landscape highlights the need for long-term genetic monitoring. Further, the retention of high allelic richness suggests that, despite anthropogenic fragmentation, this population has not yet suffered irreversible loss of diversity—a key aspect of its evolutionary resilience.

Our findings are consistent with earlier work on forest elephants in Ghana [10] and Cambodia [21], where DNA-based methods similarly outperformed traditional population estimation techniques. While genetic methods demonstrate superior accuracy, we acknowledge they require greater per-unit investment compared to dung counts. As technological advances continue to evolve, costs of fecal DNA analyses will likely decrease. The use of fecal DNA has already benefited from more affordable sequencing technologies, improved reference databases, automation, and enhanced genotyping workflow tools (e.g., automated DNA sequencers and improved genotyping assays) [20]. Collaborative facilities centralized at regional levels could enable countries to process substantial sample quantities cost-effectively, making genetic monitoring increasingly feasible. India's Synchronous Elephant Population Estimation (AISE) initiative by the Ministry of Environment, Forests and Climate Change, which incorporates genetic SECR alongside other assessment methods, exemplifies this integration. The results, currently pending, should further validate our findings while enhancing understanding of elephant distribution and habitat use patterns across diverse landscapes.

The remarkable improvement in accuracy and demographic data extraction from the same samples makes genetic SECR a powerful and cost-effective strategy, particularly when applied periodically to calibrate broader monitoring programs. Such hybrid frameworks—using genetic SECR for baseline estimation and dung counts or other rapid assessment tools for interim updates—may optimize resource allocation and efficiency [20]. Nevertheless, genetic SECR encounters logistical and financial constraints. DNA extraction from dung in tropical environments remains challenged by rapid degradation [35]. In our study, 14% of samples failed genotyping. Success rates could potentially improve through enhanced field protocols and adoption of newer technologies [5]. Importantly, our study assumed demographic closure, justified by brief sampling periods and known range stability from telemetry data. However, larger-scale or longer-term studies should account for potential violations of this assumption [13]. Incorporating a habitat mask in SECR modeling was essential for avoiding spatial bias. Our approach, which excluded non-habitat features like plantations and settlements, better reflected true elephant spatial patterns and reduced edge effects. This methodological decision aligns with best practices in spatial modeling [14] and illustrates the value of ecological realism in survey design.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study validates genetic SECR as a reliable method for estimating elephant abundance and assessing population genetics in forested landscapes. It addresses limitations inherent to dung count surveys, provides higher precision and additional biological insights, and enhances the scientific foundation for conservation planning. We recommend that genetic SECR be integrated into periodic monitoring for Asian elephants, with the potential to extend its application to other cryptic megafauna facing similar conservation challenges. Continued investment in molecular infrastructure and analytical capacity will be critical for scaling up this approach across elephant ranges in India and beyond.
