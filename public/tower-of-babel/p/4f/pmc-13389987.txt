Vernakalant: Wish you were here
Authors: Judith A. Mackall, Ohad Ziv
Published: 2026

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the primary diagnosis in approximately 600,000 emergency room visits per year in the United States.1 Approximately half of these visits result in hospital admission, significantly affecting both health care cost and utilization as well as patient care.2 Although controlling heart rate and starting anticoagulation as an initial step may be adequate, many patients are either difficult to rate control or remain symptomatic, necessitating hospital admission. Electrical cardioversion is an effective way to restore sinus rhythm but is often not the initial strategy in the emergency room. Other than the most experienced patients with AF, those who present to the emergency room usually have eaten that day, resulting in a delay for sedation. Furthermore, the requisite anesthesia and postsedation monitoring can be a significant drain on emergency room resources. For these reasons, a safe and effective means of pharmacologic conversion is ideal.

The pharmacologic agents recommended for cardioversion differ between the United States and Europe. The 2023 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/American College of Clinical Pharmacy/Heart Rhythm Society guidelines recommend intravenous (IV) ibutilide (left ventricular ejection fraction >40%), which is more effective at terminating atrial flutter than fibrillation and requires monitoring for at least 4 hours, or IV amiodarone, which is safer in heart failure and provides additional rate control, but conversion occurs between 8–24 hours.3 The European Heart Rhythm Association recently issued a clinical consensus statement on the use of antiarrhythmic medications.4 They recommend IV vernakalant or IV flecainide or propafenone in patients with structurally normal hearts and IV vernakalant or IV amiodarone for patients with heart disease, withholding vernakalant in the presence of severe aortic stenosis, moderate-severe heart failure, or recent acute coronary syndrome. Vernakalant is currently approved for use in Europe, Canada, Israel, and parts of Asia. It is not approved for use in the United States.

Vernakalant is an IV-administered, rapidly acting antiarrhythmic whose main action is to block the ultra-rapid delayed rectifier potassium channel (Kv1.5). Because of the relative absence of this current in the ventricle, the electrophysiological effects of the drug are seen primarily in the atrium. Conversion rates in placebo-controlled trials have varied (37%–71%) but have consistently been 4-fold the rate of placebo conversion. Ibutilide has an equivalent rate of conversion but is haunted by a 4% risk of torsade de pointes, making its wide use in the emergency department setting less than ideal. By comparison, vernakalant has little effect on ventricular repolarization and therefore poses a minimal risk of ventricular arrhythmias.5

In this issue of Heart Rhythm O2, Heller et al6 present a single-center experience of 328 patients with new-onset AF treated with vernakalant in their emergency department between 2016 and 2024. Although a single-center experience, the large size and close follow-up of the cohort make this study an important addition to our understanding of the real-world use of vernakalant in emergency room treatment of AF. The authors found an overall high conversion rate at 82% with a low percentage of patients reporting mild side effects. It is important to note that conversion occurred within minutes, making the use of this drug in the emergency department highly appealing.

However, there was a fatality that occurred in a patient who received the drug with an unrecognized cardiomyopathy. This highlights an important safety caveat to the use of vernakalant. Animal studies demonstrated that vernakalant has acute effects on cardiac output and left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP).5 This effect may be related to the fact that vernakalant’s channel-blocking profile includes sodium current blockade.7 As a result, serious adverse events may occur if used in patients with acute decompensated heart failure or with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. The other previously reported fatality, clearly associated with vernakalant, occurred in a patient with elevated LVEDP from unrecognized severe aortic stenosis.8 In another published series of 236 patients treated with IV vernakalant for conversion of new-onset AF, a comparable success rate of 76% was reported along with some significant side effects of hypotension, bradycardia, and ventricular tachycardia noted in patients with heart failure symptoms.9 This highlights that the safe use of vernakalant depends on the exclusion of acute heart failure or elevated LVEDP.

We agree with Heller et al6 that 1 way to mitigate this risk is to use point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) to identify patients with structural heart disease. POCUS is now widely available in US emergency rooms and is used to diagnose acute congestive heart failure with a demonstrated sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 99%.10,11 POCUS could guide management by identifying patients contraindicated for vernakalant and in whom IV amiodarone or electrical cardioversion would be preferred. Even electrical cardioversion in the setting of acute congestive heart failure is not without risk, given that periprocedural complications have been reported in up to 8% of patients.12

AF is not a benign heart rhythm. Patients with AF have morbidity and mortality from heart failure, bleeding, and thromboembolic events. The patients in the current report were deemed to have been in AF for less than 48 hours. Decades ago, Weigner et al13 demonstrated that cardioversion of patients whose AF onset was clinically deemed less than 48 hours had a very low risk of embolic events. Subsequent data have shown that not all patient embolic risk within that 48-hour window is the same. Garg et al14 demonstrated that the embolic risk of cardioversion within the 48-hour window was correlated with the CHA2DS2-VASc score. A large dataset with more than 7000 patients from the Finnish Cardioversion study corroborated this finding and also found that, in patients with a high CHA2DS2-VASc score (≥5), the embolic risk at 1 month was 2% without anticoagulation.15 It is important to note that patients in the current report were discharged on anticoagulation, and no embolic events were seen. The CHA2DS2-VASc score for these patients was not reported, so caution should be exercised when considering cardioversion in high CHA2DS2-VASc score patients, regardless of mode of conversion.

Heller et al6 present compelling real-world data demonstrating the safety and efficacy of IV vernakalant for the pharmacologic cardioversion of AF in the emergency room. This approach may have the potential to significantly reduce health care cost related to AF hospital admissions and improve patient satisfaction. In 2019, a resubmission for Food and Drug Administration approval of the drug was denied based on continued concerns for safety. With the present report, data on more than 4000 patients treated with vernakalant have now been published. The efficacy and safety of the drug and who should not receive the drug seem evident.

Considering that antiarrhythmic drugs, in addition to catheter ablation and device implantation, play a very important role in the treatment of patients with heart rhythm disorders, it is mind boggling that the last new antiarrhythmic medication approved for use in the United States was in 2009. To have a drug with a high success rate for converting AF, without a significant risk of proarrhythmia, to use in most (but not all) patients would be a big win for patients with AF in the United States. Unfortunately, for now, when a patient who went into AF last night arrives in a US emergency room and recalls having coffee with cream that morning, all we can say is, “Vernakalant, wish you were here!”
