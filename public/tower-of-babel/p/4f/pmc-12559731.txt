Consensus clustering-based undersampling for improved classification of transient events in time-domain astronomy surveys
Authors: Tossapon Boongoen, Natthakan Iam-On
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAstronomical data analytics has rapidly expanded given the advancement of data handling techniques and computing system. The race to discover new events is subject to acquiring and digesting the high volume of data from sky surveys efficiently, yet accurately. The assumption is valid for many modern astronomy projects, with the issue of big data storage on the one hand, and effective data analysis on the other. This research deals with the latter by focusing on the classification of potential transient events initially detected in time-domain astronomical surveys. Most of these candidate transients represent false positives that are the results of fault in hardware, errors in data collection and/or data pre-processing. Hence, the ability to filter these out is much needed to avoid a laborious manual assessment down the line. The problem investigated here is that training data can be highly imbalanced. For the first attempt, the coupling of oversampling methods and several classifiers provides an improvement, but generally leads to overfitting. As a solution, this paper presents a novel application of consensus clustering to undersample majority-class instances instead. It not only helps to overcome the aforementioned drawback but also strengthen the recent approach that exploits a single clustering to guide the selection of representative samples.Subject terms: Computer science, Scientific data

Introduction

The discovery and study of transient events have been one of the most exciting research subjects in modern astronomy1. The ability to detect transient incidents (i.e., here defined as astronomical sources whose brightness change significantly between observations, or those that appear as “new” sources before eventually disappearing again) leads to the investigation of rare types of astronomical events2. For instance, these classes cover a merge of dense objects (e.g., between a blackhole and a neutron star), mega-flares on sequence stars, and a tidal disruption caused by massive blackholes3. According to Meisner et al4, seeking out transient sources in deep space has become more practical due to progresses made to telescope hardware and data handling methods. These have motivated a number of interdisciplinary collaborations that aim to assist astronomers in analysing the vast amounts of raw digital data obtained from sky surveys5,6.

Specific to the current research, the data collection7 examined herein has been generated as part of the collaborative, Newton Fund project between GOTO (https://goto-observatory.org/project/) and academics, both in the astrophysics and artificial intelligence fields. To timely identify a possible transient, an attempt is made to obtain the optical counterparts to gravitational events detected by the advanced Laser Interformeter Gravitational Wave Observatory (aLIGO)8. During a full night (i.e., 10 h) of observations, GOTO can produce around 400 images, each containing approximately 20,000 bright sources. Of course, these have to be maintained systematically for further study, and accurately analysed to provide useful information for astronomers. Handling this high volume of data has been a major difficulty encountered by all wide-field sky surveys9,10. One specific challenge to transient surveys is how to discriminate a few true transient sources amongst the (potentially many more) false positives that arise due to errors in hardware or data handling. A study describing an initial attempt to address this problem was presented in February 201811. This emphasises the task of classifying each of 40,000 to 50,000 new bright sources per night as either a real event (i.e., true positive) or a bogus event (i.e., false positive). This has proven to be non-trivial as the two, or binary, classes are far from even in magnitude, i.e., it is an example of imbalance data classification12,13.

To overcome this problem, some studies have been carried out to exploit SMOTE14, one of the best known data-level techniques that simulates additional samples of the minority class. The coupling of the modified data set with both a single classification model and classifier ensemble15,16 prove to be effective at first. However, the models show signs of overfitting17 as they are not generalised to new sets of data. With this insight, the current study aims to resolve the issue using the undersampling strategy, which is usually more robust to overfitting18,19. Following the work of Lin et al20 that puts forward an initial concept of clustering-driven undersampling, the new consensus clustering-based counterpart proposed here can better preserve useful data present in the majority class, hence the improved classification goodness21,22. In summary, the contribution of this paper is threefold:

(i) Despite the development on theory and applications of consensus clustering over the past decades23, this paper presents its first exploitation for data undersampling, especially in the context of astronomical data classification. Based on the rule of thumb that the size of the reduced majority class should be the same as that of the other, consensus clustering is able to provide a set of centroids or cluster centres, which are of higher quality than that obtained from a single clustering20. This is confirmed through empirical studies across three different data sets generated in the project mentioned above7. Note that the Hybrid Bipartite-Graph Formulation or HBGF model24 is applied to provide the consensus clustering framework for the current investigation, as it has shown to be one of the models that can deliver a robust performance25.

(ii) In addition to the application of HBGF, arriving at more reliable centroids, two different alternatives of selecting the clusterings are investigated, in an attempt to form the ensemble from a pool of initial clustering results. This can be accomplished using a random selection mechanism on the one hand, and by the quality-diversity guided process26 on the other.

(iii) The new model is assessed against its baseline that makes use of a single clustering20, RUS or Random UnderSampling27 that represents a basic model to accomplish the undersampling, and other benchmark ensemble techniques: RUSBoost27, IRUS: Inverse Random UnderSampling28, HD-Ensemble12. To have a rigorous experiment, different basic classification and classifier ensemble models are exploited. The findings not only provide the baseline and improved classification performance with the undersampling approach, but also map the outcome to a wider context of imbalance classification research, where a number of samples belonging to the minority class is extremely small (i.e., less than 0.5 percent), in contrast to the data sets examined by many in the literature29.

The organisation of this paper is as follows. The next section identifies the problem definition, followed by the methodology of this study that covers the issues of classification modelling and the proposed approach to undersampling. Then, the third section presents the experimental design and results, together with the investigated data sets and different classification models. At the end, the findings are discussed and conclusions drawn in the last section, including a perspective of future work.

Method

This section firstly presents the definition of a problem being investigated, followed by a description of the framework adopted to analyse the target data. After that, the proposed undersampling method is explained.

Problem definition and analysis framework

The ability to automatically and accurately identify real sources in data collected from a wide-area sky survey has become a major factor in progressing astronomical research. There already exists a rich collection of studies that make use of classification modeling for similar tasks in the astronomy field. For example, the Dark Energy Survey Supernova (DES-SN) program30 exploits a classifier based on an ensemble of Random Forests to detect point-source transients and moving objects. Following that, classical techniques such as k-nearest neighbors (KNN) and artificial neural networks (ANN) are exploited to classify photometric supernova31. Data collection, preparation and transformation

Building on the recent proof-of-concept study7, sky-survey images are simulated and analysed. The benefit of such an approach is that it is known a priori which of the detected sources are real transients. This helps to provide an ideal testbed for classification models, including the proposed and other methods to resolve the imbalanced class problem. To note, there is a common pitfall using simulated images, i.e., they may present idealised estimations of the real ones. In order to mitigate this, one of off-the-shelf software packages named SkyMaker32 is used to generate the target data. The tool has been widely exploited in astronomy research to support approximations for the most common complications, e.g., point-spread functions or PSF of bright sources and background noise among others. Based on experience with the GOTO observations as well as other published works on optical transients, the following process is carried out to synthesise the target images.

At first, a list of sources including stars and galaxies is prepared as the primary input to SkyMaker. For stars, each of the sources is specified by three components of the position values (i.e., right ascension, RA, and declination, Dec) and brightness. For galaxies, in additional of these three basic attributes, each source is further described by two cospatial ellipses (one for the galaxy bulge, the other for the galaxy disk). These are presented by seven parameters: the ratio of bulge-to-total light, bulge radius, bulge aspect ratio, bulge orientation on sky, disk radius, disk inclination and disk orientation on sky, respectively. To generate simulated sky images, the aforementioned list of sources is created by querying two standard databases, i.e., the USNO CCD Astrograph Catalog (UCAC) for stars that are brighter than 17th magnitude, and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) for stars and galaxies fainter than 17th magnitude. The decision to combine the two catalogues is to enhance the dynamic range of resulting images provided that bright stars saturate the SDSS detector and are thus under-represented in this catalogue, while UCAC does not go sufficiently deep for the current investigation.

All sources covered by an observation of a given patch of sky with one of the GOTO telescopes are extracted from both UCAC and SDSS databases. The position of each source (RA, Dec) is then converted to a pixel-based coordinate (x, y). This is carried out using the on-sky position of the central pixel and the pixel scale (i.e., the on-sky angular size of each pixel, which is a constant 1.24 arcseconds per pixel). Specific to SDSS sources, G-band magnitudes are exploited, while the V-band magnitude is employed for those UCAC sources. Apart for the list of sources, a configuration file is also provided to SkyMaker, i.e., to identify the simulation type (e.g., whether to embed background noise in a simulated image or not) and characteristics of the telescope. The latter is tailored to the GOTO telescopes: the saturation level of the pixels (set to 65,535), the zeropoint of the telescope (i.e., the magnitude of a star that may result in one count per second; 23.5), PSF size (see below), pixel size (1.24 arcsec per pixel), and CCD size in pixels (81766132). Moreover, the PSF size is set to randomly vary (from 0.8 to 3 arcseconds) between observations. This is to improve the realism of the imaging results.

To simulate transient sources, two observations for each patch of sky are synthesised. In the second observation, new sources are injected, randomly distributed across a simulated image with brightnesses chosen randomly from a uniform distribution ranging from magnitude 14 to 19. Each simulated image is then processed using the LSST software stack33, adapted to handle the simulated images. Then, the output from the image differencing modules of this stack is delivered as input to the stage of data preparation and transformation. Figure 2 depicts examples of candidates detected in those difference images. They are classified as real transient sources (class 1) or bogus candidates (class 0). A thumbnail image of size 40 by 40 pixels is extracted for each of those candidates. Following that, different measurements are identified by astronomy experts to derive 23 attributes from the thumbnails. As the result, the initial data set X with 23 attributes, , is obtained. Proposed method for classification model development

This section presents the proposed model of consensus clustering-based undersampling and two ensemble selection alternatives, each of which will be experimentally assessed later. Based on the initial study11 of classifying the simulated GOTO data, class imbalance has been identified as the major hurdle, for which the oversampling approach has, to certain extent, led to improvements. However, the resulting classifier built from a combination of real and synthetic samples often encounters the difficulty of overfitting, such that it does not generalise well to new data. This conforms to a typical finding reported in the literature17,18.

To address this problem, the present research focuses on the undersampling counterpart, in which a model is developed based on real samples only. To further assess the goodness of proposed methods, they are compared against three other well-known approaches. The first is RUSBoost27 that combines the concepts of data sampling and boosting. At first, it initialises a weight for each sample to 1/m, where m is the number of samples for training. Then, for each of the T iterations, a dedicated training set is produced by applying RUS on the majority class. Following the work of Nanni et al40, T is set to 50, where each of the 50 training sets leads to a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier. For each test sample, the decisions of these classification models are combined using the majority vote method. The second approach is IRUS28 that aims to reverse the ratio between majority and minority classes by undersampling the majority class. Similar to RUSBoost, IRUS also generates multiple training sets based on the bagging technique. The majority vote is used again to achieve the final decision from initial outputs of 50 SVM models. The last one is a recent ensemble-level development called HD-Ensemble12. Note that the results obtained with these three compared methods are summarised from 20 trials (the same number of trials used to test the new approach).

Experimental results

At first, it is necessary to point out the preference of using F1 as a quality metric, rather than a conventional accuracy rate (i.e., ). Figure 10 presents both F1 and accuracy rates with the three datasets using the C4.5 algorithm, where no undersampling technique is employed to balance the numbers of samples between two classes. It is clearly illustrated that the accuracy measures are almost the maximum across datasets, while the corresponding F1 values are much lower. This disagreement is brought about by the fact that accuracy takes into account TN, but F1 does not. With the nature of imbalance classification encountered here, accuracy has proven to be inappropriate as its value can be hugely distorted by the magnitude of those samples correctly identified as the majority class. As a result, F1 is the main metric exploited throughout this section with additional discussions based on the measures of Precision and Recall.

F1 and accuracy rates obtained with C4.5 over the three datasets under examination. These results are obtained from the original data without using any undersampling technique.

The averaged F1 scores of CS-R and CS-O are also higher than that of CU, reflecting the advantage of consensus clustering as compared to a single clustering.

Putting the proposed work into a wider context of classifying imbalanced data, Fig. 12 shows that both of the ensemble-level techniques, namely, IRUS and RUSBoost, underperform even compared against the lowest F1 scores of the CS-O method with regard to any of the five classifiers. This is because they each produces a huge number of false positives. Thus, the Precision values of these two methods are very low (IRUS: 0.0039/0.0027/0.0035 and RUSBoost: 0.0165/0.0025/0.0123 for Data1, Data2 and Data3, respectively), hence the F1 scores shown earlier. It is possible that information specific to the majority class has been largely lost during the process of random selection of representative samples. Following that, boosting (or bagging) also introduced noise to the original data distribution such that the results are biased towards the minority. Therefore, many of the samples are (mis-)classified as true sources, leading to a large final FP value.

With all the experimental findings attained, as reported in this section, the proposed approach for solving imbalance classification through consensus clustering-based undersampling has shown great potential for the task of transient classification. However, the predictive performance is far from perfect, thus requiring additional work that may improve the classification quality. This is further discussed below.

Discussion and conclusion

This paper has presented a novel undersampling method to ease the difficulty of imbalance classification. The use of consensus clustering to select representative samples of the majority class has not been studied in the literature, prior to this investigation, despite the rich collection of algorithms and applications developed from the meta-learning framework23. Given the empirical results, the proposed approach appears to be better than the single-clustering baseline (i.e., CU) and RUS in capturing the essence of class-specific characteristics. This paper also introduces two innovative techniques to generate a preferred ensemble, with one is based simply on a random selection mechanism and the other has been designed as an optimisation of combining clustering quality and diversity. The resulting techniques, named CS-R and CS-O, prove to be more effective, not only when compared against CU and RUS, but also with the ensemble-level algorithms such as IRUS and RUSBoost.

This work offers a baseline for research in both subject areas of data science and astronomical data analytic. Table 7 shows the statistics of datasets exploited in four recent publications with the focus of tackling the imbalanced class problem. In particular, most of these studies pay attention to assessing a new method or a collection of methods on different datasets in which the percentages of minority class are between 1 and 30%. Very few40,43 have a go with a lower percentage, e.g., between 0.5 and 1%. Based on this observation, the study with the three datasets in this work (each of which has a minority class less than 0.5%) gives a great opportunity to evaluate how well the proposed and other methods handle such an extreme scenario. It turns out that the proposed approach delivers higher classification performance than the existing techniques.

In the context of astronomical data classification, at the first glance, with the best F1 scores from three datasets being 0.82, 0.76 and 0.85, the proposed approach might appear to be less helpful when compared to possible alternatives that may attain an F1 score of 0.9 or greater1,30,34,46,47. Nonetheless, these astronomy-specific studies commonly assume that the sizes of both classes are roughly the same, thus overlooking the problem of imbalance classification. The percentage of minority class for each of the referenced publications is illustrated in Figure 15. One way to increase the size of real samples is to simply generate variants of the known or conceptually real sources, reducing the challenge caused by imbalanced classes. But it might introduce another serious problem of overfitting in the learning stage. It is similar to applying an oversampling technique to an imbalanced dataset, where the resulting classifier is not likely to be generalised to new data. Another alternative to boost the size of minority class is to provide new sources by relying upon a large group of experts to help categorise them as real or else47. In so doing, the target set may become larger over time, provided that there is an initial classification method to deliver the candidates to human in the first place. At the early stage of the GOTO collaboration11, the focus has been on making the underlying classifier effective initially. As such, the study conducted in this paper is the first and significant step towards an effective detection of transients from a sky survey, with the problem of imbalance classification being gradually resolved.

There are several interesting issues worth a further investigation. Firstly, it may be possible to tackle the problem of highly imbalanced data better by exploiting the concept of context-based classification. In other words, the training data may well be clustered into a set of naturally separated groups using an unsupervised clustering algorithm and quality metrics. As a result, certain groups may belong solely to the majority class, while the others contain samples of both classes. For the former, a simple set of rules can be derived and used to quickly filter out any new sample that looks obviously like a bogus. As for the latter, group-specific classification methods may be developed through directing an unseen sample to the method corresponding to the group closest to itself. Such a context provides a means to reduce the classifier complexity, hence the time required for training and prediction. Of course, the methodology of consensus clustering can also be useful here to support robust clustering across data collections23.

Secondly, at the moment, only classical classifiers have been explored, but soft classification algorithms like a fuzzy decision tree or FDT48 may be examined similarly. With the use of such techniques, an astronomer may exploit approximate information to boost the Recall score. This capability to utilise the whole of all real sources is extremely important for a discovery in astronomy. It is also interesting to make use of CS-O and CS-R to form classifier ensembles, where each ensemble member is trained with different data variations. In this process, feature selection techniques found in the literature may also help to determine different feature subsets to form accurate ensemble members.

Finally, apart from following the traditional feature-based approach, it may be useful to explore the recent development of deep learning. It provides an alternative to explicit feature engineering, where image-based features can be created, evaluated and selected natively within the convolutional neural networks39,49. This trend has recently emerged as another attractive mechanism in the astronomy domain47. As an example, Deep-HiTS46 is a rotation-invariant CNN model that is proposed to classify images of transient candidates for the High cadence Transient Survey (HiTS).
