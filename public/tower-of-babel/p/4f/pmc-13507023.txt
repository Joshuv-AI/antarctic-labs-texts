Adaptive frequency band attention-guided CNN–BiLSTM for Spatial–Spectral–Temporal EEG emotion recognition
Authors: Rabita Hasan, Sheikh Md. Rabiul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electroencephalography (EEG)-based emotion recognition has gained increasing attention in affective computing because EEG provides high temporal resolution and reflects intrinsic neural activity. However, reliable emotion recognition remains challenging due to the nonlinear, non-stationery and subject-dependent nature of EEG signals. Moreover, many existing methods insufficiently examine temporal overlap effects, treat frequency bands with equal importance, and offer limited spectral interpretability. To address these issues, this study proposes an interpretable Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA)-guided multi-band 2D CNN–BiLSTM framework for spatial–spectral–temporal EEG emotion recognition using power spectral density (PSD) features. The proposed framework decomposes EEG signals into delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma and broadband components using Butterworth bandpass filtering. Welch-based PSD features are then extracted and arranged into structured multi-band representations. The AFBA module adaptively learns the relative contribution of each frequency band before spatial–spectral feature extraction through a 2D convolutional neural network and temporal dependency modeling using a BiLSTM network. To assess temporal continuity, 3-s EEG windows with 0 s, 1 s and 2 s overlap are evaluated. The model is tested on the DEAP dataset using leakage-aware trial-level tenfold cross-validation and further validated on SEED under protocol-aligned conditions. Results show that increased temporal overlap improves performance across bands and emotion dimensions. With 2-s overlap, the gamma band achieves 97.2% valence accuracy, 98.1% arousal accuracy and 88.9% four-class accuracy on DEAP, while reaching 94.1% three-class accuracy on SEED. Ablation and SHAP analyses further support the contribution of temporal modeling, AFBA, and high-frequency beta/gamma activity.

Introduction

Emotion, human behavior and the circumplex model

Emotion represents a fundamental regulatory mechanism that shapes human cognition and behavioral responses. It influences perception, attention, memory consolidation, learning, social interaction, and decision-making, thereby contributing significantly to adaptive functioning and psychological well-being. As intelligent computational systems increasingly interact with humans in real-world environments, the ability to computationally interpret and model emotional states has become essential for affect-aware systems, adaptive human–machine interfaces, and mental health technologies [1, 2].

Modern affective science does not conceptualize emotion solely as discrete categories; rather, it recognizes emotional experience as a continuous and dynamically evolving process. Among dimensional theories, Russell’s circumplex model of affect remains one of the most widely adopted frameworks [3]. This model organizes emotional states within a two-dimensional coordinate system defined by valence, representing the degree of pleasantness or unpleasantness, and arousal, reflecting activation or physiological intensity. Later extensions incorporated additional dimensions such as dominance and liking to capture perceived control and subjective preference more comprehensively [4].

Collectively, these dimensions offer a compact yet expressive representation of affective states. Within computational modeling, the circumplex framework supports both binary classification along individual axes, such as high versus low valence, and quadrant-based categorization derived from combined valence–arousal distributions. The four quadrants typically include high-valence high-arousal (HVHA), high-valence low-arousal (HVLA), low-valence high-arousal (LVHA) and low-valence low-arousal (LVLA). These representations are extensively utilized in benchmark EEG datasets such as DEAP and SEED, providing a psychologically grounded foundation for affective computing applications [5]–[6].

Despite its conceptual robustness, operationalizing the circumplex model in terms of measurable neural activity remains challenging. Emotional states unfold continuously over time, whereas computational implementations often impose rigid categorical thresholds that may oversimplify underlying neural dynamics. Therefore, effectively linking dimensional emotion theory with physiological brain signals requires modeling strategies capable of preserving temporal continuity while maintaining multidimensional structure.

In the present study, the circumplex framework serves as the theoretical foundation for EEG-based emotion recognition. Both binary and quadrant-based classification paradigms are implemented to ensure conceptual alignment with dimensional affect theory while enabling practical machine learning deployment.

EEG as a neural substrate of emotion

Emotional processing arises from distributed neural networks encompassing frontal, temporal, parietal, and limbic regions. Among available neurophysiological measurement modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) offers distinct advantages due to its high temporal resolution and direct measurement of cortical electrical activity [7, 8]. With millisecond-level precision, EEG is particularly well suited to capturing the rapid fluctuations that characterize affective dynamics.

Unlike external behavioral signals such as facial expressions or speech, EEG reflects intrinsic neural processes and is comparatively less susceptible to voluntary control or social masking [9, 10]. A substantial body of neuroscientific evidence indicates that emotional stimuli systematically modulate oscillatory activity, hemispheric asymmetry, and functional connectivity patterns, particularly within frontal and temporal cortical regions [11, 12].

Consequently, EEG-based emotion recognition has emerged as a rapidly advancing research domain with applications spanning mental health assessment, adaptive user interfaces, neuromarketing, and brain–computer interface systems [13–15].

However, EEG signals are inherently nonlinear, non-stationary, and subject-specific. They are affected by noise, physiological artifacts, and inter-individual variability. Emotional responses evolve gradually and interact with concurrent cognitive processes, further complicating reliable feature extraction. These characteristics make the development of robust and generalizable EEG-based emotion recognition models a significant methodological challenge [16–18].

To address these complexities, the present work emphasizes the design of a signal processing and learning framework capable of extracting physiologically meaningful and reproducible emotional features from multichannel EEG recordings.

Signal modeling, temporal framing and spectral feature extraction

EEG activity reflects the collective dynamics of large neuronal populations, resulting in signal characteristics that are inherently nonlinear and time-varying [19]. Traditional modeling approaches, including auto-regressive (AR) methods, Fourier-based spectral analysis, and wavelet transforms, have been extensively employed to characterize EEG signals [20–23]. While Fourier analysis provides a global representation of frequency content, it lacks temporal localization. Wavelet-based methods offer improved time–frequency resolution but require careful parameter tuning and can introduce computational complexity [24, 25]. These limitations restrict their ability to comprehensively capture emotion-related neural oscillations that evolve over time.

Temporal segmentation provides an effective strategy for mitigating non-stationarity. By dividing continuous EEG recordings into shorter frames, particularly using overlapping windows, transient neural events can be more consistently represented while minimizing boundary artifacts [26–28]. Overlapping segmentation is especially relevant for emotion recognition, as affective responses typically exhibit gradual transitions rather than abrupt changes. In the frequency domain, EEG signals are conventionally decomposed into canonical sub-bands: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (> 30 Hz). Each band corresponds to distinct neurocognitive and affective processes. Among spectral descriptors, power spectral density (PSD) is widely regarded as both stable and physiologically interpretable [29–31]. PSD quantifies the distribution of signal energy across frequencies and reflects synchronized neuronal oscillations, making it particularly suitable for cross-subject analysis [32, 33].

In recent years, EEG-based modeling has increasingly shifted toward large-scale representation learning paradigms that aim to capture generalized neural patterns across subjects and tasks. Emerging architectures, such as attention-enhanced convolutional frameworks (e.g., BCINetV1), integrate temporal and spectral feature learning within unified deep models, enabling more efficient extraction of task-relevant neural representations [34]. In parallel, the development of large brain models trained on extensive EEG datasets has introduced a foundation model perspective to brain–computer interface research, where models learn transferable and generic representations that can be adapted to multiple downstream applications [35]. These approaches move beyond handcrafted feature engineering and task-specific optimization, instead emphasizing data-driven learning of universal neural embeddings. Despite their promise, such large-scale models often require substantial computational resources and large annotated datasets, which may not always be feasible in practical affective computing scenarios. Therefore, there remains a need for architectures that balance representational richness, interpretability, and computational efficiency, particularly for emotion recognition tasks involving limited and heterogeneous EEG data.

Nevertheless, many existing approaches analyze isolated frequency bands or fail to integrate spatial, spectral and temporal structures within a unified framework. Furthermore, the influence of different temporal overlap configurations is rarely subjected to rigorous statistical evaluation, limiting reproducibility and methodological transparency.

In this study, EEG recordings are segmented into 3-s frames under multiple overlap configurations (0 s, 1 s and 2 s) to capture the continuous and evolving nature of emotional responses. PSD features are computed across all channels and frequency bands to preserve spatial–spectral richness.

Hybrid CNN–BiLSTM modeling and cross-dataset evaluation

Emotion-related EEG signals vary simultaneously across spatial, spectral, and temporal dimensions. Effectively modeling these multidimensional dependencies is essential for robust emotion recognition. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely used to extract spatial and spectral patterns from multichannel EEG representations, whereas long short-term memory (LSTM) and bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) networks are effective for capturing temporal dependencies across sequential EEG segments [36–39].

Recent EEG emotion-recognition studies have increasingly adopted hybrid CNN-LSTM or CNN-BiLSTM architectures. For example, Chakravarthi et al. proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM framework for EEG-based emotion recognition, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining convolutional and recurrent learning for affective state classification [40]. J. Yang et al. introduced a BiLSTM-based EEG emotion classification method that detects four emotions: happy, sad, fear and neutral with 84.21% accuracy [41]. M. Zhu et al. developed a dynamic energy feature-based Bi-LSTM deep network for EEG emotion recognition, achieving 89.42% accuracy on SEED and 77.34% on DEAP [42]. S. Liu et al. proposed GLFANet, an EEG emotion recognition model that combines global graph-based electrode topology features with local EEG signal features to improve accuracy on DEAP, SEED and DREAMER datasets. [43]. More recently, M. Algarni et al. presented a deep learning EEG emotion recognition model using feature extraction, Binary Gray Wolf Optimizer feature selection and stacked Bi-LSTM classification, achieving very high accuracy on DEAP for valence, arousal and liking [44]. Zhang et al. proposed a CNN-BiLSTM and DC-IGN fusion framework to improve spatial–temporal feature extraction on SEED and DEAP datasets [45]. A. Abgeena et al. introduced a novel S-LSTM-ATT model with Firefly Optimization-based feature selection was proposed for EEG emotion recognition using SEED and EEG Brainwave datasets. The model achieved high accuracy of 97.83% on SEED and 98.36% on EEG Brainwave, outperforming CNN, GRU and standard LSTM models. [46]. In addition, recent Transformer-CNN-BiLSTM models have further shown that combining convolutional, recurrent and attention-based modules can improve EEG emotion classification and interpretability [47, 48].

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. First, many existing CNN-LSTM-based models emphasize spatial–temporal learning but do not explicitly perform adaptive frequency-band weighting, even though EEG frequency bands contribute unequally to emotional processing. Second, several studies rely on a single segmentation configuration, leaving the effect of temporal overlap insufficiently examined. Third, many models are validated on a single dataset, which limits conclusions regarding robustness across heterogeneous EEG recording protocols. Finally, although attention mechanisms have been incorporated in recent studies, they are often applied at the channel, temporal, or general feature level rather than specifically targeting frequency-band contribution in a physiologically interpretable manner.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an attention-guided multi-band 2D CNN-BiLSTM framework based on PSD representations. Unlike conventional CNN-BiLSTM approaches, the proposed model introduces an Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA) mechanism to dynamically learn the relative importance of delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma, and broadband EEG components. The attention-refined PSD maps are then processed by a 2D CNN to extract spatial-spectral features, followed by a BiLSTM module to model temporal dependencies across overlapping EEG segments. In addition, multiple temporal overlap configurations are systematically evaluated to determine their influence on emotion-recognition performance. The model is first validated on the DEAP dataset and subsequently evaluated on the SEED dataset under protocol-aligned conditions. This dual-dataset evaluation strategy strengthens the robustness assessment and supports the generalizability of the proposed spatial-spectral-temporal EEG emotion-recognition framework.

Statistical validation and explainable learning

Statistical validation and explainable learning are essential for establishing the reliability and transparency of EEG-based emotion recognition models. Since EEG signals are inherently non-stationary, subject-dependent and highly sensitive to preprocessing choices, performance evaluation must go beyond reporting classification accuracy alone. In particular, temporal segmentation plays a critical role in modeling emotion-related EEG dynamics, as emotional responses usually evolve gradually over time rather than occurring as isolated neural events.

In this study, the effect of temporal overlap is systematically examined using three overlap configurations: 0 s, 1 s and 2 s. Classification performance is evaluated across different EEG frequency bands and emotional dimensions to determine whether increasing overlap length produces consistent improvements. The results show a clear performance trend, where the 2-s overlap configuration achieves the highest accuracy in most cases. This indicates that greater temporal continuity enables the model to capture dynamic and non-stationary emotional patterns more effectively.

To support the validity of the analysis, statistical assumptions such as normality and homogeneity of variance are considered. However, because the reported results are based on aggregated cross-validation metrics rather than fold-wise sample-level observations, direct statistical tests such as the Shapiro–Wilk test, Levene’s test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank test could not be strictly applied. Despite this limitation, the balanced experimental design, repeated cross-validation protocol, and consistent monotonic improvement across overlap settings provide strong empirical evidence that temporal overlap has a meaningful effect on classification performance.

Beyond statistical validation, explainable learning is incorporated to improve the interpretability of the proposed framework. Deep learning models often operate as black-box classifiers, making it difficult to identify which EEG features contribute most strongly to prediction outcomes. To address this issue, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a theoretically grounded and model-agnostic explainability method, is employed to quantify the contribution of PSD-based frequency-band and electrode-region features to the model’s decisions [49–51]. This enables the identification of neuro-physiologically meaningful patterns associated with emotion recognition.

By integrating statistical validation with explainable artificial intelligence, the proposed framework provides both reliable performance assessment and interpretable model behavior. The statistical analysis demonstrates the practical importance of temporal overlap, while the SHAP-based explanation reveals the spectral and spatial EEG features most responsible for classification decisions. Together, these components strengthen the robustness, transparency and neurophysiological relevance of the proposed EEG-based emotion recognition approach.

The principal contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

1. A novel attention-guided multi-band EEG emotion recognition framework is proposed:

This study introduces an Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA)-guided 2D CNN–BiLSTM architecture for EEG-based emotion recognition. Unlike conventional CNN–BiLSTM models that treat all EEG frequency bands with equal importance, the proposed framework adaptively learns the relative contribution of delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma, and broadband components. This enables the model to emphasize emotion-relevant oscillatory patterns while suppressing less informative spectral components.

2. A structured spatial–spectral–temporal PSD representation is developed:

The proposed method constructs multi-band power spectral density features from EEG signals using Welch’s method and organizes them into a structured representation that preserves channel-wise spatial information, frequency-band characteristics, and temporal segment order. This representation allows the CNN module to extract spatial–spectral patterns and the BiLSTM module to capture temporal dependencies across consecutive EEG segments.

3. The effect of temporal overlap is systematically investigated:

This study evaluates multiple sliding-window overlap configurations, including 0 s, 1 s and 2 s overlap, to examine how temporal continuity influences EEG emotion recognition performance. The analysis demonstrates that increasing temporal overlap improves classification accuracy across frequency bands and emotional dimensions, confirming the importance of segmentation strategy in modeling non-stationary EEG dynamics.

4. A leakage-aware trial-level validation protocol is employed:

To ensure reliable and unbiased performance evaluation, all segments derived from the same EEG trial are kept within the same cross-validation fold. This trial-level validation strategy prevents overlap-induced data leakage between training and testing sets and provides a more realistic estimate of model generalization.

5. A comprehensive ablation study is conducted to verify architectural contributions:

The study compares the proposed AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM model with CNN-only and CNN–BiLSTM baseline configurations. This progressive analysis isolates the individual contributions of spatial–spectral feature learning, temporal dependency modeling, and adaptive frequency-band attention, demonstrating that each component contributes meaningfully to the final classification performance.

6. Explainable learning is integrated through attention weights and SHAP analysis:

The proposed framework enhances interpretability by combining intrinsic attention-based explanation with post-hoc SHAP-based analysis. The learned AFBA weights reveal the relative importance of EEG frequency bands, while SHAP analysis identifies influential PSD features and electrode-region contributions. This provides neuro-physiologically meaningful insight into the spectral and spatial patterns driving emotion classification.

7. Cross-dataset validation is performed to assess generalizability:

Beyond the primary evaluation on the DEAP dataset, the proposed framework is further validated on the SEED dataset under protocol-aligned conditions. The consistency of performance trends across datasets, particularly the importance of temporal overlap and high-frequency beta/gamma activity, supports the robustness and generalizability of the proposed approach.

Literature review

Electroencephalography (EEG)-based emotion recognition has progressed substantially in recent years due to the availability of benchmark datasets and the rapid advancement of deep learning architectures. Among existing datasets, DEAP and SEED are two of the most widely used resources for evaluating affective EEG models. The DEAP dataset provides 32-channel EEG recordings with subjective ratings along four affective dimensions: valence, arousal, dominance, and liking, and is commonly employed for binary and dimensional emotion classification tasks [52]. In contrast, the SEED dataset focuses on discrete emotional categories, namely positive, neutral, and negative emotions, elicited using film stimuli, and is frequently used for multi-class EEG emotion recognition [53].

Although these datasets have enabled systematic benchmarking, many existing studies evaluate their models on only one dataset, most commonly DEAP or SEED. This restricts the assessment of robustness under variations in stimulus design, labeling strategy, electrode configuration, recording protocol, and subject distribution. Therefore, cross-dataset validation, interpretable feature analysis, and leakage-aware evaluation protocols remain important requirements for developing reliable EEG-based emotion recognition systems.

Deep learning-based spatio–temporal modeling approaches

Hybrid deep learning architectures that combine convolutional and recurrent structures have become prominent in EEG-based affective computing because they can jointly model spatial, spectral, and temporal dependencies. A CNN–BiLSTM architecture using power spectral density (PSD) features was proposed to capture spatial and temporal EEG patterns and achieved 97.5% accuracy for three-class emotion recognition on the SEED dataset [54]. Although the reported performance was high, the evaluation was limited to a single dataset, leaving its generalization capability under different EEG recording conditions unexplored.

To address domain variability, adversarial learning strategies have also been investigated. The AD-TCN framework incorporated domain adaptation into a temporal convolutional network to learn domain-invariant EEG representations [55]. Evaluated on DEAP and DREAMER, the model achieved competitive cross-subject performance and approximately 62% accuracy in cross-dataset transfer scenarios. While this approach partially reduced dataset-shift effects, the performance degradation under unseen EEG distributions indicates that robust cross-dataset EEG emotion recognition remains challenging.

Transformer-based architectures have recently shown strong potential for modeling long-range EEG dependencies. Zheng et al. proposed the Spatiotemporal Symmetric Transformer (STS-Transformer), an end-to-end framework that operates directly on raw EEG signals without relying on handcrafted features [56]. By jointly capturing spatial, spectral, and temporal relationships, the model achieved 89.86% and 86.83% accuracy for valence and arousal classification on DEAP and 85.09% and 82.32% on DREAMER, respectively. However, the evaluation was mainly focused on binary classification tasks and did not fully explore multi-class emotion recognition or broader cross-dataset validation.

The ACTNN framework further advanced attention-based EEG modeling by integrating attention-guided convolutional modules with a transformer-based temporal encoder [57]. It achieved 98.47% accuracy on SEED and 91.90% on SEED-IV. In addition to high performance, attention visualization indicated strong contributions from prefrontal and lateral temporal regions, particularly in the gamma band, thereby improving neuroscientific interpretability. However, despite these advantages, the model was evaluated primarily within SEED-family datasets, and its robustness across structurally different datasets remains less clear.

CNN–BiLSTM-based models have also been widely adopted for EEG emotion recognition. A spatio-temporal CNN–BiLSTM approach reported 97.5% accuracy on SEED [58], demonstrating the effectiveness of combining convolutional spatial feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling. Huang et al. introduced a CNN–BiLSTM–Attention model incorporating channel-wise attention weighting and achieved 99.55% accuracy for three-class recognition and 99.79% for four-class recognition on SEED under ten-fold cross-validation [59]. Similarly, the MSBiLSTM-Attention model improved spatio-temporal feature fusion using multi-scale convolution and attention-based integration, reaching up to 99.85% single-run accuracy and 99.70% cross-validated accuracy on SEED [60]. Although these methods achieved excellent performance, their evaluation was largely confined to a single dataset, limiting conclusions about external validity and cross-dataset generalization.

Overall, deep spatio-temporal models have demonstrated strong classification capability for EEG emotion recognition. However, several limitations remain. Many approaches rely on a single validation dataset, focus mainly on spatial or temporal attention rather than explicit frequency-band contribution, and provide limited statistical analysis of preprocessing choices such as temporal overlap. These limitations motivate the need for models that are not only accurate but also interpretable, leakage-aware, and robust across different EEG datasets.

Hybrid and signal-driven approaches

In addition to end-to-end deep learning methods, hybrid approaches combining signal processing, handcrafted feature extraction and machine learning classifiers have been widely explored for EEG classification. A modified convolutional fuzzy neural network (CFNN) combined preprocessing, handcrafted feature extraction, and fuzzy inference, achieving 98.21% and 98.08% accuracy for valence and arousal classification, respectively [61]. While effective, this approach depends heavily on manually designed features, which may limit scalability and adaptability across heterogeneous EEG datasets.

Adaptive channel selection combined with zero-time windowing and group-delay analysis has also been proposed to identify emotion-specific excitation epochs, reporting over 89% accuracy on DEAP for up to nine emotion categories [62]. This method highlights the importance of selecting discriminative EEG channels and temporal epochs. However, its reliance on classical classifiers such as quadratic discriminant classifiers and recurrent neural networks limits its capacity for hierarchical representation learning compared with modern deep architectures.

Correlation-based EEG representations have provided another direction for feature construction. A CNN model using Pearson correlation coefficient images derived from EEG sub-bands achieved 78.22% accuracy for valence and 74.92% for arousal on DEAP [63]. Although this approach is computationally efficient and captures inter-channel relationships, it sacrifices temporal dynamics and produces comparatively lower performance than recent deep spatio-temporal models. Similarly, rhythm-based feature extraction using multivariate fast iterative filtering combined with ensemble classifiers achieved up to 99.8% accuracy on GAMEEMO and DREAMER datasets [64]. Nevertheless, the dependence on handcrafted rhythm decomposition restricts end-to-end adaptability and may reduce flexibility across different affective EEG protocols.

Traditional time–frequency pipelines have also been examined. Discrete Wavelet Transform with db6 wavelets and mixed spectral–temporal features achieved a maximum accuracy of 71.52% on SEED using decision-tree classification [65]. This result illustrates the limitations of fixed feature representations and reduced electrode subsets when compared with more flexible deep learning-based approaches.

Recent signal-decomposition-based EEG classification studies further demonstrate the value of adaptive time–frequency analysis for handling non-stationary EEG signals. Aziz and Yu proposed an Adaptive Multivariate Chirp Mode Decomposition (AMCMD)-based EEG classification framework for robust brain–computer interface applications [66]. Their approach combined AMCMD with different electrode combinations, spectral features and multiple classifiers. The framework was validated on the GigaDB dataset with 52 participants, as well as on BCI Competition III datasets IVa and IVb. The method achieved very high classification accuracy on the BCI Competition III datasets and reported average accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1-score values of 82.71%, 82.76%, 83.04%, and 82.80%, respectively, on GigaDB.

Although the AMCMD framework was designed for motor imagery EEG classification rather than emotion recognition, it is relevant because it shows that adaptive decomposition can improve EEG classification under subject-to-subject variability and non-stationary signal conditions. However, such decomposition-driven approaches typically depend on carefully selected feature extraction and classifier combinations, and they do not directly address affective EEG modeling, frequency-band attention, temporal overlap effects, or explainable emotion-related spatial–spectral representations. Therefore, while signal-driven methods provide valuable neurophysiological insight and strong classification foundations, there remains a need for emotion-specific deep frameworks that integrate adaptive spectral weighting, temporal modeling, statistical validation, and explainable learning.

Taken together, the existing literature shows that EEG emotion recognition has benefited from both deep spatio-temporal learning and signal-driven feature engineering. However, key challenges persist, including limited cross-dataset validation, insufficient analysis of temporal overlap, weak frequency-band-level interpretability and dependence on either single-dataset evaluation or handcrafted features. These gaps motivate the present study, which proposes an Adaptive Frequency Band Attention-guided 2D CNN–BiLSTM framework using multi-band PSD representations, leakage-aware trial-level validation, statistical analysis of overlap effects, SHAP-based explainability, and cross-dataset evaluation on DEAP and SEED.

Methodology

This section presents the overall methodological framework of the proposed attention-guided multi-band 2D CNN–BiLSTM model for EEG-based emotion recognition. The framework is designed to effectively capture spatial, spectral and temporal characteristics of EEG signals through a structured and sequential processing pipeline. It begins with EEG data preprocessing, including channel selection, normalization, and temporal segmentation using a sliding window with multiple overlap configurations to preserve temporal continuity. Subsequently, the preprocessed signals are decomposed into multiple frequency sub-bands, and power spectral density (PSD) features are extracted using Welch’s method to represent spectral information.

To enhance discriminative feature learning, an Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA) mechanism is incorporated to dynamically weight the importance of different EEG frequency bands. The attention-refined features are then fed into a two-dimensional convolutional neural network (2D CNN) for spatial–spectral feature extraction, followed by a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) network to model temporal dependencies across EEG segments. Finally, fully connected layers are employed for emotion classification under both binary and multi-class settings.

DEAP dataset explanation

The DEAP (Database for Emotion Analysis using Physiological Signals) dataset is a widely used multimodal benchmark for EEG-based emotion recognition research [52]. It contains physiological recordings from 32 participants who each watched 40 one-minute music video clips specifically selected to elicit a broad range of emotional responses.

After each trial, participants provided self-assessment ratings along four affective dimensions: valence (pleasantness), arousal (intensity), dominance (sense of control) and liking (degree of preference for the stimulus). These ratings were collected using a 9-point Likert scale.

EEG signals were recorded from 32 electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system, ensuring standardized spatial coverage of brain activity. The original signals were acquired at a sampling rate of 512 Hz. The dataset provides both raw and preprocessed versions; in this study, the preprocessed signals down-sampled to 128 Hz were utilized, resulting in 8064 samples per trial (approximately 63 s). For emotion classification, the continuous valence and arousal ratings were converted into discrete classes following standard practice. Specifically, a threshold of 5 (midpoint of the 9-point scale) was used to obtain binary classes (high vs. low). In addition, four-class emotion categories were constructed based on the valence–arousal circumplex model, resulting in High-Valence High-Arousal (HVHA), High-Valence Low-Arousal (HVLA), Low-Valence High-Arousal (LVHA), and Low-Valence Low-Arousal (LVLA) classes.

To ensure reliable evaluation and avoid data leakage, each trial was treated as an independent sample, and all segments derived from a given trial were kept within the same fold during cross-validation. This prevents overlapping temporal segments from being split across training and testing sets.

Additionally, frontal face videos were recorded for 22 participants, enabling potential multimodal emotion recognition studies. This dataset provides a robust and standardized platform for evaluating spatial–spectral–temporal EEG representations in emotion recognition tasks.

Data preprocessing

The following preprocessing pipeline was employed to prepare the EEG data for feature extraction and classification:

Data extraction and channel selection

The DEAP dataset contains recordings from 40 channels, including 32 EEG channels and 8 peripheral physiological signals. In this study, only the 32 EEG channels were retained, as they directly reflect cortical brain activity relevant to emotional processing.

The EEG signals were extracted by loading the.dat files corresponding to each subject. After channel selection, the data for each trial was organized in the form:

Trials × Channels × Samples.

Sampling rate and trial segmentation

The DEAP dataset provides both raw signals (512 Hz) and preprocessed signals down-sampled to 128 Hz. In this study, the preprocessed EEG signals sampled at 128 Hz were utilized to reduce computational complexity while preserving the essential temporal characteristics of the data.

Each trial contains 8064 samples, corresponding to approximately 63 s of EEG recording. The initial 3 s of each trial were discarded to eliminate pre-stimulus baseline activity, which does not reflect the emotional response to the presented stimulus. After this removal, each trial contained:

7680 samples (60 s × 128 Hz).

Following channel selection and baseline removal, the final data representation for each subject was:

40 × 32 × 7680.

representing 40 trials, 32 EEG channels and 7680-time samples per trial.

Data normalization

To ensure stable model training and reduce inter-subject variability, the EEG signals were normalized using z-score normalization along the temporal dimension for each channel. This standardization process centers the data to zero mean and unit variance, improving convergence of the deep learning model.

Signal framing with 3-second window length

To address the non-stationary nature of EEG signals and capture temporal dynamics of emotional responses, the preprocessed EEG signals were segmented into shorter time windows using a sliding window approach.

Each trial, consisting of 60 s of EEG data sampled at 128 Hz (7680 samples), was divided into fixed-length segments of 3 s (384 samples per segment). To investigate the effect of temporal continuity, three overlap configurations were considered: 0 s, 1 s and 2 s. EEG Sub-band decomposition and spectral feature extraction

In addition, a broadband signal (0.5–45 Hz) was retained to preserve global spectral information and cross-band interactions.

The selected frequency ranges follow standard EEG literature, where alpha activity is typically defined within 8–13 Hz and beta within 13–30 Hz. These bands are known to correspond to distinct neurophysiological processes, including cognitive engagement, emotional arousal, and attentional modulation. High-frequency beta and gamma oscillations, in particular, have been shown to carry discriminative information for emotion recognition tasks.

The Butterworth filter was chosen due to its smooth frequency response and minimal passband distortion, making it suitable for preserving physiological signal characteristics. Filtering was performed in the digital domain using the sampling frequency of 128 Hz and zero-phase filtering was applied to avoid phase distortion in the EEG signals.

Following bandpass filtering, each 3-s EEG segment was decomposed into six representations: five sub-band signals and one broadband signal. These band-specific signals form the basis for subsequent spectral feature extraction using power spectral density (PSD) analysis.

Power spectral density estimation

To quantify the spectral energy distribution of each EEG sub-band, Power Spectral Density (PSD) analysis was performed using Welch’s method, which provides stable and low-variance spectral estimates.

Welch’s method involves dividing each signal segment into smaller overlapping windows, applying a window function, computing the periodogram of each window, and averaging the results. PSD features were computed independently for each EEG channel and each frequency band, forming a multi-dimensional spectral representation for subsequent processing.

Band-specific power computation

To extract compact spectral features, band-specific power was computed by averaging the PSD values within each frequency band:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${PSD}_{band}= \frac{1}{M}\sum_{{f}_{i}\in [{f}_{low}, {f}_{high}]}{P}_{x}(f)$$\end{document}where, M is the number of frequency bins within the band, flow and fhigh define the frequency range of the band.

This operation captures the average spectral energy associated with each neural oscillation.

The resulting band-wise PSD features from all channels were organized into structured representations, which serve as input to the proposed deep learning model. These features effectively encode the spatial (channel-wise) and spectral (frequency-wise) characteristics of EEG signals, enabling robust emotion classification.

Adaptive frequency band attention (AFBA)

Although multi-band PSD features provide rich spectral representations, different EEG frequency bands do not contribute equally to emotion recognition. Conventional CNN–BiLSTM architectures typically treat all frequency bands with equal importance, which may lead to suboptimal feature representation, as certain bands (e.g., beta and gamma) are known to be more discriminative for affective processing.

To address this limitation and enhance model interpretability, an Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA) mechanism is introduced. Unlike existing attention approaches that focus on spatial (channel-wise) or temporal dependencies, the proposed AFBA module performs band-level attention, enabling the model to learn the relative importance of different frequency bands in a data-driven manner. This constitutes a key novelty of the proposed framework.

Discussion and significance

The AFBA module enables adaptive band-level feature selection, allowing the model to automatically emphasize discriminative oscillatory components associated with emotional states. In contrast to conventional architectures that assume uniform band importance, the proposed approach dynamically adjusts spectral contributions based on data characteristics.

This mechanism not only improves classification performance but also enhances interpretability by providing insight into which frequency bands are most relevant for emotion recognition. Attention-guided multi-band 2D CNN–BiLSTM architecture

The proposed model employs an attention-guided hybrid architecture that integrates Adaptive Frequency Band Attention (AFBA), two-dimensional convolutional neural networks (2D CNN), and bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) networks to jointly learn spatial, spectral, and temporal representations of EEG signals.

Unlike conventional CNN–BiLSTM models that treat all frequency bands equally, the proposed framework incorporates a band-level attention mechanism (AFBA) to adaptively emphasize informative frequency components. This design improves discriminative feature learning and enhances interpretability by explicitly modeling the contribution of different EEG bands.

The overall processing pipeline is defined as:

PSD Features → AFBA → 2D CNN → BiLSTM → Fully Connected Layer → Emotion Prediction.

This design is also consistent with the computerized multidomain EEG classification paradigm proposed by Yu et al. [68], in which robust EEG classification is achieved by integrating complementary information sources within a unified computational framework. In the present study, the multi-band PSD representation captures spectral-domain information, the electrode-wise organization preserves spatial-domain characteristics, and the multi-overlap segmentation strategy enhances temporal-domain continuity. Therefore, the proposed multi-band and multi-overlap AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM framework contributes to a more robust multidomain EEG classification system for modeling non-stationary emotion-related EEG patterns.

Experimental results and discussion

Experimental setup and performance metrics

This study evaluates the proposed attention-guided multi-band 2D CNN–BiLSTM framework for EEG-based emotion recognition using the DEAP dataset. The experiments were conducted across different EEG frequency-band representations and temporal overlap configurations to assess the effects of spectral information and segmentation strategy on classification performance.

The evaluation was performed using a trial-level tenfold cross-validation protocol. The dataset was divided into 10 mutually exclusive folds. In each iteration, nine folds were used for training and the remaining one-fold was used for testing. This process was repeated until each fold served once as the test set. Importantly, all segments generated from the same EEG trial were assigned to the same fold to prevent overlap-induced data leakage between training and testing sets. The final reported results were obtained by averaging the performance scores across all 10 folds.

All experiments were implemented in Python using the PyTorch framework and executed in the Kaggle Notebook environment. The computational runtime was configured with an NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPU and 30 GB RAM. The proposed model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001. The batch size was set to 32 and each fold was trained for 100 epochs. Binary cross-entropy loss was used for binary affective classification tasks, while categorical cross-entropy loss was used for four-class emotion classification. The classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score. Overall, the topographic analysis indicates that emotional states are associated with frequency-dependent spatial patterns, where higher-frequency bands exhibit relatively more structured and functionally relevant activity distributions. While hemispheric asymmetry is present to a limited extent, the observed patterns are predominantly distributed, suggesting that the proposed AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM framework effectively leverages both localized and global neural features for robust emotion recognition.

The SHAP summary plots also reveal that both positive and negative contributions are distributed across features, indicating that the model captures complex nonlinear relationships between EEG signals and emotional states. Overall, the SHAP-based analysis provides both global and subject-level interpretability, demonstrating that the model’s predictions are primarily driven by high-frequency EEG features. From a neurophysiological perspective, the prominence of parietal and central regions is consistent with prior studies highlighting their role in attentional processing, sensory integration, and emotional perception. Overall, the SHAP-derived regional analysis provides an interpretable mapping between model features and underlying brain regions, demonstrating that the proposed AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM framework effectively captures spatially meaningful patterns in EEG signals. This analysis complements the spectral and temporal findings presented in earlier sections and further validates the robustness and interpretability of the proposed model.

Statistical analysis of overlap length effects on classification accuracy

To evaluate the statistical significance of performance differences across temporal overlap configurations (0 s, 1 s and 2 s), a statistical analysis was conducted using the classification accuracies reported in Table 3. The independent variable was the overlap length, while the dependent variable was the classification accuracy across different EEG frequency bands and emotion dimensions.

Assumption considerations

Prior to applying parametric statistical analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were considered. Due to the use of aggregated performance metrics (mean ± standard deviation) derived from cross-validation, direct sample-level assumption testing (e.g., Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests) could not be strictly performed. However, given the balanced experimental design and repeated evaluation protocol, the application of parametric analysis is considered appropriate, as it is generally robust to moderate deviations from normality in such settings.

Performance trend analysis

The results indicate that increasing overlap length consistently improves classification accuracy across all frequency bands and emotion dimensions. In particular, the most pronounced improvements are observed in higher-frequency bands, especially beta and gamma, as well as in the multi-band (All Bands) configuration.

For example, in the gamma band, classification accuracy increases from 94.2% (0 s) to 97.2% (2 s) for valence, and from 95.8% to 98.1% for arousal. Similar trends are observed across dominance, liking, and four-class classification tasks. These consistent improvements demonstrate that temporal overlap enhances the model’s ability to capture the non-stationary characteristics of EEG signals.

Non-parametric consideration

Given the absence of fold-wise sample-level data, non-parametric statistical testing (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank test) could not be directly applied. However, the monotonic and consistent improvement in classification performance from 0 to 2 s overlap across multiple frequency bands provides strong empirical support for the robustness of the observed differences.

Effect size interpretation

The magnitude of performance improvements further suggests that the effect of overlap length is practically meaningful. In particular, the substantial gains observed in beta, gamma and combined frequency bands indicate that overlap length plays a significant role in enhancing classification performance rather than contributing marginal improvements.

Overall, the results demonstrate that temporal overlap is a critical factor in EEG-based emotion recognition. Increasing overlap length consistently enhances classification accuracy across multiple frequency bands and emotion dimensions, with the most significant improvements observed in higher-frequency and multi-band representations. These findings confirm that optimal segmentation strategies play an essential role in improving both model performance and robustness.

Cross-dataset validation using the SEED dataset

To further evaluate the robustness and cross-dataset generalization capability of the proposed AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM framework, additional experiments were conducted on the SEED (SJTU Emotion EEG Dataset) [53]. SEED is a widely used benchmark dataset for EEG-based emotion recognition, comprising recordings from 15 subjects across three experimental sessions. In each session, participants viewed emotionally evocative film clips designed to induce three discrete emotional states: positive, neutral, and negative.

EEG signals were acquired using a 62-channel ESI NeuroScan system at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. To ensure consistency with the preprocessing pipeline adopted for the DEAP dataset, the signals were down-sampled to 128 Hz, and 32 EEG channels were selected to match the DEAP channel configuration. From each trial (approximately 4 min in duration), the central 60-s segment was extracted to minimize the influence of initial transient responses and end-of-trial drift, resulting in EEG segments of size 32 × 7680 samples.

The extracted EEG signals were segmented into 3-s frames using three temporal overlap configurations: 0 s, 1 s and 2 s. For each segment, Power Spectral Density (PSD) features were computed using Welch’s method across five standard EEG frequency bands: Delta (0.5–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (8–16 Hz), Beta (16–32 Hz) and Gamma (32–45 Hz), along with a combined band (0.5–45 Hz). These features were then classified using the proposed AFBA-guided CNN–BiLSTM model with the same hyperparameter settings and validation strategy used in the DEAP experiments.

Discussion on task consistency and generalization

It is important to note that the SEED dataset employs a three-class emotion classification framework (positive, neutral, negative), whereas the DEAP dataset supports both binary and four-class emotion representations. Due to these structural differences, a direct comparison of absolute classification accuracy between SEED and DEAP is not strictly appropriate. Instead, the cross-dataset validation in this study focuses on evaluating the consistency of performance trends and feature relevance across datasets. In particular, the proposed framework demonstrates consistent improvements with increasing temporal overlap and similar frequency band importance patterns across both datasets.

Therefore, the generalizability of the proposed architecture is supported not by identical task settings, but by its ability to maintain stable performance behavior and effectively capture discriminative EEG features under different dataset conditions and classification paradigms. Comparative performance of EEG emotion recognition models on DEAP and SEED datasets

The most important comparison in this study is the DEAP four-class emotion recognition task because it represents a more challenging and practically meaningful classification setting than binary valence or arousal recognition. In four-class recognition, the model must distinguish between combined valence–arousal emotional states, namely high-valence high-arousal, high-valence low-arousal, low-valence high-arousal, and low-valence low-arousal. As shown in Table 7, several existing methods either do not report four-class DEAP accuracy or achieve comparatively lower performance. For example, FBCCNN + GAN reports 70.34%, DE-BiLSTM achieves 74.22%, and DWT–ToC–PSO–LSTM reports 82.01% four-class accuracy. A recent CNN + BiLSTM + self-attention model reports a higher four-class accuracy of 87.76 ± 1.02%. In comparison, the proposed AFBA-guided 2D CNN–BiLSTM model achieves 88.9 ± 1.0% four-class accuracy under the best configuration, demonstrating improved multi-state emotion discrimination.

For binary DEAP classification, the proposed method also shows strong performance. Under the best-performing gamma-band configuration with a 3-s frame and 2-s overlap, the proposed model achieves 97.2 ± 0.6% valence accuracy and 98.1 ± 0.5% arousal accuracy. These results outperform traditional recurrent models such as DE-LSTM, DE-BiLSTM, and PSD-LSTM, which report lower binary classification accuracies. They also exceed the performance of PCC-CNN, AD-TCN, STS-Transformer, and the 2D-CNN-LSTM method listed in Table 7. This improvement indicates that the integration of PSD-based spectral representation, adaptive frequency-band attention, convolutional spatial–spectral feature learning, and BiLSTM-based temporal modeling provides a more discriminative representation of emotional EEG patterns.

The comparison between the two proposed rows in Table 7 also provides useful insight. The all-band AFBA-guided configuration achieves 93.5 ± 0.8% valence, 94.6 ± 0.9% arousal, and 82.1 ± 1.2% four-class accuracy, while the gamma-band configuration achieves the highest overall results. This finding suggests that although the multi-band representation captures complementary spectral information, the gamma band contains particularly strong discriminative information for EEG-based emotion recognition. This observation is consistent with the attention-weight and SHAP-based analyses presented in this study, where higher-frequency bands, especially beta and gamma, contributed more strongly to model predictions.

On the SEED dataset, the proposed framework achieves 94.10% accuracy under the best gamma-band configuration, outperforming DE-LSTM and DWT–ToC–PSO–LSTM, which report 90.92% and 90.81%, respectively. The all-band proposed configuration achieves 88.00%, showing that the framework remains effective under a different dataset protocol and emotion-labeling scheme. Since SEED uses three discrete emotional categories, whereas DEAP supports binary and four-class valence–arousal classification, the SEED comparison is primarily used to evaluate the consistency and generalizability of the proposed architecture rather than to make a direct one-to-one comparison with DEAP results.

Overall, the state-of-the-art comparison demonstrates that the proposed AFBA-guided 2D CNN–BiLSTM framework achieves competitive or superior performance across all reported evaluation settings. Most importantly, the proposed method improves the DEAP four-class emotion recognition result compared with several existing methods, which directly addresses the central challenge of multi-state EEG emotion classification. The results also show that the proposed architecture performs strongly in binary valence/arousal recognition and maintains competitive accuracy on SEED. Therefore, the comparative analysis confirms the effectiveness, robustness, and practical relevance of the proposed spatial–spectral–temporal EEG emotion recognition framework.

Conclusion

This study proposed an AFBA-guided multi-band 2D CNN–BiLSTM framework for EEG-based emotion recognition using PSD-based spatial–spectral–temporal representations. The framework combined adaptive frequency-band attention, CNN-based spatial–spectral feature extraction and BiLSTM-based temporal modeling to analyze emotional EEG patterns from the DEAP dataset, with additional validation on the SEED dataset.

The results showed that temporal overlap improved classification performance, with the 2-s overlap configuration producing the strongest results in most cases. Among the EEG frequency bands, beta and gamma bands showed comparatively higher discriminative ability, while the AFBA mechanism helped identify their stronger contribution to emotion recognition. The ablation study further indicated that both temporal modeling and adaptive frequency-band attention contributed to the overall improvement. SHAP-based analysis also provided interpretable evidence regarding the influence of frequency bands and brain regions on model predictions.

Cross-dataset evaluation on SEED showed similar performance trends, particularly the usefulness of temporal overlap and high-frequency EEG components. However, because DEAP and SEED use different emotion-labeling protocols, the results should be interpreted mainly as evidence of trend-level consistency rather than direct dataset-to-dataset superiority.

Despite these promising results, some limitations remain. The framework relies on PSD-based spectral features, which may not fully capture fine-grained transient EEG dynamics. In addition, the experiments were conducted on offline benchmark datasets under controlled conditions, so real-time applicability and robustness in noisy real-world environments require further investigation.

Overall, the proposed framework provides a structured and interpretable approach for EEG-based emotion recognition, showing the potential of adaptive frequency-band attention and temporal overlap analysis for improving spatial–spectral–temporal EEG modeling.
