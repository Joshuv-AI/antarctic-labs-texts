Retrospective analysis of diet and gut microbiota diversity and clinical pharmacology outcomes in patients with Parkinsonism syndrome
Authors: Yunxin Hu, Haoyue Wang, Yongkang Zhong, Yongqi Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) is a global neurodegenerative ailment impacting millions, causes significant degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. Emerging research underscores the gut microbiota's role in PD onset. Yet, investigations in Chinese demographics are lacking. This study thus targets a retrospective examination of gut microbiota variety and clinical aspects in Chinese PD patients.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 50 PD patients' clinical data (admitted May 2021–April 2022) and compared their gut microbiota composition and abundance via 16S rDNA V3-V4 region sequencing against 50 healthy controls. The study also explored links between disease severity and PD patients' gut microbiota.ResultsWe found that the gut microbiota diversity was increased in PD patients. Specifically, phyla Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria were more abundant, whereas Bacteroidetes was less abundant compared to controls. Gut microbiota diversity did not vary between early-vs. late-onset PD, tremor-dominant vs. non-tremor-dominant, or constipation-associated vs. non-constipation-associated subtypes. The abundance of Firmicutes was positively correlated with UPDRS (Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale) III score, NMSS (Non-Motor Symptoms Scale) score, Wexner score, and PDQ-39 (Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire-39) score; the abundance of Actinobacteria was positively correlated with UPDRS III, NMSS, Wexner, and PDQ-39 scores; and the abundance of Bacteroidetes was negatively correlated with UPDRS III, NMSS, Wexner, and PDQ-39 scores. At the family level, the abundance of Bifidobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae was positively correlated with UPDRS III, NMSS, and PDQ-39 scores.ConclusionCompared with healthy individuals, PD patients have increased gut microbiota diversity, and the abundance of Bifidobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae is associated with the severity of both motor and non-motor symptoms in PD patients.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, Gut microbiota, Motor symptoms, Non-motor symptoms

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a usual chronic neurodegenerative disease in the elderly, characterized by substantial disintegration and loss of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons, deposition of alpha-synuclein (α-SYN) and formation of Lewy bodies (LB) [1]. The dual communication between the central nervous system (CNS) and the enteric nervous system (ENS) + autonomic nervous system (ANS) of the gastrointestinal tract is called the gut-brain axis, promoted by neural, hormonal, and immune pathways [2]. This complex system allows for information exchange between the gut and brain, affecting gastrointestinal function and central nervous activity. Recent studies have shown that the interaction between the gut-brain axis is regulated by the gut microbiota, which contribute to the pathogenesis and/or progression of many neurodevelopmental, neuropsychiatric, and neurological conditions [3,4]. The gut microbiota has a profound impact on host neural activity by producing various neurotransmitters and hormones, including monoamines, GABA, and short chain fatty acids. They not only regulate neural transmission, but also affect the activation of central nervous system glial cells, the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress levels. These mechanisms play a crucial role in the progression of neurodegenerative diseases in PD, highlighting the indirect but significant impact of gut microbiota on the disease [5,6]. Some researchers have proposed the gut origin theory of PD, suggesting that PD begins in the gastrointestinal neurons and gradually progresses to the CNS, leading to damage of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and the emergence of a series of motor and non-motor symptoms [7,8]. Numerous studies abroad have confirmed changes in the composition of the gut microbiota in PD patients compared to healthy individuals, and their metabolic functions impact the activity of the gut and CNS [[9], [10], [11]]. However, there is limited research on this aspect in China. Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzes the diversity of gut microbiota and clinical outcomes in PD patients, aiming to provide a scientific basis for the pathophysiology of PD and offer new insights into its treatment.

Materials and methods

Study population

Clinical data of 50 PD patients admitted to our hospital from May 2021 to April 2022 were retrospectively analyzed as the PD group, while stool specimens of 50 healthy individuals undergoing physical examinations during the same period at our hospital were retrospectively analyzed as the control group. PD group inclusion criteria: all patients met the 2015 MDS clinical diagnostic criteria for definite or probable Parkinson's disease. Exclusion criteria: Exclusion of secondary Parkinson's syndrome, such as post-traumatic, drug-induced, post-encephalitic, vascular, etc.; Exclusion of Parkinson's plus syndromes, such as multiple system atrophy, striatonigral degeneration, progressive supranuclear palsy, and frontotemporal dementia, etc.; Exclusion of patients who could not complete scale assessments due to psychiatric disorders or severe cognitive impairment; Exclusion of those with acute or chronic gastrointestinal diseases or those who had taken steroids, immunosuppressants, antibiotics, or microbiota modulators in the past month. Control group collection: Healthy individuals or patients' relatives who underwent medical examinations at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University from March 2018 to November 2018 were gathered for the control group; all individuals in the control group were matched with the PD group based on age (±5 years), gender, and dietary habits.

Clinical presentation classification

The Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) Part III was utilized to assess motor symptoms; the modified Hoehn-Yahr (H-Y) staging criteria were used to stage the severity of clinical symptoms; the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) was employed to evaluate the degree and frequency of non-motor symptoms; the Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) was used to assess quality of life; patients were categorized into constipation and non-constipation groups based on the presence of constipation, and constipation was evaluated according to the Rome III functional constipation diagnostic criteria, with severity assessed using the Wexner score. Patients were divided into early-onset (≤50 years) and late-onset (>50 years) groups [12]. Based on the improved H-Y staging, patients were grouped into mild (stages 1–1.5), moderate (stages 2–2.5), and severe (stages 3–4) groups [13]. Tremor score was calculated as the average of item 16 (Resting tremor of hands) in Part II and items 20 (Action tremor of hands) and 21 (Rigidity) in Part III of the UPDRS. The non-tremor score was calculated as the average of items 5 (Speech), 7 (Rigidity), 12–15 (Posture, Gait, Postural Instability, Freezing), and 18–19 (Bradykinesia of face, Bradykinesia of arms), and 22–31 (Functional tests) in Parts II and III of the UPDRS. The presence of tremor-dominant or non-tremor-dominant subtypes was determined based on whether the ratio of tremor score to non-tremor score was greater than 1 [14]. This scoring method was chosen to specifically isolate tremor-related symptoms from other motor symptoms for a more nuanced analysis of PD motor phenotype.

Stool specimen collection

The researchers assisted patients in performing a specific procedure for stool sample collection. They selected a clean squat toilet or regular toilet and rinsed it thoroughly. Prior to defecation, the bladder was emptied, and subsequently, two layers of clean toilet paper were placed in the toilet bowl. The stool was then passed onto the prepared tissue.Next, the package was opened, and disposable gloves were put on. The sampling protection device (Loogsee fecal microbiome genomic preservation kit) was removed. The collection tube was opened, and a piece of stool, roughly the size of a soybean and taken from the center of the fecal mass, was collected. This sample was reinserted into the tube, and the cap was tightly sealed.Holding the collection tube, the thumb was used to press the middle of the liquid collecting tube near the breakable rod. This caused the rod inside the liquid collecting tube to break along its base, exposing a small hole. Gently squeezing the liquid collecting tube allowed the preservation solution to flow into the tube containing the stool.All the liquid from the liquid collecting tube was squeezed into the lower tube. The collection tube was gently shaken to separate the fecal sample in the concave groove of the sampling head and mix it with the preservation solution, turning the liquid in the tube turbid.Finally, the sample was stored at −80 °C to preserve the genomic material within the fecal microbiome.

DNA extraction, quality control, and sequencing

Sample extraction and sequencing were commissioned to Guangzhou KingMed Diagnostics Group Co., Ltd. Total DNA extraction was carried out on the basis of the E.Z.N.A.® Soil Kit (Catalog No. 51504, Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using NanoDrop 2000, and DNA extraction quality was determined via 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3V4 region was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) with the following expansion program: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min (PCR instrument: ABI GeneAmp® 9700). The amplification system consisted of 20 μl, including 4 μl of 5∗FastPfu buffer, 2 μl of 2.5 mmol/L dNTPs, 0.8 μl of primer (5 μmol/L), 0.4 μl of FastPfu polymerase, and 10 ng DNA template. Sequencing was carried out using the Illumina Miseq PE300 platform.

Data processing

Raw sequences (in FASTQ format) were split based on the barcodes information (unique to each sample) using the QIIME2 demux plugin. The split sequences underwent quality control, trimming (parameters: p-trunc-len-f 290, -p-trunc-len-r 256, -p-trim-left-f 26, -p-trim-left-r 26), denoising, and merging using the QIIME2 dada2 plugin, resulting in the final sOTUs (sub-operational-taxonomic-units). Subsequently, the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin aligned the representative sequences of sOTUs to the pre-trained 99 % similarity 13-8 version of the GREENGENES database (trimmed to the V3V4 region based on the 338F/806R primer) to obtain species classification information. Next, the QIIME2 feature-table plugin removed all contaminating mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences. The QIIME2 core-diversity plugin was then used to calculate diversity matrices, and the emperor plugin was utilized for visualization. Finally, methods including ANCOM, ANOVA, Kruskal Wallis, LEfSe, and DEseq2 were employed to identify differentially abundant bacteria between groups and samples.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was analyzed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software, and the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess normality for each parameter. For parametric data conforming to a normal distribution, mean ± standard deviation(‾χ±s) was used for presentation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using t-tests. Non-parametric data not conforming to a normal distribution were described using median (quartiles) and intergroup comparisons were conducted using non-parametric tests. Intergroup comparisons of all count data were carried out using the chi-squared test. Pearson correlation analysis was used for parametric bivariate normally distributed data, while Spearman correlation analysis was used for other data types. A import level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For microbiota analysis, the LEfSe test (combining non-parametric testing and linear discriminant analysis) was employed to assess differences in relative abundance of microbiota between different groups. A linear discriminant analysis (log10 LDA) score greater than 2 was considered statistically significant with a significance level of P < 0.05.αdiversity was evaluated using indices such as observed-OTU, Shannon, Faith's phylogenetic diversity (Faith-PD), and Chao1. Intergroup comparisons of these indices were conducted using the Wilcoxon test, with a significance level of P < 0.05 considered as statistically significant. Spearman correlation analysis was applied to assess the correlation between clinical phenotype data and microbiota, with a significance level of P < 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline data between the two groups

There were 28 male and 22 female patients in the PD group, within an age range of 50–68 years and a mean age of (59.24 ± 4.32) years. The body mass index (BMI) ranged from 19.5 to 26.9 kg/m2, with a mean of (23.45 ± 2.12) kg/m2. The disease duration ranged from 1 to 8 years, with a mean of (3.90 ± 1.04) years. The Hoehn-Yahr (H-Y) stage ranged from 1.5 to 2.0, with a mean of (1.75 ± 0.20). The UPDRS III scores ranged from 7 to 28, with a mean of (16.30 ± 6.48). There were 8 patients with motor fluctuations, and 3 patients with dyskinesia. Additionally, 15 patients had concomitant constipation, 22 were tremor-dominant, and 28 were non-tremor-dominant. In the control group, there were 29 male and 21 female participants, with an age range of 52–65 years and a mean age of (59.60 ± 4.48) years. The BMI ranged from 19.8 to 26.5 kg/m2, with a mean of (22.98 ± 2.05) kg/m2. There were no statistically meaningful differences in baseline characteristics, including gender, age, and BMI, between the two groups (P < 0.05), indicating comparability between the groups (Table 1).

Gut microbiota and clinical presentations in PD

Using different classification methods, PD patients were clinically categorized to analyze the differences in gut microbiota composition associated with different clinical presentations. The results showed that there were no significant diversities in gut microbiota diversity indices, including observed-OTUs, Chao1, Shannon, and Faith-PD, among patients with constipation vs. non-constipation, tremor-dominant vs. non-tremor-dominant, early-onset vs. late-onset, presence of motor complications vs. absence of motor complications, and between male and female patients (Wilcoxon test, all P > 0.05). This suggests that gut microbiota is not associated with the aforementioned clinical subtypes in PD patients.

Gut microbiota and correlation with various scores

At the phylum level, the abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria was positively correlated with UPDRS III score, NMSS score, Wexner score, and PDQ-39 score (all P < 0.05); whereas the abundance of Bacteroidetes was negatively correlated with UPDRS III score, NMSS score, Wexner score, and PDQ-39 score (all P < 0.05, see Table 6). At the family level, the abundances of Bifidobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae were positively correlated with UPDRS III score, NMSS score, and PDQ-39 score (P < 0.05, see Table 7), indicating a significant correlation between gut microbiota and the severity of PD in patients.

Discussion

According to the Braak pathological staging, the PD lesion may start earliest in the olfactory bulb and the enteric nervous system [15]. So far, studies have shown that the pathological changes in PD may involve the gut-brain axis through alterations in the gut microbiota [16]. Changes in the gut microbiota lead to misfolding of α-synuclein (α-SYN). These pathogenic elements are absorbed by the intestinal mucosa through the enteric nervous system, then retrogradely transmitted via the vagus nerve into the brainstem and the cerebrum, resulting in damage to the dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [16]. Furthermore, the gut microbiota may directly influence the host's neural activity by producing hormones and neurotransmitters that interact with the host's nervous system, such as monoamines, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and short-chain fatty acids. This can bring about central nervous system glial cell activation, the generation of pro-inflammatory signaling molecules, and oxidative stress, which may be significant mechanisms contributing to the neurodegenerative process in PD [17].

Previous research have demonstrated the relationship between the gut microbiota and PD. TAKAHASHI K et al. [18] found that in PD patients, there was a significant increase in the abundance of Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia, while the abundance of Firmicutes significantly decreased. At the genus level, the abundance of Akkermansia (Verrucomicrobia, Verrucomicrobiae), Lactococcus (Firmicutes, Bacilli), and Bifidobacterium (Actinobacteria, Actinobacteria) significantly increased. In our study, we found that some changes in the microbiota at the phylum level were similar to their findings. We observed a significant increase in Proteobacteria and an increase in the abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, while the abundance of Bacteroidetes significantly decreased. At the genus level, the abundance of Lachnospira and Bifidobacterium notably increased. In the study by LIU XR et al. [14], PD patients were more likely to have small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and PD patients with such overgrowth had a higher incidence of non-motor symptoms, with a decreased abundance of Prevotella. Prevotella is a symbiont in the colon that not only reduces plant polysaccharides, mucins, and glycoproteins in the intestinal mucosal layer but also interacts with the immune system. It is associated with the levels of short-chain fatty acids, as well as the biosynthesis of thiamine and folate. The decrease of Prevotella leads to a reduction in the synthesis of intestinal mucins, increased mucosal permeability, local or systemic exposure to bacterial toxins, and an increase in colonic α-synuclein expression. However, in our study, we found a decrease in the abundance of Prevotellaceae in PD patients, but the difference was not statistically significant. KUAI XY et al. [19] found a significant decrease in Firmicutes, and an increase in Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia, although these differences were not statistically significant; at the family level, they found a decrease in Helicobacteraceae and an increase in Veillonellaceae and Verrucomicrobiaceae. AHO VTE et al. [20] found a significant decrease in Actinobacteria and a significant increase in Proteobacteria; at the family level, they found a significant increase in Streptococcaceae and Enterobacteriaceae in the feces of PD patients. Our findings shed light on the intricate relationship between gut microbiota alterations and the vagus nerve, a crucial mediator in the gut-brain axis. The vagus nerve serves as a direct conduit for information transfer between the gut and the brain, playing a pivotal role in the modulation of neural activity and the propagation of pathogenic factors [21]. The observed changes in gut microbiota composition, particularly the significant increase in the abundance of Sphingomonadaceae (Sphingomone), Enterobacteriaceae, Streptococcaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Oxalobacteraceae, and Acetobacteraceae (Acetobacter), while the abundance of Veillonellaceae decreased in the PD group, suggest a potential mechanism for the dysregulation of the vagus nerve. These alterations could lead to enhanced vagal signaling, promoting the retrograde transport of misfolded α-synuclein and other pathogenic elements from the gut to the central nervous system. This process may contribute to the neurodegeneration observed in PD, particularly affecting dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [22].

In the human body, the dominant phyla in the gut microbiota are Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, followed by Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria [23]. The composition of gut microbiota is closely related to dietary habits in different regions. Studies have shown [24] that individuals with a predominantly whole grain-based diet have significantly higher levels of Bifidobacterium in the gut compared to those with a diet primarily based on wheat and rice. Bifidobacterium belongs to the phylum Actinobacteria. Our study confirms elevated gut microbiota diversity in PD patients versus healthy controls, echoing prior findings linking high microbial diversity to immunomodulation [25]. Yet, in PD, this diversity has a complex effect on immunity. Beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacterium may reduce inflammation and fortify the gut barrier through SCFA production, which also aids in T cell regulation [26,27]. Conversely, excessive Enterobacteriaceae and Porphyromonadaceae correlate with PD severity, promoting inflammation and possibly neuroinflammation via gut permeability issues [28]. Thus, while high microbiota diversity is usually advantageous, its mixed impact in PD reveals the microbiome's intricate role and calls for tailored interventions. However, it is worth noting that the relationship between gut microbiota diversity and Parkinson's disease is complex and multifaceted. The increase in diversity of PD patients may reflect changes in compensation mechanisms or host microbiota interactions, rather than the direct cause of the disease The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating functions such as the immune system, nutrient absorption, and metabolism. In healthy individuals, a diverse microbiome is often considered beneficial as it supports a stable ecosystem, can resist pathogen colonization, and contributes to overall health. However, in the context of PD, the specific composition of the microbiota, rather than just its diversity, seems to be more relevant to disease pathology. For example, the over representation of certain bacterial families (such as Enterobacteriaceae) and the under representation of other bacterial families (such as Prevotellaceae) have been associated with PD([29]). In addition, environmental and genetic factors, age, diet, and lifestyle choices can significantly affect gut microbiota composition and Parkinson's disease risk. Therefore, the diversity of gut microbiota must be considered in the broader context of individual health status and disease susceptibility.

Previous studies [30] have found correlations between Prevotellaceae and UPDRS-III scores, with a higher abundance of Enterobacteriaceae observed in PD lpatients exhibiting predominantly postural and gait disturbances. YOGESH B et al. [31] found increased abundances of Bifidobacterium and Enterobacteriaceae in PD patients, but no significant association was observed between Enterobacteriaceae and clinical phenotypes. However, in our study, correlation analyses revealed positive correlations between the abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria at the phylum level and UPDRS-III, NMSS, Wexner, and PDQ-39 scores. Conversely, the abundance of Bacteroidetes at the phylum level exhibited negative correlations with these clinical scores. At the family level, the abundances of Bifidobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae were positively correlated with UPDRS-III, NMSS, and PDQ-39 scores. The discrepancies with previous research findings may be attributed to the relatively small sample size in our study, causing differences in certain microbiota changes compared to prior studies. Furthermore, it is worth noting that the observed increase in the abundance of Porphyromonadaceae within the phylum Bacteroidetes in the Parkinson's group, juxtaposed with a decrease in Bacteroidetes at the phylum level, underscores the complexity of gut microbiota alterations in PD. This discrepancy suggests that shifts within a phylum can occur at the family or genus level, independent of overall phylum abundance. The enrichment of Porphyromonadaceae may reflect a compensatory mechanism or a specific response to PD pathophysiology. This family includes several genera known for their involvement in the breakdown of complex carbohydrates and production of short-chain fatty acids, which can influence gut permeability and inflammation, potentially exacerbating PD symptoms. The differential abundance at lower taxonomic levels highlights the importance of detailed microbiome profiling to understand the nuanced roles of gut bacteria in PD.

While our primary focus has been on the correlation between gut microbiota and motor symptoms in PD, it is imperative to acknowledge the broader impact on patient health, including mental well-being. PD is associated with a range of non-motor symptoms, including depression, anxiety, and cognitive decline, which significantly affect the quality of life of patients [32]. The gut microbiota-gut-brain axis hypothesis posits that alterations in the gut microbiome can influence mood and cognition through various pathways, including the production of neurotransmitters, modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and alteration of the immune response [21]. Given the observed changes in gut microbiota composition in our study, there is a possibility that these alterations may also contribute to the mental health challenges faced by PD patients. However, it must be acknowledged that this study has limitations in terms of patients' mental health. Due to our current admission approval scope, we are unable to collect data on the mental health status of PD patients, which limits our ability to analyze the impact of gut microbiota on mental health outcomes. To address these limitations, future research should conduct longitudinal studies that track changes in gut microbiota over time and investigate their impact on the mental health outcomes of PD patients. In addition, integrating genetic data can help determine which patient subgroups may benefit the most from interventions based on target microbiota.

Conclusion

In summary, compared to healthy individuals, PD patients exhibit increased diversity in gut microbiota. The abundances of Bifidobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae are related to the severity of both motor and non-motor symptoms in PD patients. However, it is important to note that the gut microbiome is influenced by a multitude of factors, including diet, medication use, and environmental exposures, which can also vary between individuals. Therefore, while our findings suggest a potential link between gut microbiota and PD, further research is needed to disentangle the complex interplay between these factors and to establish causality. The clinical application of fecal microbiota transplantation, despite its potential, remains limited due to the lack of standardized protocols and sufficient evidence-based support. More research is needed to support these conclusions, potentially offering a new approach for PD treatment in the future.
