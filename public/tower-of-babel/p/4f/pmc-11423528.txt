Optimizing anesthesia management based on early identification of electroencephalogram burst suppression risk in non-cardiac surgery patients: a visualized dynamic nomogram
Authors: Jian Chen, Wanxia Li, Qianping Chen, Zhou Zhou, Chen Chen, Yuping Hu, Yanna Si, Jianjun Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurst suppression (BS) is a specific electroencephalogram (EEG) pattern that may contribute to postoperative delirium and negative outcomes. Few prediction models of BS are available and some factors such as frailty and intraoperative hypotension (IOH) which have been reported to promote the occurrence of BS were not included. Therefore, we look forward to creating a straightforward, precise, and clinically useful prediction model by incorporating new factors, such as frailty and IOH.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively collected 540 patients and analyzed the data from 418 patients. Univariate analysis and backward stepwise logistic regression were used to select risk factors to develop a dynamic nomogram model, and then we developed a web calculator to visualize the process of prediction. The performance of the nomogram was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility.ResultsAccording to the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the nomogram showed good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.933) and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test demonstrated the nomogram had good calibration (p = 0.0718). Age, Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) score, midazolam dose, propofol induction dose, total area under the hypotensive threshold of mean arterial pressure (MAP_AUT), and cerebrovascular diseases were the independent risk predictors of BS and used to construct nomogram. The web-based dynamic nomogram calculator was accessible by clicking on the URL: https://eegbsnomogram.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/ or scanning a converted Quick Response (QR) code.ConclusionsIncorporating two distinctive new risk factors, frailty and IOH, we firstly developed a visualized nomogram for accurately predicting BS in non-cardiac surgery patients. The model is expected to guide clinical decision-making and optimize anesthesia management.Keywords: Burst suppression, non-cardiac surgery, intraoperative hypotension, frailty, dynamic nomogram, prediction

Introduction

Burst suppression (BS) is a specific electroencephalogram (EEG) pattern characterized by alternating high-amplitude oscillations of EEG activity with relatively low-amplitude oscillations or even isoelectric lines [1–3]. According to the different subgroups of each study, the incidence of intraoperative BS in adult patients undergoing general anesthesia is approximately 5.6% to 71.4% [4–6]. BS occurring during ­general anesthesia often reflects excessively deep anesthesia [7], cerebral fragility [6], insufficient cerebral perfusion [8], severe acute brain injury [9], etc. Studies showed that BS is an independent risk factor for postoperative delirium(POD), which is significantly linked to negative outcomes like higher mortality and decreased functional independence [10–13]. Predictive modeling facilitates early identification and intervention in patients at risk for BS and may improve their prognosis. Nonetheless, few studies on BS prediction models are available.

To our best knowledge, only one prediction model with moderate accuracy (AUC = 0.84) has been constructed for predicting the occurrence of BS in cardiac surgery patients [7], but not non-cardiac surgery patients. The model was based on a static model developed with multivariate logistic regression, without flexible and visualized presentation. Furthermore, most studies solely explored the risk factors for BS and did not create prediction models in non-cardiac surgery patients. In addition to some recognized factors such as age and anesthetic drug dosage, frailty and intraoperative hypotension (IOH) have been reported to be associated with BS [8,14]. However, whether and to what extent the further inclusion of frailty and IOH can improve the performance of the prediction model with BS has not yet been clarified.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore a visualized dynamic nomogram for BS in non-cardiac surgery patients under general anesthesia. We look forward to creating a straightforward, precise, and clinically useful prediction model by incorporating new factors, frailty, and IOH. This model will serve as more clinical guidance for promoting individualized anesthesia, minimizing adverse postoperative effects associated with anesthesia, and improving anesthesia protocols.

Materials and methods

Study population

In this retrospective study, we collected 540 patients who underwent general anesthesia for non-cardiac surgery at the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University from June 2021 to April 2023. Following strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, we ended up with data from 418 patients for the study. Besides, we used R version 4.2.2 statistical software to generate random sequences and randomly assigned the data in a 3:1 ratio using a random seed, which was done to create a training and a testing cohort for constructing and evaluating the model, respectively (Figure 1).

Flow chart of patient enrollment in this study.

Participants were eligible for inclusion in this study if they 1) were at least age 18 years; 2) underwent general anesthesia for elective non-cardiac surgery; 3) had American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I-III; 4) used the Sedline EEG monitors intraoperatively to guide the depth of anesthesia. Participants were excluded if they 1) had a history of general anesthesia within two months before surgery; 2) had the type of surgery for craniocerebral trauma or carotid artery; 3) with severe cardiac, cerebral, pulmonary, renal or other important organs insufficiency; 4) with polytrauma combined with craniocerebral trauma. Besides, some participants were not included due to serious data missing or their EEG data were not recorded before the induction of anesthesia. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, with the need for individual patient consent waived. [document number: 20220222-K015].

Procedures

Patients were given electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure monitoring after admission to the operating room. Electroencephalographic electrodes were positioned on the patient’s forehead before induction of anesthesia, and the EEG monitor (Sedline, Masimo Corporation, USA) was used for real-time EEG monitoring of patients. Patients received midazolam, sufentanil, propofol, etomidate, or cis-atracurium for induction of anesthesia and assisted mechanical ventilation with the tracheal intubation or laryngeal mask. During the operation, anesthesia was maintained with propofol, remifentanil, and cis-atracurium, and sevoflurane was used in combination, depending on the circumstances. The Patient State Index (PSI) was monitored continuously, the depth of anesthesia was regulated (PSI value 25-50), and controlled the fluctuation of intraoperative blood pressure was at ±20% of baseline as possible. Phenylephrine or norepinephrine was used when IOH occurred (when blood pressure decreased more than 20% of baseline, or mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 mmHg [15,16]), and urapidil or nitroglycerin was used when hypertension occurred (when blood pressure increased more than 20% of baseline or MAP exceeded basal blood pressure by 20 mmHg [17]).

Data collection

We collected the patient’s demographic data and medical history, including age, gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, etc. Routine preoperative laboratory parameters such as serum creatinine (Cr), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), blood potassium, and calcium levels. Data on inpatient treatment and clinical evaluation, such as duration of IOH, use of medications during surgery, etc. Some scores and grade indicators commonly used in medicine, such as the clinical frailty scale (CFS) score and ASA classification, were also included. In addition, to assess the severity of the IOH of patients individually, we used a MAP 20% below baseline MAP as the threshold for each patient. We combined the cumulative time and MAP depth below this threshold during anesthesia. Finally, we calculated the total area under the hypotensive threshold of mean arterial pressure (MAP_AUT) according to the trapezoidal rule [18,19].

The above data were collected by clinicians well-versed in anesthetized patients’ surgical procedures to ensure correct data entry.

Patient outcome

In this study, the outcome we predicted was the occurrence of BS in patients during general anesthesia procedures. Based on previous study and clinical practice in the field of anesthesia, patients who experienced BS were defined as those who had at least one episode of burst suppression ratio (BSR) value >10% lasting more than 1 min consecutively during maintenance of anesthesia [20] in order to cover more patients with intraoperative EEG suppression at risk of deep anesthesia. According to this definition, patients were divided into an EEG burst suppression group (BS group) and a non-EEG burst suppression group (non-BS group).

Statistical analysis

This study used the statistical software R version 4.2.2 (copyright license obtained) and SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) (copyright license obtained) for statistical analysis and model visualization. Firstly, we used the Shapiro–Wilk test to confirm whether the continuous data was by the normal distribution. According to the Shapiro–Wilk test outcomes, continuous variables were described as means with standard deviation or median value with an interquartile range. Categorical variables were reported as the number and percentage of events occurring. Secondly, differences in continuous variables between the two groups were assessed using the t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U-test if data were not normally distributed. As for categorical variables, we used the χ2-test, χ2-test (with Yate’s continuation correction), or Fisher’s exact test to compare the differences between the two groups. Finally, the probability value <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Predictors selection and construction of nomogram

Variables statistically significant in the univariate analysis were retained and incorporated into the backward stepwise logistic regression for further selection and then constructed the nomogram prediction model. To improve the predictive performance, we also estimated the multicollinearity of the selected variables using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and VIF < 5 was considered insignificant [21]. The logistic regression prediction model was constructed by integrating the selected final variables through the ‘rms’ package in the statistical software R.

This traditional nomogram gives each independent factor an initial visual score ranging from 0-100. The score corresponding to each element can be obtained by drawing a line from the position on the correlation axis to the score axis. Then the scores of each factor are summed to get the total score, and finally, the risk of occurrence of BS is obtained by converting the total score. Besides, we also constructed a dynamic nomogram to make this process more convenient and easier to use.

Validation of nomogram

We assessed the predictive performance of the developed model mainly in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. The discrimination of the model describes the ability to distinguish between patients with negative and positive outcomes, which we expressed through the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), which is also often used as the main indicator of model performance [22]. Calibration is an indication of the ability to predict actual patient outcomes. We used the Hosmer-Lemeshow test to assess the model’s calibration and the calibration belt plot [23,24] to graphically represent the model’s goodness of fit, together with its statistical confidence.

Finally, we used decision curve analysis (DCA) [25] and clinical impact curve (CIC) [26] to prove the clinical utility of the nomogram prediction model. In addition, characteristics such as specificity and sensitivity were also calculated.

Validation of nomogram

According to the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the nomogram showed good discriminative ability both in training (AUC = 0.911; 95% CI, 0.875 − 0.948) (Figure 5A) and testing cohort (AUC = 0.933; 95% CI, 0.872 − 0.995) (Figure 5B). Calibration belts were plotted in the training (Figure 5C) and testing (Figure 5D) cohorts, and the Hosmer – Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test demonstrated the nomogram had good calibration in the training (χ2 = 3.913, p = 0.865) and testing (χ2 = 14.407, p = 0.0718) sets, which indicated that the nomogram predicted probabilities of occurrence of BS was in good agreement with the actual possibilities.

Calibration belt plots of the nomogram in training cohort (C) and testing cohort (D) at 80% (light shaded area) and 95% (dark shaded area) confidence levels (bisector, dashed line is a reference line that shows perfect prediction). Besides, the results of DCA in the testing set implied that using the nomogram to predict burst suppression would generate more net benefit than the ‘treat all’ or ‘treat none’ strategies when the threshold probabilities ranged from 3% to 88% (Figure S1). In this example, if a 40% risk threshold was selected, this meant that patients would receive some therapeutic interventions if the probability of burst suppression was >40%. The net benefit was about 0.16. The CIC in the testing set showed the true number and the expected number of positive individuals as predicted by the nomogram at each risk threshold (Figure S2). For example, if a 40% risk threshold was used, 1000 patients would be screened, and then 215 patients would be predicted as being high risk, but about 154 patients were actually high risk. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, we firstly developed a dynamic nomogram model that can early and precise identification of the risk of intraoperative BS in patients undergoing general anesthesia for non-cardiac surgery to optimize clinical anesthesia management protocols. Incorporating six independent risk factors, our nomogram shows highly reliable performance with an AUC of 0.933, model sensitivity of 95.83%, and specificity of 80.25%.

Notably, in our model, the MAP_AUT as a measure of IOH influenced the occurrence of intraoperative BS. Intuitively, abnormal intraoperative blood pressure has magnitude and time or duration components [18]. In contrast to disregarding the extent to which blood pressure was below the hypotensive threshold and counting only the duration of IOH, we used MAP_AUT to measure the severity of IOH was more convincing. According to our findings, the larger the MAP_AUT, the higher the probability of intraoperative BS. However, the duration of IOH was not independently associated with BS. Similar to our results, Willingham et al. [5] found that the duration of low MAP (<55 mmHg) did not affect the incidence of BS, but BS may contribute to postoperative mortality in conjunction with low MAP. Therefore, a comprehensive IOH assessment becomes even more necessary. Blood pressure is an important determinant of organ perfusion [27]. IOH has been associated with poor postoperative outcomes (e.g. neurocognitive impairment, acute kidney injury, myocardial injury) [27,28], as well as increased postoperative mortality [18]. Low circulating blood pressure may cause cerebral hypoperfusion and impact the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO), and a decrease in CMRO corresponds to BS [2]. Based on EEG monitoring, Georaii et al. [8] intervened in blood pressure and anesthetic depth, the outcomes dramatically reduced the total cumulative burst suppression ratio (BSR), BSR duration, and maximum BSR. Interestingly, at the same time, cerebrovascular disease entered the model. Individuals with a history of cerebrovascular disease were more likely to have an intraoperative outbreak of BS than those without. The findings are not speculative; they’re clearly stated. Studies have shown that cerebral autoregulation (CA) impairment can be caused by cerebrovascular diseases such as traumatic brain injury, ischemic stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage [29]. Such patients are more sensitive to IOH and are more likely to suffer from cerebral hypoperfusion, resulting in a lower CMRO and thus increased incidence of BS. Goal-oriented blood pressure management is crucial for the functional protection of the brain and other essential organs during the perioperative period.

In addition, frailty is an important independent risk factor not present in previous studies. Age-related frailty is a clinical state of impaired homeostasis in response to routine or stress, which manifests the cumulative decline in the function of multiple physiological systems [30]. The visual chart-based CFS score is more appropriate for clinical screening and assessment than other scoring criteria for clinical frailty indices, such as FRAIL, CFS, FI-CD, etc [31]. Preoperative fragility is correlated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality and various adverse long-term consequences, including disability, falls, and a decline in quality of life [32–34]. Compared to patients without frailty, those with frailty had a longer duration of BS and a higher incidence of delirium, according to one investigation [14]. Patients who are fragile or at risk of becoming frail have a 2.7 times greater incidence of POD [33]. Pedemonte et al. [4] observed that BS during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) mediates the relationship between changes in body function and the development of POD. Also, lower albumin levels and higher free propofol levels in fragile patients may be correlated with the patients’ increased sensitivity to propofol. Alerting us that modified anesthetic management may reduce the occurrence of EEG BS in frail individuals [14].

Consistent with the previous findings, the incidence of BS increases with higher induction dosages of anesthesia drugs (e.g. propofol, midazolam, etc.) or older age. Both midazolam and propofol are classic γ-GABAA receptor agonists that inhibit many excitatory pyramidal neurons, which widely lowers metabolic activity across the cortical region and induces unconsciousness [35]. High doses of γ-GABAA receptor agonists will further amplify the inhibitory effects on cortical metabolic activity and contribute to BS. Additionally, the elderly are more vulnerable to BS [5,20,36,37]. Based on literature reports, patients over 60 had a 5.31 times higher chance of suffering BS than younger ones [5]. During general anesthesia, EEG power in all frequency bands was significantly reduced in elderly patients compared to young adults, with a more pronounced reduction in the alpha band [37]. The chance of BS increased 1.33 times for every dB fall in alpha power [36]. Thus, it is necessary to optimize anesthetic protocols for elderly individuals. The increased incidence of BS due to excessive doses of propofol and midazolam can be avoided by combining different anesthetic drugs during the induction of general anesthesia.

It is worth mentioning that our study constructed a dynamic nomogram, which overcame the static limitation of the traditional nomogram, and not only realized the visualization of the model but also made it easier to be popularized and used in the clinic. In addition, our dynamic nomogram can reduce the possibility of intraoperative BS to some extent. For example, when anesthesiologists enter different induction doses of propofol and midazolam into the system, the model will automatically calculate the accurate value of unfavorable outcome risk with 95% CI, thus assisting them in optimizing and adjusting the anesthesia protocol in a timely (Figure 6).

The risk of BS predicted by the dynamic nomogram in a patient aged 65 years with a CFS score of 5, no history of cerebrovascular disease, intraoperative blood pressure is well maintained with the midazolam dose of 2 mg, and the propofol induction dose of 2 mg/kg (a); the midazolam dose of 1 mg and the propofol induction dose of 2 mg/kg (B); the midazolam dose of 1.5 mg and the propofol induction dose of 1.5 mg/kg (C).

Several limitations to this article remain. First of all, this is a single-center study. Nevertheless, our findings provide a foundation for a multicenter collaborative effort to produce generalizable clinical prediction models. Secondly, our study did not investigate the correlation between BS and POD; further in-depth research will be conducted in the future. Thirdly, this study used PSI for intraoperative BS monitoring, and it might not completely cover the whole brain signal observation compared with standard IFCN 20-10 EEG. Fourthly, it must be acknowledged that non-blinding of PSI readings due to prevent intraoperative awareness remains unavoidable. As well, this is an unresolvable and inherent limitation that exists in most studies. Fifthly, we did not solely rely on the description of BS in the ACNS guidelines, which are widely recognized in the field of neurophysiology [38,39]. However, it may be reasonable to consider this approach acceptable due to the variations between different subject areas. Finally, no external validation was performed in our study. Therefore, further work should include collecting data from multiple centers to improve the reliability of clinical practice.

Conclusions

As the first visualized dynamic nomogram to predict the risk of BS in non-cardiac surgery patients under general anesthesia, incorporating two distinctive new risk factors, frailty and IOH, this model shows excellent performance. Our model can assist clinicians in optimizing anesthesia management by estimating the risk of BS. Additionally, we would also deploy prospective multicenter studies for external validation in the future to enhance the promotion and application value of our research findings.
