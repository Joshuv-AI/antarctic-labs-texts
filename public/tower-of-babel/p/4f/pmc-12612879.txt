Integrated Experimental and Computational Study of a Novel Schiff Base (E)‑N‑(1-Methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine as a Potent Inhibitor for Low-Carbon Steel Corrosion in Sulfuric Acid Medium
Authors: Huda Alqahtani, Sara M. Meleek, Mohamed A. Hegazy, Salah M. Rashwan, Raiedhah A. Alsaiari, Nasser Y. Mostafa, Medhat M. Kamel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This investigation describes the synthesis and structural confirmation of a novel Schiff base, (E)-N-(1-methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (MP3M), prepared by condensing 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone with 4-(3-aminopropyl)­morpholine. The newly synthesized compound was thoroughly examined by means of Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy to confirm the structure. To evaluate its effectiveness as a corrosion inhibitor for low-carbon steel (LCS) in a 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 solution, a variety of experimental and theoretical methods were employed. The experimental evaluation of its inhibition performance involved weight loss measurements (WL), potentiodynamic polarization (PDP), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The experimental results were further validated by theoretical calculations employing density functional theory (DFT) and Monte Carlo (MC) simulations. Kinetic and thermodynamic parameters were utilized to support the adsorption mechanism of MP3M onto the LCS surface. Our key findings demonstrate that MP3M functions as a mixed-type inhibitor, effectively suppressing both anodic and cathodic reactions. It exhibits a high inhibition efficiency of 88.5% at 298 K at a concentration of 0.075 mol L–1. EIS data further support this, showing an increase in charge transfer resistance with the inhibitor’s addition. The inhibition mechanism is attributed to the spontaneous adsorption of MP3M molecules onto the steel surface, a process that follows the Langmuir isotherm model and involves both physisorption and chemisorption. Quantum chemical calculations confirmed the compound’s high stability and balanced reactivity, while MC simulations revealed a remarkably strong interaction with the LCS surface, as indicated by a highly negative adsorption energy of −539.78 kJ mol–1. The good agreement between the experimental and theoretical results validates MP3M’s strong potential and establishes its use as a novel, effective corrosion inhibitor.

Introduction

Organic inhibitors constitute a critical strategy for mitigating corrosion in acidic environments, which are prevalent in numerous industrial applications. − Compounds containing heteroatoms (N, P, S) have demonstrated significant potential as corrosion inhibitors. Heterocyclic compounds have exhibited high efficacy in inhibiting corrosion in aggressive environments. They achieve this by adsorbing onto the metal surface, which in turn slows down the corrosion process. − Schiff bases, formed from amines and carbonyl compounds, with the general formula R2C = NR, constitute a well-established class of corrosion inhibitors. They have been extensively studied and proven to protect various metals like mild steel, , aluminum, and copper from acid corrosion, and even copper from neutral halide corrosion. − Importantly, Schiff bases consistently have superior corrosion inhibition efficiencies compared to their parent amines. , This enhanced performance can be ascribed to the presence of unoccupied π*-orbitals, allowing them to accept electrons back from the metal’s d-orbitals, strengthening the bond between the metal and the inhibitor. Furthermore, the facile synthesis of many Schiff bases from readily available and inexpensive starting materials renders them highly attractive for practical applications.

Hegazy et al. explored how the azomethine compound (6E,7E)-N1,N6-bis (1-methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene) hexane-1,6-diamine could inhibit corrosion in carbon steel when exposed to sulfuric acid. Their findings indicated that higher concentrations of this compound resulted in improved corrosion protection. Al-Amiery et al. created and characterized 2-(2-oxoindolin-3-ylidene) (OHB), a Schiff base derived from isatin. Their work showed that OHB effectively reduced the dissolution of mild steel in hydrochloric acid. The best results, a 96.7% reduction in corrosion, were achieved with a 0.5 mM concentration of OHB at a temperature of 303 K. Kumari and Lavanya investigated the corrosion-inhibiting properties of the Schiff base N′-[4-(dimethylamino)­benzylidene]-4-hydroxybenzohydrazide on mild steel in hydrochloric acid media. Their research, which included kinetic and thermodynamic analyses, confirmed that the synthesized compound possessed protective qualities. The experimental data suggested that the synthesized compound is a highly effective corrosion inhibitor. Kaur et al. studied the corrosion-inhibiting effects of three Schiff bases: benzylidene-pyridine-2-yl-amine (BPA), (4-methylbenzylidene)-pyridine-2-ylamine (4CH3-BPA), and (4-chloro-benzylidene)-pyridine-2-ylamine (4Cl-BPA). They reported that 4Cl-BPA provided the best corrosion protection, followed by 4CH3-BPA, with BPA offering the least protection. The researchers explained that both chlorine and methyl groups attached to the benzene ring increase electron density, leading to stronger binding of the Schiff base to the metal surface and hence better corrosion inhibition. Yousif et al. researched three novel, eco-friendly Schiff bases: AIP, AMB, and AImP, which were synthesized from amino acids and 4-amino-acetophenone. These compounds were investigated for their ability to inhibit corrosion of Q235 steel in hydrochloric acid media. Electrochemical tests showed these compounds act as mixed-type inhibitors, significantly reducing corrosion. At a 10 mM concentration, they achieved impressive maximum efficiencies: AIP at 93.15%, AMB at 96.01%, and AImP at 77.03%. The compound N,N′-bis­(salicylidene)­butylene-1,4-diamine (SB) was studied for its ability to prevent corrosion on carbon steel by Eltohamy et al. using various electrochemical and weight loss methods, the researchers examined how temperature (298–333 K) and the presence of SB affected corrosion in hydrochloric acid. They found that SB offered good protection, achieving a maximum 84% reduction in corrosion at 298 K in a 0.5 M HCl solution. Increasing the SB concentration from 50 to 300 ppm further enhanced its effectiveness. Other researchers synthesized and evaluated three bis-Schiff bases (BS2, BS4, and BS8) as corrosion inhibitors for AISI 1020 carbon steel in hydrochloric acid. Among them, BS8 demonstrated the highest effectiveness, achieving 98% inhibition efficacy. The compounds adsorbed onto the steel surface according to the El-Awady isotherm. Both scanning electron microscope and quantum chemical calculations supported the experimental findings.

A recently developed Schiff base, (Z)-2-((3-nitrobenzylidene)­amino)­phenol (NBAP), has proven highly effective at preventing XC70 steel corrosion in acidic conditions. Tests showed that NBAP achieved up to 89% inhibition efficiency by developing a protective film on the steel surface through chemisorption. Both experimental and theoretical studies confirmed its strong performance, indicating NBAP could significantly boost the durability of mild steel. Modwi et al. have created a new, eco-friendly Schiff base (Per/CS) derived from chitosan. The compound proved to be an exceptional corrosion inhibitor for Q235 carbon steel in HCl, achieving an impressive 97% inhibition efficiency at a low concentration (600 mg/L at 25 °C). The results indicated that Per/CS formed a protective film on the steel surface through a mixed adsorption mechanism, as confirmed by various surface analyses and theoretical simulations. A new, eco-friendly isatin-chitosan (Isatin-Cs) Schiff base has been developed as an efficient corrosion inhibitor for Q235 carbon steel in hydrochloric acid. It achieved 88.4% inhibition efficiency at 25 °C and 250 ppm, working as a mixed-type inhibitor that forms a stable protective film on the steel surface through a mixed physical/chemisorption process. Alyami et al. successfully created a chitosan Schiff base (Fc) and its copper nanoparticle nanohybrid (NHFc), proving their effectiveness as eco-friendly corrosion inhibitors for N80 steel in acidic conditions. The NHFc, with copper nanoparticles, showed superior inhibition at 95.01% efficiency, significantly outperforming Fc alone. The experimental and theoretical analyses verified their strong adsorption and mechanism of action, making them promising green solutions for steel protection. New research highlighted the effectiveness of two previously unexplored Schiff bases, PHA and BHA, in protecting copper from corrosion in saltwater. At just 1 mM concentration, PHA achieved 85.16% inhibition efficiency, while BHA reached 82.78%. The compounds acted as mixed-type inhibitors, primarily affecting the cathodic reaction, and adsorbed onto the copper surface following a Langmuir isotherm. The experimental data and theoretical simulations confirmed their strong performance and provided insights into their protective mechanisms. Azzouzi and others have developed IPY 1 and IPY 2, new imidazo­[1,2-a]­pyrimidine-Schiff base derivatives that are excellent corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in acidic conditions. Both compounds achieved over 94% efficiency by developing a stable, protective film on the steel. Importantly, they are also eco-friendly, showing low toxicity and good biodegradability, making them a promising sustainable option for industrial use. Recently, Deng et al. showed that while amine-terminated Schiff base polymers are more planar, aldehyde-terminated versions are surprisingly better at preventing corrosion. This is because the aldehyde group subtly affects the imine (CN) group’s sp2 hybridization and planarity, which ultimately improves their corrosion inhibition performance. Rahal et al. investigated the corrosion-inhibiting properties of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) and thiourea (TU) on mild steel in a 0.5 mol L–1 nitric acid solution. Using electrochemical techniques and quantum chemical calculations, they found that both compounds are effective inhibitors, with TU being particularly potent, reaching a 99% inhibition efficiency at a concentration of 6 × 10–3 mol L–1 at 30 °C. Abdel-Gaber and others examined the effectiveness of eucalyptus leaf extract (Eu) as a corrosion inhibitor for mild steel in 0.5 mol L–1 sulfuric acid and 0.5 mol L–1 phosphoric acid. The extract acts as a mixed-type inhibitor in both acidic solutions. The mechanism of inhibition is through the spontaneous physical adsorption of the extract’s molecules onto the steel surface. The results showed that Eu extract is a more effective inhibitor in sulfuric acid than in phosphoric acid. Abdel-Gaber et al. examined linalool and caffeine as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in 0.5 mol L–1 sulfuric acid. Researchers reported that linalool is a more effective inhibitor than caffeine. Linalool acts as a mixed-type inhibitor, while caffeine functions as an anodic-type inhibitor. Both compounds inhibit corrosion through a physical adsorption mechanism. Okra (Abelmoschus esculentus) and pomelo (Citrus maxima) leaf extracts were tested as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in hydrochloric acid. The study found that both act as mixed-type inhibitors by forming a protective layer on the steel surface. Okra extract was notably more effective than pomelo extract, with the overall protective mechanism confirmed to be the adsorption of chemical compounds from the extracts onto the metal. A recent study by Ennafaa et al. evaluated a new, nontoxic compound called N-(3-(4-methoxyphenyl) isoxazol-5-yl) methyl saccharin (MIMS) as a corrosion inhibitor for C38 steel in hydrochloric acid. Using both experimental and computational methods, they discovered that MIMS is highly effective. Experimental tests, including WL, PDP, and EIS showed that MIMS provides a high inhibition efficiency of over 92% at a 1 mM concentration.

Despite the effectiveness of the previous inhibitors, a detailed, multifaceted investigation into a novel, easily synthesized, and cost-effective compound with a unique structure is still needed to advance the field. While the literature demonstrates the efficacy of various Schiff bases, our novel compound, MP3M, is distinct for three key reasons. First, its unique molecular design, featuring both pyrrolidine and morpholine rings, provides multiple active sites, enabling superior adsorption on the metal surface. Second, the synthesis is simple and cost-effective, making MP3M a highly practical and scalable option for industrial applications. Finally, this is the first study to use a combined experimental and computational approach to thoroughly validate this specific inhibitor’s performance and mechanism, providing a complete picture of its effectiveness.

This work aims to synthesize and characterize a novel Schiff base compound, (E)-N-(1-methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (MP3M) and assesses its effectiveness as a corrosion inhibitor for LCS in a 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 solution. FTIR and 1H NMR techniques were used to confirm the structure of the MP3M compound. Different methods were used to evaluate MP3M’s performance, including WL measurements, EIS, PDP, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Also, the thermodynamic and activation parameters were calculated, and the computational modeling (DFT and MC) was utilized to better understand how MP3M interacts with the LCS surface and inhibits corrosion.

Materials and Experimental Techniques

Preparation of (E)-N-(1-Methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine

A Schiff base reaction was used to synthesize the azomethine compound. One mole of 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone reacted with 1 mol of 4-(3-aminopropyl)­morpholine, resulting in the formation of (E)-N-(1-methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (MP3M), Scheme . After cooling, the resulting precipitate was recrystallized from ethanol. The synthesized inhibitor’s structure was verified through FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy, as shown in Figure .

Solutions

A corrosive solution of 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 was meticulously prepared by diluting concentrated, analytical-grade H2SO4 (98% by weight, supplied by Merck) with purified, double-distilled water. We used 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 for our corrosion tests because it is a standard and relevant concentration for mild steel. This choice ensures we can effectively measure MP3M’s inhibition performance in a challenging yet controllable environment, making our results comparable to existing literature and relevant to industrial applications.

For the corrosion experiments, the concentration of the added inhibitor varied across a range from 5 × 10–3 to 7.5 × 10–2 mol L–1. All solutions used in these experiments were carefully formulated using double-distilled water to ensure purity and consistency.

Low-Carbon Steel Specimens

The tests utilized LCS with a composition (wt.%): 0.19 carbon, 0.05 silicon, 0.94 manganese, 0.009 phosphorus, 0.004 sulfur, 0.014 nickel, 0.009 chromium, 0.034 aluminum, 0.016 vanadium, 0.003 titanium, and 0.022 copper, and the rest was iron.

Weight Loss Measurements

The WL method was selected to assess the effectiveness of the inhibitor due to its simplicity and reliability. The experiments were conducted following standard procedures. Steel samples, each measuring 3 cm × 6 cm × 0.5 cm, were submerged for 24 h in a 0.5 mol L–1 solution of H2SO4. This is done in a closed beaker, both with and without varying concentrations of an inhibitor, at temperatures ranging from 298 to 343 K. This temperature range was chosen because it accurately reflects common industrial and practical environments where LCS corrosion is an issue and where inhibitors like MP3M would be used. Testing at significantly higher temperatures would not be relevant to the intended application. Each test was repeated three times, and the average weight loss ± standard deviation was used to determine the corrosion rate.

Electrochemical Investigations

Electrochemical experiments were conducted using a standard three-electrode system. The system included a platinum electrode as the counter electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) for reference, and a carbon steel rod serving as the working electrode (WE). The WE, which had an exposed surface area of about 0.7 cm2, was housed in a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) holder. Prior to testing, the electrode’s exposed surface was meticulously prepared. It was mechanically polished with progressively finer emery paper, degreased with acetone, rinsed with double-distilled water, and then dried. All experiments were performed at a constant temperature. PDP curves were generated by sweeping the electrode potential from −0.8 to −0.3 V vs an SCE at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s–1. To ensure a steady state before each measurement, the working electrode was submerged in the test solution at its open-circuit potential (OCP) for 30 min. EIS experiments were established at the OCP. A 10 mV sine wave was used as the excitation signal, sweeping across a frequency range of 100 kHz down to 50 mHz. All measured potentials were referenced against an SCE. The data was collected using a Voltalab 40 Potentiostat PGZ 301 with VoltaMaster 4 corrosion software.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Study

To assess the morphology of the LCS surface, samples were immersed in a 0.5 mol L–1 solution of H2SO4 for 24 h, both with and without the addition of the MP3M compound at its optimal concentration of 7.5 × 10–2 mol L–1. Following immersion, the specimens were rinsed with distilled water, dried, and then directly mounted for analysis. SEM micrographs were acquired using a Jeol BED-C 10 kV instrument to investigate the surface morphology of the adsorbed film.

Quantum Chemistry Study

Quantum chemistry simulations were performed to understand how the corrosion inhibitor adsorbs onto the LCS surface and to connect its quantum chemical properties to its observed effectiveness in preventing corrosion. The quantum and computational sections of this study employed a two-pronged approach to understand the inhibition mechanism of MP3M. First, density functional tight-binding (DFTB + 1.3) was used to conduct quantum calculations. This was done to evaluate several key electronic and thermodynamic properties of the inhibitor, including the energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (E HOMO), the energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (E LUMO), and the energy gap (ΔE). The parameters necessary for these calculations were sourced from standard Slater–Koster file libraries, with Auorg parameters specifically used for this system. −

Second, an MC simulation was performed to investigate the preferred way that MP3M molecules attach to the steel surface. The simulation was set up using a periodic slab model of Fe(110) surface with 8 atomic layers, contained within a super unit cell. A simulated annealing algorithm was used to systematically search for the most stable adsorption configurations. A 30 Å vacuum layer was included in the C axis to prevent interactions between periodic cells. The behavior of the MP3M molecule on the steel surface during this process was governed by the Compass force field.

Confirmation of the Synthesized Inhibitor’s Chemical Structure

FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopies were used to verify the structure of the prepared MP3M Schiff base.

The synthesized compound’s FTIR spectrum displayed characteristic absorption bands: a C–N stretch at 1069.93 cm–1, a C–O stretch at 1122.67 cm–1, a CN stretch at 1574.8 cm–1, and a C–H stretch at 2860.33 cm–1. Impact of Temperature

To study how temperature affects the ability of MP3M to inhibit corrosion and to determine the activation energy of the corrosion process, WL experiments were conducted across a range of temperatures (298–343 K). Table shows how the corrosion rate of LCS in a 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 changes in the absence and presence of varying dosages of MP3M. The data clearly show that as temperature increases, the corrosion rate also increases, and consequently, the inhibition efficacy decreases. In the presence of 0.075 mol L–1 of the MP3M inhibitor and at 343 K, the IEw decreases to 68.2%. Higher temperatures promote both the etching of the metal surface and the desorption of the MP3M compound from the surface. When more of the metal surface is exposed to the corrosive environment, the rate of corrosion speeds up.

The Arrhenius equation (k = A exp (−E a/RT)) was used to analyze the corrosion rate’s (k) variability with temperature (T). For this equation, R represents the ideal gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, and A is the pre-exponential factor. Figure displays an Arrhenius plot (ln k vs 1000/T) for LCS in a 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4, free of and with distinct concentrations of MP3M. The activation energies (E a), computed from the slope of these lines (−E a/R), were presented in Table . The data reveals that the E a value is higher for the inhibited solution (52.24 kJ mol–1 with 0.075 mol L–1 of MP3M) compared to the uninhibited solution (32.90 kJ mol–1). This observation points to the inhibitor forming a protective film on the metal’s surface. This film, in turn, demands more energy for the transfer of charge and mass that occurs during the corrosion process, thereby hindering it. The fact that MP3M’s inhibition efficacy decreases with rising temperature suggests that the inhibitor adsorbs at the metal surface through physical adsorption.

Electrochemical Investigations

Open Circuit Potential (OCP)Time Curves

Figure depicts the OCP of LCS in a 0.5 mol L–1 H2SO4 without and with the addition of varying concentrations of an MP3M Schiff base inhibitor over a period of 1600 s. The black curve (B), representing the corrosive environment without the inhibitor, shows a relatively stable, electronegative potential around −550 mV vs SCE, indicative of ongoing corrosion. Upon the introduction of the MP3M inhibitor, a distinct positive shift in the OCP is observed, demonstrating the inhibitor’s ability to mitigate the corrosion process. This positive shift becomes more pronounced as the inhibitor’s concentration increases. At the lowest concentration, 0.005 mol L–1 (curve 1), the potential stabilizes at a less negative value compared to the uninhibited solution. As the concentration increases from curve 2 through curve 5, a progressive and marked increase in the OCP toward more noble (positive) potentials is evident. This indicates that higher concentrations of the inhibitor lead to enhanced corrosion protection, likely due to increased surface coverage and a stronger protective film formation on the LCS. The curves also illustrate the rapid adsorption and film-forming ability of the inhibitor, as the potential shifts occur quickly within the initial period. Furthermore, the stability of the potential overtime, particularly at higher concentrations, suggests a durable and robust protective film hindering the sulfuric acid’s corrosive effect on the LCS. Thus, the graph clearly showcases the effectiveness of the MP3M Schiff base as a corrosion retardant for LCS in the given acidic environment, with its performance directly dependent on its concentration. It is worth observing that the OCP curve for the 0.025 mol L–1 concentration (curve 3) does not reach a stable plateau within the measured 1600 s, unlike the other concentrations. This is an important kinetic detail that requires clarification. The continuous, gradual increase in potential for curve 3 suggests that a steady-state condition was not fully established within the experimental time frame. This behavior can be attributed to the kinetics of the inhibitor’s adsorption at this specific concentration. In addition, at 0.025 mol L–1, the concentration may represent a critical point where the inhibitor molecules are still in the process of a slower, more deliberate adsorption onto the steel surface. It is likely that the initial protective film forms rapidly, but subsequent molecular rearrangement and compaction continue over time. This slow rearrangement leads to a gradual improvement in the film’s passivating properties, causing the potential to continue shifting in a more noble direction. This phenomenon, while visually distinct, still confirms the inhibitor’s effectiveness. The consistent positive shift in potential indicates that the film is continuously becoming more stable and protective against the corrosive environment, even if it requires a longer period to reach complete equilibrium.

The calculation of the percentage inhibition efficiency (IEp) relied on eq . 3 IEp=(1−IcorrIcorr°)×100

Icorr° is the corrosion current density when no MP3M compound is present, while I corr is the corrosion current density in the presence of the MP3M compound. The changes observed in the anodic branch suggest that the molecules of the organic inhibitor interact with the anodic sites and suppress steel dissolution. A possible mechanism involves iron cations, released during carbon steel anodic dissolution, complexing with the inhibitor and adsorbing onto the metal surface. Sulfate ions, through electrostatic attraction, initially bind to the positively charged metal surface. Subsequently, protonated organic inhibitor molecules can adsorb either directly to negatively charged sites on the metal (SO4 2–) or via electrostatic interactions with the already-adsorbed sulfate ions. This suggests that MP3M molecules may become protonated and adsorb at anodic sites.

The cathodic branch is also affected, indicating inhibition of cathodic reactions. It is hypothesized that certain functional groups within the MP3M structure react with iron ions to form complexes that subsequently adsorb onto the metal surface. This type of inhibition mechanism is well-documented in the literature. − The corrosion potential shifts observed with MP3M, regardless of concentration, were all less than 85 mV compared to the blank, confirming that the investigated compound adsorbs at both anodic and cathodic sites (mixed inhibitor). A nonmonotonic trend in corrosion potential (E corr) with increasing inhibitor concentration is a common and well-documented phenomenon for mixed-type inhibitors. This behavior occurs because the inhibitor affects both the anodic (metal dissolution) and cathodic (e.g., hydrogen evolution or oxygen reduction) reactions on the metal surface. The final corrosion potential is a mixed potential, representing the balance between these two reactions, so its value depends on the relative degree to which the inhibitor affects each one. The MP3M compound effectively lowers the corrosion current density (I corr), indicating that it significantly reduces the corrosion rate of the metal, Table . The compound is classified as a pickling inhibitor because it achieves this reduction without substantially changing the corrosion potential (E corr).

Density Functional Theory Estimations

DFT is a computational technique that helps us understand how electrons are arranged in molecules. It is especially valuable for predicting HOMO and LUMO orbitals, which are crucial for determining a molecule’s chemical behavior. Figure a shows the geometrical structure of the MP3M Schiff base compound. It provides a fundamental understanding of the molecule’s overall shape, bond lengths, and bond angles, which are crucial for predicting its physical and chemical properties. Analysis using DFT showed that for the MP3M Schiff base, the electron density of the HOMO appears to be predominantly localized over the left-hand ring system (the five-membered ring with two nitrogen atoms) and extending slightly toward the nitrogen atom involved in the CN double bond and the carbon atoms directly connected to it. There’s also some density around the nitrogen in the right-hand ring (Figure b). This localization indicates that the electrons in the HOMO are primarily concentrated in these regions. Therefore, these are the most electron-rich parts of the molecule and the most likely sites for electron donation in chemical reactions (nucleophile). On the other hand, the electron density of the LUMO is primarily concentrated around the central CN double bond (the Schiff base linkage) and the adjacent carbon atoms of the five-membered ring (Figure c). There’s also significant density on the nitrogen of the five-membered ring that is directly part of the ring and the carbon atoms that form the bond to the central carbon. These regions are the most electron-deficient regions of the molecule and the most likely sites for accepting electrons in chemical reactions (electrophile). The CN bond is often a site of electrophilic attack due to its polarity. Figure d depicts the total electron density surface of the MP3M molecule. The blue shading indicates regions of higher electron density. The blue cloud surrounds the entire molecule, indicating the overall distribution of electrons. The darker blue regions would typically correspond to areas where electron density is higher. It appears to show a relatively even distribution across the molecule, with possibly slightly denser areas around the nitrogen and oxygen atoms due to their higher electronegativity compared to carbon and hydrogen.

It is important to note the significant difference between the large negative value of the computed adsorption energy (−539.78 kJ mol–1) (Table ) and the much smaller experimental enthalpy of adsorption, ΔH ads o (−11.82 kJ mol–1) (Table ). Mechanism of Inhibition

Sulfuric acid is a strong acid that readily corrodes steel. This process involves the dissolution of iron from the steel surface, creating a positively charged surface because of the presence of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions. The steel surface will also be covered with adsorbed sulfate ions and hydronium ions. According to the PDP studies, the changes in anodic branches (Figure ) indicate that the organic inhibitor functions by inhibiting iron dissolution through a mechanism involving the complexation of Fe2+ ions, generated during anodic dissolution, with the inhibitor molecules, followed by adsorption of the resulting complex onto the metal substrate. The observed changes in the cathodic branch refer to inhibition of cathodic reactions. A hypothesis supported by literature suggests this occurs when specific functional groups of MP3M react with iron ions, resulting in complexes that subsequently adsorb onto the metal. − The fact that MP3M, at all concentrations, resulted in corrosion potential shifts of less than 85 mV indicates it is a mixed inhibitor, meaning it adsorbs at both anodic and cathodic areas.

Based on the ΔG ads° values in the range of −22.39 to −23.97 kJ mol–1, it can be concluded that the inhibitor’s adsorption onto the LCS surface is primarily a physical process with a slight chemical contribution. The Schiff base can be physisorbed onto steel in sulfuric acid primarily through electrostatic interactions between its nitrogen and oxygen atoms and the positively charged iron ions on the steel surface. Van der Waals forces and protonation of the Schiff base also contribute to the adsorption process (Figure ). Chemisorption comprises the formation of chemical bonds between the Schiff base and the steel surface. It is a stronger interaction than physisorption, leading to a more stable adsorbed layer. According to the results of DFT studies, The nitrogen atoms in the pyrrolidine ring, imine linkage, and morpholine ring have lone pairs of electrons. These lone pairs can coordinate covalent bonds with the empty d-orbitals of iron atoms. This is particularly favored with the positively charged iron ions (Fe2+, Fe3+) present due to corrosion. The imine (CN) group has a π-bond that can interact with the d-orbitals of iron atoms on the steel surface.

Mechanism of inhibition of MP3M compound on the low-carbon steel.

The imine (CN) bond in the Schiff base has π* antibonding orbitals. These orbitals could potentially accept electron density from the iron, and retrodonation occurs. This would strengthen the interaction between the Schiff base and the steel surface. This effect would cause a synergistic bonding. The Schiff base donates electrons to the steel, and the steel donates electrons back to the Schiff base, strengthening the bond between them. Figure illustrates the mechanism of inhibition.

Conclusions

A novel Schiff base, (E)-N-(1-methylpyrrolidin-2-ylidene)-3-morpholinopropan-1-amine (MP3M), was synthesized and characterized, demonstrating its effectiveness as a corrosion inhibitor for LCS in a 0.5 mol L–1 sulfuric acid. Through various chemical and electrochemical techniques, including WL, PDP, EIS, and SEM, it was shown that MP3M significantly reduces LCS corrosion by forming an adsorbed protective film. The highest inhibition efficacy, 88.5%, was achieved at 298 K with a 0.075 mol L–1 concentration, although this decreased to 68.2% at a temperature of 343 K. The adsorption of MP3M followed the Langmuir isotherm and included these two physisorption and chemisorption processes, with a predominantly physical nature. Tafel polarization studies classified MP3M as a mixed-type inhibitor. DFT and MC simulations supported these findings, revealing that nitrogen atoms in the pyrrolidine, imine, and morpholine rings coordinate with iron atoms on the steel surface, contributing to a highly negative adsorption energy (−539.78 kJ mol–1) and thus, a strong protective barrier. The small deformation of energy implies that the inhibitor retains its structural integrity, which is beneficial for long-term stability in aggressive environments. All experimental methods consistently confirmed the ability of the new Schiff base compound MP3M to mitigate LCS corrosion in a 0.5 mol L–1 sulfuric acid.
