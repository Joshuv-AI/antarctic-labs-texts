Weight loss reverses obesity-associated impairments in acute gastrointestinal stretch-induced suppression of food intake and glucose homeostasis
Authors: Maigen Bethea, Tyler Cook, Marwa Mommandi, Andrew McClennan, Allison Martin, Jasmine J. Hendrix, Chelsea R. Hutch, Alfor Lewis, Randy J. Seeley, Henning Fenselau, Silvania da Silva Teixeria, Darleen A. Sandoval
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveChemical and mechanical signals from the gastrointestinal tract are critical for regulating satiety and glucose metabolism. While both nutrient sensing in the intestine and gastric distension has been well studied, the role of intestinal stretch in these metabolic processes remain unclear. This study evaluates the role of intestinal stretch in regulating food intake and glucose homeostasis in the context of normal body weight, obesity, and weight loss occurring via both dietary intervention and vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG).MethodsWe used the nonnutritive substance mannitol to selectively induce intestinal stretch in conscious mice. We assessed food intake, glucose tolerance, and neuronal activation in mice with normal body weight, obesity, or after dietary or surgically-induced weight loss. We employed chemogenetic approaches to inhibit GLP-1R and OxtR-expressing vagal afferents, and genetic and pharmacological strategies to ablate GLP-1 signaling to explore mechanisms for mannitol-induced suppression of feeding.ResultsMannitol-induced intestinal stretch acutely suppressed food intake and improved oral glucose tolerance independent of GLP-1 signaling and vagal intestinal mechanosensation. Diet induced obesity impairs mannitol-induced intestinal stretch reductions in food intake and attenuates neuronal activation in the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) upon induction of intestinal stretch. Both dietary and surgical weight loss restored intestinal stretch-induced feeding suppression and enhanced NTS neuronal activation. Importantly, VSG heightened NTS neuronal activation in response to oral but not IP glucose.ConclusionsTogether, these data demonstrate that intestinal stretch contributes to the regulation of feeding and glucose metabolism independently of intestinal nutrient-sensing or classical gut hormones.

Introduction

Satiety is regulated by chemical and mechanical signals initiated within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Among these, gastric distension has been widely recognized as a potent suppressor of feeding. Experimental models where gastric distension is induced by insertion and inflation of a balloon within the stomach consistently demonstrate acute reductions in food consumption in both rodents and humans[[1], [2], [3]]. In fact, some have posited that postprandial sensing of gastric distension, rather than sensing of nutrients, is the primary determinant of satiety [4].

However, despite the established role of gastric mechanosensation, considerably less attention has been paid to the potential role of intestinal stretch in appetite regulation. Earlier studies postulated that mechanical stretch of the upper intestine, or the duodenum plays more of an important role in regulating satiety than gastric distention[[5], [6], [7]]. Specifically, these studies demonstrated that mannitol induced mechanical distension of the duodenum suppresses food intake in a dose dependent fashion [5,7]. More recently, a growing body of work suggests that mechanical distension of the gut is sensed through vagal afferent neurons that express either the glucagon like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) or oxytocin receptor (OxtR) and consequently these neurons play a crucial role in signaling fullness and influencing metabolic processes by suppressing the activity of hunger-promoting Agouti-related Peptide (AgRP) neurons, establishing that intestinal feedback contributes directly to feeding control [[8], [9], [10]]. Collectively, these studies support the concept that intestinal stretch is a physiologically relevant satiety signal.

Gastrointestinal stretch (both the stomach and the intestine) has been hypothesized to contribute to satiety through multiple mechanisms, including by driving secretion of glucagon like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and consequent activation of vagal mechanosensory pathways [9,11]. Still, critical questions remain regarding the relative contributions of stomach versus intestinal stretch signaling, the dependence of mechanical versus nutrient sensing pathways, and the impact of obesity on gut-brain communication. Furthermore, whether the functional consequences of intestinal stretch can be restored through dietary or surgical weight loss remains to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we increased intestinal distension with mannitol administration, a nonnutritive substance that draws water into the intestine [12], allowing us to isolate intestinal stretch from gastric stretch and nutrient-sensing mechanisms in a conscious unrestrained mouse. We assessed the impact of mannitol-induced intestinal stretch on feeding, glucose tolerance, and neuronal activation in healthy chow-fed mice, and studied how this was impacted by obesity and weight loss. We found that mannitol increased intestinal distension, reduced food intake and improved glucose tolerance independent of GLP-1 and vagal mechanoreceptor signaling. However, the effect of intestinal stretch on feeding was blunted in obesity and rescued in dietary and surgical weight loss, highlighting a novel mechanism of intestinal mechanosensory dysfunction in obesity and its reversibility with interventions that restore gut-brain sensitivity. This work therefore provides a new context for interpreting longstanding observations and identifies intestinal mechanosensory dysfunction as a modifiable feature of obesity.

In these triple transgenic mice, chemogenetic inhibition of hM4Di expressing GLP-1R or OxtR vagal neurons was achieved by injecting CNO (3 mg/kg) 1 h prior to mannitol administration.

Food intake studies

For feeding studies, mice were fasted for 20 h and given either mock gavage (insertion of an empty gavage needle) or a gavage of 500 μL of 1.33M mannitol [9]. In addition, in some studies, animals were administered 500 μL of 1% methylcellulose or 500 μL of normal saline [9]. For dose response studies (Figure 1A and Supplemental Fig. 1A), animals were given increasing volumes (500, 750, 1000 μL) of methylcellulose or mannitol. To examine the necessity of GLP-1R signaling, Exendin-9-39 (Abcam, ab141101) at 600 nmol/kg or saline was administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection 15 min before mannitol gavage. Food access was provided immediately after gavage and intake was assessed at baseline and 1 h after gavage. Similar food intake studies were performed in VSG mice with reduced mannitol volume (250 μL) to accommodate the smaller stomach (Fig 7).

Osmolality assessment

Chow-fed mice were fasted for 20 h and given a gavage of 500 μL of 1.33M mannitol solution. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture 60 min after mannitol administration and plasma glucose, sodium, potassium, and chloride were assessed at the CU Anschutz Comparative Pathology Shared Resource core. Osmolality was calculated using the formula: 2(Sodium (Na) + Potassium (K))+(Glucose/18)+(Urea/6) [14].

Feeding behavior studies

To assess feeding behavior, chow-fed male mice (∼12 weeks of age) were housed in at ∼26 °C in the BioDAQ system (Research Diets Inc.) in the Colorado NORC for two weeks to acclimate to the different environment. After a 5-day acclimation period with continuous food access in the wire bottom cages, animals were fasted for 20 h. Each animal then received 500 μL of 1.33M mannitol solution or mock gavage. Cumulative food intake and meal frequency were assessed for 24-hour post-administration.

Glucose homeostasis assessment

We used an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) to assess glucose homeostasis. Chow-fed age matched male and female C57BL/6J mice were fasted for 20 h. Animals were given a mock gavage or administered 500 μL of 1.33M mannitol 15 min prior to receiving an IP injection of glucose (2 mg/kg of 20% dextrose, timepoint 0). In separate experiments, Ex-9 (600 nmol/kg) or saline was administered IP 15 min before mannitol gavage. Blood glucose levels were collected via tail blood sampling at timepoints −15, 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, and 120 min using Accu-Chek glucometers for all GTT experiments.

For assessing gastric emptying rate and oral glucose tolerance, animals were fasted for 20 h and mock or Mannitol (500 μL of 1.33M) gavage was administered 15 min before a gavage of 40% dextrose solution spiked with acetaminophen (100 mg/kg) (200 μL flat dose). Plasma acetaminophen levels were measured using an Acetaminophen-L3k assay (Sekisui Diagnostics kit, #506-30) at 15 min post glucose gavage.

Hormone assessment

Thirty minutes after mannitol, plasma insulin (Crystal Chem, Catalog #90080) and serotonin (Enzo Life Sciences, Catalog #ADI-900-175) levels were quantified by ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions. Thirty minutes after a gavage of sitagliptin (10 mg/kg, APExBIO, Catalog A4036) and 60 min after mannitol, plasma GIP (Crystal Chem, Catalog #81511) levels were quantified according to manufacturer's instructions.

Diet induced obesity

C57BL/6J 8-week-old male mice (Jackson Laboratory, #000664) were placed on 60% high fat diet (HFD) (Research Diet, Catalog #D12492) for 8 weeks (Figure 5). For dietary weight loss, an additional cohort of C57BL/6J 8-week-old male mice were place on 60% HFD (Research Diet, Catalog #D12492) for 8 weeks and then switched to a chow diet for 4 weeks to induce weight loss. Body composition in both the HFD and dietary weight loss groups was assessed using EchoMRI (Echo Medical Systems).

Vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG)

In our current studies, new cohorts of sham or VSG mice were generated. Male and female mice were fed a 60% High Fat diet for 8 weeks. Body composition was measured before and 4 weeks after surgery using EchoMRI. Prior to surgery, mice were divided into two matched groups based on fat mass. Mice underwent either Sham or VSG surgery as previously discussed [15,16]. Body weight and food intake were monitored daily for the first 7 postsurgical days and then weekly for 7 weeks.

Immunofluorescence analyses

Chow, HFD-fed, dietary weight loss, and sham vs. VSG animals were fasted for 20 h, given mock gavage or mannitol gavage (500 μL of 1.33M Mannitol solution; 250 μL for VSG animals), and returned to their home cages for 90 min. Animals were then euthanized with Euthasol and perfused with 22 mL of PBS followed by 30 mL of 10% formalin. Brains were dissected and fixed overnight in 10% formalin and then placed in 30% sucrose for 48–72h. Tissues were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT: Tissue-Tek, #4583). Free-floating sections were cut at 30um and then blocked using 10% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #017-000-121) in 0.3% Triton X (Sigma–Aldrich, #93422) in 1 X PBS for 1h at room temperature. Two sections around −7.56 Bregma from each animal (n = 3–5) were then washed with 0.2% Triton X in 1 X PBS and incubated with primary antibodies for 24–48 h at 4C: Rat cFos, 1:500, (Synaptic Systems #226017). Alexa594 conjugated Donkey Anti-Rat IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch #712-585-153) 1:500 was used to detect indirect immunofluorescence. Slides were then imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM5500). cFos positive staining was quantified in the NTS, DMV, and AP using ImageJ. The average of the two sections were calculated and reported as the relative cFos count. Due to limited sample sizes, male and female VSG mice cfos quantification data were combined.

Statistical analysis

All data are represented as mean +/− standard error (SE). Statistical significance was assessed using either a student's t-test or a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc analysis where appropriate, using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 statistical software. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Intestinal Stretch Via Mannitol Gavage Suppresses Food Intake

To assess the contribution of gastrointestinal stretch to feeding behavior, we gavaged fasted mice with non-nutritive solutions: methylcellulose, which selectively induces gastric stretch [9], and mannitol, which primarily induces intestinal stretch [9] (Fig. 1A). Both were compared to a mock gavage (feeding needle exposure only). We selected mock gavage rather than saline, as neither manipulation affects food intake (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Notably, methylcellulose gavage failed to alter food intake relative to mock treatment, even when delivered at increasing volumes, indicating that gastric stretch alone does not suppress feeding (Supplemental Fig. 1B–C).Conversely, mannitol gavage led to a dose-dependent reduction in food intake, suggesting that intestinal, but not gastric stretch suppresses feeding (Fig 1A). However, cumulative food intake and meal frequency over 24 h remained unchanged, indicating that the anorectic effect of mannitol is immediate and acute (Figure 1B–C), in line with the notion that it drives satiation rather than satiety.

We visually confirmed intestinal distension 1 h after mannitol vs. mock administration (Fig 1D). Mannitol has been shown to increase plasma osmolality [17]. Consistent with this, we observed a non-clinical but significant increase in plasma osmolality 60 min after mock vs. mannitol treatment (315.6 ± 1.840 vs. 329.1 ± 13.51 mOsm/kg) (Fig 1E). This degree of increase in plasma osmolality was similar to what has been observed with mild dehydration with no impact on food intake [18]. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that dehydration in mice suppresses food intake, but only at plasma osmolarity levels around 335 mOsm/kg [19]. In contrast, our mannitol-treated animals exhibited a plasma osmolarity of 329.1 mOsm/kg, indicating that they were below this threshold and therefore unlikely to be dehydrated. Collectively, these data suggest that this dose of mannitol induces intestinal stretch with minimal effects on osmolality and this leads to a transient suppression of food intake.

Mannitol-induced feeding suppression is independent of GLP-1 and vagal mechanosensation

Given prior evidence linking gastrointestinal stretch to GLP-1-dependent satiety [9,10,20], we assessed whether GLP-1 or the GLP-1R was necessary for mannitol-induced feeding suppression. Using chow-fed C57BL/6J mice, we administered Ex 9–39 (Ex-9), a GLP-1R antagonist, alongside mannitol. Food intake was similarly suppressed in both mannitol only and mannitol + Ex-9 groups (Fig 2A). We also administered mannitol to Gcg-null mice, which lack the preproglucagon gene and are incapable of GLP-1 production [13]. Again, mannitol suppressed feeding to a similar extent in littermate control and Gcg-null mice (Fig 2B). Chemogenetic inhibition of GLP-1R-expresing vagal afferents also had no impact on mannitol-induced reductions in food intake (Fig 2C), reinforcing that neither GLP-1, nor the cells expressing its receptor is required for mannitol-induced suppression of feeding.

Next, we tested the role of additional intestinal vagal afferent mechanosensory pathways, focusing on neurons expressing the oxytocin receptor (OxtR). OxtR vagal afferents were found to predominantly innervate the small intestine and suppress feeding when artificially stimulated [9]. We found that chemogenetic inhibition of this vagal population also failed to blunt mannitol-induced reductions in food intake (Fig 2D). These findings indicate that intestinal-stretch induced satiety occurs independently of GLP-1 and OxtR vagal mechanosensory neurons recently linked to gastrointestinal stretch.

Intestinal Stretch Improves Glucose Tolerance via oral but Not Systemic Glucose Administration

Often, signals that regulate feeding also regulate glucose homeostasis. Therefore, we tested whether mannitol-induced intestinal stretch impacts glucose tolerance. When glucose was delivered intraperitoneally, a route of administration that bypasses the gut, mannitol gavage had no effect on glucose levels (Figure 3A–B). However, we observed a significant improvement in oral glucose tolerance following intestinal stretch, suggesting a synergistic interaction between intestinal stretch and intestinal nutrient sensing mechanisms (Figure 3C–D). The improved glucose levels were independent of changes in gastric emptying rate (Fig 3E). Additionally, we demonstrate that 8 weeks of high fat diet (HFD) feeding attenuates the mannitol induced improvements in glucose homeostasis (Figure 3F–G). Ex-9 pretreated groups exhibited higher glucose excursions, with the mock group being the highest (Figure 4A–B). Finally, we examined whether vagal oxytocin receptor (OxtR)-expressing neurons contribute to the glucose lowering effect of mannitol. Similar to inhibition of vagal GLP-1R subtypes, inhibition of vagal OxtR subtypes proved to be disposable for mannitol's beneficial effect on glucose tolerance (Figure 4E–F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that mannitol improves oral glucose tolerance independent of GLP-1 and OxtR neuronal signaling.

Intestinal stretch increases insulin secretion

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which mannitol-induced intestinal stretch suppresses food intake, we next examined circulating hormones. Notably, mannitol treatment alone significantly increased plasma insulin levels compared with mock-treated controls (Figure 5A).This suggests that mannitol is priming the pancreatic β cell to drive quicker glucose disposal, similar to cephalic insulin responses [21]. In contrast, plasma concentrations of GIP (Fig 5B) and serotonin (Fig 5C) were significantly decreased following mannitol administration. Given that our data in Figure 2, Figure 4 demonstrated that GLP-1 signaling is not required for the observed reductions in feeding or improvements in glucose tolerance, we did not pursue additional GLP-1 measurements. Weight loss via diet or Surgery Restores Intestinal Stretch Induced Satiety

We next examined whether weight loss restores responsiveness to intestinal stretch. Obese mice were switched from HFD to chow diet for four weeks, leading to significant body weight (43.15 ± 1.596 vs 37.23±-5.925) and fat mass loss (Supplemental Fig. 2A and B). Four weeks after the switch to chow, mannitol-induced reductions in food intake were restored (Figure 7A). Similarly, mannitol reduced food intake in animals that had weight loss through VSG, a bariatric procedure known to enhance gut signaling (Supplemental Fig. 2), unlike sham controls (Fig 7B). Notably, VSG animals received a reduced volume of mannitol (250 μL), a dose that is insufficient to suppress food intake in chow fed animals (Fig 1A), suggesting enhanced sensitivity to intestinal stretch following VSG. We next assessed cfos counts in our dietary (Fig 7C) and surgical (Fig 7D) weight loss animals. Quantification of cfos in our dietary weight loss animals demonstrated elevated cfos expression in the NTS and AP in response to mannitol (Fig 7E). Neuronal activation in the AP and NTS was also significantly higher in VSG groups treated with mannitol than VSG animals treated with mock gavage (Fig 7F). Discussion

Intestinal sensing of nutrients has been the primary focus of its mechanistic role in regulating feeding and glucose homeostasis. Here, we show intestinal mechanosensation is another signal that regulates feeding and glucose. Specifically, this study demonstrates that mannitol-induced intestinal stretch, but not methylcellulose-induced gastric stretch, significantly suppressed food intake and enhanced glucose tolerance. Mannitol-induced intestinal stretch improved oral glucose tolerance without impacting gastric emptying rate, or enhancing incretin, serotonin, or nutrient-induced insulin secretion. Instead, we predict that the enhanced basal insulin secretion drove improved oral glucose tolerance. These effects occur independently of GLP-1 signaling and specific vagal afferent subtypes expressing GLP-1R or OxtR, suggesting the existence of alternative pathways mediating stretch-induced satiety. Importantly, we also demonstrate that obesity impairs, and weight loss improves the feeding suppression induced by mannitol suggesting that intestinal mechanosensation is a critical factor regulating body weight.

Our findings add to growing literature that solidifies the intestine as a potent modulator of feeding behavior through many different mechanisms independent of nutrient-driven satiety signals[5], [6], [7], [9] [7,9]. While gastric distension has traditionally been considered a critical cue for meal termination [4], the relationship between gastric plus intestinal stretch and their impact on satiety is more complex than previously understood. Strikingly, gastric stretch induced by methylcellulose failed to suppress feeding, highlighting a functional dissociation between stomach and gut regulation of satiety. Studies that used a balloon or saline to induce gastric stretch demonstrate that gastric stretch alone is insufficient to induce satiation [[22], [23], [24]]. However, some research suggests that the observed effects of gastric stretch induced by inflation of a balloon placed within the stomach on feeding may largely depend upon pain [25] or aversion pathways [26] rather than satiety. It is possible that methylcellulose does not induce sufficient stretch on the stomach to drive pain or aversion and thus prevents us from observing reductions in food intake with this manipulation.

Recent advancements in neurobiology have identified distinct vagal neuronal subtypes responsible for sensing gastrointestinal stretch, particularly vagal afferents expressing GLP-1R or OxtR [9,10,27]. Previous work using chemogenetic or optogenetic activation of these GLP-1R or OxtR-expressing vagal neurons have demonstrated significant suppression of food intake [8,9]. Notably, one study demonstrated that activating these neurons attenuated hunger-promoting Agouti-related Peptide (AgRP) neurons in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, thereby implicating a gut-brain axis in which mechanosensory vagal input regulates central feeding circuits [9]. In the same study, mannitol-induced intestinal stretch also decreased food intake and reduced AgRP neuronal activity, indirectly linking intestinal mechanosensation with vagal OxtR signaling. However, that study did not examine neuronal responses in the NTS, a critical brainstem region that processes vagal input from the gut. Adding to this complexity, another study demonstrated that GLP-1R vagal neuron inhibition had no significant effect on basal feeding behavior [8]. Consistent with this, our experiments using chemogenetic inhibition of GLP-1R and OxtR-expressing vagal neurons failed to block the suppression of food intake induced by mannitol-induced intestinal stretch. While it is possible that this genetic strategy did not sufficiently target the neurons of interest as a nodose ganglion injection would, previous work demonstrated that GLP-1R vagal neuron inhibition ameliorates food intake suppression by the nausea-inducing agent LiCl [8]. This suggests that other vagal subtypes could be mediating the herein described effect or even enteric neural circuits.

The robust increase in basal insulin following mannitol treatment provides a direct mechanism for improved glucose tolerance, while reductions in GIP and serotonin suggest that classic incretin and enterochromaffin signaling are not required. The improved basal insulin and subsequent improved oral glucose tolerance is reminiscent of the cephalic insulin which postulates that meal anticipation drives an increase in insulin via a neural network that primes the system to benefit nutrient deposition response [21]. Our findings highlight a gut–pancreas axis whereby mechanical stimulation of the intestine engages insulin-dependent glucose control, offering a distinct mechanism from nutrient-driven hormonal pathways.

Of particular interest is our observation that compared to mock gavage, diet induced obese animals had blunted feeding responses to mannitol. At the same time, the NTS, AP, and DMV had similar levels of cfos expression in response to mannitol (Fig 6). Given that previous work has demonstrated a role of AgRP neurons in sensing mannitol induced stretch, and AgRP neurons are overactive in obesity, these data suggest that either the intestinal stretch sensing from the NTS to higher brain centers is impaired and/or that the AgRP neuronal activation is preventing the suppression of feeding via intestinal stretch.

Notably, we observed that the AP exhibited increased cFos labeling in response to mannitol, and this effect was more pronounced in HFD-fed animals (Fig. 6). However, cFos was not reduced relative to obese controls following VSG or dietary weight loss, even though these interventions restored the feeding-suppressive effects of mannitol. While this dissociation could suggest that AP activation is not required for the behavioral response to intestinal stretch, another possibility is that different neurons within the AP are being activated by mannitol in obesity vs. VSG and/or dietary-induced weight loss. Indeed, the AP is more highly activated by sucrose and olive oil after VSG [28]. Future studies identifying the neurons activated by these differing stimuli will be critical in understanding the AP's role in response to mannitol.

Another interesting aspect of our study is the restoration of intestinal stretch induced satiety following weight loss achieved through either dietary intervention or VSG (Fig 7). Interestingly, weight loss via dietary intervention was able to restore mannitol induced suppression of food despite modest weight loss, supporting the idea that both diet composition and body weight contribute to the mannitol induced metabolic improvements. Future studies will assess the temporal dynamics of diet switch will be critical to delineate this. Importantly, half the volume of mannitol required to reduce food intake in chow-fed mice, reduced food intake in VSG mice (Figure 1 vs. Fig 7). This finding, coupled with our observation that gut vs. circulatory glucose has greater impact on neuronal activation in VSG animals, suggests bariatric surgery fundamentally alters gut-brain communication pathways to enhance sensitivity to intestinal signals. This heightened sensitivity may represent a key mechanism underlying the superior efficacy and durability of surgical weight loss interventions compared to dietary approaches alone.

Our study has several limitations. Although we acknowledge the lack of specificity within our genetic and pharmacological models, the data strongly support the idea that intestinal stretch engages a broader, more distributed set of circuits than previously appreciated. While mannitol has been used to induce intestinal stretch, few have comprehensively characterized all of its systemic effects [9,29,30]. We observed that mannitol increased plasma osmolality (Fig 1), which independently reduces food intake, an effect that is mitigated by maintaining water access (which our mice had during food intake studies) [31]. However, it is possible that the small increase in osmolality is sufficient to activate intestinal vagal G-coupled protein 65 (GPR65) afferents that serve dual functions as both osmoreceptors and chemoreceptors, that could impact the feeding behaviors we see upon mannitol induced intestinal stretch [10,32]. Although mannitol primarily induces intestinal distension, we cannot completely exclude contributions from gastric mechanosensation with the current methods. As such, future studies employing more selective approaches will be important to distinguish contributions of stomach versus intestine to the observed feeding and glycemic responses. Lastly, the improvements in oral glucose tolerance observed in mannitol-treated animals may reflect a reduction in postprandial glucose excursions through decreased intestinal glucose absorption. Future studies will be required to directly test this possibility.

In conclusion, our study reveals a previously underappreciated role for intestinal mechanosensation in regulating food intake and glucose homeostasis, independent of nutrient sensing, gastric distension, or intestinal vagal GLP-1R/OxtR pathways. Specifically, we demonstrated that mannitol-induced intestinal stretch significantly suppresses food intake and enhances oral glucose tolerance without altering insulin secretion or gastric emptying in chow animals. Significantly, the ability of mannitol-induced intestinal stretch to reduce feeding is blunted in obesity. However, weight loss, whether via dietary or surgery interventions, restores the diminished responses to intestinal stretch-induced satiety. These insights support the emerging concept that mechanical sensing by the gut contributes to the regulation of satiation, complementing nutrient and hormone pathways. Although direct clinical application of mannitol-induced stretch is unlikely, our results lay the groundwork for exploring gut-targeted approaches that leverage mechanosensation to support metabolic health.
