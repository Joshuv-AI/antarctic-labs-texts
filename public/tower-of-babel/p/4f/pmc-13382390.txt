Neural systems supporting cognitive reappraisal in young children: An fMRI study
Authors: Michelle Shipkova, Nicolas Murgueitio, Kathryn Garrisi, Amanda Mitchell, Lucy Lurie, Micaela A Rodriguez, Meredith Gruhn, Helen Milojevich, Laura Machlin, Zoe Priddy, Maresa Tate, Tracy Dennis-Tiwary, Sarah Myruski, Katie McLaughlin, Kristen A Lindquist, Margaret A Sheridan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly in development, when emotion regulation strategy use is immature, children rely on caregivers and other social support figures to situate and guide emotional representations and experiences. Using adult scaffolding, young children appear able to implement even complex regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal. However, the neurocognitive substrates employed to accomplish this are currently unknown. The present study examined neural systems underlying scaffolded cognitive reappraisal using a fMRI task in a community sample of N = 148 children (Mage = 6.45 years, SD = 0.92; 52.03% male; 51.4% non-White). In the task, children heard brief scaffolding prompts delivered by either a primary caregiver or an unfamiliar adult (i.e., ‘stranger’) prior to sets of three trials. Each trial in a set consisted of a story describing or reappraising a subsequently presented image, followed by a self-reported affect rating. Images were either negative or neutral. When viewing negative images following scaffolded reappraisal relative to negative descriptive stories, children self-reported fewer negative affective responses. During this contrast, children also preferentially recruited dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus regions, replicating activation patterns observed in older samples during cognitive reappraisal tasks. These results represent one of the first studies of the neural correlates of cognitive reappraisal in early childhood. They reveal that scaffolded reappraisal in early childhood may function similarly to self-generated cognitive reappraisal studied extensively in late childhood and adolescence, extending our current understanding of emotion regulation development.Keywords: Early childhood, FMRI, Cognitive reappraisal, Emotional reactivity

Emotion regulation develops rapidly across early and middle childhood, laying the foundation for later social, academic, and mental health outcomes (Calkins and Marcovitch, 2010, Eisenberg et al., 2010). In early life, regulation is primarily guided externally (e.g., by caregivers or social support figures) and gradually becomes more self-directed during adolescence and adulthood (Gee, 2020, Willner et al., 2022). The capacity to use caregivers to regulate emotional responses begins as early as infancy (Cole et al., 2004, Manian and Bornstein, 2009) and is a key function of early attachment relationships.

During preschool, children begin to advance cognitive skills (e.g., executive functioning, theory of mind) that support their use of a range of cognitive strategies to regulate emotion (Cole et al., 2009, De France and Hollenstein, 2022). Across early and middle childhood, children demonstrate increasing awareness that they can modify thoughts to influence emotions (Bamford and Lagattuta, 2012, Stegge and Meerum Terwogt, 2007), and can describe sophisticated strategies, such as reinterpreting events, as early as age five (Davis et al., 2010).

Among the cognitive strategies that children can use to regulate their emotions, cognitive reappraisal – the process of reframing the meaning of an event through a positive or neutral lens (Gross, 1998) – is particularly notable for its links to adaptive outcomes in older samples (Compas et al., 2017, Gross and John, 2003, McRae et al., 2012b, Wolgast et al., 2011) and its role as a cornerstone of empirically-supported treatments for anxiety and mood disorders (Goldin et al., 2013, Kazantzis et al., 2018). The development of reappraisal capacity coincides with the development of other higher order cognitive processes (e.g., executive function, language; Atzil et al., 2018; Cohodes and Gee, 2023; Davis, 2024; Kopp and Neufeld, 2003; McRae et al., 2012a) often recruited in support of reappraisal. Evidence suggests that with adult support, the capacity of young children to engage in reappraisal begins between ages 3–5 (Stansbury and Sigman, 2000; see Willner et al., 2022 for a review). However, studies using neural measures of reappraisal provide mixed evidence regarding the effectiveness of this strategy prior to adolescence (see Willner et al., 2022). One possibility is that the neural substrates necessary for this cognitive strategy to be effective are not sufficiently developed in early childhood (Ewell et al., 2023, Gogtay et al., 2004). In this case, coaching reappraisal in early childhood would not reliably regulate child experiences on its own. Instead, reappraisal would be most effective at this stage when externally scaffolded by an adult who cues the child to reinterpret the situation and thereby supports the recruitment of emerging regulatory neural circuits. To date, very limited work has examined the neural basis of cognitive reappraisal in early-to-middle childhood. Doing so represents an important step toward understanding the development of emotion regulation and potentially guiding targeted intervention strategies in this age range.

Neural correlates of reappraisal across development

Most empirical research on the neural correlates of reappraisal, concentrated on adolescent and adult samples, suggests that reappraisal is supported by a broad network of regions in prefrontal, temporal, and parietal cortex (Buhle et al., 2014, Kohn et al., 2014, Wager et al., 2008). Specifically, self-generated reappraisal compared to passive viewing of negative images is consistently associated with activation of the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC and vlPFC) implicated in working memory, semantic retrieval, and response inhibition (Cheng et al., 2022, Ochsner and Gross, 2008). In addition, adults recruit the middle temporal gyrus and areas in the temporal parietal junction, implicated in semantic representation, working memory, and attentional control (Buhle et al., 2014, Ochsner et al., 2012). In parallel, reappraisal of negative emotional stimuli is associated with attenuated activation in the amygdala, a region important in salience detection and aversive learning (Buhle et al., 2014, Phelps and LeDoux, 2005).

Compared to adolescent and adult samples, limited research has used neuroimaging to study cognitive reappraisal in children. In the first fMRI study on reappraisal in children, Lévesque and colleagues (2004) instructed girls in middle childhood to downregulate their emotional responses to sad film clips by cognitively distancing themselves (i.e., detaching from the emotional content) and observed that downregulation was associated with activation in lateral PFC, orbitofrontal cortex, medial PFC, right anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and right vlPFC. In another study, Pitskel and colleagues (2011) instructed children from middle childhood through adolescence to passively view or decrease (“pretend its fake”) their emotional responses to disgusting images. Consistent with literature in adults, they reported reduced activation in the insula and amygdala, and increased functional connectivity between the right amygdala and right vlPFC during downregulation trials.

However, children do not appear to be fully mature in their use of reappraisal. During middle childhood, children typically exhibit weaker responses in left vlPFC compared to adolescents and young adults during regulation (McRae et al., 2012a). Multiple studies find linear improvements in amygdala downregulation by reappraisal from middle childhood to late adolescence, indicating that use of reappraisal strategies is more effective with age (Pitskel et al., 2011, Silvers et al., 2015b, Silvers et al., 2017, Stephanou et al., 2016). Studies of children have found variable evidence for dampened amygdala activation during reappraisal, including observations of no change (Lévesque et al., 2004, Pierce et al., 2022), and even enhanced amygdala activation (Dougherty et al., 2015, Silvers et al., 2017).

Additionally, most of these investigations have not considered young children’s unique social context for emotion regulation. In early childhood, achieving regulation is highly dependent on social agents, particularly caregivers (Morris et al., 2007). Prior to age nine, children commonly rely on caregivers to help situate their emotional responses (Calkins, 1994, Rossman, 1992). An important strategy that caregivers use to aid children’s regulation is scaffolding, where they help their child regulate negative emotion more effectively than what their child could achieve independently (Bibok et al., 2009). For example, in response to a negative situation, caregivers can shape children’s appraisals of the situation or provide direct support in implementing regulatory strategies (e.g., reappraisal, deep breathing; Dix, 1991; Mermelshtine, 2017). This may be particularly important in the context of reappraisal because the cognitive demands of engaging this strategy are high for children (e.g., reliance on working memory; McRae et al., 2012b).

Developmentally appropriate paradigms provide scaffolding by giving children reappraisal strategies prior to observing negative stimuli. While providing reappraisal stories may lead to more effective emotion regulation and may be more externally valid for young children, it is unclear the degree to which this approach engages the same neural and cognitive processes that are recruited in the service of reappraisal by adolescents and adults. For example, studies using electroencephalography (EEG) in young children show that amplitudes of the late positive potential [LPP; a component of the EEG signal that is often reduced following reappraisal (Hajcak and Nieuwenhuis, 2006)] were lower when negative stimuli were preceded by hearing reappraisal, rather than descriptive, stories (Hua et al., 2015, Myruski et al., 2019). Thus, it may be that providing children with reappraisal stories results in a similar pattern of neural activation to self-generated reappraisal in adults, consistent with the possibility that this taps a developmentally appropriate version of the same regulatory process. However, it is also possible that scaffolded reappraisal results in a markedly different pattern of activation, consistent with the possibility that scaffolded reappraisal is a fundamentally different process available primarily in early-to-middle childhood when attachment processes are salient (Brumariu, 2015). Considering the central role of the parent as an attachment figure, scaffolded reappraisal with a caregiver may engage a network of regions commonly observed in attachment learning and stress regulation, including the medial PFC and amygdala (Callaghan et al., 2019, Gee et al., 2014). Better documenting the neural and behavioral consequences of scaffolded reappraisal in young children will advance our understanding of the development of emotion regulation.

Purpose of the present study

The present study aims to explore the neural processes underlying emotional reactivity and regulation in early and middle childhood using a developmentally appropriate emotion regulation fMRI paradigm based on a task widely used in adults (Ochsner et al., 2004). This task has been successfully adapted for pediatric samples (Jenness et al., 2020, McLaughlin et al., 2015, McRae et al., 2012a, Silvers et al., 2012, Silvers et al., 2017, Silvers et al., 2015a) and was further adapted here, from prior studies (e.g., Myruski et al., 2019), to accommodate young children and incorporate caregiver- and stranger-scaffolded reappraisal.

We hypothesized that viewing non-reappraised negative, compared to neutral, images would be associated with heightened emotional reactivity, marked by increased neural activation in regions involved in emotion processing (e.g., amygdala, anterior insula) and increased child reports of negative affect, consistent with prior literature (Dougherty et al., 2015, Lindquist et al., 2016). We also expected that scaffolded cognitive reappraisal – collapsed across caregiver and stranger conditions – would reduce self-reported negative affect via increased recruitment of regions associated with cognitive control and semantic processing (e.g., lateral PFC, temporal cortex) and modulated activity in regions involved in emotion processing (e.g., amygdala). Given the wide age range (4–9 years) of our sample, we expected that neural and behavioral effects of scaffolded reappraisal would be more prominent with increasing age, consistent with prior literature (McRae et al., 2012a, Pitskel et al., 2011, Silvers et al., 2012, Silvers et al., 2015a). Finally, in exploratory analyses, we examined differences in children’s neural activation and behavioral reports after the caregiver- compared to the stranger-delivered scaffolds. We predicted that age would interact with the source of scaffolding (caregiver vs. stranger) in predicting both behavioral and neural responses indicative of downregulation. Because caregiver scaffolding of emotion regulation is more common in early childhood, we expected differences between caregiver- and stranger-scaffolded reappraisal to decrease with age. Hypotheses and analytic procedures were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/k98pj.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants consisted of 217 children (49.77% caregiver-reported female; age range: 4.50–9.07 years; M = 6.37, SD = 0.96) and their caregivers who participated in three visits of a larger study conducted from 2018 to 2024 (for details, see Murgueitio et al., 2024). Institutional review board approval was obtained for all study procedures before data collection. All caregivers provided written informed consent to study procedures. Children 7 years of age or older at the time of participation provided written assent to study procedures. Children below age 7 provided verbal assent. Prior to participation in any study visit, participants were screened out if the primary caregiver and child did not endorse speaking and reading English well enough to provide informed consent. Additional exclusion criteria included major medical conditions which would compromise study involvement, known neurological illness, genetic or pervasive developmental disorders, or significant prenatal substance exposure. Children were excluded from the MRI visit if their caregiver reported MRI contraindications or history of concussion. Recruitment strategies emphasized inclusion of families who self-reported verbal or physical family conflict on the Conflict Tactics Scale (Straus, 1979), child protective services involvement, experiences with homelessness, caregiver report of child experiences of trauma, a caregiver having a high school diploma or less, or a child having a minoritized racial or ethnic identity (i.e., a non-White or Hispanic or Latino ethnicity) because these criteria were indices that increased likely exposure to structural inequality or adversity. Thus, this sample was racially and ethnically diverse (see Table 1) with enriched exposure to early adversities.

Of the 217 children, parents of 209 consented to the emotion regulation task. After accounting for missing or poor-quality data (e.g., motion, technical issues, noncompliance), the final neuroimaging sample included 148 participants (52.03% male; 51.4% non-White; age range: 4.61–9.07 years, M = 6.45, SD = 0.92; for age distribution, see Figure S1 of the Supplementary Material), 107 of whom had two usable runs and 41 had one usable run. Behavioral data were available for 194 children, with 161 included in the final analysis after response-based exclusions (51.6% male; age range: 4.50–9.07 years, M = 6.49, SD = 0.97); 123 had two and 38 had one run included. Full details of exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Emotion regulation fMRI task

In the scanner, participants completed two runs of the cognitive reappraisal task (Fig. 1). The first run length was 7 min(m), 47 s(s) (46 trials) and the second was 6 m, 56 s (39 trials). Participants viewed neutral and negative images from the International Affective Picture System (Lang and Bradley, 1999, Lang and Bradley, 2008) and a normed youth image set (publicly available: https://osf.io/43hfq/).

Scaffolded cognitive reappraisal functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) task. Note. A visual representation of the fMRI paradigm used in the current study. Stories that reappraised negatively valenced images were orally delivered to children after a scaffold delivered by their caregiver or a stranger, intended to mimic strategies regularly used by caregivers and other social support figures to help children regulate emotions.

Each trial began with a scaffolding prompt delivered auditorily by either children’s own primary caregiver or a visually-matched unfamiliar adult called “stranger” throughout. While listening to caregiver or stranger reappraisal scaffolding prompts, children viewed a picture of either their smiling caregiver or the smiling stranger. During negative and neutral scaffold conditions, a blank screen was displayed while the scaffold was delivered. Caregiver pictures were provided by the caregiver or taken at in-person sessions. Stranger pictures were obtained from the Face Research Lab London Set (DeBruine and Jones, 2017). An example scaffolding prompt is, “Next you will see some pictures where kids look sick, but don’t worry they will get better.” Caregivers recorded scaffolding prompts before the visit. While all recorded the same prompts, speaking rates varied. Caregiver scaffolds averaged 7.19 s (SD = 0.8 s; min: 4.46 s, max: 11.9 s) and stranger scaffolds averaged 7.70 s (SD = 0.19 s; min: 7.32 s, max: 9.5 s). Stranger scaffolds were on average longer than caregiver scaffolds t(147) = −7.756, p < 0.001.

Immediately following the scaffold, participants were told stories that 1) described the image or 2) reappraised a negatively valenced image. Story recordings were completed by a research assistant unknown to the child and stated in a calm tone. Story duration had a mean of 4.88 s and ranged from 2.27 s to 7.69 s. Descriptive or reappraisal stories, pictures, and rating screens were presented in sets of three. Each image appeared for 3 s. Across all conditions, participants had 3 s after image presentation to rate whether the picture made them feel “thumbs up” or “thumbs down”.

Images were presented in one of four conditions: (1) neutral, (2) negative, (3) stranger-scaffolded reappraisal, and (4) caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal. Neutral images depicted nonsocial content, such as objects or landscapes (e.g., a leaf) and were paired with descriptive stories (e.g., “This is a leaf from a maple tree”). During look negative trials, participants viewed negative images with at least one person (e.g., child in a medical gown laying down) paired with negatively toned descriptive stories (e.g., “This boy is in the hospital because he is very sick. He might not get better”). During reappraisal trials, participants viewed negative images with at least one person (e.g., child getting an injection) paired with a positive reappraisal story (e.g., “This girl is getting a shot to help her body be strong, it’s working!”).

Each condition (neutral, negative, stranger-scaffolded reappraisal, and caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal) included three unique image-story pairs and was presented five times across the two runs, resulting in 15 story-image pairs per condition. A 15-second intertrial interval (ITI), which comprised a black fixation cross on a white screen with the word “WAIT!” displayed above, appeared five times (twice in run 1, three times in run 2) between separate conditions to facilitate deconvolution of the hemodynamic response (Dale, 1999). Stimuli were presented in a pseudorandomized but fixed order across participants. The stimuli, scaffolding prompts, and descriptive stories used are available on OSF: https://osf.io/tj3sh/overview. Prior to scanning, children also completed a training session to familiarize them with the task and ensure comprehension of the rating procedure, detailed in the Supplementary Material.

Data analysis

Behavioral data

Linear regression models were conducted to compare self-reported affect ratings across conditions. Emotional reactivity was operationalized as the percent of negative ratings in the Look Negative compared to the Look Neutral condition. Emotion regulation was operationalized as the percent of negative ratings in all scaffolded Reappraisal conditions compared to the Look Negative condition. To identify the impact of caregiver scaffolding, percent of negative ratings for caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal trials was compared to stranger-scaffolded reappraisal trials. Additionally, two-way analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) examined age-related differences in ratings across conditions. To account for non-independence within families (i.e., there were 22 families in the analytic sample that had 2 participating children with behavioral data and 1 family that had 3), robust standard errors were clustered by family using the R package clubSandwich (Pustejovsky and Tipton, 2023). Although paired-sample t-tests were preregistered for these comparisons, linear regression models with cluster-robust standard errors were used instead to address this non-independence; results from the paired-sample t-tests are reported in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

fMRI data acquisition and image preprocessing

Whole-brain fMRI scans were obtained on one of two 3 Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanners with a 32-channel head coil. Results included in this manuscript come from preprocessing performed using fMRIPrep 23.1.4 (Esteban et al., 2019a, Esteban et al., 2019b; RRID:SCR_016216), which is based on Nipype 1.8.6 (Gorgolewski et al., 2011, Gorgolewski et al., 2018; RRID:SCR_002502). Full details of scan acquisition parameters and detailed preprocessing steps are provided in the Supplementary Material. For each run, a high-pass filter (.008 Hz) was applied to remove low-frequency drift in the time series. Each mean image was globally scaled to a median signal intensity of 10000.

Whole-brain analyses

Individual-level fixed-effects analyses were performed using FSL’s FMRI Expert Analysis Tool (version 6.00). Mixed-effects higher-level analyses were conducted in AFNI using 3dMEMA to account for both blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal magnitude and inter-subject variance. For all contrasts, neural activity was examined while participants viewed the picture stimulus and rated the image “thumbs up” or “thumbs down.” Contrasts of interest included Look Negative > Look Neutral (emotional reactivity), Reappraisal > Look Negative (emotion regulation), and Caregiver-scaffolded Reappraisal > Stranger-scaffolded Reappraisal (caregiver-specific effects on regulation). Caregiver-scaffolded Reappraisal > Look Negative and Stranger-scaffolded Reappraisal > Look Negative were examined in exploratory analyses (see Supplementary Material). Full details of whole-brain fMRI analysis procedures are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Task-related functional connectivity

To assess functional connectivity of amygdala activation under emotional reactivity (Look Negative > Look Neutral) and reappraisal (Reappraisal > Look Negative), we performed generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analyses (McLaren et al., 2012). Time series for right and left amygdala seeds were extracted using FSL’s meants function. For each participant, an individual-level fixed-effects model included regressors for each task condition, the amygdala time series, a term interacting the amygdala time series and the condition of interest, and the same nuisance regressors used in the whole-brain analyses. Within-subject runs were combined using fixed-effects models in FSL, and mixed-effects higher-level analyses were performed in AFNI using 3dMEMA, applying the same cluster-level correction procedures as in whole-brain analyses. This analysis was not preregistered.

Covariates

Whole-brain and PPI analyses controlled for child participants’ exact age at the scanning session, caregiver-reported sex assigned at birth, a binary variable of scanner used, a head coil variable for a subset of participants who had the anterior head coil turned off during the scan (n = 11), child handedness (n = 23 left-handed), and participant medication use that could affect blood flow or cognitive processing (e.g., psychotropic or allergy medications).

In an ANCOVA, we observed a significant condition-by-age interaction, such that the difference in self-reported negative affect between Look Neutral and Look Negative trials increased with age (b = 0.073, SE = 0.025, t(61.9) = 2.89, p = .005), suggesting that older participants showed greater emotional reactivity to negative stimuli. Whole-brain analyses

Participants exhibited significantly greater activation when viewing and rating pictures during Look Negative relative to Look Neutral trials in 8 large clusters with peaks in the bilateral occipital cortex, left precentral/postcentral gyrus, left cerebellum, left supplementary motor area (SMA), left thalamus, and left posterior insula (Table 3; Fig. 2). Activation extended into brain regions including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), right middle frontal gyrus, right inferior frontal gyrus, left angular gyrus, dorsal and subgenual anterior cingulate gyrus, bilateral amygdala, and bilateral precuneus. In a smaller set of regions which included the right precentral/postcentral gyrus (extending to the right angular gyrus), bilateral inferior temporal gyrus, left angular gyrus, dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus, right SMA, left posterior cingulate cortex, left superior frontal gyrus, right posterior insula, and right orbitofrontal cortex, participants exhibited increased activation for Look Neutral compared to Look Negative trials.

Discussion

The present study examined neural processes underlying scaffolded cognitive reappraisal in young children. This is among the first studies to examine neural activation during a scaffolded reappraisal task using fMRI in young children, advancing understanding of how cognitive strategies that modulate emotional experiences develop. Our results demonstrate that this task successfully elicits both emotional reactivity and reappraisal-related neural responses, largely replicating activation patterns observed in older children and adults. This work provides novel evidence that even in early childhood, children engage neural systems implicated in salience detection, cognitive control, and affective meaning-making. At the same time, differences between caregiver- and stranger-scaffolded reappraisal suggest that social context may shape how these regulatory processes are instantiated, potentially through differential engagement of social-affective circuitry. These results extend prior work by demonstrating that scaffolded reappraisal is an effective mechanism for emotion regulation in young children and highlight how supportive external cues may shape emerging regulatory capacities.

Scaffolded reappraisal

Our hypothesis that scaffolded reappraisal would downregulate negative affect and increase activation in brain regions involved in cognitive control was supported. Across scaffolded reappraisal conditions, children reported reduced negative feelings compared to the look negative condition. In addition, children exhibited increased activation of areas involved in high-level cognition and executive function, including the dlPFC, superior frontal gyrus, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), and parietal cortex, as well as areas involved in high-level meaning-making of language, such as the middle temporal gyrus, in response to stranger- and caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal. Activation in these regions is commonly observed when adults engage in reappraisal (Buhle et al., 2014) and aligns with findings in older children using self-generated reappraisal (Lévesque et al., 2004). Together, these observations suggest that scaffolded reappraisal supports children’s regulatory processes via recruitment of brain regions involved in higher-order cognition and linguistic interpretation.

Children also showed increased bilateral anterior insula and right posterior insula activation during reappraisal. Whereas negative picture viewing was associated with greater left posterior insula activation, consistent with heightened interoceptive responses to affective salience (Craig, 2009), reappraisal recruited right posterior and bilateral anterior insula, suggesting that even in early childhood, emotion regulation engages networks that integrate bodily and homeostatic signals with subjective awareness (Menon and Uddin, 2010). Findings align with adult studies showing anterior insula involvement in salience detection and emotion regulation (Buhle et al., 2014, Kohn et al., 2014; see also Cauda et al., 2012).

We observed one notable inconsistency with prior research examining neural correlates of reappraisal. vlPFC activation has been associated with reappraisal in middle childhood (Lévesque et al., 2004) and adulthood (Buhle et al., 2014, Dörfel et al., 2014, Kohn et al., 2014). One study showed that children exhibit weaker vlPFC activation compared to adolescents and adults (McRae et al., 2012a). We did not observe vlPFC activation, potentially consistent with this finding. Older children might recruit the vlPFC to initiate or engage in complex mental strategies, such as using semantic knowledge to generate reappraisals (Cheng et al., 2022). In our task, this process is achieved externally through reappraisal stories provided to participants, and thus does not require activation of the vlPFC for successful reappraisal.

Our hypothesis that age would be associated with behavioral and neural differences during scaffolded reappraisal was not supported. This contrasts with prior developmental studies examining self-generated reappraisal which show age-related refinements across childhood (McRae et al., 2012a, Pitskel et al., 2011). The lack of age-related change in reappraisal here is likely due to this task’s scaffolded nature. In this developmentally appropriate paradigm, children were provided with reappraisals rather than required to generate them independently. Under these conditions, even young children successfully recruited cognitive control and semantic meaning-making regions in the service of regulation. These findings align with literature documenting the importance of adult scaffolding in supporting children’s regulation (Calkins and Hill, 2007, Cole et al., 2004, Dix, 1991). We extend this work by demonstrating that scaffolded reappraisal operates through activation of the same neural networks engaged by older children and adults during self-generated reappraisal.

Caregiver- vs. stranger-scaffolded reappraisal

Interestingly, children showed greater regulation in response to stranger-scaffolded reappraisal compared to caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal. This contrasts with developmental literature conceptualizing caregivers as external cues that aid children’s regulation (Callaghan and Tottenham, 2016, Gee et al., 2014, Myruski and Dennis-Tiwary, 2021), in part because humans are altricial species that rely on caregivers for survival and allostasis (Ainsworth and Bell, 1970, Campos et al., 1975, Hofer, 1994, McCoy and Masters, 1985). However, little research directly compares the effectiveness of caregiver support with regulatory scaffolding provided by non-caregivers.

One possible explanation is that caregivers’ roles as regulatory cues are most effective during automatic versus controlled processes. Most prior research in this area has focused on reactivity and automatic regulation processes which occur without explicit intent (e.g., caregiver presence or warmth; Doom et al., 2017; Gee et al., 2014; Stevens et al., 2023). Thus, caregivers might exert stronger effects on processes that are nonconscious, engage few top-down neural resources, and lack explicit regulatory intent (Braunstein et al., 2017). These interpretations align with theoretical models of attachment (Bowlby, 1969), and cross-species studies on caregiver regulation of implicit processes (Moriceau and Sullivan, 2006, Robinson-Drummer et al., 2019). Another possibility is that the caregiver scaffold was sufficiently arousing to compromise children’s reappraisal capacities. Caregivers are uniquely salient stimuli for children. Indeed, fMRI studies show that caregiver cues activate widespread salience, cognitive control, default mode, reward, somatosensory, and attention networks (Abrams et al., 2016, Valadez et al., 2024), a pattern also observed in the current sample (Murgueitio et al., 2026). Consistent with this account, caregiver-scaffolded reappraisal was linked to greater amygdala, occipital cortex, and thalamus activation than stranger-scaffolded reappraisal. Within this framework, caregivers may evoke high-arousal positive emotions that support automatic forms of regulation (e.g., safety signaling) while hindering effortful reappraisal in structured task contexts. In contrast, the reduced salience of a stranger may facilitate engagement of higher-order cognitive resources.

Overall, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of caregiver versus stranger scaffolding may depend on the type of regulatory process and the emotional salience of the social cue. Although these dynamics may be further shaped by characteristics of the present sample, drawn from a cohort with elevated adversity exposure, subsequent analyses did not indicate that adversity exposure was systematically related to the differences in caregiver- versus stranger-scaffolded reappraisal (Shipkova et al., under review). Future work examining caregiver functioning and attachment is needed to clarify how relational factors modulate the effectiveness of caregiver versus stranger scaffolding.

Emotional reactivity

During passive viewing of negative relative to neutral stimuli, children recruited neural systems involved in semantic knowledge and valuation to generate and make sense of their emotional experiences (Satpute and Lindquist, 2021), as well as regions supporting attentional capture and maintenance, independent of age and sex. As with reappraisal, activation patterns during scaffolded reactivity resembled those observed in older children, adolescents, and adults.

Children showed greater activation in the amygdala, dACC, and left posterior insula during negative image viewing, consistent with findings in older samples (ages 6–10 in Dougherty et al., 2015) and meta-analytic evidence showing salience network engagement in response to affective stimuli across the lifespan (Kober et al., 2008, Lindquist et al., 2016). We also observed increased activation in vmPFC and right dlPFC, aligning with prior findings in early childhood (Dougherty et al., 2015, Pitskel et al., 2011) and adolescence (Padgaonkar et al., 2021) showing increased dlPFC activation during emotional reactivity. However, this finding contrasts with developmental work reporting decreased vmPFC and dlPFC activation during emotional reactivity (Silvers et al., 2017). Increased dlPFC recruitment may reflect orienting toward and maintaining attention to negative stimuli, supporting engagement of cognitive control and working memory processes (Dolcos and McCarthy, 2006). Because the vmPFC supports the construction and contextualization of emotional meaning (Ochsner et al., 2012), the stories describing negative images prior to their presentation may have promoted its recruitment.

Consistent with meta-analytic evidence (Brooks et al., 2016) and prior work (Padgaonkar et al., 2021, Pitskel et al., 2011, Silvers et al., 2017), participants showed increased activation of the inferior frontal gyrus, involved in semantic retrieval, in response to negative versus neutral stimuli (Grindrod et al., 2008, Thompson-Schill et al., 1997). We also observed activation in the temporal poles, a heteromodal association area implicated in semantic processing and concept representation (Ralph, 2014, Ralph et al., 2009), and the angular gyrus, which integrates auditory and visual input to support functions including semantic processing, visual-spatial attention, and social cognition (Seghier, 2013). A lateralized pattern emerged such that the right angular gyrus was more active during negative (vs. neutral) viewing, whereas the left was more active during neutral (vs. negative) viewing. This dissociation aligns with literature linking right angular gyrus activation to attentional reorienting toward and encoding of salient information (Singh-Curry and Husain, 2009) and left activation to conceptual processing (Seghier, 2013).

Further, we observed greater activation in the occipital cortex, with clusters spanning early and late stages of visual processing within the ventral stream. This activation aligns with prior work (Padgaonkar et al., 2021, Pitskel et al., 2011, Silvers et al., 2017) and may indicate enhanced salience and sensory processing of negative compared to neutral stimuli. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that neural responses in occipitotemporal cortex are shaped by intermediate perceptual properties (Long et al., 2018) and with studies linking occipital cortex to salience-related regions (e.g., amygdala) in processing affective stimuli (Hendler et al., 2003, Kober et al., 2008).

Finally, across analyses, we observed greater cerebellum activation during the look negative condition, consistent with other work (Lindquist et al., 2012). This finding aligns with growing evidence extending the cerebellum’s role beyond autonomic and motor functions to predictive aspects of emotional processing via cerebellar-cortical loops with prefrontal and limbic regions (Kober et al., 2008, Middleton and Strick, 2001, Schmahmann and Sherman, 1998). In this study, cerebellum activation during negative (versus neutral or reappraisal) trials may reflect coordination of motor and contextual responses to negative stimuli. Such engagement may be particularly important in children to support emotional processing before prefrontal circuits fully mature and represents an interesting avenue for research.

In addition to regional activation, we assessed task-dependent functional connectivity to characterize how the amygdala interacts with other brain regions during children’s perception of negative stimuli. During passive viewing of negative relative to neutral images, children showed reduced connectivity between the left amygdala and right anterior superior temporal gyrus. The superior temporal gyrus is a heteromodal association area involved in high-level cognitive processing, including social information processing (Baron-Cohen et al., 1999), mental state attribution (Saxe and Kanwisher, 2003), and representation of abstract social concepts (Zahn et al., 2007). Reduced coupling suggests that when young children encounter negative versus neutral stimuli, there may be less integration between amygdala-driven salience responses and areas involved in higher-order social-conceptual processing.

Limitations and future directions

This study represents an important step in examining the neural underpinnings of cognitive reappraisal in early childhood. It is the first fMRI study to investigate scaffolded reappraisal in children as young as four years old. The distinct advantages of this developmentally-sensitive paradigm, compared to classic emotion regulation tasks, include more closely mirroring the ways in which reappraisal is utilized in children’s daily lives and limiting the cognitive load for young participants. However, several limitations remain. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to assess developmental change. Future longitudinal studies can chart trajectories of neural systems underlying reappraisal across early and middle childhood. In addition, the lateralized pre- and post-central gyrus effects likely reflect task-related motor activity associated with button presses. Because the picture-viewing regressor was not temporally separated from the button-press response and the “thumbs up” and “thumbs down” responses were made with the left and right hands, respectively, motor-related activation likely contributed to the observed effects. The use of multiband acceleration to achieve a short TR in the present study may also reduce subcortical temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR). Our inspections of tSNR (see Supplementary Material) confirmed sufficient signal quality in the amygdala ROIs to detect task-related activation following preprocessing, though preprocessing may not fully compensate for low underlying tSNR. Further, while this study compared caregiver- and stranger-scaffolded reappraisal, we cannot directly assess which specific strategies are most effective when administered by a caregiver that may uniquely facilitate emotion regulation. It is possible that some strategies are particularly effective when delivered by individuals who are less familiar or emotionally engaged with the child (e.g., strangers), but we cannot test this hypothesis with the current design. Future work incorporating dyadic, caregiver-specific strategies can clarify how caregiver involvement shapes scaffolded reappraisal. Because the present sample spanned a relatively wide age range, caregiver-specific advantages that might be more apparent in younger children may have been attenuated. Additionally, future studies should aim to replicate the findings presented here across different populations and in more representative samples.

Conclusion

The current study is among the first to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying emotional reactivity and scaffolded cognitive reappraisal in young children using fMRI. During passive viewing of negative versus neutral stimuli, children showed heightened emotional reactivity and increased neural activation in regions involved in salience detection, semantic processing, valuation, and cognitive control. When provided with scaffolded reappraisal, they successfully downregulated negative affect and recruited regions of the cognitive control network. Across both reactivity and reappraisal, activation patterns in young children were similar to those observed in adolescents and adults. This is remarkable, given the young age of participants and the common observation that emotion regulation by observation and parent report improves dramatically with age. We surmise that the developmentally appropriate nature of the task allowed even young children to recruit these regions for successful reappraisal. Finally, stranger-supported reappraisal led to more mature activation patterns and better reappraisal than caregiver-supported reappraisal. This finding, while surprising, highlights potential differences between reappraisal in a structured task and caregiver-mediated regulation processes associated with attachment. Altogether, these findings build on a growing body of work investigating the development of neural systems underlying cognitive reappraisal.
