Extracellular vesicles derived from Msh homeobox 1 (Msx1)-overexpressing mesenchymal stem cells improve digit tip regeneration in an amputee mice model
Authors: Sahar Shemshadi, Faezeh Shekari, Mohamadreza Baghaban Eslaminejad, Leila Taghiyar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn adult mammals, limb regeneration is limited by the absence of blastemal cells (BCs) and the lack of the regenerative signaling cascade. The utilization of transgenic cells circumvents the limitations associated with the absence of BCs. In a previous investigation, we successfully regenerated mouse phalanx amputations using blastema-like cells (BlCs) generated from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (mBMSCs) overexpressing Msx1 and Msx2 genes. Recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as potent biological tools, offering a promising alternative to manipulated cells for clinical applications. This research focuses on utilizing BlCs-derived extracellular vesicles (BlCs-EVs) for regenerating mouse digit tips. The BlCs were cultured and expanded, and then EVs were isolated via ultracentrifugation. The size, morphology, and CD81 marker expression of the EVs were confirmed through Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM), and Western Blot (WB) analyses. Additionally, WB analysis demonstrated the presence of MSX1, MSX2, FGF8, and BMP4 proteins. The uptake of EVs by mBMSCs was shown through immunostaining. Effects on cell proliferation, migration, and osteogenic activity post-treatment with BlCs-EVs were assessed through MTT assay, scratch assay, and Real-time PCR. The regenerative potential of BlCs-EVs was evaluated in a mouse digit tip amputation model using histological assessments. Results indicated that BlCs-EVs enhanced several abilities of mBMSCs, such as migration, proliferation, and osteogenesis in vitro. Notably, BlCs-EVs significantly improved digit tip regeneration in mice, promoting the formation of new bone and nails, which was absent in control groups. In summary, BlCs-EVs are promising tools for digit tip regeneration, avoiding the ethical concerns associated with using genetically modified cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72647-x.Keywords: Extracellular Vesicles, Msx1 gene, Proliferation, Osteogenesis, And Digit Tip RegenerationSubject terms: Cell biology, Developmental biology, Stem cells

Introduction

Complete digit and limb regeneration is a routine phenomenon in salamanders, rarely observed in neonatal mice and not in adult mammals1. Scar-free limb regeneration in salamanders is controlled by the formation of blastema cells (BCs) mediated by epimorphic regeneration and the expression of regenerative genes such as fibroblast growth factor (Fgf), bone morphogenetic protein (Bmp), sonic hedgehog (Shh), and Msh homeobox (Msx) genes2.

In other words, the unique features of BCs, their positional information, and related gene expression play important roles in complete limb regeneration. For example, Tanaka et al. demonstrated the importance of positional information in salamander limb regeneration. They showed that the location of Shh and Fgf8 genes expressed in the anterior or posterior positions of the blastema mass is the key factor in blastema outgrowth manifestation in axolotls. They found out that the Fgf8 and Shh genes are only expressed in the anterior and posterior mesenchymal cells. Therefore, BCs formed solely of anterior tissue were not capable of composing a limb and degenerated. However, activation of Shh signaling in these cells could rescue anterior blastema formation and Fgf8 expression. Conversely, in a blastema formed solely of posterior tissue, Shh signaling was insufficient, and ectopic expression of Fgf8 was required for Shh activity and limb regeneration3.

Due to the importance of genes carrying information that affects positional identity, in our previous study, we created blastema-like cell lines (BlCs) by overexpressing the Hox gene family, including Msx1 and Msx2, into mouse bone marrow-derived MSCs (mBMSCs) for digit tip regeneration4. Msx genes are involved in the positional information of mBMSCs. Our results showed that BlCs could significantly improve proximal digit tip amputation compared with mBMSCs as a control group. Previous studies have reported that Msx genes are directly involved in mesenchymal cell proliferation and bone formation by regulating the Fgf and Bmp signaling pathways5–8.

Recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs), such as MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs), have shown favorable regenerative therapeutic effects in various disease models, such as renal or limb ischemia9–12. EVs are small, lipid bilayer nanoparticles of approximately 70–150 nm that are secreted by cells13. The main clinical applications of EVs are their use as biomarkers, cell-free therapeutic agents, drug delivery carriers, cancer vaccines, induction of apoptosis and proliferation, and differentiation of target cells14–16. Most evidence has suggested that EVs have similar treatment impacts to their parental cells. Accordingly, the anti-apoptotic, immunomodulatory, and pro-angiogenic properties of MSC-derived exosomes and, more importantly, their ability to avoid ethical objections concerning transgenic cells have been well documented17–20. For instance, MSCs-EV increases regulatory T-cell (Treg) production in mice with an allogeneic skin graft or a graft-versus-host disease21,22. Therefore, it is expected that EVs represent the main properties of parent cells, such as immunomodulators or therapeutic effects on the target tissue, and solve the problems caused by cell therapy23–26. Yang et al. used human umbilical cord mesenchymal cell-derived exosomes (hUCMSC-Exos) in a full-thickness cutaneous wound in a diabetic rat model27. hUCMSC-Exosomes demonstrated a significant increase in the rate of wound closure, elevated expression of the CD31 and Ki67 genes, regeneration of granulation tissue, and upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGFβ-1).

More recently, EVs have shown therapeutic abilities to repair damaged cartilage and bone tissue in cartilage-related diseases including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis28–30.

Therefore, we explored the ability of EVs isolated from BlCs (BlCs-EVs) produced by Msx1 induction into MSCs to enhance limb regeneration4. In our previous study, BlCs showed an increase in the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs in vitro and an acceptable regenerative effect on digit tip amputated in mice. Due to this therapeutic effect, BlCs-EVs can be transplanted into the digit tip and amputation site, enabling the exertion of their biological effects.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we aimed to improve digit tip regeneration in a digit tip amputee mouse model by externally applying EVs extracted from BlCs (Msx1-overexpressing MSCs). We investigated whether BlCs-EVs could improve the limb regeneration rate in comparison with mBMSCs-EV in the amputated digit tip model. Furthermore, we assessed the effect of BlCs-EV on migration, cell proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of mBMSCs, in addition to the therapeutic effect of BlCs-EVs on digit tip regeneration.

Blastema-like cells (BlCs) and their characterization

We found out that BICs are highly positive for the Msx1 gene, we observed a pure cell population due to GFP+ sorting cells before they were frozen (Fig. 2A, a-c). We also observed that the expression level of the Msx1 gene was approximately 4500-fold greater than that of the native Mxs1 gene of mBMSCs (****p < 0.0001). The Msx2 gene was upregulated by 4000-fold in BlCs compared with mBMSCs as a control group (****p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B, a-b). We also examined the expression of Bmp4 and Fgf8, which are downstream of the Msx1 gene. As a result, the Bmp4 and Fgf8 genes were expressed 300- and 150-fold, respectively, in BlCs with a significant difference compared with the mBMSCs as a control group (****p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B, c-d).

Extraction and characteristics of the EVs

We collected approximately 3–4-L supernatant from cultured BlCs and mBMSCs (control group). Pellets containing 1200 µg and 1000 µg of EVs were obtained following the ultracentrifugation method at 100,000 g for 30 min, for BlCs and mBMSCs, respectively. For EV identification, the expression of CD81, one of the main EV surface markers, was measured using western blotting (BW) (Fig. 4A). We also used SEM to study the size and morphology of the EVs. As shown in Fig. 4B, the EVs were spherical with diameters ranging from 100 to 150 nm. These EVs were somewhat homogeneous, and we did not observe impurity particles. Using the DLS test, the size of the EVs was within the range of 70–150 nm (Fig. 4C), which confirms our findings.

EV-induced cells showed a significant increase on day 3 compared with the control group (*p < 0.05) (Fig. 6C). Evaluation of the osteogenic Effect of BlCs-EV on mBMSCs

We assessed the osteogenic activity of BlCs-EV on mBMSCs at various time points, including culture days 7, 14, and 21, using ARS and RT-PCR methods. Small nodule-like aggregation formation began during the first week of induction with an ascending process. At the end of the third week, many secretions of ECM and nodular aggregates were observed in comparison with the control group (Fig. 7A, a-l). A real-time PCR study revealed that the Col I gene expression level was progressively upregulated within 3 weeks in a similar pattern to Runx2 and Ocn genes, and no significant difference was observed between EV-DMEM and EV-Osteogenic groups (Fig. 7B, a). The Runx2 gene as an early marker was measured on different days of incubation, 7, 14, and 21 days (Fig. 7B, c). After 7 days, we observed a nonsignificant increase in the Runx2 gene expression level in the osteogenic, EV-DMEM, and EV-osteogenic groups toward each other. However, its expression was significantly increased compared with the control group. The Runx2 gene expression level significantly increased in all groups on days 14 and 21 (****p ≤ 0.0001). Overall, the fold changes for all genes were comparable between EV-DMEM and osteogenic groups at all time points. All genes were expressed more significantly in the EV-osteogenic group than in the other experimental groups. The Ocn gene also showed a pattern of expression similar to the Runx2 gene. We observed a significant difference in the expression level of the Ocn gene between the EV-DMEM and EV-osteogenic groups after 14 and 21 days (*p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 7B, b).

Digit tip regeneration

First, we performed a proximally digit-tip amputee model that could not spontaneously regenerate after 8 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 4). Immediately after a digit-tip amputation, wound healing at the amputee site was directed to a scar formation in the Untreated and Sham groups. We observed a closure in the epidermis completed in 4–5 days post-amputation (4–5 DPA) without any new bone or nail formation. Following the removal of blood clot from the amputation site, BlCs-EV and mBMSCs were injected between the wound epidermis and underlying tissues. Whole mount digit tip evaluation at 8 weeks post-implantation (8 WPI) showed that the epidermal closure occurred in all experimental and control groups. We observed no regeneration in the Untreated and Sham groups as expected (Fig. 8A, b,c). In addition, in the mBMSCs-EV group, an inadequate regeneration of the proximal regions occurred in digit tips (Fig. 8A, d). A compact bone and abnormal nail organ formation without any elongation occurred in this group. Furthermore, we observed a normal elongated bone and new nail formation in the BlCs-EV group (75%, n = 6) (Fig. 8A, e) that was not as growing as the Intact (n = 10) (Supplementary Table 3).

Whole mount and histological analysis of digit tip regeneration

As illustrated in the whole mount (macroscopic) assessment of Fig. 8A, a-e the best digit tip regeneration was observed in the BlCs-EV and mBMSCS-EV groups in comparison with Untreated and Sham groups. Accurate Histological analysis showed that an epidermal closure occurred in all groups (Fig. 8A, f-j). Our histological staining (H&E, AL&AL, and Masson’s trichrome) investigation indicated that no new tissue formation such as cartilage, nail, and bone stump occurred in the Untreated and Sham groups (Fig. 8A, g, h, l, m, r, s). In the EV-injected groups, the distal end of the bone stump formed a bony cap, similar to a short bone callus. Also, an abnormal nail was observed in the mBMSCs-EV group. In addition, Masson’s trichrome and AL&AL staining specifically showed that more new bone formation and elongation occurred in the BlCs-EV group than mBMSCs-EV group in the generated bone stump (Fig. 8A, n, p, t, w). The bone callus typically was formed in the BlCs-EV group in a regenerated digit site after 8 WPI, which was very similar to the Intact group. The normal nail was formed in the BlCs-EV group similar to the nail of the Intact group, although it was shorter (Fig. 8A, p, w). The bone length measurement in Fig. 8B did not show any bone growth in the Untreated and Sham groups compared with the intact group. The bone length significantly increased in EV groups. However, it did not grow as much as the Untreated group (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Digit tip regeneration occurs primarily in the distal region of the terminal phalanx in neonatal mammals, including mice and humans31. The regenerative process involves undifferentiated cells known as blastema cells (BCs), which form just beneath the amputation site32. Deficiencies in BCs, along with the absence of related genes like HOX genes, contribute to digit tip repair failure in adult mice. Notably, Hox 7 (Msx1) and Hox 8 (Msx2) genes—part of the homeobox gene family—play a crucial role in positional information and digit tip elongation. These genes are absent in other cell sources, such as MSCs, highlighting the unique regenerative ability of BCs33. In a previous study, we developed a novel BCs-like cell line (BlCs) by overexpressing the Msx1 gene in mBMSCs, and we investigated BlCs’ regenerative potential in proximally amputated digit tip4.

BlCs not only exhibited superior amputation repair capabilities compared to mBMSCs but also demonstrated intriguing properties such as enhanced proliferation and osteogenic differentiation. However, due to their transgenic nature, BlCs cannot be utilized in regenerative medicine. Recently, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a novel therapeutic agent with immunomodulatory and regenerative potential. In our study, we isolated EVs from BlCs (BlCs-EV) and assessed their effects on proliferation, migration, and osteogenesis in vitro. Additionally, we investigated their regenerative capacity in an adult mouse digit tip amputee model.

Our findings indicate that BlCs-EV can promote the differentiation of mBMSCs into osteocytes and enhance cell migration and proliferation in vitro. This underscores the similarity between EVs and their parental cells. Importantly, BlCs-EV exhibited regenerative potential for digit tip regeneration in adult mice. While previous studies have highlighted MSCs’ regenerative and anti-inflammatory properties in animal models, they remain insufficient for digit tip regeneration. Notably, MSCs-EVs also do not effectively support digit tip regeneration.

Transgenic manipulation has recently been attempted to obtain altered EVs34,35. In this study, we showed that BlCs-EV have regenerative effects such as the BlCs, origin cells, that we reported in our previous study.

Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from transgenic cells as cell-free therapies for various diseases. For example, Joen et al. demonstrated that EVs extracted from Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV)-infected cells can activate the innate immune response against viral infections. Kaposi’s Sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) is the pathogenic agent of Kaposi’s sarcoma, which is the most common cancer in acquired immune deficiency syndrome patients36.

In a separate study, Mizrak and colleagues created genetically engineered microvesicles (MVs) by enhancing the expression of a suicide gene and fusing it with protein-cytosine deaminase (CD) and uracil phosphoribosyltransferase (UPRT) in MV-secreting cells. These isolated MVs were then directly injected into pre-existing nerve sheath tumors (schwannomas) in a mouse model. Remarkably, the MVs exhibited tumor regression similar to that observed in their parent cells37.

The migration of reparative cells to the defect site is crucial for tissue repair. EVs play a key role in promoting regeneration by controlling immune responses and encouraging cell migration. Our analysis of cell migration revealed that BlCs-EV directly influences this process (see Fig. 6A). According to Ferreira et al., EVs derived from adipose tissue mesenchymal stem cells can enhance the migration and growth of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, as well as facilitate wound healing in living organisms. It was found that miR-205, via the AKT phosphorylation pathway, regulates cell migration and proliferation. Consequently, a reduction in miR-205 leads to decreased AKT phosphorylation in fibroblasts and keratinocytes, resulting in reduced migration of keratinocytes. Conversely, treatment with EVs activates the AKT pathway independently of miR-205 knockdown38.

In another investigation, Pan et al. demonstrated that extracellular vesicles (EVs) originating from glioblastoma cell lines enhanced the movement and growth of neural progenitor cells (NPCs) by activating the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway. Consequently, when the PI3K-Akt pathway in EVs from glioblastoma cells was disrupted, it led to the inhibition of proliferation and migration in treated NPCs39. While our research did not delve into the mechanism behind the migration of BlCs-EVs in cells, the noticeable cell motion suggested its influence. Previous studies have illustrated that oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) migrated towards FGF8-soaked heparin beads, a significant factor in cell migration, in a demyelinated mouse model40. Therefore, it appears that a heightened expression of the Fgf8 gene within BlCs-EVs significantly corresponds to an upsurge in cell migration and proliferation in cultured mBMSCs.

The growth of the vertebrate limb bud is a result of an interactive process between the mesenchyme and a specialized ectodermal region, leading to the activation of signaling pathways involving genes such as HOXs, FGFs, and BMPs41,42. Consequently, the inhibition of key genes associated with limb bud development can result in early embryonic abnormalities characterized by limb absence43.

BMP genes are part of the growth and differentiation factors responsible for driving the differentiation of MSCs into cartilage and bone tissues44. In our study, we noted a significant increase in the expression of the Bmp4 gene, along with other bone-related genes like Runx2, Ocn, and Col I, in cells treated with EVs after a 21-day induction period (Fig. 7).

We investigated the regenerative capacity of BlCs-EV and mBMSCs-EV in a non-regenerative adult mouse model of digit tip injury. This model involved the removal of the bone and nail from the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th phalanxes; while preserving the cartilage tissue of the joints and stump bone4. In our proximal digit tip amputation experiment, there was no growth observed in skeletal components like bone and nail (n = 10) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Our previous research identified a combination of cells induced by Msx1 and Msx2, referred to as the MSX1/2 group, as the most effective in terms of regenerative potential, comparable to the Intact group4. It is important to mention that in the current study, we only utilized EVs derived from Msx1-transduced cells, as practical constraints prevented the use of EVs from Msx2-transduced cells. Nevertheless, in the Msx1-transduced cells, we observed a satisfactory expression of the Msx2 gene through Western blotting analysis (Fig. 4D). Moreover, in the MSX1/2 group, we observed a synergistic impact of both endogenous and exogenous Msx2 and Msx1 gene expression, leading to complete regeneration of the digit tip. In this study, we focused solely on the effects of BlCs-EVs secreted by cells transduced with the Msx1 gene on digit tip regeneration. The histological findings depicted in Fig. 8 reveal that the most robust digit tip regeneration occurred in the BlCs-EV group (Fig. 8E, J, P, W) compared to other groups. This underscores the notion that incomplete digit tip regeneration, attributed to the absence of BlCs-EVs derived from cells transduced with the Msx2 gene, is logical and expected. Therefore, our future research will investigate the combined impact of MSX1-EVs and MSX2-EVs on digit tip regeneration.

Methods

The study with No. IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1398.100 ethical code, was carried out in Royan Institute, ACECR, Tehran, Iran. The Procedures for the care and use of mice were performed by the standard operating procedures approved by the Institution’s Animal Care & Ethics Committee of Royan Institute, ACECR, Tehran Iran. All methods were performed under the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported following ARRIVE guidelines.

Blastema-like cell culture, expansion, and characterization

The Msx1 transduced mesenchymal stem cells or blastema-like cells (BlCs) were obtained from our previous study4. Briefly, Frozen BlCs were thawed and seeded in a concentration of (1–2) ×106 cells into a T75 flask in a culture medium containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 units/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 2 mM glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich) incubated at humidified 5% CO2 at 37◦C with media changes every 3 days. When cell density reached 75–80% of confluency, the cells were trypsinized and transferred to a new culture dish. Subsequently, increased cells were characterized in terms of the morphology, viability, and expression of Msx1, Msx2, Fgf8, and Bmp4 genes.

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell (mBMSC) isolation, culture, and expansion

In the present study, we used 6–8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Mesenchymal stem cells were isolated from bone marrow (mBMSC). Briefly, after ketamine-xylazine-anesthetize, bone marrow was flushed out of the femur and tibia bones and suspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (see details in ref4). The cells were subsequently expanded through several passages and we used the 4-passage cells for further experiments.

Characterization of mBMSCs

Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the expressions of cell surface markers of mBMSCs. Briefly, 1 × 106 passaged-3 mBMSCs were suspended in 1 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (Gibco), then incubated with 1 µl of the phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-mouse CD105 (Becton Dickinson, eBioscience, USA), and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-mouse CD90, CD73 and CD34 (Abcam, USA) for 1 h at 4˚C. The isotype controls contained murine FITC-conjugated IgG1 and PE-conjugated IgG2b (eBioscience) as substitutes for the primary antibodies were used. Data from all samples were collected with a FACScan flow cytometer (BD FACS Caliber, BD Biosciences, San Jose CA the USA) and analyzed by Flowing software version 2.5.

Furthermore, mBMSCs were then examined for their ability to differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrogenic skeletal lineages. For osteogenic differentiation, the cells were treated with an osteogenic induction medium containing DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 0.2 mM ascorbic acid, and 1 nM dexamethasone (Gibco). Also, an adipogenic medium containing DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.5 mM indomethacin, l mM ascorbic acid (Sigma), and 1 µM dexamethasone (Sigma) was used for 3 weeks of cell culture. Afterward, deposition of calcium was measured by 1% alizarin red-S (ARS) staining (Sigma). Lipid droplets in the cells were detected using a 0.4% oil red O staining solution (Sigma). For chondrogenesis, a micro-mass culture system was utilized. Briefly, about 2–3 × 105 passage-3 mBMSCs were centrifuged at 1200 g for 5 minutes. The cell pellets were suspended in a 300 ML chondrogenic medium (Lonza, Switzerland) for 21 days at 37˚C with twice-weekly medium changes. Chondrogenic differentiation was measured by toluidine blue staining (see for more details ref4). Real-time PCR Analysis was used for mBMSCs differentiation into adipogenic (i.e., Pparg, Lpl, and Adipoq), osteoblastic (i.e., Col I, Opn, Ocn), and chondrogenic (i.e., Col II, Sox9 and Acan).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

To ensure Msx2, Fgf8, and Bmp4 gene expression, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used. First, total RNA was extracted from the cells using the NXTM-Plus method. The concentration and purity of extracted RNAs were measured using a Nanodrop (ND-1000) spectrophotometer and RNA 2100 Bioanalyzer. Reverse transcription was performed using random hexamer primers and a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Fermentas, K1632) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, as a housekeeping gene. Relative quantification of gene expression was calculated using ∆∆ct method. The sequences of the primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

EV harvesting of BlCs and mBMSCs and characterization

The supernatant of the BlC and mBMSC cultured cells was collected and filtered through a 0.22-µm pore-size filter. The debris cells were removed by centrifuging at 300 g for 10 min. Then, the supernatant was then centrifuged at 3,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to detach the microvesicles. Subsequently, EVs were separated by two steps of differential centrifugation: 20,000 ×g for 30 min, followed by centrifugation of the supernatant for 120 min at 110,000 ×g, and finally resuspended of the pellet of EVs in 1 ml PBS and kept at -70 °C.

In addition, the expression profiling of enriched EV proteins, counting CD63 (1:500, ab8219), and CD81 (1:500, sc-7637), was assessed using western blot (WB) (see for more details ref45). In brief, the protein contents of the collected EVs were measured using a standard colorimetric protein assay kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Accordingly, the extracted proteins were run on a 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, France). Blocking of the PVDF was done with Tris-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 4–5% powdered milk at 37 °C for 1 h, and then incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C for 24 h. Then, samples were incubated with the peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Bio-Rad, USA) at 4 °C overnight. Finally, the bands were visualized (ECL plus, Pierce Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and quantified using Total Lab Quant Analysis software (Total Lab Limited, England). Moreover, the Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) technique was used to investigate the size distribution of EVs. About 10–20 µg of EVs were added to a final volume of 1 ml PBS solution and were sonicated before reading, then the solution was read using a Zatasizer Nano device. Finally, a scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to determine the morphological characteristics of EVs46.

Assessment of protein expression in EVs

The protein expression levels of BMB4, FGF8, MSX1, and MSX2 were examined via the WB technique in the mBMSCs and BlCs cells-derived EVs. The EV suspension was digested with trypsin/EDTA. Western blotting was performed according to a previous study47,48. Briefly, the proteins were extracted and their concentration was measured using a standard BCA protein assay kit (Thermo, catalog. no. 23225), then proteins were run on the gel following SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto the PVDF membrane. Subsequently, due to the low yield of isolated EVs and the limitation in acquiring the complete blot images, the blots were cut off transversally, and each half of the blot was used for one antibody during subsequent steps. Then, the blots were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with TBST containing 2% BSA and 0.1% Tween 20. Then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against BMP4, FGF8, MSX1, and MSX2 proteins (Invitrogen) at 4 °C. Finally, the PVDF membranes were incubated in goat anti-rat secondary antibody (1:500, Alexa Fluor® 488, Invitrogen) and goat anti-rat secondary antibody (1:500, Alexa Fluor® 568, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were scanned and protein levels were normalized to the β-actin (1:1000) as a control. The ChemiDoc Touch Gel Imaging System and Image Lab Touch software (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) were used to record and quantify the signal intensity. Finally, the quantitative expression of the proteins MSX1, MSX2, FGF8, and BMP4 in BlCs-EVs and mBMSCs-EVs were measured (n = 1). The B-Actin level was used as an internal standard. As full as possible length blots are presented in Additional file 1: Figure Supplementary 2.

Measurement ofMsx1, Msx2, Fgf8, andBmp4genes expression in mBMSCs treated with BlCs-EV

In this view, mBMSCs were cultured in the density of 1 × 105 cells per cm2 of 6-well dishes and then were treated with the DMEM culture medium consisting of 50 µg/ml EVs. Subsequently, after 72 h of treatment, cells were collected and the gene expression was examined with Real-time PCR technique.

Assessment of BlCs-EV uptake by mBMSCs

To investigate the uptake of BlCs-EV by mBMSCs as explained in the previous study49, EVs were labeled with the fluorescent dye calcein or green-fluorescent calcein-AM (Sigma, USA), added to the mBMSC-labeled with PKH-26 (Sigma, USA), and were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. First, EVs with a 50 µg/ml concentration were dissolved in 5 ml of PBS and centrifuged at 110,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C. Then, EVs were stained with green fluorescent calcein-AM for 1–4 min. The remaining color was then neutralized by adding 2 ml bovine serum (Gibco). Subsequently, ultracentrifugation was repeated twice at 110,000 g for 2 h at 4 ° C to pellet the EVs. The mBMSCs were already labeled by the 1 µl of PKH-26 dye for 20 min under the same condition and then, approximately 4 × 10⁴ labeled cells were seeded in each well of 4-well culture dishes. When the confluence of cells reached 50–60%, the labeled EVs were added to the PKH-labeled cells. The uptake of EVs was assessed at 0 and 24 h after receiving EVs by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus BX51, Japan).

Measurement of the toxicity of EVs on mBMSCs

The 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was performed to assess the toxicity rate of EVs on mBMSCs. Cells were first seeded in triplicate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/ml in 96-well tissue culture plates. Cells were treated with concentrations of 20, 50, and 100 µg/ml EVs in each well for 3 days a week. The MTT solution (5 mg/ml) was added to each well after 1, 3, and 5 days. Then, DMSO was used to dissolve the Formazan crystals. We measured the color intensity of the formazan product at 570 nm using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan™ GO Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, USA) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Assessments of cell proliferation and migration of mBMSCs treated with EVs

The proliferation rate of mBMSCs following EVs treatment was measured by the MTT assay following effective dose selection. The cell migration of EVs-induced cells was characterized using the scratch assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 10⁵ /well of 6-well plate culture dishes. The culture medium was replaced with 2 ml of culture medium containing 30 µM mitomycin C. Upon reaching 70–80% cell density, monolayer cells were scratched using a sterile p1000 pipet tip. Washed twice with 2 ml of PBS buffer to remove debris and floating cells. Microscopic photographs of invasion were produced via fluorescence microscopy 0 h and 24 h after scratch.

Evaluation of osteogenic differentiation of mBMSCs treated with EVs

To investigate the osteogenic activity of EVs, mBMSCs were cultured in 12-well plates, and following 80% cell confluency, cells were treated with a culture medium containing EVs at 100 µg/mL concentration/ml for 21 days. Untreated mBMSCs were used as a control group. The osteogenic induction medium was used as a positive control group. The osteogenic activity of mBMSCs was assessed at three time points: 7, 14, and 21 days. Alizarin Red S staining and RT-qPCR test were used to assess the mineralized ECM and bone-specific genes in differentiated cells (Supplementary Data Table 1).

Creation of the digit tip amputation model

Eight-week-old C57BL/6 male and female mice were anesthetized by using ketamine (80 mg/kg body weight) (Rotexmedica, Germany) and xylazine (8 mg/ kg body weight) (Alfasan, Netherlands). The forelimb digits 2, 3, and 4 were amputated at the proximal non-regenerating level (see for more details ref4).

All measures for the care and use of mice were performed according to the Standard Operating Procedures approved by the Institution’s Animal Care & Ethics Committee of Royan Institute.

EV preparation and transplantation

We first prepared a solution with a final concentration of 5 µg/µl EVs into fibrin glue and PBS buffer as a Sham group. Approximately 8–10 µl of prepared solution were injected per digit into the amputation wound 4 days post-amputation (4 DPA) using a Hamilton needle under a stereo-loop (Olympus, Japan). The experimental groups consisted of EVs-injected groups, including mBMSCs-EV, BlCs-EV, and non-EV-injected groups, including Intact (control), Untreated, and Sham (fibrin glue). Subsequently, 8 weeks post-injection (8 WPI) mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation (the approved NIH method of euthanasia) used for small animals, and the first knuckle was harvested.

Histological evaluation

Harvested digits were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, weak acid treatment with Morse solution (20% formic acid and 10% tri-sodium citrate, pH 7.2) decalcified tissues efficiently at room temperature while their extracellular structure was preserved. After paraffin embedding, 6-µm thick sections were prepared for histological staining. Deparaffinization and dehydration steps were performed according to standard protocols.

Subsequently, the samples were stained with Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were also stained with Masson’s trichrome according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Gomori, procedure HT10, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Furthermore, the skeletal elements appeared with alizarin red S and alcian blue staining (Al & Al) to detect bone and cartilage, respectively, according to protocols.

Statistical evaluation

Statistical analyses were performed on datasets that involved at least three independent experiments, using an unpaired Student’s t-test that compared two groups; one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests which compared more than two groups; or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests for nonparametric results with Graph Pad Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.
