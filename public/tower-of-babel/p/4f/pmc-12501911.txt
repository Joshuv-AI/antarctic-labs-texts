Stop Canceling Surgery for Esophageal Cancer: An Analysis of Treatment Related Mortality in 60 000 Patients
Authors: Brian Housman, Shubham Gulati, Ashwin Kulshrestha, Matthew Untalan, Tara Ivic‐Pavlicic, Stephanie Tuminello, Karyn A. Goodman, Emanuela Taioli, Raja Flores
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and MethodsSurgical esophagectomy is falsely regarded as contributing little survival in resectable, early‐stage disease in favor of nonsurgical treatment with definitive chemoradiation (dCR). We examine surgery in treatment by evaluating large‐scale trends in mortality. The SEER database was queried for esophageal cancer patients from 2004 to 2020. Total of 59 754; 19 806 squamous and 34 484 adenocarcinoma. The primary outcome was the hazard ratio for overall survival defined as time from diagnosis‐to‐death. Differences between patients were assessed using χ2 tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables. Difference in survival by histology was assessed using Kaplan‐Meier curves and Cox Proportional Hazards models.ResultsA total of 47 170 (78.9%) did not undergo surgery and 12 584 (21.1%) received surgery. The hazard ratio for patients undergoing dCR for SCC was 1.929. The hazard ratio for treatment with dCR compared to chemoradiation‐followed‐by‐surgery (CRS) was 2.151 and for adenocarcinoma 2.386. Further analyses highlight groups under similar clinical conditions; including multivariate analysis, T‐Stage, overall stage, surgical era, and surgery refusal. In every category, the risk of mortality was higher in dCR compared to CRS.ConclusionsSurgery combined with chemoradiation, and/or surgery alone, confers a significant survival benefit over dCR in resectable patients.DiscussionSurgery should be considered early in medically fit patients with operable disease.

Background

The optimal management of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the esophagus remains controversial. There is a growing perception that definitive chemoradiation (dCR) has equivalent outcomes as neoadjuvant chemoradiation followed by surgery (CRS) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The esophagectomy is falsely regarded as contributing no additional survival benefit, and only serves to expose patients to perioperative risk. Emboldened with this perception, an increasing number of oncologists advocate for definitive treatment without surgery; even in patients who present with early—stage disease [1, 7, 8, 9]. Instead, surgery is only considered after chemoradiation therapy fails and patients develop local recurrence [1, 8, 10, 11]. Unfortunately, these “salvage esophagectomy” procedures are associated with a substantially higher perioperative risk and are often only considered after patients are no longer surgical candidates [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

We explore the role for surgery by evaluating large—scale trends in treatment—related mortality with a focus on definitive chemoradiation compared to chemoradiation and surgery. We then performed sub‐analyses on outcomes by (a) T‐Stage, (b) overall stage, (c) surgical era, and (d) patients who refused surgery to highlight groups with similar clinical conditions.

We hope to clarify the role of surgery in the treatment of squamous cell esophageal cancer. Without compelling evidence, abandoning the esophagectomy for SCC represents a potentially dangerous departure from the standards of modern practice.

Methods

Study Population

The National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) database was queried for all records containing an ICD‐O‐3 site recode of “Esophagus” (n = 74 918). Patients were excluded from this query if they were younger than 18 years old at diagnosis or had been diagnosed at autopsy or by death certificate. The sample was further restricted to only patients diagnosed in 2004 and after (n excluded = 12 291). Finally, patients who received a nonsurgical procedure (e.g., laser, photodynamic therapy, excisional biopsy) were also omitted (n excluded = 2873).

Variables of Interest

A composite treatment variable was created by combining individual treatment types a patient was reported to receive in SEER. For instance, if a patient's record indicated “Yes” for both chemotherapy and radiation, but “No/Unknown” for surgery, they were classified as having received “Chemotherapy and Radiation”; if a patient's record indicated “Yes” for chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery, they were classified under “Chemotherapy, Radiation, and Surgery,” and so on. In addition to treatment, other primary predictors included histology (squamous, adenocarcinoma, or other), primary tumor site (upper third, middle third, lower third, other/unknown), stage (I, II, III, IV, unknown), and grade (I, II, III, IV, unknown). Sociodemographic data, including age, sex, and race were also extracted. The primary outcome of this study was overall survival (OS), which is calculated by SEER as the time from diagnosis to death.

Statistical Analyses

Patients with squamous and adenocarcinoma histologies were compared in regard to demographics, clinical features, and overall survival. Differences between histologies were assessed using χ 2 tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables, and difference in survival by histology was assessed using Kaplan‐Meier curves and Cox Proportional Hazards models. Adjusted models were generated to account for other factors independently associated with survival. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

From 2004 to 2020 there were 59,754 patients identified with esophageal cancer that met our inclusion criteria. A total of 19 806 patients had SCC and 34 484 patients had AC. Another 5464 patients were identified as having “Other” histology.

Most patients with esophageal cancer were male (78%) but the proportion of male patients was substantially higher in patients with AC (84%). For SCC, only 64% were males (p < 0.0001).

White Americans were more commonly diagnosed with AC (57% vs. 87%, p < 0.0001). By contrast, Black Americans and Asian or Pacific Islander Americans had a much higher proportion of SCC compared to AC (23% vs. 3% and 11% vs. 2%, respectively, p < 0.0001). Patient characteristics.

Tumor Characteristics

A majority of AC is located in the distal third of the esophagus (77%), and the largest proportion of SCC is located in the middle third (33%). There was a bi‐modal distribution of T‐stage with the highest proportion of patients having T1 or T3 disease across all groups. For SCC, 24% were T1 and 27% were T3. For AC, 24% were T1 and 31% were T3.

Most patients were classified as having no (N0) or limited (N1) lymph node involvement. For SCC, 41% were N0 and 43% were N1. For AC, 38% were N0 and 45% were N1. At the time of diagnosis, the largest group of patients with esophageal cancer had stage 4 disease (32.7%, n = 19 521). This was also true for patients with both SCC (25.43%, n = 5036) and AC (36.83%, n = 12 700). Complete information on tumor characteristics is also included in Table 1.

Treatment for Esophageal Cancer

Patients underwent one of eight different types of treatment modalities: (1) chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery, (2) chemotherapy and radiation, (3) surgery alone, (4) chemotherapy and surgery, (5) radiation and surgery, (6) chemotherapy alone, (7) radiation alone, or (8) no treatment. Patients who underwent endoscopic procedures were excluded from this study.

There was substantial heterogeneity in reporting the specific form of surgery. There were different codes for Partial Esophagectomy, Total Esophagectomy, Esophagectomy with Partial Gastrectomy, Esophagectomy with Total Gastrectomy, Esophagectomy with Laryngectomy, and multiple additional codes for combinations of all the above procedures. All operations that involved esophageal resection and reconstruction with curative intent were included in the analysis.

A total of 47,170 patients (78.9%) did not undergo surgery for the treatment of esophageal cancer, and 12,584 (21.1%) received some form of surgery. Among patients with SCC, 86.9% (n = 17 215) did not receive any surgical procedure and 13.1% (n = 2591) received surgery. Among patients with AC, 73.6% (n = 25 365) did not receive surgical procedure and 26.4% (n = 9119) received surgery (p < 0.0001); which is more than double the proportion of patients compared to SCC. Complete information on the treatment by type of histology are included in Table 2.

Hazard Ratio of Mortality Risk by Treatment

The HR for mortality in patients undergoing dCR for SCC (n = 19 806) was 1.929 (95% CI: 1.801–2.066; p < 0.0001) compared to patients undergoing chemoradiation and surgery. The hazard ratio for AC (n = 34 484) was 2.386 (95% CI: 2.294–2.482; p < 0.0001) and for all forms of esophageal cancer overall was 2.151 (95% CI: 2.08–2.224; p < 0.0001).

The observed mortality risk for patients undergoing dCR was also observed in multivariate analysis controlling for age, sex, year of diagnosis and overall stage. For SCC, the HR was 1.758 (95% CI: 1.654–1.868, p < 0.0001), and for AC the HR was 1.969 (95% CI: 1.896–2.045, p < 0.0001). Chemotherapy or radiation alone were always associated with a higher risk of death. But the addition of surgery to either treatment modality conferred a survival advantage. Sub‐Analysis of Hazard Ratio

All retrospective studies will have unavoidable biases. Surgical outcomes may be favored when patients tend to be healthier with earlier stage disease. Definitive chemoradiation may be favored when more advanced tumors are associated with higher rates of surgical complications. Our goal was to limit selection bias by comparing populations with similar fitness, tumor involvement, and surgical candidacy. To this end, we repeated the analysis by T‐stage, overall stage, time period, and surgery refusal to simulate populations with similar clinical conditions.

T‐Stage

We first examined the number of patients that underwent surgery with earlier T‐stages (T1–T3). Our goal was to identify whether SCC patients had a greater proportion of unresectable disease which would distort the perception they underwent surgery less frequently. Under these conditions, SCC patients were still two—times less likely to undergo surgery. For patients with SCC, only 19.7% underwent surgery even though 59.0% of patients were diagnosed at T1–T3. By comparison, 38.0% of patients with AC underwent surgery where 63.9% were diagnosed with resectable T stages (p < 0.0001). By individual T‐Stage the number of patients who underwent surgery for SCC was 12.2% of T1 (n = 566), 24.3% of T2 (n = 427), and 24.8% of T3 (n = 1307). For AC, 30.1% of T1 (n = 2449), 42.9% of T2 (n = 1414), and 42.6% of T3 (n = 4505) underwent surgery (p < 0.0001). See Table S1 for complete information on patients that underwent surgery by T‐Stage. Information on treatments by T‐Stage can be found in Table S2.

These same patients also displayed a markedly higher risk of mortality for patients undergoing dCR compared to CRS at every T‐Stage and for every cancer type. The lowest increased hazard was 1.654 and the highest was 2.697 (p < 0.0001). See Table S2, Figures 2 and S1 for hazard ratios and Kaplan – Meier curves comparing dCR to CRS by T‐Stage.

Overall Stage

The analysis was repeated at every overall stage to include a broader picture of tumor behavior and the contribution of nodal involvement. Again, there was a markedly higher risk of mortality for patients undergoing dCR compared to CRS at every overall stage, and for every cancer type. The lowest increased hazard was 1.752 and the highest was 2.602 (p < 0.0001). See Table 6, Figure 3, and Figure S2 for hazard ratios and Kaplan – Meier curves comparing dCR to CRS by overall stage. Time Period (Before and After 2009)

Third, we repeated the analysis comparing two time periods: 2004–2009, and 2009–2020. We chose these dates in parallel to a study from Memorial Sloan Kettering that identified a decrease in institutional use of surgery and increased definitive chemoradiation after 2009 [8]. A similar trend was observed nationally. Overall, there was a 3% decrease in the number of patients receiving surgery after 2009 (23% vs. 20%, p < 0.0001) with a concomitant 3% increase in patients who did not undergo surgery (77% vs. 80%, p < 0.0001). Comparing by histology, the number of SCC patients undergoing surgery decreased by ~3% (14.9% vs. 12.3), and the number of AC patients decreased by ~5% (30.1% vs. 25.2%). For complete data on the number of patients undergoing surgery before and after 2009, see Table S4.

When comparing treatments before and after 2009, more patients underwent both dCR and CRS, but the rate of growth was higher for dCR. Among all patients with esophageal cancer, there were 3.47% more that underwent dCR compared to 2.72% more that underwent CRS. For patients with SCC, 4.87% more underwent dCR compared to 1.73% more that underwent CRS. For patients with AC, 3.52% more underwent dCR compared to 2.86% more that underwent CRS (p < 0.0001). For complete information of treatment of esophageal cancer before and after 2009, also see Table S6.

As more patients pursued nonsurgical management, the risk of death increased. After 2009, the HR for all patients undergoing dCR compared to CRS increased from 2.070 to 2.471. By comparison, the HR for surgery alone decreased from 0.859 to 0.720 (p < 0.0001). For patients with SCC, the HR for dCR also increased from 1.824 to 2.154 and the HR for surgery alone decreased from 1.328 to 1.188 (p < 0.0001). For patients with AC, the HR for dCR compared to CRS increased from 2.372 to 2.784 and the HR for surgery alone decreased from 0.724 to 0.581 (p < 0.0001). For complete data on HR for treatment before and after 2009, see Table S5.

Patient Refusal

Fourth, we identified a subgroup of 2714 patients who were offered surgery, but did not undergo a procedure. This population served to identify patients that were eligible for surgery by both stage and overall health, but instead chose to receive dCR. There was again a significantly higher risk of death in patients receiving dCR compared to CRS. For all patients, the HR was 1.679 (95% CI: 1.555–1.813, p < 0.0001), for SCC (n = 953) the HR was 1.540 (95% CI: 1.349–1.760, p < 0.0001), and for AC (n = 1479) the HR was 1.764 (95% CI: 1.592–1.955, p < 0.0001). Discussion

This study identified a higher risk of overall mortality for patients that underwent dCR compared to CRS for the treatment of squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and all forms of esophageal cancer combined. The same outcome was observed in every analysis, for every histologic subtype, and at every stage. The elevated risk of death associated with dCR was also seen when the analysis was limited to patients with resectable disease by T‐Stage, and to patients who were eligible for surgery but refused.

The highest HR for the use of dCR in SCC was 2.100 and was seen in patients with T1 disease. The lowest observed HR was 1.540 in patients that were offered surgery but refused.

The addition of surgery to any individual treatment (either chemotherapy or radiation alone) reduced the risk of death compared to nonsurgical therapy. Surgery alone was observed to confer a survival advantage over CRS in some circumstances. This is likely because surgery alone is typically offered for early‐stage disease and will therefore have better outcomes overall. Since this is the current standard of care, the observed survival advantage should reinforce this practice.

Patients with SCC were much less likely to undergo surgery. Overall, the ratio of patients with AC that received surgery compared to SCC was 1.98. This proportion was similar when the analysis was limited to patients with T1–T3 disease; T‐stages that are more likely to be resectable. Patients with AC were 2.47 times more likely to get surgery for T1 disease, 1.76 times for T2, and 1.72 times for T3. When combining all patients with potentially resectable T‐stages, patients with AC were 1.93 times more likely to get surgery. For complete data of the number of patients getting surgery by T‐Stage, please refer to Table S4.

While this finding is multifactorial, it strongly suggests that physician influence played a role. Though T‐stage by itself does not guarantee surgical resectability, and resectability does not guarantee surgical candidacy, the proportions are simply too large. Even considering potential differences in tumor biology and medical ineligibility, it is simply not credible that patients with SCC were consistently two‐times less likely to be surgical candidates.

When evaluating trends in treatment over time, we found that dCR was more common after 2009 as surgical treatment became less popular. Unfortunately, the risk of death from dCR compared to CRS increased; despite presumed improvements in chemoradiation. Conversely, the risk of death from esophagectomy alone reduced; suggesting improvements in surgical technique and perioperative care.

Despite a consistently greater HR, the magnitude was routinely lower for patients with SCC compared to AC. For example, the HR of dCR for SCC overall was 1.929 compared to 2.386 for AC. For patients with T1 disease, the HR was 2.100 for SCC compared to 2.697 for AC. While this difference may reflect statistical anomalies (smaller SCC patient population, asymmetric treatment arms, unidentified statistical confounding), it may also reflect oncologic differences in the response to treatment. SCC and AC are distinct processes each with unique risk factors, cultural prevalence, etiologies, and known differences in tumor behavior. In this data set alone, SCC is observed to have worse overall survival at 19.67 months compared to 22.96 months for AC (p < 0.0001). This and other subtle differences in tumor biology may also explain why studies limited by size, sampling bias, or confounding from excessive surgical mortality may have failed to identify a benefit to surgical esophagectomy.

Nevertheless, through multiple analyses, clinical conditions, and time periods, the data is convincing that dCR is linked to a higher risk of mortality compared to CRS.

Limitations

This study had several important limitations. As a large scale, retrospective study, details regarding clinical decision‐making were not available. Additionally, common metrics, including progression‐free survival and median survival, were not available for all patients, and were therefore not included. Since randomization was not performed, institutional biases will be intrinsic to the data set, but confounding should be minimal given the scale of the population.

The data also had no information about the type of chemotherapy used, the doses of radiation exposure, or the order of treatment delivery. We used all operations with curative—intent as an omnibus grouping for “surgery.” But since our goal was to identify the benefit of curative resection, we felt this still yields an accurate representation of outcomes especially given the number of patients.

The decrease in HR observed when surgery is added to any modality likely has multiple explanations. First, patients who undergo chemotherapy are likely to be less robust than patients who can also tolerate surgery. Second, patients receiving either only chemotherapy or radiation undergo incomplete therapy. It should come as no surprise that surgical resection would confer additional local control and a survival advantage. Lastly, because SEER measures OS from date of diagnosis and not date of treatment, it's possible that the results presented here could be subject to immortal time bias.

Conclusion

This study suggests that surgery alone or combined with chemoradiation confers a significant survival benefit over chemoradiation alone to nearly all patients with resectable esophageal cancer. Research that supports nonsurgical management should be critically evaluated, with particular attention to sample size, treatment protocols, and the impact of outdated operative mortality. While further investigation is warranted, the growing popularity of definitive chemoradiation for squamous cell carcinoma should be regarded with caution. Similarly, the salvage esophagectomy cannot be considered a dependable alternative to early surgery. Over time, cancer progression and worsening nutrition will decrease patient eligibility, and the limited pool of surgical candidates will be subjected to higher operative risk. We recommend that surgery should be considered early in all medically fit patients with operable disease.

Synopsis

The surgical esophagectomy is falsely regarded as contributing no additional survival benefit for patients with resectable, early‐stage disease. We clarify the role for surgery in the treatment of esophageal cancer by evaluating large scale trends in mortality with a focus on definitive chemoradiation compared to chemoradiation and surgery. This study suggests that surgery combined with chemoradiation—and sometimes surgery alone—confers a significant survival benefit over definitive chemoradiation to most patients with resectable esophageal cancer.
