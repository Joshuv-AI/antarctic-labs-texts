Real-World Health Care Resource Utilization and Costs Among Patients with Activated Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase Delta (PI3Kδ) Syndrome in the United States
Authors: Nicholas L. Rider, François Laliberté, Guillaume Germain, Ana Urosevic, Malena Mahendran, Amanda Harrington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study sought to describe health care resource utilization (HRU) and treatment use among patients with activated phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta syndrome (APDS) and assess the incremental HRU and health care costs associated with APDS relative to a non-APDS control cohort.MethodsPatients with APDS were identified via Symphony Health Solutions Integrated Dataverse database. Controls were selected using a 10:1 ratio and matched to patients with APDS. Rates of HRU (hospitalizations, emergency department [ED] visits, outpatient [OP] visits, other visits) per person-year (PPY) and annual health care costs were compared using rate ratios (RRs) estimated from Poisson regression models and cost ratios (CRs) estimated from gamma regression models, respectively. Use of symptomatic treatments associated with APDS was also assessed.ResultsMean (standard deviation [SD]) age of patients with APDS (n = 42) and controls (n = 420) was 16.0 (15.1) and 16.9 (17.4) years, respectively. Patients with APDS had significantly higher PPY rates of all-cause hospitalizations (RR 4.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.83–8.03; p < 0.001) and OP visits (RR 1.77, 95% CI 1.24–2.61; p < 0.001) vs controls, with OP visits driven by higher rates of OP hospital visits (RR 5.32, 95% CI 3.30–8.29; p < 0.001). Patients with APDS had annual all-cause total health care costs 10.54 times greater (95% CI 4.56–20.67; p < 0.001) than controls, driven by higher medical costs (CR 12.39, 95% CI 5.04–25.82; p < 0.001). Significantly higher costs for hospitalizations and ED, OP (office/clinic and hospital), and other visits were observed in patients with APDS vs controls. HRU rates and costs (except other visits) remained significantly higher in patients with APDS vs controls after excluding visits related to immunoglobulin replacement therapy.ConclusionHigher HRU and health care costs among patients with APDS vs controls reflect the high clinical and economic burden of APDS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03377-3.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Activated phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta syndrome (APDS), an inborn error of immunity, has a heterogeneous phenotype with signs and symptoms varying greatly among affected individuals.Despite growing clinical recognition of APDS, the real-world health care resource utilization (HRU) associated with this disease remains poorly understood, and its economic impact has not been quantified in the USA.This retrospective analysis assessed HRU, treatment use, and associated costs in patients with APDS (n = 42) compared with a matched non-APDS control cohort (n = 420) using administrative claims data from the Symphony Health Solutions Integrated Dataverse database from October 1, 2015 to March 31, 2022. What was learned from the study? Clinical manifestations observed in ≥ 50% of patients with APDS included allergies, lymphoproliferation, and respiratory symptoms; the most commonly observed treatments associated with APDS were antibiotics, corticosteroids, and immunoglobulin replacement therapy.All-cause outpatient (OP) hospital visits, hospitalizations, and OP visits per person-year were significantly higher in patients with APDS compared with the non-APDS cohort (p < 0.001 for all); total health care costs (medical + pharmacy costs) were 10.54 times greater (p < 0.001).To our knowledge, this study provides the first real-world claims-based analysis of disease manifestations and treatment use associated with APDS, highlighting the substantial morbidity, extensive supportive care, and high clinical and economic burden of APDS.

Introduction

Activated phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ) syndrome (APDS), a rare, underrecognized inborn error of immunity (IEI) first characterized in 2013, is caused by heterozygous variants in one of two genes that encode subunits of the PI3Kδ heterodimer [1–5]. Gain-of-function variants in phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit delta (PIK3CD) and loss-of-function variants in phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 1 (PIK3R1) result in hyperactivation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)/protein S6 kinase (S6K) signaling pathway, leading to immune deficiency and dysregulation [4, 6, 7].

APDS has a heterogeneous phenotype, with sign and symptom severity varying greatly among affected individuals [4]. The majority of patients experience recurrent respiratory tract infections [2, 8]. Other manifestations include bronchiectasis; persistent herpes virus infection; other viral and bacterial infections; non-neoplastic lymphoproliferation, including lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and hepatomegaly; autoimmune and inflammatory conditions; neurodevelopmental morbidity; and growth impairment [2, 9]. Lymphoma has been reported in up to 25% of patients [8, 10–12]. Most patients experience significant morbidity, and early mortality has been evidenced by a lower median overall survival of 11 years compared with the global population (64 vs 75 years, respectively) [7, 10, 13]. In a systematic literature review, the most commonly reported causes of death among 41 patients with APDS were lymphoma (24%) and infections (22%) [14].

Historically, APDS treatments have targeted clinical manifestations, with approaches tailored to individual symptoms. These may include antimicrobial prophylaxis, immunomodulatory therapies, immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IRT), and splenectomy [7, 15]. For patients with severe immune deficiency, hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) may be performed, but complications and adverse events can limit its use [7, 16–18]. In 2023, leniolisib, an oral selective PI3Kδ inhibitor, became the first and only agent approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of APDS [6, 19]. The heterogeneity of manifestations associated with APDS, the rare nature of the disease, and its recent characterization have all contributed to a clinical trajectory that is not well understood [4, 9]. Additionally, there are no studies that quantify the health care resource utilization (HRU) and economic burden incurred with the symptomatic treatment of this condition. To address this gap and highlight the unmet needs of patients with APDS, we investigated HRU and treatment use associated with the symptomatic treatment of APDS in addition to HSCT in patients with APDS and evaluated HRU and health care costs associated with patients with APDS compared with those without APDS in the USA.

Methods

Data Source

The Symphony Health Solutions Integrated Dataverse (IDV) is a multidimensional big-data platform for health care data, including open unadjudicated claims data across all payer types (i.e., commercial plans, Medicare Part D, cash, assistance programs, and Medicaid), from more than 317 million active US patients. Data collected from the Symphony Health Solutions IDV for this analysis represented a sample of patients with ≥ 1 diagnosis of any of the following: bronchiectasis, cytomegaloviral disease, enlarged lymph nodes, hepatomegaly and splenomegaly, herpes viral infections, immunodeficiencies, lymphoma, myelodysplastic syndrome, sinusitis, or pneumonia. Data were available from October 1, 2015 to March 31, 2022.

Study Design

This was a retrospective analysis of open administrative claims data from the Symphony Health Solutions IDV database. No electronic health record data were used. The study design is illustrated in Fig. 1. Patients were assessed during the clinical activity period, defined as the time from the start of clinical activity in the Symphony Health Solutions IDV to the earliest of the following: end of clinical activity or end of data availability (March 31, 2022). To minimize underestimation of outcomes given the use of open claims data, which may not capture all health care encounters, continuous clinical activity was required for inclusion in the study population and was defined as consecutive quarters with at least one pharmacy, medical, or hospital claim. Descriptive analyses were evaluated over the entire clinical activity period, whereas comparative analyses were evaluated over an observation period, defined as the shorter of the clinical activity period or three years, to establish a similar time frame between the APDS and non-APDS cohorts.Fig. 1Study design and duration. Patients

APDS Cohort

Patients with APDS (n = 31) were identified via a genetic testing program. Tokenized identifications generated for these patients were used to identify them within the Symphony Health Solutions IDV. With the introduction of a diagnostic code for APDS (D81.82) in the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) in October 2022, 11 additional patients were identified in the Symphony Health Solutions IDV. Thus, the final cohort contained 42 patients with APDS.

Non-APDS Control Cohort

All patients in the Symphony Health Solutions IDV who were not identified as a patient with APDS were considered for inclusion in the non-APDS control cohort. To compare patients with APDS with relatively healthy controls, those with a diagnosis of a primary immunodeficiency (PI) or malignancy at any time during the study period were excluded from the analysis, as these conditions were considered severe. Relatively healthy controls were chosen as the control cohort because treatments resulting in the normalization of PI3K pathway function should result in normalization or improvements in immune dysregulation and clinical signs and symptoms of APDS [20, 21]. This control cohort served as a benchmark to evaluate the incremental burden of APDS, isolating the added HRU and cost beyond that observed in the general population. Controls were then randomly selected from this group in a 10:1 ratio, with exact match on age, sex, region, insurance plan type, and year of clinical activity start to ensure comparable populations. A sex-based analysis was not conducted as the cohorts were matched on this variable; gender was unavailable. The case to control ratio ensured sufficient sample to power the comparative analyses. The case to control ratio selection was informed by historical epidemiological literature that suggested increasing the number of controls beyond 4 or 5 per case yields minimal increases in statistical power and precision [22–24].

Study Outcomes

Treatment Use

Treatment use was assessed in the clinical activity period in patients with APDS. Available therapies at the time of the study included symptom management medication (antibiotics, antifungals, corticosteroids, IRT, mTOR inhibitors, rituximab), HSCT, high-frequency chest compression (HFCC) vest, and surgeries (adenoidectomy, splenectomy, tonsillectomy).

All-Cause HRU

The following all-cause HRU measures were evaluated in the clinical activity period and the observation period: hospitalizations (visits with place of service corresponding to inpatient); emergency department (ED) visits (non-inpatient visits with procedure codes indicating an ED visit); outpatient (OP) visits (non-inpatient and non-ED visits, with place of service corresponding to “clinic,” “medical office,” and “hospital–outpatient,” classified as either OP hospital visits or office/clinic visits); and other visits (non-inpatient and non-ED visits, with place of service corresponding to “other facility or location”). ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes associated with each health care visit were identified at the patient level and used to define the most frequent reasons for each visit type. As diagnostic codes for other visits were largely underreported, procedure codes were assessed. Among the APDS cohort, a substantial number of health care visits were associated with IRT—a common off-label treatment for APDS at the time of the study. As a result, both IRT-related HRU and non-IRT-related HRU were reported.

All-Cause Health Care Costs

The following all-cause health care cost measures were evaluated: total health care costs (medical and pharmacy costs), medical costs (hospitalization costs, ED visit costs, OP visit costs [office/clinic and OP hospital visit costs], and other visit costs), and pharmacy costs. All costs were inflation-adjusted to 2020 US dollars based on the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index. Health care costs were also categorized as IRT related or non-IRT related. The Symphony Health Solutions IDV reports medical costs as charges incurred from administered procedures, resulting in an underreporting of health care costs (particularly for hospitalizations). As a result, the absolute value of health care cost differences in a comparative analysis was less informative. However, as underreporting of costs was not expected to differentially affect patients with APDS or patients without APDS, a ratio of costs (i.e., cost ratio [CR]) was applied as a reliable assessment of the magnitude of the difference in health care costs between cohorts.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide software Version 7.15 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Patient demographics were evaluated at the start of clinical activity, and clinical characteristics were evaluated during the clinical activity period. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation [SD], and median values for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Differences in clinical characteristics between cohorts were assessed using standardized differences. A standardized difference (std diff) of less than 10% was considered a negligible difference between cohorts, based on published literature suggesting this threshold reflects minimal correlation between a cohort and characteristic [25].

All-cause rates of HRU (frequency of events per person-year [PPY]) were evaluated over the clinical activity period and the observation period and calculated as the number of events divided by the person-years of observation. Rates of HRU were compared between cohorts using rate ratios (RRs) estimated from generalized linear models with Poisson distribution. As HRU data have positive values that follow a non-normal distribution and often have 0 values, nonparametric bootstrap procedures were used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values [26].

All-cause health care costs were reported per person per year (PPPY) by dividing the total costs incurred per patient during the observation period by the duration of observation. Mean costs were compared between cohorts using CRs estimated from generalized linear models with gamma distribution. To account for the non-normal distribution of cost data and likelihood of 0 values, 95% CIs and p values were generated on the basis of nonparametric bootstrap procedures [26].

Ethical Approval

Analyses in this study included only deidentified insurance claims data; therefore, institutional review board approval and patient consent for study participation or publication of study findings were not required. Permission was obtained from Symphony Health Solutions Corporation, allowing use of data from its database for this study. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments and in compliance with the US patient confidentiality requirement of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Results

Among the approximately 135 million patients in Symphony Health Solutions IDV who were not in the APDS cohort, approximately 13 million were excluded because of a diagnosis of PI or malignancy. Of the remaining 122,195,821 patients, 420 were randomly selected after exact matching to the APDS cohort on age, sex, region, insurance plan type, and year of clinical activity start (Fig. 2). The mean (SD) age of patients in the non-APDS cohort was 16.9 (17.4) years. The mean (SD) duration of the clinical activity period was 4.6 (2.1) years in patients with APDS and 2.9 (2.3) years in the non-APDS cohort. Clinical Characteristics of Patients with APDS

Clinical manifestations with a prevalence > 10% among the APDS cohort during the clinical activity period are shown in Fig. 3. All reported clinical manifestations are shown in Table S1. Clinical manifestations observed in ≥ 50% of patients with APDS compared with those in the non-APDS cohort included allergies (57.1% vs 27.4%; std diff 60.2%), lymphoproliferation (57.1% vs 4.3%; std diff 114.6%), sinusitis (54.8% vs 18.8%; std diff 74.6%), asthma (54.8% vs 21.0%; std diff 69.7%), and bronchitis (50.0% vs 11.0%; std diff 84.8%). Patients with APDS were observed to have other PI diagnoses, including other immunodeficiencies (57.1%), common variable immunodeficiency (38.1%), and combined immunodeficiencies (31.0%); patients in the non-APDS cohort with a PI diagnosis were excluded from the study. Each concurrent autoimmune disease was observed in < 5% of patients with APDS (Table 1); no autoimmune diseases were observed in the non-APDS cohort. Elixhauser comorbidities that were more frequently observed in patients with APDS than in the non-APDS cohort included chronic pulmonary disease (64.3% vs 24.8%; std diff 79.5%), followed by cardiac arrhythmias (23.8% vs 5.7%; std diff 51.0%) and fluid and electrolyte disorders (21.4% vs 7.1%; std diff 40.8%). Other comorbidities that were more frequently observed in patients with APDS than in the non-APDS cohort included sleep–wake disorders (28.6% vs 11.0%; std diff 44.2%), neurodevelopmental disorders (26.2% vs 13.6%; std diff 31.6%), and anxiety disorders (23.8% vs 15.5%; std diff 21.0%).Fig. 3Clinical manifestations observed in > 10% of patients with APDS. Treatment Use

Most patients with APDS received IRT (59.5%), with intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC) routes of administration being equally common (Tables 2 and S2). The mean (SD) age at IRT initiation was 17.9 (12.1) years, and patients remained on treatment for a mean (SD) of 2.7 (2.1) years. HRU During the Clinical Activity Period

During the clinical activity period, HRU PPY in patients with APDS primarily consisted of all-cause OP visits (13.01 visits; 7.28 office/clinic and 5.74 OP hospital visits) and other visits (6.30 visits). Rates of HRU PPY in patients with APDS for all-cause ED visits and hospitalizations were 0.73 and 0.44, respectively. Reasons for OP visits were often associated with other immunodeficiency (52.4%) and long-term current drug therapy (42.9%) as well as clinical manifestations of APDS, such as vasomotor and allergic rhinitis (52.4%), asthma (52.4%), enlarged lymph nodes (50.0%), cough (42.9%), and acute sinusitis (40.5%) (Table S3). Reasons for other visits were primarily related to immunodeficiency care (50.0%) and respiratory care (47.6%), with patients receiving home care that included IRT infusions and parenteral nutrition (Table S4). Approximately one-third of other visits were related to IRT administration. Drivers for ED visits primarily consisted of fever (other and unknown origin) and long-term current drug therapy, 23.8% each. Additional reasons or ED visits included respiratory conditions (e.g., pneumonia [21.4%], breathing abnormalities [19.0%], asthma [16.7%], cough [16.7%], and upper respiratory infection [14.3%]), immunodeficiency (16.7%), nausea/vomiting (16.7%), and viral infections (14.3%). In addition to PI-related reasons (i.e., immunodeficiency, other [19.0%] and combined immunodeficiencies [14.3%]), drivers for hospitalizations included respiratory conditions (pneumonia and asthma, 21.4% each), viral agents (14.3%) and symptoms (fever, other and unknown origin [19.0%] and nausea/vomiting [16.7%]), and long-term current drug therapy (16.7%) as well as anemia-related conditions (14.3%) (Table S3).

Where observed, physician specialties for each HRU visit type are summarized in Table S5. The number of claims with physician specialty information available for each type of visit evaluated (e.g., hospitalization, ED visit, OP visit, or other visit) was < 20% (Table S5). OP visits and other visits had the greatest number of claims with a recorded physician specialty, the most common of which were allergy/immunology (48.3% and 19.9%, respectively), pediatric (22.4% and 46.9%, respectively), family medicine (21.8% and 0.5%, respectively), and internal medicine (5.5% and 32.7%, respectively).

Discussion

The results of this study highlight the real-world clinical and economic burden of APDS in the USA. To our knowledge, this study provides the first claims-based analysis of disease manifestations and treatment use associated with APDS. It is also the first study to quantify HRU and costs incurred by patients with APDS relative to patients without APDS. Our findings provide valuable insights on the substantial morbidity of patients with APDS and highlight the large economic burden of this disease.

In this study, clinical manifestations of APDS were predominantly related to allergies, lymphoproliferation and lymph node enlargement, infections, and respiratory illnesses. In a systematic literature review of 243 patients with APDS, commonly reported infectious manifestations included pneumonia (44%), upper respiratory tract infections including otitis (29%) and sinusitis (26%), diarrhea (16%), and eye infections (11%) [9]. Similarly, in an Italian study of eight patients with APDS1, sinopulmonary infections were reported as the most common infectious manifestations, occurring in 88% of patients—50% of whom required hospitalization for severe infectious episodes [27]. In our study, the most common infectious manifestations observed in patients with APDS were sinusitis (55%), bronchitis (50%), pneumonia (48%), and otitis media (43%), corroborating published literature on APDS [9]. The most common respiratory disease manifestations were asthma (55%) and bronchiectasis (41%). Common reasons for hospitalizations and ED visits in our study included pneumonia (21.4%), fever (19%), and viral agents as the cause of diseases classified elsewhere (14.3%), highlighting the severity of these recurrent infections in patients with APDS. Although the number of observed physician specialties in the claims data was low, our data showed that patients primarily sought OP care from physicians specializing in allergy/immunology, pediatrics, family medicine, and internal medicine. To improve identification and early diagnosis of APDS, future research should assess the types of health care professionals from whom patients with APDS most often receive care and which providers initiate genetic testing and management of APDS-related manifestations.

In the absence of established guidelines defining standard of care for APDS, treatment has largely been empirical and symptom driven [6, 28]. Antibiotic and antiviral prophylaxis are commonly used to reduce the infection rates in patients with APDS; IRT may also be administered [6, 9, 28, 29]. Immune dysregulation and associated lymphoproliferation are sometimes treated with corticosteroids or mTOR inhibitors [6, 27, 28]. mTOR inhibitors, such as sirolimus, may also be used to modulate the downstream effects of PI3Kδ hyperactivation [6, 7, 27, 28, 30]. In some cases, HSCT may be recommended [7]; however, high rates of complications have been observed following HSCT, including graft instability or failure and infection [16–18, 31]. During the study period, no patients were observed to have received HSCT procedures and no FDA-approved treatments for APDS were available. Our data show a high reliance on antibiotics, corticosteroids, and IRT was observed among patients with APDS to help manage symptoms, highlighting a need for therapies that target the underlying disease mechanisms and mitigate symptoms of APDS. Use of mTOR inhibitors was infrequent and may have been limited as a result of the associated adverse event profile (e.g., anorexia, aphthous ulcers, liver and/or renal toxicity, and severe headaches) and suboptimal effect on nonlymphoproliferative manifestations [7, 10, 12, 30]. This study provided insights into the HRU of symptomatic treatments in APDS, and its findings may serve as a baseline for evaluating HRU in APDS when assessing new therapies. With the FDA approval of leniolisib, further research is warranted to understand the impact of disease-modifying treatments on long-term HRU and costs in the APDS population.

Although no studies have assessed HRU and health care costs among patients with APDS specifically, the economic burden of other PIs that share common traits with the manifestations of APDS has been previously reported. In an international study of 25 patients with predominant antibody deficiencies, average HRU in the 12 months before and 12 months after IRT initiation included 1.4 and 0.2 hospitalizations, 1.8 and 0.4 ED visits, 7.1 and 8.9 regularly scheduled office visits, and 11.6 and 3.8 unscheduled office visits, respectively [32]. Overall, our findings indicate that HRU needs of patients with APDS are similar to those of the 25 patients with PI disease. However, compared with relatively healthy controls, our results suggest significantly more health care resources are required for patients with APDS. Between 2015 and 2022 (costs adjusted to 2020 US dollars), observed non–IRT-related total health care costs were nearly four times greater in the APDS cohort relative to the non-APDS cohort, indicating that total health care costs remained significantly elevated in patients with APDS after costs associated with IRT were removed. The all-cause total health care cost for patients with APDS in our study was nearly three times higher than estimated costs for patients with a rare immunodeficiency disease [33]. This higher overall cost may reflect the severity of APDS relative to other rare immunodeficiencies, a difference in the use of IRT, the lack of adequate treatment options, and/or the novelty of APDS—all of which makes diagnosis and treatment particularly difficult. Future comparisons between APDS and other PIs or IEIs may offer clinically useful insights. This initial study begins to address key questions and provide insights into this patient population. Future studies should expand this evidence base by examining factors such as diagnostic delay and age-related impacts on HRU in patients with APDS. The lack of studies quantifying health care costs among patients with APDS and other PIs in the USA also underscores the poorly understood economic burdens of patients with these chronic conditions.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first claims-based real-world analysis of HRU and the associated costs incurred in patients with APDS, providing new insights that contribute to the limited APDS literature. However, these findings are subject to some limitations. First, the small sample size of the APDS cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings to the global APDS population. Due to the rarity of the disease, the recent implementation of the ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for APDS in October 2022, and that patients with APDS may be misdiagnosed with other PIs, our APDS cohort may not fully represent the overall population of patients with APDS and future studies evaluating HRU in these patients may be warranted. The estimated prevalence of APDS is one to two persons per million, which would equate to 333 to 666 people with APDS in the USA in 2022 [4]. Thus, the 42 patients included in this analysis represent up to 13% of the potential APDS population in the USA. Second, the comparison of costs between cohorts used CRs to account for the underestimation of hospitalization charges in the Symphony Health Solutions IDV database; however, the underestimation of costs was expected to affect both cohorts equally as this limitation was applicable to all patients and not solely one cohort. Third, there were no HSCT procedures observed among patients with APDS during the study period. The use of HSCT has been reported in approximately 13% of patients with APDS [9, 14]. First-year median costs following HSCT for malignant and nonmalignant diseases in pediatric patients have been estimated to be $251,829 to $575,512 (range $132,880–$1,471,698) (US dollars 2016) depending upon development of acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and steroid responsiveness of acute GVHD [34]. Thus, HRU and costs are likely higher than what we observed in the present study when the medical history of all identified patients with APDS is considered, which may include HSCT. Fourth, there are no patient insurance eligibility files, as the data source is a provider-based, not eligibility-based, database. Although clinical activity was used to indicate eligibility, healthier patients who had not incurred any health care costs would not have been included in the study. Finally, as a result of the nature of claims databases, coding inaccuracies, omissions, or potential misspecification in procedure, diagnosis, or drug codes could have occurred, which may have resulted in the underestimation or overestimation of clinical outcomes. However, random distribution of these inaccuracies is expected and thus assumed to be equally present in both cohorts.

Conclusion

Results from this study help to characterize real-world clinical manifestations, treatment use, HRU, and health care costs of patients with APDS and delineate the clinical trajectory of this population vs the general population. These findings highlight the significant morbidity, extensive supportive care, and high clinical and economic burden of APDS. Future studies are needed to expand upon these findings by assessing outcomes in a larger population of patients with APDS as more are identified and evaluating the effectiveness of novel therapies within the framework of HRU and health care costs of APDS and its clinical manifestations.
