Tendon Transfers in Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty: A Systematic Review
Authors: Eric S. Warren, Eoghan T. Hurley, Mikhail A. Bethell, Bryan J. Loeffler, Nady Hamid, Christopher S. Klifto, Oke Anakwenze
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this study was to evaluate clinical outcomes after tendon transfers in the setting of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA).Methods:PubMed and Embase were searched according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines to find primary clinical studies of any type of tendon transfer in the setting of RTSA.Results:Overall, 17 studies (level of evidence [LOE] I: 1, LOE II: 0, LOE III: 3, LOE IV: 13) met inclusion criteria, with 300 shoulders. Most patients were female (56.7%), with an average age of 68.7 years (range 19 to 89) and a mean follow-up of 46.2 months (range 6 to 174). 11 studies reported outcomes after combined latissimus dorsi and teres major transfer (LDTM) while eight studies reported on latissimus dorsi transfer only (LD). Improvements in commonly reported subjective and functional outcome measures were as follows: external rotation +32° (LDTM) and +30° (LD), flexion +65° (LDTM) and +59° (LD), Visual Analog Score −5.4 (LDTM) and −4.5 (LD), subjective shoulder value +43.8% (LDTM) and +46.3% (LD), and overall Constant score +33.8 (LDTM) and +38.7 (LD). The overall complication rate was 11.3%, including tendon transfer ruptures (0.7%), instability (3.0%), infection (2.0%), and nerve injury (0.3%). The all-cause repeat operation rate was 7.3%, most commonly for arthroplasty revision (5.3%). Subgroup analysis revealed that lateralized implants with tendon transfer resulted in markedly greater improvements in Constant score, flexion, ER1, and ER2 while medialized implants with tendon transfer had markedly greater improvements in Visual Analog Score, subjective shoulder value, and abduction.Conclusion:Patients undergoing tendon transfer of either combined LDTM or latissimus dorsi alone in the setting of RTSA have markedly improved subjective and functional outcomes. A moderate incidence of complications (11.3%) was noted in this patient population.

The volume of total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is rapidly increasing, and the growth rate is projected to be as high as 235% in the year 2025.1 Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) in particular is increasingly used as an effective treatment option for rotator cuff tear arthropathy, irreparable rotator cuff tears, and difficult cases of glenohumeral deformity.1,2 Patients with severe fatty infiltration of the infraspinatus and teres minor may present with a disabling loss of external rotation, and patients may present with external rotation lag sign, positive hornblower sign, and a tendency for the arm to internally rotate into the chest during active elevation.3,4 RTSA may restore active elevation and abduction but may not return active external rotation in patients with preoperative isolated loss external rotation or combined loss of active elevation and external rotation (CLEER).5678

As a result, tendon transfers can be performed alongside RTSA to augment restoration of external rotation. In 1934, L’Episcopo9 first described a transfer of the teres major to restore active external rotation in obstetric palsy. Boileau et al4 in 2007 introduced a modified L’Episcopo technique with transfer of the latissimus dorsi and teres major (LDTM) that could be performed through a single deltopectoral incision. A number of case studies have demonstrated mid-term and long-term improvements in clinical outcomes such as Constant score, subjective shoulder value (SSV), and range of motion.10-12 However, there are potential complications including transferred tendon rupture, infection, nerve injury, dislocation, and acromion fracture.1112131415161718

Previous studies have reported clinical outcome data for multiple options of tendon transfer in the setting of RTSA including isolated latissimus dorsi transfer (LD), combined LDTM, and pectoralis major; however, there are currently no updated systematic reviews that compare data for multiple tendon transfer options. Sheth et al. and Wey et al. conducted systematic reviews of latissimus dorsi with or without teres major tendon transfers, but neither included studies published after 2015, and thus, an updated systematic review is warranted.19,20 The purpose of this study was to synthesize an updated summary of clinical outcomes after RTSA with a tendon transfer of any kind. The hypothesis is that patients undergoing tendon transfer with RTSA will have subjective and functional improvement greater than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) without a complication or revision surgery rates greater than reported RTSA-alone rates.

Methods

Study Selection

The literature search was done by two authors using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, and the search results were reviewed, with a senior author arbitrating on any disagreement. The title and abstract identified in the search were screened, and potentially eligible studies received a full-text review.

Search Strategy

The following search terms were used in MEDLINE and Embase databases in January 2023 as the search algorithm: tendon transfer AND shoulder. Only articles published after 2000 were included.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria were the following: (1) primary clinical study on any tendon transfer in the setting of a TSA, (2) rotator cuff tear, (3) published in a peer-reviewed journal, and (4) published in English. The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) review studies, (2) cadaver studies, (3) biomechanical studies, and (4) abstract only.

Data Extraction/Analysis

The relevant information for the study characteristics including the study design, the level of evidence, the methodological quality of evidence (MQOE), population, clinical outcome measures, and the follow-up time points was collected by two blinded reviewers using a predetermined data sheet, with the results compared by a third independent reviewer.

The level of evidence was evaluated based on the guidelines by the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine. The MQOE was evaluated using a Modified Coleman Methodology score.21 Studies were considered excellent quality if they scored 85 to 100, good quality if they scored 70 to 84, fair quality if they scored 55 to 69, and poor quality if they scored less than 55. Clinical outcomes extracted and analyzed were (1) complications, (2) ruptures of transferred tendon, (3) revision surgeries, (4) functional outcome measures, and (5) Visual Analog Score (VAS) pain scores. A subgroup analysis of these outcomes was conducted for studies that specified the use of medialized center-of-rotation (Grammont style) versus lateralized implant systems. Student t-tests were conducted to compare outcomes between studies using medialized implants and lateralized implants. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

Literature Search

The initial literature search resulted in 1827 total studies. After removal of duplicates, 1,184 unique studies were screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 106 full texts were evaluated and assessed for eligibility. Seventeen clinical studies with 300 patients were included in this review (Figure 1).

Study Characteristics/Patient Demographics

A total of 16 unique patient groups were observed in 17 studies, with 298 patients and 300 shoulders.4,101112131415161718,22232425262728 Two studies had an overlap of patient groups, and the results of both were used.11,13 Thirteen studies were case series,4,101112131415,17,18,23,24,26,27 three were comparative studies,22,25,28 and one was a randomized controlled trial.16 The mean MQOE was 63.2 ± 7.0 (51 to 77), which is classified as fair. For comparative studies and the randomized controlled trial, only outcomes from patients with a prosthetic shoulder joint who underwent tendon transfer were included. Most patients were female (56.7%), with an average age of 68.7 years (range 19 to 89) and a mean follow-up of 46.2 months (range 6 to 174). The surgical indication for all patients included was CLEER or loss of active external rotation with radiologically proven irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears. Only one study used a two-incision technique24 while all the others performed the procedure through a single deltopectoral incision. Functional Outcomes and VAS Score

Outcomes for each type of tendon transferred are listed in Table 2. One study that did not differentiate reported outcomes for latissimus dorsi transfer alone or combined LDTM transfer17 was included in the analysis of complications and revision surgery data but was excluded in the analysis of functional outcomes and VAS score by tendon transfer type. The most commonly reported functional outcomes were active flexion (16 studies), active external rotation at 0° of abduction (15 studies), Constant score (12 studies), SSV (11 studies), and visual analog scale pain score (8 studies). The mean external rotation improved by 32° and 30° in the LDTM and LD groups, respectively. Furthermore, the active forward flexion increased by 65° and 59° in the LDTM and LD groups, respectively. Mean improvement was also found in all other outcomes regardless of tendon type with the exception of internal rotation as given in Table 2. Internal rotation was reported as either the mean spinal level reached by the patient's thumb or a score based on the patient's Constant-Murley score. Improvements for every outcome measure surpassed the MCID for RTSA as defined by Simovitch et al.29

Medialized Versus Lateralized Implants

Of the 17 studies included, 10 specified that a medialized implant design was used while five implemented a lateralized design (Table 3). Not all outcomes were reported in each subgroup, but there were notable differences between the medialized and lateralized RTSA groups in each outcome measure except for the Simple Shoulder Test score. Tendon transfers with medialized RTSAs reported significantly greater improvements in the VAS score (−6.3 vs. −4.9, P-value <0.001), SSV (+45.0% vs. +41.5%, P-value <0.001), and active abduction (+69° vs. +64°, P-value = 0.03). Tendon transfers with lateralized RTSAs reported significantly greater improvements in the Constant score (+41.6 vs. +33.2, P-value<0.001), active flexion (+72° vs. +67°, P-value = 0.01), active external rotation at patient side (+34° vs. +30°, P-value<0.001), and active external rotation at 90° of abduction (+48° vs. +36°, P-value<0.001). Of note, one study was excluded from the subgroup analysis because it did not differentiate results using a medialized implant or a lateralized implant but reported that its own subgroup analysis found no difference in results.28

Complications and Revision Surgeries

Overall, there were 34 total complications of any kind (11.3%). Partial or complete rupture of the transferred tendons occurred 2 times (0.7%; Tables 4 and 5). Tendon rupture was confirmed with dynamic ultrasonography in one study23 while confirmation methodology was not delineated in the other study.11 Nine shoulders with postoperative instability (3.0%) were observed and six shoulders with postoperative infections (2.0%). Permanent nerve injury of any kind was reported in one shoulder (0.3%). The full list of complications is provided in Table 4.

Discussion

Patients undergoing tendon transfer of either combined LDTM or latissimus dorsi alone in the setting of RTSA have markedly improved subjective and functional outcomes. This is supported by the findings in this systematic review, with functional and subjective outcome improvements substantially greater than the MCID for RTSA. Specifically, active external rotation improved greater than 30° on average for both types of tendon transfer. Internal rotation was the only studied measure that did not reliably improve for either type of tendon transfer.

Muscle tendon transfers in the shoulder can be used for multiple pathologies in the shoulder including irreparable rotator cuff tears, scapulothoracic abnormal motion, and posterior cuff deficiency in the setting of RTSA.30,31 Insufficiency of the infraspinatus and teres minor can lead to a muscular imbalance in the horizontal plane, presenting as external rotation lag and loss of active external rotation in abduction.11 Furthermore, CLEER can be addressed with RTSA for the vertical plane muscle imbalance and tendon transfer for the horizontal plane imbalance.11 Transferred muscles should be able to act on a vector similar in direction and magnitude to the deficit native muscle with minimal loss of function at the donor site.30 Boileau et al. described a single-incision technique that manages both the arthroplasty and tendon transfer through a single deltopectoral incision with bone tunnel fixation to the greater tuberosity at the level of the teres minor.4,11 Of note, the lower trapezius tendon transfer with Achilles allograft has been described with positive early results as a treatment option for irreparable posterior-superior rotator cuff tears, but unfortunately, there were no studies in the setting of RTSA that met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review.32333435

Comparing outcomes from this systematic review with outcomes of RTSA without tendon transfer may help delineate the additional effect of tendon transfer. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of outcomes and complications after primary RTSA, Galvin et al2 found improvements of 56°, 50°, and 14° for active flexion, active abduction, and active external rotation, respectively, which was met or exceeded in both tendon transfer cohorts in this study. The mean improvement in external rotation more than doubled in each tendon transfer cohort. They also found an American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Standardized Shoulder Assessment score mean improvement of 42 points and a Constant score increase of 37 points, which does not dramatically differ from our study. Furthermore, Kennedy et al36 conducted a systematic review of clinical and patient-reported outcomes and complication rates after RTSA without tendon transfer. In those with a preoperative diagnosis of cuff tear arthropathy, RTSA led to a mean 5.3-point improvement in the VAS score and a five-point increase in the Simple Shoulder Test score, comparable to our findings. Importantly, they reported a slightly lower rate of overall complications, 7.4%, than this study. Moreover, they reported 1.5% incidence of instability or dislocation, 1.6% incidence of infection, and 0.6% incidence of nerve palsy for patients with a preoperative diagnosis of cuff tear arthropathy. Similarly, meta-analysis by Galvin et al2 found an overall complication rate of 9.4% and a revision rate of 2.6%. Our review found an overall complication rate of 11.3% and a revision surgery rate of 7.3%, including an arthroplasty revision rate of 5.3%. It is important to note that the patient population indicated for a RTSA with a tendon transfer is not entirely the same as those undergoing RTSA alone because the tendon transfer is typically added for patients with a complete loss of active external rotation and irreparable superoposterior rotator cuff repairs. The improvements in active range of motion with tendon transfer do not necessarily mean that the final postoperative range of motion is superior to the RTSA-alone population because the tendon transfer group likely started at a lower degree of function.

Not all studies have found tendon transfers in the setting of RTSA to markedly improve outcomes. The study by Young et al16 was the only randomized controlled trial included in this review, and they found no notable improvements in patient satisfaction or functional outcomes in patients with CLEER and rotator cuff arthropathy undergoing RTSA with LDTM tendon transfer compared with RTSA alone. Similarly, Wiater et al28 found no notable improvements in range of motion or patient-reported outcomes in patients with a preoperative external rotation deficit undergoing RTSA with a latissimus dorsi transfer compared with those who received RTSA alone. Early studies have suggested that lateralized center-of-rotation RTSA systems may be viable alternatives to tendon transfers for treatment of CLEER. Berglund et al37 reported an improvement of nearly 50° in active external rotation for patients with CLEER after RTSA lateralized either through the glenosphere or total prosthetic lateralization. Merolla et al22 also found similar improvements in active external rotation with a lateralized humeral implant compared with a Grammont-style RTSA with LDTM transfer. Our subgroup analysis revealed that tendon transfers with lateralized implants did provide markedly greater improvements in Constant score, flexion, ER1, and ER2 than tendon transfers with medialized designs. Additional research could explore whether there are notable differences in outcomes between modern lateralized RTSA with tendon transfer and lateralized RTSA alone in patients with CLEER.

Limitations

This systematic review was limited by the relatively small number of studies available for inclusion. Although 17 studies were included, they did not all report the same outcomes, so each individual result was compiled from even less studies. Moreover, only one study was a randomized controlled trial, and the rest were either comparative studies or case series. Furthermore, the mean MQOE was 63.2, which is only fair and may require caution when interpreting results. It is also essential to note that the standard minimum follow-up for shoulder arthroplasty clinical studies is 2 years, and many of the studies included data from patients less than 2 years removed from their operation. Importantly, the influence of different implant system–specific factors was not considered in this study, and their effect on the results is not known. As previously mentioned, lower trapezius tendon transfers with Achilles allograft have more recently been introduced as another option for restoration of external rotation; however, no studies were found to meet the inclusion criteria for this systematic review.

Conclusion

Patients undergoing tendon transfer of either combined LDTM or latissimus dorsi alone in the setting of RTSA have markedly improved subjective and functional outcomes. Active elevation was restored to a similar degree as primary RTSA without tendon transfer, and the mean active external rotation gain was nearly doubled with tendon transfer. The hypothesis was partially correct in that tendon transfer in the setting of RTSA led to improvements greater than the MCID; however, complication and revision surgery rates were found to be slightly higher than those reported for RTSA alone. Yet, the influence of varying definitions of a complication on these findings is unclear. Also, tendon transfer with lateralized implant systems provided markedly greater improvements in active flexion, ER1, and ER2 compared with tendon transfer with medialized implants. The potential benefits of combined tendon transfer and RTSA should be weighed against the potential complication and revision surgery risk found in this study.
