RSL3 promotes PARP1 apoptotic functions by distinct mechanisms during ferroptosis
Authors: Dejian Chen, Fei Xie, Yimei Mo, Dan Qin, Binjiao Zheng, Lingyan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe classical ferroptosis activator RSL3 targets enzymes with nucleophilic active sites, primarily acting on glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) to trigger ferroptosis. Recent studies identify RSL3 as a potential pro-apoptotic agent. However, the mechanism by which RSL3 induces apoptosis during ferroptosis remains elusive. Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP1) determines cell fate in response to DNA damage, where its loss or cleavage by activated caspase-3 induces apoptosis to attenuate tumor progression. We elucidate a novel mechanism underlying PARP1 regulation, encompassing both its caspase-dependent cleavage and full-length depletion during RSL3-mediated ferroptosis-apoptosis crosstalk.MethodsTo investigate the role of RSL3 during ferroptosis, we treated several cancer cells of different histological types with varying doses of RSL3 to induce apoptosis. The regulatory proteins of PARP1 were analyzed using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot analysis. The N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification level of PARP1 was determined by m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)-qPCR analysis. Additionally, an RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR assay was performed to identify the target protein of the m6A site of PARP1. Furthermore, we established a mouse xenograft model of PARP inhibitor (PARPi)-resistant cells to analyze the effect of RSL3 on PARPi-resistant tumor growth.ResultsRSL3 triggers two parallel apoptotic pathways via increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production during ferroptosis: (1) caspase-dependent PARP1 cleavage and (2) DNA damage-dependent apoptosis resulting from reduced full-length PARP1. The latter occurs through inhibition of METTL3-mediated m6A modification and subsequent suppression of PARP1 translation. Moreover, we found that RSL3 retains pro-apoptotic functions in PARPi-resistant cells and effectively inhibits PARPi-resistant xenograft tumor growth in vivo.ConclusionsRSL3 orchestrates ferroptosis-apoptosis crosstalk via PARP1, demonstrating therapeutic potential against tumorigenesis, particularly in PARPi-resistant malignancies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00785-9.Keywords: RSL3, PARP1, N6-methyladenosine, Apoptosis, PARPi-resistant tumor

Background

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation [1], is mechanistically distinct from apoptosis [1]. Whereas apoptosis involves a caspase-mediated proteolytic cascade that ultimately leads to chromatin fragmentation and apoptotic body formation [2], the biochemical hallmark of ferroptosis depends on GPX4 degradation, which drives lipid peroxidation [3]. In contrast, apoptosis is initiated through mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), triggering cytochrome c (CytC) release, and subsequent activation of executioner caspases (e.g., caspase-3 and -7) [2]. These proteases orchestrate apoptosis by cleaving nuclear structural proteins and DNA repair enzymes, including PARP family members [2]. This fundamental divergence establishes ferroptosis as a metabolic collapse driven by redox imbalance, in contrast to the genetically encoded dismantling of cellular architecture during apoptosis. Intriguingly, emerging evidence reveals that ROS accumulation and p53 activation bridge these pathways in cancer biology. For example, erastin activates p53 to inhibit the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, depleting cystine uptake while provoking ROS-dependent G1 phase arrest and intrinsic apoptosis via mitochondrial permeabilization [4]. Similarly, RSL3 exerts ROS-mediated apoptotic activity in myelodysplastic syndrome cells independently of its canonical ferroptotic effects [5]. Therefore, ferroptosis inducers exhibit dual lethality by simultaneously triggering lipid peroxidation and caspase-mediated apoptosis, indicating a paradoxical phenomenon warranting mechanistic elucidation. However, the molecular framework regulating these divergent death pathways remains undefined, hindering the therapeutic exploitation of their synergistic potential.

The DNA damage response (DDR) has emerged as a critical determinant of cell fate, influencing both ferroptosis and apoptosis [6, 7]. DDR establishes a bistable system where moderate DNA damage activates repair fidelity via homologous recombination (HR), while excessive genomic stress triggers BCL-2 antagonist/killer-1 (BAK) and BCL-2-associated X protein (BAX) oligomerization, leading to mitochondrial apoptosis [8, 9]. Following DDR activation, PARP1 constructs a chromatin scaffold to recruit repair complexes such as XRCC1 to DNA damage lesions [10]. PARP1 is also a substrate for caspase-3 and caspase-7, whose cleavage generates 24-kDa and pro-apoptotic 89-kDa fragments [11]. The 24-kDa PARP1 fragment irreversibly binds to DNA breaks, inactivating and preventing DNA repair, thereby enhancing apoptosis, whereas the 89-kDa PARP1 fragment is translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and directly induces caspase-mediated DNA fragmentation and apoptosis [11]. Notably, PARP1 deficiency exacerbates H2AX phosphorylation (γH2AX) foci accumulation and comet tail moments, potentiating DDR-driven apoptosis [12]. This functional duality suggests that PARP1 may serve as a molecular integrator of ferroptotic and apoptotic signaling, leading us to hypothesize its involvement in RSL3-mediated cytotoxicity.

Despite the clinical success of BRCA-mutant cancers, acquired resistance remains a major therapeutic barrier [13, 14]. This impasse necessitates alternative approaches to reactivate apoptosis in PARPi-resistant tumors [15]. Such strategies must bypass conventional resistance mechanisms, posing a challenge that may be addressed by RSL3. Building on recent discoveries of m6A modification in DDR regulation and ferroptosis [16, 17], we hypothesized that RSL3 coordinates ferroptosis and apoptosis through PARP1-mediated convergence of epitranscriptomic control and caspase signaling.

Here, we identify PARP1 as the molecular nexus linking RSL3-induced ferroptosis to apoptotic execution via two distinct pathways: (1) RSL3 activates caspase-3, driving PARP1 cleavage into pro-apoptotic fragments, and (2) RSL3 disrupts METTL3–YTHDF1-mediated m6A modification of PARP1 mRNA, reducing its stability and translational efficiency. Pharmacological inhibition of ROS and lipid peroxidation rescues both full-length PARP1 depletion and its caspase-dependent processing, confirming ROS overload as the upstream regulator. Strikingly, RSL3 maintains apoptotic efficacy in PARPi-resistant cells and suppresses xenograft tumor growth in vivo, demonstrating the therapeutic potential against resistant malignancies. These findings establish PARP1 as a bidirectional regulator of ferroptosis-apoptosis crosstalk, providing a mechanistic foundation for targeting apoptosis-refractory cancers through dual pathway activation.

Cell viability and proliferation, cell cycle distribution, apoptosis, JC-1, and ROS analysis

Cell viability, cell cycle distribution, and apoptosis were analyzed as previously described [18]. We normalized optical density (OD)570 values to 100% cell viability using the following formula to calculate 50% inhibition concentration (IC50) values for RSL3 and olaparib: (OD Treatment–OD Blank/OD Control–OD blank) × 100. Dose–response curves were generated using nonlinear regression in Graphpad Prism. IC50 values represent the drug concentrations required to achieve 50% inhibition of maximal proliferation capacity.

MDA-MB-453, MCF7, and HCC1937 cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells/well. After overnight incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2, cells were treated with the indicated concentration of RSL3 for 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h or olaparib for 12 h. For JC-1 analysis, cells were collected and washed with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by incubating with JC-1 staining buffer for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Stained cells were analyzed on a BD Accuri™ C6 plus flow cytometry. Intracellular ROS was measured by the fluorescence probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by washing three times with serum-free medium to remove the extracellular probe. Cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of serum-free medium for flow cytometry analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate with a minimum of 20,000 cells per condition.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed in ice-cold cell lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (APExBIO, Houston, USA) for 10 min on ice. Following lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. MDA levels were quantified using the Lipid Peroxidation MAD Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Trolox-equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) analysis

Cells were harvested by direct scraping from culture dishes (without trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) treatment) and resuspended in 200 µL of ice-cold 1 × PBS. Cell suspensions were homogenized using a glass homogenizer to release intracellular antioxidants, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 ℃. The resulting supernatants were analyzed for antioxidant capacity using the TEAC testing kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on microscope coverslips in 24-well plates were treated with specified concentrations of RSL3 for 12 h. The cells were then fixed in ice-cold 100% methanol for 15 min at -20 °C and rinsed three times with 1 × PBS (5 min per wash). After additional treatments as required, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, gently washed with 1 × PBS, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in 1 × PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Following permeabilization, the cells were blocked with a buffer containing 5% BSA and 0.3% Triton-X in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with a γH2AX primary antibody (1:200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C, washed with 0.3% Triton-X in 1 × PBS, and incubated with an Alexa Fluor Plus 555-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China), mounted on glass slides, and imaged using an LSM900 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Comet assay

MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well and treated with the indicated concentrations of RSL3 for 12 h. The cells were then washed three times with 1 × PBS, suspended in 0.6% low-melting agarose, and transferred onto glass slides precoated with low-melting agarose. The slides were immersed in an alkaline lysis buffer overnight at 4 ℃ in the dark, followed by electrophoresis in an alkaline electrophoresis solution at 30 V for 25 min. Subsequently, the slides were stained with ethidium bromide (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. Images of randomly selected cells were captured using a Zeiss LSM900 laser confocal microscope, and the relative DNA tail moment lengths were measured using the Comet Assay Software Project (CASP).

Western blot

Western blot was performed using protocols as described previously [18].

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were performed using a protocol as described previously [18]. Primers for gene amplification are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

MeRIP-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from the samples using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The fragmented RNA (10 µL) was used as an input control, and the remaining RNA was incubated with MeRIP protein A/G magnetic beads to isolate m6A-methylated RNA, following the manufacturer’s instructions (IEMed, Guangzhou, China). The MeRIP-enriched RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR version 2 (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, Hunan, China). RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, Hunan, China).

RIP-qPCR

We first prepared an antibody-protein A/G mixture containing 2 µL of IgG and 2 µg of YTHDF1 (ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions (IEMed, Guangzhou, China). MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo cells treated with RSL3 were washed twice with prechilled 1 × PBS. The cells were lysed in 2 × RIP buffer supplemented with protease and RNase inhibitors for 20 min at room temperature. The cell lysate was mixed with 700 µL of Binding Enhancer and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was then added to prewashed beads and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. A 100-µL aliquot of the supernatant was saved as the Input control. The remaining supernatant (400 µL and 800 µL portions) was incubated with IgG-protein A/G mixture and YTHDF1-protein A/G mixture, respectively, for 60 min at room temperature. The samples were washed three times with RIP washing buffer, and the co-precipitated RNAs were recovered using Nucleic acid BFC and eluted with RNase-free water for 3 min. Fold enrichment was determined by RT-qPCR. The primers used for gene amplification in MeRIP-qPCR and RIP-qPCR are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Polyribosome-qPCR assay

Polyribosome-qPCR assay was performed using a protocol as described previously [19].

Constructing overexpression cell lines

Full-length human PARP1 (NM_001618.4) and METTL3 (NM_019852.5) cDNAs were cloned into pLVX-Puro vectors (Miaoling, Wuhan, China) under cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoters, incorporating C-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP) tags incorporated for immunodetection. Third-generation lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with psPAX2/pMD2.G packaging plasmids (Tsingke, Beijing, China) and the target plasmids at a 2:1:2 ratio using PEI (Maokang, Shanghai, China). Target cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 with 8 µg/mL polybrene for 48 h. Stable cell lines were selected using 0.5–1.5 µg/mL puromycin (Amresco, Shanghai, China) for 5–7 days, achieving > 90% infection efficiency. Single cells were isolated via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-mediated deposition into 96-well plates, and their expansion was monitored and validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot.

Immunohistochemistry assay

Tumor sections were deparaffinized in xylene Ӏ followed by xylene ӀӀ for 5 min each, rehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 5× sodium citrate buffer (Beyotime, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) at 100 °C for 10 min. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, tissue sections were treated with 15% H2O2 for 15 min and blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 90 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies such as anti-γH2AX (1:200), anti-Ki67 (1:200), or anti-caspase-3 (1:200) were diluted in 0.01 M Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated with the sections overnight at 4 °C. After washing for 3 × 5 min in 0.01 M TBST, sections were incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG (1:350 in 5% nonfat milk) for 55 min at room temperature. Following another 3 × 5 min TBST washes, immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 min, dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, mounted, and imaged under a Leica microscope (Leica, Germany).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, University Laboratory Animal Research of Wenzhou Medical University (approval no. wydw2024-0522; 25 January 2024) and adhered to the standards set by the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS). Female nude mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SCF) conditions. For each cell line (Kuramochi, MDA-MB-453, MCF7, HCC1937), n = 5 mice per treatment group (0.9% NaCl, RSL3, olaparib) were used, totaling 60 mice across all experiments. Kuramochi, MDA-MB-453, MCF7, and HCC1937 cells (1 × 107) suspended in Matrigel® Matrix Basement Membrane HC (BD Biocoat, Corning, New York, USA) were subcutaneously injected into the right armpit of 4-week-old Balb/c nude mice. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into three groups: control group (intratumoral injection of 0.9% NaCl), RSL3-treated group (intratumoral injection of 100 mg/kg RSL3), and olaparib-treated group (intragastric administration of 20 mg/kg olaparib dissolved in 0.9% NaCl). Treatments were administered once daily for 12 days, respectively. Tumor growth was tracked every 2 days using caliper measurements from day 0 (treatment initiation) to day 12 (treatment termination). Tumor volume (mm3) was calculated as: volume = (L × W2)/2, where L is the longest axis and W is the shortest axis. Instrument calibration was verified using reference spheres (5/10/15 mm diameters, R2 = 0.998 for linearity). On day 12, mice were euthanized, the tumors were excised, weighed, and fixed for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-test, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-test, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, and multiple t-tests with correction for multiple comparisons. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; n.s.: no significant difference). All experiments were performed in at least three biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

RSL3 induces caspase-dependent apoptosis by promoting PARP1 cleavage

To investigate the impact of RSL3 on tumor cells, we performed cell viability assays across multiple cancer cell lines representing different histological types, including hepatoma (MHCC97H, HCCLM3), osteosarcomas (SJSA-1, 143B), colorectal (LoVo, SW620, SW480), and breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-436). All cells were treated with a serial dilution of RSL3 for 12 h and exhibited sensitivity to RSL3 treatment, though IC50 values ranged from 0.34 µM to over 10 µM (Fig. 1A–D). Next, we treated various cancer cell lines with RSL3 doses above their respective IC50 values. We observed that RSL3 dramatically increased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and PARP1 while reducing their total protein levels (Fig. 1E). Consistently, RSL3-treated cells were significantly more prone to apoptosis than the control group, with apoptosis rates ranging from 7.25% to 23.4% (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). To further confirm that RSL3 induces apoptosis through caspase-dependent PARP1 cleavage, we co-treated the hepatoma cell line MHCC97H, osteosarcoma cell line SJSA-1, and colorectal cancer cell line LoVo with RSL3 and a pan-caspase apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK to inhibit caspase-3 activation. As shown in Fig. 1H, Z-VAD-FMK effectively inhibited RSL3-induced PARP1 cleavage. Additionally, Z-VAD-FMK blocked RSL3-triggered apoptosis in the tested cells (Fig. 1I, J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RSL3 induces caspase-dependent apoptosis by promoting PARP1 cleavage in various tumor cells. However, Z-VAD-FMK did not rescue the RSL3-induced reduction of full-length PARP1 (Fig. 1H). Given that PARP1 is a critical factor in the DNA damage response and severe DNA damage can also induce apoptosis [10, 20], we further explored whether RSL3 simultaneously triggers DNA damage-dependent apoptosis via full-length PARP1.

RSL3 triggers DNA damage-dependent apoptosis by decreasing full-length PARP1

Given that RSL3 induces cell cycle arrest to activate the DNA damage checkpoint [6] and considering the negligible role of Z-VAD-FMK in the RSL3-mediated reduction of full-length PARP1 (Fig. 1H), we hypothesized that RSL3 might also trigger DNA damage-dependent apoptosis via full-length PARP1. To assess this, we examined the effects of RSL3-induced DNA damage in selected cell lines (MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo). RSL3 treatment led to a significant increase in γH2AX foci (a biomarker for DNA double-strand breaks) (Fig. 2A, B) and markedly elevated γH2AX protein levels (Fig. 2C). Additionally, p53, a key regulator of the DNA damage response [21], was substantially upregulated in all RSL3-treated cells compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2C), further supporting the role of RSL3 in inducing DNA damage. This finding was further corroborated by comet assay results, which revealed longer tail moments in RSL3-treated cells (Fig. 2D, E). The DNA damage response typically coordinates cell-cycle checkpoint activation with DNA repair mechanisms. However, we observed reduced levels of cell-cycle checkpoint regulators such as CDK2, CyclinD1, CDK4, and p-Rb/Rb as well as PARP1, a critical DNA repair factor (Fig. 2C). In contrast, CHK1 and p21, key components of DNA damage-activated checkpoint signaling [22, 23], were significantly increased in RSL3-treated cells (Fig. 2C). We also noted that RSL3 induced a pronounced S-phase cell cycle arrest (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B), suggesting severe and irreversible DNA damage. Since PARP1 is a well-established sensor of DNA damage [10], we investigated whether it contributes to RSL3-induced DNA damage and subsequent apoptosis. To test this, we generated lentiviral constructs containing either a negative control (Vector) or PARP1 cDNA (PARP1 OE) and transduced MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo cells. As shown in Fig. 2F, PARP1 overexpression was successfully achieved and effectively counteracted RSL3-induced effects, restoring CDK2, CyclinD1, CDK4, and p-Rb/Rb levels while reducing γH2AX. Cell cycle and apoptosis analyses further demonstrated that PARP1 overexpression significantly mitigated RSL3-induced S-phase arrest and apoptosis (Fig. 2G, H and Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). These findings highlight the critical role of PARP1 in mediating RSL3-induced DNA damage-dependent apoptosis.

RSL3 reduces PARP1 m6A modification via the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway

To elucidate how RSL3 decreases full-length PARP1 levels, we first examined potential degradation pathways. Neither the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA nor the proteasome inhibitor MG132 could prevent the RSL3-induced reduction of full-length PARP1 (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B), ruling out a significant role for lysosomal or ubiquitin–proteasome pathways. We then investigated whether RSL3 suppresses PARP1 transcription. Using three distinct primers targeting the coding sequence (CDS) regions (5′ → 3′) of PARP1 (NM_001618) (designed via PrimerBank: https://pga.gh.harvard.edu/primerbank/), we comprehensively assessed PARP1 mRNA levels. Surprisingly, PARP1 transcripts were significantly upregulated in RSL3-treated cells (Fig. 3A). Since m6A modifications regulate mRNA translation, degradation, splicing, and nuclear export [24], we hypothesized that RSL3 might impair PARP1 translation via m6A modulation. Using WHISTLE (https://whistle-epitranscriptome.com/) [25], we identified site 3978 as a potential m6A locus in the PARP1 gene (Supplementary Table S3). However, a prior epitranscriptomic study highlighted site 3496, proximal to the 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR), as critical for PARP1 mRNA stability [25]. We thus tested whether RSL3 alters m6A modification at this site (Fig. 3B). MeRIP-qPCR confirmed significant m6A enrichment at the predicted locus, which was markedly diminished by RSL3 treatment (Fig. 3B, C).

To probe the mechanism further, we quantified mRNA levels of key m6A regulators, including methyltransferase like 3 (METTL3) and METTL14, as well as their downstream readers YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein F1 (YTHDF1), YTHDF2, YTHDF3, YTHDC1, RNA demethylases alkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), and erasers fat mass and obesity-associated gene (FTO). RSL3 significantly reduced METTL3 and YTHDF1 expression in MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo cells at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3D, E,). YTHDC1 was also downregulated in LoVo cells but unaffected in MHCC97H or SJSA-1 cells. Notably, the m6A2 Target database (http://m6a2target.canceromics.org/) predicted YTHDF1 binding to PARP1 mRNA (Supplementary Table S4), which RIP-qPCR validated. RSL3 strongly inhibited the interaction between YTHDF1 protein and PARP1 mRNA (Fig. 3F, G). YTHDF1 recognizes and binds m6A-containing mRNA to facilitate protein translation [26, 27]. For functional validation, we generated METTL3-overexpressing cell lines, which were confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 3H). METTL3 overexpression restored PARP1 m6A enrichment and YTHDF1 binding, albeit partially in RSL3-treated cells (Fig. 3I, J). Collectively, these results indicate that RSL3 attenuates METTL3-mediated PARP1 m6A modification and YTHDF1 binding, thereby reducing full-length PARP1 levels.

RSL3 enhances lipid peroxidation to repress METTL3-mediated PARP1 protein translation

Given the established role of METTL3–YTHDF1 signaling in protein translation [28], we next examined whether METTL3 expression could restore PARP1 protein translation following RSL3 treatment. Puromycin intake assay revealed that RSL3 suppressed global protein translation activity, while wild-type METTL3 overexpression rescued translation efficiency in RSL3-treated cells (Fig. 4A). Polyribosome-qPCR analysis further confirmed that METTL3 overexpression significantly restored PARP1 translation efficiency, which was reduced by RSL3 treatment (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, METTL3 overexpression partially reversed the RSL3-induced downregulation of YTHDF1 and PARP1 (Fig. 4C). Since RSL3 is known to target GPX4 and promote lipid peroxidation [3], we hypothesized that RSL3 might inhibit the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway through lipid peroxidation. Consistent with this, RSL3 treatment increased cellular ROS levels (Fig. 4D, E). Uric acid (UA), a potent antioxidant, has been reported to scavenge excess ROS and lipid peroxidation, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity [29]. Indeed, UA significantly elevated antioxidant capacity and reduced MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4F, G). We then investigated whether UA could counteract RSL3-induced suppression of the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway and caspase-3 activation. Notably, UA treatment reversed the inhibitory effects of RSL3 on METTL3, YTHDF1, full-length PARP1, caspase-3, and GPX4 protein levels, while also attenuating RSL3-induced caspase-3 and PARP1 cleavage (Fig. 4H). These results collectively demonstrate that RSL3 increases ROS levels to inhibit the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway, ultimately impairing PARP1 protein translation.

Inhibition of lipid peroxidation attenuates PARP1-mediated caspase- and DNA damage-dependent apoptosis induced by RSL3

Emerging evidence suggests that apoptosis and ferroptosis can interact synergistically or act complementarily to promote cell death [30, 31]. As a ferroptosis activator, RSL3 may therefore participate in intrinsic regulatory mechanisms linking these two cell death pathways. To evaluate the temporal effects of RSL3 on apoptosis and ferroptosis, we treated MHCC97H, SJSA-1, and LoVo cells with RSL3 at the specified concentration for varying durations (2, 4, 8, and 12 h). Notably, RSL3 treatment progressively increased both early (Annexin V-FITC+/PI−) and late apoptotic populations (Annexin V-FITC+/PI+) (Fig. 5A, B), while inducing a transient increase followed by a decrease in ferroptotic populations (Annexin V-FITC−/PI+ cells) across all treated cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S5A). This time-dependent pattern suggests that RSL-induced ferroptosis precedes apoptosis, with ferroptosis peaking and subsequently declining while apoptosis continues to accumulate throughout the observation period (Fig. 5A, B and Supplementary Fig. S5A). Given that Fer-1 inhibits ferroptosis by maintaining glutathione levels, a mechanism known to reduce UVB-induced γH2AX elevation [32] and suppress PARP1 cleavage [33], we investigated whether lipid peroxidation inhibition could mitigate RSL3-induced caspase- and DNA damage-dependent apoptosis. In cells pretreated with Fer-1 or Lip-1 before RSL3 exposure, we observed significant reversal of RSL3-induced PARP1 and caspase-3 cleavage, full-length PARP1 and METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway suppression, and γH2AX accumulation (Fig. 5C). Corroborating these findings, both Fer-1 and Lip-1 treatment reduced γH2AX foci formation and apoptotic rates compared with RSL3 treatment alone (Fig. 5D–F and Supplementary Fig. S5B). Conversely, these inhibitors substantially restored cell viability despite RSL3 exposure (Supplementary Fig. S5C). Collectively, these results establish lipid peroxidation as a critical mediator of RSL3-induced, PARP1-dependent apoptosis via both caspase activation and DNA damage pathways.

RSL3 retains its pro-apoptotic function in PARPi-resistant cell lines

Our findings in Fig. 1B demonstrate that RSL3 induces PARP1 cleavage and reduces full-length PARP1 in MDA-MB-436 cells. Given that breast cancer cells frequently develop acquired PARPi resistance, we investigated whether RSL3 maintains its pro-apoptotic function in PARPi-resistant breast cancer cells. We selected two PARPi-sensitivity cell lines, Kuromochi [34] and HCC1395 [35], and three PARPi-resistant cell lines, MDA-MB-453 [36], MCF7 [36], and HCC1937 [35, 37]. Cell viability assays revealed that PARPi-sensitivity cells showed high sensitivity to RSL3 (IC50 < 1 µM), while PARPi-resistant cells exhibited relative resistance (IC50 > 4 µM) (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Notably, these concentrations remain clinically relevant, as phase I trial data report plasma concentrations ranging from 1 µM to 8.5 µM at the biologically active 100 mg BID dose [38]. Additionally, olaparib demonstrates growth inhibition in pediatric solid tumor cell lines with a median IC50 of 3.6 µM (range from 1 to 33.8 µM) [38]. Our results showed > 50% inhibition at 5 μM in PARPi-sensitive cells (Kuramochi and HCC1395) versus < 50% inhibition at 50 μM in PARPi-resistant cells (MDA-MB-453, MCF7, and HCC1937) (Supplementary Fig. S6B). To further characterize the effects of RSL3, we treated these cells with RSL3 or olaparib for 1–5 days. RSL3 effectively reduced viability in both PARPi-sensitive and PARPi-resistant cells (Fig. 6A, B) and induced time-dependent apoptosis in PARPi-resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. S6C, D). Since mitochondrial transmembrane potential (ΔΨm) loss represents a hallmark of apoptosis, we assessed ΔΨm using JC-1 staining. RSL3 triggered time-dependent mitochondrial depolarization in PARPi-resistant cells (Fig. 6C, D). Mechanistically, p53 can directly activate BAX while suppressing BCL-2 expression [39], leading to ΔΨm reduction, CytC release, and subsequent caspase-3 activation [40]. Indeed, RSL3 treatment increased p53, CytC, and cleaved caspase-3 protein levels while reducing full-length caspase-3 and PARP1 in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, RSL3 downregulated BCL2, BCL2L1, and MYC mRNA while upregulating BAX mRNA compared with controls (Fig. 6F). These findings demonstrate that RSL3 induces apoptosis in PARPi-resistant cells through p53-mediated CytC release and caspase-3 activation.

RSL3 inhibits PARPi-resistant cell xenograft tumor growth in vivo

We next investigated the antitumor efficacy of RSL3 in vivo using mouse xenograft models derived from PARPi-sensitive Kuramochi cells and PARPi-resistant MDA-MB-453, MCF7, and HCC1937 cells (Fig. 7A). Following subcutaneous implantation into BALB/c nude mice, Kuramochi-derived xenografts demonstrated marked sensitivity to olaparib treatment, while MDA-MB-453, MCF7, and HCC1937 xenografts exhibited resistance, as evidenced by tumor weight and volume measurements (Fig. 7B–D). In contrast, RSL3 treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth parameters (size, weight, and volume) in both PARPi-sensitive and PARPi-resistant models compared with control groups (Fig. 7B–D). Importantly, neither RSL3 nor PARPi treatment caused significant body weight loss or organ toxicity, indicating good tolerance at the administered doses (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Fig. S7A–C). Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that RSL3 treatment substantially decreased cellular proliferation (Ki67 staining) while increasing DNA damage marker (γH2AX staining) and apoptosis (cleaved caspase-3 staining) (Fig. 7F and Supplementary Fig. S8A–C). Together, these data show that RSL3 effectively inhibits the growth of PARPi-resistant xenograft tumors in vivo.

Discussion

RSL3, a potent inducer of ferroptosis, exerts its antitumor effects through a coordinated interplay between ferroptosis and apoptosis, which serve as a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy [5]. Our study elucidates a dual mechanism by which RSL3 modulates tumor progression via ROS accumulation, involving both caspase- and DNA damage-dependent apoptosis. To comprehensively evaluate this mechanism, we selected four distinct histological tumor types, viz. hepatoma, osteosarcoma, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer cells, on the basis of their clinical relevance, molecular heterogeneity, and therapeutic challenges. Hepatocellular carcinoma ranks as the third leading cause of cancer mortality globally, with a 5-year survival rate less than 20% in advanced stages due to intrinsic chemoresistance [41]. Osteosarcoma predominates in adolescents and young adults, exhibiting an event-free survival rate less than 30% in metastatic disease despite aggressive therapy [42, 43]. Colorectal and breast cancers collectively account for approximately 25% of global cancer incidence, with an increasing prevalence of treatment-resistant subtypes [41]. These cell lines exhibit GPX4-dependent ferroptosis vulnerability [44–47], while osteosarcoma cells show ROS-driven apoptotic susceptibility, as shown in our previous research [18]. All four malignancies demonstrate PARP1 overexpression [48, 49] and display limited clinical responses to PARP1 inhibitors, with unsatisfactory therapeutic outcomes [50]. This multicancer approach facilitates mechanistic validation across diverse biological contexts while addressing high-priority clinical demands. Here, we observed that RSL3 orchestrates synergistic ferroptosis-apoptosis activation through a caspase-dependent signaling pathway and epitranscriptomic regulation. Mechanistically, RSL3 induces lipid peroxidation-driven ROS generation to promote PARP1 cleavage, thereby activating caspase-dependent apoptosis. Conversely, RSL3-induced ROS accumulation also represses the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway, reducing PARP1 m6A modification and subsequently decreasing PARP1 transcript stability and translational efficiency, ultimately leading to DNA damage-dependent apoptosis. Over 40% of BRCA-mutant breast cancers develop acquired PARPi resistance [51]. Similarly, breast cancer cells include both PARPi-resistant and PARPi-sensitive subtypes. We observed that RSL3 maintained apoptotic potency in PARPi-resistant breast cancer cells in vitro and suppressed xenograft growth in vivo, demonstrating that its dual regulatory effects on PARP1 cleavage and full-length suppression during ferroptosis position it as a promising candidate for overcoming canonical resistance mechanisms. This approach is consistent with the “bench-to-bedside” research paradigm, which addresses the molecular understanding of RSL3 on ferroptosis-apoptosis crosstalk through PARP1 cleavage and full-length suppression in cancers. It also provides preclinical validation for dual-pathway targeting strategies in PARPi-resistant tumors.

Ferroptosis, as a unique form of programmed cell death, plays a crucial role in tumorigenesis. Importantly, targeting ferroptosis has shown significant efficacy against apoptosis-resistant cancers through lipid peroxidation accumulation [52]. Meanwhile, growing evidence demonstrates that ferroptosis activators exert substantial pro-apoptotic effects. For example, dexamethasone not only sensitizes cells to ferroptosis but also causes intrinsic apoptosis by downregulating BCL2 and BIM expression [53, 54]. Temozolomide induces apoptosis by generating single- and double-strand DNA breaks [55]. Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, a key enzyme in pyrimidine biosynthesis, and its inhibitors such as brequinar selectively induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells [56]. These compounds also exacerbate cancer cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by inhibiting ferroptosis suppressor protein-1 (FSP1), suggesting that FSP1, a glutathione-independent ferroptosis suppressor, may participate in apoptosis regulation [57]. Erastin, a SLC7A11 inhibitor, facilitates apoptosis via p53-mediated pathways [4]. RSL3, a well-characterized ferroptosis inducer, drives ROS-mediated apoptosis through the MYB/BCL2 signaling pathway [5]. Our study further reveals that RSL3 induces caspase-3 activation and DNA damage-mediated apoptosis via PARP1 cleavage and full-length suppression across various tumor cell types, though RSL3-induced ferroptosis precedes apoptotic events. Consistent with our findings, previous research identified ferroptosis as an effective strategy for cancer cell elimination through oxidative damage induction and subsequent apoptosis, confirming the temporal delay of apoptosis relative to ferroptosis [58]. Notably, we observed that lipid peroxidation inhibition increases full-length PARP1 while inhibiting its cleavage, suggesting that RSL3-induced apoptosis may represent a secondary consequence of ferroptosis. The therapeutic potential of combined apoptosis-ferroptosis induction has been well documented [59]. For instance, novel near-infrared (NIR)-II type I, aggregation-induced emission (AIE) photosensitizers disrupt cellular redox homeostasis, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and lethal lipid peroxidation that simultaneously triggers apoptosis and ferroptosis, effectively inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, multicellular tumor spheroid growth, and in vivo tumor development [60]. Therefore, RSL3 likely suppresses PARPi-resistant tumorigenesis through coordinated ferroptosis-apoptosis synergy.

PARP1, an ADP-ribosyltransferase, catalyzes the transfer of multiple ADP-ribose units to DNA during repair processes, thereby maintaining genomic integrity. PARP1-deficient cells and mice exhibit more severe DNA perturbations, ranging from impaired DNA integrity to defective DNA repair mechanisms [61]. PARPi, including olaparib and veliparib, primarily inhibit PARP1/2-mediated PARylation and trap PARP1/2 on DNA [13, 14]. However, their clinical efficacy is limited to approximately 10–20% of BRCA1/2-mutant ovarian or breast cancer patients owing to acquired PARPi resistance [15]. PARP1 is also involved in p53-induced caspase-3 cleavage [62]. Our results indicate that RSL3 significantly enhances PARP1-mediated γH2AX accumulation while promoting p53-mediated CytC release and caspase-3 cleavage in PARPi-resistant cells, ultimately inducing apoptosis. Under physiological conditions, caspase-3 exists as an inactive zymogen (32–35 kDa). Apoptotic stimuli (e.g., RSL3-induced ROS/p53 activation in our study) initiate proteolytic processing by upstream initiator caspases such as caspase-8 or caspase-9. This generates active caspase-3 fragments (17/12 kDa) while depleting procaspase-3 reserves [63]. Crucially, activated caspase-3 exhibits autocatalytic activity, enabling both self-sustained activation and cleavage of remaining procaspase-3 molecules [64]. Although total caspase-3 protein undergoes ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation, this process does not inhibit its proteolytic activity [63]. This creates a nonlinear amplification loop that drives the exponential conversion of the zymogen pool into active fragments, accounting for the rapid depletion of full-length caspase-3 alongside the accumulation of cleavage products [64]. Our data further reveal that RSL3 induces ROS-mediated DNA damage, upregulating p53 expression. As a master regulator of mitochondria, p53 coordinates BAX upregulation, BCL-2 suppression, and CytC release [9], establishing a permissive environment for caspase cascade activation. Consequently, the observed apoptotic dominance in PARPi-resistant cells is consistent with accelerated caspase-3 activation-degradation kinetics (Fig. 6E). DNA damage also stimulates pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins, activating downstream effectors BAX to promote CytC release and initiate caspase-dependent apoptosis [8]. Although prominent PARP1 cleavage is not observed in RSL3-treated PARPi-resistant cells, RSL3 still triggers caspase-dependent apoptosis through BAX activation and CytC release. Interestingly, we observed a discrepancy between pronounced mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 6C, D) and sustained cellular viability of about 80% (Supplementary Fig. S5A, B), reflecting both the hierarchical progression of apoptotic signaling and methodological limitations in viability assessment. ΔΨm collapse represents an early commitment phase of apoptosis, preceding irreversible plasma membrane permeabilization. During this latent phase, cells maintain membrane integrity and retain several intracellular enzymes such as lactate dehydrogenase [65], thus remaining classified as “viable” despite committed apoptotic signaling [65]. This leads to a potential underestimation of early apoptotic populations and consequent overestimation of viability. Therefore, accurate viability assessment requires a comprehensive evaluation of all nonviable fractions, including early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic populations. Conventional viability assays primarily capture terminal apoptotic stages, while persistent cytosolic ATP levels may artificially sustain “viability” measurements despite ongoing caspase-3 activation [66].

How does RSL3 concurrently activate two parallel apoptotic pathways during ferroptosis? Our experiments show that RSL3 promotes PARP1 cleavage in caspase-dependent apoptosis while simultaneously reducing full-length PARP1 levels in DNA damage-dependent apoptosis. Pharmacological inhibition of ROS generation and lipid peroxidation using UA attenuated RSL3-induced caspase-3 activation and PARP1 cleavage patterns, confirming ROS overproduction as the central regulator of these dual apoptotic mechanisms. These results corroborate previous findings showing that ROS scavengers completely reverse RSL3-mediated PARP1 and caspase-3 cleavage [5]. Notably, we identified a novel regulatory axis in which RSL3-induced ROS accumulation suppresses the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway, thereby inhibiting PARP1 transcript levels. This observation corroborates established mechanisms of METTL3-mediated mRNA stabilization, whereby the m6A methyltransferase enhances PARP1 mRNA stability by recruiting YTHDF1 to its 3′-UTR [67]. These data suggest that RSL3 primarily operates through ROS generation rather than direct PARP1 interaction. Furthermore, the observed effects on PARP1 expression appear to be downstream consequences of oxidative stress. However, how does METTL3 regulate PARP1 cleavage? Emerging evidence indicates that METTL3 maintains PARP1 protein stability by inhibiting ubiquitin‐proteasomal degradation [68], while METTL3 depletion induces endoplasmic reticulum stress-mediated caspase-3 and PARP1 activation [69]. This mechanistic connection between METTL3 downregulation and PARP1 cleavage in RSL3-treated cells appears dependent on ROS-mediated oxidative stress rather than ferroptosis-independent pathways. Our data support a model where RSL3-induced redox imbalance simultaneously activates canonical caspase-dependent apoptosis and METTL3-regulated PARP1 destabilization. This dual mechanism establishes synergistic routes for apoptotic execution during ferroptosis.

How does RSL3 inhibit the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway during ferroptosis? METTL3 enhances the stability of PARP1 by recruiting YTHDF1 to target the 3′-UTR of PARP1 mRNA in the DNA damage response [67]. Indeed, we observed that RSL3 increased ROS production and lipid peroxidation to inhibit the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway, leading to the decay of PARP1 protein transcription and a reduction in full-length PARP1 (Fig. 4). We also found that ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 and Lip-1 could reverse the RSL3-induced inhibition of the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway and subsequent DNA damage-dependent apoptosis (Fig. 5). Inconsistent with our findings, Zhou et al. reported that METTL3 and its reader IGF2BP2 or YTHDF2 promotes SLC7A11 stability to inhibit ferroptosis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [70]. Liu et al. also showed that METTL3–YTHDF1 stabilizes mitochondrial carrier homolog 2 (MECH2) to reduce ferroptosis in glioma cells [28]. They found that knockdown of METTL3 increases lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation via SLC7A11 to accelerate ferroptosis. However, Meng et al. observed that METTL3 increases the stability of GPX4 to prevent ferroptosis in glioblastoma [71]. Therefore, the underlying mechanism of METTL3 in ferroptosis depends on its readers and/or the tumor type. Of note, it remains unclear how RSL3-induced ROS accumulation leads to the reduction of the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway. RSL3 may trigger ROS production, promoting aberrant redox homeostasis and disrupting m6A balance, but the precise mechanism is still unknown. Du et al. reported that METTL3 is modified by small ubiquitin-related modifier 1 (SUMO1) mainly at lysine residues K177, K211, K212, and K215, which inhibits its m6A RNA methyltransferase activity [72]. It would be interesting to evaluate whether a similar molecular mechanism operates in RSL3-induced ROS accumulation affecting METTL3.

METTL3, METTL14, and their cofactors, including Wilms tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP), VIRMA, and RBM15, catalyze m6A modification as the “m6A writer” [73]. Conversely, ALKBH5 and FTO are genuine demethylases, which can remove m6A modification from RNA as “m6A erasers” and thus keep m6A in a dynamic balance [73]. The YTH domain family of proteins, including YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, YTHDC1, and YTHDC2, have been identified as direct “m6A readers” [74]. Among these, YTHDF1, YTHDF3, YTHDC2, and a recently identified IGF2BP family, including IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3, promote mRNA translation. Additionally, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, YTHDC2, and IGF2BP1/2/3 enhance mRNA stability [74, 75]. While YTHDC1 primarily influences mRNA splicing and nuclear export [74]. As “m6A erasers,” ALKBH5 and FTO reduce m6A levels, and vice versa [76, 77]. Here, we found that RSL3 decreases the m6A methylases METTL3 and METTL14 levels while increasing the m6A demethylases ALKBH5 and FTO mRNA levels in MHCC97H and LoVo cells, suggesting an overall reduction in m6A levels. It will be intriguing to determine whether RSL3 simultaneously regulates both m6A methylases and demethylases in these cell lines, potentially reflecting a bidirectional regulatory mechanism in epitranscriptomic control following RSL3 treatment.

In summary, our study uncovers a previously unrecognized connection between RSL3 and caspase- and DNA damage-dependent apoptosis, mediated by both cleaved and full-length PARP1. Furthermore, we elucidate the mechanism underlying the role of RSL3 on antitumor properties in PARPi-resistant tumors. These findings open new avenues for exploring PARPi resistance mechanisms and identifying potential combination therapies, such as ferroptosis activators, which may improve treatment efficacy in PARPi-resistant cancers and benefit patients unresponsive to current strategies.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated how RSL3 induces caspase- and DNA damage-dependent apoptosis by modulating PARP1 cleavage and its full-length form during ferroptosis. Mechanistically, RSL3 elevates ROS generation to promote caspase-3 activation and subsequent PARP1 cleavage, which initiates caspase-dependent apoptosis. Furthermore, RSL3-induced ROS accumulation inhibits cellular antioxidant capacity, thereby impairing PARP1 m6A modification via the METTL3–YTHDF1 pathway, leading to disruption of PARP1 protein translocation. This contributes to γH2AX accumulation and severe DNA damage-induced apoptosis. In addition, our findings highlight the critical role of RSL3 in suppressing tumorigenesis in PARPi-resistant tumors, suggesting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent, particularly for PARPi-resistant cancers.
