Healthcare trajectories following cancer surgery in older adults: insights from the French health data system
Authors: Nicolas Bertrand, Héloïse Fauchon, Charline Jean, Estelle Neveu, Thomas Gilbert, Emmanuelle Leray, François Puisieux, Dominique Somme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Healthcare trajectories of older patients with cancer, particularly after surgical treatment, remain poorly described. Understanding these trajectories is crucial for improving patient outcomes and optimizing healthcare resources. This study aimed to describe the one-year healthcare trajectories of older patients following cancer surgery in France and identify their determinants.Methods This observational study was conducted using the French National Health Data System, a nationwide medico-administrative database. The study included 140,442 patients aged 75 years and older, identified through cancer diagnosis codes and surgical procedure classifications between 2018 and 2021. A multichannel sequence analysis was performed to cluster healthcare trajectories within one year post-surgery. A favourable trajectory was defined as having a low number of hospitalization days and deaths. A ridge-L2-penalized logistic regression model was employed to identify factors associated with each trajectory.ResultsFive distinct healthcare trajectory clusters were identified. The most prevalent trajectory (69.4%) was characterized by a low healthcare density, with a median one-year cumulative hospital length of stay of 12 days. Adverse trajectories were associated with older age, frailty, comorbidity, emergency admissions, prior chemotherapy, and some types of surgery (pancreas, lymph node dissection, bladder, esophagogastric, multiple sites).ConclusionsCancer surgery for patients aged 75 years and older is predominantly associated with favourable outcomes and limited healthcare utilization. The use of trajectory analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of postoperative outcomes in this population, highlighting the impact of frailty, and may inform the development of new decision-making frameworks tailored to older adults with cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07273-5.Keywords: Cancer surgery, Healthcare trajectories, Frailty

Background

The number of new cases of cancer in the over-80s is expected to triple worldwide by 2050 [1]. In 2018, an estimated 2.3 million new cancer cases (excluding non-melanoma skin cancers) were aged 80 years or older worldwide (13% of all cancer cases). Cancer-related mortality is higher in older individuals [2].

Surgery is the main treatment for localized solid cancers, regardless of age. The benefit-to-risk ratio of cancer surgery in the older population is often difficult to determine. The most frequently reported outcomes in oncological surgery are short-term (within thirty days): postoperative complications, geriatric events, increased length of stay, and intra-hospital mortality rate after surgical intervention [3–6]. Long-term outcomes are rarely evaluated, as survival beyond one year, with the exception of colorectal cancer [4, 5, 7]. Nevertheless, these outcomes’ “binary” status (presence or not) fails to capture the diversity of responses of older patients after cancer surgery. Additionally, the impact of frailty on the distinct outcomes was not evaluated in these studies.

Multichannel sequence analysis for trajectories was first developed in the social sciences in the 1980s and has more recently been extended to the field of healthcare, with some studies focusing on older persons. To our knowledge, only two previous studies have used healthcare trajectories as an outcome of interest in older patients with cancer [8, 9]. This scarcity of data results in a complex treatment decision process, mainly based on the experiences of clinicians and surgeons [10]. National health databases, such as the French National Health Data System (SNDS), enable a comprehensive exploration of healthcare trajectories on a national scale by providing detailed data on healthcare consumption, in both primary and secondary settings [11]. We hypothesized that leveraging the SNDS would be a more sensitive method for assessing the healthcare trajectories and outcomes of cancer surgery in older patients. Hence, the primary objective of this work was to describe the one-year healthcare trajectories of older patients after cancer surgery in France and to identify the factors associated with adverse trajectories. The secondary objectives were to describe the one-year mortality, institutionalization, and cumulative hospital days rates.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The SNDS is a nationwide medico-administrative programme launched in 2016, which covers approximately 99% of the French population. Two database sections were used for this work: the Medical Information System Program (PMSI), which contains data pertaining to hospitalizations, and the National Health Insurance Information System (SNIIRAM) database, which contains data related to out-of-hospital deaths. The index stays were identified as follows: (1) starting between 01/01/2018 and 12/31/2021, (2) associating a code indicating a cancer diagnosis in accordance with the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) [12] and a Common Classification of Medical Acts (CCAM) code of cancer surgery as defined by the French National Cancer Institute (INCa), [13, 14] (3) involving a patient aged at least 75 years at the time of admission. The CCAM and ICD-10 codes used for inclusion and exclusion are provided in Supplemental Table S1. Outpatient surgeries (i.e., those not requiring overnight hospitalization), hematological tumors, and prevalent metastases were excluded. In the event that a patient had multiple admissions for cancer surgery, only the initial admission was retained. The twenty-four months preceding the admission date of the index stay and the twelve months following it were considered as the periods of previous and subsequent hospitalizations, respectively. A variety of data were extracted from the index admission: the patient’s age, sex, ICD-10 principal and secondary diagnoses, including tumor type, vital status at the end of the admission, hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay if relevant, year of the index admission, mode of admission and of discharge. From the dataset comprising previous stays, only diagnoses according to the ICD-10 classification were extracted. For subsequent hospitalizations, we reported the date of admission, the length of stay, and the vital status at the end of the stay. Subsequent hospitalizations were categorized into five distinct types: day hospital, conventional hospital ward (subdivided into surgery and medicine units), rehabilitation, and at-home hospital. The type of residential setting (long-term care unit or nursing home, as opposed to no institution), and, in the event of death, the date of this occurrence were also recorded. This information was gathered over the subsequent twenty-four months following the index stay admission.

Comorbidity and frailty assessment

The Mortality-Related Morbidity Index (MRMI) was calculated for each patient [15] based on the data from the SNDS mapping tool for the calendar year preceding admission. The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) was calculated for each patient [16] based on the ICD-10 diagnoses from the index stay and the previous stays in a 24-month lookback period. Patients were classified into three categories, applying the threshold of the original study: low risk of frailty < 5, intermediate risk 5–15, and high risk > 15.

Ethics

The research teams from university hospitals and cancer centers, who have undergone the requisite training and obtained the necessary authorization, have permanent access to the SNDS for research purposes. The research team has access to the data set in accordance with the established data protection protocols. The research was reported in compliance with the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guidelines, as detailed in Supplemental Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were performed on R (The R Project for Statistical Computing) on the SNDS workspace, employing the packages “TraMineR”, “WeightedClusters”, and “glmnet” [17, 18] .

Healthcare trajectories

For each patient, the total length of follow-up (12 months) was divided into 26 periods of two weeks. For each period, a distinct status was ascribed to each patient based on their consumption of hospital care. The possible statuses were death, medicine unit, surgery unit, day hospital, at-home hospital, and rehabilitation. In consideration of the irreversibility of death and the varying degrees of care consumption, a hierarchy was established to delineate the status of individuals experiencing co-occurring events within the same two-week period. The hierarchy was as follows: death > medicine unit > surgery unit > rehabilitation unit > at-home hospital > day hospital. In the absence of reported activity (either death or hospitalization), the dimension was defined as “none”. The individual healthcare trajectories, comprising a succession of twenty-six statuses, were computed and subjected to a multichannel sequence analysis to identify clusters of healthcare trajectories [11, 19, 20]. This method was based on a dissimilarity matrix that reported the pairwise difference between patients using the optimal matching method. The focus was on timing, specifically the total time spent in each status [21]. Transition weights, detailed in Supplemental Table S3, were used. As no gold standard is defined, three distinct clustering methods (hierarchical clustering, partition around medoids (PAM), and a hybrid method) were employed to join pairwise close individual trajectories and to aggregate these clusters of two patients until the difference between clusters was optimal [22]. The quality of clustering was assessed using weighted average silhouette width (ASWw), Hubert’s gamma (HG), and pseudo-R2 [22, 23]. The final method was selected based on the aforementioned indicators, a sufficient number of patients in each cluster (≥ 5%), and the clinical relevance of the clusters as determined by the geriatricians and oncologists of our team. The clusters were described in terms of the predominant statuses involved and their times. The characteristics of patients, tumors, and index stays were documented for each cluster. The surgical procedures were classified according to their location and the number of patients who underwent them. Pertinent covariates that were found to be significant in the univariate analysis were introduced into the regression model. We defined the statistical significance with an alpha level of 5% (p < 0.05) for the univariate and multivariate analyses.

To address multicollinearity among predictors and stabilize coefficient estimates, we estimated cluster-specific associations (cluster vs. all others) by fitting penalized multinomial logistic regression models (Ridge regression). Specifically, for each of the five trajectory clusters, we constructed a binary outcome (1 = membership in the cluster; 0 = membership in any other cluster) and fitted a ridge-L2-penalized logistic regression model. Cross-validation (5-fold) was performed to select the penalty parameter λ, using the “one-standard-error” rule (λ₁ₑ), which provides a parsimonious and robust model.

Because standard errors and confidence intervals are not directly available from penalized models, we employed a nonparametric percentile bootstrap procedure to obtain 95% confidence intervals for the odds ratios. For each cluster-specific model, we executed 100 stratified bootstrap resamples—resampling cases with replacement while preserving cluster prevalence—to refit the ridge regression and extract coefficient estimates at the same λ₁ₑ. The empirical distribution of the bootstrapped coefficient estimates was then used to compute percentile-based 95% confidence intervals for the odds ratios (log-scale quantiles back‑transformed). For categorical variables (notably surgical subtypes), we explicitly set Colon surgery as the reference category before constructing design matrices, ensuring all odds ratios for surgery types were interpreted relative to colon surgery.

Healthcare indicators

The index stays and the patients from the overall study population and the analysis population were described using numbers (percentages) for qualitative data, and median [interquartile range] for quantitative data. The one-year and two-year mortality rates were presented. The cumulative hospital days proportion was calculated as the ratio of the total number of days spent in the hospital to the total number of days lived. These outcomes were described for the overall study population and each cluster group.

Results

Study population

A total of 140,442 patients aged at least 75 years met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1; Table 1), representing 3,651,492 periods of two weeks. The patients were aged 81.0 [77.0–85.0] years, 82,335 (58.6%) were women, and the most prevalent cancer type was colorectal cancer (40,384–28.8%). Regarding frailty, 69.2% of the population was considered low risk, 22.5% intermediate risk, and 8.3% high risk. A total of 9,608 patients (6.8%) were admitted from the emergency unit, 3,462 (2.5%) died during the index hospital stay, and 112,095 (79.8%) were discharged home.

Healthcare trajectories

Cluster description

Our analysis identified five distinct clusters (Table 1; Fig. 2, Supplemental Figure S1): “Low Healthcare Density” (Cluster A), “High Healthcare Density” (Cluster B), “Ambulatory/Chemo” (Cluster C), “Early Death” (Cluster D), and “Late Death” (Cluster E). The PAM method was retained with an ASWw of 0.47, an HG of 0.85, and a pseudo R2 of 0.62.

Clusters’ chronograms (individual data). For each cluster, patients’ status are represented for each two-weeks period with distinct colors: green for surgery, purple for medicine, blue for rehabilitation, pink for day hospital, brown for at-home hospital, grey for death, and light yellow for none. One line represents one patient

Cluster A “Low Healthcare Density” (97,465 patients – 69.4%) was characterized by a low prevalence of hospital stays within one year following the index stay. Of note, except for the first two-weeks period of index hospitalization, 33,815 patients of this cluster reported no other period of hospitalization (or death).

Cluster B “High Healthcare Density” (17,661 patients – 12.6%) included individuals who spent a considerable number of days in the hospital, primarily in medicine or rehabilitation units.

Cluster C “Ambulatory/Chemo” (10,654 patients – 7.6%) comprised patients with predominantly day hospital stays.

Cluster D “Early Death” (7,980 patients – 5.7%) comprised patients who died within four months of the index stay.

Cluster E “Late Death” (6,682 patients – 4.8%) comprised patients whose mortality mainly occurred beyond six months from discharge.

Female patients showed a reduced likelihood of belonging to the “Early Death” (Odds-Ratio OR 0.72; 95% confidence interval [0.68–0.75]) and “Late Death” (OR 0.89 [0.86–0.91]) clusters. As age increased, patients were less likely to pertain to the “Ambulatory/Chemo” cluster (OR 0.66 [0.65–0.74] for patients aged 80-84y, OR 0.15 [0.14–0.27] for patients aged 90+) and more likely to pertain to the other adverse clusters. A similar trend was observed with HFRS categories: patients at a higher risk of frailty (HFRS > 15) were more likely to be in the “High Healthcare Density”, “Early Death” (OR 3,5 [3.08–4.01]), and “Late Death” clusters, and less likely to pertain to the “Ambulatory/Chemo” cluster. A higher MRMI was also associated with being in the “High Healthcare Density”, “Early Death”, and “Late Death” clusters. Admission from the emergency unit and prior chemotherapy were also identified as factors associated with all adverse trajectories. Regarding cancer surgery, lymph node dissections, bladder, esophagogastric, multiple, and pancreas surgeries were associated with all adverse trajectories. A full description of the population by surgery type is provided in Supplemental Table S5.

Healthcare indicators

The overall one-year mortality rate was 13.3% (18,758 patients). This ratio reached 100% in the “Early Death” and “Late Death” clusters, while it remained below 8% in the remaining clusters (Table 2). The overall two-year mortality was 22.2% (31,115 patients). Of note, the thirty-day mortality by surgery group is detailed in Supplemental Table S7.

A total of 6,444 individuals (4.6%) were institutionalized during the year following the index stay. The median cumulative hospital LOS within the year following the index admission was 21 [8–52] days. Patients spent 6.8% [2.5–19%] of their living days in hospital. This ratio was the highest in the “Early Death” cluster (100% [73%-100%]) and the lowest in the “Low Healthcare Density” cluster (3% [2%-8%]).

The median LOS for hospitalizations occurring within the year following the index admission varied according to the type of hospital unit. It increased from 5 [2–11] days in medical and surgical units (excluding day hospital) to 26 [15–41] days in rehabilitation units.

Discussion

The present study describes the healthcare trajectories of patients aged 75 years and over within one year after cancer surgery. The analysis yielded five distinct healthcare trajectories. The majority of patients (69.4%) exhibited a favorable trajectory named “Low Healthcare Density”, characterized by a few days of hospitalization (median 12 days) in the year following the index admission, and a one-year mortality rate of 2.4%. Several clinical, socio-demographic, and oncological characteristics were identified as being associated with specific trajectories. Patients were less likely to be assigned to the “Ambulatory/Chemo” cluster if they were older and at a higher risk of frailty. Given that the majority of patients in this cluster received chemotherapy (97.2%), these findings suggest that practitioners select patients for chemotherapy based on these characteristics, which are already associated with toxicity and tolerability in the literature [24]. We observed that emergency surgery was associated with all pejorative patterns, which is consistent with the literature [4, 25]. Age was also associated with adverse trajectories, especially the “Early Death” and “Late Death” patterns [4, 6, 25]. Frailty risk (HFRS), comorbidities (MRMI), were associated with the “High Healthcare Density”, “Early Death”, and “Late Death” clusters. Pancreas surgery was the surgery type most strongly associated with these three patterns.

To date, there has been a paucity of published data on this topic. One French study, however, has analyzed the trajectories of older patients with cancer in a CGA-referred cohort [8].

It is noteworthy that the frequency of surgeries declined towards the end of the study period, even when the years 2020 and 2021, which were affected by the global pandemic, were excluded. Two potential explanations for this trend are the increase in outpatient surgeries and a more rigorous patient selection process. Patients at low risk of frailty, as indicated by HFRS, were more frequent in our study (69.2%) compared to previous validation cohorts (42.4% and 45.4%) [16, 26]. This suggests that patient selection in our study population may have prioritized those with lower frailty risks, aligning with findings from geriatric oncology populations where about 60% also had HFRS < 5 [27]. Another argument in favor of patient selection is the low number of persons living in an institution in the present work (2.1%). In comparison, a French nationwide study estimated that 10% of individuals aged 75 years and over lived in an institution in 2019 [28].

Some methodological choices require further discussion. First, outpatient surgeries excluded from this study were mainly skin cancer surgeries (75.9%), which are known to be safe procedures in older adults. To maintain a certain level of homogeneity in the population, metastases codes were also excluded to explore only surgeries of primary tumors. The choice of two-week periods was consistent with the median LOS in different types of hospitalization units. The statuses hierarchy has a direct impact on the dissimilarity matrix, hence the trajectory shape. We defined a hierarchy, based on clinical “seriousness” but alternative hierarchies could have been used. The grouping of tumor surgeries was unavoidable due to the vast number of types, but the relevance of these groups is open to question. For example, the pancreas surgery group encompasses operations of the head and the tail of the pancreas, which are associated with markedly different morbidity rates. Ridge penalized logistic regression was chosen over other methods because Ridge regression provides stable estimates despite collinearity, handles clusters of different sizes, and retains all predictors. We considered propensity score matching and weighted logistic regression to be irrelevant due to multiple comparisons and small cluster sizes, respectively. However, Ridge penalized regression tends to shrink coefficients to stabilize estimates when collinearity and/or different-size clusters are present. Therefore, regarding our results, the direction of the associations described in “Cluster Membership Determinants” is more relevant to observe than the exact value of ORs, which cannot be considered as precise estimates in this methodology.

The present study has limitations. Due to the retrospective design and the use of medico-administrative data, it is not possible to eliminate the potential for coding bias, especially on diagnoses (metastases, malnutrition…). No data on tumor staging, clinical geriatric phenotype, or social support were available, although these characteristics might have an impact on healthcare trajectories [3, 8]. Indeed, the clinical geriatric phenotype was described in the work of Jean et al. [8] suggesting a high prevalence of impairments also in the surgical group. Several elements, not described in the present work, might explain the outcomes, as the presence of pre-operative comprehensive geriatric assessment or the type of surgical technique [29, 30]. Additionally, this work concentrated on hospitalization data, thereby excluding a significant portion of the healthcare sector, such as outpatient consultations with specialists or general practitioners.

The present study also has certain strengths. This is the first study to explore the healthcare pathways of older patients after inpatient all types cancer surgery, whereas the majority of studies focus on colorectal cancer. The data pertaining to surgical procedures, hospitalization, and mortality are at low risk of coding bias, thereby fostering confidence in the selection of the study population and the patterns of healthcare utilization. The proxy used for frailty (HFRS) has been validated in several studies on older individuals, also in the field of cancer [16, 26, 27, 31, 32, 33]. In addition to addressing a novel topic, an innovative statistical analysis was performed, enhancing the potential of medico-administrative databases. The SNDS is quasi-exhaustive, which ensures that the population of the present study is more representative of older persons with cancer than geriatric oncology cohorts where inclusion relies on frailty screening tools. Furthermore, the utilization of nationwide linked data enables the identification of any hospitalization that occurs in a hospital other than the index admission. The outcome is inventive and, going beyond mortality or postoperative complications, may assist in defining major cancer surgeries in a more comprehensive manner.

Conclusions

The present study describes the French population of older persons with surgically treated cancer and their healthcare trajectories within one year and indicates that favorable patterns were the most frequent. This type of outcome represents the burden of care rather than survival and is therefore a more comprehensive measure. The risk of frailty is significantly associated with adverse trajectories, underscoring the importance of CGA for individuals at risk. Additional studies focusing on specific cancer types would be beneficial. Furthermore, studies linking the SNDS with cohorts with clinical data are essential to explore additional determinants of adverse trajectories and to develop tailored individual healthcare pathways.
