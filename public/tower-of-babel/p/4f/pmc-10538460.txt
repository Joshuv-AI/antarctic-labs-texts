Roles of interferon regulatory factor 4 in the AKI-CKD transition, glomerular diseases and kidney allograft rejection
Authors: Jianling Song, Ben Ke, Weiping Tu, Xiangdong Fang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) is expressed in immune cells and is a member of the interferon regulatory factor family. Recently, it has been found that IRF4 plays important roles in the acute kidney injury (AKI)-chronic kidney disease (CKD) transition, glomerular diseases and kidney allograft rejection. In particular, the relationship between IRF4 and the AKI-CKD transition has attracted widespread attention. Furthermore, it was also found that the deficiency of IRF4 hindered the transition from AKI to CKD through the suppression of macrophage-to-fibroblast conversion, inhibition of M1-M2 macrophage polarization, and reduction in neutrophil inward flow. Additionally, an examination of the crucial role of IRF4 in glomerular disease was conducted. It was reported that inhibiting IRF4 could alleviate the progression of glomerular disease, and potential physiopathology mechanisms associated with IRF4 were postulated. Lastly, IRF4 was found to have detrimental effects on the development of antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) and T-cell-mediated rejection (TCMR).Keywords: Interferon regulatory factor 4, AKI-CKD transition, glomerular disease, kidney allograft rejection

Introduction

Interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) is a member of the family of interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), which play an important role in the immune system. It is not regulated by IRFs, and its expression is restricted to immune cells [1,2]. In addition to the regulation of B-cell and T-cell maturation [3,4] and isotype switching [5], contributing to the T-helper 2 (Th2) cytokine response [6] and the induction of Th17 responses, IRF4 is implicated in regulating the development of antigen-presenting cells [7,8]. Furthermore, the binding of IRF4 to myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (myD88) inhibits toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and TLR4 signaling, thereby affecting innate immunity [9]. Furthermore, IRF4 affects the expression of interleukin 10 (IL-10) and thereby alleviates macrophage-mediated inflammation [10–12]. IRF4 has also been associated with macrophage polarization. Deficient IRF4 decreases M2 macrophage expression [13]. Thus, it is clear that IRF4 is important for the development, maturation, differentiation and polarization of immune cells.

Globally, chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a severe health problem that increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality [14,15]. The global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for CKD in 2019 were approximately 2% [16]. From 1990 to 2019, DALYs for CKD increased by 93% [16]. By 2030, CKD is expected to be the 13th leading cause of death globally, and by 2040, it will be the fifth leading cause of death [17,18]. Therefore, the search for treatment options to delay the progression of CKD to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is urgent. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, the underlying etiologies of CKD are as follows: diabetes mellitus type 1, diabetes mellitus type 2, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, and other unspecified causes [19]. However, understanding the pathogenesis of CKD remains a challenge. In this paper, we will review the roles of IRF4 in the acute kidney injury (AKI)-CKD transition, glomerular diseases (GDs) (including membranous nephropathy [MN], IgA nephropathy [IgAN] and lupus nephritis [LN]) and kidney allograft rejection to reveal how IRF4 is involved in kidney pathophysiology and to provide valuable ideas for establishing new therapeutic options.

IRF4 and the AKI-CKD transition

AKI is a grave disease with a high morbidity and mortality rate [20], with a large proportion of survivors progressing to CKD or ESKD [21–-23]. AKI is a risk factor that is often overlooked but holds significant importance in the development of CKD. It is crucial to note that a normal serum creatinine level should not be automatically interpreted as a complete "recovery" from AKI [24]. Accordingly, numerous research endeavors have been undertaken to identify more effective biomarkers, including urinary dickkopf-3 [25], plasma neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) concentration [26], and plasma proenkephalin-A [27,28]. Several factors influence the transition from AKI to CKD; one is weak recovery from AKI, which progresses to CKD [29,30]. For recovery from AKI, macrophage proliferation and polarization play a key role [31]. Macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype may help reduce inflammation but may also lead to tissue fibrosis [32]. Significantly, the augmented presence of M2 macrophages assumes a pivotal role in the transition from AKI to CKD in humans, as these macrophages possess the capacity to facilitate said transition [33]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the expression of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), pro-fibrotic factor, by M2 macrophages [34]. Furthermore, M2 macrophage polarization is facilitated by IRF4 [13]. Consequently, it is plausible to suggest that IRF4 exerts an influence on the differentiation and polarization processes of macrophages, thereby impacting the AKI-CKD transition.

First, IRF4 deficiency impedes the AKI-CKD transition, probably inhibiting the transformation of macrophages to fibroblasts. A study by Mei-Xin Chen [35] et al. showed that IRF4 deficiency inhibits folic acid (FA)-induced post-AKI progressive CKD, suppresses the inflammatory response during progressive CKD, and reduces myeloid-derived myofibroblast accumulation during progressive kidney fibrosis. Most importantly, IRF4 was found to hinder macrophage differentiation of myofibroblasts during CKD occurring after FA-induced AKI. Furthermore, IRF4 knockdown reduces myeloid fibroblast accumulation in obstructive nephropathy and impairs the conversion of M2 macrophages to myofibroblasts (M2MMT), inhibiting the AKI-CKD transition. In addition, IRF4 deficiency inhibits myeloid fibroblast accumulation, M2MMT, and kidney fibrosis progression [36].

Next, IRF4 deficiency impedes the AKI-CKD transition, possibly by blocking M1-M2 macrophage polarization. A sustained M1-dominant inflammatory response was demonstrated in an ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) mouse model, as M1 macrophages showed prolonged G1 phase arrest, which was associated with hindered signaling by colony stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1)/IRF4 to activate M1-M2 macrophage polarization [37]. Besides, IRF4 deficiency in bone marrow cells prevents the progression of kidney fibrosis after serious IRI, mainly due to the impairment of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling pathway and subsequent reduction in circulating monocyte recruitment, as well as the reduction in kidney M2 macrophages [29]. Additionally, George Lorenz [30] and his colleagues found that mice lacking IRF4 show increased tubular cell losses and defects in the clearance of infiltrating macrophages, which are associated with increased expression of proinflammatory M1 macrophage markers and reduced expression of M2 macrophage markers. Thus, IRF4 expression was elevated in the IRI mouse model and showed restricted M1-M2 macrophage polarization. IRF4 deficiency prevents M1-M2 macrophage polarization and thus ameliorates kidney fibrosis after IRI.

In addition, IRF4 deficiency may impede the AKI-CKD transition by reducing neutrophil influx. IRF4 was found to be significantly elevated five days after IRI. The inhibition of postischemic AKI by IRF4 was associated with a decrease in neutrophil influx early after IRI. Clodronate prevented the exacerbation of IRI and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]) and chemokines (CXC ligand CXCL 2 [CXCL2] and C-C chemokine ligand 2 [CCL2]) expression in IRF4-deficient mice [38].

IRF4 deficiency was found to protect the kidneys directly. The absence of IRF4 directly affected macrophages, leading to the apoptosis of isolated macrophages, reduced macrophage migration in vivo, and decreased kidney macrophage infiltration [29, 39]. Furthermore, IRF4-deficient mice exhibited lower tubular injury scores and a decreased presence of renal interstitial fibrosis, accompanied by a decrease in pro-fibrotic and fibrotic components such as TGF-β1, TGF-β2, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibronectin, and collagens I, III, and IV [29,30]. Additionally, IRF4 deficiency led to the downregulation of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-23α, IL-1α, IL-1β, and CCL3, among others [38,39]. The evidence collectively indicates that the deficiency of IRF4 exerts a direct protective influence on the kidney.

IRF4 and GDs

GDs place a heavy burden on patients and health systems and are a major causes of ESKD. The etiologies and clinical symptoms of GDs are diverse, but GDs share many common symptoms (such as proteinuria and hematuria), treatments (such as immunosuppression), and outcomes (such as CKD and ESKD). Patients with GDs have a higher incidence of cardiovascular events, and primary and secondary GDs patients have mortality rates that are 2.7 and 3.9 times higher than those of the general population, respectively [40]. In addition, treating patients with GDs can result in significant side effects such as obesity, infections, and cancer [41]. Most importantly, the considerable psychological burdens experienced by patients arise from the uncertain prognoses and susceptibility to rapid deterioration associated with GDs. Therefore, exploring the physiopathological mechanisms of GDs is important to slow the disease progression. Several recent studies have identified the key contribution of IRF4 in developing GDs, including IgAN, MN, and LN. Therefore, we will review the role of IRF4 in IgAN, MN, and LN. IRF4 and IgAN

IgAN is a frequent form of glomerulonephritis worldwide. It was first described in 1968 [42]. The gold standard for diagnosing IgAN is kidney biopsy, and pathology shows histopathological lesions of IgA-containing immune complexes deposited in the glomerular tegument region, as well as the proliferation of mesangial cells and accumulation of extracellular matrix [43]. Approximately 20-40% of IgAN cases advance to ESKD within 20 years after disease diagnosis [44]. ESKD burdens the nation, society, and families with a heavy economic burden. Therefore, it is vital to explore the pathogenesis of IgAN to delay the progression of IgAN to ESKD [45].

The activation of the complement system [46], mucosal activation of the innate immune response [47], and TLRs [48] are the more typical pathogenic mechanisms of IgAN. In recent years, significant advances have been made in understanding the genetics of IgAN in-depth genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Several large GWAS have been performed in IgAN, and identifying IgAN susceptibility loci provides valuable insights into the inheritance of IgAN [49–53]. A recent meta-analysis of GWAS identified three new susceptibility loci, including IRF4 [54]. This advances the biological understanding of IgAN by unraveling immune and inflammatory responses.

IRF4 is a negative regulator of TLRs [9]. TLRs have been previously implicated in the pathogenesis of IgAN through the activation of mucosal immunity, oxidative stress, and the dysregulation of the Th1/Th2 balance [48,55]. In a study by Tomohiro [56] et al. IRF4 was found to decrease cytokine (IL-2, IL-4, IL-9 and TNF) responses to multiple TLR ligands (TLR2, TLR6, TLR12, TLR40 and TLR70). IRF4 also impairs T-cell activation and function by inhibiting the binding of TLR7 and myD88 [57]. In addition, TLR4 plays an important role in B-cell proliferation, driving plasma cell differentiation and promoting cellular secretion of immunoglobulins [58]. After 24 h of uninterrupted lipopolysaccharide (TLR4 ligand) stimulation, B-cells lacking IRF4 failed to undergo differentiation into plasma cells, potentially due to the inhibitory effect of IRF4 on TLRs [59]. Furthermore, it was found that similar to TLR ligands, IRF4 can also bind to myD88 and negatively regulate the expression of proinflammatory factors [9,60,61]. Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain 2 (NOD2) ultimately hinders the activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) through the TLR pathway. This inhibition is closely linked to the induction of IRF4, which assumes a pivotal role in suppressing inflammatory responses [62,63].

In summary, IRF4 is a newly identified genetic susceptibility site for IgAN, and IRF4 is associated with TLRs. IRF4 is a negative regulator of TLRs, which impairs T-cell activation and function, drives plasma cell differentiation and suppresses proinflammatory factors and inflammatory responses. TLRs have been reported to be associated with the pathogenesis of IgAN. Therefore, our hypothesis posits that IRF4 functions as an inhibitor of TLR-mediated immune responses to IgAN, potentially accomplished through the mechanism mentioned above.

IRF4 and MN

MN is the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in nondiabetic white adults [64] and is caused by the formation of subepithelial glomerular immune complexes and the formation of autoantigens (e.g. phospholipase A2 receptor [PLA2R] [65] and thrombospondin type-I domain-containing 7 A [THSD7A] [66] against podocytes. Approximately 25% of patients with primary membranous nephropathy without immediate and effective treatment will develop ESKD [64]. Research on the pathogenesis of MN is necessary. In addition to the formation of autoimmune complexes and antigen-antibody reactions, genetic factors also play a role in the pathogenesis of MN. A recent genetic study identified a significant risk for MN at a locus of IRF4 [67]. In addition, a study by Leticia [68] et al. found that IRF4 expression was upregulated in TNF superfamily member 12 (TWEAK)-induced podocytes and that tacrolimus inhibited the upregulation of IRF4, a process achieved by inhibiting TWEAK/fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (Fn14) activation and reducing chemokine secretion by immune cells [69]. Therefore, the role of IRF4 in the TWEAK/Fn14 signaling pathway in MN deserves further discussion.

The inflammatory response induced by TWEAK is mediated by NF-κB activation [70]. The TWEAK/Fn14 signaling pathway promotes fibrosis and inflammation [71]. Drugs (such as Triptolide, Zhen-wu-tang, and Panax notoginseng) for the treatment of MN ameliorate podocyte injury and proteinuria in MN mice by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [72–74]. Briefly, the main constituents of Panax notoginseng consist of Astragalus and Stahlianthus involucratus (King ex Bak.) Craib ex Loesener, while the primary components of Zhen-wu-tang comprise Radix Aconiti Lateralis Preparata, Poria cocos (Schw.) Wolf., Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz., and others [72,73]. Additionally, Triptolide, an essential bioactive compound, is derived from the medicinal plant Tripterygium wilfordii Hook. F [75]. Importantly, IRF4 and NF-κB exhibit novel positive feedback loops, with IRF4 being a target of NF-κB and NF-κB capable of activating IRF4 gene expression [76,77]. Furthermore, IRF4 interacts with tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and receptor-interacting serine-threonine kinase (RICK), leading to the inhibition of TRAF6 and RICK ubiquitination, consequently downregulating NF-κB expression [78,79]. Thus, the mutual regulatory relationship between IRF4 and NF-κB may unravel the pathogenesis of MN.

In conclusion, IRF4 is a newly identified risk site in MN. Tacrolimus reduces IRF4 expression by blocking the activation of TWEAK/Fn14, thereby alleviating MN. NF-κB activation plays a key role in the inflammatory response induced by TWEAK, and IRF4 is a target site for NF-κB. Therefore, we hypothesized that TWEAK/Fn14 may mediate IRF4 by activating NF-κB, leading to the pathogenesis of MN.

IRF4 and LN

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) can damage several organ systems, a systemic autoimmune disease [80]. Various heterogeneous elements, such as genetic factors, epigenetic modifications, immune regulation, hormones, and the environment, contribute to the development of SLE [80–82]. Approximately 50% of patients diagnosed with SLE exhibit kidney damage. Lupus nephritis (LN) has been identified as a significant contributor to mortality in patients with SLE [83]. Hence, studying the mechanism of LN development is essential to slow the disease progression of LN and thus reduce mortality. Recently, IRF4 was found to be involved in the pathogenesis of LN, and the regulatory relationship of IRF4 in LN is worth exploring.

Maciej-Leh [84] found that IRF4 deficiency seriously damaged the maturation and subsequent autoantibody production of plasma cells [85] and the maturation of Th1 and Th17 effector T-cells. Additionally, IRF4 deficiency reduced the mRNA expression levels of the CCL2, CCL5, CXCL2, and CXCL10, preventing the evolution of lupus-like immune complex glomerulonephritis. Moreover, there was a significant association between gene polymorphisms and susceptibility to LN. Liu [86] et al. investigated the association between IRF4 and genetic susceptibility to LN in a Chinese population. In non-LN patients, the genotype frequencies of the A and G alleles of IRF4 were 71.7% and 28.3%, respectively. In the LN group, the A and G allele frequencies of IRF4 were 69.7% and 30.3%, respectively. There was no significant difference in the genotype frequency of IRF4 gene variants between the non-LN and LN groups. Thus, IRF4 was not strongly correlated with LN in the Chinese population.

Overall, IRF4 may attenuate the autoimmune response to LN and reduce the progression of glomerulonephritis by reducing the maturation of plasma cells, Th1 and Th17 cells, and the secretion of chemokines [84,85], yet IRF4 is not associated with genetic susceptibility to LN [86].

IRF4 and kidney allograft rejection

Kidney transplantation is an ideal therapy for patients with ESKD and has been shown to significantly improve patient prognosis and early survival [87,88]. However, rejection remains a significant threat and the primary independent risk factor for the long-term survival of transplanted kidneys [89,90]. Rejection reactions are categorized into four types in a clinical context based on their mechanism of occurrence, pathological changes, onset time, and clinical characteristics. These types include hyperacute rejection (HAR), acute accelerated rejection (AAR), acute rejection (AR), and chronic rejection (CR) [91]. Rejection can also be classified into T-cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) and antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR), based on molecular and cellular processes [92]. In particular, ABMR is the most severe manifestation of donor-specific antibody pathogenicity [93]. A recent study has revealed that 17% of graft loss occurring within the first year following a kidney transplant can be attributed to AR [94]. Thus, exploring new therapeutic options to combat AR effectively is imperative. IRF4 has been found to play an essential role in biological processes such as the immune response, cytokine signaling, and molecular mechanisms of cell proliferation and regulation [5,95,96]. Therefore, the role of IRF4 in AR needs to be further explored.

IRF4 appears to promote the development of rejection after allogeneic transplantation, mainly through the promotion of immune cell differentiation. On the one hand, IRF4 expression was significantly increased in the circulating T-follicular helper (TFH) cells of ABMR patients [97] and promoted T-cell differentiation to TFH cells [98]. Excitingly, bortezomib inhibited the proliferation and differentiation of TFH cells through the miR-15b/IRF4 axis, thereby improving ABMR [99]. In addition, increased IRF4 expression was observed in activated memory (AM) B-cells in ABMR patients [100], promoting the differentiation of AM cells to plasma cells. On the other hand, IRF4 expression was found to be significantly increased in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of TCMR patients and was accompanied by a decrease in the expression of immunomodulatory proteins such as programmed death 1 (PD-1) and Helios in kidney transplants [101]. IRF4 has an impact on T-cell differentiation and function, making it possible to apply IRF4 as a target for TCMR.

The current first-line treatment regimen for acute TCMR is intravenous glucocorticoid infusion, and treatment with anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) or antilymphocyte globulin (ALG) is required for hormone-refractory TCMR and severe acute TCMR [102,103]. The main goal of treating acute ABMR is to remove antibodies and inhibit their reproduction. Treatment options include (1) the use of plasma replacement and immunoadsorption; (2) the blocking or delaying of the effects of antibody-mediated primary and secondary tissue damage, such as through high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG); and (3) the continuous production of antibodies in vivo can be inhibited or removed with drugs such as CD20 monoclonal antibodies (rituximab), proteasome inhibitor bortezomib, and anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab) [104]. As seen above, the downregulation of IRF4 inhibits the differentiation of T-cells, plasma cells, and TFH cells. However, no drugs directly inhibit IRF4, and it is still unclear whether other drugs for the treatment of AR inhibit IRF4. Therefore, finding and developing drugs targeting IRF4 may be a new direction for treating TCMR and ABMR.

In conclusion, IRF4 is detrimental to ABMR and TCMR, and IRF4 may promote the differentiation of TFH cells, plasma cells, and T-cells that contribute to the development of ABMR and TCMR. It has been demonstrated that bortezomib alleviates ABMR by downregulating IRF4, which increases the confidence that IRF4 can serve as a therapeutic target for ABMR and TCMR. Although the inhibition of IRF4 may protect against allograft rejection, further studies are needed to find the best way to inhibit this factor without interfering with immune cells’ normal development and function.

Conclusion and prospects

In this paper, we have reviewed the role of IRF4 in the AKI-CKD transition, GDs, and kidney allograft rejection. In the AKI-CKD transition section, deficiency of IRF4 prevents the AKI-CKD transition by inhibiting macrophage-to-fibroblast transformation, hindering macrophage M1-M2 polarization, and reducing neutrophil inward flow. However, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the effects of IRF4 deficiency on these three processes have yet to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, it is important to note that the above data were derived from studies conducted on rodent models. Thus, further investigation is needed to determine how these findings can be applied to human AKI. In the GDs section, IRF4 has been identified as a newly discovered susceptibility locus for IgAN and MN, but the specific pathogenesis of IRF4 in IgAN and MN is unclear. Although the roles of IRF4 in IgAN and MN are hypothesized in this review, in vitro and in vivo, experiments are still needed to validate these hypothesized roles. In addition, IRF4 deficiency slows the progression of LN by attenuating the autoimmune response and the development of glomerulonephritis. Therefore, drug development targeting IRF4 is urgently needed for to treat LN. Finally, IRF4 is detrimental to ABMR and TCMR. Bortezomib has now been found to improve ABMR by inhibiting IRF4. The inhibition of IRF4 may be a target for the future treatment of ABMR and TCMR.

Drug development targeting IRF4 may warrant further investigation as a potential area of focus for future research, particularly concerning kidney disorders processes. For instance, the activation of IRF4 by naringenin has been found to facilitate M2 macrophage polarization [105], while the inhibition of IRF4’s impact on M2 macrophage polarization has been observed with the administration of YIV-906 [106] and growth arrest factor-5 (GAS5) [107]. However, the applicability of these drugs and RNA molecules in treating kidney disorders necessitates additional development. Furthermore, gene therapy targeting IRF4 may hold promise for managing GDs.
