Effectiveness of transthoracic minimally invasive device closure for perimembranous ventricular septal defects in proximity to the aortic valve
Authors: Guidong Liu, Feng Zhang, Xiaobi Huang, Wenqing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransthoracic minimally invasive device closure (TDC) via the transthoracic approach can be used to treat ventricular septal defect (VSD). However, studies investigating transthoracic closure in pediatric patients with perimembranous ventricular septal defects (PmVSDs) located close to the aortic valve and without preoperative aortic regurgitation (AR) are still lacking. The aim of this study was to compare the efficacy and safety of TDC and conventional surgical repair (CSR) for PmVSDs measuring ≤2 mm from the aortic valve in pediatric patients without preexisting AR.MethodsA retrospective cohort study involving 119 pediatric patients treated between March 2017 and April 2024, including 51 who underwent TDC and 68 who received CSR, was conducted. Baseline characteristics were balanced using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Primary outcomes included operation time, duration of ventilator support, chest tube drainage volume, postoperative hospital length of stay, and complications at 6 months postprocedure.ResultsThe TDC group had significantly shorter operating times (70.95 vs. 146.59 minutes), shorter ventilation duration (97.12 vs. 219.98 minutes), less blood loss (30.54 vs. 115.15 mL), and shorter postoperative hospital stays (4.45 vs. 6.13 days; all P<0.001). The residual shunt (0% vs. 2.9%) and atrioventricular block (AVB) (0% vs. 5.9%) rates were lower in the TDC group, whereas new-onset mild valvular regurgitation occurred in 3.9% of TDC patients compared with 0% in the CSR group. Overall morbidity was 3.9% in the TDC group vs. 8.8% in the CSR group (P=0.46). No mortality or late reinterventions were observed during follow-up.ConclusionsTransthoracic device closure under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance is a safe and effective alternative to CSR for selected cases of PmVSDs adjacent to the aortic valve, resulting in faster recovery and favorable short-term outcomes.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Transthoracic minimally invasive device closure (TDC) for pediatric perimembranous ventricular septal defects (PmVSDs) ≤2 mm from the aortic valve [no preexisting aortic regurgitation (AR)] is safer and more effective than conventional surgical repair (CSR): shorter operation time (68 vs. 147 min), shorter ventilation duration (1.6 vs. 4.1 hours), less blood loss, shorter hospital stay (4.4 vs. 6.2 days), lower residual shunt (0% vs. 2.9%) and atrioventricular block (0% vs. 5.9%), similar overall morbidity (3.9% vs. 8.8%), and no mortality or late reinterventions. TDC was associated with 3.9% new mild valvular regurgitation (CSR: 0%).

What is known and what is new?

• Ventricular septal defects are common congenital heart defects; PmVSDs near the aortic valve increase aortic valve prolapse/AR risk; CSR is a traditional therapy; and TDC is a minimally invasive option.

• TDC under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance is a safe alternative for selected pediatric PmVSDs (≤2 mm from the aortic valve, no AR), with faster recovery and favorable short-term outcomes.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• TDC should be prioritized for eligible pediatric patients. Clinicians may adopt TEE-guided TDC to improve recovery. Future studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up are needed to confirm the durability of these findings. Guidelines should be updated to include TDC as a preferred option for such cases.

Introduction

Minimally invasive transthoracic device closure performed under echocardiographic guidance is a method used to treat ventricular septal defects (VSDs) (1). Approximately 1.5% to 10.6% of patients with perimembranous ventricular septal defects (PmVSDs) have aortic valve prolapse (AVP) or aortic regurgitation (AR) (2). The proximity of the PmVSD to the aortic valve increases the likelihood of AVP/AR, and AR develops or worsens more frequently after surgery in patients with PmVSDs with AVP and AR than in those without AVP (3). The Guidelines for Interventional Treatment of Common Congenital Heart Disease in Children [2025] note that a VSD combined with mild to moderate AVP, in the absence of moderate or severe aortic valve regurgitation, constitutes a relative indication for interventional procedures (4).

In this study, we aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of transthoracic minimally invasive device closure (TDC) and conventional surgical repair (CSR) for identifying PmVSDs ≤2 mm from the aortic valve in pediatric patients without preexisting AR.

Background

VSDs are common congenital heart defects; approximately 160,000 new cases are reported in China every year, and the prevalence of VSD is estimated to be 20% (5).

Methods

Clinical data

The clinical data of 803 patients with simple PmVSDs admitted to Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital between March 2017 and April 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. One hundred nineteen patients with PmVSDs within 2 mm of the aortic valve underwent surgical treatment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital (No. EYLL-2025-084). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study.

The inclusion criteria were patients aged 1 to 11 years with a confirmed diagnosis of constricted VSD on the basis of preoperative chest X-ray, 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), and transthoracic echocardiography. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed the VSD as a PmVSD, with the defect located less than 2 mm from the upper margin of the aortic valve. No other malformations that required concurrent treatment were present, and no aortic valve regurgitation was observed (Figure 1A).

Echocardiographic evaluation and procedural steps of VSD occlusion. Echocardiography revealed that the distance between the upper margin of the VSD and the root of the aortic valve was less than 2 mm (A). An eccentric VSD occluder was used. A 2–3-cm incision was made between the 4th and 5th costal spaces on the right side of the sternum (B). “S”-shaped metal catheter (C). The tip of the S-shaped hollow metal catheter was advanced into the left ventricle via the right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, and VSD (D). The symmetrical occluder was delivered to the VSD site via the transright atrial approach using the delivery sheath (E). The eccentric occluder was delivered to the VSD site via the trans-right atrial approach using the delivery sheath (F). A 3–4-cm incision was made in the substernal segment to redirect the flow through the shunt in the VSD toward the apex of the heart (G). After implantation of the occluder, no residual shunt or AR was observed in the interventricular septum. (H) A symmetrical VSD occluder was used (I). The exclusion criteria were a VSD more than 2 mm from the aortic valve, other intracardiac malformations requiring concurrent surgical treatment, and a systolic pulmonary artery pressure >40 mmHg on transthoracic echocardiography.

In this study, data from patients with PmVSDs within 2 mm of the aortic valve treated at our hospital (Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital) in recent years were obtained. Eligible patients were treated according to the wishes of their guardians after being informed in detail about the risks of surgery and possible complications. The patients were divided into two groups—those who received CSR (n=68) and those who received TDC (n=51)—to evaluate the success rate and incidence of complications of TDC therapy relative to CSR.

Surgical methods

All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia in the supine position, and mechanically assisted ventilation was provided via oral intubation.

CSR group

Traditional repair under cardiopulmonary bypass was performed in the CSR group. The pericardium was suspended through the median sternal incision. After the establishment of extracorporeal circulation, the ascending aorta was cross-clamped, and myocardial protective fluid was injected into the aortic root. The anal temperature was gradually reduced to 28–31 ℃. The VSD was repaired by a continuous patch suturing trans-right atrial approach. One pericardial drainage tube and one mediastinal drainage tube were inserted after the operation.

TDC group

In the TDC group, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) (Philips 5C ultrasonic diagnostic equipment, Washington State, USA; the probe was a s7-3t esophageal probe with a frequency of 3–7 MHz) was performed before the operation. This step was repeated to further clarify the intracardiac structure and determine the size of the defect, the direction of the shunt bundle blood flow, the size and number of the diverging mouth, the distance between the upper margin of the defect and the aortic valve, and the presence of prolapse or regurgitation of the aortic valve. If the TEE results suggested that TDC was contraindicated (e.g., for moderate-to-severe AR or nonperimembranous VSDs), CSR of the VSD was performed. Two paths were chosen according to the direction of the VSD shunt beam flow. If the shunt flow through the VSD was directed away from the apex of the heart, a 2–3 cm parasternal incision was made between the 4th and 5th costal spaces on the right side of the sternum (Figure 1B), the pericardium was incised, puncture was performed through the right atrium near the atrioventricular groove, and a purse-string suture was placed. Using an “S”-shaped metal catheter with a hollow (lumen-containing) structure (Figure 1C), the catheter tip was introduced into the left ventricle under TEE guidance through the right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, and VSD (Figure 1D). The guide wire was placed through the metal catheter, the metal catheter was replaced with the occluder delivery sheath tube, and the occlusive device was placed at the defect site of the VSD (Figure 1E,1F). The occlusive device was released after confirming that any residual shunt, new valve reflux or intracardiac conduction block was absent. A drainage tube was not placed postoperatively. If the shunt flow through the VSD was toward the apex of the heart, a surgical skin incision was created in the lower part of the sternum. A 3–4 cm incision was made on the skin of the lower sternum, and the puncture point was selected on the surface of the right ventricle. The occluder was implanted and released via the right ventricular surface using the VSD route, and no residual shunt, new valve regurgitant or conduction block was detected. A pericardial drainage tube was inserted after surgery (Figure 1G). A total of 14 children underwent device closure through a lower sternal incision.

On the basis of the VSD diameter measured via TEE, a symmetrical or eccentric occluder was selected by adding 2 mm. A symmetrical occluder (VSD Occlude, Shanghai Shape Memory Alloy Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was preferred in cases without AVP; this occluder is made of nickel-titanium memory alloy wire woven with a symmetrical double disc sheet structure. An eccentric occluder was preferred in patients with AVP if a new AR occurred after the implantation of the occluder (Figure 1H,1I). This occluder is made of nickel-titanium memory alloy wire woven, with a double-disc sheet structure, a single-sided design of the left ventricular surface, zero upper edge to leave space for the aortic valve, and a lower edge is inlaid with metal raised marks (“Mark” marks). The occluder delivery system was equipped with a guide wire, push rod, and sheath tube. Surgical repair of the VSD was performed if the AR did not improve.

After the operation, patients in both groups were transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) while still intubated. After their condition stabilized, they were transferred from the CICU to the general ward for follow-up treatment. The surgery was successful for all the patients in both groups.

Six months after the surgery, the patient visited our hospital’s outpatient department for a follow-up echocardiogram and ECG.

Recording the transthoracic echocardiography findings of both groups in a quiet state before surgery and at 6 months postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The patients were initially stratified into two groups according to the procedure type: CSR and TDC. The study period was divided into two intervals: 2017–2020 and 2021–2024.

To address potential confounding due to nonrandom treatment assignment, we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) to balance baseline characteristics between groups. The propensity score for receiving TDC (vs. CSR) was estimated using a logistic regression model that included the following covariates: sex, age, weight, defect diameter, shunt diameter, subaortic distance, preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (preLVEF), preoperative left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (preLVDD), and treatment year. The adequacy of balance achieved by weighting was assessed by comparing the standardized mean differences (SMDs) of all covariates before and after weighting, with a threshold of <0.1 indicating satisfactory balance.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the CSR and TDC populations. After IPW adjustment, categorical variables are summarized as counts and weighted percentages, and all continuous variables are reported as weighted means with standard deviations. Our primary analysis used inverse probability weighted linear regression, which does not require normality assumptions for valid inference in large samples. Between-group comparisons after weighting were performed using weighted linear regression for continuous outcomes, with the survey analysis procedures accounting for the inverse probability weights. The surgical outcomes in both groups are presented as point estimates of the treatment effect along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

P values were two-tailed and were not adjusted for multiplicity. Significance was defined as P<0.05. No data were missing.

Discussion

Comparison of outcomes between groups

Age was similar between the groups. Compared with those in the CSR group, the operation time, duration of ventilator support, postoperative hospital length of stay, and chest tube drainage volume were lower in the TDC group. The complication rates were similar between the groups. New-onset valve regurgitation was similar between the groups. Both pediatric patient groups had a good prognosis.

Compared with the CSR group, the TDC group presented significantly shorter operation times, less blood loss, less ventilator support duration, and shorter postoperative hospital stays. In the TDC group, one patient required reoperation because of excessive drainage fluid caused by retrosternal bleeding. Additionally, new mild AR and tricuspid regurgitation occurred postoperatively in one patient each. Both groups had small residual shunts, but echocardiography revealed that they had resolved by 6 months postsurgery. Four patients in the CSR group had new complete right bundle branch blocks, whereas no new ECG abnormalities were found in the TDC group.

After regression analysis with the IPTW treatment, the following results were observed:

Postoperative shunt and postoperative AVB were not observed in any patients in the device closure group, whereas numerous cases occurred in the CSR group. These results suggest that device closure may serve as a strong protective factor against these complications.

New-onset AR, however, was absent in the CSR group but was observed in two patients in the device closure group. These findings highlight a potential safety signal and underscore the need to further evaluate the possible relationship between device closure and new-onset AR.

During the procedure, substantial sheath retraction may entangle the surrounding tricuspid chordae tendineae with the guide wire on rotation, causing chordal injury and varying valvular regurgitation. For eccentric occluders, intraoperative TEE must accurately visualize the long axis of the ventricular septum at the largest cross-section of the defect. This criterion ensures that the occluder’s marker faces the cardiac apex and that the prolapsed aortic valve leaflet lies as much as possible on the occluder’s left ventricular surface to prevent abrasion.

Challenges and technical considerations.

Although minimally invasive VSD device closure achieves reliable results in children with AVP, challenges still exist (6). VSDs combined with noncoronary valve prolapse may be risk factors for AR (7). It was previously believed that the treatment of PmVSDs with AVP device closure was difficult, and surgical repair was considered the best treatment.

Owing to gradual developments in TDC of VSDs under the guidance of TEE, minimally invasive TDC of PmVSDs within 2 mm of the aortic valve has been performed at our hospital. TDC under the guidance of TEE is suitable for all types of VSDs, including those classified as perimembranous, and it is not associated with strict peripheral vascular requirements. It is suitable for young children with low body weights, is associated with easier establishment of tracks, and is convenient to perform (8).

This study was a retrospective analysis. Surgical repair has long been the main treatment for such VSDs. As surgeons gain experience with TDC, attempts have been made to close VSDs with smaller edge distances, and good therapeutic results have been achieved.

Complications and their management

During the operation, if the sheath tube must be retracted considerably, the tricuspid chordae tendineae around the defect may become tangled on the cable when the latter is rotated, resulting in damage to the chordae tendineae and varying degrees of regurgitation (9). When the VSD is close to the aortic valve, an ADO-II occluder or eccentric occluder can be used. Symmetrical occluders are suitable for treating VSDs with aneurysms (10,11). To place the eccentric occluder, the long axis of the ventricular septum on the largest section of the defect needs to be accurately displayed to ensure that the marker point of the occluder is oriented in the apical direction and that the prolapsed aortic valve leaflet is placed on the left ventricular surface of the occluder as much as possible to prevent wear of the aortic valve leaflet. Before the device is released, TEE is necessary to repeatedly observe the defect and aortic valve and identify any residual shunt at the ventricular level and new-onset AR. In the absence of obvious aortic valve regurgitation before the operation, the small occluder may need to be replaced after initial placement. If significant reflux persists, closure treatment should be abandoned, and surgical repair should be performed. In a study of the TDC of subarterial VSDs, no cases of AR were reported at the 3- or 5-year follow-up (12). A residual shunt is a common complication after treatment for PmVSDs. After surgical repair, residual shunts are prone to occur at the upper and lower edges of the patch, which are not easily exposed. The occurrence of residual shunts after interventional closure is mostly related to an occluder that is either too small or too large, and the morphology of the VSD is also related to the formation of a shunt (13).

Four patients in the CSR group in this study had new complete right bundle branch blocks, whereas no new ECG abnormalities were found in the TDC group. Surgical treatment involves the use of a patch to repair the defect. If an inappropriate suture method, traction device, or clamp is used to repair a right VSD, the risk of a right bundle branch block is greater than that of a left bundle branch block (14). Although new-onset arrhythmia did not occur in the TDC group in this study, some studies revealed that young age, low weight, proximity of the defect to the atrioventricular valve and aortic valve, presence of membranous aneurysms, occluder type, and intraoperative technique are key factors influencing the occurrence of arrhythmia after closure (15). In this study, compared with percutaneous defect closure, TDC features a shorter surgical path, eliminates the need for guidewire exchange maneuvers inside the heart, and reduces injury to the tissues surrounding the VSD, thereby lowering the incidence of arrhythmias.

Recommendations for future studies and guidelines

With the continuous maturity of the technique of transthoracic closure and the wide promotion of its clinical application, the existing guidelines for the treatment of heart disease have gradually developed. These guidelines help doctors consider the specific situation of patients more comprehensively and scientifically for formulating more reasonable and effective treatment plans.

Compared with traditional surgery, minimally invasive surgical methods reduce patient pain and recovery time, enabling patients to return to normal life and work more quickly. This approach will also improve patient satisfaction and compliance with treatment, further promoting the improvement and development of guidelines for the treatment of heart disease. We hope to establish standard guidelines in the future.

Despite its advantages, transcatheter closure still has certain limitations. First, the procedure demands precise anatomical positioning and sizing of the defect. For instance, large VSDs or those with suboptimal anatomical locations may compromise the function of the aortic or atrioventricular valves following occluder placement, potentially causing valve regurgitation. Second, the surgical technique is highly complex and must be guided by advanced imaging modalities such as TEE. The occluder device adopted in this study is made of nickel-titanium alloy, which has the risk of metal ion leaching into the blood and may induce adverse reactions such as human allergies. If ceramic occluder devices or absorbable material occluder devices are used in the future, this problem is expected to be avoided. Even minor procedural errors can lead to occluder displacement, dislodgement, or residual shunting. Moreover, there remains a risk of complications, including conduction disturbances, arrhythmias, and thrombus formation.

Given the retrospective design of this study, unmeasured confounding could not be fully eliminated despite IPTW adjustment. Additionally, as a single-center investigation, the findings have limited generalizability, with observed outcomes potentially influenced by institutional expertise and local clinical practices—an effect that is particularly pronounced for technically complex procedures such as TDC. The small sample size further compromised the statistical power, whereas the short follow-up duration precluded definitive conclusions regarding long-term efficacy.

Conclusions

In the TDC group, all the children were discharged uneventfully, with no severe complications and no deaths. Under echocardiographic guidance, TDC of PmVSDs within 2 mm of the aortic valve without AR is a safe and feasible procedure for pediatric patients with PmVSDs.
