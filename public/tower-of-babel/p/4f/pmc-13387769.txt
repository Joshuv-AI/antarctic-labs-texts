Can We Identify Severe Dysbiosis at the Bedside?
Authors: Umair Ansari, Jihad Mallat
Published: 2026

The microbial core shared by most humans is integral to host homeostasis, contributing to nutrient metabolism, immune system development, vitamin biosynthesis, and colonization resistance against pathogenic microorganisms (1). However, this balanced ecosystem is profoundly disrupted in patients admitted to the ICU (1, 2). Dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial richness and evenness together with pathogen overgrowth, leads to loss of colonization resistance (3) and creates an ecological niche that favors the proliferation of pathogenic and often multidrug-resistant organisms, including vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) (2, 4–6). Increasing evidence suggests that dysbiosis is not merely a consequence of critical illness but may also contribute to adverse clinical outcomes. Accordingly, reduced alpha diversity has been associated with prolonged hospitalization, secondary infections, organ failure, and increased mortality, whereas higher microbial diversity is associated with more favorable outcomes and is generally considered a marker of a more resilient microbial ecosystem (7–9). Together, these observations provide a strong biological rationale for microbiome-targeted therapies in critically ill patients.

Despite this compelling rationale, translating microbiome-targeted interventions—including probiotics, prebiotics (e.g., inulin), fecal microbiota transplantation, and emerging live biotherapeutic products—into meaningful clinical benefit has proven challenging. These therapies aim to restore microbial diversity, promote beneficial microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, and reestablish colonization resistance. Nevertheless, randomized clinical trials have generally yielded inconsistent or disappointing results (10–13), likely reflecting the combined effects of broad-spectrum antibiotics, the complexity of host-microbiome interactions, and the marked heterogeneity of ICU populations. An important, and potentially modifiable, limitation is imprecise patient selection. Because patients enter the ICU with widely varying degrees of microbiome disruption, nontargeted enrollment inevitably includes individuals with relatively preserved microbial diversity who are unlikely to benefit from microbiome restoration, thereby diluting any treatment effect. Conversely, patients with severe dysbiosis may derive the greatest benefit but remain difficult to identify rapidly without specialized microbiome sequencing, which is not routinely available in clinical practice. These considerations underscore the need for practical bedside strategies to identify patients with low baseline alpha diversity who are most likely to benefit from microbiome-targeted interventions.

In this issue of Critical Care Explorations, Ma et al (14) address this important clinical challenge through a secondary analysis of a previously published randomized, placebo-controlled trial of prebiotic inulin (10) involving 90 patients with sepsis admitted to a medical ICU. Rather than relying on real-time microbiome sequencing, the investigators sought to identify readily available clinical and microbiological variables that could predict baseline gut microbial alpha diversity at ICU admission. Rectal swabs obtained within 24 hours of admission underwent both 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing and microbiological culture, and patients were stratified into tertiles of alpha diversity, with median Shannon indices of 1.17, 2.79, and 3.52 in the low-, intermediate-, and high-diversity groups, respectively. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression identified three independent predictors of lower alpha diversity: location before ICU admission, a culture-proven infection within the preceding year, and rectal colonization with VRE at ICU admission. Together, these variables formed a simple three-factor prediction model, representing one of the first attempts to translate complex microbiome profiling into a pragmatic bedside framework for identifying critically ill patients with severe dysbiosis.

At first glance, the three predictors identified by the authors appear unsurprising. Previous exposure to healthcare, prior infections, antibiotic use, and colonization with multidrug-resistant organisms have all been associated with disruption of the gut microbiome. The originality of the study by Ma et al (14), however, lies not in the predictors themselves but in the outcome being predicted. Rather than identifying patients at risk for a specific pathogen, the investigators used familiar bedside variables to infer an ecological property of the gut microbiome—baseline alpha diversity. This represents an important conceptual shift, moving microbiome assessment from specialized sequencing laboratories toward a clinically applicable bedside assessment of microbiome vulnerability.

Beyond its conceptual novelty, the model is appealing because of its simplicity. Two of its three predictors—location before ICU admission and a history of culture-proven infection during the preceding year—can be obtained immediately from the medical record without additional testing. The third predictor, rectal colonization with VRE, is determined using a relatively inexpensive surveillance culture that is already routinely performed in many institutions and is available far more rapidly than microbiome sequencing. Notably, before multivariable adjustment, patients exposed to three or more antibiotic classes in the preceding three months had significantly lower alpha diversity than those exposed to fewer antibiotic classes. Although antibiotic exposure was not retained in the final model because of collinearity with prior healthcare exposure and culture-proven infection, these findings support the biological plausibility of the proposed predictors and suggest that prior culture-proven infection may serve as a pragmatic surrogate for cumulative antimicrobial exposure.

The study also has several notable methodological strengths. The analysis was based on data from a prospective randomized clinical trial with standardized collection of rectal swabs within 24 hours of ICU admission and quantitative 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The authors systematically evaluated a broad range of demographic, clinical, microbiological, and medication-related variables before deriving a parsimonious three-variable model. Nevertheless, as with any exploratory prediction model, several methodological and conceptual issues deserve careful consideration before this approach can be translated into routine clinical practice.

Despite its conceptual appeal, several limitations should temper enthusiasm for immediate clinical implementation. First, this was a secondary analysis of a single-center randomized trial involving only 90 patients with sepsis admitted to a medical ICU. The prediction model was both derived and evaluated within the same dataset, without internal resampling, cross-validation, or external validation, increasing the risk of overfitting and optimistic estimates of model performance. Furthermore, conventional measures of predictive performance, such as discrimination and calibration, were not reported, making it difficult to judge the model’s clinical utility.

Second, the definition of the outcome warrants careful consideration. The model relied on cohort-specific tertiles of the Shannon index rather than an externally validated biological threshold for severe dysbiosis. Although converting a continuous measure into categories can simplify model development and interpretation, it produces study-specific cutoffs, reduces statistical power, and may limit generalizability to other ICU populations. The authors also did not demonstrate that the lowest tertile corresponded to a clinically meaningful threshold associated with adverse outcomes. Therefore, the model identifies patients in the lowest third of this cohort, rather than those meeting a validated definition of severe dysbiosis.

The association between antibiotic exposure and microbial diversity also appears more nuanced than the final model indicates. Although exposure to three or more antibiotic classes was linked to lower alpha diversity, other exposure measures—including total antibiotic days, unique antibiotic days, and time since the most recent antibiotic course—were not significantly associated with the Shannon index when analyzed as a continuous variable. These findings suggest that antimicrobial exposure and microbiome depletion may not be fully captured by a single metric and warrant further study.

Finally, the model’s generalizability is uncertain. Because the study included only patients with sepsis who were eligible for a prebiotic trial, its findings may not apply to broader ICU populations. The source of sepsis was not reported, despite the possibility that different infection syndromes, especially intra-abdominal infections, may affect the intestinal microbiome in distinct ways. Furthermore, prior culture-proven infection was used as a practical surrogate for cumulative antimicrobial exposure, but this information may be unavailable for patients transferred from other institutions with incomplete microbiological records. Prospective multicenter validation in diverse ICU populations is therefore needed before the model can be used routinely in clinical practice or research.

The optimal microbiome metric for guiding therapeutic decisions remains uncertain. Alpha diversity is appealing because it is simple, widely used, and combines species richness and evenness into a single measure. However, it does not capture microbial identity or function, and patients with similar Shannon indices may have substantially different microbial communities that affect colonization resistance and host physiology. Future prediction models should therefore move beyond aggregate diversity measures by incorporating taxonomic composition, functional profiles, microbial metabolites, or other multiomic markers that better reflect microbiome function. Studies should also determine whether identifying severe dysbiosis improves the targeting of microbiome-directed therapies and leads to better clinical outcomes. Only then can microbiome risk stratification become a meaningful part of precision critical care.

In conclusion, the study by Ma et al (14) represents an important exploratory step toward bedside assessment of gut microbiome vulnerability in critically ill patients. Although the proposed model is not yet ready for clinical implementation, it provides a pragmatic framework for future investigation and validation. Ultimately, the success of this approach will not be judged by its ability to predict low alpha diversity, but by whether it enables more effective microbiome-targeted therapies and improves patient outcomes.
