Molecular mechanisms linking adipose tissue-derived small extracellular vesicles/exosomes to the development or amelioration of obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes-related complications
Authors: Linfeng Chen, Fatemeh Amraee, Sahar Sadegh-Nejadi, Mostafa Saberian, Seyed Arsalan Ghahari, Xiaolei Miao, Giuseppe Lisco, Reza Afrisham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by impaired glucose storage and utilization in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is particularly prevalent among individuals with obesity, as excess weight fosters significant health complications, including inflammation and insulin resistance, ultimately contributing to the development of T2DM. It is believed that the crosstalk between adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and the endocrine pancreas underlies the glucose dysmetabolism associated with excess weight. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) can play a role in mediating intercellular crosstalk and could be involved in the pathogenesis of T2DM and related complications in individuals with obesity. Exosomes/small EVs (sEVs) derived from adipose tissue may contribute to either the progression or management of metabolic syndromes, primarily through their effects on insulin resistance. Hence, the current narrative review was designed to evaluate the effect of adipose-derived sEVs in insulin resistance and its related complications. These sEVs have been isolated from white adipose tissue (WAT), brown adipose tissue (BAT), adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs), as well as from plasma and other tissues of obese individuals and animal models. The present review showed that sEVs derived from adipose tissue and/or obese individuals and animal models may contribute significantly to the onset and enhancement of insulin resistance. However, sEVs extracted from ADSCs of healthy adipose tissue could enhance insulin sensitivity via their bioactive components (e.g., microRNAs). Additional complementary studies are required to validate the dual role of adipose-derived sEVs on the amelioration or progression of insulin resistance-related complications.Keywords: Adipose-derived exosomes, Obesity, Diabetes, Insulin resistance, Small extracellular vesicles

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by marked insulin resistance and gradual pancreatic beta-cell failure, resulting in impaired glucose storage and utilization in peripheral tissues [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is the most prevalent form of diabetes and is basically characterized by the classic dysfunctional triad of insulin resistance, reactive hyperinsulinemia, and hyperglycemia [1–3]. T2DM is more common in obese than normal weight individuals [4]. Previous research suggests that the risk of developing T2DM increases with excess weight severity [5, 6]. Chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum stress, aging, and lipodystrophy can contribute to obesity-related insulin resistance and foster T2DM onset [7]. Adipose tissue expansion is associated with adipocyte hypoxia, necrosis, and apoptosis, which collectively trigger inflammatory activation [8]. M1-polarized macrophages located or migrated in the adipose tissue produce pro-inflammatory cytokines [9] and significantly impair insulin signaling pathways. The adipose tissue also has a function in releasing extracellular vesicles (EVs) that regulate intra-cellular communication [10].

Exosomes, one of the most important types of EVs, are classified as small EVs (sEVs) [11, 12]. The molecular contents of these vesicles include proteins, DNA, RNA, mRNA, microRNAs (miRNAs), and lipids [11, 13]. sEVs can be obtained from various cell types, including stem cells, hepatocytes, and adipocytes [14]. sEVs derived from adipose tissue regulate glucose metabolism by mediating inter-organ crosstalk [15]. These influence the local environment and the function of distant organs such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and pancreas; thereby, impairing glucose metabolism [16]. Notably, the composition and biological activity of adipose-derived sEVs are significantly altered in obesity, highlighting their dual role as both contributors to metabolic dysfunction and potential therapeutic targets [17]. There is growing interest in the role of sEVs in mediating insulin resistance, as a better understanding of involved molecular mechanisms is essential for the future development of target therapies. Therefore, this narrative review was designed to evaluate the molecular mechanisms by which sEVs/exosomes derived from adipose tissue, including white adipose tissue (WAT), brown adipose tissue (BAT), adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs), and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells, as well as those originating from plasma and other tissues in obese individuals and animal models, contribute to the development or mitigation of obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes-related complications.

Extracellular vesicles

EVs are lipid bilayer-bound vesicles that appear to be secreted by various cell types into the extracellular environment. A lipid bilayer membrane covers the enclosed contents during intercellular transport. Once thought to be cellular debris, EVs are now recognized as essential participants in intercellular communication [18]. It has become increasingly clear that EVs represent a diverse and heterogeneous population with distinct characteristics and functions. However, their classification is complex and challenging due to their overlapping characteristics and the continual discovery of new subtypes [19]. Currently, there are no purification methods that allow quantitative separation of different EV subclasses, which makes it difficult to assign specific functional properties to each subtype [20]. Despite these challenges, efforts have been spent to establish standard guidelines and nomenclature to facilitate research and discussion in this field [19].

In general, EVs are classified into three main biogenesis-based categories: exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies [18]. According to the MISEV2023 guidelines, the term ‘exosome’ refers to EVs from internal compartments of the cell that are released via the multivesicular body (MVB), while the term ‘ectosome’ (a.k.a., microvesicle, microparticle) refers to EVs from the cell surface. Thereby, these terms related to presumed biogenesis pathways should be used only with caution and strong evidence. Overall, ISEV suggests the use of the generic term ‘EV’ and operational extensions of this term. Therefore, the direct use of secretion pathway-based terminology such as “exosomes” and “microvesicles” is no longer recommended [12].

Another widely used approach for classifying EVs is based on their size. ISEV recommends operational size-based terms such as sEVs (generally < 200 nm) and large EVs (lEVs) generally > 200 nm), because their biogenesis is often difficult to trace in practice. However, there is no strict consensus on exact size cut-offs, and many EVs isolated by dUC or filtration methods show overlapping size profiles [12]. sEVs include exosomes and ectosomes, while lEVs include ectosomes (some EVs overlap with exosomes), migrasomes, apoptotic bodies, and large oncosomes [21]. sEVs may include both exosomes and small ectosomes. Although sEV and exosome are not synonymous, [12] the terms sEVs and exosomes are used interchangeably in numerous scientific research [22].

Given the limitations of current EV characterization methods, and the lack of specific biogenesis markers, we used the general term “small extracellular vesicles (sEVs)” to encompass these heterogeneous populations.

Biogenesis of extracellular vesicles

Among EVs subtypes, exosomes have been extensively studied and demonstrated to be crucial mediators of intercellular communication involving multiple physiological and pathological processes [23]. Understanding exosome biogenesis is essential for developing functional hypotheses. In general, exosome biogenesis is preceded by endocytosis. Endocytic vesicles are generated by clathrin mediated endocytosis, caveolin-dependent endocytosis and clathrin- and caveolin-independent endocytosis [24].

The formation of early endosomes as a result of the invagination of the plasma membrane is the first step in the process of EV biogenesis along the endosomal pathway. These structures develop into MVBs, which produce intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) through inward budding. Ceramide, a cone-shaped sphingolipid, often facilitates this process [18]. During maturation, MVBs can change the composition of ILVs by importing cargoes from several organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, trans-Golgi network, and other cytosolic compartments [24]. When MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane, ILVs are secreted as exosomes. Alternatively, MVBs may fuse with lysosomes or autophagosomes, resulting in degradation [19]. Exosomes move through extracellular space and interact with recipient cells through several factors [25]. Internalized exosomes are inserted into early endosomes, with the potential for possible further release through endosome recycling [26].

Currently, two main types of exosome biogenesis are recognized: endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent and ESCRT independent pathways. This classification is based primarily on the generation of ILVs, which also includes how cargos are sorted into ILVs [23]. Although the ESCRT machinery is very important to their biogenesis, alternative pathways exist. The ESCRT pathway, including proteins like ALIX, TSG101, Chmp4, and SKD1, contributed to MVBs formation and subsequent exosome release. Proteins like CD63, CD81, TSG101 (careful: TSG101 is involved in both pathways), ARF6, and heparanase are involved in exosome formation through ESCRT-independent mechanisms. These complex pathways demonstrate the complicated link between exosome biogenesis and cellular functions and the diverse mechanisms that govern it [21].

In summary, endosome synthesis, MVB formation, cargo sorting, and extracellular release are all steps in exosome biogenesis. On the other hand, exosome absorption involves targeting the recipient cell, internalization, and re-release [24].

Intercellular communication via small extracellular vesicles

Effective intercellular crosstalk is essential for fundamental biological processes and reproduction in multicellular organisms [27]. In eukaryotes, this communication is facilitated by extracellular molecules, including hormones, cytokines, neurotransmitters, miRNAs, and growth factors [28]. One noteworthy form of cellular communication in eukaryotes involves the use of EVs, specifically sEVs [11]. sEVs/exosomes have a cup-shaped morphology with a diameter ranging from 40 to 200 nm [11, 29, 30]. Distinguished by a lipid bilayer membrane, these vesicles contain a molecular composition that encompasses proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids [13, 31]. Each constituent is pivotal in maintaining cellular homeostasis and facilitating communication both within and between organs [32]. As a result, sEVs are detectable in a variety of corporeal fluids such as plasma, sweat, saliva, tears, breast milk, and urine [29, 33–36]. Adipose tissue-derived sEVs (sAdEVs) originate from multiple cellular sources, including adipocytes, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and ATMs [37].

Moreover, sEVs play a role in cellular signaling pathways [38]. As a result, these sEVs participate in diverse physiological and pathological processes, including immune regulation, tissue repair, aging, vascular calcification, and insulin resistance [39–42]. Recent discussions have delved into the extraction of sEVs from the placenta, contributing insights into their correlation with gestational diabetes [43]. sEVs and their derived substances may also have therapeutic potential, as they can repair insulin signaling and regulate insulin secretion [44–46].

Obesity, adipose-derived small EVs, and their pathological and therapeutical implications

As mentioned earlier, EVs derived from the adipose tissue play a role in physiological and pathological conditions. In physiological conditions, EVs regulate energy storage and consumption in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and liver. However, in pathological conditions, they may contribute to the development of cancer, endocrine disorders, obesity [47], and T2DM [48].

In individuals with obesity, adipocytes undergo cellular stress and hypertrophic expansion, accompanied by changes in sEVs composition [49]. In obesity, dysfunctional adipocytes exhibit irregular sEVs secretion which may promote the development of chronic comorbidities and related complications [49]. For instance, palmitic acid-induced 3T3–L1 adipocyte enlargement leads to insulin resistance in cardiomyocytes [50]. The membrane composition of sEVs may also affect insulin sensitivity and glucose control. For instance, the membrane of exosomes collected from the high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice intestine was predominantly made of phosphatidylcholine (PC) instead of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (normally predominant in lean mice). When intestinal exosomes enter the portal circulation, they are taken up by the liver, where membrane PC can bind to and activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, interfere with gene expression, and negatively regulating the insulin signaling pathway [i.e., suppressing insulin receptor substrate (IRS)-2 and protein kinase B (AKT)] [51].

sAdEVs can also aid in the management of metabolic syndrome and T2DM [52–54]. Both obesity and T2DM are conditions resulting from energy excess in the body. sAdEVs can modify gene expression and cellular activation pathways; thereby, influencing food intake and mitigating diabetes-related complications [55, 56]. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT)-derived sEVs (but not those from subcutaneous fat) inhibit insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation in hepatocytes, thus contributing to insulin resistance in the liver and related consequences (e.g., steatosis). AKT phosphorylation was negatively related to monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) levels [57]. Other experiments indicated that sEVs from the VAT of individuals with obesity induced transforming growth factor-beta pathway dysregulation when integrated into hepatocytes and stellate cells, thus providing another pathological mechanism of liver steatosis [58]. Recent findings suggested that ATM-derived sEVs expressed high levels of miR-500a-5p when exposed to high-glucose levels. This condition increased inflammasome activity in adipocytes by suppressing the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) anti-inflammatory response in adipocytes [59].

On the other hand, sEVs offer significant therapeutic potential due to their ability to deliver molecules selectively to target tissues. For example, in a mouse model, sEVs derived from ADSCs promote macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory (M1) to an anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype upon uptake [60]. Recent advances in sEVs engineering have explored their use as carriers for therapeutic cargo, including miRNA mimics, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and anti-inflammatory compounds, to mitigate insulin resistance [61]. For instance, exosome-based therapies targeting the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT and adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathways have shown promise in restoring insulin sensitivity in preclinical studies [62]. Furthermore, miRNA-loaded sEVs secreted by adipose tissue can positively regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis and free-fatty acid metabolism, establishing a link between adipose tissue dysfunction and hepatic steatosis [63]. Similarly, sEVs signaling between adipose tissue and skeletal muscle cells can restore insulin action by positively modulating glucose transporter protein type-4 (GLUT4) exposure on skeletal muscle cell and adipocyte membranes [64].

The impact of adipose-derived small EVs/exosomes on the progression of insulin resistance and diabetes-related complications

This section highlights the mechanistic insights of adipose-derived sEVs/exosomes on the progression of insulin resistance and diabetes-related complications. In this context, Dang et al. revealed a previously unknown mechanism that led to insulin resistance in obesity. They reported that adipocyte-derived exosomes from leptin-deficient mutant (ob/ob) mice and HFD-induced obese mice reduced insulin sensitivity by decreasing AKT phosphorylation levels and glucose uptake in alpha mouse liver 12 cells. They also found that exosomes isolated from the adipose tissue of obese mice expressed lower miR-141-3p concentration than exosomes from healthy adipose tissue. These exosomes upregulated the expression of the phosphatase and tensin homolog gene on chromosome 10, which negatively affected the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in hepatocytes [68]. In addition, it has been reported that EVs derived from hypoxic adipocytes impaired insulin-mediated glucose uptake in adipocytes by decreasing AKT phosphorylation [69]. Wen et al. demonstrated that exosome-derived miR-802-5p from hypertrophic 3T3-L1 adipocytes impairs cardiac insulin sensitivity by reducing the chaperonin heat shock protein 60 expression in rats. These results suggest a new mechanism by which the epicardial adipose tissue compromises cardiac performance [50]. In 2023, Zhang et al. suggested that circulating exosome-derived miR-22-3p regulated skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity based on an animal model of rhythm disturbance. A cell model of rhythm disorder clarified that exosome-derived miR-22-3p from adipocytes targeted skeletal muscle and fostered insulin resistance. Thus, the miR-22-3p overexpression led to insulin resistance by reducing p-AKT and p-IRS-1 protein levels [70].

Adipose tissue-derived miRNAs can lead to insulin resistance through several mechanisms, including a decrease in IRS-1 expression level [71, 72]. In this regard, Li et al. discovered that serum exosomal miR-222 derived from gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT) worsens insulin resistance by inhibiting IRS1 expression in the liver and in skeletal muscle in HFD-induced obesity in mice [72]. It has also been reported that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) was a target of adipocyte-derived exosomal miR-27a which reduced the expression of IRS-1 and GLUT4 in skeletal muscle, hereby promoting insulin resistance in both db/db mice and HFD-induced obese mice [41].

Furthermore, according to a study performed by Zhang et al., miR-210 and miR-92a secreted from WAT-derived exosomes suppressed the adipose tissue browning and enhanced the expression of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR-1) in BAT in a high-altitude hypoxia mice model [73]. In 2024, Wang et al. found that exosomes from adipose tissue of individuals with obesity expressed high levels of miR-23b. The WAT-derived miR-23b promoted weight gain, adipocyte hypertrophy, and insulin resistance. As well, miR-23b suppressed thermogenesis and positively regulated the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) expression via ELF4 [74]. Moreover, Kariba et al. reported that brown adipocyte-derived exosomal miR-132-3p could reduce lipogenic gene expression in mice hepatocytes exposed to low temperatures by suppressing the sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 1 (SREBF1) expression [75]. Also, miR-450a-5p in the adipose tissue-derived exosome-like vesicles (ELVs) could promote adipogenic differentiation by suppressing the WNT1 inducible signaling pathway protein 2 (WISP2) [76].

The lipidome of adipose-derived extracellular vesicles (AdEVs) plays a significant role in lipid mediator-mediated signaling pathways and has an important function in the formation and stability of EV membranes. The high amount of phospholipids and cholesterol in AdEV subtypes reflects their membranous origin and bilayered structure. Lipid sorting into AdEVs appears to be a regulated process, as these vesicles are significantly enriched in ceramides, sphingomyelins, and phosphatidylglycerol species compared to the parent membranous environment of the secreting VAT. Obesity induces distinct AdEV lipid fingerprints that serve as mediators of obesity-associated metabolic dysfunction. Moreover, AdEVs may have a role in neutral lipid mobilization due to the high concentration of triacylglycerols. Notably, enrichment of ceramides and their derivatives, saturated fatty acids, and arachidonic acid lipid derivatives serve as potent second messengers in stress related pathways, particularly those associated with insulin resistance and inflammation [77–79]. In addition, in line with this, Kumar et al. indicated that lean mice developed insulin resistance after being given exosomes isolated from the feces of obese, HFD-fed mice or from patients with T2DM. HFD changed the composition of lipids in intestinal exosomes from PE in lean animals to PC in obese animals. The exosomes from obese animals are taken up by hepatocytes, where exosomal PC binds to and activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. This activation suppressed the expression of insulin-signaling genes, including IRS-2, PI3K, and Akt, leading to insulin resistance [51]. Crewe et al. reported that AdEVs were enriched in sphingolipids, which can be potent second messengers in pathological states such as T2DM and may serve as markers of nutrient stress responses [80, 81]. Furthermore, Blandin et al. identified an AdEV-lipid signature in both healthy and obese mice. They identified specific lipid fingerprints for plasma, VAT, and AdEVs, including prominent increases in PC, lysoPC (LPC) species, sphingomyelin, ceramides, cholesterol esters and free cholesterol commonly observed in the plasma of ob/ob and HFD obese mice as compared to their lean controls [77]. Despite these findings, little attention has been paid to the potential of AdEVs to transport lipids, such as ceramides or diacylglycerols, which are recognized as potent mediators of insulin resistance development in skeletal muscle or liver [82, 83].

In addition to insulin-resistant cells like hepatocytes, adipocytes, and myocytes, the brain is now recognized as an insulin-sensitive organ that plays a crucial role in systemic energy homeostasis and glucose metabolism. Impairments in brain insulin signaling are observed in conditions such as obesity and T2DM. Moreover, defects in insulin transport across the blood–brain barrier and disrupted neuronal insulin receptor signaling have been associated with central insulin resistance, which contributes to memory impairment and cognitive dysfunction [84]. AdEVs and their cargo mediate inter-organ communication between adipose tissue and the brain. These EVs can be transferred into the brain via a process dependent on membrane proteins and accumulate in neurons, particularly in the hippocampus. New evidence reveals that adipose tissue-derived EVs AdEVs from HFD-fed mice or patients with diabetes cause substantial synaptic loss and cognitive impairment. Wang et al. demonstrated that AdEV-carried miR-9-3p from HFD-fed mice suppressed brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression and induced significant synaptic damage, particularly in the hippocampus. These data suggest that AdEVs or their cargo miRNAs could represent promising targets for pharmaceutical interventions against cognitive impairment [85]. Moreover, Gao et al. reported that adipocytes of obese mice secrete EVs enriched with the long noncoding RNA MALAT1 compared to those derived from lean adipocytes, which can transfer into hypothalamic anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons in vitro. In experimental models, EVs from obese mice increased appetite and weight when administered to lean mice. The authors suggested that MALAT1 functions as a competitive endogenous RNA interacting with miR-181b and miR-144, thereby activating the mTOR signaling pathway. Activation of this pathway reduced the activity and expression of appetite-suppressing POMC neurons, leading to increased energy intake and body weight. The authors acknowledge certain limitations of their study, particularly the lack of experimental data on metabolic indices, glucose tolerance, and insulin sensitivity. Therefore, they stated that it remains unclear whether the observed changes in food intake and body weight are directly associated with overall metabolic health [55, 86].

The effect of adipose tissue-derived small EVs/exosomes on inflammation-induced insulin resistance

It has also been indicated that insulin resistance associated with obesity is caused by adipose tissue inflammation. The crosstalk between sEVs released by inflamed adipocytes and insulin-sensitive target tissues could be involved in the pathogenesis of obesity-induced insulin resistance. In line with this, Pan et al. verified that epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) released miR-34a-containing exosomes, and macrophages absorbed these exosomes, leading to a shift in macrophage polarization from M2 to M1 due to reduced Krüppel-like factor 4 (Klf4) levels. This shift led to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, fostered tissue fibrosis, and decreased adiponectin levels, resulting in glucose intolerance and systemic insulin resistance. They also found a significant link between the miR-34a/Klf4 axis in visceral adipose tissue and insulin resistance in obese individuals [42]. In addition, Deng et al. discovered that adipose tissue of ob/ob mice secreted ELVs, which activated macrophages through the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and TIR domain-containing adaptor protein inducing interferon-β (TRIF) pathway to promote macrophage recruitment to adipose tissue and other systemic site (e.g., to liver). Then, the macrophages fostered insulin resistance by releasing TNF-α and IL-6 [87]. Furthermore, Song et al. found that Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) from insulin resistant adipocyte-derived exosomes (IRADEs) regulated M1 polarization via Ptch/PI3K signaling. The macrophages treated with IRADE made adipose tissue less sensitive to insulin by decreasing the hormone-sensitive lipase and IRS-1 expression. Furthermore, the authors mentioned an increase in the number of Shh-positive circulating exosomes in T2DM patients [88]. In 2022, findings confirmed that adipocyte-derived exosomal miR-1224 stimulated the release of inflammatory factors and inhibited M2 macrophage polarization by suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Ultimately, this mechanism promoted obesity-induced adipose tissue inflammation [89]. According to the research conducted by Tang et al. (2023), exosome-derived miR-27b-3p from visceral adipocytes affected vascular endothelial cells and promoted endothelial inflammation and atherogenesis by activating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway and downregulating PPARα [90]. In addition, exosomal circular RNAs derived from adipose tissue are also crucial to diabetic wound healing. Zhang et al. found that exosome-derived hsa_circ_0075932 from the human adipose tissue of healthy individuals directly interacted with pumilio RNA-binding family member 2 (PUM2), an RNA-binding protein that activated the AuroraA kinase and triggered the NF-κB signaling pathway. As a result, apoptosis and inflammation were promoted in human keratinocytes [91].

Furthermore, Lago-Baameiro et al. analyzed adipose tissue-to-liver interactions in obesity through EV-obesesomes to clarify their role in obesity-associated hepatic metabolic dysregulation. They revealed that obesesomes promoted inflammation and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines upon interaction with macrophages. When interacting with hepatocytes, these vesicles impaired insulin sensitivity (evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of Akt and S6), altering lipid and glucose metabolism. In addition, EVs secreted by steatotic hepatocytes (steatosomes) induced insulin resistance and altered lipid and glucose metabolism in healthy hepatocytes. These vesicles decreased the expression of PPARα, PPARβ, and perilipin 3. Proteomic analysis demonstrated that steatosomes contain liver disease–associated proteins, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for early detection and disease monitoring, as well as their involvement in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease progression [92].

The effect of adipose tissue macrophage-derived small EVs/exosomes on inflammation and insulin resistance

ATMs have a role in regulating both systemic and local inflammatory responses and mediate insulin resistance [93]. Ying et al. reported that ATMs-derived exosomes from obese mice led to insulin resistance when administered to lean mice. However, treating obese mice with exosomes from lean-derived ATMs ameliorated glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity without affecting body weight. They also indicated that ATM-derived exosomal miR-155 might target PPARγ, leading to the downregulation of GLUT4 in obese mice. In fact, miR-155 had an inhibitory effect on insulin signaling in adipocytes, hepatocytes, and myocytes [94]. Additionally, according to a study conducted by Liu et al., ATM-derived exosomal miR-29a exhibited high expression levels in obese mice and could be conveyed to adipocytes, hepatocytes, and myocytes, promoting insulin resistance and glucose intolerance [95]. Other experimental results found that exosomes from lean mice upregulated miR-222-3p and downregulated Bim expression, resulting in in vitro macrophage repolarization from a pro-inflammatory towards an anti-inflammatory profile [96].

In addition, Tian et al. also found that macrophage-derived exosomes from VAT exposed to high glucose levels expressed high levels of miR-210. When co-cultured with 3T3-L1 adipocytes, these exosomes significantly impaired glucose uptake and suppressed the NADH dehydrogenase ubiquinone 1 alpha subcomplex 4 (NDUFA4) of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex [97].

The impact of circulating small EVs/exosomes from obese individuals/animal models on insulin resistance-related complications

The data from a human study indicated that circulating exosomes from obese women could disrupt insulin signaling pathways. Plasma exosomes derived from obese women significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (p-GSK3β) and glycogen in HepG2 cells. They also found an elevation in intracellular triglyceride content and a decrease in fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) release in hepatocytes [34]. It has been established that obesity altered the miRNA profile of plasma exosomes in mice, specifically leading to upregulation of miR-122, miR-192, miR-27a-3p, and miR-27b-3p. When administered to lean mice, exosomes from obese mice targeted PPARγ to induce hepatic steatosis and inflammation in eWAT and contribute to dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose tolerance [98]. Moreover, according to research reported by Wang et al., considerable differential expression of mmu-miR-674-5p in serum exosomes from obese mice as compared to lean mice suggested that it may be involved in the modulation of insulin resistance. The miR-674 family, particularly mmu-miR-674-5p, plays a significant role in regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [99, 100]. They revealed differential expression of the Mtch2, Dsg2, Camk1d, TRAF3IP2, Eef1a2, and Mknk2 genes, which were linked to glucose-lipid metabolism regulation mediated by miR-674-5p. Additionally, the researchers observed that miR-674-5p expression was significantly influenced by Insulin-like Growth Factor 2 (IGF-II). Further pathway analysis using Pathcards indicated a strong association between IGF-II and the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [99]. In addition, Hubal et al. reported that gastric bypass surgery altered circulating adipocyte-derived microRNAs, with 29 key microRNAs linked to improved insulin sensitivity and to pathways such as insulin receptor signaling and the WNT/β-catenin pathway. The improvement in glucose homeostasis was reflected by reduced branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) levels and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) correlations. BCAAs, which contribute to insulin dysregulation, were associated with multiple exosomal microRNAs, including let-7a-5p (targeting INSR, IRS2, and KRAS) and miR-16-5p (targeting INSR, IRS1, and IRS2) [101].

AdEVs contain highly adipocyte-expressed proteins, such as adiponectin, fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), or perilipin-1, which can be located either within the lumen or on the surface of the AdEVs [102]. Functional classification shows that VAT-derived vesicles in obesity were enriched in proteins involved in adipose tissue inflammation and insulin resistance, such as TGFBI, CAVN1, CD14, mimecan, thrombospondin-1, FABP4, and AHNAK. According to the study conducted by Camino et al., mimecan and TGFBI were identified as biomarkers for obesity comorbidities. They demonstrated that mimecan was upregulated in AdEVs from obese patients (particularly in VAT-derived vesicles) and in obese patients with a history of T2DM compared to healthy lean controls. In addition, a substantial increase in TGFBI-positive EVs was observed in obese individuals with a previous diagnosis of T2DM compared to non-diabetic individuals, suggesting that circulating TGFBI-containing EVs may help monitor T2DM status in obese patients [103]. Consistent with these findings, EVs isolated from obese VAT showed a significantly higher proportion of proteins associated with insulin, IGF-1, TRAIL, syndecan, and other signaling pathways [104]. In addition, Saraswathi et al. examined the proteomics profile of serum exosomes from normal individuals and those with obesity and insulin resistance. In total, 69 proteins were detected as differentially expressed [23, 46]. In obese insulin-resistant individuals, upregulated proteins included insulin signaling markers such as Vacuolar Protein Sorting 13 Homolog C (VPS13C), TBC1 Domain Family Member 32 (TBC1D32), and Transthyretin (TTR); inflammatory indicators (NF-κB, CRP), and B cell activation markers such as Immunoglobulin Lambda Variable 4–69 (IGLV4-69), Immunoglobulin Kappa Variable 1D-13 (IGKV1D-13), and Immunoglobulin Heavy Variable 4–28 (IGHV4-28). Conversely, insulin-sensitizing proteins including adiponectin (ADIPOQ), ficolin-3 (FCN3), and zinc α2-glycoprotein (AZGP1) were significantly downregulated. The authors proposed that dysregulated proteins including VPS13C, ADIPOQ, and IGKV1D-13 could serve as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets for preventing or delaying T2DM [105].

Moreover, the recent studies indicate that exosomes derived from insulin-resistant adipocytes contain proteins that are strongly associated with insulin resistance and T2DM, such as calreticulin, S100A6, mimecan, PARK7/DJ-1, PPIB, and tenascin [106]. Accordingly, Eguchi et al. reported that perilipin A levels were significantly increased in circulating AdEVs from both diet-induced obesity mice and obese humans with metabolic syndrome. In diet-induced obesity mice, glucose tolerance was impaired, while in obese humans, metabolic dysfunction, insulin levels, and HOMA‑IR were elevated [107].

The impact of small EVs/exosomes isolated from adipose-derived stem cells on the amelioration of insulin resistance and its related complications

This section highlights the mechanistic insights and therapeutic potential of ADSC-derived sEVs/exosomes on the amelioration of insulin resistance and its related complications. In line with this, Jin et al. have discovered that ADSC-derived exosomes alleviated type 1 diabetes (T1D)-related renal complications by reducing podocyte apoptosis, serum creatinine, proteinuria, and blood urea nitrogen in diabetic mice. Mechanistically, exosomes derived from the ADSC enhanced miR-486 expression and inhibited the Smad1/mTOR signaling pathway, in turn, resulting in podocyte injury attenuation and autophagy in mouse podocyte clone-5 cells [56]. In another study, miRNA-215-5p originating from ADSCs was found to reduce the epithelial-mesenchymal transition of podocytes by inhibiting the expression of zinc finger e-box binding homeobox 2 (ZEB2) [54]. In 2021, Wang et al. discovered that there was an increase in 215 miRNAs and a decrease in 369 miRNAs in hypoxic as compared to normoxic-ADSCs exosomes. They found that the upregulation of three miRNAs (miR-21-3p, miR-31-5p, and miR-126-5p) and the downregulation of two miRNAs (miR-146-a and miR-99b) could modulate the immune response, fibroblast proliferation, maturation, and migration. Indeed, diabetic wounds and inflammation were improved by activating PI3K/AKT signaling pathways in BALB/c nude mice. These findings provide a new strategy to cure diabetic wound healing using hypoxic exosomes derived from ADSC [108]. Moreover, Shi et al. revealed that mmu_circ_0000250 enhanced angiogenesis in diabetic wound healing in vivo when delivered via exosomes derived from ADSC, as well as reduced apoptosis by activating autophagy. Furthermore, it was confirmed that mmu_circ_0000250 amplified the therapeutic potential of ADSC-derived exosomes in diabetic wound healing by upregulating sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and absorption of miR-128-3p. SIRT1 had antioxidant functions and anti-inflammatory effects [109].

In 2022, Liang et al. indicated a new mechanism for mmu_circ_0001052 from exosomes derived from ADSC. They found that mmu_circ_0001052 inhibited the apoptosis of human umbilical vein endothelial cells and increased the angiogenesis in diabetic mice with diabetic foot ulcers through miR-106a-5p, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and the fibroblast growth factor 4 (FGF4) pathway [110]. It has also been reported that Circ-Snhg11 overexpression in hypoxic exosomes derived from ADSC suppressed hyperglycemia-induced endothelial cell injury and accelerated wound healing in diabetic mice by promoting M2 macrophage polarization and upregulating the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) expression through sponging miR-144-3p [111]. In addition, according to a research done in 2023 by Yin et al., ADSC‐exosomes containing circRps5 fostered M2 polarization. The overexpression of circRps5 increased collagen synthesis, promoted wound healing, and attenuated the inflammatory response [112]. Overall, the level of evidence about the application of AdEVs and exosomes to diabetes-related wound healing is based on preclinical but promising results, as indicated by two recent systematic reviews [113, 114]. In a study done by Zhao et al., ADSC-derived exosomes transferred active STAT3 into macrophages, which activated arginase-1. Thereby, treatment of obese mice with ADSC-derived exosomes improved metabolic homeostasis, including a 27.8% increase in insulin sensitivity, reduced obesity, and improved glucose tolerance. These exosomes induced M2 macrophage polarization, reduced inflammation, and promoted browning in WAT of diet-induced obese mice [60].

Mechanistic studies demonstrate an existing connection between atherosclerosis and adipose tissue-derived exosomes. It has been reported that exosomes from SIRT1-overexpressing ADSCs increased the expression of C-X-C motif chemokine 12 (CXCL12) and nuclear factor E2 related factor 2 (Nrf2) in endothelial progenitor cells of patients with acute myocardial infarction. These two factors promoted migration and tube formation of endothelial progenitor cells and injection of exosomes from SIRT1-overexpressing ADSCs promoted the recovery of myocardial function, reduced the infarction size, attenuated post-infarction left ventricular remodeling, induced neo-angiogenesis, and decreased myocardial inflammation [115].

Furthermore, studies have also demonstrated the antiapoptotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and angiogenesis-modulating properties of exosomes isolated from adipose-derived MSCs in diabetic retinopathy [116], as well as relevant improvement of lipid metabolism and inflammation in non-alcoholic hepatic steatosis [117, 118]. Moreover, Kim B et al. found that MSC-derived exosomes attenuated liver steatosis and insulin resistance in diet-induced obese mice by activating the FGF21-adiponectin axis through the AMPK-SIRT1- PGC-1α pathway [119].

The impact of adipose-derived small EVs/exosomes on the amelioration of insulin resistance and its related complications

The proteomic analysis of the WAT secretome conducted by Hartwig et al. identified 817 exoadipokines (proteins transported by exosomes). The authors associated SERPINF1, COL5A3, COL1A1, C3, RBP4, GPD1, and TUBA1C with endocrine disorders, metabolic diseases, and T2DM [120]. Among various adipokines, oligomeric forms of adiponectin are mainly located on the external surface of sAdEVs. These sAdEVs act as stable carriers of adiponectin that bind to the adiponectin receptors, showing insulin-sensitizing effects in vitro. When these adiponectin-enriched sAdEVs, obtained from lean adipose tissue, are injected into mice fed a HFD, they prevent obesity, insulin resistance, and inflammation in both the liver and adipose tissue [121]. Additionally, adiponectin is known to regulate exosome biogenesis and secretion through binding with T-cadherin [122]. Besides adiponectin, another notable EV content is Nucleophosmin3 (NPM3), which has been linked to various metabolic processes. The role of NPM3 in the function of sEVs-BAT was examined in HFD-induced obese mice. Mice were intravenously injected with either sEVs-BAT or sEVs-BAT with NPM3-knockdown (sEVs-BAT-siNPM3). Administration of sEVs-BAT mitigated HFD-induced weight gain, reduced inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) and eWAT mass, alleviated adipocyte hypertrophy, improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, lowered fasting glucose levels, and decreased pro-inflammatory gene expression (such as IL-1β and IL-6) in adipose tissue. These effects were abolished when NPM3 was knocked down in BAT-sEVs. Previous studies have reported that the NPM family is associated with various endocrine and metabolic disorders, including impaired aerobic glycolysis, altered insulin receptor expression, obesity, and aging. However, the biological functions of NPM3 remained largely undefined, particularly in the regulation of adipose tissue homeostasis [123]. Additionally, as demonstrated by Kulaj et al., adipocyte-derived EVs from diet-induced obese mice carried insulinotropic protein cargo that enhanced the first phase of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, which was not observed in EVs from lean mice. Therefore, the status of insulin resistance in adipose tissue is communicated to pancreatic beta-cells by EVs to meet the increased insulin demand [124]. Accordingly, more studies are needed to validate whether adipocyte-derived EVs from obese humans can improve or impair insulin resistance.

White adipose tissue-derived small EVs versus brown adipose tissue-derived small EVs

WAT and BAT have fundamentally different metabolic roles. BAT is a thermogenic fat that is a promising weapon to combat obesity and metabolic disease [125] and plays a crucial role in maintaining glucose homeostasis, improving β-cell function, and reducing insulin demand [126]. In contrast, expanded WAT in obesity serves as an energy reservoir and secretes pro-inflammatory signals. Consistent with these roles, sEVs/exosomal signals from WAT versus BAT differ markedly in their impact on insulin sensitivity.

In obesity and T2DM, WAT releases sEVs enriched in miRNAs and cytokines that promote insulin resistance [127]. For instance, gonadal WAT exosomal miR-222 is markedly upregulated in obese humans with insulin resistance and HFD-fed mice [72]. These WAT-derived exosomes suppress IRS1/AKT signaling in liver and muscle, thereby impairing insulin action and glucose tolerance [128]. Similarly, exosomes from obese WAT activate inflammatory macrophages (via TLR4/RBP4 pathways) and elevate TNF-α/IL-6 levels, further driving systemic insulin resistance [127]. Thus, WAT-derived sEVs generally carry “bad” signals that exacerbate metabolic dysfunction and T2DM.

In contrast, sEVs/exosomes from healthy, activated BAT convey good metabolic signals. In obese mouse models, intravenous BAT-derived exosomes reduced adiposity, lowered blood glucose, and improved glucose tolerance [129]. Proteomic and miRNA analysis suggest BAT-derived exosomes are enriched in mitochondrial proteins and regulatory miRNAs. For instance, BAT exosomal miR-99b targets the liver’s FGF21 gene to improve glucose metabolism, as well as miR-132-3p suppresses the lipogenic factor SREBF1 [75, 128]. These effects collectively enhance insulin sensitivity. In humans, direct studies are limited, but BAT activation (e.g. by cold) is known to improve glucose disposal, implying BAT-derived exosomal factors may mediate part of this benefit [125].

In general, the current evidence points to a clear dichotomy: WAT-derived sEVs (especially from obese or inflamed fat) carry pro-diabetic cargos that induce insulin resistance, whereas BAT-derived sEVs carry anti-diabetic cargos that enhance insulin sensitivity. This functional contrast underscores an important mechanistic axis in adipose tissue crosstalk. Incorporating studies of WAT-derived sEVs versus BAT-derived sEVs enriches our understanding of insulin resistance and suggests future research directions exploring BAT-derived sEV therapies for metabolic diseases.

Translational challenges in small extracellular vesicle therapeutics

SAdEVs show promise in modulating metabolism in diabetes and obesity, but their clinical translation is impeded by multiple practical hurdles. Large‐scale manufacturing is difficult; therapeutic doses require expansion of donor adipose cells under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions, yet typical yields are very low and batch‐to‐batch consistency is poor. For example, Li et al. note that current cell‐culture and purification technologies restrict the implementation of standardized and mass production of exosomes, and emphasize that scalable manufacturing processes are urgently needed [130, 131]. In practice, even MSC-derived exosomes (including those from adipose MSCs) have low productivity and no standardized GMP protocol exists [132]. Similarly, isolation and characterization methods lack standardization: each lab uses different EV‐purification techniques (ultracentrifugation, filtration, precipitation, etc.), yielding vesicles of varying purity and content. The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles has published MISEV guidelines to harmonize characterization standards (e.g. marker panels, purity metrics) [12], but adoption is uneven and many studies report incomplete EV profiling. This methodological variability makes it hard to compare results or ensure that a preparation of adipose‐derived exosomes contains the intended bioactive cargo.

Regulatory and safety issues add further complexity. Regulatory agencies classify exosome therapeutics as biologics or cell‐derived products, requiring rigorous quality control, but no EV‐based drug has yet been approved [133]. Key concerns include the intrinsic heterogeneity of cell‐derived exosomes and unknown mechanisms of action; even adipose MSCs produce vesicles whose composition varies with culture conditions. Preclinical reports also highlight potential hazards of MSC‐EVs. Exosomes can trigger immune reactions or thrombosis and may carry unwanted cargo (e.g. pro‐coagulant factors or pathogens). Thus, sponsors must demonstrate consistent particle identity, potency and safety (e.g. lack of oncogenic or inflammatory activity) before clinical use, a process complicated by limited analytical tools for complex EV mixtures [133, 134].

In addition, exosome storage and stability are limiting. sEVs are inherently fragile. In liquid they rapidly degrade or aggregate, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles can disrupt membrane integrity and cargo [135]. Although cryopreservation or lyophilization methods exist, there is no consensus best practice and shelf‐life data are sparse. As a result, defining a stable clinical formulation of sAdEVs (with preserved bioactivity and sterility) remains an unresolved challenge. The therapeutic promise of sAdEVs in metabolic diseases is tempered by practical obstacles. Overcoming them will require concerted efforts to develop GMP‐compliant biomanufacturing platforms, unified isolation/characterization standards, clear regulatory pathways, and robust storage protocols.

Conclusion

This narrative review aimed to assess the impact of adipose tissue-derived sEVs/exosomes on molecular mechanisms related to obesity-related metabolism dysfunction, such as insulin resistance, diabetes, and diabetes-related chronic complications. The literature reports experiments in which sEVs were obtained from WAT, BAT, ADSCs, ATMs, and plasma or other tissues of obese individuals/animal models. The leading results indicate that adipose tissue-derived exosomes, especially visceral adipose tissue, or obtained from obese individuals and animals contribute significantly to the induction and progression of insulin resistance, as preclinical evidence robustly suggests. On the other hand, a few studies have so far been conducted and published to address the therapeutic potential of sEVs. Exosomes/sEVs extracted from ADSCs could improve insulin resistance and can also have therapeutic potential for treating chronic diabetes-related complications. So, further research is needed to confirm these results in patients with metabolic disorders associated with insulin resistance or diabetes and to examine safety issues. Basic investigation is also needed to better understand how obesity and related metabolic disorders affect the formation, content packaging, and release of sEVs from adipose tissue to modify, create, and enhance sEVs extraction for a wide range of uses [49].
