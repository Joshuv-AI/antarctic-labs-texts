Impact of Impella 5.5 duration support and clinical outcomes in cardiogenic shock: Evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Mrunalini Dandamudi, Srinivas Vunnam, Tooba Rehman, Muhammad Aqib Faizan, Miguel A. Samaniego-Laguna, John Cedric Mojica, Juliana Giorgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Impella 5.5 is FDA-approved for up to 14 days of support in patients with cardiogenic shock, however, its extended use is increasingly employed as a bridge to advanced therapies.AimTo compare outcomes with short-term (≤14 days) vs prolonged (>14 days) Impella 5.5 support in patients with cardiogenic shock.MethodsMultiple databases were searched through September 2025 to identify relevant studies, and 5 studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing total of 1,440 patients. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using a random-effects meta-analysis in R-software.ResultsProlonged Impella 5.5 support (>14 days) was associated with a higher likelihood of successful bridging to heart transplantation (RR: 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.83; p = 0.049), and with lower in-hospital mortality rate (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.53–0.84; p = 0.011). However, native heart survival or recovery (RR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.54-0.90; p = 0.026) was significantly lower with prolonged support. There was no significant difference in bridging to durable LVAD between the groups (RR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.54-1.36, p = 0.28). Prolonged Impella support was associated with higher risks of insertion-site hematoma (RR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.07-2.24; p = 0.036) and infection (RR: 3.36, 95% CI: 2.71-4.17; p = 0.002), while stroke rates were similar between groups (RR: 1.34; 95% CI: 0.96-1.89; p = 0.074).ConclusionsExtending Impella 5.5 support beyond 14 days in patients with cardiogenic shock appears advantageous for select patients, conferring improved survival and higher likelihood of successful bridging to heart transplantation. However, significantly higher risk of implant-site hematoma and infection necessitates meticulous vascular management, infection-prevention strategies and close clinical surveillance.

Background

The Impella 5.5 is a surgically implanted temporary microaxial flow pump1 designed to provide robust left ventricular support, delivering flows of up to 5.5 liters/min. By actively unloading the left ventricle, the device facilitates immediate hemodynamic stabilization and serves as a critical bridge to recovery, to durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD), or heart transplantation in patients presenting with cardiogenic shock.2 Currently in the United States, the Impella 5.5 with Smart Assist System is Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for up to 14 days of use in the management of ongoing cardiogenic shock following acute myocardial infarction (MI), cardiac surgery, or cardiomyopathy (including peripartum or myocarditis) where left ventricular failure remains refractory to medical and conventional therapies, such as inotropes, vasopressors, or intra-aortic balloon pump support.3, 4, 5 Whereas, Impella 5.5 is approved in Europe for up to 30 days duration for cardiogenic shock.6 Despite the regulatory limitation of 14 days, contemporary clinical practice often necessitates deviations from this time frame.7, 8 Multicenter data, including reports from the Cardiogenic Shock Working Group, indicate a significant subset of patients requires extended Impella 5.5 support to achieve myocardial recovery or to bridge to advanced heart-failure therapies.9 Consequently, off-label use of this device for prolonged durations is becoming increasingly common in high-acuity settings.8, 10, 11, 12

However, high-quality evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of prolonged Impella 5.5 support remains limited, largely confined to retrospective single-center experiences that vary in patient selection, duration of support, and reported outcomes.13, 14 This gap in literature created clinical uncertainty regarding risks of device-related complications, such as hemolysis, thrombosis, or infection, vs the benefits of extended hemodynamic support. Establishing the safety profile of prolonged Impella 5.5 support is crucial to guide patient selection, optimize timing for escalation, and inform bridging strategies toward recovery or advanced therapies. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing in-hospital outcomes among patients with cardiogenic shock supported with Impella 5.5 for extended duration (>14 days) vs standard duration (≤14 days), to evaluate the comparative safety, efficacy, and bridging success associated with prolonged device support. Our aim was precisely to synthesize the existing evidence around these critical issues, thereby providing a data-driven foundation for ongoing clinical discussions and decision-making, despite the inherent limitations of observational studies. In addition to US experience, several European centers have reported favorable outcomes with prolonged Impella 5.0/5.5 support as a bridge to advanced therapies, particularly in settings where regulatory approval permits support up to 30 days. These studies have demonstrated the feasibility of extended support and successful bridging to transplantation or durable LVAD in selected patients.15, 16 However, most of these reports do not stratify outcomes according to duration of support, limiting their eligibility for inclusion in duration–based comparative meta-analyses, such as the present study.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Statement guidelines.17 The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under record number CRD420251171642.

Search strategy and data extraction

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the Cochrane Library to identify the studies published through September 2025 comparing outcomes of prolonged (>14 days) Impella 5.5 support in patients with cardiogenic shock compared to the standard duration of 14 days. Data were extracted from published reports, and study quality was assessed in accordance with Cochrane recommendations.

Two authors (M.D. and S.V.) screened studies independently and extracted data using a predefined data-extraction framework. Duplicate records were identified and excluded using Rayyan software, followed by full-text screening of the studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus or consulting a third author (M.S.).

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion in this meta-analysis was limited to studies meeting the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials or observational studies; (2) comparing outcomes of Impella 5.5 support <14 days vs >14 days in patients with cardiogenic shock; (3) studies including adult patients >18 years were only included; (4) reporting at least 1 outcome of interests.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they (1) did not fulfill the inclusion criteria; (2) lacked a comparator group; (3) included the pediatric population; (4) were reviews, conference abstracts, protocols without results, or duplicate data from the same cohort; (5) had no outcomes of interest described.

End-points and outcomes

Primary outcomes of interest were in-hospital mortality, native-heart recovery, and bridge to heart transplant. Secondary outcomes include incidence of stroke, bridge to LVAD, and insertion-site hematoma or infection.

Quality assessment

Two authors (M.A. and T.R.) assessed the risk of bias for each study, using the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I tool)18 in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. We resolved disagreements through discussion or consulting a third review author (M.S.) when required. Risk of bias was evaluated across the seven ROBINS-I domains: (1) bias due to confounding, (2) selection of participants, (3) classification of interventions, (4) deviations from intended intervention, (5) missing data, (6) measurement of outcomes, and (7) selection of reported results. Each trial was judged as having a low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias for each domain. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was subsequently assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework,19 following Cochrane recommendations, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).20 Pooled effect estimates were derived using the Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood estimator for τ²), computing the risk ratios (RR) along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The interstudy heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and quantified through Higgins and Thompson’s I² statistic, with statistical significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. Heterogeneity was classified as 0% (none), ≤25% (low), ≤50% (moderate), and >50% (substantial).21 The robustness of pooled estimates was assessed using leave-one-out analyses. Subgroup analyses were not performed owing to insufficient data for meaningful stratification at the study level. The meta-regression22 analyses were performed using R software to explore the association between etiology of cardiogenic shock (acute MI or acute decompensated heart failure [ADHF]) and selected outcomes, with results displayed using a bubble plot.

A total of 1,619 articles were identified, and 1,228 articles were screened based on title and abstract after excluding 391 duplicates. Results

This meta-analysis synthesized outcomes from 5 observational studies.9, 23, 24, 25, 26 The in-hospital mortality was reported in 4 studies comprising a total of 1,106 patients and was significantly lower in patients with extended Impella 5.5 support beyond 14 days (RR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.53-0.84; I2 = 0%; p = 0.011, Figure 3) compared with standard duration (≤14 days). Prolonged Impella support beyond 14 days was also associated with higher likelihood of bridging to heart transplant, based on data from 3 studies (RR: 1.96, CI 1.01- 3.83; I2 = 32.5%; p = 0.049, Figure 4). In contrast, the rates of bridging to LVAD did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (RR: 0.86, CI 0.54-1.36; I2 = 0%; p = 0.28, Figure 5). Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analysis

The pooled analyses demonstrated null or minimal heterogeneity across most outcomes (I2 = 0%). The only exception was the outcome of bridge to transplant, which exhibited moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 32.5%, Supplementary Figure 3). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed the robustness of this finding, with consistent effect estimates across all iterations (RR range 1.85-2.45). The study by Feng et al contributed the greatest proportion of heterogeneity (49%), though exclusion of this study did not alter the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimate.

Meta-regression

Owing to the relatively small number of studies included, the meta-regression analyses were exploratory and limited in their ability to fully assess the influence of baseline study characteristics. Meta-regression was performed to evaluate the association between the proportion of patients with ADHF or acute MI with cardiogenic shock supported by Impella 5.5, and key outcomes, including NHS/recovery, stroke, and in-hospital mortality. The proportion of patients with acute MI or ADHF was not significantly associated with the incidence of stroke. However, in-hospital mortality was significantly lower in studies with a higher proportion of ADHF (coefficient of −2.1, p: 0.045) but no significant association was observed with the proportion of acute MI patients (coefficient of 1.65, p: 0.43). The NHS or recovery was significantly associated with both acute MI (coefficient of 0.48, p = 0.007) and ADHF (coefficient of 3.23, p = 0.043), suggesting that baseline etiology may influence myocardial recovery trajectories in patients supported with Impella 5.5. (Supplement Figures 2.1-2.6).

Risk of bias

The quality of the included studies was assessed using ROBINS-I tool18 (Supplementary Figure 1). Four of the included studies were judged to have a serious risk of bias, primarily due to residual confounding inherent to the observational study design. In contrast, the registry-based study by Hong et al23 was judged to have an overall low risk of bias, reflecting robust adjustment for confounding variables and the prospective nature of the registry. All studies demonstrated low risk of bias in domains of patient selection, classification of interventions, and selection of reported outcomes. The remaining domains were judged to confer a moderate risk of bias across studies, except the study by Kanwar et al9 that showed serious concerns related to outcome measurement. Overall, the predominance of observational study designs and associated risks of residual confounding warrant cautious interpretation of the pooled results.

Grade assessment

The GRADE assessment demonstrated variable levels of certainty across outcomes related to extended Impella support duration beyond 14 days (Supplementary Table 2).

The evidence for insertion-site hematoma was rated as moderate certainty, based on 3 studies comprising 444 participants, with no serious risk of bias or inconsistency (I² = 0%) for the higher risk of implant-site hematoma in patients with extended Impella support. Similarly, insertion-site infection showed moderate-certainty evidence across three studies (n = 174), with consistent findings, no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%), and a significantly higher infection risk associated with extended Impella support (RR = 3.36; 95% CI: 2.71-4.17).

In contrast, NHS or recovery was supported by high-certainty evidence derived from 3 studies (n = 573) with low heterogeneity and precise effect estimates, demonstrating significantly lower recovery rates in patients with extended Impella duration beyond 14 days.

The risk of stroke, evaluated across 5 studies (n = 707), was rated as moderate-certainty evidence. Although no significant difference was observed between standard and prolonged Impella support duration, the certainty was downgraded for imprecision due to the wide CI and low event rates. Similarly, bridging to durable LVAD was rated moderate-certainty, based on 3 studies (n = 541), with the certainty downgraded for imprecision, as the CI crossed the null value (RR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.54-1.36), indicating no statistically significant difference between groups.

Bridging to heart transplantation was supported by moderate-certainty evidence from three studies (n = 573) demonstrating a significantly higher likelihood of transplantation in patients with Impella support exceeding 14 days. The certainty was downgraded 1 level for imprecision due to the borderline CI and observational nature of the included studies.

Finally, in-hospital mortality was also supported by moderate-certainty evidence derived from four studies (n = 587). Although the pooled estimate demonstrated strong consistency and precision, the certainty was downgraded by one level due to non–randomized study designs.

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesizes the comparative evidence of standard duration (≤14 days) vs extended (>14 days) Impella 5.5 support in cardiogenic shock. Our meta-analysis suggests that extended Impella 5.5 support is increasingly used as a bridging strategy and is associated with improved survival and a higher likelihood of bridge to heart transplantation, but with trade-offs in terms of fewer native-heart recoveries and greater device-related complications, particularly insertion-site hematoma and infection, which necessitate careful patient selection and vigilant management.

Clinical outcomes and benefits

The increased success rates in bridging to heart transplantation (RR: 1.96, p = 0.049) align with emerging evidence that extended mechanical circulatory support (MCS) with prolonged Impella 5.5 can provide hemodynamic stability and end-organ function and thereby improve candidacy for advanced therapies, especially heart transplantation. However, the prolonged support with Impella 5.5 is associated with significantly lower NHS (RR: 0.70, p = 0.026), which can be attributed to selection bias, as it implies that patients expected to recover early are typically explanted earlier, whereas those requiring extended support may inherently have more severe irreversible myocardial injury limiting potential for recovery, or end-stage heart failure physiology that predisposes them to durable LVAD or heart transplantation strategies.27 This prompts us to the importance of early and accurate identification of patients who are most likely to benefit from prolonged support.28

The lack of a significant difference in bridging to durable LVAD between the 2 groups (RR: 0.86, p = 0.28) implies that the decision for LVAD implantation depends less on the support duration and more on intrinsic patient factors, such as right ventricular function and other factors.29 In contrast to heart transplantation, prolonged support may mitigate the urgency of donor availability and several other factors.30

Importantly, European experience with Impella 5.0/5.5 has provided additional insight into the feasibility of prolonged support strategies. Several studies from high-volume centers have demonstrated that extended Impella support can safely bridge patients to durable LVAD or heart transplantation, particularly when implemented within structured multidisciplinary shock programs. These reports support the concept that prolonged temporary MCS may provide a therapeutic window for end-organ recovery and optimization of candidacy for advanced therapies, reinforcing the findings of the present meta-analysis.

The reduction in mortality (RR: 0.67, p = 0.011) among patients on prolonged Impella 5.5 support despite reduced NHS suggests that, in a subset of patients, prolonged temporary mechanical support with Impella provides necessary time for myocardial recovery or optimization of end-organ function to definitive therapies. Several small studies demonstrated evidence of improved survival with prolonged duration of Impella 5.5 support in various etiologies and clinical settings.13, 31, 32, 33, 34

Safety considerations and complications

Although some benefits were noticed with prolonged support of Impella 5.5 in cardiogenic shock, which are accompanied by increased risk of insertion-site hematoma (RR: 1.55, p = 0.036) and infection (RR: 3.36, p = 0.002), this emphasizes the need for meticulous vascular access management, collaboration with infectious-disease expertise for aggressive preventive measures, and vigilant monitoring in patients receiving extended Impella support. These findings are biologically plausible; longer indwelling times increase the risk of vascular injury, hematoma formation related to anticoagulation, and bacterial colonization.2, 35, 36

While the rates of stroke were not significantly different between the 2 groups, the trend toward increased risk with prolonged support (RR: 1.34, p = 0.07) warrants attention. Though this implies that thromboembolic risk may not escalate linearly with duration, this warrants further investigation, given the anticoagulation requirements during the Impella support.

Proposed algorithm

Based on our meta-analysis, which demonstrated both important benefits (improved survival, higher likelihood of successful bridging to heart transplantation) and key risks (increased infection and insertion-site hematoma, lower native-heart recovery) of prolonged Impella 5.5 support, we propose the following evidence-informed, practice-oriented algorithm to guide clinical decision-making in the management of cardiogenic shock.

European centers—where Impella 5.5 is approved for up to 30 days—demonstrate that structured, protocol-driven programs can optimize outcomes with prolonged support. These programs typically employ standardized implantation techniques, daily multidisciplinary surveillance, clear criteria for weaning or transition, and defined pathways for extended-support candidates,15 offering a practical model for high–acuity US centers.

Limitations

While our meta-analysis is being meticulously conducted, several limitations still exist in the interpretation of the findings, like any other trials or studies. First, all the studies included were observational and retrospective in nature, predisposing to selection and confounding biases. Variations in patient population, clinical setting, and institutional management protocols limit the generalizability of our conclusions. Notably, patients receiving prolonged support likely differ systematically from those with shorter support in unmeasured ways not fully captured by databases (e.g., underlying disease chronicity, comorbidity burden, and center-level practices). Second, heterogeneity is observed across the studies in key domains, including indications for Impella use, anticoagulation regimens, device management protocols, thresholds for escalation to durable MCS or transplant, and definitions/ascertainment of complications. This precludes direct comparisons and underscores the need for a standardized reporting framework in future studies.

Third, although the total sample size was substantial, the limited number of comparative studies constrained the ability to perform subgroup and sensitivity analyses that could clarify effect modification by indication (e.g., acute MI vs decompensated heart failure), center experience, or concurrent therapies. Additionally, all studies included in our meta-analysis were conducted within the United States, reflecting our deliberate focus on outcomes stratified according to the FDA-approved 14-day duration limit for Impella 5.5 use. Importantly, although the device carries similar clinical indications in both regions, the regulatory approvals differ in the United States; the Impella 5.5 is FDA-approved for up to 14 days of temporary MCS, whereas in Europe it is CE-marked for use up to 30 days. Multiple European studies have consistently demonstrated favorable outcomes with prolonged Impella 5.0/5.5 support—including improved survival, effective bridging to durable LVAD, and successful bridging to heart transplantation—often extending support well beyond 14 days.15, 16 However, these European cohorts/registries37, 38, 39 could not be included in our quantitative analysis because they did not stratify outcomes by duration of support (≤14 vs >14 days), which was essential for the comparative framework of our study.

Finally, follow-up durations varied, and long-term outcomes (beyond hospitalization or 30-90 days) were incompletely reported in some studies, which limits insight into the durability of the observed associations and highlights the need for protocolized longitudinal assessments.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter registries with robust risk adjustment, and ideally randomized trials, are needed to validate the findings of our study and define the optimal threshold of Impella 5.5 duration and better isolate the effect of duration of Impella 5.5 support from underlying clinical indication. Standardized definitions of complications, uniform reporting of anticoagulation and infection-prevention practices, and longer-term follow-up (including functional status and quality of life) are essential to clarify net clinical benefit. Further research to optimize timing for transition from temporary to durable MCS or listing for transplant, as well as evaluation of infection- and bleeding-prevention bundles in prolonged Impella support, is warranted.

Throughout, clinicians must balance potential survival benefits against device-related risks and prioritize shared decision-making in this critically ill population.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, prolonged Impella 5.5 support beyond the FDA-recommended 14-day duration was associated with lower in-hospital mortality and a higher likelihood of successful bridging to heart transplantation, improving survival in select patients with cardiogenic shock, particularly those with chronic end-stage heart failure. These benefits were accompanied by increased risks of insertion-site hematoma, infection, and lower likelihood of native-heart recovery, likely reflecting the preferential use in patients with advanced heart-failure physiology.

Prolonged Impella 5.5 support may therefore serve as an effective bridge strategy when applied in experienced centers, with multidisciplinary expertise, vigilant complication prevention strategies, and timely transition to definitive therapies. Future prospective studies are needed to refine patient selection criteria, establish standardized monitoring for complications, and evaluate long-term functional outcomes associated with extended support strategies.
