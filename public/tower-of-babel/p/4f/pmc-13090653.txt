Yoga practice duration and its influence on type 2 diabetes management: A meta-analysis of Asian population
Authors: Anu Mahajan, Jhanvee Shah, Arti Muley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe study compares the effectiveness of yoga practice duration in managing type 2 diabetes (T2D) among the Asian population. Out of 664 results, 22 randomized clinical trials were identified with 1,720 participants, aged 18–65 years, with intervention periods ranging from 5.5 to 24 weeks. Yoga therapies of ≥12 weeks show significant decrease in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels (Standard Mean Deviation [SMD] = −0.70, p = 0.0001, I2 = 89%), while interventions of <12 weeks did not have statistically significant difference (SMD = −1.04, p = 0.28, I2 = 93%). Weight loss was significant for interventions <12 weeks (SMD = −0.60, p = 0.05, I2 = 0%) but not for those ≥12 weeks (SMD = −0.58, p = 0.17, I2 = 92%). Lipid profile changes varied, with significant reductions in the lipid profile (total cholesterol) (SMD = −0.34, p = 0.05, I2 = 85%) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL; SMD = −0.30, p = 0.05, I2 = 79%) for interventions of ≥12 weeks, while high-density lipoprotein (HDL; SMD = 0.37, p = 0.04, I2 = 85%) increased significantly in this group. Yoga interventions of ≥12 weeks were associated with significant improvements in glycemic control, particularly reductions in HbA1c, along with favorable changes in the lipid profile, among T2D individuals.Subject areas: applied sciences, biological sciences, physical activity

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents a major global public health challenge, with type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounting for more than 90% of all diabetes cases worldwide. The burden of T2D continues to rise across both developed and developing regions, driven by rapid urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, and increased consumption of energy-dense diets.1,2 According to global estimates, the burden of T2D continues to rise across both developed and developing regions, placing significant strain on healthcare systems and contributing to premature morbidity and mortality.3,4

Beyond hyperglycemia, T2D is associated with a spectrum of chronic complications, including neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, and an increased risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, substantially impairing the quality of life and increasing healthcare costs.5 These complications substantially impair functional capacity, reduce quality of life, and increase healthcare utilization. Effective long-term glycemic control is therefore essential not only for symptom management but also for preventing disease progression and complications.

Pharmacological interventions are the first line of treatment for managing blood sugar levels, including several oral hypoglycemic agents and insulin.6 However, long-term pharmacotherapy is often accompanied by adverse effects, variable adherence, and progressive loss of efficacy, highlighting the need for complementary lifestyle interventions that are sustainable and culturally acceptable.

Accumulating evidence indicates that Asian populations exhibit a distinct and heightened susceptibility to T2D, independent of overall obesity, because of genetics, visceral adiposity, and insulin resistance.1,2,7 Compared to Western populations, Asians develop T2D at lower body mass index (BMI) thresholds, primarily due to higher visceral adiposity, increased insulin resistance, and early β-cell dysfunction. This phenotype, often described as the “thin-fat Asian,” underscores the disproportionate metabolic risk despite relatively lower anthropometric measures.6 These ethnic-specific metabolic traits significantly influence the response to lifestyle and behavioral interventions. Interventions that improve insulin sensitivity, autonomic balance, and stress-mediated metabolic pathways may therefore yield differential effects in Asian populations.

In this context, yoga has emerged as a potential non-pharmacological adjunct for diabetes management. Yoga, a mind-body practice that has deep roots in Indian origin, not only improves physical health through the practice of asanas (physical exercise) but also significantly affects calming the body through breath-controlling exercise, pranayama, and meditation.8 These components collectively influence musculoskeletal activity, autonomic regulation, stress response, and metabolic homeostasis.9 Several studies have shown the positive effect of yoga on improving overall metabolic parameters, including blood sugar levels or improving lipid profiles.10,11,12 Many studies also advocate yoga as a non-pharmacological intervention for managing diabetes.13,14 A recent study with 13 clinical trials with 1,335 participants found that yoga significantly improves blood sugar levels, cholesterol levels, and anthropometric changes in type 2 diabetes (T2D) individuals.13 These findings suggest that yoga may be an effective complementary therapy that can be used in conjunction with traditional medical treatments without adverse side effects.

While many research studies focus specifically on the temporal aspect of yoga practice, the following research question is raised: “how well does the duration of yoga practice in the case of type 2 diabetes management predict its success?” In other words, it is essential to understand whether short-term practices have any similarly notable effects in the study in question, as do long-term ones. Thus, both immediate gains and long-term transformations prompted by yoga must be unveiled. This is valuable because, even though yoga has become an increasingly popular T2D treatment accompaniment, the temporally comparative aspect remains overlooked. Some studies prioritize short-term consequences, while others are devoted to revealing the benefits of long-term yoga versions.15,16,17 Therefore, a holistic understanding remains only partially disclosed.

Given the unique metabolic characteristics of Asian populations and the widespread cultural integration of yoga in this region, a systematic evaluation of duration-dependent effects of yoga practice is warranted. Clarifying whether longer durations of yoga practice confer greater and more sustained metabolic benefits has important implications for clinical recommendations, intervention design, and patient adherence.

By systematically synthesizing and quantitatively evaluating the available evidence in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, this meta-analysis aims to address existing uncertainties regarding the effects of yoga-based interventions on glycemic outcomes in individuals with T2D. Through duration-based stratification, the analysis seeks to distinguish short-term from sustained effects, thereby contributing to a clearer interpretation of temporal intervention efficacy. The findings are intended to inform clinical and public health perspectives by providing an evidence-based assessment of yoga as a potential adjunct to standard diabetes care, without implying causality or prescriptive recommendations. This synthesis may support future research, guideline development, and context-specific decision-making within culturally relevant diabetes management frameworks.

Primary objective

The primary objective of the study is to compare the effectiveness of yoga for less than 12 weeks versus more than 12 weeks of practice in managing T2D, specifically among the Asian population.

The methodological recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration17 were strictly followed to validate study quality, minimize bias, and ensure the reliability of pooled estimates. To evaluate the temporal effects of yoga interventions on glycemic outcomes, studies were stratified based on intervention duration (<12 weeks versus ≥12 weeks). This duration-based classification was biologically justified by the physiological properties of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), which reflects mean glycemic exposure over approximately 8–12 weeks in accordance with the erythrocyte lifespan (M1). Consequently, interventions of shorter duration may not adequately capture sustained changes in HbA1c, supporting the validity of this stratified analytical approach.

The complete study identification and selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1), ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the review methodology. The study identification and screening process is summarized in Figure 1. A total of 664 records were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect using the predefined search strategy. After automatic removal of duplicate records (n = 323), 341 unique studies remained for screening. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (J.S. and Anu Maajan) to validate the completeness and relevance of the search process.

PRISMA diagram

During title and abstract screening, 283 records were excluded for one or more of the following reasons: non-randomized study design, inclusion of non-diabetic or mixed populations without separate analysis for T2D, absence of yoga as the primary intervention, lack of relevant glycemic outcomes, or use of non-human subjects. The remaining 58 studies were subjected to full-text eligibility assessment.

Of the 58 full-text articles assessed, 36 were excluded for predefined reasons: ineligible study setting (n = 8), absence of prespecified outcomes (n = 6), lack of an appropriate comparator group (n = 4), exclusion of yoga as the intervention of interest (n = 3), inappropriate study design (n = 4), inclusion of non-diabetic populations (n = 5), and unavailability of full-text articles (n = 6). Following this rigorous, multi-stage screening process, 22 randomized controlled trials met all the inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review.

Study outcome

Primary outcome

The primary outcome includes changes in fasting blood glucose (FBG), post-prandial blood glucose (PPBG), HbA1c, insulin, and homeostasis model assessment-estimated insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcomes include anthropometric changes such as weight (Wt), waist circumference (WC), BMI, and improvement in the cardiac markers such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), and both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Study characteristics

The selected 22 studies were divided into two subgroups: <12 weeks of intervention, which included 9 studies, and ≥12 weeks of intervention, which included 13 studies. Overall, the total number of participants was 1,720. 1,329 samples (686 intervention and 643 control) were from ≥12 weeks of intervention, and 391 samples (intervention: 205, control: 186) were from <12 weeks of intervention. All the studies involved in the review were from the Asia continent. Most of the studies were from India, followed by Iran, Japan, and China. All the participants included were adults with T2D. The duration of yoga varied from 5.5 to 24 weeks. The results of risk of bias are summarized in Figures 2 and 3. Most studies demonstrated either a low risk of bias or some concerns, particularly in relation to the randomization process. Fourteen studies (63.6%) were judged to have low risk in the randomization domain, while eight studies (36.4%) raised some concerns, primarily due to limited reporting of allocation concealment. All studies were judged to have low risk for deviations from intended interventions. For missing outcome data and outcome measurement, twenty-one studies (95.5%) were assessed as low risk, with one study showing some concerns. Importantly, no study met the RoB 2 criteria for high risk of bias, and the overall risk-of-bias judgments were evenly distributed between low risk (50%) and some concerns (50%).

HbA1c: yoga interventions of ≥12 weeks duration resulted in a statistically significant reduction in HbA1c compared to control groups (Standard Mean Deviation [SMD] = −0.70, p = 0.0001), indicating improved long-term glycemic control. In contrast, interventions lasting <12 weeks did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in HbA1c (SMD = −1.04, p = 0.28), suggesting that sustained practice is required to influence chronic glucose regulation.

FBG: both short-term (<12 weeks) and long-term (≥12 weeks) yoga interventions were associated with significant reductions in FBG levels compared to controls (SMD = −1.01, p = 0.002; and SMD = −0.63, p < 0.00001, respectively). These findings suggest that yoga has a relatively rapid effect on basal glucose metabolism.

PPBG: similarly, yoga interventions significantly reduced PPBG irrespective of duration, with greater effect sizes observed in interventions lasting <12 weeks (SMD = −1.17, p < 0.0001) compared to ≥12 weeks (SMD = −0.75, p < 0.00001). This indicates improved post-meal glucose handling following yoga practice.

Insulin and HOMA-IR: only three studies assessed insulin resistance parameters. In the subgroup analysis of interventions lasting <12 weeks, yoga was associated with a marked reduction in HOMA-IR (SMD = −2.11, p < 0.0001), suggesting a substantial short-term improvement in insulin sensitivity. However, the limited number of studies precludes definitive conclusions regarding long-term effects on insulin dynamics.

Anthropometric measurements

Wt: weight loss from yoga interventions for less than 12 weeks was significantly lower than the yoga interventions for <12 weeks, resulting in modest but statistically significant reductions in body weight compared to controls (SMD = −0.60, p = 0.05). In contrast, interventions lasting ≥12 weeks did not show a significant effect on body weight (SMD = −0.58, p = 0.17).

WC: no significant difference in WC was observed between yoga and control groups for interventions lasting ≥12 weeks (SMD = −0.02, p = 0.92).

BMI: yoga did not significantly influence BMI regardless of intervention duration (<12 weeks: SMD = −0.20, p = 0.49; ≥12 weeks: SMD = −0.14, p = 0.38), suggesting that the metabolic benefits of yoga may occur independently of substantial changes in overall adiposity.

Lipid profile

TC: yoga therapies lasting more than 12 weeks significantly reduced TC compared to controls (SMD = −0.34, p = 0.05); however, for interventions lasting less than 12 weeks, the difference was not statistically significant (SMD = −0.44, p = 0.18).

TGs: regardless of the length of the intervention, there was no noticeable difference in TGs between the yoga and control groups (SMD = −0.19, p = 0.07 for ≥12 weeks; SMD = −0.17, p = 0.56 for <12 weeks).

LDL: like TC, yoga therapies lasting more than equal to 12 weeks significantly reduced LDL in comparison to controls (SMD = −0.30, p = 0.05); however, for interventions lasting less than 12 weeks, the result was not statistically significant (SMD = −0.17, p = 0.56).

HDL: compared to controls, yoga programs lasting 12 weeks or more significantly increased HDL (SMD = 0.37, p = 0.04); however, the difference was not statistically significant for interventions lasting less than 12 weeks (SMD = −0.19, p = 0.86).

VLDL: regardless of the length of the intervention, there was no discernible change in VLDL between the yoga and control groups (SMD = −0.09, p = 0.74 for ≥12 weeks; SMD = 0.24, p = 0.34 for <12 weeks).

Blood pressure

SBP: both short-term and long-term yoga interventions significantly reduced SBP compared to controls (<12 weeks: SMD = −1.18, p = 0.009; ≥12 weeks: SMD = −0.42, p = 0.03).

DBP: a significant reduction in DBP was observed only in interventions lasting <12 weeks (SMD = −0.62, p = 0.003). The effect was not statistically significant for interventions ≥12 weeks (SMD = −0.25, p = 0.09).

Summary of yoga’s effects on diabetes-related outcomes

Overall, yoga interventions demonstrated beneficial effects on multiple components of diabetes management, particularly glycemic control (FBG, PPBG, and HbA1c), insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and blood pressure. Short-term interventions appeared to exert rapid effects on glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance, whereas longer-term practice was necessary to achieve sustained improvements in HbA1c, lipid profile, and HDL cholesterol. Notably, these metabolic benefits were largely independent of significant changes in BMI or WC, suggesting that yoga may improve cardiometabolic health through mechanisms beyond weight loss.

Discussion

The present meta-analysis yields several critical findings regarding the effect of yoga on various health markers in the Asian population. Our review included 22 studies published in the last two decades, substantially increasing the overall evidence base. Quality assessment revealed that all included studies were either of low risk or had some concerns regarding bias, with none categorized as high risk. The results consistently highlight the critical role of yoga intervention duration, which significantly impacts outcomes across glycemic control, blood pressure, lipid profile, and anthropometric measurements.

Glycemic control and metabolic pathways

Consistent with prior research, our findings strongly support the use of yoga interventions for improving health markers in both healthy and diabetic individuals.39,40,41 Specifically, yoga significantly improved glycemic control, evidenced by reductions in both FBG and PPBG levels.

The metabolic benefits of yoga are likely mediated through multiple, interrelated physiological pathways. Practices such as slow pranayama, relaxation techniques, and meditation are known to shift autonomic balance toward parasympathetic (vagal) dominance.42,43 This enhanced vagal tone, operating through cholinergic pathways, improves pancreatic beta-cell responsiveness and insulin secretion.44 Concurrently, the observed reduction in sympathetic overactivity lowers hepatic glucose output and mitigates peripheral insulin resistance.

Furthermore, while changes in BMI were modest, yoga may preferentially reduce visceral and ectopic fat, which is metabolically more relevant than overall weight loss.45 Reduced visceral adiposity decreases the flux of free fatty acids to the liver and pancreas, thereby decreasing lipotoxicity and enhancing insulin signaling.46

The duration of practice is a critical factor. The effect of yoga on long-term glycemic markers, specifically HbA1c, was evident only with interventions lasting more than 12 weeks. Shorter durations (less than 12 weeks) showed an insignificant effect on improving HbA1c, a finding corroborated by a recent systematic review of 13 studies.47,48 This suggests that duration reflects cumulative physiological exposure, allowing for time-dependent adaptations in insulin sensitivity and metabolic regulation to consolidate, leading to more consistent improvements in long-term markers like HbA1c. Conversely, protocol composition—such as the difference between slow (parasympathetic-enhancing) and fast (sympathetic-enhancing) pranayama—determines the mechanistic direction of these effects.29 Future research should incorporate protocol-specific analyses to refine evidence-based recommendations.

Lipid profile and cardiometabolic parameters

Our findings regarding cardiometabolic parameters align with evidence that prolonged yoga practice positively impacts lipid profiles and blood pressure. Significant decreases in LDL, elevations in HDL, and reductions in TC were seen only in treatments lasting longer than 12 weeks, underscoring the duration dependence of these lipid-modifying effects.44 Conversely, no appreciable variations were observed in VLDL or TGs. This trend is consistent with a pooled study of 15 studies (1,161 participants) showing that yoga reduces TC and TGs in adults with central obesity.45

For blood pressure, both SBP and DBP showed substantial decreases for interventions of both lengths (less than and greater than 12 weeks), suggesting that blood pressure response may be less duration-dependent than lipid parameters, though DBP in interventions longer than 12 weeks did not reach significance. Even short-term interventions (e.g., 1 month) have been shown to significantly reduce blood pressure.47

Regarding anthropometric parameters, weight decreased significantly with yoga interventions of less than 12 weeks, whereas no significant effects were observed with interventions lasting more than 12 weeks. This inverse, duration-dependent finding for weight underscores the need to consider intervention duration when assessing yoga’s diverse impact on various health markers: sustainability and holistic benefits. The sustainability of yoga practice is paramount for achieving and maintaining long-term health benefits. Short-term practice often yields transient benefits that are insufficient for lasting changes, whereas long-term practice is associated with more pronounced and sustained improvements. For example, a 1-year randomized clinical trial demonstrated that yoga was more effective than other exercises in reducing HbA1c levels and managing comorbidities.48

Sustainability is critical not only for metabolic control but also for quality of life. Participants reported improved quality of life and reduced reliance on pharmacological drugs for diabetes management, with one study showing a reduction in drug intake28 and improved quality and duration of sleep.27 The holistic nature of yoga—integrating physical exercise, breathing, mindfulness, and gratitude—fosters physical and psychological resilience against diabetes-related stressors. This is supported by studies showing that yoga significantly reduces stress levels and increases mindfulness. Beyond the glycemic and lipemic profiles, this study also shows improvement in renal parameters (creatinine levels)23 and antioxidant status.26,35 Maintaining a consistent yoga routine reinforces physiological benefits, promotes essential behavioral changes (increased physical activity and improved stress management), and, when combined with healthier dietary choices, provides an effective and sustainable strategy for T2D management.

Based on pooled evidence from randomized controlled trials, yoga should be considered a supportive adjunct to standard care for individuals with T2D. The meta-analysis indicates that interventions of ≥12 weeks are required for significant improvements in long-term markers like HbA1c and lipid profiles, whereas shorter durations predominantly influence fasting and post-prandial glucose levels and short-term weight loss. These duration-dependent effects are consistent with established physiological mechanisms, underscoring the need for sustained and appropriately structured yoga interventions in clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

The current study has several advantages. First, short-term (less than 12 weeks) and long-term (more than 12 weeks) yoga therapies were thoroughly evaluated for their effects on different cardiometabolic parameters in diabetic participants, which was the first of their kind. The reviews adhere to the Cochrane and PRISMA guidelines, which improve the validity and consistency of their conclusions. 22 studies with a low risk of bias were included. Numerous outcomes were evaluated in the review, such as blood pressure, anthropometric measures, insulin sensitivity, lipid profile, and glycemic management. This enables a comprehensive understanding of yoga’s effects on T2D management.

There were also a few restrictions on this study. First, the types, frequencies, and lengths of the yoga interventions varied across the included studies, limiting the broad applicability of the results. Second, the review excluded pertinent papers published in languages other than English and only considered studies published in English, which may have introduced a linguistic bias. Finally, the study did not consider additional lifestyle factors, such as diet and physical activity, that may have influenced the results in the included studies. This meta-analysis was restricted to studies conducted in Asian populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to non-Asian ethnic groups. However, this restriction was intentional, given the distinct metabolic characteristics of Asian populations, including higher insulin resistance and visceral adiposity at lower BMI thresholds. Future studies should evaluate whether the duration-dependent effects observed in this review are consistent across diverse ethnic populations.

Conclusion

The comprehensive review of the study’s findings emphasizes a critical relationship between the duration and consistency of yoga practices and their efficacy in managing T2D. While a short-term yoga intervention, typically defined as lasting less than 12 weeks, is shown to yield initial positive effects, such as reductions in FBG and PPBG levels, these benefits are often temporary and may not fully impact all key physiological parameters associated with the disease.

Conversely, the data strongly suggest that long-term yoga practices, extending for a duration of 12 weeks or longer, are associated with significantly more pronounced, enduring, and holistic benefits. Participants who engaged in these longer interventions demonstrated a statistically significant improvement not only in FBG and PPBG levels but, more importantly, in the primary long-term marker of glycemic control: HbA1c. This improvement in HbA1c is a crucial indicator of sustained blood sugar management over several months, highlighting the value of commitment to the practice.

Furthermore, the review uncovered that these longer-term yoga interventions (lasting at least 12 weeks) provided systemic benefits beyond just glucose regulation. They were consistently linked to improvements in other significant cardiometabolic risk factors, including a better lipid profile, lower blood pressure readings, and a reduction in inflammatory biomarkers when compared to control groups. Although shorter yoga programs (less than 12 weeks) showed occasional positive trends in these secondary outcomes, the results lacked the consistency and overall strength seen in the longer programs.

In conclusion, the collective evidence firmly establishes yoga as an effective complementary therapy for the management of type 2 diabetes. The most substantial and comprehensive therapeutic benefits are clearly achieved with yoga programs that maintain a duration of 12 weeks or longer for metabolic consolidation. These findings provide a clear evidence-based recommendation for clinicians to integrate long-term yoga practice into a comprehensive diabetes management plan, especially for patients seeking to incorporate a sustainable mind-body discipline into their ongoing care regimen.

Future recommendations

The researchers may consider the following future recommendations to address the shortcomings and further strengthen the evidence. This includes applying formal dose-response or meta-regression approaches using continuous exposure variables (e.g., total weeks of intervention and weekly practice minutes) to more precisely characterize the relationship between yoga dose and metabolic outcomes. Additionally, future trials and secondary analyses should explicitly differentiate yoga modalities (e.g., posture-based, breathing-based, meditation-based, or multicomponent protocols) to determine whether specific components confer differential benefits.

In addition, studies incorporating stratification by baseline glycemic severity, duration of diabetes, and treatment status (e.g., insulin use)—preferably through individual participant data or standardized subgroup reporting—would help clarify which patient subgroups derive the greatest benefit from longer-duration yoga practice. Future trials should also report adherence, attendance, and fidelity of intervention delivery to distinguish prescribed duration from actual exposure.

Finally, research with longer follow-up periods (≥6 months) is needed to assess the durability of effects and relapse risk, and to explore yoga’s influence on broader clinically meaningful outcomes such as medication use, quality of life, and diabetes-related complications, as well as its potential synergistic effects with other lifestyle interventions (e.g., dietary modification and stress management).

Method details

Protocol and reporting guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines and followed methodological recommendations outlined by the Cochrane Collaboration. The study was designed to evaluate duration-dependent effects of yoga interventions on metabolic outcomes in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) within Asian populations.

Eligibility criteria

Study eligibility was determined using the PICOS framework. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with T2DM were included. Only studies conducted in Asian populations were eligible. The intervention consisted of yoga-based practices, including multicomponent yoga programs, posture-based yoga (asanas), breathing techniques (pranayama), meditation, or laughter-based yoga. Comparator groups included usual care or no intervention.

Primary outcomes included glycemic control measures such as fasting blood glucose (FBG), postprandial blood glucose (PPBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin levels, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Secondary outcomes included anthropometric parameters (weight, BMI, waist circumference), lipid profile markers (TC, TG, LDL, HDL, VLDL), and blood pressure (SBP and DBP).

Studies published in English between 2004 and 2024 were considered. Non-randomized studies, observational studies, conference abstracts, reviews, editorials, studies involving non-Asian populations, pregnant individuals, adolescents (<17 years), elderly participants (>75 years), or participants with severe comorbid conditions were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect databases from January 2004 to December 2024. The search strategy incorporated relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords including “yoga,” “yoga therapy,” “yoga exercise,” “type 2 diabetes,” “T2DM,” “HbA1c,” “glycemic control,” “blood glucose,” and “insulin resistance.”

Search results were limited to human clinical trials published in English with full-text availability. All identified records were imported into Covidence systematic review software for screening and duplicate removal.

Study selection process

Two reviewers (AM, JM) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Potentially relevant studies underwent full-text assessment against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with adjudication by the corresponding author when necessary. The complete study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. Extracted data included author name, publication year, country, sample size, participant characteristics, diagnostic criteria, duration and components of yoga intervention, comparator details, and reported metabolic outcomes. When required, means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted or calculated for meta-analysis. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of reported results. Each domain was classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. Overall risk of bias was determined according to RoB 2 guidance. Assessments were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Outcomes and duration-based stratification

To examine temporal effects, studies were stratified into two categories based on intervention duration: short-term (<12 weeks) and long-term (≥12 weeks). This classification was biologically justified by the physiological lifespan of erythrocytes, as HbA1c reflects average glycemic exposure over approximately 8–12 weeks. Therefore, interventions shorter than 12 weeks may not fully capture sustained changes in HbA1c levels.

Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed separately within each duration subgroup.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) software. Continuous outcomes were pooled using a random-effects model to account for between-study variability. Standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each outcome when at least two studies reported comparable data.

A negative SMD indicated beneficial effects of yoga relative to control for outcomes such as HbA1c, FBG, PPBG, lipid parameters, and blood pressure. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05.

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and Chi-square (χ2) test. Heterogeneity thresholds were interpreted as follows: 0–24% (low), 25–49% (moderate), 50–74% (substantial), and ≥75% (considerable). Where substantial heterogeneity was observed, subgroup analyses based on intervention duration were conducted. Heterogeneity was considered to be significant at a p-value of ≤0.05.

Publication bias was not formally assessed due to the limited number of studies available for certain outcomes within subgroup analyses.
