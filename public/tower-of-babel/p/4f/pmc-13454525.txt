Early sleep after action observation and motor imagery improved manual dexterity in patients after surgery for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures
Authors: Federico Temporiti, Alessia Bertola, Fabio Mattavelli, Andrea Gusella, Simona Vecchi, Alessandro Pozzi, Giorgio Pivato, Roberto Gatti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures induces a decay in manual dexterity. Action Observation and Motor Imagery (AOMI) mitigate motor performance decline during hand immobilization, and early sleep after such approaches has been reported to further enhance the consolidation of motor learning in healthy subjects. Here, we investigated the effects of early sleep after AOMI training sessions in subjects with immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures. Fifty-four subjects with immobilization of fourth and fifth fingers after fixation of metacarpal and phalangeal fractures were randomized into 3 groups undergoing a 2-week training. AOMI-sleep and AOMI-control groups performed AOMI of transitive manual dexterity tasks between 8:00 and 10:00 p.m. or between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. respectively, while Control group observed landscape video-clips before sleeping. AOMI-sleep group demonstrated greater manual dexterity improvements than AOMI-control and Control groups at immobilization and training end (T1) and 1-month follow-up (T2). AOMI-sleep group revealed higher active range of motion of fourth and fifth fingers at T1 and T2, and greater hand grip strength at T2 than Control group. These findings suggested that AOMI followed by early sleep during the postoperative immobilization period may improve outcomes of subjects with metacarpal and phalangeal fractures.

Introduction

Metacarpal and phalangeal fractures represent common traumatic injuries in adult population, which sometimes require surgical management and a subsequent immobilization period1. Immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures has been reported to induce a decay in hand performance, leading to manual dexterity deficits that persist over time after splint removal2,3. Such deficits have been described to limit upper limb integration in routinary daily activities, requiring personalized rehabilitative interventions to enhance motor and functional recovery at immobilization end2–5. In fact, immobilization has been reported to modulate use-dependent brain plasticity and deteriorate sensorimotor abilities, leading to a decay in hand movement accuracy and tactile function6. Literature data have proved that a few hours of index finger immobilization induced a decrease in corticospinal excitability in healthy subjects7. Furthermore, neurophysiological investigations have demonstrated that immobilization of the ring and little fingers of the hand resulted in decrease of motor cortex excitability of the representation of immobilized hand muscles and at the level of muscles with potential overlapping motor representations. Moreover, light-to-mild limitation of use of the whole hand has been reported in these subjects8.

Mirror Neuron System based interventions such as Action Observation and Motor Imagery (AOMI) have been reported to mitigate motor performance decline induced by upper limb restraint in healthy subjects and individuals with hand immobilization for musculoskeletal conditions9–12. AO implies the observation of video-clips including motor contents, while MI consists of the mental rehearsal of the observed tasks in the absence of execution13,14. Indeed, the observation and imagination of actions have been described to activate Mirror Neuron System substrates responsible for action execution. These brain activations have been reported to promote the acquisition or improvement of motor skills through the building of a motor memory and allow for training the sensorimotor system when active movements are not possible9,11,15.

When considering strategies to maintain or enhance motor abilities in the absence of active movements performance, literature data also suggested that early sleep delivered after AOMI training sessions boosted improvements in manual dexterity in healthy subjects16. In particular, sleep has been reported to facilitate memory consolidation of motor patterns acquired through the systematic observation and imagination of motor tasks, transforming newly acquired motor memories into more stable memory traces16,17. Surprisingly, Temporiti and co-workers also reported additional manual dexterity gains at 1 month after an AOMI-intervention end, highlighting an optimization of motor performance promoted by early sleep after training end16.

Against this background, it is reasonable to speculate that an AOMI-training followed by early sleep after each training session delivered in the immobilization period would mitigate manual dexterity deficits and enhance hand performance in subjects with surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures However, the lack of investigation of the effects of early sleep after AOMI-training sessions on manual dexterity in subjects undergoing immobilization after fixation surgery for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures represents a gap of knowledge. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the effects of early sleep after AOMI training sessions in subjects with immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures.

Methods

Participants

Fifty-four participants were enrolled between April 2024 and December 2025 according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) age between 18 and 60 years, (2) fixation surgery for fractures of fourth or fifth metacarpal or proximal phalanx bones of the dominant hand, (3) right-hand dominance according to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory18. Exclusion criteria were damage of additional upper limb structures requiring surgical repair (e.g., ligaments, tendons or nerves), concurrent musculoskeletal or neurological conditions affecting upper limb function, habitual practice of sports, jobs or hobbies requiring advanced manual skills or nocturnal sleep deprivation, diagnosis of psychiatric conditions or sleep disorders (e.g., insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, REM or non-REM behavior disorders) or intake of medications influencing the physiological sleep pattern. Moreover, participants with post-surgical complications such as infection, complex pain regional syndrome, tendon adhesion or screws bending were excluded.

Ethical approval was obtained from the responsible Ethical Committee of the IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital prior to the start of the study (approval number: CLF23/03, September 2023). All participants provided written informed consent. The study design and conduct comply with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki and were prospectively registered as a clinical trial on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06306885, registration date: 12/03/2024). Informed consent from participants or their legal guardians for publication of identifying information/images in an online open-access publication was also obtained.

Study design and intervention

The study was a three-armed single-blind randomized controlled trial carried out at the Hand Surgery and Physiotherapy Units and Motion Analysis Lab of the IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital of Milan, Italy. Participants were enrolled from among patients admitted to the Emergency Department of our Institute undergoing surgery for fractures of fourth or fifth metacarpal or proximal phalanx bones. Surgical procedures were carried out under brachial plexus regional anesthesia by 3 orthopedic surgeons of the same Unit using open reduction and internal fixation technique with screw-and-plate fixation and Kirschner wires19. After the first postoperative week, patients underwent a check-up and were asked to wear a static ulnar gutter thermoplastic splint for the subsequent 2 weeks. In particular, the splint was standardized among all patients and included the fourth and fifth fingers, second, third, fourth and fifth metacarpal bones, all carpal bones and wrist (Fig. 1). During the check-up, patients were assessed for eligibility by a researcher blinded to the randomization list to ensure allocation concealment. In particular, allocation concealment was implemented using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by a statistician according to a simple computer-generated random sequence and opened sequentially after the enrollment of each participant. Specifically, participants were randomized into AOMI-sleep (n = 18), AOMI-control (n = 18) and Control (n = 18) groups and instructed by a hand therapist on the training to be executed during the immobilization period. All participants were asked to observe 16-min video-clips, 6 days per week, for 2 weeks (12 sessions). Participants allocated to AOMI-sleep and AOMI-control groups watched video-clips showing 4 transitive manual dexterity tasks performed with the right hand by age and gender-matched healthy subjects and delivered from a third (1 min per task) and first-person (3 min per task) perspectives20,21. During first-person perspective observation, participants were also asked to perform synchronous kinesthetic motor imagery of the observed task22,23. Participants were asked to stay as still as possible during AOMI-training sessions and complexity of observed and imagined tasks increased from the first to the second week of training. In particular, tasks of the first week included hammering a nail, screwing in a bolt, screwing in a screw with a screwdriver, typing on a keyboard, while motor contents of the second week included buttoning up the cuff of a shirt, removing a pill from its packaging, picking up a pill with tweezers and threading a thread through the eye of a needle16. AOMI-sleep and AOMI-control groups underwent the same training, but AOMI-sleep group performed the training sessions between 8:00 and 10:00 p.m., whereas AOMI-control group performed the training sessions between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. Participants allocated to the Control group observed video-clips showing landscapes without motor contents between 8:00 and 10:00 p.m. Moreover, participants allocated to AOMI-sleep and Control group were asked to go to sleep within 1 h after training execution. All participants were asked to avoid daytime sleep during the training period, the use of electronic devices (e.g., laptop, TV or smartphone) before sleeping and lifestyle changes able to affect sleep habits. After the 2-week immobilization period, participants’ splint was removed, and they started a standardized rehabilitative program under the supervision of a hand therapist with more than 10 years of experience. The program consisted of 2 sessions per week for 4 weeks and included passive mobilizations and active exercises aimed at increasing range of motion of metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, tendon-gliding exercises to prevent restrictive adhesions and manual dexterity and grip strength task-oriented exercises24.

Assessment

Participants were assessed for manual dexterity, fingers active range of motion, grip strength, pain, upper limb function and quality of life by a hand therapist with more than 10 years of experience and blinded to allocation group.

Manual dexterity was assessed using the Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) as primary outcome and the Nine Hole Peg Test (NHPT) with kinematic indexes. During PPT, participants were asked to insert pegs into holes using the right (R), left (L), both hands (B) and assemble pegs, collars and washers through bimanual hand movements (Assembly) as quickly as possible. They had 30 s to accomplish the R, L and B tasks, while 60 s were assigned to execute the Assembly task. The number of inserted pegs is the score, and higher values indicate better manual dexterity. Participants performed 3 trials for each task, and a mean score was computed25. During NHPT, participants had to grasp 9 pegs from a container placed on a table, insert them into a nine-hole grid, and replace the pegs into the container as quickly as possible. An 8-camera optoelectronic system (SMART-DX, BTS, Italy) was used to record the performance. Retro-reflective markers were placed on both acromial angles and jugular incisura to define the trunk, on the right and left middle phalanx of the index finger to track hand trajectories and on the left distal and left and right proximal corners of the table to define the global reference system. Kinematic data were filtered using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter (cut-off 4 Hz) and the following indexes were calculated adopting the algorithms described by Temporiti and co-workers: total and single phases times (peg-grasp, peg-transfer, peg-in-hole, hand-return and removing), and normalized jerk, mean and peak of velocity during peg-grasp and hand-return phases26. After a familiarization trial, 2 trials per side were performed, and the shortest trial was considered.

Range of motion was assessed in terms of total active motion (TAM) at the level of the fourth and fifth fingers using a digital goniometer according to the American Society of Hand Therapists recommendations and standardized procedures described by Lewis and co-workers27,28. In particular, TAM was computed as the difference between the total degrees of maximum active flexion and the total extension deficit across the aforementioned joints of each finger of the right hand, normalized for the TAM of the same fingers of the unaffected hand. Finally, TAM of the fractured finger and immobilized fingers were reported.

Grip strength was assessed through the Jamar (Lafayette Instrument Co., Lafayette, Indiana) dynamometer according to the American Society of Hand Therapists recommendations27. Three trials were performed, the mean score was computed and normalized for the score of the unaffected hand29,30.

Perceived pain was investigated at rest and during the PPT using the Visual Analogue Scale, upper limb function was assessed using the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (Quick DASH), while quality of life was assessed through European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions (EQ5D) including the EQ5D descriptive system and EQ5D-VAS31,32.

The PPT, NHPT with kinematic indexes, VAS, Quick DASH and EQ5D were administered at participants’ enrollment during the check-up (1 week after surgery, T0), immobilization and training end (3 weeks after surgery, T1) and rehabilitative program end (7 weeks after surgery, T2). Moreover, TAM was collected at T1 and T2, while grip strength was assessed at T2. Finally, participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics and imaginative abilities (Kinesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire, KVIQ) were assessed at baseline, while the number of sleep hours per night and sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, PSQI) during training period were also collected at T1.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated a-priori based on PPT as primary outcome. It was estimated that a total of 18 participants per group was required to achieve an effect size of Cohen’s d = 1 with an alpha error of 5% and 80% power between AOMI-sleep group and AOMI-control group at T133. Data were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and missing data were handled using the worst-case scenario method. One-way ANOVA and Chi-Square test were used to investigate between-group differences in terms of participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline, PSQI and number of sleep hours per night during the training period. A General Linear Model with Time as within-subject factor and Group as between-subject factor was used to analyze between-group differences over time in terms of PPT, NHPT with kinematic indexes, TAM, Quick DASH and EQ5D. Moreover, Kruskal–Wallis and Friedman tests with post-hoc Bonferroni tests were used to investigate between-group and within-group differences in terms of pain. Finally, One-way ANOVA was used to investigate between-group differences in terms of hand grip strength. In the presence of significant interactions or main effects, Bonferroni post-hoc tests were carried out. The effect size was computed for the primary outcome and expressed as mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval (CI95) and Cohen’s d. Cohen’s d was interpreted as small (between 0.2 and 0.5), medium (between 0.5 and 0.8), large (between 0.8 and 1.3) and very large (greater than 1.3)33. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 30.0 for Windows and the level of statistical significance was set at alpha = 0.05.

Manual dexterity

Discussion

Early sleep after AOMI training sessions enhanced manual dexterity in subjects with immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures. In fact, subjects who underwent AOMI-training followed by early sleep during the immobilization period had better manual dexterity than subjects who underwent the same training at least 12 h before sleeping or control intervention.

The current results demonstrated the ability of Mirror Neuron System based approaches followed by early sleep to mitigate the detrimental effects of restricted hand use during immobilization after hand surgery. Motor learning promoted by AOMI occurred thanks to the Mirror Neuron System peculiarity to show an activity during observation, imagination and execution of motor tasks15. In particular, the observation of actions recruits neural substrates involved in motor execution through a mirror mechanism. This mechanism allows the central nervous system to encode visual inputs into a motor representation of observed acts, promoting motor memory formation11,34. Moreover, the imagination of a task just observed prolongs the Mirror Neuron System recruitment in time and extends neural activity to a wider brain network, further enhancing motor learning11,15. Furthermore, early sleep occurrence after each training session led to increased consolidation of motor patterns acquired through AOMI-training. The current study results are consistent with literature data reporting that early sleep after AOMI boosted manual dexterity gains in healthy subjects and extended the applicability of such intervention to subjects with surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures in order to avoid the decay in hand performance and speed up the subsequent rehabilitation program after immobilization end. However, it is worth acknowledging that adequate sleep monitoring and supervision during training sessions may be challenging in clinical practice. Interestingly, better manual dexterity promoted by early sleep after AOMI training sessions resulted to be more marked especially at 1-month follow-up. In fact, this intervention seemed to minimize motor skills decay as well as boost the neural competencies required during post-immobilization rehabilitation through the building of an adaptive linking between sensory inputs and pre-existing motor patterns able to facilitate motor relearning induced by the rehabilitative pathway9,11,14,34. Moreover, higher manual dexterity shown by AOMI-sleep group at 1-month after immobilization end may reflect additional advantages in terms of motor control, which have been amplified after partial resolution of peripheral impairments such as joint stiffness and oedema24,35. Surprisingly, subjects who underwent early sleep after AOMI training sessions also revealed manual dexterity improvements at the level of the untrained hand, suggesting a cross-effect induced by MNS-based intervention. This phenomenon may also explain better hand performance achieved by AOMI-sleep group during bimanual dexterity tasks16,34,36.

Active range of motion at immobilization end resulted to be higher in subjects who underwent AOMI followed by early sleep. When considering neural mechanisms subtended to hand movements, assessment procedures imply the request to perform isolated finger movements requiring adequate coordination and neuromuscular activity of digital muscles37,38. Therefore, the stimulation of the sensorimotor system induced by AOMI followed by early sleep during the period of restricted hand use may have mitigated neuromuscular control decay, enabling higher range of motion during the request of active fingers movements after immobilization. Furthermore, higher grip strength at the end of the rehabilitative program in AOMI-sleep group supported the ability of such intervention to increase the neuromuscular drive through a facilitation of motor unit recruitment and/or discharge rate39,40. Our results also suggested a sleep-dependent maximization of such gains at 1-month follow-up. It is worth noting that no specific grip strength tasks were delivered to participants during the AOMI-training, and these findings may appear in contrast with studies reporting that AOMI-benefits are usually task-specific41. However, some trained tasks included the observation and mental simulation of upper limb tasks requiring participants to grasp an object (e.g., hammer, screwdriver) with grip strength levels reasonably closer to their own maximal strength. Literature data have shown that AOMI of tasks requiring an increase in force output generation effort led to an increase in corticospinal excitability42. Therefore, ameliorations in grip strength detected in the current study may derive from neuroplastic changes leading to higher corticospinal conduction and enhancement in motor unit firing rates39.

Finally, when considering sleep-dependent benefits induced by AOMI-training in the study population, no between-group differences were found in terms of upper limb function and quality of life. The lack of motor improvements transfer to disability and quality of life domains may depend on the use of adaptations with the unaffected upper limb during daily activities in the study population43. In fact, adaptive strategies may have led to compensating for upper limb disability and maintaining a satisfactory quality of life.

Some limitations of the current study deserve to be highlighted. First, subjects were enrolled from a single center, sample size was relatively small and potential adherence bias cannot be excluded, limiting the generalizability of the current study results. Second, training sessions were unsupervised, and fluctuations in attentive abilities during the training cannot be excluded. However, patients’ adherence was ensured through a diary and daily messages on a smartphone and a standardized education session on the training was delivered after the first evaluation session. Second, patients’ chronotype was not checked using specific tools and different chronotype among groups might have affected the results. However, such potential differences were expected to be equally distributed among groups, based on the randomized design of the study. Finally, sleep quality and quantity were monitored using self-reported tools during training period and no objective sleep measures able to assess sleep architecture and onset were collected. Indeed, neurophysiological assessments during sleep might have ensured higher accuracy in monitoring sleep architecture and motor learning correlates induced by MNS-based interventions.

Conclusions

The current study findings suggested that AOMI followed by early sleep after each session improved manual dexterity in subjects with immobilization after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures. This approach may represent an easily applicable and cost-effective approach to mitigate manual dexterity decay during immobilization period and enhance recovery in patients after surgical fixation for metacarpal and phalangeal fractures.
