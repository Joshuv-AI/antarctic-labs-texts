Letter to the Editor regarding “Effect of thoracic paravertebral block and erector spinae plane block on postoperative gastrointestinal function”
Authors: Shuya Tang
Published: 2025

Dear Editor

I read with considerable interest the study examining the effects of thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) and erector spinae plane block (ESPB) on gastrointestinal function following thoracoscopic lobectomy [1]. While the authors demonstrate meaningful improvements in gastrointestinal recovery with regional anaesthesia techniques, I wish to highlight several methodological concerns regarding baseline characterization and subgroup analysis that may limit the clinical applicability of these findings.

My primary concern centres on the inadequate baseline gastrointestinal assessment. The study lacks documentation of preoperative bowel function patterns, gastric emptying capacity and baseline autonomic function status. Given that recent gastroenterological research emphasizes the critical role of autonomic modulation in gut function, this omission becomes particularly relevant for thoracic regional blocks that potentially influence sympathetic innervation pathways. Additionally, the absence of standardized nutritional screening represents a significant gap, as malnutrition substantially affects perioperative gastrointestinal outcomes independent of anaesthetic technique [2].

Regarding subgroup analysis, the study fails to stratify patients based on clinically relevant variables that could substantially influence outcomes. Age stratification represents a critical oversight, considering that elderly patients demonstrate fundamentally different gastrointestinal physiology, altered drug pharmacokinetics and modified autonomic responses compared to younger populations. Similarly, the research overlooks systematic assessment of relevant comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, gastroesophageal reflux disease and inflammatory conditions that could interact unpredictably with regional anaesthesia effects on gut function [3,4].

The investigation also lacks consideration of surgical complexity variables such as operative duration, anatomical challenges or conversion rates [5], which generate varying inflammatory responses that could confound gastrointestinal recovery patterns. Furthermore, chronic medication use, particularly proton pump inhibitors, prokinetic agents or opioid therapy, receives no systematic evaluation despite their fundamental influence on baseline gastrointestinal function.

I recommend that future studies incorporate comprehensive preoperative gastrointestinal assessment protocols, systematic nutritional screening and stratified analyses based on age, comorbidities and surgical complexity. Such methodological enhancements would provide more robust evidence for clinical decision-making and improve the generalizability of findings to diverse patient populations encountered in routine thoracic surgical practice.
