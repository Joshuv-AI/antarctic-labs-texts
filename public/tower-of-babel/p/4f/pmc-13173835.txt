Challenging diagnosis: prolidase deficiency presenting as nonhealing ulcers and pancytopenia complicated by gluten enteropathy—a case report
Authors: Viplove Gupta, Vaibhav Yadav, Ved Prakash Pandey, Sanjay Dubey, Yogendra Jamra, Akash Pawar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlidase deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder resulting from mutations in the PEPD gene, leading to impaired collagen turnover and a broad spectrum of clinical features. Its manifestations include recurrent ulcerative skin lesions, recurrent infections, and hematological abnormalities. Celiac disease, another multisystem disorder, presents with chronic diarrhea, malabsorption, and iron deficiency anemia. The coexistence of both conditions is exceedingly rare and may lead to diagnostic delays.Case presentationWe report the case of an 18-year-old male individual of Asian Indian ethnicity from a rural background with lifelong ulcerative skin lesions, recurrent infections, and chronic diarrhea. At age 15 years, he was diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia, requiring multiple blood transfusions. Investigations revealed raised anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies and villous atrophy on duodenal biopsy, confirming celiac disease. Despite gluten-free diet initiation, his symptoms persisted. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a homozygous pathogenic splice site mutation (c.504-2A > G) in the PEPD gene, confirming prolidase deficiency. The patient was treated with dietary modifications, iron, vitamin C, manganese supplementation, and oral l-proline powder. Significant healing of skin ulcers and hematological improvement were noted at 6 months.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of considering rare metabolic disorders such as prolidase deficiency in patients with chronic skin lesions, infections, and pancytopenia. Early genetic testing and multidisciplinary management are essential when faced with overlapping clinical features.

Introduction

Prolidase deficiency (PD), caused by mutations in the PEPD gene [1], impairs the metabolism of proline-containing dipeptides, leading to a range of clinical manifestations, including dermatological, immunological, and hematological abnormalities. This report presents an early diagnostic overview of a young male individual with recurrent soft tissue infections that were further complicated by nutritional deficiency, leading to iron deficiency anemia, which, on evaluation, turned out to be gluten enteropathy. The final diagnosis of prolidase deficiency with gluten enteropathy (celiac disease) was made only after extensive clinical and genetic evaluations [2].

Case presentation

An 18-year-old male individual of Asian Indian ethnicity from a rural background in central India presented to our clinic with a lifelong history of recurrent skin lesions and infections. The patient was born at home after a full-term pregnancy with no immediate postnatal complications.

Clinical history and presenting complaints: Since the third day of life, the patient experienced recurrent ulcerative skin lesions that primarily affected his scalp, face, upper limbs, trunk, and bilateral lower limbs. The lesions were described as painful, itchy, and often associated with pus discharge or bleeding. These ulcers recurred every 2–3 months and typically required medical intervention. The lesions healed over a period of 1–2 months, leaving behind scar marks.

In addition to his dermatological complaints, the patient had recurrent respiratory tract infections, including episodes of cough, cold, and ear discharges. These episodes, associated with fever and pain, began around 3–4 years of age and occurred every 1–2 months, often necessitating medical treatment.

The patient also reported frequent episodes of loose stools since childhood, occurring every 2–3 months. These episodes were not associated with fever or blood and rarely required medical intervention [2, 3].

Clinical timeline

Hematological and gastrointestinal findings: At the age of 15 years, the patient presented with progressive generalized weakness and shortness of breath and was diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia. He was hospitalized and treated with two units of red cell concentrate (RCC), leading to an improvement in his symptoms. Over the following 3 years, he required additional transfusions, receiving a total of six units of RCC by the age of 18 years.

Despite receiving iron supplementation, the patient’s iron deficiency anemia recurred. There was no history of gastrointestinal bleeding, hematemesis, or other signs of overt blood loss.

Family and personal history: The patient’s brother has an intellectual disability (intelligence quotient [IQ] score 72) since childhood, and his mother died in a road traffic accident. The patient himself was educated only up to primary school. He followed a vegetarian diet, had a normal appetite, and reported no significant alterations in bowel or bladder habits.

Physical examination: On examination, the patient was conscious and cooperative, weighing 54 kg and standing 164 cm tall (body mass index [BMI]: 19.1 kg/m2). Pallor was evident, and edema was present over the right lower limb, but no cyanosis, lymphadenopathy, or jaundice were detected. His cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal examinations were unremarkable except for splenomegaly confirmed by ultrasound, with the spleen measuring 17 cm.

This patient was initially evaluated for differentials such as primary immunodeficiency syndrome, Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome, and inherited metabolic disorders. However, upper gastrointestinal (GI) biopsy revealed villous atrophy with crypt hyperplasia, and tissue transglutaminase antibodies were strongly positive—suggestive of celiac disease. Further clinical exome sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencing platform using paired-end 2 × 150 chemistry. It revealed a homozygous, likely pathogenic, splice site mutation (c.504-2A > G) in intron 6 of the PEPD gene (transcript NM_000285.4) with a variant read depth of 48× (Table 3). Discussion

The defective collagen turnover [4] caused by the enzyme deficiency resulted in the recurrent skin ulcers—a classic, though often missed, manifestation of prolidase deficiency. Recurrent infections, particularly upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) and ear infections [5], are common in PD, likely related to immune dysfunction.

The anemia and pancytopenia observed in this patient were likely multifactorial, a combination of iron deficiency, chronic inflammation, and hypersplenism secondary to splenomegaly. The presence of celiac disease played a significant role in this patient’s illness. Villous atrophy on biopsy and markedly raised tissue transglutaminase antibodies confirmed the diagnosis. Gluten-sensitive enteropathy contributed to his chronic diarrhea, iron deficiency anemia, and poor absorption of essential nutrients [2, 3, 6]. In addition, celiac disease may have worsened the underlying immune dysfunction, increasing his risk of infections in a case already predisposed owing to prolidase deficiency.

Massive splenomegaly with dilated portal vein suggesting portal hypertension was a significant clinical feature. Although FibroScan was not available in our setting,the abdominal ultrasound was suggestive of normal hepatic echotexture without nodularity or coarseness. This absence of sonographic cirrhotic changes, combined with preserved synthetic liver function, raise possibility of non-cirrhotic portal fibrosis (NCPF) or other etiology for portal hypertension, likely secondary to the chronic inflammatory state and recurrent infections associated with prolidase deficiency.

The diagnosis of prolidase deficiency was delayed owing to the rarity of the condition and the nonspecific nature of the presenting symptoms. The diagnostic delay in this case was not only clinical but also socioeconomic. Coming from a rural background with limited financial resources, our patient faced significant barriers in accessing tertiary care. The high cost and limited availability of advanced genomic tools, such as clinical exome sequencing, often delay the diagnosis of orphan diseases, as is evident in this case. Genetic diagnosis was key in establishing PD in this case. The c.504-2A > G mutation is a known pathogenic splice-site variant, resulting in dysfunctional prolidase enzyme and impaired collagen turnover. This case highlights the importance of considering rare metabolic disorders in patients with recurrent skin lesions, chronic infections, and hematological abnormalities.

Management and follow-up: The patient was counseled on a strict gluten-free diet, and family members were taught to identify hidden sources of gluten in everyday Indian meals. Management of prolidase deficiency in our patient centered around preventing secondary infections and promoting healing [7] of existing skin ulcers through nutritional and supportive measure. Unlike oral prolidase enzyme replacement, which has variable bioavailability due to gastric digestion, we opted for oral l-proline supplementation. The aim was to directly replenish the intracellular proline pool necessary for collagen turnover and wound healing, effectively bypassing the recycling defect.

The treatment regimen included:

Parenteral iron carboxymaltose followed by oral iron.

Manganese chloride 5 mg/day.

Vitamin C supplementation 500 mg twice daily (BD).

Oral l-proline supplementation was initiated at a dose of 10 g/day (administered as divided doses with water and vitamin C; this was sourced as pharmaceutical-grade l-proline powder).

General nutrient and hygiene care, including daily wound dressing.

As shown in Fig. 3, the patient’s skin ulcers improved markedly over 6 months of treatment. His gastrointestinal symptoms abated and his hemoglobin showed a steady rise, paralleling improved skin healing. Conclusion

Prolidase deficiency is a rare but serious condition [1] that should be considered in patients with recurrent ulcerative skin lesions, infections, and pancytopenia. Early recognition and appropriate genetic testing are crucial for diagnosis. This case also illustrates the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to managing complex, multisystem diseases such as prolidase deficiency and gluten enteropathy [2, 8].
