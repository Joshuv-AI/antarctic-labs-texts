Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest outcomes following EMS resuscitation protocol revision during the COVID-19 pandemic: a population-based study
Authors: Ryota Kaichi, Yoshinori Matsuoka, Tatsuya Norii, Yasukazu Hijikata, Chisato Miyakoshi, Taro Matsuoka, Naoto Hanamura, Naoto Yuge, Futoshi Toda, Takahiro Itaya, Yoshie Yamada, Yusuke Ogawa, Yosuke Yamamoto, Koichi Ariyoshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimWe aimed to evaluate the association between emergency medical service (EMS) protocols with enhanced infection control precautions against COVID-19 and the outcomes of patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), as well as various EMS-related time intervals, in Japan.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cohort study of OHCAs in Kobe, Japan, between 2016 and 2023. In May 2020, the Japanese resuscitation protocols for EMS were revised in accordance with the guidance of the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation. The outcomes of interest were favourable neurological outcomes and several time metrics related to EMS activities. We evaluated changes in outcomes before and after the protocol revision using segmented regression for interrupted time-series analyses.ResultsA total of 10,126 patients were enrolled during the period. The proportion of favourable neurological outcomes decreased after the protocol revision: the change in the intercept was −3.4 % (95 % confidence interval [CI], −5.2 % to −1.7 %) and in the trend was 0.01 % (95 % CI, −0.02 % to 0.04 %). Delays were observed in airway management completion (186 s [95 % CI, 131 to 241] for the intercept and 6.13 s [95 % CI, 4.22 to 8.03] for the trend) and establishment of intravenous access (99 s [95 % CI, 55 to 143] for the intercept and 3.93 s [95 % CI, 2.84 to 5.02] for the trend).ConclusionOHCA outcomes worsened after the protocol revision, with delays in several key EMS interventions. When encountering future pandemics, resuscitation protocols should be designed to balance infection control and the quality of advanced life support.Keywords: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Emergency medical services, Resuscitation protocol revision, COVID-19, Interrupted time-series analysis, Population-based study

Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, poor outcomes in patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) have been reported.1, 2, 3 These unfavourable outcomes were partly attributed to fear of infection, which affected bystander behaviours such as hesitancy to perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)4, 5 or delays in calling emergency medical services (EMS).4, 5 Furthermore, reductions in the use of public-access defibrillation (PAD)6 and lower proportions of bystander chest compressions during the COVID-19 pandemic have been reported.7 Meanwhile, in 2020, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) and the American Heart Association released guidance that influenced prehospital resuscitation practices by EMS providers.8, 9 This guidance emphasized that CPR involves aerosol-generating procedures and recommended the use of airborne personal protective equipment for patients suspected or confirmed to have COVID-19. Subsequently, experts have raised concerns that excessive infection control precautions may negatively affect the quality and efficacy of CPR.10, 11

Consistent with the ILCOR statement, the Japanese Society for Emergency Medicine (JSEM) issued guidelines for managing patients with cardiac arrest during the COVID-19 pandemic in April 2020.12 These guidelines were subsequently adopted nationwide by the Fire and Disaster Management Agency. These EMS resuscitation protocol revisions pose risks of potential delays in initiating chest compressions, prioritisation of airway management over other resuscitation measures to mitigate infection risks, and prolonged scene times, all of which could negatively affect outcomes in patients with OHCA. However, the actual impact of these protocol changes on the outcomes of patients with OHCA remains largely unknown.

Although COVID-19 was downgraded to a category equivalent to seasonal influenza under the Infectious Diseases Control Law in Japan in May 2023,13 the revision of EMS resuscitation protocols remains in effect in some regions. This situation provides a unique opportunity to assess the association between EMS resuscitation protocol revisions and outcomes among patients with OHCAs. We therefore conducted a population-based study using an interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) to evaluate the impact of these protocol revisions on OHCA outcomes and to explore optimal strategies for balancing infection control precautions and CPR quality, with the goal of informing future emergency response guidelines in the post-pandemic era.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a population-based cohort study in Kobe City, Japan, using ITSA. Kobe City has a population of 1.5 million residents in an area of approximately 557 km2, including both urban and rural communities. As of 2017, the municipal government provided EMS through 10 fire and ambulance stations, with 29 emergency dispatch centres.14 EMS personnel perform CPR according to Japanese guidelines and revised protocols,12, 15 which are based on the American Heart Association and ILCOR guidelines.9, 10 The Ethics Committee of Kobe Medical Center General Hospital approved the study protocol (zn250407). The requirement for informed consent was waived, as all information that could identify patients in this study was removed from the database.

Selection of participants

From all patients with OHCA in the registry, we enrolled patients aged 18 years and older who experienced cardiac arrest in Kobe City between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023. We excluded patients whose cardiac arrest was due to trauma or other exogenous causes (e.g. suicide, drowning, burns), as well as cases involving inter-facility transfers or physician-staffed ambulances.

Data collection

The Kobe OHCA Registry prospectively collected data from all patients with OHCA, using the Utstein style, who were treated by EMS personnel and transported to hospitals in the city.16 Cardiac arrest was defined as the cessation of mechanical cardiac activity, as determined by the absence of signs of circulation.16 In Japan, EMS personnel are not permitted to terminate resuscitation in the field; therefore, nearly all OHCA patients were transported to the nearest appropriate hospital. This registry collected data from the paramedics in charge of each patient, who completed a run sheet with clinical and operational details. Additionally, all survivors were followed up for 1 month, and their outcomes were obtained from the hospitals.

We extracted patient demographics and event-related variables from the registry, including age, sex, witness status, bystander CPR, arrest location, initial cardiac arrest rhythm (shockable rhythm, pulseless electrical activity, or asystole), epinephrine administration, and EMS defibrillation. Additionally, we obtained data on prehospital airway management, including the method used (endotracheal intubation, supraglottic airway device, and bag-valve mask) and the location of airway management (on-scene or in the ambulance). Information on venous access was collected, including whether an intravenous (IV) line was established, attempted but discontinued, or not attempted, as well as its location (on-scene or in the ambulance). The following EMS-related time variables were extracted: emergency call receipt, dispatch command, EMS departure, EMS arrival at the scene, EMS contact with the patient, initiation of chest compression by EMS, completion of airway management, completion of IV line establishment, epinephrine administration, EMS departure from the scene, and hospital arrival. Finally, 1-month survival and neurological status after cardiac arrest were obtained.

Revision of the EMS resuscitation protocol

We compared the outcomes of interest before and after the implementation of the revised EMS protocol, introduced by the JSEM in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and officially adopted by the Kobe City Fire Department on 1 May 2020.12 The key protocol changes included three major modifications. First, early instrumented advanced airway management was recommended to minimise aerosol generation during CPR, with chest compressions temporarily interrupted during airway management procedures to reduce aerosol dispersion.9 Second, the Communications Directive was revised to require paramedics to collect COVID-19-related information from patients and/or their families during emergency calls. Third, EMS providers were required to wear personal protective equipment before arriving at the scene as standard practice.8

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was the proportion of surviving patients with a favourable neurological outcome at 1 month after cardiac arrest. A favourable neurological outcome was defined as a Cerebral Performance Category score of 1 (good performance) or 2 (moderate disability).16 The secondary outcomes included the proportion of patient survival at 1 month and various EMS-related time variables: call-to-dispatch time (interval from emergency call receipt to dispatch command), dispatch preparation time (interval from dispatch command to EMS departure), time from call to arrival (interval from emergency call to EMS arrival at the scene), time on scene (interval from EMS arrival at the scene to EMS departure), time to chest compression initiation (interval from EMS contact to initiation of chest compressions), proportion of advanced airway management, time to complete airway management (interval from EMS contact to completion of airway management), time to establish venous access (interval from EMS contact to establishment of venous access), time to drug administration (interval from EMS contact to administration of epinephrine), and prehospital transport time (interval from EMS departure from the scene to hospital arrival). All the time intervals were measured in seconds.

Statistical analysis

We described the characteristics and outcomes of patients with OHCA before and after the revision of the EMS resuscitation protocol. Continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages.

In the primary analysis, we estimated the impact of the revised EMS resuscitation protocol on the outcomes of patients with OHCA using a segmented regression model for the ITSA.17, 18, 19 We examined two measures: intercept change, which represents the immediate shift in outcome levels following the implementation of the revised EMS resuscitation protocol, and trend change, which reflects a shift in the post-intervention trend independent of the baseline trend, influenced by population-level aspects of the protocol revision. We first fitted a simple linear regression model that included only a continuous time variable (1-month intervals) using monthly aggregated outcome data, with mean values calculated for each outcome. We then applied an additional linear regression model, incorporating a continuous variable for the time from the start of the observation period, an indicator variable distinguishing between the pre- and post-intervention periods, and a continuous variable representing the time after the intervention. To account for autocorrelation, standard errors were corrected using the Newey-West estimation method, with a lag of 12. Additionally, sine and cosine functions were incorporated as Fourier terms to adjust for seasonal variations, and all estimates were reported with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). For the sensitivity analysis, we applied ITSA with outcome data aggregated and averaged on (1) a weekly (Sunday to Saturday) and (2) a quarterly basis (Q1: January–March; Q2: April–June; Q3: July–September; Q4: October–December) to assess the robustness of the findings. In these analyses, standard errors were corrected using the Newey–West estimation method with a lag of 12 (weekly) and 4 (quarterly) to account for autocorrelation. Seasonal variations were adjusted for using Fourier terms.

We also conducted two supplementary analyses to account for the indirect influence of COVID-19, such as changes in bystander behaviour and restricted access to and quality of medical care during the pandemic. First, we added the state-of-emergency declaration issued by the Japanese government to the model used in the primary analysis. Second, we compared outcomes using a segmented regression model as in the primary analysis, before and after the reclassification of COVID-19 to a category comparable to that of seasonal influenza. The analysis was restricted to patients with OHCA after revision of the EMS resuscitation protocol.

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/SE (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA) and R software (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population

From a total of 11,357 adult patients with OHCA, 525 cases were of traumatic origin, 638 were due to exogenous causes (328 suicides, 275 drownings, and 35 burns), and 68 were attributed to other causes (55 inter-facility transfers and 13 physician-staffed ambulance cases). These cases were therefore excluded from the analysis. Finally, 10,126 patients were included in the present study (Fig. 1).

Table 1 demonstrates the characteristics of the patients before and after the EMS protocol revision. Among the 10,126 cases included in the study, 5,370 (53 %) occurred before protocol revision (pre-revision group) and 4,756 (47 %) occurred after revision (post-revision group). Prolonged on-scene time was observed (124 s, CI: 98 to 149 for the intercept change, and 4.31 s, CI: 3.43 to 5.19 for the trend change). Notably, delays were observed for time to complete airway management (186 s, CI: 131 to 241 for the intercept change), initiate chest compressions (15 s, CI: 9.8 to 21 for the intercept change), and establish venous access (99 s, CI: 55 to 143 for the intercept change). An upward change in the slope was observed for time to complete airway management (6.13 s, CI: 4.22 to 8.03), time to establish venous access (3.93 s, CI: 2.84 to 5.02), and time to drug administration (5.69 s, CI: 4.25 to 7.13). In contrast, the slope appeared to remain steady before and after the protocol revision, such as call-to-arrival time (0.32 s, CI: −0.73 to 1.39) and time to chest compression initiation (−0.19 s, CI: −0.46 to 0.08). These findings remained robust across sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Discussion

In this analysis of a population-based cohort, we identified two key findings. First, OHCA outcomes worsened following the implementation of the revised EMS resuscitation protocol in 2020. Second, EMS personnel required more time for key interventions, including airway management—including tracheal intubation and supraglottic devices—and establishment of intravenous access. Given the complexity of other factors (e.g., pandemic outbreaks, lockdowns, government policies), it is difficult to determine what proportion of the decline in favourable neurological outcomes was attributable to the protocol change. At least, the findings of the present study indicate that infection control–enhanced EMS resuscitation protocols may be associated with delays in performing resuscitative procedures, highlighting the inherent challenges in carefully balancing infection control precautions with high-quality advanced life support.

Among the several potential mechanisms, protocol-related changes in EMS activity may account for the poor OHCA outcomes observed in the present study. First, several factors associated with airway management may have contributed to these findings. We observed delays in the completion of airway management, which may have adversely affected the outcomes.20 Furthermore, under the revised EMS resuscitation protocol, EMS personnel prioritised airway management over establishing IV lines to reduce aerosol generation. However, a recent multicentre observational study demonstrated that epinephrine administration before advanced airway placement was associated with improved outcomes.21 Second, we identified evident delays across multiple EMS time metrics. Although we could not determine which specific delay had the greatest impact, the cumulative effect of these changes may have compromised resuscitation effectiveness in the prehospital setting. Third, uninterrupted high-quality chest compressions are the cornerstones of successful OHCA resuscitation. However, infection control-enhanced protocols recommend pausing chest compressions during advanced airway management to minimise the risk of aerosol exposure. Additionally, a previous report indicated that the use of airborne personal protective equipment may reduce CPR quality.10, 11 In summary, bundles of changes aimed at minimising viral transmission during resuscitation may have unintended detrimental consequences for patients with OHCA.

The strength of the present study lies in its use of a quasi-experimental ITSA with a comprehensive population-based database of all OHCA cases in Kobe. Our findings underscore the challenges of designing protocols that maintain CPR quality while ensuring rescuer safety during infectious disease pandemics. Looking ahead, we must anticipate future outbreaks of infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, which require strict aerosol precautions. In such situations, resuscitation protocols must be tailored to the local epidemiological context, considering which cases warrant infection control measures and to what extent these measures should be applied. A flexible, context-specific approach to resuscitation protocols is essential to balance infection control and optimal patient care.

This study has some limitations. First, changes in bystander behaviour, limited medical resources, and quality of care during the pandemic may have influenced OHCA outcomes. Two supplementary analyses were conducted. We added the “state of emergency” variable into the statistical model to account for the influence of the COVID-19 surge, which demonstrated results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S3). We performed an additional ITSA before and after the reclassification of COVID-19. Despite expectations that the effects of ambulance diversion and resource shortages would have diminished, the neurological outcomes of patients with OHCA had not improved after reclassification (Supplementary Table S4). Taken together, the two supplementary analyses incorporating changes in bystander behaviour and medical infrastructure demonstrated consistent results across the different analytic models. Second, we could not isolate the effects of specific components of the revised EMS resuscitation protocol. Since the protocol included multiple simultaneous modifications, it was difficult to determine which elements contributed most directly to the observed outcome changes. Future investigations using granular patient-level intervention data may provide more precise insights into the impact of individual protocol elements. Third, other potential breakpoints within the study period may also have influenced OHCA outcomes. Although our primary analysis focused on the time of protocol implementation, these additional events could have contributed to the observed decline in favourable neurological outcomes. Fourth, the present study could not provide detailed insights into how to balance infection control with the quality of advanced life support. Further evidence and experiences need to be accumulated from different regions and countries, so that ILCOR and other organisations can provide well-balanced recommendations for both rescuer safety and patient outcomes in future pandemics. Finally, the findings of the present study may not be generalizable to other regions with different EMS structures and resuscitation strategies. For example, many EMS systems in Asia employ a “scoop and run” model, whereas Western systems typically adopt a “stay and treat” approach, which may contribute to improved survival outcomes in OHCA patients.22 Although our findings indicate the importance of protocol changes in prehospital care quality, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to healthcare settings with different operational frameworks.

Conclusion

The outcomes of patients with OHCA worsened after EMS revised resuscitation protocols to enhance infection control during the COVID-19 pandemic, which also led to delays in several key prehospital interventions. Important lessons should be drawn from the COVID-19 pandemic, and resuscitation protocols that balance infection control with the provision of high-quality advanced life support should be developed in preparation for future pandemics.
