The molecular impact of sonoporation: A transcriptomic analysis of gene regulation profile
Authors: Xinxing Duan, Jennifer MF Wan, Alfred CH Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HSP genes were typically upregulated after sonoporation.•Pro- and anti- apoptotic signaling were both triggered by sonoporation.•Unsonoporated cells involved more genetic changes than sonoporated cells after MB-US exposure.•Inflammatory and oxidative stress responses were stimulated in unsonoporated cells after MB-US exposure.Keywords: Sonoporation, Ultrasound, Microbubbles, Cell signaling, Apoptosis, Heat shock proteins, Oxidative stress, Immune response

Introduction

Sonoporation has been well recognized as a promising strategy for drug/gene delivery [1], [2]. The most common way to induce sonoporation is microbubble-mediated ultrasound (MB-US) exposure, in which ultrasound is delivered in the presence of microbubbles to trigger acoustic cavitation that is characterized by microbubble oscillations and possibly its inertial collapse at high ultrasound pressure. With the mechanical impact delivered by acoustic cavitation, membrane perforation can be generated with limited ultrasound intensity. Accordingly, sonoporation has demonstrated its effectiveness in fostering the uptake of therapeutic agents with high spatiotemporal specificity in the absence of excessive thermal effects, thereby exhibiting merit as a non-invasive and precise strategy for drug delivery and gene therapy [3], [4], [5], [6]. It is recognized that effective delivery practice requires efficient molecule internalization while maintaining cell viability [7]. Yet, previous data on ultrasound-based drug delivery has raised concerns about its mediocre efficiency and related instigation of cell death [7], [8], [9], [10].

To improve the drug delivery efficiency of MB-US exposure, it is critical to maintain high cell viability while attaining a high sonoporation rate. Alas, such an efficiency optimization endeavor is fundamentally challenging to realize without a clear understanding of the bioprocesses and molecular signaling influenced by sonoporation. So far, morphological changes such as cytoskeleton reorganization [11], [12] and membrane blebbing [13], [14], as well as downstream apoptosis [10], [15], have been found to take place after sonoporation. In addition, calcium ion (Ca2+) signaling is known to be acutely triggered by sonoporation due to the Ca2+ influx through perforation site [8], [16]. In relation to Ca2+ influx, membrane potential [17], [18] and intracellular ROS level [19], [20] has also been found to be altered by sonoporation, while endocytosis [1], [21] and exocytosis [22], [23] activities may be enhanced to facilitate the membrane repair after sonoporation. As such, it is well possible for sonoporation to generate a cascade of intracellular changes, but the molecular mechanisms underpinning these changes and their relationship with the post-sonoporation membrane repair and cell survival were barely reported. Without filling this knowledge gap, our understanding of sonoporation-induced bioeffects would remain biased, and the optimization of sonoporation could only rely on random experiment trials or base hypotheses on other established membrane perforation methods such as electroporation.

In this study, we seek to map the complexity of sonoporation-induced bioeffects by examining the gene responses of sonoporated cells. Recognizing that subcellular activities and their biological functioning are intrinsically mediated by gene regulatory networks, our investigation will specifically aim to address the post-exposure mRNA variation of HL-60 leukemia cells in response to microbubble-mediated sonoporation so as to provide new information on genetic regulation changes induced by sonoporation. In approaching this task, whole transcriptome shotgun sequencing (RNA-seq) was employed to screen a large pool of genes of cells that are subjected to MB-US exposure. By comparing the exposed cells to the sham control, the mRNA transcripts that are significantly upregulated and downregulated can be identified in a high-throughput manner. For this investigation, our overarching hypothesis is that MB-US exposure may induce transcriptomic alteration to the exposed cells. Note that low-intensity ultrasound exposure in the absence of microbubbles has been previously reported to be able to induce a range of genetic responses [24], [25]. It is thus necessary to also clarify whether and how the gene responses may vary in the presence of microbubbles especially when acute membrane integrity disruptions brought about by sonoporation are expected. Such a study is particularly important for ultrasound-mediated gene therapy when specific gene responses are desired and other unnecessary ones preferred to be prevented.

Methods

Overview of experimental procedure

The sonoporation procedure used in this study was based on our established protocol, which ensured the acoustic exposure on a population of cells was meticulously controlled [3]. The exposure conditions applied in this study were consistent with our previous studies in which a multitude of cell responses to sonoporation were discovered, so that comparability and traceability of the new findings can be established. Note that in vitro sonoporation may yield heterogeneous bioeffects: that is, cells could be unsonoporated (no molecular uptake), sonoporated with low molecular uptake, and sonoporated with high molecular uptake [26], [27], [28]. Thus, we have harvested the three subpopulations separately and offered the mRNA transcriptomic comparison among them to clarify whether there were molecular differences among these heterogeneous subpopulations of exposed cells. Such an ‘-omics’ analysis approach allows us to acquire collective quantification of pools of molecules and further seek patterns of structural and functional behaviors of cells [29], [30], [31].

Fig. 1 illustrates the experimental procedures used in this investigation. In brief, 1 h before the ultrasound exposure, a 37 °C degassed water bath was prepared. Routinely cultured HL-60 leukemia cells (see Section 2.2) were extracted and resuspended to the experimental density 2x107 cells/ml. Calcein (C481, Invitrogen, USA) was added to the suspensions 10 min before the exposure. Microbubbles (see Section 2.4) were supplemented into the suspension at cell-to-bubble ratio 1:1 at 3 min before the exposure, after which the suspension was immediately transferred to the cell chamber and the prescribed pulsed ultrasound exposure was applied (see Section 2.3). After a 30 s exposure period, cells were taken out of the chamber and were re-incubated for 5 min in a 37 °C, 5 % carbon dioxide (CO2) incubator.

To facilitate post-exposure bioassay analysis, propidium iodide (PI) (P4170, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to the suspension 5 min after ultrasound exposure (see Section 2.5). The samples were then incubated for another 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed twice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Afterwards, flow cytometry and cell sorting were performed to separate different groups of cells (see Section 2.6). It took 1 h to complete the cell sorting and then the sorted groups were incubated in the 37 °C, 5 % CO2 condition for 90 min. The sorted cells were then collected, and their total RNA was then extracted for each group followed by processing for RNA-seq and the data analysis (see Section 2.7). This bioassay analysis took place at 3 h after ultrasound exposure.

Cell culture and preparation

HL-60 leukemia cells (CCL240, ATCC, USA) were chosen as the experimental cell line in this study. Owing to its intrinsic deficiency in pro-apoptotic regulatory proteins [32], this immortal cell line was naturally more resistant to pro-apoptotic stress, and thus it is suitable for serving as an effective controls in our investigation of anti-proliferation effects [9]. The HL-60 cells were routinely cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (R8758; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and were supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (ATCC). The cell culture incubator was kept at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Cell density was constantly monitored to ensure that they did not exceed 106 cells/ml. As such, an exponential cell growth can be maintained. HL-60 cells were sub-cultured every three days. During sub-culture, cells were harvested in a sterilized 15 ml centrifuge tube (BDAA352196; BD Biosciences) and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, followed by removal of the supernatant and resuspension with fresh RPMI medium.

Ultrasound exposure protocol

The acoustic exposure was conducted on a customized exposure platform which was introduced in detail in our previous work [3]. The exposure platform had three levels: the bottom level to position the transducer, the middle level to mount a secure-sealed cell chamber, and the top level that was a removable panel padded with acoustic absorber to prevent incident waves from reflecting back towards the cell chamber. The dimensions of the platform and the cell chamber were designed to achieve a relatively homogeneous acoustic field in the cell sample zone of the cell chamber and avoid undesired wave reflections. Hydrophone measurements were conducted to characterize the ultrasound field at the mounting site of the cell chamber within the acoustic exposure platform. The location and dimensions of the cell chamber were set to ensure the cells could be placed within a region with a peak negative pressure fluctuation range of 1.6 dB [3].

During experiments, the platform was immersed in a 37 °C degassed water bath. Sinusoidal signals were generated by a waveform generator (33120A; Agilent Technologies, USA) and coupled into a 50 dB-gain broadband amplifier (2100L; E&I, USA), subsequently output to the transducer (Advanced Devices, US). Waveform frequency was set to 1 MHz, which corresponds with the center frequency of the transducer. The pulse repetition frequency (PRF) was set to 1 kHz and the pulse length was 100 cycles, which corresponds to a 10 % duty cycle. In each run of experiments, cells were treated by this pulsed ultrasound exposure for 30 s. In situ acoustic measurement was conducted and calibrated constantly to assure the consistent acoustic dose. The spatial-averaged peak negative pressure was measured to be 0.50 MPa at the cell sample zone. The spatial-peak, pulse-averaged intensity was estimated to be 9.71 W/cm2, and the spatial-peak, time-averaged intensity was 0.97 W/cm2. Accordingly, the delivered acoustic energy was estimated to be 29.1 J/cm2.

Microbubble application

Commercial microbubbles (TS-108, Targeson, USA; now acquired by TRUST Bio-Sonics, Taiwan) were employed to be the artificial gas nuclei for acoustic cavitation triggering sonoporation. The microbubbles have the gas core of perfluorobutane encapsulated by phospholipid. The sizes of the microbubbles were relatively small with mean diameter of 2.09 µm, and less than 1.7 % of the population were larger than 5 µm according to the manufacture’s data. During experiments, microbubbles were added to the cell suspension just before the ultrasound exposure at a cell-to-bubble ratio of 1:1. Note that the choice of cell-to-bubble ratio plays a significant role in the resulting sonoporation efficiency [33]. The 1:1 cell-to-bubble ratio was empirically found to provide a balance between inducing substantial sonoporation (>30 %) and minimizing instant cell death (<20 %) in our previous studies[3], [34]. With the setting of this study, over 99 % of the supplemented microbubbles were found to have collapsed after the 30 s exposure, as observed under bright-field microscope.

Identification of subpopulations after MB-US exposure

Identification of subpopulations was achieved through calcein/PI bi-fluorescent labeling of the cell samples followed by bivariate fluorescent analysis using a flow cytometer (Aria III, BD Biosciences). As introduced in Section 2.1, calcein (10 μM) and PI (75 μM) were added as the fluorescent probes to the cell chamber, respectively, before ultrasound exposure and 5 min after exposure to detect cells with permeabilized membrane (allowed calcein entrance) and cells without short-term membrane resealing. Note that the 5-min post-exposure incubation was provided to allow the sonoporated cells to reseal the perforation sites and to restore the physical barrier against the influx of extracellular molecules. In each flow cytometry experiment, calcein fluorescence was measured with a 530/30 nm filter, and PI fluorescence was detected with a 616/23 nm filter. In each flow cytometry run, data for 104 cells per sample was acquired at a rate of 300 events/s. Cells showing positive and negative PI fluorescence were deemed respectively the non-viable and viable cell populations after MB-US exposure. Accordingly, using PI/calcein bivariate analysis, we were able to recognize the viable sonoporated subpopulation (SNP+; with PI- and calcein+ ), viable unsonoprated subpopulation (SNP-; with PI- and calcein-) and non-viable subpopulation (PI+ regardless of calcein fluorescence). Since non-viable cells had lost integrity and could not support normal molecular assays, our investigation had focused on the two post-exposure viable cell populations (SNP+and SNP-). Note that the SNP+group had distributed as two subpopulations with different calcein uptake levels (see Fig. 1c), specifically low uptake (LUP) and high uptake (HUP) subpopulations as determined based on the calcein fluorescence intensity of sonoporated cells through flow cytometry and confocal microscopy according to our established method [3]. Hence, three viable groups of cells of interest after MB-US exposure were obtained for RNA-seq analysis: SNP-, LUP and HUP (the latter two being SNP+subpopulations). In this study, sham control (CTL) and the cells exposed to ultrasound in the absence of microbubbles (US) were also performed to facilitate comparative analysis. In total, there were five cell groups in this study.

Cell sorting by flow cytometry

After identifying each subpopulation, the cell sorter configured in the Aria III flow cytometer was immediately applied to the post-exposure cell sample to obtain the SNP- and SNP+ groups. Non-viable cells were excluded from the sorting procedures. During each sorting experiment, 5×106 cells for each targeted subpopulation were collected. Note that cells were sorted with high-speed flow up to a rate of 3000 events/s to ensure the targeted number of cells can be harvested efficiently. During cell sorting process, the sheath fluid of the flow cytometer and the sample container were maintained at 4 °C to maintain cell viability [35]. The harvested cell populations were immediately re-incubated and were later used for biological assays at 3 h after ultrasound exposure. Note that cells in the CTL and US groups also underwent the identical cell sorting procedures.

Whole transcriptome shortgun sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) contents were first isolated from the treated cell samples with TRIzol reagent (15596026, ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA quality was validated via two methods: purity test by a microplate spectrophotometer (Multiskan™ GO, ThermoFisher, USA) and integrity test by agarose gel electrophoresis. The sequencing library was prepared using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations. Briefly, mRNA was purified from the total RNA using oligo-attached beads containing poly-T oligo. The mRNA was first fragmented randomly by the addition of fragmentation buffer. With these short fragments as templates, the first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M−MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H-). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs of the purified double-stranded cDNA were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After adenylation of 3′ ends of DNA fragments, NEBNext Adaptor with hairpin loop structure was ligated to prepare for hybridization. The library fragments were then purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). DNA fragments of the selected size (150–200 bp) were purified by AMPure XP beads and amplified by PCR. The amplified library was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq™ 2000 sequencing machine, which was conducted by the commercial service provided by Novogene Co., Ltd. Raw data and reference gene lists were obtained from Novogene. Raw reads were aligned and mapped to the reference genome Ensembl followed by calculation of gene expression level by FPKM (Fragments Per Kilo bases per Million reads). Differential expression analysis was performed by using the DEGseq R package (V1.12.0). The threshold of differential gene expression was determined by fold change and q-value (adjusted p-value) according to: |log2 (FoldChange)| > 1 and q-value < 0.005.

Main effect of sonoporation: Upregulation of heat shock protein (HSP) genes

After MB-US exposure, the two sonoporated groups showed high similarity in gene response. Table 1 listed the differentially expressed genes of LUP and HUP. There were five overlapping genes between the two groups, which could be recognized as the common gene responses of HL-60 cells to sonoporation. Note that sonoporation-specific responses should exclude the responses of the SNP- group whose membrane integrity remain undisrupted. Among the five common responsive genes to sonoporation, four genes (HSPA6, HSPA1A, HSPA1B and DNAJB1) serve the function of encoding a specific member of the human heat shock proteins (HSPs). In terms of the expression level, the most remarkable changes were found in three genes (HSPA1A, HSPA1B and HSPA6), which all showed at least 4-fold change in expression for both LUP and HUP groups. Note that the three genes all belong to the HSP70 genes that encode a specific HSP family, i.e. HSP70 protein family (HSPs of ∼ 70 kDa molecular weight).

Among the identified genes, HSPA1A and HSPA1B are highly homologous tandem genes (often referred to together as HSP70-1), which serve major stress-induced survival role among HSP70 gene family [36]. HSPA1A and HSPA1B are expressed at low levels in normal conditions, but under stress, their expression would be dramatically increased, as was the case for sonoporated cells. HSPA6 gene is another stress-inducible gene but only responsive to severe stress insults. Apart from the three HSP70 genes, the expression of DNAJB1, one of the HSP40 genes (encoding ∼ 40 kDa HSPs) also changed over 2-fold in the two sonoporated groups. HSP40 proteins, as the co-chaperones of HSP70s, can regulate and facilitate substrate targeting of HSP70s, thereby serve to stimulate the ATPase activity of HSP70s and promote protein folding and prevent misfolded protein aggregation of the cells [37], [38]. Another gene also upregulated in both groups was RHOB, which showed a change of 1.7- in LUP and 1.2-fold in HUP respectively. RHOB protein is a Ras-related small GTP-binding protein, which is known to be a pro-apoptotic protein and a tumor suppressor by mediating cell apoptosis [39], [40], [41].

Differences between the two sonoporated groups were found in the expressions of three genes. As can be seen in Table 1, IER5 gene was upregulated in the LUP group (1.1-fold) but not in the HUP group. FOS and JUN genes were upregulated in the HUP group (1.4-fold) but not in the LUP group. FOS and JUN encode leucine zipper proteins that form the transcription factor complex AP-1. AP-1 complex plays a general role in the transduction of signals from the membrane to the nucleus and has been implicated as regulators of cell proliferation, differentiation, and transformation [42]. IER5, as an immediate-early response gene, can mediate positive transcriptional regulation of several chaperone genes during the heat shock response in a HSF1-dependent manner [43]. Overexpression of IER5 was also found to be able to inhibit cell growth and enhance the cell apoptosis [44].

As shown in Table 2, there are more genes affected by MB-US exposure for the SNP- group than the two SNP+ groups. Most gene variation in the LUP and HUP groups also appeared in the SNP- group with one exception (the FOS gene). The four upregulated HSP genes (HSPA6, HSPA1A, HSPA1B, and DNAJB1) were consistently observed across the SNP-, LUP, and HUP groups. The gene functions involved in the SNP- group exhibited more profound changes than that of the SNP+ groups. First, cells of the SNP- group had more stress-related genes upregulated, including more family members of HSPs (HSPB1 of HSP27 family and HSPH1 of the HSP110 family) and the typical oxidative-stress responsive genes of TXNIP, CHAC1, SESN2, and SLC7A11. Second, more apoptosis or cell survival related genes were upregulated such as NR4A1, BAG3, MXD1, DDIT4 and TSPYL2. Third, the SNP- cells had three immunity-related genes downregulated after MB-US exposure, while the gene responses of the two SNP+groups were all gene upregulation.

Discussions

Pro- and anti-apoptotic signaling are both triggered by sonoporation

Sonoporation has been reported to influence cell survival for more than a decade [10], [15], [46], but the exact mechanism has yet to be fully elucidated. Previously, sonoporation was found to be able to trigger apoptosis [10], [15], [47], while the cytoprotective system could also be mobilized after exposure to facilitate anti-apoptotic functions [3]. How these pro- and anti-apoptotic activities are balanced and/or competing against each other is vital for the post-exposure fate of sonoporated cells and, in turn, is a key consideration for establishing the efficiency of ultrasound-mediated drug delivery. In this investigation, our findings obtained at 3 h after sonoporation have provided new targets that might mediate the apoptotic signaling and have suggested potential cytoprotective pathways via HSP70 functioning. These key findings are summarized in Fig. 5.

Possible model for the intracellular molecular signaling of the sonoporated cells.

Our RNA-seq analysis has shown that, in sonoporated cells, three HSP70 genes together with one HSP40 gene were significantly upregulated. HSP70 has been known to be able to directly unfold misfolded proteins in the stressed conditions and thus inhibit apoptosis and other caspase-independent cell death. Moreover, HSP70 could protect the cells against apoptosis by suppressing JNK activation [48], [49] which is a part of a pro-apoptotic signaling cascade that reduces release of cytochrome c and Smac from mitochondria [50], [51]. HSP40 proteins are the co-chaperones that bind to HSP70 proteins and stimulate the ATPase activity of HSP70 proteins [52]. It has been known that the cooperation of HSP70 and HSP40 could inhibit BAX translocation to the mitochondria to prevent apoptosis [53]. Note that, in our earlier studies, the translocation of BAX to mitochondria was found to be promoted by upregulation of BAX and thus facilitated the downstream apoptotic signaling [10]. Our new findings here have suggested that the upregulation of HSP70 after sonoporation might antagonize the mitochondria-mediated apoptotic process and thereby enhance cell survival.

As shown in our data (Table 1), while the anti-apoptotic genes were upregulated, pro-apoptotic genes such as RHOB and IER5 were also activated by sonoporation. The Ras-related small GTPases RHOB is well known to mediate apoptosis in transformed cells and regulate the actin cytoskeleton organization [39], [40], [41]. Upregulation of the RHOB gene has been identified as part of the immediate early response to various growth and stress stimuli, which plays an essential role in apoptotic signaling [54], [55]. IER5 as another immediate early response gene has been reported to promote apoptosis and inhibit proliferation in stressed cells [43], [44], [56], [57]. The post-exposure increases of both RHOB and IER5 indicated that the pro-apoptotic responses were also triggered, albeit at relatively low levels, by sonoporation. Taken together, sonoporation may stimulate bifurcated signaling involved in apoptosis. Likely, sonoporation triggered early apoptotic signaling immediately, whereas the cells initiated the self-protective HSPs to antagonize the potential apoptosis threats. Eventually the cell survival would depend on which side of the pro- and anti-apoptotic reactions could dominate the intracellular mobilization and whether the cell homeostasis could be restored. Thus, in sonoporation-based therapy, it should be taken into consideration that not only the delivered drug/gene but also the sonoporation-induced intracellular signaling would alter cell survival and thereby affect the therapeutic efficacy.

Unsonoporated cells post MB-US exposure showed strong immune and oxidative responses

Besides triggering apoptosis-related molecular signaling changes in sonoporated cells, MB-US exposure was found to bring about a different form of molecular impact on unsonoporated cells as summarized in Fig. 6. Specifically, multiple immunity-related genes (CLC, EPX, and IL8) were found to be downregulated in the unsonoporated cells (see Table 2). CLC (Charcot-Leyden Crystal Galectin), a characteristic protein of granulocyte especially for eosinophils and basophils (∼10 % of the total cellular proteins), performs an important role in inflammatory responses [58], [59], [60]. EPX, a peroxidase gene that encoded the granule protein specifically expressed by eosinophil [61], [62], is involved in the inflammatory responses by mediating genesis of oxidants, thereby playing an essential role in regulating the oxygen-dependent microbicidal system of eosinophil [63], [64], [65], [66]. IL8, produced by various types of cells upon stimulation with inflammatory stimuli [67], [68], is a gene that encoded the protein which is a member of the chemokine family.

Possible model for the intracellular molecular signaling of the unsonoporated cells.

MB-US exposure could also bring oxidative stresses to unsonoporated cells. Both pro- and anti-oxidative genes (TXNIP, CHAC1, SESN2, and SLC7A11) were found to be upregulated after exposure. TXNIP gene encodes a thioredoxin-binding protein which function as a major redox regulator by inhibiting the anti-oxidative function of thioredoxin and thereby resulting in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and apoptosis [69], [70]. CHAC1 encodes a member of the gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase proteins and is a pro-apoptotic member of the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway. CHAC1 protein was reported to play an important role in negatively regulating the oxidative balance of the cell through decreasing glutathione [71], [72]. SESN2 gene encodes a member of the sestrin family, which are the stress-responsive proteins functioning as antioxidants through decreasing intracellular ROS [73], [74]. SLC7A11 encodes a member of the heteromeric amino-acid transport system which is highly specific for cysteine/glutamate. The upregulation of SLC7A11 can scavenge ROS via regulation of glutathione and thereby protect the cells from oxidative stress [75], [76]. Apparently, the oxidative responses of the unsonoporated cells also showed a degree of complexity. The upregulation of TXNIP and CHAC1 can both inhibit the anti-oxidative activities of the cell and induce ROS accumulation, while SESN2 and SLC7A11 can diminish the intracellular ROS. Responses of cells to oxidative stresses are usually associated with the regulation of apoptosis.

Nuclear activities were influenced by MB-US exposure

MB-US exposure might also potentially influence nuclear activities, as evidenced by the significantly higher level of FOS and JUN genes in the HUP cells after MB-US exposure. The products of the two genes—FOS and JUN proteins, belonging to the transcription factor activator protein 1 (AP-1), form a heterodimeric complex that interacts with the DNA regulatory element AP-1 binding site in the nucleus. Through DNA binding and modulating gene expressions, FOS and JUN involve in a wide range of cellular processes, including cell growth, cell cycle progression, differentiation, and apoptosis [77], [78], [79], [80].

Our results indicated that more MB-US-induced changes might occur in the nuclei of the exposed cells. This trend was more prominent in the unsonoporated cells, as more nuclear activity-related genes were upregulated. For instance, NR4A1 encodes the steroid nuclear hormone receptor. Overexpression of NR4A1 was found to involve in growth inhibition and induction of apoptosis [81]. Nuclear export of NR4A1 has also been found to associate with the apoptotic activities in many cases [82]. DDIT4 is a DNA-damage-inducible transcript located on chromosome 10, which can be activated by DNA damage and different stressors. DDIT4 was reported to mediate autophagy and apoptosis through mTOR signaling pathway [83]. Overexpression of DDIT4 could potentially promote anti-apoptotic proteins and decrease expression of pro-apoptotic proteins [84]. TSPYL2 encodes testis-specific Y-encoded-like protein 2, which is localized to the nucleolus where it functions in chromatin remodeling [85].

Implications of the variation between the sonoporated, unsonoporated and US groups

It should be emphasized that, when subjected to the same MB-US exposure, sonoporated and unsonoporated cells (i.e., with and without membrane integrity disruption) responded quite differently. The only common response was that the HSPs, specifically HSP70 and HSP40, as the highly conservative protective proteins responsive to environmental, physical and chemical stresses, were upregulated in both sonoporated and unsonoporated cells. Comparing with sonoporated cells, unsonoporated cells not only had a stronger upregulation of HSP70 and HSP40 genes, but more protective mechanisms were also initiated. For example, the upregulated HSPB1 encodes HSP27, which could support cell survival through inhibition of apoptosis, ferroptosis and protects actin filament from fragmentation [86]. HSPH1 encodes HSP110, which acts as a nucleotide exchange factor of HSP70s thereby mediating death inhibition [87]. Also, HSP110 could prevent the aggregation of denatured proteins in cells under severe stress.

According to our results (Table 1, Table 2), unsonoporated cells mobilized more cytoprotective activities than that for sonoporated cells. This observation was consistent with our earlier findings that unsonoporated cells exhibited less apoptosis and higher survival rate than sonoporated cells after MB-US exposure [88]. Note that the intracellular orchestration of unsonoporated cells is after all complicated, as evidenced by the upregulation of inflammation and oxidative-stress related genes, and moreover, a number of genes mediated apoptosis and/or other cell deaths were also activated aside from the HSP genes. For example, BAG3 belongs to the BAG proteins that bind to HSP70 ATPase domain and inhibit its chaperone activity in a Hip-repressible manner. BAG3 plays an important role in multiple biological processes such as apoptosis, cytoskeleton organization and autophagy [89], [90], [91]. MXD1 usually functions as a member of the MYC/MAX/MAD network of basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper transcription factors. Together they can mediate cellular proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis [91], [92]. MXD1 can inhibit cell apoptosis with the functional HLHZip region [93]. Also, as mentioned in Section 4.3, NR4A1, DDIT4 and TSPYL2 can play a role in apoptosis and/or cell cycle progression.

Interestingly, cells that were sonicated in the absence of MBs showed quite different gene responses with all the other groups of MB-US-exposed cells (see Table 3). These cells barely shared any similarity in the gene response with respect to sonoporated cells. They also only showed three commonly regulated genes with unsonoporated cells, all of which are related to immune functions (CLC, EPX, and IL8). Nevertheless, most differentially expressed genes of sonicated cells without MBs showed low-fold changes. This trend is after all within expectation because the applied ultrasound intensity was relatively low with a spatial-peak, time-averaged intensity of 0.97 W/cm2, which is only slightly above the regulatory limit of 0.72 W/cm2 set forth by the US Food and Drug Administration for diagnostic ultrasound imaging.

The differences in the transcriptomic response between unsonoporated and sonoporated cells can perhaps be explained by recognizing that the extent of mechanical impact delivered by acoustic cavitation to these two cell populations are fundamentally different. Even though all sonicated cells received the same ultrasound dose, individual sonicated cells may have experienced a different acoustic cavitation impact because of two factors: 1) the distance between each cell and microbubble may be different since they are randomly distributed in the cell chamber; 2) the microbubbles were of a polydisperse size distribution, so they expectedly had different cavitation dynamics. As a result, some sonicated cells may remain unsonoporated, while some may become sonoporated with heterogenous levels of model drug uptake. Since disruption of membrane integrity did not occur among unsonoporated cells, their post-exposure signaling behavior would expectedly be not identical to that of sonoporated cells. In contrast, the US group had a different transcriptomic response than that of unsonoporated and sonoporated cells because cells in the US group were merely subjected a mild mechanical impact induced by ultrasound at a relatively low intensity in the absence of microbubbles (and correspondingly, the lack of acoustic cavitation).

Perspectives for future investigations

To foster further understanding on the molecular impact of sonoporation, at least three avenues can be investigated further. First, it would be worthwhile to track the apoptosis regulatory network and dive deeper into the apoptotic signaling of the exposed cells. It is crucial to pinpoint the critical molecular targets through meticulous examination of signaling pathways involved and, in turn, unfold the landscape of the pro- and anti- apoptotic signaling to foster accurate assessment of cell recovery after sonoporation over a long period. Such insights would empower researchers with the tools and targets that could be selected to be of use in modulating cell death and thereby improve the efficiency of the MB-US delivery practice.

Second, it is necessary to probe into the nuclei and investigate the potential alteration of nuclear activities. One possible direction could be to explore how the transcription process was affected since the activation of multiple transcription regulators such as FOS, JUN, NR4A1 etc., has been found in our study. Another potential direction to study intranuclear change is to investigate whether and/or how the DNA was damaged by sonoporation. Here we showed a few genes responsive to DNA damage, such as DDIT4, RHOB and TSPYL2, have been upregulated after MB-US exposure. It is thus reasonable to speculate the DNA structure might be damaged during the exposure, which may lead to various signaling provoked and participated in the cell survival competing race.

The third avenue which could be of interest is to revisit the MB-US exposure not solely as an agent delivery method but also as a maneuver that modulates the unsonoporated cells’ intracellular activities. As such, MB-US exposure has allowed the delivery of exogenous agents into the target cells as well as effected on the adjacent cells. This could particularly be of use in tumor therapy where there is a complexed microenvironment in situ counteracting the therapeutic efficiency.

The results in this work also provide new perspectives for in vivo studies on sonoporation-based therapeutics. The clear genetic distinction between the sonoporated and unsonoporated groups after exposure indicates that cells within the exposure area may exhibit different reactions after the practice of sonoporation. Moreover, the extensive genetic response of the unsonoporated cells suggests that the MB-US exposure has the potential to modify their cell viability and resistance to external stress. In a drug delivery treatment such as chemotherapy, the sonoporated cells may be eliminated by the internalized drug after exposure. In the meantime, those unsonoporated cells without drug permeability may also be profoundly influenced. Such impact could be taken into account in sonoporation-mediated medicine design and prognosis. Also, efforts are demanded to investigate whether and how sonoporation may modify the drug resistance and efficacy in the context of in vivo drug delivery to benefit clinical applications.

Conclusion

Sonoporation via MB-US exposure has become a popular paradigm to facilitate drug and gene delivery. However, the bioeffects induced by sonoporation, such as apoptosis and other survival events, have still been an ill-studied topic. To delve into the downstream molecular responses induced by sonoporation and MB-US exposure, this investigation has employed next-generation sequencing technology to analyze the expression of the complete set of RNA transcripts of the heterogeneous groups exposed by MB-US exposure. The gene profiling in this study has provided us with new insights into the multitude of downstream bioeffects induced by sonoporation. Such a comprehensive understanding of the molecular impact of MB-US exposure is important for enhancing the efficacy of ultrasound-mediated drug delivery and related therapeutics.
