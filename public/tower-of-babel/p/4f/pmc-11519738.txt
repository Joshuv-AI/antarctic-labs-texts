The GDF6-FTO axis modulates the innate immune and inflammatory response to human respiratory syncytial virus
Authors: Zhu Li, Ling Zhang, Yi Liu, Haitao Li, Ling Gong, Xinran Tan, Jiahua Tian, Hang Pi, Bingyao Wang, Yu Zhao, Daishun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is one of the most common respiratory pathogens, causing a significant disease burden in young children and the elderly. Up to now, there is no specific drug in the clinic. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a ubiquitous RNA modification in eukaryotes that regulates disease processes caused by viral infections. We here find that the depletion of the demethylase FTO stabilizes the mRNA of growth differentiation factor (GDF6), enhances the levels of type I interferon (I-IFN), and reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby exerting an antiviral effect. Furthermore, a deficiency in the binding protein IGF2BP1 results in decreased GDF6 expression, which in turn reduces I-IFN production. We further demonstrated changes in m6A levels in RSV-infected throat swabs. In summary, we have demonstrated that RSV infection induces changes in host m6A modifications, and FTO-mediated m6A modifications ensure antiviral immunity by promoting the stability and protein translation of GDF6 mRNA.Subject areas: Molecular biology, Immunology, Virology

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a member of the Pneumoviridae family, and is an enveloped virus with a non-segmented negative-sense RNA genome, which has two subgroups, A and B. Currently, the most common subgroup in China is subgroup A.1,2 In particular, infants, toddlers, and the immunocompromised have the highest disease burden from RSV.1,3 Globally, in children younger than 6 months, 1.4 million hospital admissions, and 27,300 in-hospital deaths were due to RSV-ALRI.4 Children infected with RSV are also at a higher risk of developing chronic diseases, including allergic rhinoconjunctivitis,5 recurrent wheezing, and asthma.6 Currently, only palivizumab7 (for the prevention of high-risk infants) and nirsevimab8 (for the treatment of lower respiratory tract diseases caused by RSV in newborns) have received regulatory approval as monoclonal antibodies. Ribavirin is the only recognized antiviral medication for the treatment of RSV, but its safety concerns and limited efficacy have restricted its practical use.9,10 Therefore, the search for specific and non-specific methods to treat RSV infections remains a pressing issue to be addressed.

Recent studies have shown that RNA modifications are involved in the viral replication process and play a significant role. Notably, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most common modification of eukaryotic mRNAs and is involved in the regulation of a variety of cellular and pathological processes, including viral infections.11 m6A is added cotranscriptionally to nuclear pre-mRNAs by a multicomponent protein complex consisting of catalytic subunit methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and RNA-binding platform METTL14. METTL16 is cognate with METTL3, was reported to control the cellular SAM level and catalyze the m6A group onto the U6 small nuclear RNA. The removal of m6A from the decorated mRNA is catalyzed by the demethylase fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) or a-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5).12,13 Previous studies have indicated that m6A levels in the host cell transcriptome undergo changes during viral infections, which can either enhance host resistance (such as innate immunity and stress responses) or increase susceptibility to viral infection.14 During the course of Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV) infection, the RNA helicase DDX46 recruits the demethylase ALKBH5 to remove m6A from MAVS, TRAF3, and TRAF6 transcripts, increasing their nuclear retention and severely attenuating the antiviral factor interferon type I response.15 In Herpes Simplex Virus 1 (HSV-1) infection, hnRNPA2B1 blocks the demethylase FTO’s pathway on cGAS, IFI16, and STING transcripts, increasing methylation level and nuclear export, resulting in a stronger antiviral response.16 Like the role of m6A in regulating the HCV life cycle, the knockdown of host methyltransferases METTL3 and METTL14 increased ZIKV production while the silencing of demethylases ALKBH5 and FTO suppressed ZIKV production. The difference was that the m6A machinery decreased HCV production by affecting the assembly of virus, yet m6A modification inhibited ZIKV production by impacting replication of ZIKV.17 These studies collectively confirm the role of m6A in regulating innate immunity during viral infections, highlighting the crucial role of m6A modification in the process of viral infection.

Growth differentiation factor 6 (GDF6), also known as bone morphogenetic protein 13 (BMP-13), belongs to a subfamily of the BMP family. Research has indicated that GDF6 could downregulate pro-inflammatory factors, including tumor necrosis factor (TNFα), interleukin-1, and interleukin-6 (IL-1/6), and is involved in the protection of intervertebral discs.18 This suggests that GDF6 plays a regulatory role in inflammation, however, expression profile and function of GDF6 during virus infection is still unclear.

The current study explored the role of m6A modification following RSV infection in BEAS-2B cells and mouse models, and its relationship with clinical features in patient infected with RSV was then explored. In this study, RSV infection can lead to a decrease in overall m6A levels in the host, while FTO levels significantly increase. Mechanistic studies showed that FTO regulates antiviral immune responses, including upregulating type I-IFN and reducing the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1/6, ultimately impacting the replication of RSV virus, which is dependent on m6A modification of GDF6. Further analysis showed that GDF6 exerts its activity by modulation the IL-6/MyD88 signaling pathway. Together, our findings provide insight into how the m6A machinery, especially GDF6, is activated upon viral infection and highlight the central role of m6A in enhancing antiviral immune responses through the GDF6/FTO axis.

Results

Association of RSV infection with low-level of m6A mRNA methylation

To clarify the functional role of m6A modification in RSV infection, we first detected the level of m6A modification in mRNA of BEAS-2B cells after RSV infection. Using an m6A colorimetric and mass spectrum (MS) analysis, we found that m6A RNA modification decreased after RSV infection, which was dependent on both time and dosage (Figures 1A–1C). Similarly, throat swab samples were collected from infants and young children who were clearly diagnosed with RSV infection. Compared to healthy infants and young children, m6A abundance also decreased (Figure 1D). The RNA dot blot result showed that the m6A level in BEAS-2B cells after RSV infection was relatively lower than that without RSV infection (Figure 1E). Next, we examined the mRNA expression levels of the most common methyltransferases, METTL3/14/16, as well as the only two demethylases, FTO and ALKBH5.The results showed that the mRNA expression levels of different gene changed differentially at 24h after RSV infection. FTO, ALKBH5 and METTL3 mRNA expression were up-regulated in RSV-infected group compared with controls (Figure 1F). To further validate, we analyzed the changes in m6A-modified genes following RSV infection in the GSE3397 dataset through the GEO database, and only the mRNA expression level of FTO was consistent with our experimental results (Figure 1G). Considering that RSV infection could lead to a decrease in host m6A levels, we hypothesize that the demethyltransferases FTO may play a primary role during the RSV infection process. Subsequently, we assessed the protein expression of FTO and found that it was upregulated at both 24 h and 48 h (Figures 1H–1J). We also detected the levels of FTO in throat swab samples (Figure 1K) and in vivo (Figures S1G, S1K, and S1L), which were consistent with the results from the BEAS-2B cells. Furthermore, we also found that the levels of FTO in the throat swab samples were negatively correlated with m6A modification (Figure 1L). Conclusively, decreased m6A modification is observed during RSV infection, and it may be mediated by the increased expression of FTO.

RSV infection inhibited cell proliferation, activated innate immunity, and promoted the release inflammatory cytokines

BEAS-2B cells represented the first line of defense against viral infection. We first examined the virus replication after infecting cells with different TCID50. It was observed that with increasing infection time, the virus titer of RSV gradually increased (Figure 2A). In order to evaluate whether RSV infection affects cell proliferation, we used the CCK-8 assay to measure cell proliferation at different time points post-infection. We observed that the proliferation level of RSV-infected cells was lower than that of uninfected cells, with the most significant decrease observed in the 400 TCID50 group (Figure 2B). We next assessed the effects of RSV on the transcription level of inflammatory cytokines. Real-time PCR assay revealed a significant increase in the gene expression levels of inflammatory cytokines IL1α, IL1β, IL6, and CXCL8 in bronchial epithelial cells (Figures 2C–2F). Furthermore, we examined the expression of type I interferon α and β, the expression levels of IFN-α1 and IFN-β1 increased following RSV infection (Figures 2G and 2H). As expected, the protein levels of TLR7, MyD88, RIG-I and IL-6 were upregulated significantly in RSV-infected group compared with the control group (Figures 2I–2O). We further examined the above indicators in a mouse model infected with RSV. We measured viral titers, RSV-N, IFN-α1, IFN-β1, and IL-6 mRNA levels in mouse lung tissue (Figures S1B–S1F), and the protein levels of IL-6 and IFN-β in serum were detection by ELISA (Figures S1I and S1J), all of these showed significant increases in the infected group. HE staining showed that the alveolar cavity size was consistent in the normal group, the alveolar epithelial cells were more regular, the bronchioles were neatly arranged, and the alveolar interstitium was not significantly diseased. In the RSV infection group, the alveolar cavity size was different, alveolar epithelium was absent, interstitial disorder was present, a large number of inflammatory cells were infiltrated in the alveolar and peribronchiolar areas, the alveolar wall was thickened, and the lung histopathologic score was significantly increased (Figure S1A). The above research results indicated that RSV infection could inhibited cell proliferation and activated the expression of TLR7 and MyD88, subsequently promoting the transcriptional level of multiple inflammatory cytokines, thereby activating the immune response.

FTO is required for antiviral immunity in vitro

To clarify the mechanism by which FTO regulates antiviral immunity, we generated FTO knockdown immortalized normal human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) using lentiviral vectors (Figure 3A). Then we performed whole-genome RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Interestingly, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed the genes involved in immune response, innate immune response, and inflammatory response in FTO-deficient cells (Figure 3B). Consistently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that FTO was also required for the expression of antiviral genes during virus infection (Figure 3C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) reflected the activation of genes involved in inflammatory response after FTO depletion were decreased (Figures 3D and 3E). We then focused on the expression of type I IFNs, inflammatory cytokines, and ISGs after RSV infection. Surprisingly, the expression of type I IFNs, ISGs and TLRs (5,7,9,10) was significantly increased, while inflammatory cytokines were decreased in FTO-deficient BEAS-2B cells, especially IL-1 and IL-6 (Figure 3F). Consistent with these findings, real-time PCR analysis showed that FTO deficiency suppressed inflammation in RSV-induced BEAS-2B cells (Figures 3G–3L).

FTO regulates viral replication and inflammatory response

In this study, we have pointed out that RSV infection could reduce the host’s m6A level and inhibit cell proliferation. To evaluate the role of FTO, we examined the m6A level in cells with FTO knockdown, and the results confirmed that knocking down FTO could restore the host’s m6A methylation level (Figure 4A). We also observed a partial recovery of cell proliferation in the FTO knockdown group compared to the control group (Figure 4B). In addition, the viral load was lower in FTO knockdown group than in control group (Figure 4C), and we further observed a decrease in the expression levels of RSV-G/F proteins and mRNA through WB and real-time PCR detection in the FTO knockdown group as well (Figures 4D–4F). Conversely, overexpression of FTO promoted RSV-induced inflammatory response (Figures 4G and 4H), and the levels of type I interferon α1 and β1 also increased (Figures 4I and 4J). From this, we reasoned that FTO was critical for host defense against viruses.

GDF6 is a direct target of FTO

We performed a methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) analysis of BEAS-2B cells with RSV infection for 24h. According to the MeRIP-seq analysis, m6A modifications were typically located in a consensus “RRACH” motif (R = G or A, H = A, C, or U) (Figure 5A), and m6A peaks were particularly abundant in the vicinity of 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) near stop codons (Figure 5B), as previously reported.19 KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that m6A methylation was closely related to inflammation and immune pathways (Figure 5C). The RNA-seq analysis identified that 256 genes were down-regulated and 301 genes were up-regulated in BEAS-2B cells with FTO knockdown (Figure 5D). Next, we filtered the common genes that were down-regulated in MeRIP-seq and up-regulated in RNA-seq, resulting in the identification of 11 potential genes (Figures 5E and 5F). The results of real-time PCR confirmed the decreased expression of 4 genes (KCNG2, LGSN, GDF6 and ABLIM2) after RSV infection, which is consistent with the RNA-seq data (Figure 5G). Then, we selected the top two most down-regulated genes (GDF6 and ABLIM2) and proceeded to validate them in FTO-knockdown cells, revealing a significant increase in GDF6 (Figure 5H). We also detected the levels of GDF6 in throat swab samples and in vivo (Figures S1H, S1K, and S1M), which were consistent with the results from the BEAS-2B cells infected with RSV (Figure 5I). Western blot further confirmed that GDF6 abundance was suppressed in RSV-infected BEAS-2B cells and was up-regulated following FTO knockdown (Figures 5J and 5K). Collectively, these results indicate that GDF6 is a potential target of FTO.

GDF6 reduces viral replication and RSV-induced inflammatory responses in vitro

To investigate the functional roles of GDF6, we established GDF6 over-expression BEAS-2B cells, as verified by real-time PCR and Western blot (Figures 6A and 6B). Knockdown of GDF6 decreased the RSV-G mRNA and protein (Figures 6C and 6D) while reducing RSV replication (Figure 6E). Real-time PCR analysis showed that knockdown of GDF6 reduced the RSV-induced production of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1 and IL-6 (Figures 6F–6H) while upregulating the I-IFN (Figures 6I and 6J). Mechanistically, knockdown of GDF6 inhibited MyD88 activation (Figure 6K). These results supported that GDF6 mediates RSV-induced inflammation and viral replication.

Identification of GDF6 as a potential m6A-modified gene

To ascertain the presence of m6A modifications in GDF6 mRNA, we conducted an online analysis using the SRAMP platform. Our findings suggested that GDF6 mRNA likely harbors multiple m6A motifs, all of which were located within the coding sequence (CDS) region. Among them, the sequences GGACU and GAACU exhibited a very high confidence, positioned at positions 424 and 1199, respectively (Figure 7A). To further validate this, the sequences GGACU and GAACU were validated by performing m6A RIP-PCR (methylated RNA immunoprecipitation). The findings showed that anti-m6A antibody significantly enriched GDF6 mRNA level in BEAS-2B cells (Figures 7B and 7C). As previously confirmed, the m6A level was increased after FTO knockdown (Figure 7A). Subsequently, MeRIP-qPCR showed that m6A levels of GDF6-424 and GDF6-1199 mRNA were also significantly increased after FTO knockdown (Figures 7D and 7E). To explore the impact of m6A modification on GDF6, 3-Deazaadenosine (DAA, a global methylation inhibitor) was used and the findings showed that DAA significantly decreased mRNA and protein expression of GDF6 in BEAS-2B cells with or without FTO knockdown (Figures 7F–7J). Together, these results demonstrate that m6A modification affects the expression of GDF6.

FTO regulates GDF6 mRNA stability through m6A modification mediated by IGF2BP1

m6A modification-mediated of GDF6 mRNA was preliminarily verified. To further explore this effect, luciferase reporters containing either the wild-type or mutant GDF6 were constructed to explore the effect of m6A modification on GDF6 expression (Figure 8A). Findings from luciferase reporter assays showed that luciferase activity of wild-type GDF6 was significantly increased after FTO knockdown. However, knocking down FTO showed no effect on the luciferase activity of mut-type GDF6-424 and GDF6-1199. These findings indicate that FTO plays a role in m6A modification of GDF6 (Figure 8B). Expression of GDF6 increased significantly with increase in the level of m6A modification, implying that its m6A modification may increase stability of GDF6 mRNA. Subsequently, RNA stability assays were conducted and RNA stability curves showed that half-life of GDF6 mRNA was prolonged after FTO knockdown in BEAS-2B cells (Figures 8C and 8D). Therefore, upregulation of GDF6 induced by FTO can be attributed to increase in stability of GDF6 mRNA caused by m6A modification. Although m6A modification can be modulated by ‘writers’ and ‘erasers’, its functional outcomes are depended on selective recognition of m6A sites by ‘readers’. IGF2BPs (Insulin like growth factor 2 mRNA binding proteins) modulates stability of mRNA plays a role as a m6A reader.

Next, the depletion of IGF2BPs using small interfering RNA (siRNA) was verified through real-time PCR (Figures 8E–8G). Analysis showed that si-IGF2BP1, but not IGF2BP2/3 significantly downregulated GDF6 mRNA and protein in FTO knockdown cells (Figures 8H–8J). In summary, these findings indicate that FTO inhibits GDF6 mRNA stability and expression through m6A modification mediated by IGF2BP1.

GDF6 is required for antiviral immunity in vitro

To evaluate the physiological importance of GDF6 in antiviral immunity, the depletion of GDF6 using siRNA was verified through real-time PCR in BEAS-2B cells (Figure 9A). We then measured RSV-N mRNA through real-time PCR after FTO knockdown, much more virus was detected in si-GDF6 cells (Figure 9B). In contrast, the induction of IFN-α1 and IFN-β1 was decreased when using si-GDF6 compared to that without using si-GDF6 in FTO knockdown cells (Figures 9C and 9D), indicating that FTO-GDF6 axis might exist to regulate antiviral immune responses.

Discussion

To sum up, we showed that host’s m6A modifications play a critical role in viral replication. Knockdown of m6A demethylases FTO in host cells affected viral replication. In addition, inhibiting the m6A modification in the infectious FTO knockdown cells increased GDF6 mRNA and protein expression. Notably, we found that FTO targeted GDF6 and regulated its modification to modulate viral replication and immune response. Thus, our findings demonstrated that m6A modification represents an important component in the regulation of RSV replication.

m6A has been confirmed to exist in the infection of both RNA and DNA viruses, and as research progresses, the functions of m6A methylation in virus-host interactions have been reported. Assessment of the internal m6A modification status of host RNA following RSV infection using MeRIP, LC-MS/MS, colorimetric quantification and dot blot all revealed that alterations in m6A modification levels. LC-MS/MS analysis confirmed that host RNA following RSV infection contains m6A with a ratio of m6A/A of ∼0.013%. We also found that the expression of m6A-related proteins was altered by virus infection. FTO, the key components of demethylases, was up-regulated in vitro and vivo. We also found that FTO levels were higher in RSV-infected patients compared to uninfected individuals. Due to the narrower respiratory passages and thicker mucus in infants and young children, their airways can easily become blocked after RSV infection, leading to symptoms such as respiratory distress and, subsequently, the development of serious respiratory complications like pneumonia. Therefore, we further analyzed the imaging data of children infected with RSV. Among the 16 children with RSV infection, only 9 had chest CT or X-ray data available. The results showed that 6 of them exhibited inflammatory infiltrations in the lungs, characterized by scattered or patchy infiltrates, as well as ground-glass opacities. Through m6A colorimetric analysis, we observed that the m6A methylation levels of total RNA in throat swab samples from the RSV-infected group were lower than those in the uninfected group, which is consistent with the cellular level. The data analysis above revealed that lower m6A levels appeared to be associated with a higher rate of inflammatory lung infiltrations in the pediatric patients. However, only 56% of the children in the RSV-infected group had imaging data available, and the sample size was limited. Therefore, we need to increase the sample size and improve imaging examinations to conduct a more accurate analysis.

In addition, m6A modification has been linked to viral replication and gene expression. The m6A methylation of the SARS-CoV-2 genome negatively regulates viral infection. Meanwhile, viral infection also triggers an increase in host m6A methylation, resulting in changes in the localization and motifs of m6A methylation within host mRNAs.20 During the infection of oral epithelial cells with herpes simplex virus, downregulating the expression of FTO can increase the production of type I interferon, thereby promoting the clearance of the virus.21 The innate immune system recognizes the invading pathogens, including viruses, by employing a group of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). After recognizing the pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) of viruses, PRRs trigger innate immune signaling and initiate the transcription of antiviral immune genes, including type I IFNs, which are the primary antiviral effectors. Certainly, in our animal model, it has been shown that the level of type I interferon and inflammatory factors in both blood supernatant and lung tissue is significantly increased in RSV-infected mice, knockdown of FTO down-regulated RSV replication and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1/6/8, with the exception of type I interferon. Not only that, we here found that FTO targeted GDF6, which affected its m6A activity and enhanced antiviral immunity by promoting protein translation. We also assessment of the m6A modification status of GDF6 RNA following RSV infection using MeRIP-qPCR revealed that alterations in m6A modification levels. Moreover, mutation of the m6A modification sites in GDF6, following FTO knockdown, led to a decrease in luciferase activity. Additionally, inhibiting m6A modification using DAA in FTO knockdown cells with RSV infection decreased both GDF6 mRNA and protein expression.

We further found that si-IGF2BP1, but not IGF2BP2/3 significantly downregulated GDF6 mRNA and protein in FTO knockdown cells, which was similar to the effect of DAA. The results showed that m6A binding protein, IGF2BP1, could recognize the m6A modification of GDF6, thereby influencing its transcription process. Currently, the role of GDF6 in the context of viruses remains unclear. In our study, we found that GDF6 negatively regulated RSV replication, reduced the release of inflammatory factors, and enhanced the immune response. However, we obtained opposite results when using small RNA interference to downregulate GDF6 expression. The findings for the current study are summarized in the schematic model shown in Figure 9E.

Collectively, we present evidence supporting the crucial role of m6A demethylase FTO and its target GDF6 effect on the replication of RSV and antiviral immune response. The findings indicated that FTO epigenetically promotes expression of GDF6 through IGF2BP1-mediated m6A modification. These changes result in the host’s enhanced antiviral action through the upregulation of I-IFN. In addition, our study suggests a novel molecular mechanism of RSV infection progression regulated by FTO/GDF6 axis and provides valuable insights for development of efficient therapeutic strategies against RSV.

Limitations of the study

While we demonstrated that FTO regulates the function of GDF6 both in m6A modification and translation, there are still several limitations in this study. First, considering that the airway epithelium is the first line of defense against RSV infection, we chose BEAS-2B cells as the in vitro study model for this research, rather than A549 or other cell lines. On the other hand, previous studies have noted that the changes in m6A modification levels induced by RSV infection are inconsistent across different cell lines. As pointed out by Figueroa et al.,11 this may be due to differences in viral strains, cell models, and infection methods. Therefore, in this study, we conducted our research exclusively in the BEAS-2B cell line. Second, exactly how GDF6 relies on m6A to exert its function, as well as its interaction domain with FTO, remains unresolved. Third, studies have indicated the possibility of detecting GDF6 expression in the supernatant.22 In this study, we did not conduct a localization analysis for it, and it is advisable to collect blood samples from patients in future experiments to assess the levels of GDF6. Lastly, which small molecular compounds interact with GDF6 to mediate its antiviral immune upon virus infection remains to be solved. Based on the above issues, we will explore these areas in future research, aiming to provide a reliable foundation for the treatment of RSV.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical samples

This study included children (under 5 years of age) who were definitively diagnosed with RSV-A infection and underwent throat swab testing in the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University from November 2020 to February 2021. Participation for children was subject to a signed and witnessed informed consent form by the patient or his/her carer or guardian for children (under 16 years).

Exclusion criteria: 1) combined with other common respiratory viruses, including human metapneumovirus, influenza virus (A and B), human parainfluenza virus 1–4, human coronavirus, human rhinovirus, human adenovirus, human enterovirus, human bocavirus; 2) immunosuppression (defined as receiving solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, receiving chemotherapy, having a history of HIV or AIDS, or using steroids for more than 30 days).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the medical ethics committee of First People’s Hospital of Zunyi (The Third Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University, protocol code n. 2020-049, approved on 8 October 2020).

Cells and RSV infection

BEAS-2B (human bronchial epithelial cells) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), HEp-2 (Human epidermoid larynx carcinoma cells) and HEK293T (Human embryonic kidney 293T cells) were kindly provided by Prof. H.T.L. All cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were grown in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C. Confluent BEAS-2B cells in 12-well-plates were infected with RSV. After 90 min of adsorption, the inoculum was removed and the cells were washed three times with DMEM. Fresh DMEM (supplemented with 2% FBS) was added, and the infected cells were incubated at 37°C. At different time points post-inoculation, the supernatant and cells were harvested by three freeze-thaw cycles, followed by centrifugation at 1,500 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The virus titer was determined by TCID50 assay in HEp-2 cells.

Virus and viral replication kinetics

The human long strain of RSV was used in this study which was obtained from ATCC. RSV was grown and titrated on HEp-2 cells monolayers. The cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed by light microscopy at 5–7 days post inoculation. Viral titer (2 × 10−3.1 TCID50/mL) was calculated by Reed and Muench method.23 All experiments with the live RSV followed the approved standard operating procedures of the NRCEM biosafety level 2 facility.

Animals and RSV infection

SPF-grade female BALB/c mice aged 5–6 weeks (17–19 g) were purchased from Bai Shitong Animal Experimental Center, in Xiangzhou District, Zhuhai City, Guangdong Province, China. For RSV infection, mice were anesthetised with 5% isoflurane and infected intranasally with RSV long strain for 3 days (100μL/day, 2 × 10−3.1 TCID50/mL). All animals were weighed daily. Data analysis was performed 5 days post infection. The left lungs were removed, aerated, and fixed in a centrifuge tube filled with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h for paraffin section. The right lungs were removed and stored in a refrigerator at −80°C for subsequent RNA, protein extraction and lung tissue homogenization analysis.

Method details

Colorimetric quantification of RNA m6A methylation

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was analyzed by NanoDrop. The changes of m6A level in BEAS-2B cells were measured by EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Colorimetric) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 200ng RNAs were used for each sample analysis. Briefly, m6A was detected using a specific capture anti-m6A antibody, followed by colorimetric quantification by reading the absorbance at 450nm. A standard curve was generated using known m6A methylated RNA (range from 0.02 to 1ng of m6A) as a positive control. The m6A level was calculated from each RNA samples based on their OD450 values.24,25

LC-MS/MS for m6A/A determination

Total RNA from each sample was quantified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 instrument and ∼5μg total RNA was used to isolate mRNA using NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. Purified mRNA was quantified using Qubit RNA HS Assay kit (ThermoFisher, Q32855). mRNA was hydrolyzed to single nucleosides and then nucleosides were dephosphorylated by enzyme mix. Pretreated nucleosides solution was deproteinized using Satorius 10,000-Da MWCO spin filter. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on Agilent 6460 QQQ mass spectrometer with an Agilent 1260 HPLC system using Multi reaction monitoring (MRM) detection mode (Shanghai, China).

Dot blot assay

An RNA m6A dot blot assay was used to measure the m6A content in the poly-A tailing of total RNA. In brief, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNAs (1000 and 1200 ng respectively) were double diluted and spotted onto a nylon membrane (Beyotime, FFN10). Then the membranes were ultraviolet crosslinked and blocked in 5% nonfat milk, then was incubated with the m6A antibody overnight. The membranes were finally incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Signals were detected using LI-COR/Odyssey infrared image system (LI-COR Biosciences). Then 0.02% methylene blue in 0.3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) was used to show the amount of total RNA.

MeRIP-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol, and DNAse was added to remove DNA. MeRIP assays were performed using the m6A RNA Enrichment Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Target m6A-containing fragments were pulled down using a beads-bound m6A capture antibody, and RNA sequences in both ends of the m6A-containing regions were cleaved using Cleavage Enzyme Mix. The enriched RNA was then released, purified and eluted. RT-PCR was performed following MeRIP to quantify changes in target gene m6A methylation. The m6A sequences are presented in Additional file 1 (Table S1).

Cell transfection and lentivirus infection

GDF6, IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, IGF2BP3 and NC siRNAs were obtained from Tsingke Biotech (Guangzhou, China). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Recombinant lentiviruses containing sh-FTO was constructed to stably knockdown FTO stably. Nontargeting shRNA (sh-Ctrl) was used as negative control. GDF6 and FTO were overexpressed using recombinant lentiviruses containing complete coding sequences of these genes. Empty lentivirus (Vector) was used as negative control. Infected cells were selected by puromycin. Transfection and infection efficiency were determined using real-time PCR and western blotting. All of their sequences are presented in Additional file 1 (Table S2).

Luciferase reporter assay

Fragments from GDF6 containing the wild-type m6A motifs (GDF6-Wt) and mutant m6A motifs (m6A was replaced by T, GDF6-Mut) were subcloned into pMIR-REPORT firefly luciferase vector (Genewiz, Suzhou, China). Wild type and mutant GDF6 luciferase plasmids were transfected into BEAS-2B cells with knockdown FTO, using Renilla luciferase expressing vector pRL-TK (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Relative luciferase activity was determined using dual-luciferase reporter assay system. Relative firefly luciferase activity normalized to Renilla luciferase activity was then determined. The wild-type and mut-type sequences are presented in Additional file 1 (Table S3).

Transcriptome sequencing(RNA-seq) and methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq)

RNA-seq were performed by LC Bio Technology CO.,Ltd (Hangzhou, China), and MeRIP-seq were performed by E-gene Biotech (Shenzhen, China). rRNAs were eliminated from the total RNA before performing RNA-seq and RNA libraries were constructed. Quality control and quantification of the library was conducted and RNA-seq was performed on illumina NovaseqTM 6000 platform. For m6A-sequencing, about 20 μg of total RNA from each sample was fragmented using 10× RNA Fragmentation Buffer (Invitrogen) by incubating in a preheated thermal cycler for 10 min at 70°C. Fragmented RNA was pelleted by ethanol precipitation. The MeRIP libraries using eluted RNA were constructed using SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit version 2 (Takara/Clontech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All libraries were analyzed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and quantified by real-time PCR before sequencing.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

According to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit, 1000ng RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA. Real-time PCR analysis was performed using SYBR PCR mix kit according to supplier instructions. GAPDH as housekeeping control for real-time PCR. Sequences of the primers are provided in Additional file 1 (Table S4).

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio, Beijing, China) on ice for 20 min, follow by centrifuge at 12,000 rpm at 4° for 20 min. Then the supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was quantified by BCA method. Cell lysates were mixed with 5×loading buffer (Invitrogen) and heated at 100°C for 10 min. Next, equal amounts of total lysate were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, proteins were transferred to PVDF membrane, incubated first with blocking solution, then with appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Membranes were then washed, incubated with appropriate peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed by ECL. Protein band intensity was measured and quantified by ImageJ software.

CCK-8 analysis

Cell proliferation ability was determined by CCK-8 assay. BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 96-well plates (4000 cells/well) in 100 μL of DMEM containing 10% FBS for 24 h. Then the culture medium was aspirated and discarded, and the experimental groups were respectively infected with different TCID50 RSV. After adsorption for 90 min, 10% FBS medium was added into each well. 10 μL of CCK-8 was added in each well at 12, 24, 48, 72 h respectively, and kept for an additional 2h. Absorbance at 450nm was measured using the spectrophotometer. Untreated cells were used as the negative control, and solutions containing DMEM and CCK-8 without cells was used as blanks.

ELISA analysis

Serum supernatants were collected after the intervention according to the previous grouping, and IL-6 and IFN-β (Wuhan, China, Elabscience) were used to detect them respectively. This test was carried out regarding the kit instructions.

RNA stability

To assess GDF6 stability, cells were incubated with actinomycin (1 μg/mL) to terminate transcription. Samples were collected at 0, 3, 6, 9 and 12 h after termination. The total RNA was extracted, and the GDF6 expression was determined by real-time PCR.

Histopathology

For the histopathological analysis, the left lungs were embedded in paraffin blocks, cut into 4-μm sections and stained with H&E. The peribronchial and perivascular inflammation was scored according to BARENDS et al.26 as absent (0), minimal (1), slight (2), moderate (3), marked (4) or severe (5). Inflammation scores were calculated by two independent researchers in a double and blind manner.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 software (IBM, Chicago, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software (San Diego, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicates, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t test or analysis of variance. Spearman rank correlation was used to correlation analysis. Differences in means were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Significance levels are: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
