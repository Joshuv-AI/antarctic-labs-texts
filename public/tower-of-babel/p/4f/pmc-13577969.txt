Clinical applications of vascular and non-vascular interventional procedures for various urologic diseases: a pictorial essay
Authors: Euntaek Hwang, Doo Ri Kim, Young Hwan Kim, Jung Hee Hong, Byoungje Kim, Mu Sook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOwing to the recent aging of the population and the increasing number of patients at high surgical risk, the role of interventional radiology in diagnosing and treating urologic diseases has continually expanded in scope and importance. It has evolved from a simple complementary diagnostic tool to a first-line treatment strategy, encompassing applications ranging from basic image-guided biopsy to urinary drainage procedures, double-J stent placement, endovascular management, and tumor ablation. This pictorial essay presents representative clinical cases to illustrate the core concepts and characteristic imaging findings of these procedures. This approach is intended to provide practical insights for physicians in the urology and radiology departments, thereby guiding appropriate interdisciplinary treatment strategies in clinical settings.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00261-026-05485-2.Keywords: Radiology, Interventional, Urologic diseases, Drainage, Endovascular procedure

Introduction

Imaging-guided interventions are changing the diagnosis and treatment of various urologic diseases. Interventional radiology (IR) has several advantages over most surgeries, as it is minimally invasive, have shorter recovery times, lower risk of complications, and can be repeated when necessary. Therefore, the indications for IR to treat urologic disorders have been growing [1]. In particular, population aging has increased the number of patients with complex underlying diseases, leading to more cases in which traditional surgical approaches are hindered by the risks imposed by general anesthesia. In this clinical context, IR is undergoing a paradigm shift from simple salvage therapy to first-line strategies to improve survival rates and preserve patients’ quality of life. Furthermore, technological advances in imaging and IR have greatly improved diagnostic accuracy, as well as treatment efficiency and precision for urologic diseases.

Despite the expanding use and ongoing development of IR, comprehensive pictorial essays that systematically integrate procedural steps and imaging findings remain relatively scarce. Therefore, this review presents core concepts and representative cases of major vascular and non-vascular interventions for urologic diseases to provide practical clinical guidance.

Vascular interventional procedures

Vascular interventional procedures are advanced, specialized, and minimally invasive procedures that target the blood vessels associated with the genitourinary organs, including the kidneys, prostate, testis, and penis. Representative procedures include embolization to control bleeding or block abnormal blood flow, and angioplasty and vascular stenting to restore blood flow by dilating narrow vessels, and varicocele intervention.

Vascular access is typically established via the transfemoral route under ultrasound guidance, and the modified Seldinger technique is used to access the vessel [2]. Intravascular embolic agents are broadly classified as temporary or permanent and solid or liquid agents, and are selected based on their purpose, including the clinical setting, target vessel size, and required occlusion duration (Table 1) [3].

Transcatheter angioembolization (TAE) of the renal artery

TAE in renal and postoperative retroperitoneal bleeding.

TAE is a well-established treatment option for managing bleeding from renal trauma, pseudoaneurysms, or arteriovenous fistulas after biopsy or surgery (Fig. 1a) [1]. It is also effective for hemorrhage secondary to cyst rupture in patients with cystic kidney disease (Fig. 1b) or postoperative retroperitoneal hemorrhage following nephrectomy (Supplementary Fig. 1) [4].

TAE for the management of renal tumors

In addition, TAE is used to treat renal tumors. Current guidelines recommend elective embolization for angiomyolipomas (AMLs) ≥ 4 cm; nonetheless, TAE is highly effective in reducing tumor size and preventing bleeding (Fig. 2a) [5]. For renal cell carcinoma (RCC), TAE is performed preoperatively or as an adjunctive treatment before radiofrequency or cryoablation and can also be used for the palliation of non-operable disease (Fig. 2b) [5]. It is predominantly used as a palliative treatment for metastasis to other organs, including the bone and liver (Supplementary Fig. 2) [6].

Transcatheter embolization of male reproductive organ diseases

Prostate artery embolization (PAE)

PAE is a minimally invasive procedure that was introduced to treat lower urinary tract symptoms caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia and has a similar clinical effect to transurethral resection of the prostate [5]. The procedure can be performed under local anesthesia with no skin incision. It has a short recovery time, enabling an early return to daily activities, and can be repeated if necessary. Owing to these advantages, PAE is relatively safe, even in older patients at high risk (Fig. 3a) [7].

Penile artery embolization

Priapism is a persistent erection lasting > 4 h that is unrelated to sexual stimulation. Non-ischemic high-flow priapism, commonly caused by penile or perineal trauma, is a well-known indication for embolization (Fig. 3b) [8]. However, in very rare cases, malignant priapism can occur, usually caused by penile metastasis of urogenital cancer, when malignant cells invade the cavernous sinus. Therefore, to alleviate symptoms and improve the quality of the patient’s remaining life, penile artery embolization (Fig. 3c) can be an effective treatment option [9].

Embolization of varicocele

A varicocele is formed from an abnormal, tortuous, dilated vein within the pampiniform plexus of the spermatic cord and is usually asymptomatic. The direct association between varicoceles and male infertility remains a subject of debate; however, there is a clear correlation with a progressive decline in testicular function. In cases of scrotal pain that are unresponsive to conservative treatment, varicocele embolization may provide effective pain relief (Fig. 3d) [10].

Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting for renal artery stenosis (RAS)

Interventional treatment is indicated for hemodynamically significant RAS with a trans-stenotic pressure gradient ≥ 20 mmHg that does not respond to medical treatment or is accompanied by target organ damage [1]. The balloon or stent size is selected based on the diameter of the healthy, unaffected renal artery. Balloon-expandable stents are frequently used because they can provide optimal expansion and precise positioning at the lesion site (Fig. 4a) [11]. Transplant RAS is the most common vascular complication following kidney transplantation. In duplex ultrasonography, a peak systolic velocity ratio > 2 between the renal and external iliac arteries indicates clinically significant RAS. Interventional treatment is considered a first-line option for transplant RAS and has a technical success rate of > 90% (Fig. 4b) [12].

Non-vascular interventional procedures

Non-vascular interventional procedures refer to various percutaneous procedures performed under imaging guidance. Representative procedures include percutaneous drainage of abscesses, hematomas, or cysts; percutaneous nephrostomy for urinary diversion in cases of urinary tract obstruction or to create a conduit for urinary tract treatment; and suprapubic cystostomy. When planning percutaneous procedures, it is important to select the method that provides the most direct and shortest path to the target [7]. This means avoiding the colon, liver, spleen, pleura, and vascular structures. Generally, the procedure requires particular caution in patients with an international normalized ratio > 1.5 or a platelet count < 50,000 /cm3 [13].

Percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD)

PCD is the gold standard for treating abscesses, hematomas, lymphoceles, and pseudocysts. After reviewing images captured before the procedure, the puncture site and percutaneous route are selected under ultrasound or computed tomography guidance to place the catheter in the lesion site (Fig. 5a) [14].

Percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD). a Pre-procedure coronal computed tomography (left) image reveals a renal abscess in the left kidney (black arrows). The renal abscess cavity is punctured with a 21-gauge needle (middle, white arrow), and contrast is injected to confirm proper access (middle, black arrowhead). An 8.5 Fr pigtail catheter (right, black dashed arrow) is successfully placed for drainage. b PCD for renal cyst and subsequent sclerotherapy with absolute alcohol. Pre-procedure coronal CT image (left) reveals an 8.5-cm-sized left renal cyst (white arrows). Sclerotherapy with absolute alcohol is performed through the catheter inserted into the cyst (middle). Follow-up CT (right) shows marked cyst size reduction (white dashed arrows)

Renal cysts, including cortical and parapelvic cysts, are common and usually asymptomatic. However, large, infected, or bleeding cysts can be accompanied by symptoms such as flank pain. PCD and sclerotherapy (Fig. 5b) have a success rate of 75–100% as the primary treatment for renal cysts. Various sclerosants are available, but absolute alcohol is the most commonly used. Sclerotherapy is performed by completely draining the fluid in the cyst and injecting absolute alcohol, not exceeding 100 mL. Complications of sclerotherapy using absolute alcohol include bleeding, drug reactions, inflammation, fat necrosis, and retroperitoneal fibrosis [14].

Percutaneous urinary diversion

Percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN)

PCN is used for urinary tract obstruction, urinary extravasation, and diversion purposes. It is also used to provide a conduit for other treatments, such as percutaneous nephrolithotomy and ureteral stent placement (Fig. 6a, b) [15].

Knowledge of renal vascular anatomy, calyceal orientation, and the perirenal environment on imaging is crucial for achieving safe access while minimizing complications. The optimal approach for safe PCN is via the lateral margin of the paraspinal muscle, traversing the watershed zone (Brodel’s avascular zone) toward the posterior calyx. This method can mitigate bleeding risk. For urinary tract diversion, puncturing the lower calyx is the best approach. Conversely, puncturing the middle calyx facilitates subsequent procedures by providing a conduit for ureteral stent placement or nephrolithotomy. Upper calyx puncture should be avoided because of the risk of intercostal artery or nerve injury, pneumothorax, and catheter displacement. However, for transplanted kidneys, a lateral and upper-pole approach is required to avoid peritoneal puncture [13].

Pigtail catheters are predominantly used for PCN; however, straight, Malecot, double pigtail, and Foley catheters may also be used, depending on the specific circumstances. If a Kumpe catheter is inserted before a percutaneous nephrolithotomy, it can secure a path from the stone location to the distal ureter, greatly facilitating large-bore tract dilatation, sheath insertion, and intrarenal navigation (Supplementary Fig. 3) [7, 16]. Generally, PCN catheters should be changed every 2–3 months [15].

Suprapubic cystostomy

Suprapubic cystotomy is performed in patients who require long-term catheterization. Access should be obtained through the midline of the rectus sheath, approximately 2–3 cm above the pubic symphysis, and directed toward the bladder. Before the puncture, a bladder capacity of at least 300 mL should be maintained (Fig. 6c). The first catheter change after cystotomy should be performed after at least 4 weeks, followed by every 2–3 months [13].

Antegrade ureteric stent placement via the PCN tract

Antegrade ureteric stents are used to alleviate urine leakage or stricture by providing ureteral splinting or a conduit for urinary flow. Regarding the selection of stents, various designs and materials are utilized depending on the clinical contexts. The most widely used internal drainage device is a double-J plastic stent, which covers the full ureteral length and has multiple holes in the shaft and at either end (Fig. 6d) [15]. The appropriate stent length is selected based on the patient’s height, but a length of 22–24 cm is typically used [17]. While double-J stents typically require exchange every 3–6 months [15], conventional metal stents (e.g., Wallstent) or specialized covered metallic stents (e.g., Allium or Uventa) are available as durable alternatives in selected cases [18]. In contrast, a nephroureteral stent—which features locking loops in both the renal pelvis and bladder—serves as an internal-external device, incorporating an external drainage apparatus for versatile access [15].

Additionally, to treat ureteral stricture in transplanted kidneys, a tandem ureteral stent procedure can be performed by placing two 12 cm-long double-J stents side-by-side (Fig. 6e) [19].

Antegrade retrieval of a foreign body

Percutaneous antegrade retrieval of a foreign body via the PCN tract can be performed under fluoroscopic guidance without nephroscopy. It is conducted to remove an entrapped or fractured double-J stent (Fig. 7a) or a fractured catheter (Fig. 7b); however, it can also be used to remove a ureteral stone (Fig. 7c) or a fungus ball. Various instruments can be used to remove foreign bodies from the urinary tract, including loop snares, baskets, and forceps [14, 15].

Realignment of ureteral or urethral injuries

Iatrogenic injury to the ureter is an extremely rare but serious complication that can arise during urogenital and gynecologic surgery. The most common mechanism of injury is complete or partial transection. If ureteral injury is recognized during surgery, repair can be attempted immediately; however, the injury is not identified immediately in 50–70% of cases. Therefore, IR aids in the diagnosis and treatment of ureteral injury. Despite the ongoing debate regarding the treatment of ureteral injury, an interventional anterograde approach may be initially considered. If the anterograde approach fails or in cases of complete ureteral transection, a rendezvous approach (Fig. 8a–c) can be attempted before surgical repair [20].

Realignment of ureteral and urethral injuries. a–c Combined interventional-endoscopic (rendezvous) technique for iatrogenic ureteral injury. a Delayed-phase coronal computed tomography shows urinary leakage (black asterisk) between the rectum and bladder (black arrows) with contrast opacification of the left distal ureter (black arrowhead). b A snare catheter (black arrow) is advanced antegrade through the percutaneous nephrostomy tract toward the left ureter transection site and captures a retrograde guidewire (black arrowhead) introduced cystoscopically (left). A double-J stent (right, black dashed arrows) is successfully deployed over the guide wire, traversing the transected segment, and an antegrade pyelography confirms restored ureteral patency without urinary leakage. The rendezvous technique is illustrated schematically in c. d Primary interventional urethral realignment for traumatic urethral rupture. A retrograde urethrography (left) shows complete anterior urethral rupture (black arrow) without filling of the proximal urethra and extravasation at the bulbar urethra (black asterisk). A guidewire (middle, black dashed arrows) is successfully advanced across the urethral rupture site and into the bladder. A 14 Fr Foley catheter is placed over the guide wire to maintain alignment (right)

The main treatment principle for traumatic urethral injury is to restore the normal continuity of the injured urethra. Treatment methods differ depending on the cause, location, and severity of the injury. However, for non-penetrating urethral injury, the effectiveness of early primary endoscopic or interventional realignment is viewed more positively than before. Primary interventional urethral realignment (Fig. 8d) is a procedure to maintain urethral alignment by passing a guide wire through the injured urethral segment and into the bladder under fluoroscopic guidance, followed by inserting a Foley catheter along the wire. Seating the catheter tip firmly against the rupture point can provide sufficient back-up support, facilitating smoother wire advancement into the bladder [21]. These minimally invasive techniques enable the restoration of urinary tract continuity and may obviate or delay surgical reconstruction in selected patients.

Tumor ablation

Radiofrequency, microwave ablation, and cryoablation are thermal ablative therapies in which energy is delivered directly to induce cell damage and necrosis. These therapies are recognized as evidence-based approaches for the treatment of localized small RCC measuring ≤ 4 cm. In particular, these methods are used for alternative management in patients in whom surgical access is challenging, such as older patients with many comorbidities or those in whom functional renal parenchyma must be preserved, including those with a single kidney, kidney failure, or von Hippel–Lindau syndrome (Fig. 9) [22].

Radiofrequency ablation of renal cell carcinoma. a A renal mass is identified in the lower pole of the right kidney on computed tomography (CT) (white arrow) and ultrasonographic images (white dashed arrows). b Immediate post-ablation ultrasonography shows a hyperechoic zone (white arrows) representing microbubbles secondary to tissue vaporization. c Follow-up CT confirms complete ablation (white arrow) without evidence of local tumor recurrence

Conclusions

IR plays an important role in the management of various urologic diseases by enabling minimally invasive, image-guided treatments. Given the complex pathophysiology of urogenital disorders, the integrated application of vascular and non-vascular interventions, guided by close interdisciplinary collaboration between clinicians and radiologists, is essential for optimizing patient-specific treatment strategies.
