The feasibility of a set of physical resilience measurements in older patients admitted to the acute medical unit
Authors: K. D. J. Bos, J. A. H. R. Claassen, F. H. Bosch, C. J. M. Doggen, S. J. H. Bredie, R. J. F. Melis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost patients admitted to the Acute Medical Unit (AMU) are 60 years and older, have several multimorbidities, and are frail. During the short AMU stay, it can be challenging to determine whether these patients can recover safely at home or require an extended hospital stay. Determining physical resilience could support clinicians in deciding between discharge or prolonged hospitalization, because recovery from an acute condition is strongly related to a person’s resilience against deterioration. This research aims to evaluate the feasibility of two potential resilience measurements, sit-to-stand challenge (SSC) and grip work assessment (GWA), in acutely ill patients 60 years and older admitted to the AMU.MethodsA two-centre feasibility pilot study including 50 patients 60 years and older admitted to the AMU, the Netherlands, was conducted. Participants completed ‘The Older Persons and Informal Caregivers Survey Short Form’ about their health-related quality of life and performed the SSC and GWA in the first 48 h of admission. Patient experience with these methods was evaluated.ResultsOf the participants, 46% could complete the SSC in 12 (median;q1-q3 = 10–13) minutes and 74% could complete the GWA in 5 (median;q1-q3 = 4–5) minutes. The majority of participants experienced the resilience measurements as doable and not burdensome. Significant barriers to perform measurements were a deteriorated patient condition and patients in isolation who could not be measured.ConclusionUtilizing the GWA appeared feasible for older patients admitted to the AMU, whereas the SSC frequently presented difficulties due to clinical conditions and technical challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06252-6.

Introduction

The decision to discharge older patients to their home environment or to continue their hospitalization is a delicate balance, especially in acutely ill patients at the Acute Medical Unit (AMU). In the Netherlands, patients who arrive at an emergency department are frequently admitted to the AMU, which primarily admits patients with acute illnesses from various specialties needing acute care, where they are closely observed for a maximum of 48 h, after which patients are discharged home, or care is escalated to an admission in a specialized ward or even the intensive care unit. Most patients with acute illnesses admitted to the AMU are 60 years and older, have several comorbidities, and often are frail. In these patients, the standard tests to evaluate recovery capacity, such as clinical observation, vital sign measurements, and standard diagnostic tests, may be inadequate, making assessing their diminished recovery capacity or resilience challenging. This limitation could lead to incorrect discharge decisions, which can have severe consequences for older patients. Premature patient discharges may result in complications and subsequent hospital readmissions, while extended hospital stays increase the risk of complications, ultimately endangering the independence of older patients [1].

One approach to accurately assess readiness for discharge or follow-up hospitalization involves measuring physical resilience, which is the individual’s capacity to resist (functional) decline and recover physical health following a stressor [2]. Although there is currently no standardized accepted method to measure physical resilience, it is hypothesized that we can gain an impression of the physical resilience of a patient by applying paradigms such as stimulus–response and fatigability [3–5]. These methods include, e.g., the 'grip work assessment' (GWA), measuring muscle fatigability, and the 'sit-stand challenge' (SSC), evaluating the blood pressure (BP) response to the hemodynamic challenge induced by postural change. The BP response to postural change has been shown to predict survival [6, 7].

These measurements, previously utilized to determine the cardiovascular response, muscle strength, and endurance [8], offer several advantages over other potential stimulus response or fatigability tests. First, these measurements are safe and already established in clinical practice. For instance, the GWA, a modified procedure to measure handgrip strength, is a component of the Fried Frailty Criteria included in the routine Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment conducted in geriatric wards [9]. Similarly, the SSC, a modified procedure to the supine-to-stand challenge, is used to diagnose orthostatic hypotension in geriatric or neurology wards [10]. Second, these assessments possess several practical advantages: they are relatively easy to perform, require minimal resources and training, and can be completed quickly [11, 12]. Furthermore, these assessment techniques have been validated in diverse populations, demonstrating their applicability and reliability [10, 13, 14].

While these resilience measurements have been implemented in other departments, their application in acutely ill patients 60 years and older in the hectic setting of the AMU is unexplored. In this study, we aim to evaluate the feasibility of a set of standardized resilience measurements in acutely ill patients 60 years and older admitted to the AMU. We will determine the feasibility by determining the number of patients who could complete both resilience measurements, the time to complete, the number of successful measurements, and the overall experience of the participants.

Methods

Participants and design

This feasibility study was designed as a prospective observational study in older patients aged 60 and over who were admitted to the AMU. The study was conducted at two medical centres in the Netherlands: one university hospital (Radboud University Medical Centre (Radboudumc) Nijmegen), and one teaching hospital (Rijnstate Hospital Arnhem). Patients aged 60 years or older, admitted to the AMU for internal medicine, and able to speak and read Dutch were considered eligible for inclusion. Furthermore, only patients for whom measurements could be performed within 48 h after admission were included. Notably, there were no exclusion criteria. In the university hospital, the institutional review board waived the need for formal review for this type of research according to Dutch law (CMO 2022–16108). In the teaching hospital, the study protocol was approved by the lokale haalbaarheid commissie (2023–2127). All participants gave written informed consent after being informed about the study protocol.

Materials, procedures and data analysis

When a patient who met the inclusion criteria was admitted to the AMU, the researcher asked the patient to participate. In cases where asking the patient was impossible, the researcher documented the underlying reason. These reasons were categorized into five groups, encompassing organizational factors (e.g., premature patient discharge), medical considerations (e.g., clinician-perceived frailty or illness), lack of clinician permission for asking the patient, isolation for infection prevention, and other reasons.

If a patient gave informed consent, the patient completed the “The Older Persons and Informal Caregivers Survey-Short Form” (TOPICS-SF), the sit-to-stand challenge (SSC), the grip work assessment (GWA), and a feasibility questionnaire. The SSC and the GWA were repeated once within four hours to determine the consistency in the repeated measurements, assuming no change in clinical condition. Besides the resilience measurements, we collected the participants’ demographics, the reason for admission, comorbidities, medication use, and the discharge date and location from the electronic medical records.

TOPICS-SF

The TOPICS-SF is a patient-reported outcome measure in geriatrics, encompassing the domains subjective health, quality of life, (social) functioning, and psychological well-being [15, 16]. Based on questions about daily functioning as part of the TOPICS-SF, the activities of daily living (ADL)-scores and instrumental activities of daily living (iADL)-score were calculated. The ADL and iADL score range from 6 to 24 and 4 to 16, respectively, where higher scores indicate greater dependence. Additionally, the Frailty Index (FI) was calculated based on 35 items of the TOPICS-SF, including the comorbidities, (social) functioning, physical well-being, and subjective health scores. The FI can range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate more frailty.

The sit-to-stand challenge

The SSC determined the blood pressure (BP) recovery after standing up. The challenge consists of 5 min of sitting, followed by standing up and standing for 3 min. During this challenge, the BP was measured continuously using the Finapres NOVA (Finapres Medical Systems, Enschede, the Netherlands) [17]. During the measurements, participants held their hand at heart level, supported by an arm sling to prevent hydrostatic pressure artefacts.

BP data obtained during the SSC were processed in MATLAB (2023a, MathWorks Inc., Natick, USA) using custom-written semi-automatic scripts [18]. Subsequently, two researchers assessed the data visually on suspected artefacts or errors. After pre-processing and selecting the data, the baseline SBP and DBP were defined as the average value between 40 and 10 s before standing up from a sitting position, followed by the determination of SBP and DBP recovery at 60 s after standing up. These recovery measures were presented as the percentage difference of baseline according to Eq. 1:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B{P}_{recovery}=\frac{mean \left(B{P}_{55-60}\right)- B{P}_{baseline}}{B{P}_{baseline}}*100$$\end{document}

With BP55-60 being the mean blood pressure between 55 and 60 s after standing up, and BPbaseline being the mean blood pressure at baseline [7].

Based on the BP recovery percentage, the participants were classified into three groups with different recovery patterns. Participants with a full-recovery pattern were defined to have a recovery to > 95% of their baseline BP, while participants with a partial-recovery pattern had 80%—95% recovery, and participants with a no-recovery pattern had < 80% recovery [7].

Grip work assessment

The GWA was used to determine the grip strength (GS), fatigue resistance (FR), and grip work (GW). This assessment followed the protocol used in clinical practice [6]. The GS was measured at the dominant hand with the Eforto® setup, a system based on the Martin Vigorimeter [19]. The seated participant was asked to put the arm and hand in a specific position as mentioned in the protocol and, at the same time squeeze the rubber ball three times as hard as possible, with a rest period of 30 s between successive attempts. The highest of three attempts was noted to determine maximal GS [6, 19]. The FR test immediately followed the GS test.

FR, defined as the time it takes for GS to decrease to 50% of its maximum, was measured by instructing the participant to exert maximal force while squeezing the rubber ball for as long as possible [20]. Finally, the GW is determined by the multiplication of GS and the FR using Eq. 2 [21]:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GW=0.75*G{S}_{max}*FR$$\end{document}

With GW being the grip work (kilopascal (kPa)*s), GSmax being the highest maximal grip strength reached during GSmax and FR-test (kPa), and FR being fatigue resistance (time (s)). The results for the GWA were presented separately for males and females due to the differences between the sexes.

Feasibility questionnaire

A semi-structured feasibility questionnaire, based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [22], was used to ask participants about their effort expectancy, the availability of resources and support, the experience of discomfort, and the experienced (dis)advantages. The eight questions were asked to the participants immediately after completing the resilience measurements for the first time. The questionnaire can be found in Additional file 1.

Study outcomes

The primary study outcomes were the number of participants (%) who could complete the standardized set of resilience measurements, the number of successful measurements (%), the time needed to perform the resilience measurements, and the experience of the participants (e.g., discomfort during measurement and satisfaction). A successful measurement is a completed measurement without artefacts or errors.

The secondary outcomes were the reason why it was not possible to perform the resilience measurements, the (i)ADL-score, the FI, the time between admission and measurement and between measurement and discharge. Additionally, we determined the resilience measures GW, GS, FR, and BP recovery. Finally, the consistency in repeated measurements was investigated.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data or median (q1 – q3) for otherwise distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (proportions).

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated between the first and the second (about 4 h later) measurement. This was calculated to determine the percentage of the total variance attributable to between-person differences.

The sit-to-stand challenge

Within the cohort of 50 participants, 23 participants (46%) completed the SSC, while six participants (12%) initiated the SSC but were unable to finish it due to increasing symptoms such as increasing fatigue or feeling lightheaded. Two participants (4%) had to leave the AMU during the SSC. Additionally, technical complications were encountered in five cases (10%): in two cases the Finapres device did not work, and in three cases, the FinaPres could not be attached. Finally, fourteen participants (28%) could not start the SSC due to medical reasons (i.e., haemodynamic instability or inability to stand on legs due to infection or excessive pain) impeding their standing ability. During and after the SSC, several complaints were reported, including feelings of light-headedness (n = 5, 21%), instability (n = 5, 21%), dizziness (n = 3, 13%), and other complaints such as sensations of haziness, pain in the feet, legs, and the back (4, 17%).

Among the 23 completed measurements, six measurements (26%) show some suspected artefacts or errors deviating from the expected pattern of an increase in BP followed by a transient dip after standing up. These artefacts or errors could be attributed to factors such as the absence of an arm sling (n = 1), coughing during the measurement (n = 1), cold or poorly perfused fingers (n = 2), or unexplained significant fluctuations in the signal (n = 2). These six challenges were marked as unsuccessful challenges. The median (q1 – q3) duration of the SSC was 12 [10, 13] minutes in 23 participants.

The grip work assessment

Within the cohort of 50 participants, 37 participants (74%) completed the GWA. Eleven participants (22%) initiated the GWA, but they did not reach 80% of the maximum GS. This is a prerequisite for determining the FR. Additionally, one participant (2%) had to withdraw from the GWA before completion. Finally, one participant (2%) could not start the GWA due to post-exertion fatigue following the SSC. Complaints during and after the GWA were muscle pain (10%) and sensations of dizziness (2%).

During the 48 assessments which were started, connectivity issues arose in four cases (8%), leading to the disconnection between the Eforto device and the mobile phone. Consequently, remeasurement was necessary in these instances. In two other cases (4%), the Eforto device generated an unrealistic high (> 140 kPa) third GS measurement, rendering it impossible to complete the measurement since achieving the necessary 80% threshold was unattainable. The median (q1-q3) time to complete the GWA was 5 (4–5) minutes in 37 participants.

The consistency in repeated measurements

This study intended to measure all participants twice (with 4 h between the measurements) to determine the consistency of the results of the SSC and GWA. However, this was only possible for nine participants (38%) for the SSC and fourteen participants (29%) for the GWA. For both GWA and SSC, the main reason a repeated measurement was not possible because the patient was already discharged to home or another department in the hospital (87%). For the SSC, two participants (13%) deteriorated clinically, which made measuring a second time impossible. For the GWA, two participants (8%) deteriorated clinically, and one participant (4%) was not able to persist in the second measurement.

Participant experience

The sit-to-stand challenge

Twenty-five participants completed the feasibility questionnaire about the SSC. All participants reported the clarity of instructions provided for the challenge as clear and comprehensible. Most participants found the challenge straightforward and perceived it as a simple exercise. None of the participants found the SSC to be painful. Nevertheless, three participants indicated that standing during the assessment was fatiguing. Furthermore, two participants expressed reservations about the utility or relevance of the SSC in their specific circumstances, suggesting a potential query regarding the assessment's purpose or benefit. Overall, most participants who completed the challenge (18, 72%) scored on the fatigue scale the SSC as no or mild fatigue with a median (q1-q3) fatigue score of 2 (1–4), indicating low fatigue experienced by participants during the assessment.

The grip work assessment

Forty-eight participants completed the feasibility questionnaire about the GWA. Like the SSC, all participants found the instructions for the GWA to be straightforward and easy to comprehend. Furthermore, none of the participants reported experiencing pain during the assessment. The user experiences concerning the GWA varied. Most participants found the assessment manageable and rated it good or satisfactory. Nine participants were neutral to the assessment, indicating a lack of strong positive or negative feelings. Seven participants reported finding the GWA challenging, suggesting some individuals may have encountered difficulties while performing the task. Overall, most participants who completed the assessment (17, 58%) scored on the fatigue scale the GWA as no or mild fatigue with a median (q1-q3) fatigue score of 3 (1–6), reflecting a low fatigue level.

The standardized set of resilience measurements

Almost all participants think it is a good idea to make the measurements standard practice if participants can perform them. Some participants specifically mentioned as an advantage that it is good to look at fitness. Despite overall positive reception, a minor subset of participants (n = 7) expressed reservations about the measurements'utility. One participant indicated it was too tiring to do this on top of all the provided care and examinations already being performed.

Discussion

In this study, we determined the feasibility of a set of physical resilience measurements in acutely ill patients 60 years and older admitted to the AMU. Our results showed that utilizing the GWA appears feasible for these patients, whereas the SSC frequently presented difficulties due to clinical conditions and technical challenges and it may be necessary to be repeated for a reliable measurement.

The inclusion process

In this study, we focused on feasibility based on patient related factors. Of the 135 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 50 were included in the analysis to determine feasibility based on patient related factors. The primary organisational reason that patients were not eligible was isolation for infection prevention because the devices could not be properly disinfected. Another reason why patients could not be approached for the resilience measurements was due to their poor clinical condition. In these cases, patient safety was prioritised over maximising participation. Furthermore, it is already clear that discharge to home is impossible for these patients. Resilience measurements would provide a limited additional value in such scenarios. Finally, some patients found participating overwhelming and withheld informed consent.

The feasibility of the measurements

The feasibility of the SSC was limited to less than half of the participants, mainly because of their clinical condition. These results align with Melo et al. [23], where nearly half of the patients were unable to perform the five times SSC post-intensive care unit discharge due to medical permission and physical constraints [23]. Although not all participants could complete the challenge, those who initiated it found it painless and doable, a finding consistent with existing literature [24]. In approximately 10% of the cases, we encountered technical and practical issues with the FinaPres, prompting a need for further research to explore potential solutions.

While the SSC faced some challenges as described above, the GWA was found to be feasible, encountering only some technical problems in a few cases. The feasibility results are comparable to other studies [11, 25, 26]. This indicates that the GWA can be implemented in the standard care of the AMU to investigate whether these outcomes offer valuable insights into determining the discharge destination.

The results of the sit-to-stand challenge and grip work assessment

The SSC and GWA results were comparable to other studies [7, 14, 27]. The repeated measures showed a poor ICC for all outcomes except for the GS. Existing literature reports a low ICC (around 0.50) for SSC results [10], but our study reveals an even lower ICC, possibly due to the limited sample size (n = 9). The good and moderate ICC of GS and GW align with findings of other studies [14, 26], while the low ICC for FR contrasts with the existing literature [20]. This low ICC may be explained by variations in motivation during the assessments between the two measurements. Despite the researcher’s consistent motivational efforts, ensuring participants consistently apply maximum effort remains a challenge. Given the mix of increased and decreased FR values, it appears unlikely that a learning effect or systematic difference is a reason for the low ICC.

Although this study was not designed for reliability purposes, for implementation of a decision support system, low ICC results in our study indicate that averaging over more than one repetition is likely necessary to determine a reliable result especially for the SBP/DBP recovery and the FR in setting of AMU. Further research must determine the optimal frequency and feasibility of repeated measurements.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study lie in its two-centre design and adherence to standard care, providing a broad and representative selection of the patient population. Including a university and teaching hospital enhances the generalizability of findings across diverse healthcare settings. Additionally, conducting the study within the framework of standard care ensures that the results reflect actual clinical scenarios, enhancing the external validity of the findings.

However, certain limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the exclusive focus on internal medicine participants introduces a potential source of bias, as this population may exhibit distinct characteristics compared to, for instance, neurological or surgical patients. Nevertheless, other studies have shown that even in patients with hip fractures, for example, the GWA is applicable [25]. Second, there was an inability to measure patients in isolation for infection prevention. This limitation is a notable consideration given that isolated patients may particularly benefit from resilience measurements. Although this issue has since been resolved, it was not possible to take measurements in this group during the study period. Applying measurements to people in isolation will result in a broader applicability of the resilience measurements across various clinical scenarios. Third, our results cannot be generalized to patient groups who are much more ill than included here. We excluded too ill patients, because we did not want to burden them with study participation. Yet, patient related barriers to perform the measurements may differ for patient who are more ill than the patient eligible for our study. Fourth, the small sample size, particularly for repeated measurements, complicates this aspect's analysis. For the feasibility aspect, we included enough participants; however, further research should focus on determining the necessary number of measurements per participant and the insights these measurements offer regarding participants’ resilience. Fifth, we only asked the participants about their experience of the measurements and not the physician or nurse, while they will eventually take the measurements and interpret the results. Finally, in this study, we made no direct translation to physical resilience. Even though we did measure physical resilience paradigms with the SSC and GWA, we did not evaluate the significance for the participant’s resilience. As a concept, physical resilience is influenced by various organ systems encompassing, e.g., the immune, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal systems [28]. In further research, other organ systems should be considered. In addition, the recovery process is essential to determine the ultimate physical resilience. In this study, we did not prospectively monitor the recovery process (e.g., monitoring the readmissions). Further research should examine what the physical resilience measurements say about the recovery of patients and, therefore, physical resilience.

Further research and clinical relevance

An essential follow-up step involves determining the predictive value of the GWA and SSC measurements as physical resilience measures for clinical outcomes on top of standard diagnostic practice. This step is consistent with the ongoing development of the conceptualisation of physical resilience. For instance, the literature is divided on what the 'physical' aspect should refer to. In the meantime, implementing these measurements and collecting data could enhance our understanding of the relationship between the physical resilience measures and recovery trajectories within the AMU, which in turn may add to the ongoing conceptual debate [29]. To elaborate on this, incorporating follow-up assessment would enable an examination of the justification for home discharge. Integrating GWA as a standard measure at the AMU holds significant potential and is a feasible first step. Before considering the integration of SSC in standard care, alternative devices for measuring SSC outcomes should be explored. Finally, when implementing measurements, it is important to be aware of how comorbidities may limit people's ability to perform them. This feasibility study has shown that it will be necessary to consider other resilience measures than the ones used here for certain patient groups. In addition, it is important, to be aware of the potential burden of resilience measurements and take measures to avoid overloading patients.

Conclusion

The application of the GWA seems feasible for older acutely ill patients admitted to the AMU. In contrast, the SSC encounters frequent challenges, both technical and clinical, which makes it less feasible in the AMU setting.
