I wanted to refuse when they wanted to put me on oxygen: perception of patients and caregivers toward oxygen therapy in Mbale regional referral hospital, Eastern Uganda
Authors: Jamiirah S. Mutesi, Ashley Nakawuki, Eunice Namubiru, Paul Oboth, Rebecca Nekaka, Jacob. S. Iramiot, Immaculate Mbwali, Rose C. Nabirye, Joshua Epuitai, Pamella R. Adongo, Lydia VN. Ssenyonga, Samuel Olowo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxygen refers to a colorless, odorless, tasteless gas that is essential for proper body functioning and survival. However, the effectiveness of oxygen therapy can be influenced by the perceptions of patients and caregivers. Understanding patients’ and caregivers’ perceptions of oxygen therapy directly impacts healthcare practices. Consequently, the information gained from such research can be used to develop interventions that can help promote oxygen therapy’s benefits, leading to the acceptance and compliance with oxygen as treatment. This studied aimed to explore the perception of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH).MethodsThis study was conducted in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital involving in-depth interviews with patients and caregivers. The study employed a descriptive qualitative study. The qualitative data collected was thematically analyzed using the Braun and Clerk’s six step approach. The sampling technique was purposive sampling, sample size determination involved saturation sampling technique and the data collection tool used was in-depth interview guides. A total of 46 interviews were conducted including 16 patient interviews and 30 caregiver interviews.ResultsBoth patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy as a life-saving yet fear-inducing intervention, spiritual and emotional interpretations of oxygen therapy, perceived physical and technological aspects of oxygen therapy. Both patients and caregivers reported that their information about oxygen therapy was mainly obtained from schools, communities, hospitals, and the internet.ConclusionsThe perceptions of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy do not necessarily differ from each other. Patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy in terms of; oxygen therapy perceived as a symbol of impending death like oxygen equals the end-of-life; emotional and psychological distress caused by oxygen therapy like fear and anxiety; spiritual and faith-based interpretations of oxygen therapy like attribution of survival to divine interventions. We recommend the implementation of community sensitization campaigns and health education programs on oxygen therapy to improve awareness and understanding of oxygen therapy. In addition, counselling and psychological support should be provided to patients and caregivers struggling with emotional distress and fear related to oxygen therapy. Further research is needed to assess the effectiveness of health education and awareness interventions in improving the perceptions of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15195-6.

Background

Oxygen refers to a colorless, odorless, tasteless gas that is essential for proper body functioning and survival [1]. Oxygen therapy refers to the administration of oxygen to a concentration of pressure greater than that in the surrounding atmosphere to improve tissue oxygenation when levels are reduced due to illness or injury for-example in severe respiratory distress, hypoxemia, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [2, 3]. It is also used in the management of pulmonary hypertension, acute myocardial infarction, short-term therapy like in post anesthesia recovery and chronic lung disease patients during exercises [4, 5].

Globally each year, 374 million people need medical oxygen: 364 million in acute medical and surgical contexts, and 9 million with long-term oxygen needs due to COPD [6]. About 306 million of the 374 million people who need oxygen live in low and middle-income countries, 253 million in South Asia, east Asia and the Pacific, and Sub-Saharan Africa [6].

A study in Nigeria found out that caregivers were refusing oxygen treatment for their children due to misconceptions and pervasive negative narratives around oxygen therapy in their communities [7].

Despite the known benefits and applications of oxygen therapy, the perception of people varies widely. A study in Nigeria found out that in some cases, oxygen therapy is perceived as a death sentence [8]. In addition, individuals assume benefits of oxygen even outside medical prescription, for example, oxygen bars where oxygen is assumed to reduce stress, increase relaxation and energy [9]. Yet, Unregulated or excessive use of oxygen can potentially cause adverse effects [10]. Consequently, evidence based public information is essential to prevent and mitigate such misconceptions and promote acceptance of oxygen therapy among caregivers and patients. Available evidence demonstrates that the perception of patients towards oxygen therapy influences the compliance and acceptance of oxygen as treatment [11].

Despite these insights, little is known about the general public’s perception of oxygen therapy in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH) especially across different demographic groups and cultural contexts. This gap creates an opportunity to explore the view of different populations towards oxygen therapy and what factors shape these perceptions.

Understanding the patient’s and care giver’s perceptions towards oxygen therapy has a direct impact on healthcare practices. More so, the information gained from such research can be used to develop interventions that can help to promote the benefits of oxygen therapy, leading to the acceptance and compliance to oxygen as treatment. In fact, the knowledge obtained can be used to guide healthcare policy and preparation for future healthcare emergencies such as pandemics.

Despite the known benefits of oxygen therapy in improving the quality of life, many patients are still hesitant or refuse oxygen therapy. This has been reported to be influenced by their misconceptions and beliefs about oxygen therapy. These negative perceptions result into late or failure to administer oxygen therapy. Late initiation of oxygen therapy negatively impacts on the quality of life, social wellbeing and last phase of life [12].

A study carried out in three government funded District hospitals in Eastern Uganda which are under MRRH catchment area, reported that the local population was fearful of oxygen therapy and hesitant to consent for administration of oxygen therapy to their children [11]. The reason for the hesitance of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy is not clearly documented. Therefore, understanding the perception of the caregivers and patients towards oxygen therapy may help to fill the existing gaps. Consequently, the aim of this study is to explore the perception of the caregivers and patients towards oxygen therapy at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative study design to explore the perceptions of patients and caregivers toward oxygen therapy. It emphasized a detailed description of the experiences and events about oxygen therapy as well as capturing the essence of participants’ experiences, perceptions and beliefs [13, 14].

Study setting

The study was mainly facility-based at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital, a community-based hospital, in different wards that held patients on oxygen therapy, including the medical ward, surgical ward, casualty, surgical high dependency unit (HDU), and postnatal high dependency unit (HDU). Mbale Regional Referral Hospital, a government-funded and owned hospital, was located in the center of Mbale city, 250 km east of Kampala, the capital city of Uganda. The hospital served over 4.6 million people in 16 districts of Busia, Tororo, Butaleja, Pallisa, Budaka, Mbale, Bududa, Sironko, and Manafwa. Namisindwa, Butebo, Kapchorwa, Kibuku, Bukwo, Kween, and Bulambuli [15, 16].

Study population

The target population included the patients who had undergone oxygen therapy and were at the point of discharge, as well as their caregivers, and those caregivers who had ever taken care of patients on oxygen therapy in MRRH. The group of interest was required to be adult patients who received oxygen therapy and the adult caregivers who took care of patients on oxygen therapy.

Sampling technique

This study employed a purposive sampling method, a non-probability sampling technique in which decisions for participant inclusion were taken by the researcher based on knowledge, judgment, or capacity and willingness to participate in the research. Purposive sampling was important in that it provided researchers with the justification to generalize from the studied sample, it provided a wide range of non-probability sampling techniques for the researcher to draw on, and proper care was taken when selecting the sample [17, 18].

The researcher selected patients and caregivers who had had an exposure or experience with oxygen therapy and were willing to participate in the study.

Sample size determination

In this study, the sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation, which refers to the point at which no new themes, codes, or meaningful insights emerge from the data [19]. Initially, based on previous qualitative literature on saturation, it was estimated that approximately 16 participants comprising patients and caregivers would be sufficient to achieve saturation [20]. However, data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, and recruitment continued until thematic saturation was fully achieved. Saturation was assessed separately for patients and caregivers. While saturation among patients was reached earlier, additional caregiver interviews continued to generate new perspectives and experiences regarding oxygen therapy. Consequently, the final sample comprised 46 participants including 16 patients and 30 caregivers, at which point no substantially new information was emerging from subsequent interviews.

Data collection tools

The primary data collection tool was an in-depth interview guide. The in-depth interview guide was semi-structured and developed by the researchers based on the study objectives and existing literature on perceptions toward oxygen therapy. The guide was organized into several sections including: introduction and rapport building, collection of socio-demographic information, consent and ethical considerations, participants’ experiences with oxygen therapy, perceptions and beliefs regarding oxygen therapy, and support, information, and education received about oxygen therapy. Open-ended questions were used to allow participants to freely express their experiences and views. In addition, probing and follow-up questions were incorporated to obtain clarification, encourage elaboration, and explore emerging issues in greater depth. Separate interview guides were developed for patients and caregivers, although both explored similar thematic areas related to experiences, emotional responses, beliefs, fears, perceived benefits, and sources of information regarding oxygen therapy.

Data collection procedure

The data collection involved in-depth interviews with the patients and caregivers to explore their perceptions of oxygen therapy. In-depth interviews allowed for a comprehensive understanding of participants’ experiences and beliefs in their own words [21]. The researcher conducted the interviews in the local languages best understood by the participants, which included English, Luganda, and Lumasaba with the help of language experts who had a high level of knowledge and experience in the language, who translated the interview guide into the local languages. Each interview was conducted face-to-face in a private, comfortable, and quiet setting to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. The interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min, and audio recordings, after obtaining consent from the participants, were taken for accurate transcription and analysis. Field notes were also taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations. Participants were assured of their anonymity and their right to withdraw from the study at any point without consequences.

Trustworthiness

This study employed the four principles of trustworthiness by Lincoln and Gubba of Credibility, Conformability, transferability and dependability [22, 23] as expounded below:

Credibility

Credibility refers to how true is the collected data, the interpretation and representation of the data by the researcher. The researcher ensured credibility through thorough data collection including, prolonged engagement which facilitated rapport with informants and fostered rich detailed responses. Secondly the researchers ensured reflexivity with the informants to prevent researcher bias. Reflexivity means acknowledgement of the researcher’s own thoughts, feelings and actions and how they influence the participants’ responses [24]. In this study reflexivity was ensured by maintaining reflexive journals. In addition, the researcher maintained an audit trail that documented the researcher’s decisions and assumptions which was reviewed by co-investigators so as to draw the same study conclusions [25] Finally, the researcher described fully the strategies performed and the vivid quotes from participants, so that the reader can critique the credibility of the study.

Confirmability

Confirmability refers to the extent to which the researcher demonstrates that the data represents the participants responses and not researcher’s biases, motivations or interests [26]. Confirmability was ensured through maintaining an audit trial, reflexive journal and triangulation [27]. The researchers used reflexive journal in order to reflect on, tentatively interpret and plan data collection.

Dependability

Dependability refers to the consistency of findings under similar conditions or over time [26].

In this study, dependability was ensured through stepwise replication where two researchers analyzed the same data separately and compared the results. Inconsistences that arose were later discussed and addressed to improve the dependability, and for similar findings [28].

Transferability

Transferability refers to findings that can be applied to other contexts, that the findings have meaning to the individuals not involved in the study and readers can associate the findings with their own experiences [25]. Transferability was maintained through providing thick descriptive data concerning the methodology to allow comparison of the context to other contexts to which transfer might be contemplated [27].

Data analysis

The researcher employed reflexive thematic analysis using the Braun and Clerk approach to analyze the qualitative data. This consists of six (6) phases [29] which were applied as described below:

Data familiarization; this involves immersing oneself in the data to understand depth and breadth of the content, searching for patterns as well as meanings. This was done through transcribing audio data, reading data set and note taking. Generating codes; Generating of initial codes to organize the data, with full and equal attention given to each data item. This was done by labelling and organizing items into meaningful groups and a total of sixty-one codes (61) were generated as indicated in Tables 1 and 2. Generating themes; this includes sorting of codes into themes and identifying meaning of and relationships between codes. This was done through diagramming or mapping and writing themes and their defining properties and a total of five (5) themes were generated. Theme review; this consists of identifying coherent patterns at the level of the coded data and reviewing entire data set as a whole. This was done through ensuring there is enough data to support a theme, collapsing overlapping themes and reworking and refining codes and themes. Theme defining and naming; This includes identifying the story of each of the identified themes and fitting the broader story of the data set to respond to the research questions. This was done through cycling between the data and the identified themes in order to organize the story. Report production; this comprises presenting of a concise and interesting account of the story told by the data, both within and across themes. This was done by writing a compelling argument that addresses the research questions and by writing beyond the simple description of the themes. In summary the data was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis guided by Braun and Clarke’s approach [29]. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English where necessary. The researchers repeatedly read the transcripts to gain familiarity with the data and identify meaningful patterns related to participants’ perceptions of oxygen therapy. Initial codes were generated manually from the transcripts and similar codes were grouped into categories. The categories were further reviewed and organized into broader themes and subthemes based on similarities, relationships, and recurring patterns in the data. The themes were continuously refined through discussions among the researchers to ensure consistency and accurate representation of participants’ experiences. Representative participant quotations were selected to support the identified themes and provide contextual understanding of the findings.

The perception of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy

The identified themes were closely interconnected and collectively shaped participants’ overall perceptions of oxygen therapy. Both patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy as a life-saving and restorative intervention, while also associating it with impending death and interpreting its use through spiritual and faith-based perspectives. In addition to these shared perceptions, patients described oxygen therapy as physically uncomfortable and intrusive and reported experiencing emotional, cognitive, and social disruptions during its use. Caregivers, on the other hand, perceived oxygen therapy as financially burdensome within healthcare facilities, while also expressing positive perceptions of healthcare advancements and psychological reassurance associated with its administration. These interconnected themes demonstrate that perceptions of oxygen therapy were not shaped by a single experience but rather emerged through the interaction of emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, and experiential factors. Collectively, these findings illustrate the complexity of participants’ perceptions and highlight both the shared and distinct experiences of patients and caregivers. The individual perceptions of patients and caregivers are presented in detail below;

The perception of patients towards oxygen therapy

The perception of patients towards oxygen therapy in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital was both positive and negative with findings discussed under five themes in Table 1. These include: emotional, cognitive and social disruptions in oxygen therapy, oxygen therapy as a symbol of impending death, perceived physical discomfort and intrusiveness of oxygen therapy, spiritual and faith-based interpretations of oxygen therapy and perception of oxygen therapy as life-saving and restorative.

Theme 1: Emotional, cognitive and social disruptions in oxygen therapy

The emotional, cognitive and social disruptions in oxygen therapy are discussed further under five subthemes: these include disorientation and helplessness during initiation of oxygen therapy, emotional distress during oxygen therapy, stigma and social discomfort surrounding oxygen use, secondhand traumatic narratives and myths about oxygen therapy and post-oxygen confusion and altered awareness.

Subtheme 1.1: Disorientation during initiation of oxygen therapy

Patients reported that oxygen therapy was initiated to them while unconscious and they woke up to shock and confusion of seeing themselves on oxygen therapy without knowing what had happened to them during the time of being unconscious.…I woke up one day, there were things (nasal prongs) on my nose, I could not understand. And then, when I tried removing them, they told me not to remove them. Then, the people around me, they looked scared. So, I was also scared… [PII-12]

…I woke up one day, there were things (nasal prongs) on my nose, I could not understand. And then, when I tried removing them, they told me not to remove them. Then, the people around me, they looked scared. So, I was also scared… [PII-12]

Subtheme 1.2: Emotional distress during oxygen therapy

Worry, fear and inevitable anxiety following the initiation of oxygen therapy were reported by patients due to feelings of hopelessness and bewildered by thoughts of losing life soon. Some patients also stated that the room and the equipment that deliver oxygen therapy also contribute to their emotional distress as well.…it (oxygen therapy) can’t fail to worry you, even if you are the strongest, it really worries. It really worries for sure… [PII-02].…I feel so sad because it’s not something easy what comes to my mind, I see myself like a dead body being put into the grave just to fight for a life… [PII-02]

…it (oxygen therapy) can’t fail to worry you, even if you are the strongest, it really worries. It really worries for sure… [PII-02].

…I feel so sad because it’s not something easy what comes to my mind, I see myself like a dead body being put into the grave just to fight for a life… [PII-02]

Subtheme 1.3: Stigma and social discomfort surrounding oxygen use

Patients stated that they were stigmatized by the people surrounding them whenever they were seen on oxygen therapy thus resorted to isolation or social withdrawal due to fear of loss. Some patients did not want to talk about oxygen therapy around other people because they would always associate it with death.…I don’t really tell people when I’m sick and on oxygen…That kind of feels like someone will be stigmatized. They will always be thinking that, probably you’ll die in a minute. Because that’s the stigma that comes with it… [PII-05]

…I don’t really tell people when I’m sick and on oxygen…That kind of feels like someone will be stigmatized. They will always be thinking that, probably you’ll die in a minute. Because that’s the stigma that comes with it… [PII-05]

Subtheme 1.4: Post-oxygen confusion and altered awareness

Patients stated that oxygen therapy had effects on them even when it was stopped. The effects were both cognitive consisting of altered mental status and emotional consisting of worry, fear and anxiety.…but right now, when am in normal senses like this, it’s when I can tell you the truth that I worry that it (oxygen therapy) somehow scares… [PII-02]

…but right now, when am in normal senses like this, it’s when I can tell you the truth that I worry that it (oxygen therapy) somehow scares… [PII-02]

Theme 2: Oxygen therapy as a symbol of impending death

The perception that oxygen therapy is a symbol of impending death is discussed further under four themes. These include oxygen therapy is equated to end-of-life, fear of permanent dependence on oxygen therapy and conditional acceptance of oxygen therapy based on symptom severity.

Subtheme 2.1: Oxygen therapy is equated with end-of-life

Patients were worried about their life ending soon because of the belief that whoever is initiated on oxygen therapy has minimal chances of survival and is therefore nearing death. There was a belief that oxygen is initiated to an already dead person just he or she has not been confirmed dead yet. Some patients admitted that refusal of oxygen was rare because it is the last option for them to survive.…when I was initiated on oxygen, I thought I was going to die… [PII-12]

…when I was initiated on oxygen, I thought I was going to die… [PII-12]

Subtheme 2.2: Oxygen perceived as critical care for severe illness

Most of the patients reported that oxygen therapy was a fast and effective intervention for emergency situations especially during respiratory distress. Oxygen therapy meant that the situation is getting out of hand and some believed that oxygen therapy can manage chronic illnesses like asthma.…What I understand is, okay, they give you oxygen when you are not able to breathe. That is what I know. And when you may be very sick… [PII-12]

…What I understand is, okay, they give you oxygen when you are not able to breathe. That is what I know. And when you may be very sick… [PII-12]

Subtheme 2.3: Fear of permanent dependence on oxygen therapy

Concerns about the fear of dependence on oxygen therapy were raised by patients due to the belief that the first contact with oxygen therapy predicted more other contacts with it and to some, it meant that you are to get oxygen therapy all your life.…what I think that seems to be right, when you are initiated on oxygen, you might get used and need to be on it all your life because when I remove the nasal prongs tentatively to breathe in the surrounding air too, I feel like frightened as if the air am breathing is scaring me… [PII-13]

Patients attributed survival to divine intervention with belief that it’s God who heals and survival is by God’s will despite the medical intervention. Perception of oxygen therapy as a life-saving intervention was discussed under three subthemes. These included oxygen therapy as supportive and healing, oxygen therapy as a source of physical and emotional relief and reduced fear through exposure and familiarity.

Subtheme 4.1: Oxygen perceived as supportive and healing

Patients reported that oxygen therapy was supportive to those unable to support themselves while unconscious or breathless helping them to regain some life while some patients stated that if only oxygen was close to where they stay, they would be already healed and free from their long-term chronic illness.…they put it on you and it bring back some life sometimes the person has fainted when the heart is far, when you need oxygen so that you gain consciousness and you can’t support yourself and it’s oxygen to support you so that you regain life… [PII-02]

…when I put it (oxygen therapy) and it helped me, I could breathe well and it made me calm down…[PII-04]

…this is not the first time am put on oxygen; I have been put several times. It couldn’t scare me in anyway…[PII-08]

Subtheme 5.1: Oxygen therapy perceived as invasive or unnatural

The perception of caregivers towards oxygen therapy

Several key themes were identified reflecting how caregivers perceive oxygen therapy. These themes encompassed situations of hope, fear and widespread misconceptions regarding the use of oxygen therapy as elaborated in Table 2. The themes further describe the fears and emotional distresses faced by the caregivers following the initiation of oxygen therapy on the other hand, the feelings of hope and psychological comfort following positive outcomes in patients who have received oxygen therapy.

Theme 1: Psychological reassurance attributed to oxygen therapy

Psychological reassurance was discussed under: emotional reassurance and psychological comfort and trust in health workers as reassurance during oxygen therapy.

Subtheme 1.1: Emotional reassurance and psychological comfort

The psychological reassurance was expressed in diverse ways among caregivers, most notably through expression of hope for life and positive treatment outcomes:…Because at least when he was put on that Oxygen, he was trying now to come back, to normalize, and he was not struggling like he was… [CGI-08]

…Because at least when he was put on that Oxygen, he was trying now to come back, to normalize, and he was not struggling like he was… [CGI-08]

Subtheme1.2: Trust in health workers as reassurance during oxygen therapy

Caregivers expressed their psychological reassurance through the trust they placed in health workers especially in situations of uncertainty or fear:…for me in my heart I am just following their decisions and I believe they know why they put her on Oxygen, so I cannot interfere with them… [CGI-17]

…for me in my heart I am just following their decisions and I believe they know why they put her on Oxygen, so I cannot interfere with them… [CGI-17]

Theme 2: Oxygen therapy perceived as a life-saving intervention

This perception encompasses the area of; oxygen therapy as essential for survival and oxygen therapy improves someone’s condition.

Subtheme 2.1: Oxygen therapy as essential for survival

According to the caregivers, some reported that they welcomed the idea of putting their patients on oxygen because they knew that oxygen would help the patient to have life.

Subtheme 2.2: Oxygen improves someone’s condition

The perception that oxygen is a life-saving intervention was based on the caregiver’s opinion, some caregivers reported that oxygen is a support system to assist those with difficulties in breathing.…that it’s (oxygen) the air that helps the patient when unable to breathe. It helps so that the patient is able to breathe well… [CGI-12]

…that it’s (oxygen) the air that helps the patient when unable to breathe. It helps so that the patient is able to breathe well… [CGI-12]

Theme 3: oxygen therapy perceived as expensive in healthcare facilities

This perception constitutes the area of oxygen therapy perceived as expensive in healthcare facilities.

Subtheme 3.1: Oxygen therapy perceived as expensive in healthcare facilities

This perception was rooted in caregivers who have been in private facilities and a prescription for oxygen therapy was quite expensive. This brought them into a thinking that oxygen is expensive in all health facilities. Consequently, caregivers hesitate to initiate oxygen therapy due to fear of expenses. However, some caregivers found out that the services were actually entirely in government hospitals.

Theme 4: Spiritual and psychological distress of caregivers

The spiritual and psychological distress was discussed under: anxiety and fear triggered by oxygen therapy, faith and divine attribution and attribution of patient deterioration to oxygen equipment.

Subtheme 4.1: Anxiety and fear triggered by oxygen therapy

The inadequate knowledge about when, how and why oxygen therapy is used generates anxiety among the caregivers with the most asked question being: “… for how long is it (oxygen) going to be there?” [CGI-02]. This undermines the caregiver’s psychological wellbeing and may impede cooperative engagement in the patient’s care plan.

Subtheme 4.2: Faith and divine attribution

During moments of oxygen therapy, some caregivers frequently turned to prayer and placed their trust in God as the ultimate healer despite the concurrent medical interventions. Consequently, their confidence in clinical treatment was undermined, and the resulting anxiety shifted their reliance away from oxygen therapy towards divine intervention.

Subtheme 4.3: Attribution of patient deterioration to oxygen equipment

Some caregivers directly attributed unexplained adverse changes in the patient’s condition to the oxygen delivery equipment:…the person may come out on oxygen because they believe that the tubes affect. I don’t know how it affects but they say, it is going to affect the person… [CGI-16]

…the person may come out on oxygen because they believe that the tubes affect. I don’t know how it affects but they say, it is going to affect the person… [CGI-16]

Theme 5: Perceived as a symbol of impending death

Oxygen therapy perceived as a symbol of impending death constitutes the area of; oxygen therapy equated with end-of-life care, oxygen therapy is an emergency measure, fear rooted in traumatic medical outcomes, oxygen as a symbol of medical danger, oxygen as a dual symbol of life and death, perceived as a last resort measure.

Subtheme 5.1: Oxygen therapy equated with end-of-life

Most of the caregivers reported that in their experiences, oxygen therapy is in most cases administered to the critical patients, often when recovery is unlikely. The association of oxygen use and the final moments of a patient’s life has led caregivers to perceive it as a sign that death is near. Some caregivers believed that when a patient is initiated on oxygen, either their life is no more, they are dying soon or they are already dead:…they say that your patient may be put on oxygen and they keep on lying to you yet the patient already died. They keep on telling you that the patient is alive yet the patient died long time back…[CGI-07]

…they say that your patient may be put on oxygen and they keep on lying to you yet the patient already died. They keep on telling you that the patient is alive yet the patient died long time back…[CGI-07]

Subtheme 5.2: Oxygen therapy as an emergency measure

Some caregivers associated the initiation of oxygen with urgent medical intervention, typically involving patients who were struggling to breathe, unconscious or visibly in critical condition. This framed oxygen therapy as a marker of severe illness.

Subtheme 5.3: Fear rooted in traumatic medical outcomes

This captures the deep emotional fear and anxiety experienced by caregivers from past encounters where oxygen therapy was associated with poor clinical outcomes particularly death. These traumatic experiences contributed to the development of a lasting psychological link between oxygen therapy and fatality, resulting in persistent fear whenever oxygen therapy was introduced during patient care.

Subtheme 5.4: Oxygen as a symbol of medical danger

Some caregivers viewed oxygen with a degree of fear and suspicion, believing that its use could worsen the patient’s condition. Oxygen therapy was seen as risky, extremely dangerous and fatal depending on the caregiver’s previous experiences with deteriorating patients on oxygen.…quite risky because when eeehh, someone is undergoing that oxygen therapy, you really see there’s a time where it reaches and as if he’s not breathing… [CGI-04].

…quite risky because when eeehh, someone is undergoing that oxygen therapy, you really see there’s a time where it reaches and as if he’s not breathing… [CGI-04].

Subtheme 5.5: Oxygen as a dual symbol of life and death

Some caregivers viewed oxygen as a critical care intervention capable of sustaining life in patients with breathing difficulties, yet at the same time, it was strongly associated with the end of life especially when administered to critical patients.

Subtheme 5.6: Perceived as a last resort measure

Some caregivers associated the initiation of oxygen therapy with the final and most desperate medical intervention suggesting that the use of oxygen indicated that the patient was at the verge of death and that all other treatment options have failed.

Theme 6: Positive perception of healthcare advancements

This perception is further described under the recognition of advanced medical technology.

Subtheme 6.1: The recognition of advanced medical technology

Some caregivers acknowledged the advanced machines used delivering oxygen therapy to patients, which are believed to contribute to the provision of quality care.

Comparison and contrast between patients’ and caregivers’ perceptions toward oxygen therapy

Both patients and caregivers at Mbale Regional Referral Hospital expressed mixed perceptions toward oxygen therapy, reflecting experiences of hope, fear, emotional distress, spirituality, and misconceptions. However, the nature and intensity of these perceptions differed depending on whether the individual was directly receiving oxygen therapy or observing a loved one receiving it.

Similarities between patients and caregivers’ perceptions

Oxygen therapy was viewed as both life-saving and fear-inducing

Both patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy as an intervention capable of saving life, especially during severe breathing difficulty or critical illness. Patients described oxygen as supportive, restorative, and calming, while caregivers viewed it as essential for survival and improvement in breathing.…they put it on you and it bring back some life sometimes the person has fainted when the heart is far, when you need oxygen so that you gain consciousness and you can’t support yourself and it’s oxygen to support you so that you regain life… [PII-02]…that it’s (oxygen) the air that helps the patient when unable to breathe. It helps so that the patient is able to breathe well… [CGI-12]

…they put it on you and it bring back some life sometimes the person has fainted when the heart is far, when you need oxygen so that you gain consciousness and you can’t support yourself and it’s oxygen to support you so that you regain life… [PII-02]

…that it’s (oxygen) the air that helps the patient when unable to breathe. It helps so that the patient is able to breathe well… [CGI-12]

At the same time, both groups associated oxygen therapy with fear, anxiety, and severe illness. Oxygen therapy was commonly linked to critical conditions and death, leading to emotional distress in both patients and caregivers.…it (oxygen therapy) can’t fail to worry you, even if you are the strongest, it really worries. It really worries for sure… [PII-02]… for how long is it (oxygen) going to be there? [CGI-02]

Oxygen therapy was associated with impending death

A strong similarity was the perception of oxygen therapy as a sign of approaching death. Patients believed that being initiated on oxygen meant they were near death or had little chance of survival. Likewise, caregivers associated oxygen therapy with end-of-life care, critical illness, and failed treatment attempts. Both groups therefore interpreted oxygen therapy not merely as treatment, but also as a symbolic indicator of medical deterioration.…when I was initiated on oxygen, I thought I was going to die… [PII-12]…they say that your patient may be put on oxygen and they keep on lying to you yet the patient already died. They keep on telling you that the patient is alive yet the patient died long time back… [CGI-07]

…when I was initiated on oxygen, I thought I was going to die… [PII-12]

…they say that your patient may be put on oxygen and they keep on lying to you yet the patient already died. They keep on telling you that the patient is alive yet the patient died long time back… [CGI-07]

Presence of fear, anxiety, and psychological distress

Fear and emotional distress were prominent in both groups. Patients experienced worry, helplessness, shock, and anxiety during oxygen therapy, especially during initiation. Similarly, caregivers reported fear, uncertainty, and psychological distress, particularly when they lacked adequate knowledge about oxygen therapy or had previously witnessed poor patient outcomes. Thus, oxygen therapy created emotional instability for both the recipient and observer.

Spiritual and faith-based interpretations

Both patients and caregivers relied on spiritual beliefs and divine intervention during oxygen therapy. Patients attributed survival to God’s will and believed healing ultimately came from God. Similarly, caregivers turned to prayer and trusted divine intervention alongside medical care. In both groups, faith served as a coping mechanism during times of uncertainty and critical illness.

Misconceptions and negative beliefs about oxygen therapy

Misconceptions surrounding oxygen therapy existed among both patients and caregivers. Patients feared dependence on oxygen and viewed it as unnatural, while caregivers believed oxygen equipment could worsen the patient’s condition or symbolize medical danger. These misconceptions contributed to fear, hesitation, and mistrust toward oxygen therapy.

Differences between patients’ and caregivers’ perceptions

Direct physical experience versus observational experience

Patients described first hand physical experiences with oxygen therapy, including discomfort from nasal prongs, difficulty communicating, altered bodily sensations, coughing, and feelings of intrusiveness as further described under theme 2.0, subtheme 2.1. They also reported cognitive effects such as confusion and altered awareness under theme 1.0, subtheme 1.4.

Caregivers, however, did not experience physical discomfort directly. Their perceptions were mainly based on observation, interpretation, previous experiences, and emotional reactions toward the patient’s condition as quoted under theme 5.0, subtheme 5.3.

Patients experienced more cognitive and emotional disruptions

Patients uniquely reported disorientation, unconsciousness during initiation, post-oxygen confusion, helplessness, and altered awareness under theme 1.0, subtheme 1.1. These experiences arose because they personally underwent oxygen therapy and its physiological effects. Caregivers instead experienced psychological distress characterized by uncertainty, fear of losing the patient, and traumatic memories from previous clinical encounters described generally under theme 5.0.

Social stigma was more pronounced among patients

Patients described stigma, social withdrawal, and fear of being judged when seen on oxygen therapy. Some avoided informing others about their condition because oxygen therapy was socially associated with death as described under theme 1.0, subtheme 1.3. Caregivers did not explicitly discuss social stigma directed toward themselves. Their concerns were more focused on patient survival and treatment outcomes as seen under theme 2.0, subtheme 2.2.

Caregivers expressed greater trust in health workers

Caregivers frequently reported trusting health workers and following medical decisions regarding oxygen therapy. This trust offered them reassurance during uncertainty as quoted under theme 1.0, subtheme 1.2. Patients, however, focused more on their personal feelings and experiences rather than trust in healthcare providers as discussed under theme 1.0, subtheme 1.0.

Financial concerns were unique to caregivers

Only caregivers raised concerns regarding the cost of oxygen therapy, particularly based on experiences from private health facilities. Fear of expenses sometimes caused hesitation toward oxygen initiation as quoted under theme 3.0, subtheme 3.1. Patients did not directly discuss financial burdens associated with oxygen therapy.

Caregivers recognized technological advancement

Caregivers uniquely acknowledged oxygen delivery equipment as advanced medical technology contributing to quality healthcare as seen under theme 6.0, subtheme 6.1. This positive perception of healthcare advancement was absent among patients, who focused more on personal physical and emotional experiences.

Overall, both patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy as a paradoxical intervention: it symbolized both hope for survival and fear of death. While both groups experienced emotional distress, spiritual reliance, and misconceptions, patients’ perceptions were shaped primarily by direct physical and psychological experiences, whereas caregivers’ perceptions were influenced more by observation, prior experiences, financial concerns, and trust in healthcare professionals.

The findings therefore demonstrate that perceptions toward oxygen therapy are multidimensional and deeply influenced by emotional, social, spiritual, and experiential factors among both patients and caregivers.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the perception of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH). In this study, the perception of patients and caregivers is discussed explicitly into different themes.

Perception of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy

The main findings of this study are as follows: (i) oxygen therapy as a life-saving yet fear-inducing intervention; (ii) spiritual and emotional interpretations of oxygen therapy; (iii) perceived physical and technological aspects of oxygen therapy.

The patients and caregivers reported that oxygen therapy is perceived as an intervention that assists individuals who are unable to breathe properly, enabling them to breathe better and sustain life. The participants expressed emotional comfort and reassurance in perceiving oxygen therapy as a symbol of hope and life-saving intervention. Patients in particular, described it as soothing and comforting upon initiation. Similarly to our findings, a scoping review by Britto, Bakare [30] demonstrated that patients felt safe and comfortable as they begun to feel the flow of oxygen, while caregivers felt emotional relief upon seeing an improvement in the patients’ condition [30]. The main differences between our study and the Britto, Bakare [30]’s study were that, it was a scoping review with most of the articles reviewed based in high income countries and physical burden, as well as negative attitudes and feelings of both patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy were reported. Furthermore, on perception of oxygen therapy as a life-saving intervention that relieved breathlessness and improved comfort, more similar findings were reported in a qualitative study conducted in Portugal where patients and caregivers recognized that oxygen therapy improved quality of life and breathing comfort despite the challenges associated with long-term therapy [31]. In addition, Collier, Breaden [32] found that caregivers viewed oxygen therapy as essential in relieving breathlessness and sustaining life, although emotional and practical burdens remained present.

However, oxygen therapy was also perceived as a fear-inducing intervention as participants reported episodes of fear, worry, anxiety and hopelessness, particularly reflected in Theme 1 (Emotional, cognitive and social disruptions in oxygen therapy) and specifically Subtheme 1.2 (Emotional distress during oxygen therapy) among patients. Among caregivers, similar experiences were reflected in Theme 4 (Spiritual and psychological distress of caregivers), particularly Subtheme 4.1 (Anxiety and fear triggered by oxygen therapy). These findings suggest that emotional distress was a shared experience across both participant groups, although it originated from different lived experiences. Basing on their prior lived experiences such as witnessing the death of a loved one on oxygen therapy, or through interactions with peers, caregivers and patients developed the perception that oxygen therapy is strongly associated with end-of-life with perceptions that individuals placed on oxygen therapy either are going to die, have already died or have minimal chances of survival. This perception corresponds directly to Patient Theme 2 (Oxygen therapy as a symbol of impending death), particularly Subtheme 2.1 (Oxygen therapy equated with end-of-life), and Caregiver Theme 5 (Perceived as a symbol of impending death), especially Subtheme 5.1 (Oxygen therapy equated with end-of-life). The consistency of these themes across both participant groups strengthens the interpretation that oxygen therapy continues to symbolize impending death within this setting. For some, oxygen therapy was interpreted as a clear sign that life is nearing its end. Similar findings have been reported in a descriptive cross-sectional study in Nigeria where it was found out that 21.1% patients and 19.5% caregivers believed that oxygen therapy was solely for terminally ill patients [33]. In another study carried out in Wisconsin, USA, it was found that the respondents stated that oxygen use was initiated to patients who were within days of their expected death [34]. The experiences of social stigma discussed under Patient Theme 1, particularly Subtheme 1.3 (Stigma and social discomfort surrounding oxygen use), illustrate how oxygen therapy extended beyond a medical intervention to become a socially stigmatizing experience. Patients who received oxygen therapy more frequently often perceived a strong sense of social stigma and expressed fears of becoming permanently dependent on oxygen therapy. For some, the stigma is so profound that it led to social withdrawal, as those around them may associate oxygen use with impending death and thus hesitate to form meaningful relationships, viewing them as temporary. Furthermore this findings of this study revealed both patients and caregivers frequently associated oxygen therapy with severe illness, impending death, and poor prognosis have been reported in other Similar related studies where oxygen therapy was perceived as emotionally distressing and strongly associated with disease deterioration and end-of-life care. A qualitative study by Collier, Breaden [32] reported that caregivers experienced significant emotional distress while caring for patients receiving long-term oxygen therapy and often associated oxygen use with worsening illness and loss of autonomy. Similarly, Bueno, Campos [35] found that patients receiving long-term oxygen therapy experienced fear, sadness, psychosocial distress, and altered self-image associated with oxygen use. In the Ugandan context, Dauncey, Olupot-Olupot [36] also reported that communities and caregivers expressed fear and reluctance toward oxygen therapy due to misconceptions and poor understanding regarding its benefits. These findings support the current study and suggest that fear surrounding oxygen therapy may be influenced by previous experiences, community beliefs, and limited knowledge about oxygen therapy, particularly in low-resource settings. Nevertheless, a number of patients reported gradually adapting to the use of oxygen, particularly as the frequency of therapy increased. Participants also voiced concerns about long-term dependence, lamenting the loss of natural air and describing their current experience as relying solely on “unnatural” air provided by oxygen concentrators and cylinders. Studies in North England found that patients had similar concerns regarding stigma and dependence on oxygen therapy [37, 38].

Several participants reported that oxygen was initiated without their awareness, often during periods of unconsciousness. This was demonstrated in Patient Theme 1, particularly Subtheme 1.1 (Disorientation during initiation of oxygen therapy) and Subtheme 1.4 (Post-oxygen confusion and altered awareness). As a result, they were unaware of the events that transpired while the therapy was being administered. Upon regaining consciousness, patients described finding themselves on oxygen therapy without any understanding of how or why it had been initiated. This lack of awareness elicited feelings of shock and confusion. This is an illustration that unconscious initiation of oxygen therapy contributed to shock, confusion, and the perception that oxygen is reserved only for critically ill patients. Consequently, many participants associated oxygen therapy with unconsciousness and perceived it as an intervention reserved for critically ill patients. This perception reinforced the belief that oxygen therapy is primarily an emergency measure, other than being a supportive component of care [39, 40].

The emotional reassurance experienced by caregivers was also attributed to their trust in healthcare providers. Caregivers’ emotional reassurance was reflected primarily in Caregiver Theme 1 (Psychological reassurance attributed to oxygen therapy), especially Subtheme 1.2 (Trust in health workers as reassurance during oxygen therapy). Caregivers reported having confidence in the competence of health professionals and expressed that they did not question the providers’ ability to deliver appropriate care. This trust stemmed from the belief that healthcare providers are knowledgeable, capable, and have thoroughly assessed the patient’s condition before initiating oxygen therapy. Therefore, unlike patients, whose perceptions were shaped largely by direct physical experiences, caregivers gained reassurance through confidence in healthcare professionals and their clinical decisions. Such trust has been reported to contribute significantly to the acceptance of oxygen therapy as a supportive treatment [39, 41, 42]. However, some patients narrated that they were unfamiliar with the concept oxygen therapy, largely due to residing in rural areas where they had never previously encountered or interacted with it.

This study reveals that spiritual and faith-based interpretations significantly influence the perceptions of both patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy. The influence of spirituality was consistently reflected in Patient Theme 3 (Spiritual and faith-based interpretations of oxygen therapy) and Caregiver Theme 4, particularly Subtheme 4.2 (Faith and divine attribution). Caregivers often viewed the initiation of oxygen therapy as an indication of a critical health status, prompting the need for sincere prayers and reflecting a heightened sense of anxiety and concern about the patient’s chances of survival. Many participants expressed a strong belief in divine healing alongside medical interventions, suggesting that survival is ultimately determined by God’s will, and positive outcomes are met with gratitude to God. Such faith-based beliefs provided emotional comfort, especially during moments when medical interventions alone did not offer sufficient reassurance. Explicitly linking these themes demonstrates that spirituality functioned as a common coping mechanism across both participant groups despite differences in their experiences of oxygen therapy. A study carried out in Poland found out that individuals with a higher level of spirituality experienced fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression where as those with more negative religious coping, showed more anxiety and depression symptoms [43]. Additionally, some patients shared a belief in fate, expressing that life and death are predetermined; if it is one’s time to die, it will happen regardless of medical treatment, and if it is not, the person will survive. These perceptions underscore the complex interplay between medical understanding and spiritual beliefs in shaping attitudes towards oxygen therapy. The interaction between fear, spirituality, and limited knowledge highlights the complexity of perceptions toward oxygen therapy in low-resource settings. Participants who lacked adequate information about oxygen therapy were more likely to associate it with death, hopelessness, or medical danger. In such situations, spirituality often became an important coping mechanism that helped participants deal with uncertainty and emotional distress. Similar findings have been reported in recent qualitative studies showing that limited understanding of oxygen therapy contributes to fear and delayed acceptance, while spiritual beliefs influence coping and treatment perceptions among critically ill patients and caregivers [35, 44]. These interconnected findings suggest the importance of combining patient education with culturally sensitive counseling and emotional support during oxygen therapy initiation.

This study identifies discomfort and a sense of intrusiveness as key factors shaping their perceptions. The physical discomfort experienced by patients corresponds directly to Patient Theme 5 (Perceived physical discomfort and intrusiveness of oxygen therapy), particularly Subtheme 5.1 (Oxygen therapy perceived as invasive or unnatural). Several patients described the nasal prongs as invasive, stating that they pierced the nose and often led to coughing, which in turn caused secretions to enter the oxygen tubes, an experience they found highly unpleasant. This theme was unique to patients because caregivers experienced oxygen therapy indirectly through observation rather than direct physical exposure. A study by Quinn-Lee, Weggel [34] carried out in Wisconsin found that 78% of the respondents admitted that oxygen therapy was an irritant [34]. Beyond physical discomfort, patients also expressed that the oxygen itself did not feel like normal air; it was perceived as unusually warm and lacking freshness, which contributed to a sense of unease. Furthermore, some patients felt that being on oxygen interfered with their ability to move freely. In these cases, they noted that communication became more reliant on gestures or sign language, further reinforcing the perception of oxygen therapy as a restrictive and disruptive intervention. A study carried out by Kelly and Maden [39] similarly found that patients reported discomfort with use of nasal prongs suggesting that oxygen therapy is not entirely benign but rather discomforting and intrusive [39]. Concerns regarding the financial cost of oxygen therapy emerged exclusively among caregivers under Theme 3 (Oxygen therapy perceived as expensive in healthcare facilities). Unlike patients, caregivers worried that oxygen therapy would impose unaffordable expenses, particularly in private health facilities. This belief contributed to hesitancy in initiating or accepting oxygen therapy. Several respondents indicated a reluctance to begin treatment due to fears of accumulating medical expenses while in private healthcare settings. Even when the need for oxygen therapy was recognized, some patients hesitated because the facilities offering such services were located far from their homes, resulting in additional transportation costs. These financial concerns were often shaped and reinforced by personal experiences and community conversations, further heightening anxiety and hesitation around starting oxygen therapy. Nevertheless, some participants were aware that oxygen therapy is provided free of charge in government-funded facilities and expressed a desire for similar affordability in private healthcare institutions. A study carried out in Spain had similar findings that the oxygen delivery equipment were perceived to be too expensive [45].

This study reveals that patients and caregivers obtain information about oxygen therapy from a variety of sources worldwide, including schools, communities, hospitals, and the internet. Some participants residing in rural areas reported that, although oxygen therapy was frequently discussed within their communities, they had never had the opportunity to observe how it functions or what the equipment actually looks like. Consequently, their first encounter with oxygen therapy in a hospital setting provided them with greater exposure and a clearer understanding of its purpose and application.

In regards to interconnection among themes, the themes identified in this study were closely interconnected rather than occurring independently. The relationships between Patient Themes 1–5 and Caregiver Themes 1–6 demonstrate that participants’ perceptions were multidimensional rather than isolated. For example, emotional distress (Patient Theme 1; Caregiver Theme 4) reinforced perceptions of impending death (Patient Theme 2; Caregiver Theme 5), while spiritual beliefs (Patient Theme 3; Caregiver Theme 4.2) and trust in healthcare workers (Caregiver Theme 1.2) helped moderate these fears. Expounding further, fear of oxygen therapy was often reinforced by inadequate knowledge, previous traumatic experiences, community misconceptions, and the frequent association of oxygen therapy with severe illness and death. These fears were further shaped by emotional distress and uncertainty experienced during hospitalization. At the same time, spirituality and faith-based interpretations influenced how participants understood illness, survival, and recovery, with many participants simultaneously relying on both medical treatment and divine intervention. Trust in health workers and previous positive experiences with oxygen therapy also appeared to reduce fear and improve acceptance of oxygen therapy among some participants. These findings demonstrate that perceptions toward oxygen therapy are multidimensional and are shaped through the interaction of emotional, cultural, spiritual, social, and experiential factors rather than isolated individual beliefs [31, 35, 36].

Although oxygen therapy has been widely studied in relation to clinical effectiveness and healthcare delivery, there remains limited qualitative evidence exploring how patients and caregivers in low-resource African settings perceive and interpret oxygen therapy. Most recent studies have focused on home oxygen therapy and chronic respiratory illnesses [32] in high-income settings, with limited attention to hospitalized patients and caregivers in sub-Saharan Africa. The current study therefore contributes important contextual evidence regarding the emotional, spiritual, cultural, and experiential meanings attached to oxygen therapy among patients and caregivers in Uganda. These findings may inform culturally sensitive communication, counseling, and patient-centered oxygen therapy practices in similar low-resource healthcare settings.

Implications of the study

This study reveals that oxygen therapy is perceived both as a life-saving intervention and as a symbol of impending death especially in emergencies. This dual perception interferes with timely acceptance of oxygen therapy, often resulting in delays in care. These findings imply a critical need to educate patients and caregivers about oxygen therapy and to implement community sensitization programs aimed at raising awareness and addressing prevailing misconceptions about oxygen therapy.

Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of providing psychological support to both patients and caregivers during the course of oxygen therapy, given the emotional distress associated with its initiation and use. This support can be strengthened through involvement of religious or spiritual leaders, as participants often perceived divine interventions as a source of hope and emotional reassurance.

Study limitations and strengths

This study was purely qualitative which cannot be generalized to other settings however, the use of in-depth interviews as well as including both caregivers and patients increases the transferability of the findings to other settings.

Some challenges were encountered during participant recruitment and data collection. A few participants were only comfortable communicating in local languages, which created occasional language-related barriers during interviews. To address this, trained language assistants helped with translation and interpretation of the interview guides and responses to ensure accurate communication. In addition, some critically ill patients who had received oxygen therapy were unable to participate because of their medical condition or impaired communication, which may have limited the inclusion of some experiences and perspectives.

Conclusion

This study looked at the perception of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital. The findings of this study demonstrated that the perceptions of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy do not necessarily differ from each other. Patients and caregivers perceived oxygen therapy in terms of; oxygen therapy perceived as a symbol of impending death like oxygen equals the end-of-life; emotional and psychological distress caused by oxygen therapy like fear and anxiety; spiritual and faith-based interpretations of oxygen therapy like attribution of survival to divine interventions; oxygen therapy perceived as a life-sustaining intervention like oxygen therapy heals; psychological reassurance attributed to oxygen therapy like oxygen is a hope for life.

Patients further perceived oxygen therapy in terms of; perceived physical discomfort and intrusiveness of oxygen therapy like oxygen is invasive and unnatural; and finally, cognitive disruptions of oxygen therapy like confusion post-oxygen therapy.

Caregivers as well further perceived oxygen therapy in terms of; oxygen therapy is perceived as expensive in healthcare facilities; and finally, the positive perception of healthcare advancements like modernization of oxygen delivery system.

Recommendations

First, there’s need for implementation of community sensitization campaigns involving local leaders, politicians, religious leaders and community health workers to promote awareness and address stigma faced by patients on oxygen therapy and the misconceptions about oxygen therapy in the communities.

Secondly, patients and caregivers should be health educated on oxygen therapy using local languages or if possible, using visual aids so as to address psychological distress during initiation of oxygen therapy.

Additionally, counselling and psychological support is very essential for patients and caregivers struggling with emotional distress and fear associated with oxygen therapy if preferably done by trained personnel so as to promote psychological discomfort during the initiation of oxygen therapy.

Finally, further research is needed to assess the effectiveness of health education and awareness interventions in improving the perceptions of patients and caregivers towards oxygen therapy.
