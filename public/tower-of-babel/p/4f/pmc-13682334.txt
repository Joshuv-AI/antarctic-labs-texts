Generation and characterization of a serum albumin-humanized mouse model and its utility for detection of albumin adduct formation in vivo
Authors: Xiangmeng Wu, Weiguo Han, Lei Yin, Weizhu Yang, Jonathan Sheng, John Han, Robert J Turesky, Laura S Van Winkle, Qing-Yu Zhang, Xinxin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn albumin-humanized mouse model was generated via crossbreeding between albumin-transgenic and albumin-null mice and characterized. Immunoblot analyses confirmed that only human, but not mouse, albumin was expressed, and they revealed that the level of serum human albumin in the albumin-humanized mouse was 5–6 times the level in the albumin-transgenic mouse. Serum albumin levels (measured in plasma) were 3–4 times lower, whereas globulin levels were 2 times higher, in the albumin-humanized than in wild-type mice. The transgene integration site was localized via optical genome mapping to mouse chromosome 14, between 2 marker positions that are 143-kilobase pairs apart. A neighboring gene (alpha fetal protein) to albumin on the human chromosome was confirmed absent in the transgenic mouse. A transgene-mouse genome junction sequence was further identified, enabling a new protocol to distinguish between hemizygous and homozygous albumin-transgenic mice. Transgenic human albumin formed covalent adducts with naphthalene 1,2-oxide (NAO) ex vivo at the Cys34 residue, which was recovered in the peptide 31LQQCPF36 following tryptic/chymotryptic digestion. LQQC∗[NAO]PF, structurally confirmed by mass spectral comparisons with synthetic adduct standards, was also detected in protease-digested plasma from homozygous albumin-humanized mice that were exposed to naphthalene through intraperitoneal injection (200 mg/kg) or inhalation (20 ppm). Pharmacokinetic analysis of plasma NAO-albumin, in comparison with NAO-glutathione, revealed a later time at maximal concentration (9.6 versus 2 hours), lower maximal concentration (0.21 versus 12 μM), and longer elimination half-life (79.7 versus 2.3 hours) for NAO-albumin following intraperitoneal naphthalene injection; the persistence of NAO-albumin adduct was also demonstrated following inhalation naphthalene exposures.Significance StatementAn albumin-humanized mouse was generated and characterized to demonstrate its utility for detecting in vivo formation of reactive metabolite adducts with human albumin. Key findings, including detection of the albumin adduct with naphthalene 1,2-oxide (NAO) at Cys34 following exposure of mice to naphthalene, a ubiquitous pollutant and possible human carcinogen, and persistence of the NAO-albumin adduct in plasma relative to NAO-glutathione adducts, indicate that the model will be valuable for discovering biomarkers of exposure and potential toxicity for numerous drugs and other xenobiotics.Key words: Albumin, Humanized mice, Naphthalene, Protein adduct, Genome mapping, Biomarker

Introduction

Serum albumin, the most abundant protein in blood, is an important carrier of various hormones, fatty acids, and many xenobiotic compounds in the circulation.1 It is also a target protein for the formation of covalent adducts with reactive xenobiotic metabolites, which are important for assessing human risks of adverse health effects, such as drug toxicity and carcinogenesis.2,3 An albumin-humanized mouse model, where human albumin (HA) is produced in the liver, while mouse albumin (MA) is abolished, is currently unavailable, but it would facilitate efforts to study in vivo formation of reactive metabolite adducts with HA. Substantial sequence differences exist between MA and HA (by 28% in amino acid homology), which lead to species differences in the specific albumin peptides and amino acid residues targeted for adduct formation.4 Thus, direct studies on adduct formation with HA in an in vivo model are needed. Although initial studies on possible adduct formation can be performed ex vivo with HA, the availability of an albumin-humanized mouse model would enable direct studies on the relationships between xenobiotic exposure and adduct formation in vivo, which would be more difficult to do in humans due to safety concerns. Furthermore, an in vivo model, which integrates xenobiotic absorption, distribution, metabolic activation, systemic circulation, and excretion, will provide insights into the toxicokinetics of adduct formation and disposition; allow determination of albumin adduct formation and persistence under various exposure and physiological conditions; and enable identification of possible correlations between toxicant-HA adducts (biomarkers of toxicant exposure and bioactivation) and various toxicity endpoints for exposures to toxicant-containing environmental mixtures like fire smoke.

We have previously reported the generation and initial characterization of a HA-transgenic mouse.4 To produce this transgenic model, an uncut bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone containing HA and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) genes was used for microinjection into fertilized eggs of C57BL/6J mice. The transgenic mice had no apparent biological or behavioral deficiencies. The transgenic HA gene transcript was detected in the liver, but not in other organs examined, including lung, heart, intestine, kidney, skeletal muscle, and brain, consistent with the tissue specificity of albumin gene expression.5,6 HA protein was detected in the serum of the transgenic mice at all ages tested (7 days to 5 months, male and female), but its abundance in adult mice was found to be much lower than MA through estimations of the relative abundance of the 2 proteins in copurified MA and HA. The transgenic albumin, confirmed to be a full-length protein by mass spectral deconvolution analysis, was found to react ex vivo with iodoacetamide (IAA) at Cys34, captured as 31LQQC∗[IAA]PF36, and with NO2-PhIP, a reactive metabolite of 2-amino-1-methyl-6-phenylimidazo[4,5-b]pyridine (PhIP), a carcinogen formed in cooked meat,7 at Cys34 and Tyr411 residues, detected as 31LQQC∗[desaminoPhIP]PF36 and 411Y∗[desaminoPhIP]TK413, respectively. However, the ability of the transgenic HA to form covalent adducts with reactive xenobiotic metabolites in vivo has not been examined.

In the present study, we generated an albumin-humanized mouse via crossbreeding between the HA-transgenic mouse4 and an albumin-null mouse.8 The new albumin-humanized mouse was characterized to confirm the expression of HA and loss of MA, and to assess possible effects of MA gene deletion on the expression level of the HA. The humanized model was also studied to pinpoint the location of the transgene integration site, identify junction sequences between mouse and human genes, and clarify whether the human AFP gene, adjacent to the albumin gene in human chromosome and included in the DNA construct used to produce the HA-transgenic mouse, is present in the transgenic mouse genome. Finally, the ability of the transgenic HA to form covalent adducts with reactive xenobiotic metabolites was demonstrated using naphthalene 1,2-oxide (NAO), a reactive metabolite produced by P450-mediated metabolism of naphthalene (NA), which is an abundant environmental pollutant and possible human carcinogen.9 Given the known ability of NAO to form adducts with the cysteine residue in reduced glutathione (GSH), and the ability of Cys34 residue in HA to adduct to electrophiles,2,10 we focused our efforts on the detection and characterization of NAO adducts with HA at the Cys34 residue, which was recovered in the peptide 31LQQCPF36 following tryptic/chymotryptic digestion. The structures of the detected LQQC∗[NAO]PF adducts were confirmed by mass spectral analysis; the abundance and toxicokinetics of the NAO-HA adducts were determined in mice exposed to NA through intraperitoneal injection or nose-only inhalation and compared with those of NA-GSH adducts. Our findings demonstrate the usefulness of the albumin-humanized mouse model for detecting in vivo formation of reactive metabolite adducts with HA, which is valuable for developing biomarkers of exposure and potential toxicity for numerous drugs and other xenobiotics.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

NA, DL-dithiothreitol (DTT), HA, β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (reduced tetra (cyclohexyl ammonium) salt), and formic acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Acetaminophen-glutathione (internal standard for NA-GSH measurement) was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals. NA-GSH standard and NAO were generous gifts from Drs Alan R. Buckpitt and Dexter Morin (University of California at Davis). The NAO preparation was a mixture of stereoisomers; the isomeric ratio or purity was not determined. The peptides LQQCPF, LQQCPFEDHVK, and 13C6-[L]QQCPF were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Trypsin and chymotrypsin (sequencing grade) were purchased from Promega. Methanol and water (liquid chromatography mass spectrometry [LC-MS] grade) were purchased from Fisher Scientific. Milli-Q water, prepared from demineralized water, was used throughout the study. Unless otherwise stated, all other chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Generation of the albumin-humanized mouse model

The albumin-humanized mouse was generated via crossbreeding between a HA-transgenic mouse developed in our lab,4 and an albumin-null mouse [C57BL/6J-Albem8Mvw/MvwJ, JAX stock #025200, the Jackson Laboratory].8 Mouse pups were screened via probe-based TaqMan polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis using 2 independent sets of PCR primers and probes for MA and HA genes. For MA gene, sequences of primers and probes, provided by the Jackson Laboratory, were as follows: forward primer, 5′-caaaacaagagcccgaaaga-3′; reverse primer, 5′-ctctggcctttcaaatggtg-3′; wild-type (WT) probe, 5′-/Hex/tgacaaccccagcctgc/3IABkFQ/-3′; null probe, 5′-/6-FAM/tgacaacccagcctgcc/3IABkFQ/-3′. For HA gene, sequences of primers and probes, provided by IDT, were as follows: forward primer, 5′-gtcaacatgctgagctttaatagg-3′; reverse primer, 5′-ctagacttacggctgctgaat-3′; probe, 5′-/6-FAM/cccttgcctagcccaacagaaga/3IABkFQ/-3′. All primers and probes were synthesized by IDT. Genomic DNA isolated from mouse tail biopsies was used as template for genotyping; PCR was performed on a QuantStudio3 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using PrimeTime Gene expression master mix (IDT) and the following cycling condition: initial activation at 95 °C for 3 minutes, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 seconds and 60 °C for 30 seconds.

Immunoblot analysis

Mouse serum (5 μL) was diluted 200× or 1000× with 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, and then mixed 1:3 with 4X Bolt LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), of which 10-μL aliquots were used for immunoblot analysis. Immunoblot analysis was performed as described,4 with use of a goat anti-HA antibody that does not cross-react with MA (Catalog # A80-229A, Bethyl Laboratories), or a goat anti-MA antibody that does not cross-react with HA (Product # PA1-30899; Invitrogen), at a 1:3000 dilution. A rabbit anti-goat IgG-HRP (A5420, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as secondary antibody at 1:5000 dilution. Authentic serum HA and serum MA proteins (Sigma-Aldrich) were used as controls. Immunoreactive proteins were visualized with use of SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo scientific) and luminescent images were recorded and quantified by a Gel-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Blood chemistry analysis

Heparinized plasma samples, collected from nonfasting male and female mice (n = 5), age 3–4 months, were analyzed using an Abaxis VetScan VS2 veterinary chemistry blood analyzer (second generation), with Comprehensive Diagnostic Profile reagent rotors, for levels of alanine aminotransferase, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, amylase, total calcium, creatinine, globulin, glucose, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, total bilirubin, total protein, and urea nitrogen. Analysis was conducted at 37 oC, with 100 μL plasma per sample.

Genome mapping and insertion site-specific genotyping

Optical mapping of the HA-transgenic mouse genome was performed by Bionano Genomics. The isolation of ultra-high molecular weight DNA from the liver of a homozygous albumin-humanized mouse, labeling of genomic DNA fluorescently at the CTTAAG motif using the DLE-1 enzyme, linearization of the labeled DNA, image analysis of individual DNA molecules, and sequence motif assembly into de novo genome maps were performed as described recently for the mapping of the insertion site of the CYP2A13/2B6/2F1 transgene in another mouse model.11 Structural variants (SVs) in the assembled genome were called against the in silico DLE-1-digested reference mouse genome (GRCm38) and HA gene-containing BAC clone sequence (RP11-580P21). Data were analyzed with Bionano Access and Bionano Tools on Saphyr Computer Servers (Bionano Genomics). Marker positions for the reference mouse and human sequences were assigned based on fully assembled maps, whereas marker positions for the transgenic mouse genome were assigned for each de novo assembled map before alignments were made with the reference maps.

For validation of the insertion site obtained from optical genome mapping, the breakpoint region was amplified with PCR. PCR was carried out using a GoTag Long PCR kit (Promega). Genomic DNA (200 ng) from an albumin-humanized mouse was used as PCR template. PCR conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 2 minutes followed by 30 cycles of 92 °C for 30 seconds, 65 °C for 15 minutes, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 minutes. Human genomic DNA from HepaRG cells (ThermoFisher) was used as a positive control. PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis or were purified and then subjected to additional PCR analysis or Sanger DNA sequencing. For confirmation of the absence of the AFP gene in the albumin-humanized mouse genome, PCR conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 2 minutes followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 seconds, 60 °C for 30 seconds and 72 °C for 1 minute, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 minutes. For insertion site-specific detection of HA transgene, multiplexed PCR was carried out using the GoTag PCR kit and ∼200 ng gDNA as template; PCR conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 2 minutes followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 seconds, 62 °C for 30 seconds and 72 °C for 1 minute, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 minutes. Primer sequences and coordinates are listed in Table 1. Primers were designed with Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). For agarose gel analysis of DNA, a 1-kb Plus DNA ladder (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used for size determination; DNA was detected by staining with ethidium bromide. Sanger DNA sequencing was performed at the University of Arizona Genetics Core facility.

Synthesis of NAO-LQQCPF and NAO-13C6[L]QQCPF adducts

Approximately 2 mg of NAO was added to 200 μL of methanol containing 100 μg/mL LQQCPF or 13C6[L]QQCPF. The mixtures were incubated under gaseous argon, on ice, in sealed polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf), for 1 hour, a condition that permits complete conversion of the peptides to NAO adducts. The mixture was then diluted with 100 volumes of methanol before high-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) analysis for NAO-LQQCPF and NAO-13C6[L]QQCPF adducts characterization. Absence of any unreacted peptides in the completed reaction mixture was confirmed by the LC-MS analysis, providing the basis for assuming the amounts of adduct produced to be equal to the amount of LQQCPF added. NAO-LQQCPF and NAO-13C6[L]QQCPF adducts were further purified by HPLC and quantified by comparing peak areas to the original standard mixture collected; the fraction of NAO-LQQCPF or NAO-13C6[L]QQCPF eluted from HPLC was concentrated under nitrogen in a 40 °C water bath and used for NAO-HA adduct quantification in biological samples.

Ex vivo formation of NAO-HA adduct in the plasma of albumin-humanized mice

Approximately 2 mg of NAO was incubated with 100 μL of plasma from albumin-humanized mice in a sealed Eppendorf tube under gaseous argon at 25 °C for 2 hours. A 5-μL aliquot of the NAO-incubated plasma (∼200 μg protein) was further processed for digestion with trypsin and chymotrypsin (2 μg each). In brief, the plasma sample was mixed with 10 volumes of methanol and vortexed for 30 seconds, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 minutes to remove free NAO. The pellet was reconstituted in 150 μL of digestion buffer [(50 mM ammonium bicarbonate-hydrochloric acid buffer, pH 7.5, containing 5 mM each of CaCl2 and DTT] and then heated at 60 °C for 30 minutes to denature protein. The mixture was then incubated with trypsin and chymotrypsin (2 μg each, added in 150 μL) at 25 °C for 20 hours.

Thereafter, the protein digest was diluted with 850 μL of Milli-Q water and loaded onto an ISOLUTE C18 cartridge (25 mg/1 mL; Biotage) preconditioned with 1 mL methanol followed by 1 mL water. The cartridge was then washed with 1 mL of water followed by elution with 1 mL of methanol. The elution was dried under nitrogen at 40 °C and reconstituted with 100 μL of 80% methanol (in H2O, v/v) containing 1 mM DTT for HPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Animal treatments for studying in vivo formation and clearance of NAO-HA adduct in albumin-humanized mouse

All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All mice used in this work were maintained in an environment with controlled temperature and humidity and a 12-hour dark/light cycle, and with food and water provided ad libitum. Mice (albumin-humanized, male, 3–4 months old) were exposed to NA via intraperitoneal injection or inhalation. For intraperitoneal injection, NA was dissolved in corn oil at 20 mg/mL and mice were dosed at 200 mg/kg. Approximately 10 μL of blood was collected from the tail vein 0.1 hour before and 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours after NA injection for plasma preparation. For inhalation exposure, mice were exposed to NA vapor at 20 ppm for 4 hours each day, for 3 days, in a 24-port Oral-Nasal Aerosol Rodent Exposure System (CH Technologies), as previously described.12 Blood samples (∼40 μL each) were collected at 0 hour after the termination of each 4-hour exposure, and 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours after the termination of the last exposure. Plasma was prepared by centrifugation of heparinized blood at 10,000g for 5 minutes at 4 °C and was stored at –30 °C for up to 14 days until analysis. The NA doses used were based on previously reported doses that cause lung toxicity in mice.13,14

Plasma samples were processed as described above for ex vivo formed NAO-HA adducts in mouse plasma, except that 5 μL each of 13C6[L]QQCPF (13.5 μM, internal standard for LQQCPF quantification) and NAO-modified 13C6[L]QQCPF (135 nM, internal standard for NAO-LQQCPF quantification) were added to the reconstituted plasma protein prior to the addition of trypsin and chymotrypsin. The volume of plasma used for analysis was 5 μL for mice exposed via intraperitoneal injection and 20 μL for mice exposed via inhalation. The amounts of internal standard added were close to the concentrations of LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF detected in the plasma of NA-exposed mice.

LC-MS analysis of LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF

LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF were detected using an HPLC-MS/MS system consisting of an Agilent 1290 ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies) and a Sciex Qtrap 6500+ Mass Spectrometer (AB SCIEX) equipped with an electrospray ionization source. Data acquisition and integration were controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 Software. Chromatography was performed on an EclipsePlus C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm, Agilent) at 35 °C, with mobile phase A containing 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v) and mobile phase B containing 0.1% formic acid in methanol (v/v), and with 10 μL samples injected. Elution was at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min, with a solvent gradient as follows: 10% B (0–3 minutes), 10% B to 40% B (3–6 minutes), 40% B to 55% B (6–26 minutes), 55% B to 90% B (26–26.1 minutes), 90% B (26.1–31 minutes), 90% B to 10% B (31–31.1 minutes), and 10% B (31.1–35 minutes). The MS was operated in the positive ion mode, using electrospray ionization, under these conditions: ion spray voltage and temperature, at 5500 V and 500°C, respectively; curtain gas, gas 1, and gas 2, at 30, 50, and 20 psi, respectively. For characterization of NAO-LQQCPF, the fragment ion information was acquired in the product ion mode with the precursor ion [M+H]+ at m/z 879.3 and collision energy (CE) at 25 eV. For quantification of LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF, multiple reaction monitoring mode was used with a dwell time of 100 milliseconds per transition, and the specific transitions monitored for [M+H]+ were m/z 735.5/370.1 (CE 40eV) for LQQCPF, m/z 741.5/376.1 (CE 40eV) for 13C6-[L]QQCPF, m/z 879.3/733.2 (CE 20eV) for NAO-LQQCPF, and m/z 885.3/739.2 (CE 20eV) for NAO-13C6-[L]QQCPF.

For quantification of LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF in trypsin/chymotrypsin-digested plasma samples, blank C57BL/6 mouse plasma was used as surrogate matrix to construct calibration curve. LQQCPF (1.4–140 μM) and NAO-LQQCPF (0.68–68 nM) standards, along with corresponding internal standards (13.5 μM 13C6[L]QQCPF and 135 nM NAO-modified 13C6[L]QQCPF, 5 μL each), were added to 150 μL of methanol-precipitated and reconstituted plasma protein in the digestion buffer before the addition of trypsin and chymotrypsin, exactly as described for the digestion of HA in the plasma from albumin-humanized mice. The calibration curves were fitted via linearly weighted least-squares regression (1/χ2). The correlation coefficient was >0.99 for both LQQCPF (range, 1.4–140 μM; LOQ, <1.4 μM (low limit of calibration range used)) and NAO-LQQCPF (range, 0.68–68 nM; LOQ, 0.68 nM). The recovery through solid phase extraction was >75% and the precision (relative standard deviation) was <10% for both analytes. In addition, no isobaric interference was found at the respective retention times of LQQCPF and NAO-LQQCPF. The recovery of LQQCPF from total HA in trypsin/chymotrypsin-digested plasma was estimated to be ∼13%, calculated from LQQCPF abundance in digested plasma samples (∼28 μM) and plasma levels of total albumin (Table 2; 1.4 g/dL or 210 μM) in male albumin-humanized mice. Accordingly, plasma levels of the NAO-HA adduct were estimated by multiplying the fraction of adducted peptide (concentration of LQQC∗[NAO]PF divided by the concentration of LQQCPF) by the total HA concentration (210 μM) in albumin-humanized mice.

Other methods, statistics, and data analysis

Plasma NA-GSH levels were determined by LC-MS analysis in the supernatant fraction after methanol precipitation of plasma protein (described above for protein adduct analysis), using a previously described protocol.15 Statistical analysis was conducted using the GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad). Data are expressed as means ± SD, and differences with P < .05 (unpaired t test) were considered statistically significant. Pharmacokinetic parameters, including Tmax, Cmax, and elimination half-life (t1/2), were calculated using PK solver (Microsoft), assuming a noncompartmental model.

Results

Serum HA levels in the albumin-humanized mouse

Hemizygous (HA+/MA–/–) and homozygous (HA+/+/MA–/–) albumin-humanized mice were generated via intercrosses between HA+/MA+/– mice, which were produced via crossbreeding between the original HA-transgenic mouse (HA+/MA+/+),4 and an albumin-null mouse (MA–/–).8 HA+/+/MA–/– and HA+/+/MA+/+ mice were identified through breeding tests with WT mice, where they produced pups that are 100% HA+/MA+/– and HA+/MA+/+, respectively. These were used to establish breeding colonies to produce HA+/+/MA–/– and HA+/+/MA+/+ mice for all subsequent studies.

The absence of MA, and presence of HA, in HA+/+/MA–/– mice was confirmed through immunoblot analyses using species-specific anti-albumin antibodies (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1A, the anti-HA antibody detected HA standard as well as a band of the same size as the HA standard in the serum of both HA+/+/MA+/+ and HA+/+/MA–/– mice but did not detect MA standard or any signal in the serum of MA–/– or MA+/+ (WT) mice. Conversely, the anti-MA antibody detected the MA standard and a band of the same size in the serum of HA+/+/MA+/+ and WT mice, but not HA standard or any signal in the serum of MA–/– or HA+/+/MA–/– mice.

Serum HA levels were compared between albumin-humanized (HA+/+/MA–/–) and HA-transgenic (HA+/+/MA+/+) mice to determine whether the absence of MA, which may compete with HA for neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated recycling,8,16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 would result in an increase in HA levels. As shown in Fig. 1B, where equal amounts of serum from the HA+/+/MA–/– and HA+/+/MA+/+ mice were compared, HA was more abundant in the albumin-humanized mice than in the HA-transgenic mice. Densitometry analysis indicated the difference to be ∼6-fold (Fig. 1C). The magnitude of the difference in HA levels was further confirmed when serum from the albumin-humanized mice was diluted 5 times and then compared with serum from HA-transgenic mice (Fig. 1D). Densitometry analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1) confirmed that the band densities in HA+/+/MA+/+ samples were similar to those of 5-time diluted HA+/+/MA–/– samples.

Blood clinical chemistry parameters in the albumin-humanized mice

The albumin-humanized mice were healthy, maintained normal body weights, and bred normally, compared with WT mice. To determine whether subtle differences occur in this new mouse model, we further compared it with WT mice for clinical chemistry parameters in the blood. As shown in Table 2, serum albumin levels, measured using the Bromocresol green dye binding method,22 were significantly lower in the albumin-humanized mice (HA+/+/MA–/–) than in WT mice (reduced by 70% in males and 78% in females), reflecting the absence of MA and the fact that serum levels of the transgenic HA in HA+/+/MA–/– mice are lower than those of MA in WT mice. Notably, the level of serum albumin in HA+/+/MA–/– mice determined using the VetScan (dye binding) method was similar to the values determined by immunoblotting with a specific anti-HA antibody (Fig. 1C). Total protein levels23 were also lower in the HA+/+/MA–/– than WT mice, but the calculated levels of globulins (difference between total protein and albumin level) were 2.2–2.4 times higher, indicating compensatory increases in the levels of other major serum proteins.8,24, 25, 26, 27, 28

Small but statistically significant sex differences (lower in female) in plasma levels of HA (by 24%), total protein (by 15%), and globulins (by 12%) were found in the albumin-humanized mice, and in plasma globulin levels in the WT mice (by 19%) (Table 2). Genotype- or sex-related differences were generally small, if any, in the other parameters examined, including alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, amylase, total calcium, creatinine, glucose, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, total bilirubin, and urea nitrogen; a few of the differences were statistically significant.

Genomic structure of the HA transgene insert

The HA-transgenic mouse was generated using a human BAC clone that contained not only the albumin gene, but also the AFP gene.4 A genomic PCR was performed to determine whether both genes are present in the HA-transgenic mouse genome. As shown in Fig. 2A, HA gene (238 bp PCR product, see Table 1) was detected in a reference human genomic DNA as well as genomic DNA from a HA+/+/MA–/– mouse, but not in MA+/+ (WT) mouse, whereas human AFP (316 bp PCR product, see Table 1) was detected only in the human genomic DNA, but not in the mouse genomic DNA.

Thus, the mapping result confirmed that the AFP gene was absent in the genome of the albumin-humanized mouse.

Impact of the transgene insertion on mouse genome

To identify possible disruption to the mouse genome by human transgene, the insertion site on mouse Chr. 14 was further characterized. There was no annotated gene within the deleted mouse sequence, between markers 10719 and 10727, though a putative gene was indicated in the reference mouse genome (Fig. 2B). Efforts were made to map the 2 breakpoints (left and right) in the albumin-humanized mouse genome, where the mouse and human sequences connected (Fig. 3). PCR was performed with a series of primer combinations to amplify across putative breakpoints. The left breakpoint is somewhere between markers 2315 and 2316 in the albumin-humanized mouse genome, which are 1308 bp apart (Fig. 3A). However, attempts to obtain this fragment for further mapping and sequencing were unsuccessful due to the presence of vast repeat sequences that reduce PCR specificity. The region between markers 2316 and 2319 did not align with either reference mouse genome or the human BAC sequence, possibly representing new sequences generated from recombination of the human and mouse sequence near the breakpoint.

A new genotyping method for differentiating between hemizygous and homozygous HA-transgenic mice

The new knowledge of the precise transgene insertion site at the right side and the mouse genome fragment that was replaced by the human transgene made it possible to design a new genotyping method using primers that are within the region of the mouse genome that is replaced by the transgenes. As shown in Fig. 3A, PCR primers WT-DF and WT-DR, both located on the deleted sequence near the right breakpoint, would amplify a 744-bp PCR product from WT allele, but not from the transgenic allele. Conversely, PCR primers ALB-F and ALB-R, which are specific for the human ALB gene, would amplify a 238-bp PCR product from the transgenic allele, but not from the WT allele. The specificity of the genotyping method was confirmed in experiments using genomic DNA from WT, hemizygous, and homozygous transgenic mice (Fig. 3D). The genotypes of the transgenic mice were previously verified by a breeding test, where mating between a homozygous transgenic mouse and a WT mouse would produce pups that are 100% positive for the presence of the transgene, detected using a previous genotyping method for the transgenic allele.29

Ability of the transgenic HA to form adducts with NAO at Cys34

To demonstrate the utility of the albumin-humanized mouse model in studies of xenobiotic metabolism and toxicity, we set out to assess the ability of HA to form adducts with reactive metabolites in vivo, using NAO as a test compound and focusing on the highly nucleophilic Cys34 residue of HA as the likely target for adduction. Cys34 is recovered in the peptide 31LQQCPF36 following tryptic/chymotryptic digestion.4 Four possible isomeric LQQC∗[NAO]PF adducts would be expected from the reaction of NAO with LQQCPF, all with a theoretical molecular weight of 878 Da (Fig. 4A). However, in the reaction products of NAO with LQQCPF, only 3 peaks were detected by HPLC-MS/MS with [M+H]+ at m/z 879, with chromatographic retention times at 19.72, 20.12, and 20.65 minutes, respectively (Fig. 4B). Based on the retention time, the 3 adduct peaks were named LQQC∗[NAO]PF-1, LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2, and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3. The signal intensity of LQQC∗[NAO]PF-1 was too low to yield a clear MS2 spectrum, but LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3 showed informative MS2 spectra that are nearly identical to each other (Fig. 4C and D) and, when compared with the MS2 spectrum of LQQCPF (Fig. 4E), support the proposed assignment of the adduct structure (Fig. 4D and F). The MS2 spectrum of LQQC∗[NAO]PF shows the b2 (m/z 242), y2 (m/z 263), and b3 (m/z 370) ions that are identical to those found in LQQCPF; the m/z 492, m/z 599, and m/z 861 fragment ions corresponding to y3-H2O, b4-H2O, and MI (undigested molecular ion)-H2O, respectively, that contain 1,2-dihydro-hydroxyl-NA (146 Da) at Cys34 (losing 2H when forming the S-C bond, for a net change of 144 Da); the m/z 471 and 439 fragment ions corresponding to the loss of 1,2-dihydro-hydroxyl-NA (NAO plus 2H) from b4 and a further loss of S from m/z 471, respectively; and the m/z 733 and m/z 715 fragment ions corresponding to the loss of 1,2-dihydro-hydroxyl-NA (NAO plus 2H) from the MI and a further loss of H2O from m/z 733. Coincidentally, the m/z 492 fragment ion also corresponds to the loss of 1,2-dihydro-hydroxyl-NA from y4.

Evidence that NAO can form adducts with Cys34 when reacting with intact HA was obtained by detecting 31LQQC∗[NAO]PF36 adducts in tryptic/chymotryptic digests of total plasma proteins following incubation of NAO with plasma from albumin-humanized mice. Interestingly, unlike the profile of LQQC∗[NAO]PF adducts formed between NAO and LQQCPF (Fig. 5A), where 2 isomeric adduct peaks (LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3) were abundantly detected, only one isomeric adduct peak (LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2) was abundantly detected in the proteolytic digests and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-1 and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3 peaks were barely detected (Fig. 5B). The reaction in Fig. 5A occurred in methanol; similar results were obtained when the reaction occurred in water (Supplemental Fig. 2). These results indicated that NAO could form adducts with Cys34 when reacting with intact HA and that the reaction shows obvious regio- or stereoselectivity.

In vivo formation and persistence of NAO-HA adducts in NA-exposed albumin-humanized mice

The formation of NAO-HA adduct was investigated in albumin-humanized mice exposed to NA through intraperitoneal injection (200 mg/kg, once) or inhalation (20 ppm, 4 hours each day, consecutively for 3 days). NAO-HA was detected in plasma as LQQC∗[NAO]PF following proteolytic digestion of total plasma protein with trypsin/chymotrypsin. LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 (major) and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3 (minor) peaks, but not LQQC∗[NAO]PF-1, were detected at 8 hours (near Tmax) after intraperitoneal NA injection (Fig. 6A and B), whereas only LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 was detected immediately after the termination of the last inhalation exposure (Fig. 6C). The MS2 spectrum of LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 from the in vivo samples (Fig. 6D) was identical with that of synthesized LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 (Fig. 4C), further confirming the formation of NAO-HA in vivo.

The persistence of NAO-HA adduct in circulation was examined by measuring plasma NAO-HA levels at different times following a single intraperitoneal injection of 200 mg/kg NA (Fig. 7A), or immediately following termination of inhalation exposure (at 20 ppm for 4 hours) for 1–3 days and at various times thereafter (Fig. 7B). The previously reported production of an abundant, single-missed-cleavage peptide 31LQQCPFEDHVK34, in addition to the terminal product 31LQQCPF36, following digestion of HA with trypsin and chymotrypsin,30 suggested the presence of 31LQQC∗[NAO]PFEDHVK34 in the proteolytic digest of NAO-HA in mouse plasma. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 3, 31LQQC∗[NAO]PFEDHVK34 was detected at ∼80% of the abundance of 31LQQC∗[NAO]PF36 in the same NAO-incubated and then proteolytically digested human plasma sample. Discussion

A major goal of this study was to generate and characterize an albumin-humanized mouse model. To that end, we have successfully generated the new mouse model and identified several important features that make the mouse model useful. The expression of HA and loss of MA, as demonstrated by immunoblot analysis using species-specific anti-albumin antibodies, was as expected (Fig. 1), so is the absence of any adverse biological phenotypes, including in growth and reproduction. Through optical genome mapping (Fig. 2), we also revealed that the insertion of the human transgene did not disrupt any known mouse genes, consistent with the lack of any biological phenotype. The mapping result also indicated that the AFP gene, adjacent to the ALB gene in human chromosome 14 and present in the transgene construct, was not present in the albumin-humanized mouse. This finding simplifies data interpretation for functional studies on the human ALB gene using this mouse model as it removes any concerns about possible contributions from the AFP gene.

Optical genome mapping uses enzyme-catalyzed labeling of specific DNA sequence motifs and a light microscope-based image acquisition to generate high-resolution maps of marker distribution in the entire genome.31 The use of the optical genome mapping approach to characterize transgene structure and insertion sites in transgenic mice is still relatively new, first reported in our recent study on the CYP2A13/2B6/2F1-transgenic mouse.11 In the present study, the human ALB transgene structure was relatively simple (Fig. 2), compared with the CYP gene cluster in the earlier study, and we were able to identify the precise mouse-human sequence junction on the right side, which provided not only concrete evidence for the transgene location, but also basis for designing a new genotyping method to distinguish between homozygotes and hemizygotes (Fig. 3). The left side junction sequence has also been localized to a 1308-bp region. Thus, we provide another demonstration of the utility of the optical gene mapping method for characterizing transgene insertion in mouse models. Furthermore, the data are helpful for future efforts to create ALB variants through gene editing.

The plasma level of HA in the original HA-transgenic mouse was found to be much lower than that of MA.4 The large excess of MA appeared to suppress the ability of the HA to form adducts with a reactive chemical NO2-PhIP. These factors, which cause significant reductions in sensitivity for detection of HA adducts in vivo, prompted the current effort to develop albumin-humanized mouse models that only express HA. Interestingly, serum HA levels in albumin-humanized mice were 5–6 times higher than in the albumin-transgenic mouse (Fig. 1), a finding that can be explained by the lack of competition from MA for the common FcRn carrier protein that is important in the recycling of serum proteins.8,16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 This competition was mutual, as it was also apparent for serum MA levels in the original HA-transgenic mouse, which were ∼11% lower than in WT mice, presumably due to competition from the HA (Ding, X. and Wu, X., unpublished data). The serum half-life of injected HA in a previous study was greater in mice having the FcRn gene (2.6 days) compared with mice lacking the FcRn gene (2.1 days), suggesting that mouse FcRn protein can bind HA to increase its stability, though not very strongly.8 Nonetheless, serum HA levels in the humanized mice were still lower (3–4 times) than serum MA levels in WT mice (Table 2). This decrease in albumin level was accompanied by increases in other proteins, presumably globulins (2.2- to 2.4-fold), which are known to occur in analbuminemia, to maintain colloid oncotic pressure and prevent severe fluid imbalance.32,33 The increase in HA levels will facilitate HA adduct detection using this mouse model. The loss of MA, the decreases in total albumin levels, and the increases in globulin levels will likely change the capacity for noncovalent ligand binding to serum proteins at least for some compounds, and the specific impact for a given compound may depend on which serum protein(s) it is preferentially bound to in WT mice.

NAO-albumin adducts have been reported previously in mice and rats following NA exposure; the adduct was detected using mass spectrometry as cysteinyl conjugates following proteolytic digestion.34,35 Our current study is the first report of the in vivo formation of NAO-HA adducts following NA exposure. Further, unlike previous detection of cysteinyl conjugates with NAO, we detected NAO-HA as a peptide adduct, 31LQQC∗[NAO]PF36, thus localizing the adduct to the Cys34 residue, which was also the site of adduction with reactive N-oxidized metabolite of NO2-PhIP that was previously demonstrated ex vivo with serum from the HA-transgenic mouse.4 Notably, though Cys34 is a common target for reaction with electrophiles,2,4,36,37 it remains possible that NAO may react at other residues, including His, Lys, and Tyr,2,4,38,39, eg, Tyr411, which formed adduct with NO2-PhIP, detected as Y∗[desaminoPhIP]TK.4 Additionally, the ability of other NA metabolites, such as 1,2-NQ and 1,4-NQ, to form Cys adducts with HA in the albumin-humanized model remains to be examined. Previous studies have reported detection of NQ adducts with mouse35 and rat34 albumin following NA exposure in vivo, and of NQ adducts with HA in human serum from several different population groups in biomarker studies.40, 41, 42, 43 The availability of this albumin-humanized mouse model will now enable more complete characterization of the human-specific adducts formed in vivo with various NA metabolites and at different sites, circumventing potential species differences in the reactivity of different albumin residues with various electrophiles, as highlighted previously for the differential formation of the NO2-PhIP adduct at the Cys34 of MA (nondetection) and HA.4

A single dominant adduct peak, corresponding to LQQC∗[NAO]PF based on MS/MS spectra analysis, was detected when the plasma of albumin-humanized mice was incubated with NAO ex vivo (Fig. 5B, LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2), or when albumin-humanized mice are exposed to NA via either intraperitoneal injection or inhalation. A minor adduct peak with a slightly greater retention time, LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3, was barely detectable. In contrast, when NAO reacted directly with LQQCPF, 3 adduct peaks were detected: a minor adduct (LQQC∗[NAO]PF-1) and 2 major adducts (LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2 and LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3), with LQQC∗[NAO]PF-3 being the most abundant (Fig. 5A). The 3 adduct peaks probably represent regio-/stereoisomers of the adduct, though their isomeric assignments have not been made (Fig. 4A); previous studies have shown detection of 3 regio-/stereoisomers of NAO-GSH conjugates as reaction products between NA metabolites and reduced GSH.44 Notably, the difference in isomeric profiles for the adducts formed with the LQQCPF peptide and those formed with intact HA protein indicates possible 3-dimensional structural constraints in the HA ligand binding site (beyond the LQQCPF peptide structure) for adduct formation, favoring the 1- or 2-carbon position and/or the R- or S-conformation. In that connection, previous studies of NAO adducts with mouse and rat albumin have shown the detection of both 1- and 2-isomers for the cysteinyl conjugates with NAO, with the 1-isomer being more abundant.34,35 It remains to be confirmed whether the dominant peak detected for NAO-HA (LQQC∗[NAO]PF-2) corresponds to the adduct at the 1-position of NAO.

The plasma half-life of NAO-HA was estimated to be 63–80 hours in mice exposed to NA via inhalation or intraperitoneal injection (Table 3). In comparison, the plasma half-life of intact HA in mice was estimated to be 4.2 days (∼100 hours), when HA was injected intravenously to MA-null mice,8 which suggests that the adducted HA is less stable than intact HA. A similar decrease in plasma half-life was observed in male F344 rats for NAO-rat albumin adducts, where the adduct had a half-life of ∼2 days, compared with that of intact albumin (2.5–3 days).45 Notably, the half-life of the NAO-HA adduct in humans is expected to be much longer than in the current humanized mouse model as the replacement of mouse FcRn (present in the current model) with the human FcRn, which recycles albumin and other serum proteins, extended the half-life of intact HA to 24.1 days, which is similar to HA half-life in human plasma (21 days).8 This prolonged NAO-HA half-life, relative to the short half-life of other NA metabolites, such as NAO-GSH conjugates (Fig. 7A, Table 3), makes the NAO-HA adduct an attractive biomarker for monitoring NA exposure and bioactivation in humans and the albumin-humanized model valuable for establishing important toxicological parameters, such as dose-response relationships and association with toxic endpoints in various target organs.

The sex difference in plasma HA levels in the albumin-humanized mouse was consistent with known sex difference in humans46,47 and contrasted with the lack of sex difference in plasma albumin levels in WT mice (Tabel 2). This sex difference is not surprising since the HA transgene uses the HA promoter, which exerts human-like regulation of albumin expression. Notably, though HA adduct levels were determined only for males, a sex difference in adduct levels is not expected, since the plasma HA concentrations (600 μM in WT, and 159 (female)–210 (male) μM in albumin-humanized; Table 2) were at least 1000 times higher than the HA adduct levels detected (Fig. 7).

In summary, we have successfully generated and characterized an albumin-humanized mouse model and assessed the ability of HA to form adducts with reactive metabolites in vivo, using NAO as a test metabolite and focusing on the highly nucleophilic Cys34 residue of HA as the target for adduction. Whereas the model can be further enhanced by incorporating human biotransformation enzymes to mitigate possible species differences in xenobiotic metabolism and disposition, the methods developed and data obtained using the current model for NAO-HA detection will be valuable for biomarker studies in human populations exposed to various NA-containing environmental mixtures, such as fire smoke, tobacco smoke, and petroleum products. Furthermore, given the reactivity of multiple residues in HA (eg, Cys, His, Lys, Tyr)2,4,38,39 with reactive metabolites produced by many therapeutic drugs, such as clozapine and olanzapine (antipsychotic drugs),48 and various tyrosine kinase inhibitors,49,50 the albumin-humanized mouse model provides unique opportunities for assessing risks of toxicity associated with drug therapy in humans. These opportunities include facilitation of the development of analytical methods to detect and quantify drug-HA adducts in humans; correlation of plasma drug-HA adduct formation with drug-induced cytotoxicity or genotoxicity in vivo and drug-protein adduct formation in target tissues that may not be accessible for direct examination in humans; and determination of the relationships between drug dose or pharmacokinetics and the types or amounts of drug-HA adducts formed.
