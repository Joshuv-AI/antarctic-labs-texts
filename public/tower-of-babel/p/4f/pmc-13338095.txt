Effectiveness and Safety of Prophylactic Low‐Dose Emicizumab in Young Children With Hemophilia A: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Guichi Zhou, Liya He, Chunyan Wang, Yashuang Deng, Dong Peng, Zhaonan Liu, Sixi Liu, Xiaoying Fu, Ying Wang, Xiaoqin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundReal‐world data on low‐dose emicizumab in young children with hemophilia A are limited.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 26 children (median age: 2.2 years) receiving individualized low‐dose emicizumab (2019–2025).ResultsOver 48 months, the annualized bleeding rate decreased from 5.31 to 0.60 (p < 0.001); the median maintenance dose was 0.81 mg/kg/week. FVIII equivalent levels correlated with dose (Quality of life [CHO‐KLAT]) improved from 57.2 to 69.8 (p < 0.001). Subclinical joint changes progressed despite bleeding control. No thrombotic events occurred.ConclusionsLow‐dose emicizumab effectively reduces bleeding and improves quality of life, supporting its feasibility in resource‐limited settings.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.Keywords: children, emicizumab, hemophilia A, low‐dose prophylaxis, resource‐limited settings

Introduction

Hemophilia A is an X‐linked bleeding disorder caused by factor VIII (FVIII) deficiency. Prophylactic FVIII replacement is the standard treatment, but challenges such as venous access difficulties and inhibitor development persist. Emicizumab, a bispecific antibody mimicking FVIII cofactor function, enables subcutaneous administration with an extended half‐life [1].

While clinical trials (HAVEN series) confirm emicizumab efficacy across ages and inhibitor status [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], real‐world data on low‐dose regimens in very young children remain scarce. This study focused on low‐dose emicizumab prophylaxis to clarify its effectiveness and safety in young Chinese children with hemophilia A, focusing on bleeding outcomes, joint health, and quality of life.

Patients and Methods

This multicenter retrospective study enrolled 26 children with moderate/severe hemophilia A (25 severe, 1 moderate) from three hospitals in Guangdong Province, irrespective of whether they had FVIII inhibitors at enrollment from June 2019 to May 2025. Emicizumab was individualized used, converted to 7‐day equivalent (Q7D EQUAL) doses. For example, a regimen of 3.0 mg/kg administered biweekly was converted to 1.50 mg/kg Q7D EQUAL.

Bleeding events were recorded during the 183 days prior to emicizumab initiation (or from birth for infants aged < 183 days) and throughout follow‑up until May 1, 2025. Annualized bleeding rates (ABRs), spontaneous ABR (ASBR), traumatic ABR (ATBR), and joint ABR (AJBR) were calculated as (number of bleeds ÷ total observation days) × 365.25. Reasons for switching to emicizumab, adverse events, and surgical treatments were documented. Parents were instructed to report any trauma, bleeding, or adverse events requiring recombinant FVIII (rFVIII) or rFVIIa. The research team conducted follow‑up telephone calls and administered questionnaires to capture breakthrough bleeding events. Equivalent FVIII levels were measured via bovine chromogenic assay [7]. Joint structure was assessed using the Hemophilia Early Arthropathy Detection with Ultrasound in China (HEAD‐US‐C) [8] and health‑related quality of life (HRQoL) by the Chinese version of the Canadian Haemophilia Outcomes‐Kids’ Life Assessment Tool (CHO‐KLAT2.0) [9].

No a priori sample‑size calculation was performed; the sample comprised all eligible patients. Paired bleeding rates before and after treatment were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‑rank test due to non‑normal distribution. Spearman's rank correlation assessed the association between weekly emicizumab dose (Q7D EQUAL) and FVIII equivalent levels. All tests were two‑sided, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Analyses were performed on available data using SPSS 28.0.1.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Overall, 26 pediatric patients (median 5.1 years, range: 1.6–14 years) with hemophilia A (severe:moderate = 25:1) were enrolled. Median age at emicizumab initiation was 2.2 (0.4–13.4) years, including four infants < 1 year (Table 1). Prior treatment consisted of prophylactic standard half‐life (SHL) FVIII substitution in 16 of 26 patients (61.5%) (12–40 IU/kg, mostly twice or thrice weekly). Ten patients (38.5%) developed inhibitors, with a historical median peak concentration of 182.4 BU/mL (IQR: 9.13–1280.0).

Follow‐up until March 1, 2025.

Among nine high‐responding inhibitor patients, five received immune tolerance induction (ITI) treatment. Two patients failed ITI and continued on emicizumab prophylaxis alone, and one received concomitant ITI with FVIII. At study entry, seven patients had positive inhibitors (median titer 76.8 BU/mL, IQR: 8.80–139.5). Two patients (7.7%) were previously untreated, and eight (30.8%) had < 20 exposure days. Median follow‐up was 48 months (range: 31–70).

Emicizumab Administration

Only one patient (3.8%) received the standard 4‐week loading dose. Others started with a median loading dose of 2.5 mg/kg (IQR: 2–2.75) at various frequencies (weekly: 44%; biweekly: 52%; every 10 days: 4%). The median Q7D EQUAL maintenance dose was 0.81 mg/kg (IQR: 0.53–1.00). No thromboembolic or thrombotic microangiopathy events were reported.

Bleeding Outcomes

Four patients (15.4%) had no bleeding before emicizumab; after initiation, 10 patients (38.5%) had zero bleeds. Overall, 57 bleeding events were documented in 16 of 26 patients (61.5%) following emicizumab prophylaxis. Seven patients experienced 21 major bleeding events, which were mostly trauma‐related and managed with 1–2 doses of FVIII or rFVIIa, no adverse events from concomitant use were reported.

After a median follow‐up of 48 months, significant reductions were observed in ABR (5.31 [0–19.96]→0.60 [0–5.06], p < 0.001), ASBR (1.61 [0–13.97]→0.15 [0–3.79], p = 0.008), ATBR (3.46 [0–15.97]→0.46 [0–3.05], p = 0.001), and AJBR (2.38 [0–17.96]→0.04 [0–4.33], p = 0.012) (Figure 1A–D).

FVIII Equivalent Levels and Dose Correlation

In 20 patients with available measurements, the median FVIII equivalent level was 7.70% (5.75%–11.88%), positively correlated with the weekly emicizumab dose (r = 0.5438, p = 0.0132; Figure 2). Joint Status and HRQoL

In 20 of the 26 patients, HEAD‐US‐C scores increased from baseline (mean 2.2) to follow‐up (4.2, p = 0.001), indicating some subclinical joint progression despite bleeding control. Mean CHO‐KLAT scores improved from 57.23 ± 16.63 to 69.79 ± 13.32 (p < 0.001), and the socioeconomic context module (SEC) scores rose from 61.77 ± 9.26 to 71.57 ± 10.52 (p < 0.001; Table 2).

Discussion

This real‑world study demonstrates that low‑dose emicizumab prophylaxis (median 0.81 mg/kg/week) effectively reduces bleeding and improves quality of life in young Chinese children with hemophilia A overextended follow‐up (median 4 years), including those with inhibitors. The ABR reduction from 5.31 to 0.60 is comparable to the reductions observed in the HAVEN studies (which used standard doses of 1.50 mg/kg Q7D EQUAL), despite our cohort receiving approximately 46% lower maintenance doses [10].

The observed ABR reduction aligns with pivotal trials [2, 3, 4], supporting the feasibility of dose de‑escalation in resource‑limited settings and recent pharmacokinetic‐guided dose reduction strategies [9]. Moreover, our results provide longer follow‑up and more detailed laboratory correlates.

The positive correlation between weekly emicizumab dose and FVIII equivalent levels (r = 0.54, p = 0.013) provides a pharmacodynamic basis for individualized dosing. Notably, even partial FVIII‑mimetic activity—as reflected by the FVIII equivalent levels (median 7.70%) observed in our cohort—has been shown to meaningfully improve hemostasis. This is consistent with the real‑world study from China demonstrating that low‑dose emicizumab regimens achieve excellent bleeding control in the majority of pediatric patients [11]. Furthermore, a pilot study showed that switching from PK‑guided FVIII prophylaxis (with trough levels of 2.0–5.8 IU/dL) to low‑dose emicizumab resulted in significantly improved bleeding outcomes, supporting the concept that even partial FVIII‑mimetic activity can be clinically beneficial [12]. These findings are particularly relevant for infants and toddlers, in whom higher bleeding risk must be balanced against drug cost and parental burden of administration [13].

The 61.5% breakthrough bleed rate—higher than HAVEN reports—likely reflects active toddler behavior and relative underdosing. However, these episodes were predominantly trauma‐related and easily managed with minimal bypassing agents, suggesting that low‐dose prophylaxis provides sufficient baseline hemostasis for daily activities while requiring on‐demand support for significant trauma.

Importantly, the discordance between clinical bleeding control and subclinical joint progression (rising HEAD‐US‐C scores) warrants attention. Despite zero clinical joint bleeds in many patients, ultrasound detected progressive synovial changes, suggesting that current low‐dose regimens may prevent clinical bleeds but not subclinical micro‐bleeds. This supports the need for optimized dosing targets or enhanced joint monitoring in this vulnerable population [13, 14].

Study limitations include the retrospective design, small heterogeneous sample, lack of MRI to detect micro‐bleeds, and absence of a control group. Nevertheless, these real‐world data from very young patients—including infants—provide valuable evidence for low‐dose emicizumab use in resource‐constrained environments.

In conclusion, low‐dose emicizumab prophylaxis is effective and safe in young children with hemophilia A, offering a feasible treatment option in resource‐limited settings. Future prospective studies should evaluate long‐term joint outcomes with dose optimization strategies.
