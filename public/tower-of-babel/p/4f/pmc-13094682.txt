Current Trends in the Epidemiology of Obesity and the Association Between Obesity and Metabolic Liver Disease (MASLD/MASH)
Authors: Mirjana Grocic, Madison Nguyen, Arun J. Sanyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExcess adiposity and obesity are increasingly prevalent, with incidence rising in many parts of the world; both trigger a state of metabo‐inflammation in adipose tissue, creating a systemic milieu characterised by insulin resistance and pro‐inflammatory and fibrogenic states. Along with systemic inflammatory cues, perturbations in systemic and individual organ metabolic homeostasis result in metabolically driven cell stress, injury and death; this activates innate immune signals promoting fibrogenesis and organ dysfunction. The phenotype of end‐organ diseases is determined by the balance between metabo‐inflammatory signals and homeostatic restorative factors. Some of the key end‐organ diseases associated with obesity and metabo‐inflammation include atherosclerotic vascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease and steatohepatitis have recently been recognised as a part of a cluster of non‐communicable diseases that account for the majority of premature deaths worldwide. The epidemiology, biological relationships of obesity to MASLD, mechanistic relationships between MASLD and other non‐communicable diseases, and the clinical impact on patients are reviewed in this manuscript. We also include a basis for obesity‐oriented treatment to manage MASLD. In summary, we provide a rationale for a holistic approach to the care of individuals with MASLD with a focus on treating obesity as a root cause, while monitoring liver status to determine the need for additional liver‐targeted therapeutics.

Introduction

Excess adiposity is highly prevalent in the general population and is categorised as overweight (body mass index (BMI) 25–29.9 kg/m2) or obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) [1]. Up to 74% of individuals with obesity and 90% of individuals with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2) have excess hepatic fat accumulation, which is defined by > 5% hepatic steatosis. This hepatic phenotype, linked to excess adiposity, is known as metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). MASLD has two principal phenotypes: metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver (MASL), which was previously referred to as nonalcoholic fatty liver, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a more histologically active form of the disease (Table 1) [2, 3, 4]. The activity of MASLD reflects ongoing metabolic overload and injury to the liver, whereas the resultant scarring severity is reflected in the disease stage. Both activity and stage are key aspects of MASLD and inform the current understanding of the disease; these factors are assessed via liver histology (Table 2) [5]. The development of MASLD and its progression has important health implications for affected individuals. The current review provides a summary of the relationship between obesity and MASLD from an epidemiological and disease development perspective. The review also summarises current approaches to assessment and therapy, particularly in the context of obesity management in a holistic manner.

Epidemiology and Trends in Obesity and MASLD

For the past half‐century, obesity has remained one of the most rapidly progressing global health challenges. The worldwide prevalence of individuals who are overweight and obese has tripled since 1975. Approximately 2 billion adults are overweight and more than 650 million adults are obese, reflecting an epidemic driven by a variety of factors, including modern lifestyle choices, decreased physical activity and increased consumption of energy‐dense and nutrient‐poor foods [6, 7, 8]. Obesity affects all global regions, but the burden is asymmetrically distributed. In the United States, there has been an increase in obesity from 30.5% in 1999 to 42.4% in 2018 [6]. Other high‐income countries (HICs), such as Russia, Turkey and Mexico, report similar rates [6]. Even regions with historically low prevalence of obesity, such as the Western Pacific, have experienced a fivefold increase in obesity in recent decades. Furthermore, 50% of global obesity is concentrated in 10 countries, including the United States, China, India and Brazil [9].

Incidence and Prevalence of Obesity

From 1990 to 2020, the global prevalence of adult obesity increased by 104.9% in females and by 155.1% in males, with prevalence increasing most rapidly in north Africa and the Middle East. When stratified based on gender, the prevalence of obesity tripled in males and doubled in females during the same time interval [10]. Notably, while the prevalence of males who are overweight and obese was higher than females before 40 years of age, the opposite is true for individuals greater than 40 years of age in central Europe, eastern Europe, southeast and east Asia and Oceania. In contrast, the prevalence of females who are overweight and obese remained consistently high throughout adulthood in north Africa, the Middle East, sub‐Saharan Africa and south Asia [9]; this gender difference is partially related to higher average body fat composition, along with complex social and cultural determinants of health.

The prevalence of obesity increases with age and peaks between 50 and 65 years, due to the culmination of chronic metabolic and environmental exposures. Childhood obesity affects approximately 340 million children and adolescents worldwide [11]. Rates of childhood obesity have plateaued in HICs, yet continue to uptrend globally, highlighting the persistent disparities between various socioeconomic and cultural regions [7]. In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), higher socioeconomic status has been associated with greater obesity risk, whereas in HICs, obesity disproportionately affects disadvantaged groups [7].

Projections for the Burden of Obesity and MASLD

Projections estimate that given current trends in obesity, about 58% of the world's population will be overweight or obese by 2030 [7]. Despite significant research efforts and public health awareness, no country has been able to reverse obesity trends. Efforts to slow the obesity epidemic will require a combination of lifestyle modifications, environmental and policy changes and effective and widely available pharmacotherapy options, such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs).

With the worldwide increase in obesity, the rate of MASLD development and associated complications has also increased. Obesity is the strongest risk factor for MASLD, with prevalence of MASLD in adults with obesity noted to be as high as 60%–90%, compared to 10%–20% in adults with normal weight (Table 3) [12, 13]. In a meta‐analysis of over 8 million patients, the pooled global prevalence of MASLD was estimated at 25.24%, with highest prevalence in South America and the Middle East and lowest prevalence in Africa [14]. The prevalence of MASLD increases incrementally with age; however, there is limited data in those over 70 years of age [14]. Additionally, there is limited data on the incidence of MASLD; one meta‐analysis indicates an incidence rate of 52.34 per 1000 in Asia and 28 per 1000 in Israel [14]. About 41% of patients with histological MASH had progression of fibrosis, with an annual progression rate of 0.09%. The risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) increases with the severity of fibrosis. In patients with MASLD, the incidence of HCC was 0.44 per 1000 person‐years with liver‐specific mortality of 0.77 per 1000 person‐years and overall mortality of 15.44 per 1000 person‐years. In patients with MASH, the incidence of advanced fibrosis was 67.95 per 1000 person‐years; the incidence of HCC was 5.29 per 1000 person‐years with liver‐specific mortality of 11.77 per 1000 person‐years and overall mortality of 25.56 per 1000 person‐years. Patients with MASLD and specifically MASH, have higher liver‐specific and overall mortality [14].

MASLD is projected to continue rising sharply over the next few decades, driven by global obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndromes. Models estimate that 36.8% of the United States adult population (101.2 million people) will have MASLD in 2030 and will further increase to 41.4% (121.9 million people) by 2050 [15]. As result of the expected increase in MASLD prevalence, clinically significant fibrosis and cirrhosis prevalence are also expected to increase. HCC incidence is expected to double secondary to increased numbers of individuals with advanced fibrosis. By 2030, the prevalence of cirrhosis is anticipated to increase by 168%, with HCC increasing by 137% and liver‐related deaths increasing by 178% [16].

The Influence of Obesity on the Development and Progression to MASLD

Obesity is a major independent risk factor for the development of MASLD, with a 3.5 times higher risk in individuals with obesity compared to individuals without obesity. Individuals with obesity and MASLD have increased severity of liver disease and worse outcomes [17, 18]. In individuals with morbid obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m2), there is a 95% prevalence of MASLD with higher rates of fibrosis and HCC [19].

The Systemic Milieu of Obesity and the Development of MASLD

The systemic milieu of obesity describes changes in the body's internal environment characterised by structural and functional changes to gut cells, activation of the immune system, impairment of metabolic flexibility, insulin resistance, adipose tissue dysfunction and lipotoxicity (Figure 1) [20]. With increasing global rates of obesity, it is important to understand how the systemic milieu promotes hepatic steatosis [20]. Obesity and high nutrient intake alter the proximal intestines in an adaptive response; these adaptations cause alterations in wall permeability, protective functions and microbiota composition resulting in injury, inflammation and hepatic fat accumulation [21]. Gut dysbiosis and increased adiposity result in activation of the innate and adaptive immune systems, inducing an inflammatory response and subsequent liver damage. Additionally, increased visceral adiposity results in impairment of metabolic flexibility and insulin resistance, a hallmark of MASLD and leads to increased lipid load within the liver [22]. The lipid load, through a series of mechanisms, induces hepatocellular stress and injury, generating pro‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrotic molecular cues. Continued lipotoxic load and related cellular injury lead to cell death, chronic inflammation and fibrogenic remodelling of the liver architecture; simultaneously, regenerative mechanisms are also triggered. The balance between hepatic injury and related inflammation, fibrosis, regeneration and tissue repair determines the overall trajectory of MASLD. In general, MASH progresses to cirrhosis over a period of many years; importantly, this period is often clinically silent, underscoring the need to actively identify MASLD, including the level of fibrosis, to treat and reverse the disease course.

Impact of genetic factors and disease initiators/triggers on MASLD pathogenesis. The development and progression of liver disease is influenced by two major factors: Genetic risk and disease initiators/etiologic triggers. Genetic factors influence the ability of the body to repair liver damage. Additionally, etiological triggers, such as increased adipose tissue and alcohol use, worsen liver injury. In combination, genetics and etiologic triggers impair metabolic flexibility that leads to metabolic overload, cell stress, inflammation and fibrosis; ultimately, these factors result in MASLD/MASH. Genetics, etiological triggers and continued injury in the setting of MASLD/MASH result in parenchymal extinction, vascular changes, cholestasis, regeneration and contribute to the formation of cirrhosis [20].

The Link Between Obesity and MASLD Starts in the Intestines

Several cell types exist within intestinal epithelium that play vital roles in absorption and immune regulation. Enterocytes participate in the absorption of nutrients and regulate transcellular and intestinal permeability, goblet cells produce mucin, tuft cells play a role in immune surveillance and enteroendocrine cells assist in hormone regulation, motility and interactions with the gut microbiota [21]. Intestinal cells allow for immune protection, regeneration and adaptations to maximise nutrient intake based on dietary composition.

Individuals with obesity have increased intestinal stem cell proliferation, resulting in structural and functional changes to the proximal intestinal epithelium. Some of these changes include increased goblet cells in the jejunum and increased villus length and crypt depth in the duodenum and jejunum [23]. An increase in proximal intestinal alkaline phosphatase, an enzyme that manages enterocyte protein and mRNA expression, further drives fat absorption in the setting of a high fat diet [23]. Together, these adaptations to an obesogenic diet increase intestinal permeability and fat absorption in the intestines.

High nutrient intake in obesity also affects gut microbiota and leads to dysbiosis. MASLD severity is associated with variable changes in gut microbiota, including decreased butyrate‐producing bacteria and increased alcohol‐producing bacteria; these changes increase intestinal permeability and inflammation [21, 24]. Gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability, ‘leaky gut’, activate the innate and adaptive immune system, promoting a systemic pro‐inflammatory state [21]. Additionally, endotoxins from gut microbiota, specifically lipopolysaccharides from gram‐negative bacteria, are absorbed due to increased gut permeability, a condition also referred to as metabolic endotoxemia; these endotoxins, in combination with a leaky gut, increase inflammation and promote insulin resistance in the liver [21, 24].

Metabolic Inflexibility Is the Missing Link Between Insulin Resistance and Hepatic Steatosis

Metabolic flexibility refers to the potential of individual tissues and the body to switch substrates for energy generation based on their availability. In the inter‐digestive state, insulin levels remain low and stimulate lipolysis; as a result, free fatty acids (FFAs) are released into the bloodstream and are taken up by muscle. Within the muscle, the FFAs are oxidised to generate energy. Following a meal, insulin levels surge, shutting off lipolysis in adipose tissue and enhancing glucose uptake by muscle, switching from fatty acids to glucose as a source of energy [25].

Obesity is associated with systemic inflammation, insulin resistance and mitochondrial abnormalities in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue; consequently, insulin resistance is key in the impairment of metabolic flexibility (Figure 2), resulting in lipid accumulation in peripheral tissues [26].

The role of metabolic inflexibility in the formation of MASLD. In healthy individuals, metabolic flexibility describes the body's ability to utilise various forms of energy depending on the nutrients available at that time. In a fed state, healthy individuals will utilise glucose for energy because of insulin‐sensitive metabolic flexibility. In a fasting state, healthy individuals will store FFAs for future energy needs. With obesity, increased adiposity contributes to impairment in metabolic flexibility through insulin resistance and inefficient fatty acid oxidation. In the fed state, patients with obesity will store fatty acids in abundance due to impaired insulin signalling and increased blood glucose concentrations associated with a high nutrient intake. The liver will store these excess fatty acids, particularly in the setting of saturated peripheral tissues, contributing to MASLD formation.

Adiposity leads to impaired insulin signalling and adipose tissue dysfunction [27]. Adipocytes hypertrophy until they have reached their storage capacity; due to the discrepancy between metabolic substrate overload in adipocytes, blood supply of lipids and cellular energy demands, there is activation of the innate immune system and adipose tissue inflammation. Adipokines, including interleukin 6 (IL‐6) and tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), suppress insulin's inhibition of lipolysis; as a result, increased FFAs are released from adipose tissue [28]. In individuals with obesity, gut‐related cues impact macrophage infiltration and GLP‐1 receptor expression and signalling into adipose tissue; this further modulates insulin sensitivity [29]. Due to increased FFAs, insulin signalling is further impacted by GLUT4 expression on the surface of skeletal muscle, leading to the reduced ability to clear glucose loads and insulin resistance [29].

In the insulin‐resistant state, fasting insulin levels are elevated, yet adipose tissue doesn't respond appropriately to the suppression of lipolysis. In the fasted state, lipolysis and FFA levels are increased; these FFAs may be taken up, but are not oxidised efficiently in muscle, resulting in accumulation of FFAs and triglycerides. The remaining FFAs are either taken back into adipose tissue or the liver [30]. In the post‐prandial state, striated muscle clearance of glucose is impaired; as a result, there is a higher systemic glucose load and excess glucose delivery and uptake in the liver. The accumulation of triglycerides in the liver is a function of synthesis versus clearance. With excess FFAs and glucose delivery, the liver converts glucose to FFAs via de novo lipogenesis, resulting in triglyceride storage in hepatic lipid droplets [31]. Of note, there are several gene variants such as the PNPLA3 I148M mutation that modulate lipoid trafficking from lipid droplets in the liver and have been linked to a predisposition to MASLD [32, 33].

From the Development of Steatosis to Progressive Liver Disease and Cirrhosis

Obesity and MASLD both exacerbate the rate of, and complications of, other liver diseases including hepatic steatosis, fibrosis, cirrhosis and HCC. Repeated and long‐lasting inflammation and deposition of extracellular matrix creates significant damage, particularly when multiple insults occur at once. In patients with MASLD and advanced fibrosis, there is a tenfold increase in adverse liver outcomes [34].

Mechanisms of Progression From Steatosis to Hepatocyte Injury, Inflammation and Fibrosis

While there are many etiologies of steatosis including alcohol and viral hepatitis, in MASLD, there is a significant impact from adiposity and insulin resistance [35]. The progression of steatotic liver disease from hepatic steatosis to cirrhosis is dependent on genetic predisposition and disease initiators, like alcohol and obesity [36].

Much of the literature has focused on the accumulation of triglycerides as the hallmark of MASLD; while this is true, there is also widespread dysregulation of lipid metabolism across all classes of lipids [37]; this dysregulation results in the accumulation of biologically active lipid molecules. Biologically active lipid molecules cause injury to hepatocytes and trigger activation of cell stress and death signalling. Bile acids are a type of lipid molecule, synthesised in the liver and play an integral role in the absorption of fats within the digestive tract, maintenance of the gut microbiota, regulation of cholesterol metabolism and signalling for lipid and glucose metabolism [38]. With obesity, disruption in bile acid homeostasis compounds metabolic disturbances, creating a self‐sustaining cycle of lipid overload and damage within the liver. Both primary bile acids, such as taurocholate, and secondary bile acids, such as deoxycholate, have multifaceted cellular functions and can promote hepatocellular injury [39, 40]. Individuals with MASLD have increased cholesterol synthesis in the liver with accumulation of free cholesterol, which is also highly cytotoxic [41, 42]. Ceramide and eicosanoid accumulation are other hallmarks of obesity‐related metabolic disruption in the liver and contribute to hepatic injury and inflammation [37, 42].

Hepatic macrophage populations further promote inflammation and set the stage for more fibrogenic states [43, 44]; together, toxic metabolites, cytokines, hepatocellular injury‐related products and cellular cues activate hepatic stellate cells. Hepatic stellate cells work with macrophages and hepatocytes to maintain liver structure and function via activation of myofibroblasts and fibrogenesis [45].

The Impact of Obesity on Progression and Rates of MASLD‐Associated Cirrhosis (MAC)

Progression to MAC occurs in 22% of patients with obesity and metabolic dysfunction [46]. In individuals with MAC, Child class A patients have better overall outcomes and lower risk of decompensation as compared to hepatitis C virus‐associated cirrhosis (HAC). In Child classes B and C, complications and outcomes are worse. Compared to patients with hepatitis C virus, MAC has a lower risk of decompensation and lower mortality overall; however, the population burden of MASLD and MAC is much higher than hepatitis C and the total population burden of cirrhosis is largely related to MASLD [47]. MASLD is now the leading indication for referral for transplant and a leading cause of hepatocellular cancer [48]. Once cirrhosis develops, the long‐term outcomes are a function of portal hypertension; this can occur insidiously and many patients are diagnosed with cirrhosis for the first time after presenting with a complication of portal hypertension, such as encephalopathy. The most common decompensation events in patients with MAC are hepatic encephalopathy and then ascites, whereas variceal haemorrhage is less common, but still a serious life‐threatening outcome of cirrhosis [49]. Sepsis is the most common cause of death in patients with MAC and HAC, suggesting recognition and early treatment of infections is incredibly important. Approximately 7%–14% of patients with MAC are referred for transplant evaluation [50]. Patients with MAC have comorbidities that increase their transplant‐related risks. Early referral of MAC patients provides the opportunity to assess their comorbidity profile and, with this information, individuals can be optimised for transplant eligability [47]. After transplantation, obesity can be worsened and contributes significantly to post‐transplant mortality [51].

The impact of obesity and MASLD on development of cirrhosis cannot be discounted, particularly since most individuals with MASLD remain asymptomatic until they are diagnosed with decompensated cirrhosis [24].

The Impact of Obesity on Progression and Rates of HCC

In the setting of hepatic fibrosis, MASLD also increases a patient's risk for HCC [45]. MASLD is a risk factor for HCC which accounts for 90% of primary liver cancers [52]. MASLD is identified as the precipitating liver pathology in 59% of patients with HCC [53]. It is unclear why some individuals develop HCC, in earlier stages of fibrosis, as compared to others [54]. While MASLD‐associated HCC rates are higher in patients with advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, there is no clear correlation between the amount of liver fibrosis and the potential risk for HCC [46]. Patients with MASLD‐associated HCC are more likely to be older, obese, and have metabolic disorders compared to HCC from other pathologies. In patients with type 2 diabetes, there is a three times higher incidence of HCC development than the general population and is associated with increased risk of mortality [55].

Overall, obesity contributes a significant amount to the progression of liver disease from the initial stages of hepatic steatosis to other associated complications such as fibrosis, cirrhosis and HCC. By understanding and treating obesity early on, we can help prevent some of the devastating impacts of these diseases on morbidity and mortality rates worldwide.

Impact of Obesity and MASLD on Other Non‐Communicable Diseases (NCDs)

NCDs are the leading contributor to premature mortality worldwide and account for over 41 million deaths, nearly three‐quarters of all global deaths, in 2017 [56]. Premature mortality is defined as death between the ages of 30 and 69. The rates of premature NCD mortality have slowly declined, but these figures are inconsistent across different diseases, regions and socioeconomic groups [57]. LMICs account for 82% of premature NCD deaths and the relative risk of premature death from NCDs is 1.5 times higher than the rate observed in HICs [57]. These discrepancies are likely due to poverty, rapid urbanisation and unregulated economic activity [57]. Even within HICs, trends continue to shift; increases in premature mortality in the United States have more recently been driven by accidental deaths, suicide and chronic liver disease [56].

Effect of Obesity on NCDs

Obesity is a complex and chronic metabolic and inflammatory condition that increases the risk for end‐organ diseases. Obesity has been linked to NCDs including cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, cancer, chronic respiratory disease and neurodegenerative disorders [58]. Obesity decreases life expectancy by 5–20 years, depending on the severity of co‐morbid conditions [59].

The four major causes of premature NCD mortality worldwide include CVD, cancer, diabetes and chronic respiratory illnesses [56]. CVD accounts for the largest number of deaths globally. Individuals with BMI > 30 kg/m2 have a two‐ to threefold higher risk of cardiovascular‐associated death, and a threefold increased risk of death from cancer [59]. Cancer is the second leading cause of premature NCD death. Obesity has been linked to malignancies including endometrial, ovarian, breast, hepatocellular, kidney, oesophageal and colon cancers [60, 61]. Excess weight has been identified as a risk factor for early death in gastrointestinal, prostate and reproductive cancers [59].

Obesity Increases the Risk of NCDs

Obesity is associated with significantly higher rates of hypertension, heart disease, dyslipidemia, osteoarthritis, diabetes, chronic bronchitis, depression and sleep apnea [60, 62]. Persistent insulin resistance associated with obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes. The rise in premature mortality from diabetes remains an ongoing challenge, especially in regions with increasing rates of obesity and limited access to healthcare. Obesity is additionally linked to osteoarthritis through inflammatory pathways. Mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, have been found to exhibit a bidirectional relationship with obesity [60]. Lastly, respiratory conditions such as obstructive sleep apnea and asthma are more prevalent in individuals with obesity. In contrast to other NCDs, mortality from chronic respiratory diseases has declined, likely due to global reductions in tobacco use [56]. Theoretical reductions in BMI by 1 unit could reduce the prevalence of these conditions on a population‐wide level; this information suggests that multiple comorbid conditions rise sharply with obesity [60, 62].

Effect of MASLD on NCDs

MASLD has a significant impact on NCDs such as CVD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), type 2 diabetes and extrahepatic malignancies (Table 4) [64, 65]. Advanced forms of MASLD carry a higher risk of all‐cause mortality, with CVD being the primary cause of death [63]. The incidence of heart failure is also increased in MASLD regardless of other metabolic risk factors [66, 67]. MASLD promotes CVD through various mechanisms, including atherosclerosis, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and hypertrophy, cardiac valve calcifications and arrhythmias. Additional studies indicate that CVD may accelerate liver fibrosis [68].

MASLD is also an independent risk factor for CKD and diabetes, and is associated with increased risk of extrahepatic cancer. MASLD/MASH are independent risk factors for progressive loss of renal function, waste production accumulation, and disruptions in fluid and electrolyte imbalances [64]. There are five major mechanisms that contribute to the relationship between MASLD and CKD: impaired antioxidant defences, low‐grade systemic dysfunction, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system activation, disruption of lipid metabolism and the production of nephrotoxic metabolites including indoxyl sulphate (IS), p‐cresyl sulphate (PCS) and trimethylamine N oxide (TMAO); these factors result in increased oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, vascular permeability, fibrotic deposition and glomerular sclerosis [64].

An increase in overall cancer risk is seen across the full spectrum of MASLD [69, 70]; this relationship is likely due to chronic inflammation, adipokine signalling via adipokine or leptin, and other inflammatory pathways triggered by production of reactive oxygen species, insulin‐like growth factor and insulin resistance [71].

In patients with type 2 diabetes, there is a bi‐directional relationship between poor glycemic control and worsening outcomes in MASH [64]. The impact of obesity and insulin on the impairment of metabolic flexibility and resulting MASLD has been discussed previously; however, it is important to recognise that MASLD also has implications on patients with type 2 diabetes through similar mechanisms.

Overall, MASLD is an independent risk factor for widespread injury throughout the body and implications in long‐term outcomes of multiple diseases. Understanding how these diseases impact each other allows for widespread opportunity for impact on the progression and treatment of obesity and MASLD.

NCDs and the Future

The US Sustainable Development Goal is to globally reduce premature mortality by 30% in 2030 [72]. In studies from 1990 to 2017, there was a decline in premature mortality from NCDs by 1.2% per year in men and 1.4% in women [56]. Widespread medical and public health advances have reduced premature mortality internationally, while mortality due to chronic diseases has decreased since 1999 in the United States [56] In order to achieve further goals, intensified prevention strategies are needed to address obesity and associated metabolic disease and to improve access to healthcare and management of chronic conditions.

The Role of Obesity Care in the Treatment of MASLD/MASH

Treatment of obesity is one of the major ways to treat MASH, since obesity has a strong impact on the progression of MASLD [73]. Treatment of obesity includes weight loss via lifestyle changes, such as caloric restriction, improved diet and increased aerobic exercise, emerging medications and bariatric surgery. Data shows that any weight loss is beneficial to help reverse steatosis, likely due to the dose‐dependent relationship between weight loss and liver inflammation, ballooning and resolution of MASLD [74].

Lifestyle and Dietary Modifications

Lifestyle changes and dietary modifications are essential in the treatment of both obesity and MASLD/MASH [75]. Aerobic physical activity, alone, decreased plasma aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase levels and steatosis in the absence of weight loss [76]. In order to meet these recommended nutritional changes, the Mediterranean diet is typically recommended, not only for improvement in hepatic steatosis, but due to its cardiometabolic risk factor reduction and mortality benefits [77]. The Mediterranean diet emphasises consumption of olive oil, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, legumes, whole grains, fish and other seafood. Patients who adhere to the Mediterranean diet have demonstrated improvement in insulin sensitivity and hepatic steatosis [78]. In addition, the Mediterranean diet encourages limited intake of sugars, refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, ultra‐processed foods and red meat.

Caloric restriction is another effective practice for both short‐ and long‐term weight loss. Continuous caloric restriction requires reduction of daily caloric intake by 20%–40% without adjusting meal frequencies. More recently, intermittent calorie restriction has gained popularity due to potential improvements in dietary adherence compared to continuous restriction; this diet has proven equally efficacious in terms of weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic risk (insulin and body fat reduction) [79, 80, 81].

Patients with MASLD should be advised to limit consumption of ultra‐processed foods high in sugar and saturated fat content and should avoid sweetened beverages, as these increase the risk of steatohepatitis and fibrosis progression, independent of caloric intake [73, 77].

Pharmacologic Agents for Treatment of Obesity

Weight loss of 10% is needed to improve fibrosis, with increasing benefit at up to 20% weight loss [82]. The implication is that weight loss alone, particularly at lower amounts, will not completely reverse fibrosis; however, there is still benefit in modest improvement of weight on overall liver markers and steatosis. Weight loss is associated with loss of both lean body mass and adipose tissue; this puts patients at risk for sarcopenia (low muscle mass), which must be monitored closely [83].

Pharmacologic therapies have been increasingly prescribed for obesity and weight loss. Specifically, GLP‐1 RAs induce weight loss through activation of the hypothalamus, promoting satiety and decreasing appetite. Consequently, decreased caloric intake is presumed to be the mechanism of weight loss associated with GLP‐1 RAs [84]. Liraglutide (Saxenda) and semaglutide (Wegovy), both GLP‐1 RAs, have been associated with weight loss of 5.5% and 12.5%, respectively [84]. In one trial of patients with obesity treated with semaglutide, weight reduction of 5.5% (6.6 kg) corresponded with 30% liver fat reduction and 35% hepatic fat volume reduction, measured by magnetic resonance imaging [85]. Alternative agents, including dual agonists such as tirzepatide (Zepbound), activate GLP‐1 and glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptors. GIP can stimulate additional insulin secretion or enhance effects and tolerability of GLP‐1 RAs. In the SURMOUNT trial, tirzepatide was associated with greater weight loss, compared to placebo, in patients with and without type 2 diabetes, and the effects were maintained at 88 weeks [84]. While there are other FDA‐approved drugs for weight loss, including Contrave (naltrexone and bupropion), Qysmia (phentermine and topiramate) and Orlistat (Xenical/Alli), there are very limited data on their utility for MASH [86].

Anti‐Obesity and Liver‐Targeted Therapies of MASH

Resmetirom, a liver‐directed, thyroid hormone receptor β‐selective agonist, was the first FDA‐approved therapy for non‐cirrhotic MASH with moderate to advanced fibrosis in 2024. Resmetirom showed improvement in MASH resolution and fibrosis regression compared to placebo, despite not causing weight loss or improvement in insulin resistance [87, 88]. In patients at risk of developing cirrhosis, semaglutide is now approved for the treatment of individuals with MASH and moderate to advanced liver fibrosis. In the ESSENCE 3 trial, once‐weekly semaglutide demonstrated MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, and improvement in fibrosis by at least 1 stage without worsening of steatohepatitis [80].

There are several next‐generation GLP‐1 RAs under development and currently in Phase 2 and 3 trials (Table 5) [82, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93]. Tirzepatide significantly increased the rates of resolution of MASH and improved fibrosis [82]. Survodutide, Elfinopegdutide and Cotadutide are dual GLP‐1 and Glucagon receptor agonists and have demonstrated effective weight loss and improved liver histology in Phase 2 trials of MASH [91, 92, 93].

Efruxifermin, Pegozafermin and Efimosfermin are fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) analogues. FGF21 is a hormone produced by the liver in response to metabolic stress and acts primarily on the liver, adipose tissue and central nervous system, targeting hepatic fibrosis and inflammation in MASH. Efruxifermin has demonstrated reduction and normalisation of liver fat content and Pegozafermin has been associated with liver fat content reduction [87].

It is important to note that fibrosis does not improve in all patients who experience weight loss, and some patients are fibrosis non‐responders. Therefore, it is critically important to monitor the liver status of patients with Stage 2 or higher fibrosis, who are receiving weight loss therapy with a GLP‐1 RA, and consider fibrosis non‐responders for liver‐targeted therapy with Resmetirom.

Bariatric Surgery

Furthermore, surgical interventions for weight loss have demonstrated coinciding improvement in MASLD. In a study comparing patients who underwent Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB) to adjustable gastric banding (AGB), both groups demonstrated improvement in MASLD. However, the RYGB group experienced greater weight loss of 26% compared to 21% in the AGB group and a more significant improvement in MASLD, respectively. These results were consistent when evaluated at 1 and 5 years [94]. In another study of patients with class 2 obesity, MASH resolution up to 5 years post‐procedure was noted in 66% of patients who underwent RYGB, 12% who underwent sleeve gastrectomy, and 22% who underwent gastric banding [95]. In the BRAVES study involving patients with MASH fibrosis, 70% of patients in the RYGB group, 70% of the sleeve gastrectomy group, and 19% of the lifestyle modification group had MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis at a 1‐year follow up; this suggests that bariatric surgery is more effective than lifestyle interventions and medical therapy alone for the treatment of MASH [96]. In another study involving patients with advanced fibrosis, 50% of patients with advanced fibrosis demonstrated improvement in fibrosis after bariatric surgery, alongside MASH resolution and weight loss [97]. In summary, patients with compensated MASH‐associated cirrhosis and obesity have demonstrated improved outcomes with bariatric surgery compared with nonsurgical management such as lifestyle and dietary interventions and pharmacologic therapies, as described above [98].

Conclusion

In summary, obesity and excess visceral adiposity are associated with insulin resistance and systemic metabo‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrogenic states. MASLD is a key consequence of these states and is a leading cause of metabolic ill‐health and related morbidity and mortality. To more adequately address these concerns, individuals with obesity should be screened and evaluated for MASLD early and regularly. Early screening allows for a proactive approach to management with reduction in risk of adverse outcomes. In managing high‐risk individuals with obesity, at risk of MASLD, weight loss should be the cornerstone of prevention. Additionally, while working on weight loss, continued screening of high‐risk individuals allows for early recognition of the adverse outcomes associated with obesity and liver disease. When fibrosis cannot be effectively reversed by weight loss alone, additional therapies with liver targeted approaches should be considered. While we await long‐term data on the impact of MASLD treatment on non‐hepatic outcomes, the availability of approved therapies and the progression of MASLD to cirrhosis, if left untreated, provide a strong rationale for assessment and treatment of MASLD according to existing practice guidelines.
