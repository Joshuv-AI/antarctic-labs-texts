Colour perception screening and shade-matching performance among undergraduate dental students: an exploratory observational study
Authors: Sergi Torné-Durán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisual shade selection remains widely used in restorative and prosthetic dentistry and is influenced by interobserver variability, illumination conditions, and individual visual factors. However, the relationship between colour perception screening findings and shade-matching performance in undergraduate dental students remains insufficiently explored.ObjectiveTo assess colour perception screening findings and shade-matching performance among undergraduate dental students using the Ishihara test and practical shade-selection tasks.MethodsAn exploratory cross-sectional study was conducted including 80 fourth- and fifth-year dental students enroled in the prosthodontics course at the University of Barcelona during the 2017–2018 academic year. Colour perception was screened using the Ishihara 24-plate test. Shade-matching performance was evaluated through two practical tasks: pairing corresponding tabs from two Chromascop shade guides and selecting the reference shade of a ceramic crown. Incorrect responses, shade-selection discrepancies, and completion time were recorded. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics and bivariate tests selected according to the type of variable and expected cell frequencies.ResultsEighty students participated in the study (33.75% male, 66.25% female). Ishihara screening findings compatible with colour vision deficiency were observed in 2.5% of participants, while isolated incorrect plate responses were observed in a larger proportion of students. Shade-matching discrepancies were frequent in both practical tasks. Self-reported ophthalmological conditions were associated with a higher number of shade-selection discrepancies (p < 0.05). Longer completion times tended to be associated with more discrepancies, although this association was not statistically significant. No significant differences were observed according to sex.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this exploratory study, shade-matching discrepancies were common among undergraduate dental students and were not limited to participants with Ishihara screening findings compatible with colour vision deficiency. These findings support the relevance of structured training in colour science and shade selection during prosthodontic education. Further studies using standardized perceptual and instrumental reference methods are needed.Subject terms: Health care, Dentistry

Introduction

The human visual system is an organized network of specialized cells responsible for encoding and interpreting visual stimuli. Tooth colour perception depends on the interaction between polychromatic hard and soft dental tissues and incident light reaching the retina. However, this process may be influenced by optical phenomena such as metamerism.

Shade selection is a critical step in prosthetic dentistry and remains one of the main challenges in achieving aesthetic dental restorations. This process relies on human colour perception, which is trichromatic and influenced by the interaction between illumination conditions, the observer, and the optical characteristics of dental tissues [1, 2].

Successful tooth shade selection and reproduction are essential clinical steps in restorative dentistry and play a decisive role in aesthetic outcomes and patient satisfaction. The increasing importance of dental aesthetics has emphasized the need for accurate colour matching, which remains a complex and often unpredictable process [3–5]. In recent decades, portable colorimeters, spectrophotometers, and digital imaging systems have been introduced to assist clinicians in shade selection [6]. More recent evidence has reinforced the role of digital workflows in shade selection, including spectrophotometric methods and intraoral scanners [7, 8].

Despite these advances, visual shade matching remains widely used in daily clinical practice and continues to be associated with significant interobserver variability.

Colour vision deficiency is relevant in clinical practice and may influence tasks that require chromatic discrimination. In dentistry, individual visual factors may affect shade-matching performance. For this reason, evaluating colour perception screening findings together with practical shade-selection tasks is of educational and clinical interest.

In addition, contemporary research has highlighted that even clinicians with normal colour vision may exhibit variability in shade-matching performance, suggesting that factors beyond clinically detectable colour vision deficiency may influence visual shade-matching performance [10, 11].

Colour is a visual perception generated in the brain through the interpretation of neural signals transmitted by retinal photoreceptors. When an object is illuminated, part of the electromagnetic spectrum is absorbed while the remaining wavelengths are reflected and interpreted as colour [12]. The retina contains rods and cones, which convert light stimuli into neural signals and enable chromatic vision through cone cells sensitive to different wavelengths [1, 13]. Because of the complexity of the visual system, multiple functional differences may occur [14].

Visible light corresponds to wavelengths ranging from approximately 360 to 760 nm. Objects reflect specific wavelengths that determine perceived colour, making vision dependent on adequate illumination [12, 13]. Colour classification systems, such as the Munsell system, were developed to describe colour in three dimensions [15]. Although widely used in dentistry, limitations in perceptual uniformity led to the adoption of the CIELAB system by the Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage [12, 13].

The colour of natural teeth results from the interaction of light reflected from enamel and light scattered and reflected by dentin, which is the primary source of tooth colour [16]. Variations in enamel thickness, translucency, and surrounding environmental factors further influence perceived colour. The harmony of colour in dental restorations is therefore one of the main determinants of patient satisfaction [16–20].

Colour perception is influenced by multiple external and individual factors, including illumination, observer position, visual fatigue, and psychological variables [5, 16, 20]. Differences related to age, gender, and experience have been reported, although findings remain inconsistent [5, 10, 21–23]. Overall, interindividual variability in colour perception is widely acknowledged [12, 13, 24].

In clinical practice, appropriate lighting conditions and standardized observation protocols are essential for reliable shade selection. Metamerism represents an additional challenge, as materials may appear identical under one light source but differ under another [2, 9, 13, 24]. Both visual and instrumental methods are used for shade determination, with conventional colour guides remaining the most common despite their inherent subjectivity and limitations [13, 16, 24–26]. Normal colour vision depends on the coordinated function of cone photoreceptors. When one or more cone systems are absent or altered, colour vision deficiency occurs [1]. Colour blindness affects up to 8% of men and 0.5% of women, and a significant proportion of affected individuals are unaware of their condition [27]. Colour vision deficiencies may be congenital or acquired and can vary in severity and clinical relevance.

Numerous tests have been developed to screen colour perception differences, including pseudoisochromatic plates and colour discrimination tests [28–30]. Among these, the Ishihara test is widely used due to its simplicity and effectiveness as a screening tool for red-green colour vision deficiencies [2, 31].

From an educational perspective, recent evidence suggests that targeted training can improve shade-matching ability in dental students and that performance may vary according to level of experience and observer-related factors [11, 32]. However, recent literature suggests that undergraduate dental students may not receive sufficient training in visual perception and colour science, which may contribute to variability in clinical performance.

Despite the recognized importance of colour perception in restorative dentistry and the recent technological advances in shade selection, limited attention has been given to individual visual factors during undergraduate dental education. Most studies focus on experienced clinicians, whereas recent evidence on dental students remains limited [32].

Understanding whether colour perception screening findings and other self-reported visual factors are associated with shade-matching discrepancies during early clinical training may help improve educational strategies. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess colour perception screening findings and shade-matching performance among undergraduate dental students using the Ishihara test and practical shade-selection tasks.

Materials and method

Study design

An exploratory descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted involving 80 fourth- and fifth-year dental students at the University of Barcelona. Data collection was carried out during the 2017–2018 academic year. At the time of data collection, conventional visual shade matching using standardized shade guides represented routine clinical practice in undergraduate prosthodontic training.

Procedure

All measurements were performed by the same examiner. After signing informed consent, participants first completed the Ishihara test. Subsequently, they were asked to match 10 shades using two unordered Chromascop guides, one of which had the identification codes covered. Finally, participants were asked to select the shade of the ceramic crown using an unordered Chromascop guide with visible codes. For the Ishihara test, an incorrect response was defined as a plate response that did not match the number provided in the test key. For the Chromascop pairing task, a discrepancy was defined as a tab paired with a non-corresponding shade. For the crown selection task, a discrepancy was defined as selecting a shade other than the predefined reference shade 2B. (Fig. 2)

All tests were performed under standardized dental chair lighting conditions. The time required to complete each test was recorded.

Data collection

All collected data were recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Variables included age, sex, Ishihara screening findings, self-reported ophthalmological conditions, time required to complete each test, number of incorrect Ishihara responses, and number of shade-matching discrepancies.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive statistical analysis was performed. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test when two groups were assessed and one-way ANOVA when more than two groups were compared. Categorical variables were analysed using Pearson’s chi-square test when expected cell frequencies were adequate; Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were below 5. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Dental Hospital of the University of Barcelona (CEIC HOUB, approval code 2017-17).

Results

A descriptive and bivariate statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 15.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Student’s t-test for independent samples was used for continuous variables when two groups were compared, one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, Pearson’s chi-square test was used for categorical variables with adequate expected frequencies, and Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were below 5. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Colour perception plays a fundamental role in restorative dentistry, as achieving natural and aesthetically pleasing outcomes remains a primary clinical objective. Patient dissatisfaction related to tooth colour has been widely reported, with previous studies indicating dissatisfaction rates ranging from 12% to over 30%, underscoring the clinical relevance of reliable shade selection [17, 33].

Human colour perception is influenced by multiple factors, including lighting conditions, physiological variables, environmental context, and interobserver variability [2, 5, 12, 13, 16, 20, 21, 23, 28]. The difficulty of accurately measuring tooth colour in the oral environment has also been emphasized, given the influence of tooth morphology, surface texture, translucency, and surrounding structures [9, 16, 18, 24]. In the present study, shade selection was performed outside the oral cavity, which may partially explain why some environmental factors described in previous investigations could not be directly confirmed.

A high degree of interobserver variability was observed, in agreement with previous studies reporting limited agreement among clinicians during shade selection [12, 21, 28]. as well as more recent investigations confirming the persistence of variability despite technological advances [11].

Differences in lighting conditions across dental units may have contributed to this variability, as supported by earlier investigations highlighting the influence of illumination on colour perception [13, 16, 20].

Although the combined use of visual and instrumental methods has been recommended to improve shade-matching performance [19], the present study relied exclusively on the Chromascop shade guide, which remains one of the most commonly used systems in clinical practice [13, 16, 20]. This approach reflects routine educational conditions, but it does not provide an instrumental gold standard and should therefore be interpreted in terms of shade-selection agreement and discrepancies rather than definitive performance measures.

The Ishihara test was used as a screening tool for red-green colour perception differences, acknowledging its limitation in grading severity and its inability to provide a complete ophthalmological diagnosis [2, 30, 34]. Therefore, the present study reports incorrect plate responses and screening findings compatible with colour vision deficiency, rather than definitive ophthalmological diagnoses.

Isolated incorrect responses on the Ishihara test were observed in 28.75% of participants. These findings were not interpreted as diagnostic, particularly when only a small number of plates were missed. Although the association was not statistically significant, a tendency was observed whereby participants with more than two incorrect Ishihara plate responses also showed a higher number of shade-matching discrepancies.

Regarding the shade guide pairing test, no previous studies using the Chromascop system were identified. Therefore, comparisons were made with studies using the Vita shade guide. Certain shade confusions reported in the literature differ considerably among studies, and no clear consensus exists regarding which hues are most frequently mismatched [5, 21, 23]. The present findings also revealed discrepancies in confused tones, reinforcing the notion that shade matching remains highly subjective and influenced by multiple variables.

Time required for shade selection has been associated with visual fatigue and increased discrepancy rates [13]. In the present study, participants who required more time tended to make more discrepancies in both the guide pairing and crown shade selection tests. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, the observed trend supports previous recommendations advocating for rapid shade selection to minimize visual fatigue. These findings are also consistent with recent studies suggesting that visual fatigue and prolonged observation time may negatively affect shade-matching performance [10].

The influence of sex, age, and experience on shade-matching ability remains controversial. Some studies suggest that younger or more experienced female clinicians perform better [5, 16, 17], whereas others report no significant differences [2, 21, 35, 36]. In the present study, no statistically significant differences were found between male and female participants, supporting studies that report no sex-related influence on shade selection performance.

Regarding clinical experience, a statistically significant association was found between academic year and discrepancies in the shade guide pairing test. Interestingly, fourth-year students made fewer discrepancies than fifth-year students, contradicting findings that associate greater experience with improved colour matching [13, 37]. This unexpected result may be explained by increased confidence, reduced attention to detail, or cumulative visual fatigue among more advanced students. These findings are in line with recent evidence indicating that shade-matching ability among dental students may vary depending on training and individual perceptual factors [32].

The presence of self-reported ophthalmological conditions was significantly associated with a higher number of shade-selection discrepancies. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the study did not include a complete ophthalmological examination and did not evaluate the influence of visual correction methods. Future studies should include standardized visual assessment and instrumental shade references to clarify the relationship between visual factors and shade-matching performance.

Due to the low number of participants with Ishihara screening findings compatible with colour vision deficiency, it was not possible to conclusively confirm the alternative hypothesis. Nevertheless, the results suggest that shade-selection discrepancies are not limited exclusively to individuals with screening findings suggestive of colour vision deficiency. Therefore, structured training and assistance during shade selection may be beneficial for dental students more broadly.

Although data collection was conducted during the 2017–2018 academic year, the core mechanisms underlying visual colour perception and shade selection remain unchanged. Conventional visual shade matching using shade guides continues to be widely used in clinical practice, despite the increasing availability of digital technologies. Furthermore, variability in colour perception and the influence of individual visual factors remain relevant challenges in dental education, as supported by recent literature [38]. Therefore, the findings of the present study remain applicable to current clinical and educational contexts. In addition, the growing emphasis on aesthetic dentistry in recent years further increases the clinical relevance of reliable shade selection [39].
