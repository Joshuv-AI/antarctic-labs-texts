Increasing both strength and toughness in ceramic-matrix composites via bioinspired porous interphases
Authors: Wang Hong, Xu Pang, Han Yan, Zheyi Zhang, Longbiao Li, Zhongwei Zhang, Daining Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ceramic-matrix composites face a persistent challenge: the trade-off between strength and toughness. Inspired by the mineral bridge architecture of nacre, we propose a reverse interphase design that contrasts with conventional dense-laminar pyrolytic carbon given the active incorporation of nanopores. Multiscale characterization and simulations reveal a dual reinforcement mechanism: nanopores reduce the interfacial debonding strength and induce crack deflection that protects fibers from brittle fracture. Meanwhile, the resulting rough fracture paths enhance interfacial frictional stress and load transfer, thereby improving the matrix bearing capacity and energy dissipation. This asymmetric modulation of interfacial properties simultaneously preserves fiber integrity and maximizes energy dissipation. The resulting single-tow Cf/SiC composites exhibit 903 MPa tensile strength, which is 38% higher than that of conventional designs, and a 1.8-fold increase in fracture energy. The interphase-enabled mechanisms identified here are intrinsically scalable, with their effectiveness further demonstrated in architectured ceramic-matrix composites. This work demonstrates a shift from empirical optimization toward theory-driven interface design and establishes a viable route to overcome the classical strength–toughness dilemma in structural composites.

Introduction

Ceramic-matrix composites (CMCs), as next-generation high-temperature structural materials, possess exceptional properties such as thermal and oxidation resistance, as well as high specific strength, providing significant advantages in applications such as aerospace engine hot-section components and nuclear reactor cladding materials1,2. However, interphase design has long faced a dilemma: overly strong interfacial bonding causes matrix cracks to directly penetrate the fibers, deteriorating fiber strength, whereas overly weak interfaces, while avoiding catastrophic brittle fracture, greatly compromise the load-transfer capability of the interphase and thus impair the load-bearing function of the matrix3,4. Previous studies have typically relied on symmetric design strategies, such as interphase thickness variations5–7 and multilayer architectures8–13, material modification14–18, and fiber surface treatments19, in an attempt to balance interfacial bonding strength. However, these approaches still struggle to resolve the fundamental conflict in achieving simultaneous improvements in both strength and toughness of CMCs, thereby constraining their potential for breakthrough performance enhancement.

Drawing inspiration from nature offers an effective strategy to address the above challenges. Natural mineralized composites, formed over hundreds of millions of years of evolution through biologically controlled mineral deposition, exhibit extraordinary mechanical properties. Among the most representative examples is the nacre structure within the abalone shell (Fig. 1a). In nacre, aragonite platelets are connected by an organic matrix approximately 50 nm thick and periodically linked by mineral bridges20 (Fig. 1b, c). This hierarchical arrangement gives nacre a unique combination of high strength (70–100 MPa) and exceptional fracture toughness (4–10 MPa·m1/2) exceeding those of single-crystal aragonite21–23. Recent studies on biomimetic interfaces in CMCs have mainly focused on replicating hierarchical architectures mechanically24–26 or mimicking biological interfacial nano-asperities via interlocked interface designs27. These studies have demonstrated the potential of hierarchical interfacial structures in nature for optimizing composite material performance. In this study, we address the longstanding challenge of simultaneously achieving high strength and toughness in CMCs. Inspired by the nanoporous mineral bridges between aragonite platelets in nacre, we propose and experimentally validate a porous interphase design strategy that is distinct from previously reported porous interphases in oxide-based systems28,29, in which controllable nanopores are constructed by tailoring the microstructure of a pyrolytic carbon (PyC) interphase (Fig. 1e). Multiscale mechanical testing, microscopic structural analysis, and combined theoretical and computational simulations reveal that these nanopores serve a dual role: reducing interfacial bonding strength to promote crack deflection and tortuous propagation, while generating rough fracture surfaces that significantly enhance interfacial shear stress. This asymmetric modulation of interfacial strength and frictional performance enables effective fiber protection and efficient energy dissipation at larger structural scales (Fig. 1f–h). Based on this mechanism, single-tow Cf/SiC composites fabricated with the porous interphase exhibit a tensile strength exceeding 900 MPa (38% higher than that of a conventional interphase) and a work of fracture 1.8 times greater, verifying the feasibility and superiority of the proposed design. This study focuses on single-tow composites to isolate interphase-controlled mechanisms, and further validation in three-dimensional (3D) architectured ceramic-matrix composites demonstrates that the proposed design principles possess intrinsic scalability at large scales. Beyond introducing a porous interphase for synergistic improvement in strength and toughness, this work establishes an integrated research framework that combines quantitative characterization, multiscale modelling, and parameterized experiments, providing a viable technical route and theoretical foundation for designing high-damage-tolerance CMCs.

Results

Design and microstructure of porous PyC interphase

In contrast to traditional PyC interphases that emphasize a highly crystallographic texture30–32, a chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) method was used to intentionally reduce the texture of PyC, creating a transitional layer with controllable nanoporous features between the SiC matrix and carbon fibers. Dense PyC interphases were also prepared for comparison. Figure 1e illustrates the process of producing single-tow Cf/SiC composites with interphases through CVI, which involved two steps. First, methane, a precursor with a high decomposition temperature, was utilized. The pyrolysis behavior of the precursor was adjusted by varying the deposition parameters (e.g., temperature, pressure, and residence time) to deposit PyC interphases with different microstructures (i.e., porous and dense PyC) on the fiber surface. Second, using methyltrichlorosilane, H₂, and N₂ gases under a consistent temperature, pressure, and deposition time, a SiC matrix of uniform thickness and composition was deposited on the interphase surfaces. These mini-composites exhibit a typical multiscale structure with numerous reinforcing fibers. The fiber surfaces were successively coated with the PyC interphase and the SiC matrix (Fig. 2b). Each mini-composite had a diameter of approximately 1 mm, with the fiber-aligned direction designated as the longitudinal (N) direction (Fig. 2b, c). Transverse cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2d, e) revealed distinct interphase morphologies. The dense PyC interphase exhibited a compact, well-organized layered structure, while the porous PyC interphase displayed a loosely packed, disordered architecture similar to the porous interfaces observed in biological materials. Both interphases were approximately 500 nm thick. The variation in structural density stemmed from differences in the level of texture order.

Bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1) further validated the well-ordered graphite layers within the dense PyC interphase, characterized by tightly aligned stacks. In contrast, the porous PyC interphase displayed an overall disordered lattice with localized clusters of amorphous carbon. Twisting among short graphitic layers contributes to a high density of nanopore defects. Selected-area electron diffraction and fast Fourier transform analysis in specific regions provided orientation angle (OA) values (insets in Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1), revealing that the porous PyC structure had broader diffraction features. The OA value of 108° for porous PyC was significantly higher than the OA value of 49° for dense PyC, indicating a lower degree of crystallographic texture orientation34.

The micropore features in the porous PyC interphase were examined through sample sectioning, polishing, high-magnification SEM imaging, and subsequent pore segmentation and extraction using machine-learning techniques (Fig. 2h–l, Supplementary Note 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2). These pores exhibited a nonuniform distribution, with band-shaped pores alternating between graphite layers (Fig. 2h). In the longitudinal cross-section, the pores randomly dispersed, with characteristic spacings of tens of nanometers (Fig. 2i–k and Supplementary Note 2). These pores originate intrinsically from the PyC growth mechanism under the selected CVI conditions, rather than from stochastic or post-formed defects. The pore area distribution followed a lognormal pattern (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, the individual pores were slightly elongated horizontally (Fig. 2l) and aligned parallel to the fibers’ tangential direction (Supplementary Fig. 4). The observed pore morphology is thought to be influenced by the sequential deposition during the CVI process35. Quantitative geometric analysis of the pores in the transverse cross-section (Supplementary Fig. 2) suggested that these pores could be approximated as oblate spheroids with principal-axis lengths of approximately 5.94, 4.66, and 3.64 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5). The porous PyC exhibited pore characteristics similar to those of interconnected aragonite layers in nacre, achieving the intended biomimetic structural design.

Micromechanical experiments

To study the fracture mechanical properties of interphases, compression experiments were conducted on micropillars with different interface tilt angles (i.e., 30°, 45°, and 60°) fabricated via focused ion beam (FIB) milling (Fig. 3a). The micropillars, composed of a mini-composite with porous and dense PyC interphases, had typical dimensions of approximately 3 μm in radius and 7 μm in height (Supplementary Fig. 7). The typical load–displacement curves obtained under the displacement control mode (displacement rate: 10 nm/s) for both porous and dense PyC interphases are depicted in Fig. 3b (full data in Supplementary Fig. 8). The shear stress under fracture conditions was determined by identifying the critical fracture load for various interface tilt angles. Utilizing the Mohr–Coulomb fracture criterion36 enabled linear fitting to derive two crucial parameters associated with interface fracture properties: the interfacial debonding strength τd and the interfacial friction coefficient μi (Fig. 3c). Both interfaces displayed brittle fracture behavior. The interfacial debonding strengths τd for the porous and dense PyC interphases were 198.0 ± 10.8 MPa (fitted value ± standard error, n = 9 independent micropillars) and 366.1 ± 21.2 MPa (n = 9), respectively, with the corresponding internal friction coefficients μi being 0.32 ± 0.02 and 0.13 ± 0.04 (Fig. 3c). To further investigate the sliding friction properties of the interfaces, fiber push-in tests were performed on mini-composite samples containing both porous and dense PyC interphases (Fig. 3i). The typical load–displacement curves obtained under the displacement control mode (10 nm/s) are shown in Fig. 3j (full data in Supplementary Fig. 9). The original displacement data were adjusted by subtracting the fiber’s elastic response to derive the slip displacement. Using the Marshall–Oliver model37, the interfacial sliding friction stress τs was determined as 7.52 ± 1.87 MPa (mean ± standard deviation, n = 10) for the porous PyC interphase and 5.44 ± 0.83 MPa (n = 10) for the dense PyC interphase. The higher friction stress of the porous PyC interphase suggests a potentially rougher sliding surface and greater variability, indicating increased tortuosity in crack propagation. In summary, the porous PyC interphase enables an asymmetric performance transition by simultaneously reducing the interfacial debonding strength and increasing the frictional stress. This unusual transformation may hold the key to overcoming the strength–toughness trade-off in composites.

As mechanistic probes of interfacial failure, micromechanical experiments yielded quantitative interfacial parameters, revealing pronounced differences in fracture locations and crack propagation paths between the porous and dense PyC interphases. These differences are reflected by the distinct features observed on the fracture surfaces. Post-experiment micropillar compression of the porous PyC interphase revealed a cross-section exhibiting porous characteristics clearly (Fig. 3d). The top view exhibited a residual fish-scale-like PyC structure (blue arrow) and a rough fracture surface (Fig. 3d, inset). Subsequent fiber push-in tests (Fig. 3l) unveiled random transverse microcracks (yellow arrows) extending within the interphase, forming a tortuous crack path with a maximum roughness Rz of 216.7 nm (Fig. 3n). In contrast, the dense PyC interphase maintained its complete PyC composition post-micropillar compression (Fig. 3g). The top view displayed smooth grooves on the fiber surface, indicating crack propagation along the matrix/interphase (M/I) interface (Fig. 3g, inset). The cross-section from the push-in test (Fig. 3m) provided direct evidence of crack propagation within the M/I bonding layer, showing a relatively straight crack path and a maximum roughness Rz of 133.3 nm (Fig. 3o). Full-view cross-sectional observations revealed that interfacial pores in the porous PyC interphase facilitated a debonding mechanism leading to crack propagation from the interface edge, followed by cohesive propagation within the interphase, resulting in tortuous fracture paths and rough fracture surfaces. In summary, the porous PyC interphase exhibits a dual functional effect of actively inducing crack deflection and generating rough fracture surfaces. The alteration in local fracture behavior highlights its purposeful control over crack evolution.

Microscale damage mechanisms

Thin slices prepared after the micropillar compression tests were used for TEM imaging to further investigate the fracture mechanisms (Fig. 3e, f, and h). TEM revealed significant crack propagation in the porous PyC region (approximately 500 nm thick) between the carbon fiber and polycrystalline β-SiC under applied loading (Fig. 3e). Cracks initiated in array-like patterns within the interphase and developed a tortuous propagation path, particularly along regions rich in nanopores (yellow arrows in Fig. 3e). High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging (Fig. 3f) confirmed that this nonlinear propagation mode was driven by two primary factors: initial nanopores (dark circular holes, marked by white arrows, with an average diameter of ~5 nm) acting as stress concentrators and the aggregation and propagation of cracks due to overlapping stress fields at the crack tip caused by adjacent pores (yellow arrows). High-resolution TEM (Fig. 3f, inset) revealed regions of textured disordered amorphous carbon (approximately 5–8 nm in size) along the crack path, indicating local plastic deformation that resulted in lattice distortion of the graphite microcrystals. In contrast, the damage to the dense PyC interphase exhibited typical linear fracture characteristics (yellow arrows in Fig. 3h), with cracks propagating in a straight line along the interphase/fiber (I/F) boundary, resulting in interlayer debonding attributed to interlayer sliding. Quantitative atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis of the fiber push-in test samples confirmed the differences in the interfacial damage modes. The fracture surface of the porous PyC interphase displayed deep internal grooves (Fig. 3p), whereas in the dense PyC, the fracture occurred at the interface between the interphase and the fiber or matrix, leaving the interphase undamaged and intact (Fig. 3q). The difference in the fracture behavior is attributed to the crack propagation path controlled by the minimum energy dissipation mechanism associated with the interfacial nanopore structure. Microcracking, rather than dislocation slip, was the dominant deformation mechanism in the porous PyC interphase.

The atomic-scale model further clarifies the role of microporosity in initiating interfacial microcracking. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations compared the shear responses of PyC interphases with different OAs (49° for dense PyC and 108° for porous PyC), as shown in Fig. 4a–c. Dense PyC exhibits moderate interlayer crosslinking via sp³ hybridization, enhancing the interlayer connectivity and suppressing shear sliding (Fig. 4c). Additionally, misoriented nanodomains further hinder crack propagation, collectively enhancing the shear resistance of the dense interphase. Consequently, shear failure tends to localize along weaker interfaces and propagates unidirectionally. In contrast, porous PyC has more disordered subdomains and a greater degree of interlayer crosslinking across expanded voids (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 16), significantly reducing the interfacial shear strength and causing multidirectional damage within the interfacial region (Fig. 4a). Notably, the pores in the simulation were approximately 5 nm in size, consistent with experimental observations using SEM and TEM (Figs. 2h and 3f). Beyond influencing the initiation of microcracks, interphase pores actively modulate the crack propagation modes and shape the fracture surface morphology. To clarify the impact of pore-related parameters on fracture behavior within the interfacial domain, theoretical analyses and simulation methods were utilized. A micropillar compression model was developed, incorporating triaxial oblate ellipsoidal pores in the interphase, using finite element/phase-field methods (FEM/PFM). This model captured the effect of the pore architecture on the fracture surface morphology (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Note 3). Simulation results revealed that porous interphases facilitated crack propagation along interconnected pore networks, leading to rough fracture surfaces similar to those observed in micropillar compression tests (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 12a; cf. Fig. 3d). In contrast, crack initiation in nonepore systems predominantly occurred at the fiber/matrix interfaces due to stress concentration, resulting in smooth fracture surfaces (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 12b; cf. Fig. 3g).

Multiscale strengthening and toughening mechanisms

Previous research has emphasized the inherent trade-off between strength and toughness in traditional CMCs, where interfacial engineering has predominantly relied on symmetric design strategies to balance interfacial bonding strength. However, these approaches often do not separate interfacial strength from frictional properties and neglect crack path modulation strategies. In this study, we introduce nanoscale porosity into the interphase to achieve asymmetric interfacial regulation. This results in a 46% reduction in interfacial shear debonding strength (from 366.1 to 198.0 MPa) and a 38% increase in frictional shear stress (from 5.44 to 7.52 MPa) (Fig. 3c, k). This design enables the interphase to asymmetrically tailor interfacial properties through its internal porous structure, protecting the fibers while enhancing load transfer between the fibers and matrix, thereby effectively dissipating energy and suppressing catastrophic failure. Compared to conventional interfacial design strategies, this parameter-decoupled approach enables Cf/SiC mini-composites to exceed the mechanical performance limits of traditional dense interphases. Compared with other interfacial design strategies for single-tow composites6,13–19, this approach achieves a high fracture strength of approximately 903 ± 32 MPa (mean ± standard deviation, n = 4). Moreover, it represents a 38% improvement over standard designs, while retaining a high fracture strain of 1%. From the perspective of fracture evolution, the porous interphase controls crack propagation through its internal pore architecture, interacting across length scales with higher-order structural features to enhance the strength and toughness of composite materials. This is enabled by several key mechanisms operating at distinct hierarchical levels. At the single-pore level, the elliptical geometry of the interphase pores results in an anisotropic stress intensity distribution, with values along the major axis nearly double of those along the minor axis (Supplementary Note 5). These pores also exhibit a preferred orientation aligned tangentially to the fibers (see distribution pattern fitted in Supplementary Fig. 4e), promoting crack blunting and deflection perpendicular to the fiber direction via the Cook–Gordon effect39 (Fig. 5g mechanism i and Supplementary Note 5). At the mesoscale, the coalescence of randomly distributed lateral cracks between pores alters crack propagation trajectories and increases the morphological irregularity of fracture surfaces (Fig. 5g mechanism ii and Supplementary Fig. 13g, h). At the interfacial scale, the reduced fracture toughness of the porous PyC interphase shifts crack deflection behavior from primarily unidirectional to bidirectional, potentially reducing stress concentrations on the fiber surface (Fig. 5b, c, and g mechanism iii). Moreover, the percolation of cracks through the pore network gives rise to tortuous, interlocked fracture paths that substantially enhance load-transfer efficiency and interfacial frictional resistance (Fig. 5b, c, and g mechanism iv). Together, these hierarchical mechanisms guide a controlled crack path evolution from a nanopore geometry to microscale structural interactions, ultimately governing the mechanical response at the fiber/matrix level.

Thus, the porous PyC interphase thus performs dual functions by aiding multidirectional crack deflection and promoting the formation of rough fracture surfaces. This protects the fibers and simultaneously alters matrix cracking behavior. Synchrotron-based in situ measurements and post-deformation analyses (Fig. 5d, e), along with quantitative assessments (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 22), indicate that Cf/porous PyC/SiC mini-composites have a significantly higher main crack density Nc (19/mm) than Cf/dense PyC/SiC (10/mm), while the average crack opening displacement (COD) is notably lower (2.4 μm vs. 4.6 μm). This indicates that deformation energy is more efficiently utilized in creating new cracks rather than in enlarging existing ones. These results emphasize a synergistic toughening mechanism where the porous PyC interphase enhances the matrix’s mechanical performance by simultaneously reducing the COD (Fig. 5g mechanism v) and increasing the crack density Nc (Fig. 5g mechanism vi), thereby enhancing both the load-bearing capacity and energy-dissipation efficiency.

Application and performance evaluation of porous PyC interphases in 3D composites

To verify the applicability of the interphase-enabled strengthening toughening mechanisms revealed at the microscale to architectured composites, porous PyC interphases were incorporated into 3D-Cf/SiC composites. In this process, the biomimetic design does not aim to replicate the overall geometry of nacre, but rather extracts and translates its porous interfacial feature into the fibre/matrix interface, consistent with recent bioinspired materials studies that emphasize the translation of key structural motifs across scales40. Single tow mini-composites are primarily employed to characterize fiber–matrix interfacial debonding and sliding. In contrast, 3D composites inevitably experience complex stress states under service conditions and involve the evolution of multiple damage modes41,42. Dense and porous PyC interphases with comparable thicknesses (~ 500 nm) were deposited onto the carbon fibers, followed by infiltration of a uniform SiC matrix using CVI process (Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24), as shown in Fig. 6a. In this processing route, the performance differences among the composites originate predominantly from the interphase architecture, rather than from geometric or processing variations. The resulting 3D-Cf/SiC composites exhibit higher fracture toughness and relatively high flexural strength compared with previously reported Cf/SiC composites43–52 (Fig. 6b). Therefore, the porous PyC interphase significantly enhances the damage tolerance of the material. Figure 6c depicts a systematic comparison of the mechanical and environmental performance of Cf/porous PyC/SiC and Cf/dense PyC/SiC 3D-composites. With the introduction of a porous PyC interphase, the room-temperature mechanical properties are markedly enhanced. Specifically, the tensile strength increases from 158.5 ± 36.6 to 385.4 ± 14.1 MPa (n = 3, Fig. 6c(i)), the flexural strength increases from 206.0 ± 25.5 to 298.0 ± 25.2 MPa (n = 3, Fig. 6c(ii)), and the fracture toughness increases from 5.3 ± 1.1 to 30.1 ± 2.5 MPa·m1/2 (n = 3, Fig. 6c(iii)). Detailed analyses are provided in Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Figs. 26–28. Meanwhile, this strength advantage is retained under high-temperature tensile conditions, where the tensile strength increases from 80.0 ± 11.8 to 308.1 ± 47.8 MPa (n = 3, Fig. 6c(iv), Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 29). Under the high-temperature oxidative environment, the mass loss of composites with a porous PyC interphase is only slightly higher than that of those with a dense PyC interphase (an increase of approximately 4%, Fig. 6c(v)). Therefore, the effect of interphase porosity differences on the overall oxidation behavior is limited (Supplementary Note 9 and Supplementary Fig. 30). This is due to the porous PyC interphase in this study predominantly containing isolated, closed pores. However, it does not form continuous diffusion channels, thereby reducing the risk of oxygen transport pathways. Digital image correlation (DIC) strain fields obtained during notched-beam bending tests further elucidate the differences in damage evolution mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 28d, e). In composites with dense PyC interphases (Fig. 6d), deformation rapidly localizes into a dominant through-thickness crack with limited crack deflection capability, leading to premature failure. In contrast, composites incorporating porous PyC interphases (Fig. 6e) exhibit pronounced interlaminar transverse cracking and distributed strain localization, reflecting effective crack deflection, interfacial debonding, and multiscale energy dissipation. SEM observations of fracture surfaces further corroborate these findings. The fracture surfaces of composites with dense PyC interphases (Fig. 6f) are relatively flat, with smooth fiber surfaces and limited fiber pull-out. This indicates a strong interfacial bonding and predominantly brittle fracture behavior. However, composites with porous PyC interphases (Fig. 6g) retain pronounced residual PyC on the fiber surfaces and exhibit a markedly rough fracture morphology. Therefore, crack deflection and enhanced interfacial friction mechanisms remain operative. These observations link the microscale interphase-controlled mechanisms to macroscopic improvements in strength, toughness, and damage tolerance.

Overall, the performance advantages conferred by porous PyC interphases are fully manifested and validated at the scale of 3D-composites. This enhancement is attributable to the cooperative activation of interfacial mechanisms within individual fiber bundles, whereby the porous PyC interphase promotes energy-dissipating processes such as crack deflection, interfacial sliding, and fiber pull-out. These mechanisms are repeatedly triggered within each bundle and further amplified and accumulated throughout the three-dimensional architecture, primarily in the X/Y in-plane directions, where load transfer is bundle-dominated, and the fiber volume fraction is higher. Therefore, the 3D composite can be regarded as a structural-scale implementation of the biomimetic porous-interphase concept established at the single-tow scale. Consequently, catastrophic fracture is significantly delayed at the macroscopic scale, rendering the advantages of interphase structural design more pronounced. Under failure conditions dominated by complex crack pathways, porous PyC interphases also exhibit considerable design potential for improving the mechanical reliability and environmental adaptability of three-dimensional ceramic matrix composites.

Discussion

Nacre, a typical biomineralized material, showcases a complex interfacial structure with porous mineral bridges linking aragonite platelets. This hierarchical structure simultaneously facilitates crack deflection and enables efficient load transfer through rough sliding interfaces, contributing to its remarkable combination of strength and toughness23,53–55. Inspired by both the structural configuration and strengthening-toughening mechanism of this natural interface, we strategically introduced nanoscale porosity into the PyC interphase by tailoring its texture through CVI. Our integrated experimental and modeling investigations reveal that the content, geometry, and spatial arrangement of the interphase pores critically dictate localized fracture processes and modulate higher-order phenomena such as matrix load transfer and energy dissipation. These synergistic effects translate into substantial improvements in both the strength and toughness of the mini-composites. The interphase-enabled failure mechanisms observed at the single-tow scale are effectively transmitted and amplified within 3D-composite architectures. Cooperative crack deflection, interfacial sliding, and matrix crack dissipation across fibre bundles collectively enhance damage tolerance at the structural scale. While prevailing interphase engineering strategies have mainly focused on adjusting bonding strength across interfaces, our findings highlight the often-overlooked but pivotal role of the internal architecture and intrinsic properties of the interphase. This study establishes a design paradigm for damage-tolerant brittle matrix composites and broadens the design space for advanced functionally layered interfacial systems. Furthermore, developing systematic control over the pore architecture of the porous PyC interphase and establishing quantitative structure–property relationships at the composite level will provide an avenue for driving rational interfacial design.

Methods

Materials preparation of mini-composites

Cf/SiC mini-composites with two types of actively controlled pyrolytic carbon (PyC) interphases were produced using chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) process. Initially, a single continuous carbon fiber tow T300™ (Toray Co., Ltd., Japan) was wound onto a graphite frame, placed in a graphite mold, and positioned in the Al₂O₃ chamber of a CVI deposition furnace. The chamber temperature was then raised and stabilized at 1150 °C, while methane (CH₄) served as the carbon source. Per our prior research56, to achieve uniform PyC interphases with distinct textures but consistent submicron-scale thickness, the deposition conditions for porous PyC were set to a gas residence time of 0.1 s, pressure of 5 kPa, and total duration of 3 h. For dense PyC, the corresponding parameters were 1 s, 20 kPa, and 0.5 h, respectively. Specifically, porous PyC was deposited under conditions of a relatively high pressure and an extended gas residence time, promoting homogeneous gas-phase reactions of CH₄. These conditions result in more complete gas-phase decomposition and an increased molar ratio of C₂ to C₆ species among the intermediates. C₂ species predominantly drive the growth of the PyC interphase, attaching to the jagged edges of PyC nuclei and forming protruding five-membered carbon rings (C₅) on the basal planes. This leads to distorted growth of the PyC basal planes, highly disordered orientation of the graphite layers, and the emergence of numerous pore defects within these locally disordered regions due to their enhanced capacity to accommodate structural distortions and minimize energy56,57. In contrast, dense PyC is formed under low-pressure conditions and short gas residence times, suppressing the thermal decomposition of CH₄. In this scenario, both C₂ and C₆ species contribute to PyC growth, favoring the formation of extended, coplanar six-membered carbon rings (C₆). Consequently, the PyC develops a dense and well-ordered graphite-like layered structure (Fig. 1e). After the interphase deposition, MTS with a nitrogen–hydrogen (N₂–H₂) gas mixture was used as the precursor for the subsequent CVI deposition of the SiC matrix. This process was conducted under a constant pressure of 5 kPa and temperature of 1100 °C for 8 h to ensure ample SiC infiltration between fiber tows for a continuous and dense matrix. Following natural cooling to room temperature, the samples were extracted from the furnace and trimmed to obtain intact single-tow mini-composites. The deposition parameters are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Electron microscopy

The polished samples were analyzed using high-resolution cold-field scanning electron microscope (SEM) Regulus SU8230 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a working distance of 12 mm. Before imaging, an ultrathin Pt layer was sputter-coated onto the sample surface using an ion sputtering coater MC1000 (Hitachi) to increase the electrical conductivity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) samples were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) system Helios G4 UC (FEI, Hillsboro, Oregon, USA). The detailed preparation steps were as follows: (1) the sample was embedded in resin, cured, and polished to expose the surface of interest; (2) a 2-μm-thick Pt protective layer was deposited on the target region; (3) grooves were milled on both sides of the Pt strip using an FIB, producing an approximately 2-μm-thick lamella; (4) a U-shaped cut was made using an FIB, and the lamella was lifted out with a micromanipulator, transferred to a Cu TEM grid, and fixed in place via Pt deposition; and (5) the lifted lamella was progressively thinned using an FIB at 10 kV with beam currents of 0.79, 0.43, and 0.23 nA. Final wide-area cleaning was conducted at 5 kV and 41 pA to achieve a clean surface and minimize damage. TEM characterization was performed by Talos F20X G2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in the standard bright- and dark-field imaging modes. Elemental mapping was conducted via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using an integrated Super-X EDS system (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM was utilized to image the interphase region post-fiber push-in testing at room temperature under ambient conditions. The imaging was conducted using a Bruker Dimension FastScan system (Karlsruhe, Germany) equipped with an Icon scanner. RTESPA-300 probes were used for imaging at a scan rate of 1–2 Hz, with the operational parameters adjusted as required. The AFM images were post-processed using the Bruker Nanoscope Analysis software (Version 3.0), which involved third-order flattening of the image and contrast adjustment.

Preparation of micropillars

Mini-Cf/SiC composite segments, each approximately 1 cm long, at tilt angles of 30°, 45°, and 60° in room-temperature-cured epoxy resin (Spurr Low-Viscosity Embedding Kit, Polysciences Inc.). The cross-sections with different interfacial inclinations were polished sequentially using diamond paste with particle sizes of 10, 5, 2, and 0.5 μm on a polishing machine TLE-Z3S (Changzhou Taylor Instrument Technology, Changzhou, Jiangsu, China). An ultrathin Pt layer was sputter-coated onto the polished surfaces to minimize charging effects during subsequent electron microscopy and ion milling. The micropillars were fabricated via FIB milling with a Helios G4 UC system, using a top-down annular milling strategy with Ga⁺ ions at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV. To mitigate ion-beam-induced damage, the beam current was gradually reduced during coarse and fine milling steps, from 9.3 to 2.5 nA and finally to 0.43 nA. Surrounding grooves with diameters exceeding 10 μm and depths greater than 3 μm were introduced to ensure adequate clearance for micropillar compression testing. The resulting micropillars, featuring interphases at various angles, had diameters, heights, and taper angles of approximately 3, 7 μm, and 3°–5°, respectively.

Micropillar compression

These experiments were conducted at room temperature using a Nano Indenter G200 system (KLA, Hayward, California, USA). A custom flat-ended conical punch indenter (tip diameter of approximately 3 μm, cone half-angle of 60°) provided by Synton-MDP (Helmstrasse, Nidau, Schweiz) was utilized for uniaxial loading. Prior to testing, positional calibration and thermal drift compensation were conducted. The load was applied in the displacement-controlled mode at a displacement rate of 10 nm/s.

Fiber push-in

Mini-Cf/SiC composite segments were inserted perpendicular to the surface in an epoxy resin cured at room temperature. Uniaxial tension

Tensile tests were conducted on mini-Cf/SiC composite samples using a universal testing machine (CMT400, Suns, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). The composites were initially cut into 8-cm-long segments. Kraft paper sheets (30 × 30 × 1 mm) were affixed to both ends using AB epoxy adhesive and cured for over 24 h, leaving a 2-cm gauge length in the center for strain measurement. The Kraft paper-wrapped ends were then secured to wedge grips for axial loading. The tests were performed under displacement-controlled conditions at a standard loading rate of 0.5 mm/s until specimen failure. To aid strain measurement, a fine black speckle pattern was applied to the sample surface. Images were captured under controlled lighting conditions using a camera with a telephoto lens. Digital image correlation (DIC) was conducted using the open-source 2D digital image correlation MATLAB program, Ncorr (v1.2.2) to compute the average strain over the gauge length (Supplementary Fig. 21). Four specimens (n = 4) were tested for each condition, and the reported values are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Synchrotron in situ mechanical testing

The preparation of specimens for in situ tests followed a protocol similar to that of for macroscopic uniaxial tensile tests, with a gauge length of 1 cm to suit miniaturized testing constraints. The tests were performed at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) beamline BL13HB using a microtomography instrument. The X-ray beam energy was set at 15 keV, with an imaging resolution of 0.65 μm per volume pixel. A custom stainless-steel in situ loading fixture was utilized to clamp both ends of the specimen between the base of a micro-tensile actuator and a rotation stage (Supplementary Fig. 22a). Tensile loading was incrementally applied until fracture occurred. Subsequently, the sample stage was rotated by 180° to capture 1080 projections of the non-fractured region. Phase retrieval reconstruction was performed using the PITRE_BM software (v3.1)62 (Supplementary Fig. 22b). The reconstructed volumes were visualized using the Avizo software package (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), and crack features were identified using an integrated deep-learning model (Supplementary Fig. 22c).

The initial configurations were optimized using the conjugate gradient method and then relaxed under the Nose–Hoover NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm for 60 ps. The system was heated to 1200 K at a rate of 5 K/ps and held for 60 ps to enhance inter-subdomain bonding, followed by cooling to 1 K over 100 ps and equilibration for another 60 ps to release internal stress. All subsequent shear simulations were conducted at 1 K to minimize thermal noise. During shear loading, the bottom 10 Å of atoms was fixed, while the top 5-Å layer was assigned a constant velocity of 0.1 Å/ps along the x-axis. A linear velocity gradient along the z-axis was applied to the free region to ensure steady shear. Periodic boundaries were set in the y-direction, with free boundaries in the x- and z-directions, and the simulation was conducted in the NVE ensemble to maintain energy conservation.

Preparation and characterization of 3D-Cf/SiC composites

3D-Cf/SiC composites were fabricated by CVI using stitched laminated carbon-fiber fabric preforms (Nanjing Fiberglass Research & Design Institute Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). First, a PyC interphase was deposited onto the carbon fibers by CVI, followed by infiltration of the SiC matrix using MTS as the precursor. By tailoring the CVI deposition conditions, two types of PyC interphases, namely dense and porous PyC, were obtained while maintaining comparable interphase thicknesses of approximately 500 nm. Detailed fabrication procedures, CVI parameters, and microstructures of the 3D-Cf/SiC composites are provided in Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 4, and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24. A WDW-50 universal testing machine (Jinan Jinyin Feng Instrument Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China) was employed to evaluate room-temperature mechanical properties. Uniaxial tensile, three-point bending, and single-edge notched beam tests were performed to determine the tensile strength, flexural strength, and fracture toughness, respectively. The experimental procedures and data analysis methods are presented in Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Figs. 26–28. High-temperature tensile tests were conducted at 1200 °C using a high-temperature mechanical testing system equipped with a vacuum/gas-controlled furnace (Institute of Composite Structure Research, School of Astronautics, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China). SEM Regulus SU8230 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to examine the fracture surface morphologies of all specimens after mechanical damage testing. Detailed experimental procedures and analyses are provided in Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 29. The oxidation resistance of the 3D-Cf/SiC composites was evaluated by isothermal oxidation mass-loss tests conducted in air at 1200 °C using a silicon–molybdenum rod–heated muffle furnace (Zhengzhou Xin Han Instrument Equipment Co. Ltd., Zhengzhou, China). Detailed oxidation procedures and analyses are provided in Supplementary Note 9 and Supplementary Fig. 30.

Statistics and reproducibility

All representative microstructural and fracture-surface images were selected from independently repeated experiments. For each interphase condition, SEM and TEM observations of Cf/SiC mini-composites were performed on three independently prepared specimens. Micropillar compression experiments were repeated using three independent micropillars at each of the three inclination angles, resulting in n = 9 independent micropillars per interphase condition. Post-compression fracture observations were performed on these independently tested micropillars. Fiber push-in tests, tensile fracture observations of Cf/SiC mini-composites, and fracture-surface observations of 3D-Cf/SiC composites were each performed on three independently tested specimens for each interphase condition. Each independently prepared or tested specimen, or each independently tested micropillar, was considered one independent experimental unit. No technical replicates were pooled to establish the reproducibility of representative images.
