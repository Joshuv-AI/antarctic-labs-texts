Split-Spectrum Amplitude-Decorrelation Optoretinography Detects Impaired Photoreceptor Function in Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Liang Zhou, Elizabeth White, Alexander Matteson, Anna Andrews, Steven T. Bailey, Brandon J. Lujan, Yali Jia, David Huang, Siyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess photoreceptor functional impairment in eyes with early-to-intermediate age-related macular degeneration (AMD) using OCT-based split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography (SSADOR).DesignProspective observational comparative study.ParticipantsAdults ≥50 years of age with early or intermediate AMD and age-matched control subjects.MethodsSplit-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography measures flash-evoked OCT amplitude fluctuations within the photoreceptor outer segment band to objectively quantify photoreceptor light responses. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of irreversible vision loss among older adults worldwide.1 In the early and intermediate stages of AMD, photoreceptor impairment may occur before clinically observable structural degeneration (e.g., atrophy). Symptoms often include slowed adaptation when transitioning from a bright to a dimly lit environment, diminished low-light vision, and decreased macular sensitivity.2, 3, 4 These functional impairments are frequently associated with large drusen and subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs), both of which are established risk factors for disease progression.5, 6, 7 Understanding when and where photoreceptor dysfunction occurs can further stratify progression risk, help to elucidate AMD pathogenesis mechanisms, and may inform future therapeutic developments.

A major unmet need in AMD is the lack of sensitive, reliable, and quantifiable measures capable of detecting and documenting such early or subtle photoreceptor dysfunction prior to irreversible vision loss.8,9 Current visual function tests are insufficient: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) evaluates only central vision and is insensitive to early or extrafoveal dysfunction. Microperimetry and visual field testing can cover a larger retina area; however, sampling remains sparse. Measurements depend on subjective feedback, and therefore, are vulnerable to variability from learning effects, fatigue, and attentional lapses.10,11 While electroretinography provides direct measurements of the photoreceptor electrophysiology, its use in routine clinics is limited by substantial logistical burden.12 Electroretinography is also highly variable and has low spatial resolution, which makes detecting early focal defects challenging.12,13 Alternative visual tests, such as low luminance visual acuity and rod-mediated dark adaptation tests, have shown promises at identifying earlier impairments; however, they are still susceptible to many of the same challenges identified earlier.14,15

Optoretinography (ORG) has emerged as a promising imaging-based approach for assessing photoreceptor function in vivo.16 By detecting and quantifying light-evoked photoreceptor image changes, ORG provides rapid, objective, and spatially localized evaluation of their function while maintaining precise correspondence with anatomical structures.17,18 Existing ORG techniques, meanwhile, often depend on highly specialized instruments, such as adaptive optics, ultrahigh-speed phase sensitive OCT, or combining the 2, which lead to small imaging fields and high technical complexity and costs, and has prevented wider clinical translation.18,19

To overcome this limitation, our group developed the split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography (SSADOR), an OCT-based functional imaging approach that enables rapid assessment of photoreceptor light responses across a clinically relevant macular area (3 × 3 mm2).20,21 Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography characterizes photoreceptor light responses by measuring associated OCT signal changes within the photoreceptor outer segment (OS) layer.20,22 Previous studies have shown that SSADOR mean decorrelation, calculated within predefined regions of interests, correlated with stimulus intensity in healthy eyes and detected reduced photoreceptor responses in hereditary retinal diseases.20,22 However, its ability to characterize early photoreceptor dysfunction in AMD has not been established.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply OCT SSADOR, or any ORG techniques, to objectively and quantitatively assess photoreceptor light responses in eyes with AMD. We hypothesize that SSADOR can detect a reduction in light-evoked decorrelation in AMD eyes compared with age-matched controls, reflecting impaired photoreceptor function. Additionally, we hypothesize that SSADOR decorrelation metrics can achieve higher sensitivity than visual acuity at identifying early or subtle photoreceptor dysfunction. Finally, we evaluated the relationship between SSADOR measurements and structural drusen parameters to explore potential structure–function association in AMD. These findings may support the development of SSADOR functional photoreceptor imaging as a sensitive and reliable tool for early disease detection, monitoring, and risk stratification in AMD.

Methods

Human Subject Enrollment

This prospective, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Casey Eye Institute, Oregon Health & Science University (Portland, OR) between July 2023 and April 2025 and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The university’s institutional review board approved all study protocols; and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Twenty-two patients with early-to-intermediate dry AMD and 12 age-matched healthy controls were enrolled. Only 1 eye per participant was included to avoid intereye correlation bias in statistical analyses. When both eyes met eligibility criteria, 1 eye was randomly selected. Inclusion criteria for the dry AMD group included age ≥50 years and the presence of drusen. Age-related macular degeneration staging was determined according to the Beckman classification: early AMD was defined as medium drusen (63–125 μm) without pigmentary abnormalities, and intermediate AMD as large drusen (>125 μm) or pigmentary abnormalities in eyes with at least medium drusen. Eyes with geographic atrophy or choroidal neovascularization were excluded.23 Control eyes had no AMD pathology on clinical examination or OCT scans. Additionally, subjects with other ocular or systemic diseases affecting the retina, significant media opacity, or inability to maintain stable fixation during imaging were excluded.

All participants underwent complete ophthalmic examination including subjective refraction, dilated fundus examination, fundus photography, and spectral-domain OCT imaging. Best-corrected visual acuity was measured using a Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution for analysis.

OCT Functional Photoreceptor Imaging and SSADOR Processing

Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography characterizes photoreceptor light responses by measuring light-evoked OCT signal variations. The origin of the photoreceptor functional contrast is analogous to OCT angiography blood flow contrast, the latter of which originates from blood cells moving between the successively repeated B-scans. Meanwhile, SSADOR detects and quantifies subtle optical scattering and nanometer-scale length changes within the photoreceptor OS using repeated volume scans before and after flash stimulus. To further enhance sensitivity, we tuned the effective coherence length of OCT imaging light (i.e., an empirically optimized fourfold split spectrum), which allowed detecting and quantifying aggregated photoreceptor responses without resolving and tracking individual cells.20,24,25

Subsequently, SSADOR functional imaging protocol consisted of 5 consecutively repeated OCT volumes. Each volume consists of 600 × 200 A-scans and covers a 3 × 1 mm2 field of view (Fig 1). A 200-ms white-light flash delivered at the start of the third volume photoactivated approximately 15% of cone opsins, a level empirically optimized in our earlier studies that provided consistently measurable photoreceptor responses while minimizing saturation and adaptation artifacts. Imaging was performed using a prototype high-speed, ultrahigh-resolution spectral-domain OCT instrument (250 kHz A-scan rate; axial resolution ∼1.8 μm in the retina).26 Each scan took about 2.5 seconds. Three scans were montaged to generate a 3 × 3 mm2 fovea-centered field of view, which was designed to balance instrument performance requirements, OCT sampling density, and clinical imaging burden. Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography functional photoreceptor imaging was performed after pharmacologic dilation and 5 minutes of dark adaptation. A ≥2-minute recovery interval was used between scans. The adaptation and recovery period were found to be sufficient to standardize baseline photoreceptor adaptation status and ensure consistent measurement between scans.27 The operator monitored the acquisition in real-time and retake the scans in the event of excessive eye movements or poor OCT signal strength.Figure 1OCT SSADOR functional photoreceptor imaging. Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography processing followed the same steps as our prior publications, which included 3-dimensional spatial registration and calculating the amplitude decorrelation between the preflash and postflash OCT volumes.21 A semiautomatic segmentation algorithm isolated the photoreceptor OS band. The algorithm first detects the centerline of the ellipsoid zone (also termed inner segment – OS junction) and the posterior boundary of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE); the RPE reference line is then shifted anteriorly to the posterior boundary of the interdigitation zone (also termed the cone OS tips). The resulting space between the ellipsoid zone and the interdigitation zone defines the OS band. The boundaries were manually corrected as needed. Finally, we averaged the decorrelation values across along the depth direction (i.e., mean projection) within the OS to generate an en face response map. Additionally, en face maps of mean OCT amplitude from the photoreceptor OS were calculated using the same registered and segmented dataset.

SSADOR Functional Photoreceptor Metrics

We characterized photoreceptor light responses using SSADOR-derived decorrelation value. In addition to the en face maps, mean decorrelations were calculated using predefined regions of interests. Regional analysis was performed using the standardized ETDRS grid. The macula was partitioned into the following subfields: (1) the fovea, defined as the central 1 mm-diameter circle; (2) the parafovea, defined as the annulus between 1-mm and 3-mm diameters, further subdivided into temporal, inferior, nasal, and superior quadrants; and (3) the total 3-mm macular area, encompassing all regions within the 3 mm-diameter.

Structural OCT and Drusen Quantification

High-resolution, motion-corrected 6 × 6 mm2 fovea-centered structural OCT scans were acquired during the same imaging session.28 The motion-corrected scans provided an anatomic reference for montaging respective SSADOR maps.

To extract drusen parameters, we segmented the posterior RPE boundary and the Bruch membrane using a custom MATLAB-based algorithm. The segmentation results were reviewed by 2 expert graders (L.Z. and S.C.) and corrected manually when necessary. Drusen were identified as a ≥14 μm separation between the RPE and Bruch membrane. This threshold approximates the mean RPE cell body thickness in the macula (∼12–16 μm) as reported in high-resolution histologic studies and has been adopted in ultrahigh-resolution OCT investigations to distinguish soft drusen from other AMD-specific deposits (e.g., basal laminar deposits).29,30 Drusen height was defined, on a per A-scan basis, as the axial distance between the elevated posterior RPE boundary and the Bruch membrane. Drusen volume was calculated by integrating drusen height across all A-scans within the central 1-mm and 3-mm ETDRS regions for each eye.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.1; R Core Team 2024). Analysis of covariance was used to compare mean decorrelation values between AMD and control groups for each ETDRS region, with age included as a covariate. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons across the 7 ETDRS regions. Effect sizes (Cliff’s Delta) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of group differences. Linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between age and SSADOR decorrelation values in control eyes. Associations between SSADOR metrics and drusen volume, drusen height, or BCVA in AMD patients were assessed using linear regression or Spearman correlation, as appropriate. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography scans were successfully obtained from all enrolled participants, including 22 eyes with AMD and 12 control eyes. The Effects of Drusen on SSADOR and BCVA

Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography decorrelation was visibly reduced in retinal regions overlying large confluent drusen compared with adjacent unaffected areas within the same eye (Fig 2C–D), whereas normal retina showed uniformly high signals (Fig 2A–B). The lower SSADOR response within the fovea subfield also qualitatively corresponds to the higher drusen burden in that area. Specifically, mean drusen volume was 0.014 ± 0.013 mm3 in the fovea, 0.057 ± 0.035 mm3 in the parafovea, and 0.071 ± 0.045 mm3 across the 3-mm macula. After normalization to retinal area, drusen burden was highest in the fovea (0.018 mm3/mm2), approximately twice that of the parafovea (0.009 mm3/mm2, P = 0.002).

However, quantitative regression tests indicated no significant correlation between SSADOR decorrelation and total drusen volume in either the fovea (R2 = 0.001; P = 0.885) or the full 3-mm macula (R2 = 0.0001; P = 0.967, Supplementary Figure 3A and B, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Similarly, BCVA was not significantly associated with drusen volume in either region (R2 = 0.176, P = 0.052 and R2 = 0.072, P = 0.227; Supplementary Figure 3C–D, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Reduced SSADOR signals were also qualitatively observed in regions containing SDD (Fig 2E–F). Because SDD were present in only 4 eyes, no quantitative subgroup analysis was performed.

Relationship between OCT Signal Magnitude and SSADOR Decorrelation

To evaluate whether SSADOR decorrelation is influenced by variations in OCT signal magnitude, we examined the relationship between OCT amplitude and SSADOR decorrelation. The correlation between OCT amplitude and SSADOR decorrelation was weak in both normal (R2 = 0.008, P < 0.001) and AMD eyes (R2 = 0.031, P < 0.001). While P values were significant, it is a result of the large number of pixels; the low correlation coefficients indicate that SSADOR decorrelation is independent of OCT reflectivity (Supplementary Figure 5A, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). We further evaluated whether OCT amplitude alone could differentiate AMD from normal eyes. Although OCT amplitude was modestly reduced in AMD eyes (P = 0.035 for the 1-mm foveal subfield; P = 0.06 and 0.07 for the 3-mm and parafoveal regions, respectively), its diagnostic performance was limited (AUROC = 0.75; Supplementary Figure 5B, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), substantially lower than that achieved using SSADOR decorrelation. These findings suggest that SSADOR signals are unlikely to be explained solely by differences in OCT reflectivity magnitude.

Discussion

In this study, we applied SSADOR, a novel OCT-based functional imaging approach, to quantify light-evoked photoreceptor responses in eyes with early-to-intermediate AMD and age-matched controls. This technique enables objective, quantitative evaluation of disruptions to the phototransduction process before clinically noticeable visual impairment or cellular loss. Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography mean decorrelation was significantly reduced in AMD eyes across all central 3-mm ETDRS subfields, with the greatest reduction observed in the fovea. In addition, foveal mean decorrelation demonstrated superior performance when compared to BCVA for differentiating AMD from normal eyes, supporting the potential of SSADOR as a sensitive tool for detecting early photoreceptor dysfunction.

Our results are consistent with prior visual function studies, which revealed photoreceptor deficits in early and intermediate AMD eyes using full-field electroretinogram and other functional assessments (i.e., cone contrast test, low luminance visual acuity, and microperimetry-based retinal sensitivity).2, 3, 4,31,32 Most of these functional assays have shown greater sensitivity than BCVA for detecting photoreceptor dysfunction in AMD. Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography further provides spatially resolved photoreceptor functional assessment that is anchored to anatomical images of the retina. The preferential reduction in foveal mean decorrelation suggests that foveal photoreceptors may experience damage earlier than what is currently acknowledged.

Interpretation of the SSADOR Decorrelation

Previous experimental studies, both ex vivo and in vivo, have shown that light stimulation induces optical property (i.e., scattering) and structural responses within photoreceptor OS that are detectable using various ophthalmic imaging techniques.25,33,34 While SSADOR does not directly measure the associated physical quantities (e.g., optical path length changes), it nevertheless captures their downstream imaging effects, manifesting as OCT signal fluctuation. Specifically, SSADOR assesses these light-evoked photoreceptor OS changes by comparing OCT volumes before and after light stimuli (i.e., OCT amplitude decorrelation). Our earlier work on normals and eyes with inherited retinal disease confirmed that SSADOR decorrelation correlates with the magnitude of photoreceptor responses to light stimuli, by varying stimulus intensity and comparing to microperimetry-derived mesopic sensitivity, respectively.

Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography decorrelation is decoupled from the OCT amplitude, because it is a normalized representation of light-evoked signal change. Practically, however, low signal areas can have artifactually high decorrelation due to noise contributions. In our study, this measurement artifact was mitigated through rigorous quality control during the imaging session, and sufficient signal-to-noise ratio was achieved in all imaged eyes in our cohort. Supporting this interpretation, we observed only a weak correlation between OCT signal magnitude and SSADOR decorrelation. In addition, OCT reflectivity alone had substantially lower discriminative performance than SSADOR decorrelation for differentiating AMD from normal eyes.

Together, these findings support the interpretation that SSADOR provides a surrogate marker of light-evoked photoreceptor responses. Reduced SSADOR decorrelation may reflect photoreceptor dysfunction arising from impaired light-adaptive responses, subclinical OS structural alterations, loss of photoreceptor cells, or a combination of these mechanisms.

Relationship between SSADOR Decorrelation and Clinical AMD Lesions

Reduced SSADOR decorrelation was observed overlying large confluent drusen and SDD on en face SSADOR maps. However, quantitative analyses revealed no significant association between SSADOR decorrelation and drusen volume within either the foveal or central 3-mm macular region. These findings suggest that overall drusen burden alone does not fully explain local SSADOR alterations and photoreceptor dysfunction.

We propose that the lack of association is primarily attributable to our result that photoreceptors overlying small drusen can exhibit vastly different functional states, with SSADOR measured light responses ranging from normal to severely diminished. Our observation is consistent with the natural history of AMD progression. First, areas with small drusen may represent relatively healthy retina at earlier disease stages (i.e., high decorrelation). Second, previous studies have shown that several drusen phenotypes, despite being small, can disrupt the overlying cone OS (i.e., low decorrelation)—likely through downward extrusion toward the RPE and consequent impairment of RPE and choroidal support.35 Third, collapsing/collapsed drusen, despite their minimal volume, are associated with outer retinal atrophy and severe photoreceptor dysfunction (i.e., low decorrelation).36 Our observation reaffirm the long hold notion that drusen alone is an imperfect indicator of photoreceptor health.37 Additionally, our results suggest that SSADOR may capture photoreceptor functional impairments attributable beyond the pathogenesis mechanism of drusen, for example, microenvironmental factors such as RPE integrity, local metabolic support, or choroidal perfusion. Because this study was cross-sectional, we were unable to provide a comprehensive view of drusen and drusen-related factors; longitudinal studies that tracking SSADOR and drusen changes and incorporating structural and microvascular imaging will be needed.

We also observed reduced SSADOR signals overlying SDD lesions. However, the low SDD incidence in our cohort (4 of 22 eyes) and their predominant location at the outer boarder of the parafoveal ring precluded formal analysis. Future studies employing a larger field of view (e.g., 6 × 6 mm2) or focusing on the individual lesion-level assessment are required.

Predominant Photoreceptor Contributions

The imaging protocol used in this study likely generates cone-weighted responses. The relatively high intensity of the white flash (∼15% estimated cone opsin bleaching), together with the short adaptation time (5 minute dark adaptation and a 2–3 minute recovery interval), would be expected to preferentially stimulate cone pathways. Although rods are numerically more abundant in parafoveal regions, cones occupy a substantial proportion of the macular area due to their larger diameter, accounting for up to approximately 80% of photoreceptor coverage at 1.5 mm eccentricity based on histologic studies.38 Accordingly, the specific SSADOR protocol used here is expected to predominantly reflect cone-mediated responses. Therefore, our results suggest that cone photoreceptors may be impacted early in AMD pathogenesis. However, future studies incorporating experimental paradigms tailored to isolate rod-mediated or cones-mediated responses are needed to elucidate the cellular origins of the SSADOR signals and to confirm our finding.

Clinical Relevance

Unlike traditional visual function tests such as visual field testing, dark adaptation, and microperimetry, which are subjective or partially subjective,2,3,39 SSADOR provides objective, spatially resolved measurements of light-evoked photoreceptor responses across a clinically relevant macular area. The stronger discrimination observed with SSADOR compared with BCVA supports its potential utility as a sensitive endpoint for detecting early photoreceptor alterations.

Importantly, SSADOR can be implemented using conventional OCT imaging platforms and can evaluate large retinal regions rapidly. In the present study, imaging was performed using a prototype high-resolution OCT system. However, the underlying methodology is broadly compatible with current and next-generation commercial OCT platforms, suggesting potential scalability for clinical studies and longitudinal monitoring of retinal diseases.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, most notably being the first study to apply ORG functional imaging modality in AMD. The study enrolled 22 AMD patients and 12 age-matched controls, which is the largest cohort among all published ORG studies. By employing a prospective design and a noninvasive, OCT-based functional imaging method, we were able to spatially map light-evoked photoreceptor responses across the macula, with high sensitivity at detecting AMD-associated changes. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, validation in larger independent cohorts will be required to establish generalizability. Although we tried to reduce overfitting of the AUROC using a single prespecified biomarker without multivariate modeling, the risk still exists. A larger scale clinical study, potentially involving multiple sites, are beneficial and required to establish reproducibility. Second, the resolution of the current OCT system does not permit visualization of individual photoreceptors, preventing definitive discrimination between functional (i.e., cellular disruption) and structural (i.e., cellular death) sources of signal change. Third, repeatability of SSADOR measurements has been validated in healthy subjects and inherited retinal diseases but not yet specifically in AMD, where fixation instability and photoreceptor heterogeneity may influence measurements in more severe cases.

Conclusions

Split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation optoretinography provides a quantitative imaging-based measure of light-evoked photoreceptor responses across the macula. Using this approach, we detected reduced photoreceptor signals in early-to-intermediate AMD eyes compared with controls, often preceding measurable visual acuity loss. These findings support SSADOR as a sensitive tool for detecting early photoreceptor dysfunction with potential applications in disease monitoring and therapeutic trials.
