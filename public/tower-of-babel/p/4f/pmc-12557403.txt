Chronic noninvasive ventilation in COPD: towards the identification of a responder phenotype
Authors: Tim Raveling, Jorine E. Hartman, Renzo Boersma, Peter J. Wijkstra, Marieke L. Duiverman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeChronic noninvasive ventilation (NIV) is an effective treatment for patients with chronic hypercapnia due to COPD, but the improvement in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) varies significantly among individuals. We conducted a cluster analysis to identify clusters of patients based on their HRQoL response to chronic NIV.Patients and methodsPatients with COPD and an indication for chronic NIV were prospectively followed. Gas exchange, lung function, exercise capacity and HRQoL were measured at baseline and at 6 months. HRQoL was measured with the Severe Respiratory Insufficiency questionnaire (SRI). The cluster analysis was performed using the self-organising map–Ward's method. In addition, we compared patient and disease characteristics, changes in outcomes and survival differences between the clusters.ResultsA total of 301 participants were included in the analysis and 214 patients completed the 6-month follow-up. We identified three distinct clusters of patients with equal NIV use but different HRQoL response: a “good responder” phenotype of patients, characterised by a clinically relevant improvement on the SRI, a high number of exacerbations, poor baseline HRQoL and high rates of mood disorders; a “moderate responder” phenotype, characterised by milder gas exchange and lung function impairment, and relatively preserved baseline HRQoL; and a “poor responder” phenotype of patients in whom HRQoL worsened and survival was greatly impaired. This cluster included patients with the most severe lung function and gas exchange impairment and low rates of systemic comorbidities.ConclusionOur cluster analysis suggests that chronic NIV is most likely beneficial in patients with frequent exacerbations, severely impaired HRQoL and a high incidence of mood disorders. Future randomised controlled trials should investigate whether the different HRQoL responses between the clusters are caused by NIV.

Introduction

For patients with severe COPD, chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (CHRF) is considered a treatable trait [1]. Chronic noninvasive ventilation (NIV) has been shown to improve health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and survival in patients with CHRF in a clinically stable condition [2, 3]. However, a meta-analysis of individual patient data found only a modest pooled effect on HRQoL [4]. Moreover, in clinical practice, it is recognised that great variety exists concerning individual responses to chronic NIV. This differential response might be due to the great heterogeneity in end-stage COPD phenotypes. Nevertheless, whether specific patient characteristics are associated with a good clinical response to NIV is currently unknown [5].

In recent years, the interest in clinical phenotyping of patients with obstructive lung disease has increased [6–8]. In a group of patients with moderate to severe COPD treated with chronic NIV, Janssens et al. identified two distinct patient phenotypes [9]: the “respiratory phenotype”, which consisted of patients with severe airway obstruction, low body mass index (BMI) and low rates of systemic comorbidities, and the “systemic phenotype”, consisting of obese patients with less severe airflow obstruction and high rates of systemic comorbidities. They found an evident survival difference between these two phenotypes, favouring the systemic phenotype. However, from these data, it cannot be concluded that the respiratory phenotype responded less favourably to the NIV, as multiple studies have shown that characteristics of this phenotype, such as severe airflow obstruction and low BMI, are negatively associated with survival, independent of being treated with NIV [10–12].

Moreover, in progressive, incurable diseases such as COPD, improving symptoms and HRQoL is considered one of the most important outcome parameters [13]. Specific questionnaires have been developed to assess HRQoL in patients with chronic respiratory failure. The broad translation and validation of these questionnaires highlight the need and importance to assess HRQoL in this patient group [14, 15]. However, the response to chronic NIV has been shown to be variable in terms of HRQoL [16]. To our knowledge, no research has been conducted that thoroughly studies patient characteristics associated with improvements in HRQoL among patients on chronic NIV.

In most countries, persistent hypercapnia is the only strict criterion that determines the indication for chronic NIV in COPD, regardless of clinical stability [17, 18]. This is deemed insufficient, given the large variety in clinical responses. Identifying patients likely to experience a good clinical response to chronic NIV is essential, not only to improve patient selection but also to improve patient education and manage their expectations. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to identify clusters of patients with COPD based on their responses to chronic NIV in terms of improved HRQoL. In addition, we compared treatment outcomes and survival between the clusters.

Method

Participants and study design

This study used data from two prospective studies conducted by the University Medical Center Groningen between 2016 and 2024: the RECONSIDER trial (NCT02652559) and the RECAPTURE trial (NCT03053973) [19, 20]. All participants provided written informed consent, and the medical ethics review board approved the protocols. The main objectives, the outcomes and the full inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in the supplementary material. In short, patients with severe COPD (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of <50% of that predicted and a FEV1/forced vital capacity ratio of <0.7) in a clinically stable disease state (meaning no COPD exacerbations in the preceding 4 weeks) and an indication for chronic NIV (arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) >6.0 kPa) were eligible. Participants were prospectively followed at the outpatient clinic for 6 months after NIV initiation and additional long-term survival data were obtained from all participants up to 1 May 2024.

Measurements and definitions of comorbidities

Patients were screened at the outpatient clinic, during which anthropometric data, the use of long-term oxygen therapy and medications were recorded. Exacerbations and/or hospitalisations for a pulmonary cause were objectively assessed by requesting data from the referring pulmonologist and general practitioner, and requesting pharmacy records. An exacerbation was defined as an increase in respiratory symptoms requiring a course of antibiotics and/or steroids and included exacerbations treated both at home and in hospital. Arterial blood gas samples were taken from the radial artery, while spontaneously breathing room air. Spirometry, a lung diffusion capacity test and body plethysmography were conducted, and participants performed the 6-min walk test (6MWT) [21]. To screen for comorbidities, a venous blood sample was obtained, medical records were screened and participants completed the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [22]. Patients with a clinical suspicion of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), defined by snoring, apnoea and complaints of disrupted sleep, were screened using overnight polygraphy. We identified the following seven comorbidities: OSA, congestive heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, atherosclerosis, hyperglycaemia, anxiety and depression. Full definitions of the comorbidities are shown in the supplementary material. Participants completed the Medical Research Council (MRC) Dyspnoea Scale to measure dyspnoea [23]. The Severe Respiratory Insufficiency questionnaire (SRI) was used to assess HRQoL. The SRI is developed specifically to measure HRQoL in patients treated with respiratory support and has been validated for patients with COPD [24, 25]. It ranges from zero to 100, with a higher score indicating better HRQoL, and consists of seven subdomain scores and a summary score. The minimal clinically important difference of the SRI is approximately 5 units [26].

NIV initiation and monitoring

All patients were initiated on NIV by registered respiratory nurses either in the pulmonary ward or at home. Gas exchange, including transcutaneous carbon dioxide tension (PtcCO2) and oxygen saturation, was measured during the night while patients were receiving their regular amount of oxygen supplementation, using a sensor applied to the earlobe (Sentec DM, software V-STATS version 4.0, Sentec AG, Therwil, Switzerland). NIV was initiated according to a standardised protocol that targeted a mean carbon dioxide tension (PCO2) of <6.0 kPa or a reduction of at least 20% in (mean) PCO2, compared with the measurement without NIV [27]. A detailed description is presented in the supplementary material. NIV was delivered through a full-face mask by a pressure-cycled ventilator, providing both an inspiratory- and an expiratory positive airway pressure, with a backup respiratory rate (BiPAP A30, Philips Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA, or Stellar 150, ResMed Inc., San Diego, CA, USA; Prisma VENT50-C, Löwenstein Medical SE, Bad Ems, Germany). If needed, supplementary oxygen was applied through the ventilator. Monitoring of efficacy was done at the patients' homes using a nocturnal PtcCO2 measurement.

Statistical analysis

We used the self-organising maps–Ward's method to identify clusters. This hierarchical agglomerative cluster method is based on the Kohonen algorithm and clusters participants based on overall similarities. The method is described in more detail in the supplementary material. We included the following variables in the analysis: BMI, number of acute exacerbations in the year prior to inclusion, blood gases (PaCO2 and arterial oxygen tension (PaO2)), FEV1 (% of predicted value), residual volume (RV) to total lung capacity (TLC) (RV/TLC), diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide (% of predicted value), the 6MWT, the presence of any of the seven comorbidities, the MRC Dyspnoea Scale score, the SRI summary score and the 6-month change in the SRI summary score. To ensure that clusters reflected the response to NIV in terms of improved HRQoL, the change in the SRI score was given a weighting factor of 1.3, compared with the other variables. The number of clusters that could be generated was unrestricted.

Data are presented as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range. Normality of the distribution was tested by visual inspection of normal probability plots and histograms. Baseline and treatment characteristics were compared between the clusters using the one-way analysis of variances, or nonparametric equivalent, with post hoc comparison conducted using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test for the analysis of variance and Mann–Whitney U-test for the nonparametric equivalent. Treatment outcomes (number of exacerbations during the 6 months of follow-up and the 6-month changes in the 6MWT, FEV1, PaCO2 and PtcCO2) were also compared between the clusters. We investigated if survival differed between the clusters using a multivariate Cox regression analysis, with cluster included as predictor. Age, sex, BMI, number of acute exacerbations in the 12 months prior to inclusion, baseline FEV1 and bicarbonate level were included as confounders. Only data from participants who were initiated on NIV were used and participants who stopped chronic NIV were censored at the date of last use.

A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22; New York, USA). The cluster analysis was performed using Viscovery-SOMine (version 7.2; Viscovery Software, Vienna, Austria).

Three clusters of participants were generated (figure 2 and table 1). Cluster 2 comprised 146 (49%) participants who showed a moderate improvement in the SRI summary score after 6 months (mean change 4.9±11.3 units). These participants had significantly higher results on the 6MWT, higher FEV1 and lower RV/TLC than clusters 1 and 3. They scored the highest baseline SRI summary scores and suffered less dyspnoea than those in clusters 1 and 3. OSA was more prevalent in this cluster.

Lastly, cluster 3 comprised 84 (28%) participants. In this cluster, the SRI summary score decreased (mean change −4.4±7.6 units). Participants were characterised by the most severe disease, as indicated by the lowest FEV1 and highest RV/TLC. Furthermore, gas exchange (by PaCO2 and PaO2) was more severely impaired than in the other clusters and the rate of systemic comorbidities was lower.

No significant differences were observed between the clusters in the number of acute COPD exacerbations during the follow-up period, nor in the changes in the 6MWT and the FEV1 (table 2). The median survival was 41 months for cluster 3 (non-responders), which was lower than for clusters 1 and 2 (52 and 56 months, respectively). The cluster variable was a significant predictor of survival, independent from confounders such as BMI, FEV1 and the number of exacerbations (table 3 and figure 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify a phenotype of patients with COPD who are most likely to benefit from chronic NIV in terms of improved HRQoL. Our analysis revealed three distinct clusters of patients: a “good responder” phenotype, characterised by a large improvement in HRQoL; a “moderate responder” phenotype; and a “non-responder” phenotype of patients, in whom HRQoL deteriorated and survival was significantly impaired. These results indicate that patient selection needs to be optimised since CHRF is not necessarily a trait that is treatable by NIV in all patients with CHRF due to COPD.

This study provides unique insights into the characteristics of patients with COPD that are associated with the response to chronic NIV. We identified a cluster of patients who improved their HRQoL by more than twice the minimal clinically important difference. These patients experienced severely impaired HRQoL and frequent exacerbations prior to the start of NIV, with low FEV1 and high rates of both systemic and mood comorbidities (cluster 1). On the contrary, HRQoL was reduced in a significant proportion of another cluster of patients, accompanied by severely impaired survival. These patients represent a distinct “non-responder” phenotype, characterised by the most severe airway obstruction, hyperinflation and gas exchange impairment, with lower incidences of comorbidities (cluster 3). Compared with the cluster analysis by Janssens et al., our cluster 3 corresponds to the “respiratory” phenotype identified in their study [9]. A unique finding of our results is that cluster 1 exhibited lung function and BMI that were comparable to those of cluster 3, but had significantly better survival than cluster 3. Since exacerbations are a key contributor to survival, we would expect worse survival in cluster 1 [28]. Moreover, other parameters associated with survival in this population, such as BMI, FEV1 and hyperinflation, do not differ substantially between clusters 1 and 3, and are more severely impaired than in cluster 2 [10, 12]. These results possibly suggest that NIV improves survival most in cluster 1, although we acknowledge that the absence of a control group limits drawing a definite conclusion.

An open question arises regarding the cause of this differential response to chronic NIV. Notably, this variation does not seem to be caused by adherence to the NIV, nor by differences in pressure support provided by the NIV, as these factors were equal between the clusters. This implies that the differential response is caused by patient or disease characteristics. An important determinant of HRQoL is exacerbation frequency. Multiple studies have shown that frequent exacerbations negatively impact HRQoL [29–31]. Our results confirm this, as cluster 1 had the worst HRQoL and highest exacerbation frequency in the year prior to inclusion. Interestingly, there were no differences between the clusters in the number of exacerbations during the 6-month follow-up. This might indicate that NIV reduces exacerbations in a subgroup of patients suffering frequent exacerbations (cluster 1), and this reduced exacerbation frequency contributes to the improved HRQoL. Notwithstanding these results, the relationship between HRQoL and characteristics of COPD is complex, and HRQoL is affected by more than just exacerbations [32]. Mood disorders such as anxiety and depression, which can significantly impact HRQoL, are common in patients with severe COPD [33]. These mood disorders were highly prevalent in cluster 1. Interestingly, patients in this cluster showed the greatest improvement on the anxiety subdomain of the SRI (supplementary table 3). The mechanisms through which chronic NIV may affect anxiety are unknown, but it can be hypothesised that anxious patients adopt an unfavourable breathing pattern that NIV may correct. Lastly, persistent hypercapnia might be a contributing factor, given that the reduction in diurnal and nocturnal hypercapnia is equal between the clusters, but both PaCO2 and PtcCO2 were higher at baseline in cluster 3. We advocate for future randomised studies to explore the response to NIV within the non-responder phenotype. Should a beneficial effect indeed be absent in these patients, it would be justifiable to refrain from chronic NIV. Instead, alternative interventions, such as pulmonary rehabilitation or (bronchoscopic) lung volume reduction, are likely more beneficial.

What may also be of importance is the higher prevalence of heart failure and pulmonary hypertension in cluster 1. These conditions are likely to exacerbate the already impaired HRQoL of patients with end-stage COPD. NIV has the potential to affect these conditions both negatively and positively. It is known that the increased intrathoracic pressure caused by NIV directly affects the venous return and cardiac output [34]. Despite these acute effects, a small study of 11 patients with severe COPD with a known heart condition did not find detrimental effects of chronic NIV on cardiac functioning during spontaneous breathing after 6 weeks [35]. However, the reduced cardiac output may also be a beneficial result of the reduced work of breathing and improvements in gas exchange [34]. In addition, chronic NIV seems to improve indirect parameters of cardiac functioning such as the prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (proBNP) [36, 37]. It must be acknowledged that most studies on the effect of NIV on cardiac functioning have been limited to patients without coexisting heart failure. To date, the effect of chronic NIV on cardiac function, especially in patients with coexisting heart failure, remains uncertain. Our data may suggest a positive effect of NIV and the results at least justify further research into the effect of chronic NIV in patients with COPD and heart failure.

We hypothesise that the difference in response between the “good responder” phenotype (cluster 1) and the “non-responder” phenotype (cluster 3) may be attributed to the underlying pathology causing impaired lung function. In cluster 1, low FEV1 and air trapping could be indicative of small airway disease and inflammation of the airways. Conversely, in cluster 3, emphysematous destruction of lung parenchyma may be the primary pathology, leading to lung function impairment similar to that of cluster 1. The high prevalences of exacerbations and systemic comorbidities might be supportive of this hypothesis, as low-grade inflammation might also contribute to these characteristics [38, 39]. This hypothesis could be studied by investigating inflammatory markers, and by investigating parameters indicative of small airway disease and emphysema obtained through computed tomography. Unfortunately, these parameters were not available, so we were unable to study this hypothesis. We advocate for more in-depth research into the clinical phenotyping of this specific population, incorporating these parameters. Such studies would provide valuable insights into the underlying pathophysiology contributing to differential treatment responses.

Our results may also contribute to enhancing the cost-effectiveness of chronic NIV, which is currently a concern. In 2022, Hall et al. showed considerable uncertainty regarding the cost-effectiveness of NIV in stable COPD [40]. This is a concern as financial resources for home ventilation may be limited. Furthermore, it is estimated that the number of patients with COPD will continue to rise until 2050 by an estimated 20%, thereby increasing the number of patients who may require chronic NIV [41]. Improving patient selection will help ensure that chronic NIV remains available to those patients with COPD who will benefit the most.

Surprisingly, we observed comparable ventilatory settings across the three clusters, despite significant differences in patient characteristics and settings that were tailored to the individual patient. For example, given the higher prevalence of OSA in cluster 2, higher levels of expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) may have been expected. The decision to increase EPAP is based on a combination of factors, including the apnoea/hypopnoea index from the ventilator and visual assessment of the flow and pressure curves indicating upper airway obstruction. Notably, patients in cluster 2 were not severely obese, and so potentially required low levels of EPAP to manage upper airway obstruction. In contrast, patients in clusters 1 and 3 had more severe hyperinflation. In these clusters, EPAP may have been increased to counteract expiratory flow limitation resulting from small airway obstruction. These factors probably contributed to the relatively consistent ventilation settings observed across all three clusters, despite the heterogeneity in patient characteristics.

A strength of our study is that we conducted our analysis in a large cohort of patients with severe COPD, without high rates of concomitant aetiologies for CHRF such as severe obesity or OSA. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the absence of a control group, we are not informed about the natural disease course within the clusters. Consequently, the observed differences in HRQoL and survival cannot be solely attributed to NIV. Second, missing data on the HRQoL outcome is a concern, as only 60% of participants had complete data on the change in the SRI. Third, not all comorbidities were assessed objectively; polygraphy data were available for 71% of participants, and cardiac ultrasound data were available for 31%. We found low to average prevalence for OSA, pulmonary hypertension and congestive heart failure, compared with other studies, so we acknowledge that some cases may have been missed [42–44]. Fourth, we were unable to account for other factors that could have influenced HRQoL responses, such as new treatments for mood disorders, changes in social circumstances or adverse events unrelated to COPD. This is particularly relevant because the largest improvement in HRQoL was observed in the SRI anxiety domain in cluster 1, while the largest deterioration was seen in the SRI social functioning domain in cluster 3. Fifth, although all participants were free from exacerbations for at least 4 weeks, we did not document the exact time interval between the most recent exacerbation and the baseline visit. As a result, it is possible that participants in cluster 1 had experienced a more recent exacerbation, and their HRQoL improvement may partially reflect the natural recovery process. Finally, since this study is a post hoc analysis, our results should be interpreted as exploratory. We recommend conducting further randomised studies to confirm our findings before definitively withholding NIV from patients meeting the criteria for cluster 3.

Conclusion

For the first time, to our knowledge, we identified three clusters of patients with COPD with a differential response to chronic NIV with respect to HRQoL, despite equal adherence to the NIV. Frequent exacerbators with poor HRQoL and high rates of mood disorders are most likely to improve their HRQoL, whereas HRQoL seems to deteriorate in patients with the most severely impaired lung function, gas exchange impairment and low rates of systemic comorbidities. Whether this difference in HRQoL can be attributed to the NIV needs to be studied in randomised controlled trials.
