23 years of public policy towards universal health coverage in Mexico. A cross-sectional time-series analysis using routinely collected health data, 2000–2022
Authors: Mariana Álvarez-Aceves, Lina Sofía Palacio-Mejía, Mauricio Hernández-Ávila, Edgar Leonel González-González, Carlos Arturo Castro-Del Ángel, Leslie Guzmán-Sandoval, Juan Eugenio Hernández-Ávila
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMexico's health system has undergone multiple reforms over the past two decades to expand coverage. This study examines their association with public health expenditure, infrastructure, human resources, hospital admissions and premature mortality, providing evidence to guide investment strategies and health policy.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional time-series analysis using routinely collected data (2000–2022). The primary outcome was premature mortality; secondary outcomes included public health expenditure, number of hospital beds, consulting rooms, physicians, nurses, and hospital admissions. Trends were assessed with marginal models using linear splines aligned with governmental transitions, comparing populations with (PWSS) and without (PLSS) social security coverage.FindingsPublic health expenditure increased from 2000 to 2012, with steeper growth for PLSS, but plateaued for both groups during 2012–2018. Hospital bed gap narrowed between 2003 and 2006; however, the ratio in Ministry of Health hospitals serving PLSS (5.84 per 10,000) remained 20.1% lower than in social security institutions serving PWSS (7.31). Physician availability improved for PLSS, yet disparities persisted. Hospital admissions in Ministry of Health hospitals increased from 2004 to 2012, then declined by −5.73% annually after 2018; admissions in social security institutions decreased by −2.4% annually during 2012–2018. Among PLSS, preventable and treatable mortality rose after 2012 (+3.26% and +5.54% annually, after 2018), while both indicators declined steadily among PWSS.InterpretationDespite major reforms with significant public expenditure increase, inequities between PLSS and PWSS, fragmentation, accessibility and quality challenges persist. Addressing the root causes of health inequities in Mexico will require not only health system reforms but also tackling structural vulnerabilities linked to widespread informal employment, which limits access to health and social protection.FundingNational Institute of Public Health, Ministry of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation of Mexico.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a literature search in PubMed during the first quarter of 2024, using the keywords “universal health coverage,” “health system,” “Mexico,” “Seguro Popular,” “health reform,” and “performance evaluation.” We included papers published before January 2024, without language restriction. This search provided the basis for our analysis of the evidence. Mexico's health system has experienced significant transformations, driven by reforms aimed at achieving universal coverage. Evaluating these reforms has often focused on limited indicators, such as healthcare access and quality, with their impact on health outcomes minimally explored.

Some studies highlight improvements in healthcare access, particularly during the implementation of Seguro Popular, reporting reduced out-of-pocket expenditures. However, these findings have been met with controversy. Concerns have been raised about the cost-effectiveness and equity impacts of Seguro Popular. Additionally, some authors argue that government health expenditure increased significantly, and others claim that the system's efficiency was negatively affected.

A World Bank report indicates that life expectancy in Mexico stalled around 2000, unlike in other countries, such as Colombia, Chile, and Argentina, where it continues to grow. Avoidable mortality in Mexico remains higher than in these neighboring countries.

Overall, despite narrow pieces of evidence indicating progress, a comprehensive evaluation of Mexico's health reforms, particularly their effect on health outcomes, is missing, pointing out the need for more broad assessments.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this study is the first comprehensive nationwide analysis of Mexico's health system reforms from 2000 to 2022 (covering 3.6 administrations) and its association to population's health changes employing a logical framework. We analyzed the trends on government health expenditure (input), number of hospital beds, consulting rooms, physicians and nurses (processes); hospital admissions (outcomes), and premature mortality (impact), using national administrative health records and health accounts, comparing population with and without social security. Our results provide critical insights into the implementation of public health policies and how their evolution and shifts in the political landscape affect the path to achieving universal health coverage.

Implications of all the available evidence

Despite notable improvements in government health expenditure, infrastructure, and service availability for the uninsured population in Mexico, these advances did not translate into sustained reductions in mortality amenable to health care. Preventable and treatable mortality even increased after 2012 among those lacking social security, while populations with social security experienced consistent declines. This divergence highlights persistent inequities and underscores that universal health coverage remains an unachieved goal. To achieve lasting gains, reforms must go beyond expanding access and prioritize the quality and continuity of care. Evidence from the past two decades, as well as from other Latin American countries, suggests that reducing system fragmentation, ensuring stable financing, and strengthening governance are critical for success. Ultimately, addressing the root causes of health inequities in Mexico will require not only health system reforms but also tackling structural vulnerabilities linked to widespread informal employment, which limits access to social protections and entrenches disadvantage.

Introduction

Since 1983, constitutional reforms have recognized the right to health as a fundamental principle within Mexico's legal framework. However, fragmentation among public health providers and limited public investment have hindered significant progress toward achieving equitable access to available health resources.1 Despite numerous national health policy reforms, efforts to restructure the healthcare system into a more integrated and efficient framework have fallen short. Universal health coverage remains unattained, and health disparities persist, emphasizing the need for urgent strategic investment and policy innovation.

Healthcare coverage eligibility is legally linked to employment status, varying based on whether the employment is in the public or private, formal or informal economic sectors.2 For individuals employed in the formal economy, whether from the government or private sector, health access is provided through a fragmented structure managed by four federally operated public monopolies created between 1940 and 1960 (Table 1). Regardless of the specific institution providing healthcare services, this population is collectively referred to as “population with social security (PWSS)” in Mexico, as all beneficiaries are entitled to a set of social benefits, despite differences in service provision among institutions. The informal sector typically includes individuals without formal contracts, those in temporary or unstable jobs, self-employed workers without business registration, and unpaid family workers; such Health reforms with the aim to decentralize health services (1983–1999) resulted in the establishment of 32 state health service administrations supervised by the Ministry of Health/State Health Services (MoH/SHS) providing services to the PLSS.3

Evaluations of these reforms concluded that they failed to improve efficiency, instead worsening health inequities between states and undermining care quality.5,6 State systems remained inefficient and low in productivity.7 Out-of-pocket expenditure continued to rise. In 2003, a major step toward universal health coverage began with the creation of the System for Social Protection in Health and its package of free healthcare services under Seguro Popular. The program aimed to strengthen the 32 State Health Services and was expanded by successive administrations from 2006 to 2018. Its primary goal was to increase state resources to cover unemployed or informal workers and their families—over 50 million Mexicans lacking health services.

Early evaluations of Seguro Popular indicated positive outcomes, such as increased use of hospitalization services, expanded access to primary care, and greater use of childbirth services in public hospitals.8,9 Reports also noted an 11% reduction in infant mortality between 2004 and 201910 and a 23% decrease in catastrophic health expenditures.11 However, these achievements contrast with national trends, as mortality from chronic diseases has risen, life expectancy has stagnated over the past 24 years, and government health expenditure as a percentage of Gross Domestic Product has remained comparatively low and unchanged relative to other Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries.

A more in-depth description of the Mexican health system valid until 2018 is available at the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development Reviews of Health Systems: Mexico 2016,2 and the Mexican Health System of the Supplementary Material.

During the 2018–2024 administration, major reforms dismantled the System for Social Protection in Health, centralized drug and supply procurement, and recentralized State Health Services. A new institution, the Institute of Health for Well-being (INSABI), and later IMSS-Bienestar, was created to serve the PLSS (Table 1). The reform sought to unify medical care under the Mexican Social Security Institute (IMSS), the country's main social security provider, but only 23 states signed federalization agreements, leaving nine with their own State Health Services.

Thus far, comprehensive evaluations of the system-wide changes over time resulting from these reforms on the health of the population have not been conducted. We propose a thorough analysis using a logical framework to examine inputs, processes, outcomes, and impacts in Mexico from 2000 to 2022, with the aim to examine the association of healthcare expenditure with infrastructure (hospital beds and consulting rooms) and human resources (number of physicians and nurses), hospital admissions, and premature mortality as the main outcome.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study examines 23 years of public policy in Mexico with the aim of assessing premature mortality as the main outcome, and the trends in government expenditure, healthcare infrastructure and human resources, as well as hospital admissions as secondary outcomes to explore the evolution of disparities between PLSS and PWSS.

This study is based on a national-level analysis covering the entire Mexican population, classified according to access to social security, and including all hospital admissions registered by public healthcare institutions serving PLSS and PWSS, as well as all vital events occurring in Mexico each year, using administrative data. As such, no sampling procedure was applied. All inferences are closed to these population groups and analysis period confidence intervals are provided as a measure of the precision of the estimates, not as a tool for statistical inference.

Administrative health data used in this study are collected following standardized procedures (NOM-035-SSA3-2012),12 and are subsequently consolidated and validated by the General Directorate of Health Information, ensuring consistency and comparability across healthcare subsystems. Population

The population base was estimated using data from the 2000, 2010, and 2020 national censuses published by the National Institute for Statistics and Geography,13 and interpolating for non-census years. Based on these data, we classified the Mexican population into two groups according to reported access to social security and health services. Individuals were coded as having social security if they reported affiliation with any of the formal-sector institutions listed in Table 1 (PWSS).

PLSS included individuals who self-reported not having access to social security and those who reported no health service affiliation or didn't know if they were affiliated. A more detailed description of both groups, as well as the procedures to estimate population in non-census years is available in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figure S1.

Government health expenditure

To assess changes in total and per capita government health expenditure after public policy reforms or changes in government, we used data from the National Health Accounts (Table 2).

Infrastructure and resources

We analyzed data on healthcare infrastructure (hospital beds and consulting rooms) and human resources (physicians and nurses) published by the General Directorate of Health Information.15 The datasets contain detailed information regarding MoH/SHS facilities (hospitals and clinics) and resources for 2000–2022. We also included a statistical report, published by the IMSS in 2023, containing data on facilities (hospitals and clinics) and resources for 2000–202216 and the statistical yearbooks of the Mexican Institute of Social Security and Services for State Workers (ISSSTE).17 We were unable to obtain yearly information on resources for the rest of the social security institutions, but IMSS and ISSSTE cover 98% of PWSS, as shown in Table 1. Adding information from MoH/SHS, we cover almost the entire Mexican population. Ratios of hospital beds, consulting rooms, physicians, and nurses were reported per 10,000 population.

Hospital admissions data

We used different patient-level data sources described in Table 2. We totalized the annual number of hospital admissions by institution and compared trends in total admissions and rate per 100,000 people between PLSS and PWSS. Using information on patient residence from the Automated System for Hospital Discharges, we analyzed the variation in the rate of hospital admissions by municipality (2004–2019). To investigate if changes were associated with the socioeconomic characteristics of the municipality, we classified them by marginalization index,14 a complex measure that classifies municipalities according to social exclusion level.

A more detailed description of hospital data and the procedures to calculate hospital admissions rates as well as variations by marginalization index are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Mortality

The mortality analysis, based on the National Death Certificate Database (Table 2) focused on comparing causes amenable to the health system by social security status. This indicator measures deaths from specific causes occurring before age 75 that could potentially be avoided through prevention policies (preventable mortality) and/or timely and effective health care treatment (treatable mortality). It serves as an indicator of health system quality within a country and is useful for tracking improvements in the health system over time. Not all causes of death are considered amenable to health care; thus, only a subset of overall mortality is classified as preventable or treatable according to this method. A decline in these types of mortality is interpreted as a positive outcome, reflecting improvements in the effectiveness of prevention strategies and health care delivery. We used a list of causes adapted from the Canadian Institute of Health Information21 that includes 80 groups of causes of death, based on ICD-10 codes, considered amenable to the health system.

We also analyzed preventable mortality excluding injuries, according to the ICD-10 codes corresponding to Group III of the WHO Global Health Estimates 2000–2019.22

Using the census data described, we standardized mortality (preventable and treatable) by age group, sex, and access to social security status using the direct method and world standard population.23 Treatable and preventable mortality rates were reported by 100,000 people. Further information on mortality classification for this study is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical models

We analyzed yearly trends using time-series cross-sectional marginal models with panel-corrected standard errors and Prais–Winsten estimators,24, 25, 26 which are conditional to the estimates of the temporal autocorrelation parameters. This kind of statistical modeling has proved useful in the study of policy implementation or changes over time.24 We incorporated linear spline terms in our models, with knots (points of inflection) placed, a priori, at the years 2006, 2012, and 2018, corresponding to changes in government to account for 3.6 presidential periods. This specification was chosen to capture potential shifts in trends aligned with political and institutional transitions.

Results

Government health expenditures

From 2000 to 2012, total expenditure and per capita health expenditure demonstrated substantial growth. After 2012, this growth stagnated.

In 2000, overall per capita health expenditure, expressed in U.S. constant dollars (base year 2013 = 100%; $0.0784 USD per 1 Mexican peso), averaged $191.42. This increased rapidly, reaching $343.75 by 2012, followed by a plateau to reach $364.28 by 2022. Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1 summarize the absolute figures and trends of government health expenditure by target population (total, PWSS, and PLSS). In 2000, per capita health expenditure averaged $121.78 for PLSS and $263.87 for PWSS, representing a 116% difference between these two populations.Fig. 1Government expenditure in health, Mexico 2000–2022. Per capita health expenditure increased more rapidly for PLSS than for PWSS between 2000 and 2012, averaging 8.98% annually (95% CI: 7.17–10.81) from 2000 to 2006 and 7.07% (95% CI: 5.46–8.70) from 2006 to 2012 (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, the gap in government health expenditure between PLSS and PWSS narrowed. Per capita health expenditure rose from $121.85 to $312.01 for PLSS and from $264.02 to $426.96 for PWSS. Between 2012 and 2018, per capita health expenditure stalled for both groups but showed a slight increase from 2019 to 2022. By 2022, the inequity in per capita health expenditure had narrowed compared to 2000. Nevertheless, a residual disparity of 37.38% in favor of PWSS persisted at the end of the study period.

Infrastructure and resources

In 2003, MoH/SHS-managed hospitals had 31,549 beds, 12% lower than the 35,859 beds in social security facilities. By 2006, this difference had narrowed to approximately 5% (34,005 for MoH/SHS vs. 35,714) and remained small throughout the study period. However, the gap in the bed ratio per 10,000 population between MoH/SHS and social security institutions showed relatively little change, with MoH/SHS consistently lagging by 23.8% in 2003 (5.62 vs. 7.38 per 10,000), 17.3% in 2010 (6.0 vs. 7.25), and 23.7% in 2022 (5.76 vs. 7.55). The most notable growth in MoH/SHS bed availability occurred between 2006 and 2012, with an annual increase of 0.72% (95% CI: 0.35–1.08), but it is not different from the growth rate of the social security institutions and declined in subsequent years (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2).Fig. 2Infrastructure and human resources for health, Mexico 2003–2022. Infrastructure and human resources for health, Mexico 2003–2022. MoH/SHS = Ministry of Health/State Health Services; SSI = Social security institutions; CI = Confidence interval.

Although no significant increase was observed in the bed ratio per 10,000 inhabitants, the number of hospitals for PLSS increased from 422 to 800 (89%) between 2001 and 2022, while for PWSS it increased from 257 to 285 (10.89%). MoH/SHS hospitals are generally small and have fewer beds; however, this increase may indicate an important improvement in geographic accessibility by enhancing the distribution of these beds across the territory.

Throughout the entire period, the ratio of consulting rooms per 10,000 for PWSS remained consistently lower compared to PLSS. The 2006–2012 ratio for PLSS and PWSS exhibited an annual growth rate of 3.51% (95% CI: 2.98–4.05) and 2.3% (95% CI: 1.90–2.73), respectively. The ratio for PWSS declined in growth rate thereafter, while the ratio for PLSS continued to grow between 2012 and 2018, although at a slower annual rate of 0.91% (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2).

The ratio of physicians per 10,000 people was lower for PLSS compared to PWSS throughout the entire period. However, greater annual growth rates in 2006–2012, 4.28% (95% CI: 3.81–4.74); and 2012–2018, 1.86% (95% CI: 1.42–2.30) for PLSS reduced the gap. Regarding the ratio of nurses per 10,000 people, a similar pattern was observed; the ratio for PLSS surpassed that for PWSS in 2020. This was because the growth rate remained significantly higher for PLSS than PWSS during all the periods, with the ratio of nurses per 10,000 for PWSS decreasing in 2018–2022 (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2).

Hospital admissions

Regarding hospital admissions, health service providers had significant differences. In hospitals serving PLSS, a sustained increase is observed from 2000 to 2015, followed by a decline up to 2019. In 2020, hospital admissions in the public sector reduced significantly. The IMSS recovered in 2021 to pre-pandemic levels, but the MoH/SHS did not. In the private sector, admissions remained constant, with a slight increase starting in 2016 and a sharp rise during 2021 and 2022, probably reflecting the COVID-19 pandemic effect (Fig. 3). Regarding preventable and treatable mortality rates per 100,000 population, significant differences were observed. First, injuries had a substantial contribution on both groups, but this was higher in PLSS. For preventable mortality, a very different trend appears. In 2000, the difference between the groups was not significant; however, PWSS had a notable and progressive decline in both preventable and treatable mortality rates from 2000 to 2022. Discussion

This study empirically examines 23 years of public policy across four government administrations and three ruling parties in Mexico, aiming to achieve universal health coverage and promote health equity between PWSS and PLSS. To assess the association of health reforms with health outcomes, we used cross-sectional time-series datasets to analyze (a) government health expenditure as a system input, (b) number of beds, consulting rooms, physicians and nurses as process indicators, (c) hospital admissions as a secondary outcome, and (d) premature mortality as primary outcome indicator. Our analysis draws on census data, national administrative health records, vital statistics, and health accounts, comparing trends and disparities between PWSS and PLSS, and the institutions that serve them, from 2000 to 2022.

According to National Health Accounts,15 government spending as a share of Gross Domestic Product for PLSS rose steadily from 0.84% in 2003 to 1.42% in 2009, narrowing the gap with PWSS (1.67% in 2009) but averaging 0.25 points lower across the period (15%). This trend suggests that Seguro Popular boosted PLSS spending without reducing expenditure for the insured. In Mexico, health expenditure is the second-largest public disburse, yet its Gross Domestic Product share (3.2%) remains low compared to Argentina (>5%), Costa Rica, and Colombia (>4%).29 Healthcare access in Mexico still depends heavily on employment and out-of-pocket payments,30 which declined slightly during the study period but require further reduction to advance universal health coverage.

Differences in government per capita health expenditure growth (Fig. 1) suggests that Seguro Popular had a greater financial effect on the uninsured, reflecting a partially progressive allocation of resources. Infrastructure and resource availability also improved for this group (Fig. 2). Since 2003, the absolute number of hospital beds increased, narrowing the gap between social security institutions and MoH/SHS, but the bed-to-population ratio remained constant, indicating growth only kept pace with population growth. Consulting rooms were considerably more numerous in MoH/SHS, distributed across small urban, semiurban, and rural clinics, responding to PLSS dispersion. Between 2006 and 2012, MoH/SHS showed larger absolute growth in consulting rooms than social security institutions, which was also reflected in the consulting room-to-population ratio.

The physician and nurse population ratio gap (2003–2012) narrowed, especially for nurses. After the 2012 ruling party change, indicators stagnated until 2018. From 2018 to 2022, government health spending rose for both groups, driven mainly by COVID-19 response.

Rising per capita health expenditure expanded healthcare resources—personnel, facilities, beds, and equipment—leading to greater hospital utilization. Admissions rose steadily until 2015, two years past the changes in ruling party, after which they stagnated and began to decline in 2017. This shift was not associated with overall hospitalizations in social security institutions, but there was an increase in private-sector hospitalizations. According to our data, 25% of hospital admissions occurred in private facilities, with a peak reaching 34% in 2021, likely due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Unfortunately, in this database, patients’ social security affiliation status information is not available.

Despite these gains, preventable and treatable mortality did not improve in PLSS, which remained high and rose after 2012, coinciding with stagnant government spending during 2012–2018. On the other hand, in spite of expenditure stagnation, PWSS showed consistent declines in preventable mortality and slower but steady declines in treatable mortality, widening disparities between the two populations.

Several interconnected mechanisms may explain the observed trends in mortality among PLSS. Many individuals from high-marginality municipalities likely had accumulated health risks due to delayed care and socioeconomic disadvantage—an expression of the “inverse care law,” where those who need care the most receive it the least.31,32

The rollout of the System for Social Protection in Health reduced geographical disparities in access for PLSS by expanding hospitals and primary care clinics. Demand increased the most in municipalities with greater marginalization. This indicates that disadvantaged areas, many of them without hospital services before Seguro Popular, experienced the largest growth in service utilization—consistent with Seguro Popular objective and the concentration of PLSS in these areas, helping mitigate the “inverse care law.” However, given their history of limited healthcare, these populations may already have suffered significant deterioration before benefiting from the new services.

Structural inefficiencies—such as fragmented governance, poor resource allocation, and emerging health needs—likely affected service quality and constrained the system's ability to deliver equitable care.

The dependence of social security linked to employment in the formal sector has limited access to continuous, coordinated care, particularly when changes in employment disrupt affiliation. From a sociological perspective, institutional fragmentation and weak governance structures, such as the lack of use of a universal population ID number, constrain service delivery and accountability. In this context, the creation of parallel systems for those employed in the informal sector has institutionalized lower health entitlements for PLSS, reinforcing so far, their exclusion from comprehensive care and social benefits.33,34 Institutionalizing a unique health system that ensures quality services for the entire population, similar to the English and French models, could achieve universal coverage in Mexico and eliminate inequities. However, it is important to consider that these countries have low levels of informal employment (6.5% in the United Kingdom and 3.1% in France), while in Mexico, informality affects 55.5% of the labor force.35

Overall, the interaction between system-level inefficiencies, emerging health needs, and structural disadvantages help explain why improvements in expenditure alone have not produced health gains for PLSS. Future qualitative and mixed methods research could help clarify these mechanisms and support more targeted policy interventions.

A World Bank report shows that Mexico has suboptimal performance in international comparisons of age-standardized rate per 100,000 population for both preventable (435) and treatable (257) mortality. In contrast, comparable neighboring countries, such as Peru, Brazil, Colombia, Chile, and Costa Rica, reported rates of 405 to 148 preventable deaths per 100,000 and 132 to 76 per 100,000 treatable deaths. All these countries have consistently shown a downward trend in avoidable mortality rates for both men and women, which remain higher in Mexico.36 According to this report, life expectancy in Mexico stagnated around 2000, unlike in those countries, where it continued to rise.

According to the National Survey of Health and Nutrition 2012, private healthcare use in Mexico is widespread across all insurance groups. Among individuals with any type of public or private affiliation, 76.2% reported receiving outpatient care in private facilities. Among those formally affiliated with public social security institutions, the use of private services was substantial: 30.9% of IMSS affiliates, 28.4% of ISSSTE affiliates, and 31.1% of the dismantled Seguro Popular beneficiaries sought care in the private sector.37 These findings suggest that reliance on private services is not limited to affluent or formally employed populations, but may instead reflect persistent barriers to access, long wait times, or perceived lower quality in public services.

Countries such as Brazil, Colombia, Chile, and Turkey have pursued structural reforms that offer valuable lessons.38, 39, 40, 41 Colombia, Brazil, and Chile have restructured their health systems by separating governance, service provision, and financing to enhance efficiency and equity, making significant progress in reducing mortality amenable to the health system for their population.38, 39, 40 In Mexico, the fragmentation of the health system has limited the effectiveness of reforms seeking the separation of functions.

Brazil's Unified Health System is grounded in universality, primary care, and decentralization with citizen participation, achieving notable improvements in access and health outcomes.38 Colombia adopted a regulated competition model under Law 100, expanding coverage through health insurers, though equity and quality challenges persist.39 Chile advanced a mixed public-private model with explicit guarantees (GES) to improve access and accountability.39 Turkey executed a comprehensive reform that unified its health system, strengthened infrastructure, introducing performance-based incentives.41 These reforms succeeded to varying degrees, but common lessons emerge: the importance of reducing fragmentation, separation of functions, strengthening primary care, and ensuring long-term policy coherence and sustained implementation.

Mexico implemented Seguro Popular in 2003, aiming to strengthen its healthcare system. However, the initiative struggled with fragmented structures and ambiguous governance, hindering the clear separation between financing and service provision.42 In 2020, the Instituto de Salud para el Bienestar replaced Seguro Popular to address these shortcomings, yet challenges in coordination, quality and separation of functions persist within the current IMSS-Bienestar framework.

When compared to other nations, Mexico has made limited progress towards consolidating a unified healthcare system. This highlights the urgent need for a robust regulatory framework and governance that ensures a more effective coordination across separated functions.

Our data suggest that increasing government budget allocation to improve accessibility alone was not sufficient to address health inequities or improve the overall performance of the health system. Additional resources must be accompanied by structural reforms that strengthen governance, separation of functions, coordination, and the quality-of-service delivery. Monitoring the progress of health sector reforms—particularly in terms of health expenditure to improve quality of care —will be essential. For instance, given that a relatively small share of the spending is allocated to primary care and preventive services, prioritizing funding in these areas is crucial, especially for PLSS.

We acknowledge some limitations in our study. It relies on routinely collected health data originally gathered for administrative purposes. There may be classification biases and inaccuracies in the recording of key variables, potentially affecting the validity of some estimates. Additionally, difficulties in the integration of different health information systems and heterogeneity in applying data collection standards may limit comparability between populations with and without social security coverage. To contextualize the reliability of our data, we compared our estimates on public-sector healthcare resources—such as the number of physicians—with figures from other sources, including the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019,43 Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development Health Statistics,44 and the WHO Global Health Observatory (2021).45 These sources report total national figures, covering both public and private sectors. After adjusting for the estimated number of private-sector physicians, our estimates are consistent with those reported internationally. This comparison supports the validity of the administrative data used in our analysis and underscores the relevance of disaggregating by health system sector when interpreting cross-national indicators. We would have liked to analyze data prior year 2000, but no information on access to social security was recorded in censuses before that year, so denominators were not available.

In addition to data quality concerns, we acknowledge the possibility of model misspecification bias. Although we evaluated alternative spline structures and assessed key assumptions such as residual normality and autocorrelation, our models may still be affected by omitted variables, unmeasured confounding, or functional form misspecification. Another limitation of our study is the utilization of the reverse transformation of the coefficients to facilitate interpretation of the results. Given that log-transformed coefficients obtained from the models lack an intuitive interpretation, we used the back transformation (exponentiation) of the coefficients to produce a final interpretation based on the original scale of the variables. Nevertheless, this process often introduces bias due to the nonlinearity of the exponential function. When comparing the back-transformed estimates with the observed values, we did not observe a pattern of bias that would compromise the validity of the results. Moreover, since our analysis is based on complete population-level administrative data rather than a sample, the potential impact of such transformation bias is low, as no generalization beyond the study population is intended. These limitations highlight the need for cautious interpretation of the estimated trends and point to the value of complementary qualitative and mixed methods approaches in future research.

Gains in health related to changes in public health policies are not immediate, especially in mortality indicators, such as those analyzed here. It may take more than two decades to show. Nevertheless, preventable mortality in PWSS showed a slight decrease during the period 2006–2012, but the lack of growth in public expenditure in health may have hindered the benefit of Seguro Popular and preventable mortality began to rise again. The federalization initiative, launched by the federal government in 2022 has fallen short as only 23 states signed the federalization agreement of the newly created IMSS-Bienestar. We are now at the same starting point as we were in 1987 with some states receiving federalized health services while others are still decentralized. Some studies suggest that decentralization worsened inequities among states and health service subsystems, so perhaps recentralization is the way to go, as Turkey did.

Public policies must address not only access to services but also the quality, continuity, and timeliness of care to achieve universal health coverage. To inform actionable policy strategies, we propose assessing healthcare system performance at the micro regional level, focusing on medical units and their capacity to deliver effective primary care, especially for chronic conditions. As part of this effort, we are developing a monitoring platform that integrates health data at the medical unit level, available at our institutional website. This tool aims to support evidence-based interventions to reduce avoidable hospitalizations and premature mortality.46

Without a long-term vision for public health reforms focused on quality of care, Mexico will be unable to capitalize and consolidate health advances that lead to substantial improvements in health outcomes. We urge health authorities in Mexico to learn from the past 23 years and the experience from countries in the region to plan for long-term, long-vision public health policies. Separating governance, finance, and service provision enhances efficiency, transparency, and quality through improved health system monitoring, ensuring better health for Mexico's population in the coming years. Moving forward, it is essential to avoid abrupt policy shifts and ensure that reforms are based on evidence, stability, and long-term sustainability, reducing the fragmentation of the health system.

Ultimately, the root of health inequalities in Mexico lies not within the health system itself, but in the broader economic structure—where approximately half of the workforce is employed in the informal sector without formal contracts, social protections, or access to healthcare. Meaningful progress toward health equity and the reduction of its social determinants will require confronting this fundamental structural imbalance.
