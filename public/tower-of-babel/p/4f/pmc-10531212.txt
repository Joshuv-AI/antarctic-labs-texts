Management and outcomes after liver transplantation for progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Anna Maria Kavallar, Christoph Mayerhofer, Denise Aldrian, Tatsuya Okamoto, Thomas Müller, Georg Friedrich Vogel
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) is a heterogeneous rare congenital cholestatic liver disease. Disease progression might necessitate liver transplantation (LT). The aim of this study was to describe the outcome of PFIC1-4 patients after LT.Methods:Electronic databases were searched to identify studies on PFIC and LT. Patients were categorized according to PFIC type, genotype, graft type, age at LT, time of follow-up, and complications and treatment during follow-up.Results:Seventy-nine studies with 507 patients met inclusion criteria; most patients were classified as PFIC1-3. The median age at LT was 50 months. The overall 5-year patient survival was 98.5%. PFIC1 patients with diarrhea after LT were at significant risk of developing graft steatosis (p < 0.0001). Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 100% [95% CI: 73.9%–100%] for surgical biliary diversion to ameliorate steatosis and 94.9% [95% CI: 53.7%–100%] to improve diarrhea (n = 8). PFIC2 patients with bile salt export pump (BSEP)2 or BSEP3-genotype were at significant risk of developing antibody-induced BSEP deficiency (AIBD) (p < 0.0001), which was reported in 16.2% of patients at a median of 36.5 months after LT. Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 81.1% [95% CI: 47.5%–100%] for rituximab-based treatment regimens to improve AIBD (n = 18). HCC was detected in 3.6% of PFIC2 and 13.8% of PFIC4 patients at LT.Conclusions:Fifty percent of PFIC1 patients develop diarrhea and steatosis after LT. Biliary diversion can protect the graft from injury. PFIC2 patients with BSEP2 and BSEP3 genotypes are at significant risk of developing AIBD, and rituximab-based treatment regimens effectively improve AIBD. PFIC3 patients have no PFIC-specific complications following LT.

INTRODUCTION

Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) describes a spectrum of heterogeneous rare autosomal recessive cholestatic liver diseases with onset from infancy to childhood.1 To date, several subtypes of PFIC are recognized.1 Mutations in ATPase phospholipid transporting 8B1 cause FIC1 deficiency/PFIC1. Deficiency in the bile canalicular bile salt export pump (BSEP)/PFIC2 results from mutations in ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 11. Gene defects in multidrug-resistance protein 3 (MDR3)/PFIC3 originate from mutations in ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 4; PFIC4 is caused by mutations in the tight junction protein 2 gene, which encodes for the tight junction protein 2 (zonula occludens 2). PFIC1-4 is the most common type. Further subtypes comprise mutations in the nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H results in Farnesoid X receptor deficiency and mutations in MYO5B results in Myosin Vb deficiency.2–7 Among these, recently, several new genes have also been associated with PFIC: ubiquitin-specific peptidase 53, lipolysis stimulated lipoprotein receptor, and WD repeat domain 83 opposite strand gene.8

Despite the differences in pathophysiology, which are excellently summarized here,1,9 all types of PFIC result in cholestasis and cholestatic pruritus. Clinically, PFIC can be classified in low gamma-glutamyl-transferase (GGT) (eg, PFIC1,2,4) and high GGT (PFIC3) cholestasis. Often, clinical discrimination is difficult, and genetic testing for underlying variants is recommended.

Therapeutic approaches aim at alleviating cholestasis and the accompanying pruritus symptoms. Ursodeoxycholic acid and rifampicin are commonly used. Ursodeoxycholic acid seems to be particularly effective in MDR3 deficiency.10 Reducing the systemic bile acid load by surgical biliary diversion of the bile acid flow, to either the abdominal wall or the colon, aims to bypass the active reabsorption of bile acid in the terminal ileum by the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter/intestinal bile acid transporter (IBAT). This approach can improve native liver survival.11–13 A similar effect can be achieved with the recent therapeutic option of pharmacologic IBAT inhibition (IBATi).14,15

Although therapeutic approaches help to alleviate cholestasis and the accompanying pruritus symptoms, end-stage liver disease (ESLD), intractable pruritus, or the development of HCC may necessitate liver transplantation (LT).12,13

After LT, subtype-specific complications are reported, which may lead to graft failure and result in retransplantation (re-LT). In PFIC1 patients, the onset or aggravation of diarrhea and the occurrence of graft steatosis is known.16,17 Steatosis can progress with inflammation to graft fibrosis and loss and might even recur after re-LT. Yet, the pathophysiology is not understood. Patients with BSEP deficiency might develop autoantibodies against BSEP [antibody-induced BSEP deficiency (AIBD)] and suffer from disease recurrence.18 AIBD might also progress to graft loss and necessitate re-LT.

While the natural history of PFIC1 and PFIC2 is increasingly well understood,1,12,13 the outcome after LT for all types of PFIC has not been systematically addressed so far. This study aims to describe LT and the outcome after LT for the 4 most common types of PFIC, including event-free graft survival and the identification of specific risks, and evaluate potential therapeutic options reported.

METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines.19 All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul and was approved by the appropriate institutional review committee of the Medical University of Innsbruck (Nr. 1405/2022).

Search strategy and data extraction

For study selection, systematic literature research was performed using the following bibliographic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search terms “PFIC1,” “PFIC2,” “PFIC3,” “PFIC4,” “FIC1,” “ATPase phospholipid transporting 8B1,” “BSEP,” “ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 11,” “MDR3,” “ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 4,” “zonula occludens 2,” and “tight junction protein 2,” together with “liver transplantation” or “transplantation” were used. Conference proceedings from major annual meetings (European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition; North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology & Nutrition) and trial registries from clinicaltrials.org were also included. The date of the last search was March 2023. Only English articles were considered for further analysis. Less frequent or well-established PFIC subtypes (eg, nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H, myosin Vb (MYO5B), kinesin family member 12 (KIF12), lipolysis stimulated lipoprotein receptor, ubiquitin-specific peptidase 53, and WD repeat domain 83 opposite strand gene) were not included in this study.

Two investigators (Anna Maria Kavallar and Georg Friedrich Vogel) independently performed screening of the mentioned databases through abstract and title search. All papers were categorized, and original studies were extracted. If an article was considered eligible by either of the 2 reviewers, the full text was retrieved where available.

After categorization, a second search was performed on full-text articles to check for methodical quality and fulfillment of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The two investigators (Anna Maria Kavallar and Georg Friedrich Vogel) extracted the data elements from all preselected studies into an Excel spreadsheet. These data included authorship, year of publication, type of study, sex, anthropometrics, age of onset, type of symptoms, genotype, type and age at SBD, age at LT, type of LT, follow-up age, complications during follow-up (steatosis, diarrhea, AIBD, interventions, rejection, and retransplantation), and type of immunosuppressive regimen. In the case of missing follow-up data, age at LT plus 1 month was assumed. In cases of incomplete data, we tried to contact the authors for further study information. Data were controlled, cross-checked, and confirmed by both the reviewers (Anna Maria Kavallar and Georg Friedrich Vogel). Based on tabulated data, studies were again assessed for inclusion eligibility and data consistency by both reviewers. Missing data on a substantial level led to study exclusion. In the case of any disagreement, the issue was solved by discussion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The prerequisite for study inclusion was a diagnosis of PFIC1-4 based on clinical and/or genetic criteria and having received liver transplantation. Observational studies, case series, and case reports were included. Reviews and commentaries were excluded. Publications by the Natural Course and Prognosis of PFIC and Effect of Biliary Diversion (NAPPED) consortium, which comprise large, partly published cohorts, were excluded to avoid duplicate data.12,13,20

Study quality and bias assessment

Assessment of quality for the included studies was performed with the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools for cohort studies, case series, and case reports.21 Additionally, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist for conference abstracts was used if no full-text article was retrievable.22 For abstracts of case studies, certain items of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist were considered not applicable. Studies were considered with low, moderate, or serious risk of bias if they fulfilled more than 80%, between 60 and 80%, or less than 60% of the requested items.

Outcome measures

Baseline data including pre-LT measures (eg, age, sex, age at diagnosis, genotype, SBD, and extrahepatic manifestations) and LT-specific data (eg, indication, age at LT, type of graft, and SBD at LT) for PFIC subtypes were collected and reported. Post-LT outcome measures were overall survival, graft survival, and event-free graft survival (eg, occurrence of subtype-specific complications: diarrhea and/or steatosis for PFIC1, AIBD for PFIC2, and rejection). Associations with parameters (genotype, graft type, age at LT (younger or older than 5 y), year of LT (before or after 2010), preexisting diarrhea, graft rejection, steroid treatment, and immunosuppressive regimen) on event-free graft survival were assessed. The cutoff to separate 2 groups with respect to the year of LT was set to 2010 as it separates the cohort into equal groups. Therapeutic options were collected, and the random effects’ model was used to calculate pooled efficiency by meta-analysis. Results were visualized using a forest plot.

Genotype categorization

Genotype categorization was done in accordance with the NAPPED consortium publications for ATPase phospholipid transporting 8B1/FIC113 and ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 11/BSEP.12 The categorization for ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 4/MDR3 was defined as follows: MDR3-1 (no protein-truncating mutation), MDR3-2 (1 protein-truncating mutation), and MDR3-3 (2 protein-truncating mutations).

Data synthesis, harmonization, and statistical analysis

Overall patient and graft survival was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier estimator. The effect of parameters on event-free survival was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier estimator. Time to event was stated as medians with IQR. The year of transplantation, in the case of inaccurate information, was estimated by subtraction of the age of the last follow-up minus the age at LT from the year of publication. Correlation analysis of genotype and AIBD occurrence was done using Cramer’s V. All statistical analyses were performed using ggplot, meta, metaphor, rcompanion (Cramer’s V), survival, and survminer packages in R statistical software (version 4.1.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For Kaplan-Meier estimator analyses, the Cox log-rank test was used. A significance level of 0.05 was applied.

RESULTS

Study cohort

The study cohort comprises 507 patients from 79 studies (Figure 1): 23.9% PFIC1, 49.7% PFIC2, 20.7% PFIC3, and 5.7% PFIC4 (see Supplemental Tables S1-4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545 for the full list of included studies).

Overall, 38 case reports, 23 case series, 9 cohort studies, and 9 abstracts were included in the final analysis. We identified 29 studies with low, 32 with moderate, and 18 with serious risk of bias (Supplemental Table 5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545). In the entire cohort, 27.8% were female [141/507, 197 not available (NA)], genetic confirmation was available in 54.8% (278/507) of patients, median age of onset was 1.75 months (interquartile range (IQR) 11.25), median age at LT was 50 months (IQR 40) (Figure 2A, B), median follow-up was 80 months (IQR 65.5), overall 5-year patient survival was 98.5% (Figure 2C), and 5-year graft survival was 95.9% (Figure 2D). PFIC1

The systematic literature search identified 121 patients with PFIC1 from 34 studies (Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545). Of these, 38% (46/121, 30 NA) were female. The median follow-up was 113 months (IQR 112). Genetic diagnosis was available in 44.6% of patients (54/121). Categorization of the genotypes according to the NAPPED-categorization13 grouped 35.2% (19/54) of patients to FIC1-A, 27.8% to FIC1-B (15/54), and 38.9% (21/54) to FIC1-C. The median age of symptom onset was 2 months (IQR 2) (55/121, 67 NA) and 39.7% (48/121, 68 NA). In 28.9% (35/121, 83 NA), extraintestinal manifestations were reported: among them, 10 patients presented with diarrhea, 3 patients with pancreatitis, and 2 with hearing loss. In 24.8% of all patients (30/121), SBD was performed before liver transplantation: 93.3% (28/30) underwent partial external biliary diversion and partial internal diversion, and total internal diversion was carried out in 1 patient each. The median age of SBD was 29 months (IQR 0). HCC did not occur in any of the patients, neither before transplantation nor was it detected in the explanted liver. The median age at LT was 54 months (IQR 27) (Figure 2A, B). Living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) was reported in 28.9% (35/121, 51 NA) of patients and deceased donor (DD) LT in 28.9%. Post-LT 5-year survival was 98.1% (Figure 2C), and 5-year graft survival was 94.7% (Figure 2D). ESLD necessitated LT in 52.9% (64/121, 17 NA), which was accompanied by severe pruritus in 18 patients (17.6%). SBD was performed during LT in 5 patients. Biopsy reports after LT were available in 12.4% of patients (15/121) with a median fibrosis of 66% (IQR 30). Ten PFIC1 patients required re-LT (Figure 1D): in 3 patients due to steatosis and 1 patient due to graft rejection. Diarrhea was reported in 49.6% (60/121, 38 NA) at a median of 1.5 (IQR 2.75) months after LT. Allograft steatosis occurred in 50.4% (61/121, 34 NA) at a median of 4 months (IQR 8.5) after LT. Patients presenting with diarrhea after LT were at significant risk of developing graft steatosis (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3A, Table 2). Other parameters (eg, graft type, steroid administration, age at LT, or year of LT) did not impact graft steatosis risk (Table 2). SBD after LT to treat diarrhea and/or steatosis was performed in 6.6% (n = 8/121) patients at a median of 38 months (IQR 23.5) after LT: Total internal diversion in 4 patients and total external biliary diversion in 4 patients. Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 100% [95% CI: 73.9%–100%] for SBD to ameliorate graft steatosis and an overall efficacy of 94.9% [95% CI: 53.7%–100%] for SBD to improve diarrhea (Figure 3B, C). Only limited information on extrahepatic complications’ manifestations after LT was available (24/121): sensorineural hearing loss was to be reported in 2 patients (8.3%), recurrent pancreatitis in 3 patients (12.5%), pancreatic insufficiency in 8 patients (33.3%), and poor growth in 11 patients (45.8%).

PFIC 2

The systematic literature search identified 252 children with PFIC2 from 36 studies (Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545), with 23% being female (58/252, 129 NA). The median follow-up was 80 months (IQR 76). Patients presented with first symptoms at an average age of 2 months (IQR 6). Genetic information was available for 55.2% of patients (139/252). Categorization of the genotypes according to the NAPPED-categorization12 grouped 25.2% (35/139) of patients to BSEP1, 44.6% (62/139) to BSEP2, and 38.1% (53/139) to BSEP3. SBD was reported in 12.6% of patients (32/253, 162 NA) before LT at a median age of 29 months (IQR 45). LT was performed at a median age of 37 months (IQR 27). ESLD was the major indication for LT with 44.7% (113/252, 129 NA). In 4.2% (9/252, 129 NA), pruritus was reported to be the cause of LT. HCC was reported in 3.6% of patients (12/252) at the time of LT. LDLT was performed in 13.9% of patients (35/252, 182 NA), of whom 19 patients received a living-related donor graft. In 13.9% (35/252, 182 NA), DD LT was performed. Post-LT 5-year survival was 99.1% (Figure 2C), and 5-year graft survival was 95.2% (Figure 2D). The cause of death (8/253) was recorded to be ESLD in 3 patients (37.5%), severe cholestasis and cardiac arrest in 2 patients (25%), gastrointestinal bleeding in 2 patients (25%), and surgical complications during LT in 1 patient (12.5%). AIBD, after LT, was reported in 16.3% (40/252, 26 NA) of patients at a median of 36.5 months (IQR 49.25) after LT. Symptoms of AIBD were pruritus (78%, 32/41), accompanied by jaundice in 14 patients (34.1%), low GGT cholestasis in 5 patients (12.2%), and diarrhea in 3 patients (7.3%). Potential trigger events of AIBD were reported in 21.9% (9/41) of patients: decrease of immunosuppressive treatment in 4 patients (9.7%), fluctuating cyclosporine through levels in 2 patients (4.9%), the switch from tacrolimus to cyclosporin and a high dose of antibiotics (cotrimoxazole, azithromycin, and amoxicillin) in 1 patient (2.5%), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-infection in 1 patient (2.5%). Patients with BSEP2 and BSEP3 were at significant risk of developing AIBD (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4A, Table 2). AIBD showed a stronger correlation with BSEP3 than with BSEP2 (Cramer’s V 0.7921 vs. 0.2164) compared to BSEP1, respectively. Other parameters (eg, graft type, rejection, immunosuppressive regimen, age at LT, or year of LT) did not impact AIBD risk (Table 2). Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 81.1% [95% CI: 47.5%–100%] for rituximab-based treatment regimens to improve AIBD (Figure 4B). Re-LT was necessary in 6.7% (17/252, 59 NA) (Figure 1D) due to ESLD in 7 patients (52.9%), and 2 patients were reported to be still listed. 13.1% (33/252, 196 NA) had episodes of graft rejection.

PFIC 3

The systematic literature search identified 105 patients with PFIC3 from 24 studies (Supplemental Table S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545). There were 30.5% female (32/105, 20 NA) patients in this cohort. The median follow-up was 60 months (IQR 59.3). The median age of onset of symptoms was 15 months (IQR 20). Genetic information was available for 60% of patients (63/105). Genotype categorization grouped 58.7% of patients to MDR3-1 (37/63), 12.7% to MDR3-2 (8/63), and 28.6% to MDR3-3 (18/63). The median age at liver transplantation was 73 months (IQR 74). LDLT was performed in 26.7% of patients (28/105, 66 NA), of whom all received a living-related donor graft. In 10.5% (11/105, 66 NA), DD transplantation was performed. Post-LT 5-year survival was 97.6% (Figure 2C), and 5-year graft survival was 98.2% (Figure 2D). No PFIC3-specific complications after LT were reported. There was no available data for the cause of death. Only 2.9% of patients (3/105, 22 NA) were retransplanted (Figure 1D), and the cause of re-LT was not reported.

PFIC 4

The systematic literature search identified 29 patients with PFIC4 from 9 studies (Supplemental Table S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A545), with 24.1% being female (7/29, 18 NA). The median follow-up was 31 months (IQR 66). Genetic confirmation was available in 22 patients (75.8%). With the exception of 1 patient with homozygous missense mutations, all other patients identified were reported to have loss of function mutations. The initial symptoms were low GGT cholestasis in 20 of 29 patients (68.9%), jaundice and hepatomegaly in 3 patients (10.3%), cholestasis in 4 patients (13.7%), variceal bleeding and HCC in 1 patient (4%), and acute on chronic liver failure in 1 patient. The median age of onset was 2 months (IQR 2). Extrahepatic manifestations were reported in 10.3% of patients (3/29): 1 patient presented with unexplained recurrent subdural hematomas despite normal clotting after 8 years of first symptoms, 1 patient suffered from chronic respiratory disease, and 1 presented with sensorineural deafness. In 13.8% of patients (4/29), HCC was diagnosed before or at the time of LT. LT was performed at a median age of 30 months (IQR 38). LDLT from related donors was reported in 13.8% of patients (4/29, 22 NA), and 10% (3/29, 22 NA) received a DD graft. The 5-year patient and graft survival rate was 100% (Figure 2C, D). No PFIC4-specific complications after LT were reported. Re-LT was not reported (Figure 2D), and no data on rejection were available.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review of 79 studies describes the outcome of PFIC patients after LT and illuminates specific complications that might arise in certain PFIC subtypes following LT. PFIC subtypes 1-3 comprised the largest number of studies and patients, and only few studies were identified on the more recently recognized subtype 4. This impacted the strength of subsequent (meta-) analyses, and we thus, focused our quantitative analyses on PFIC1-3.

The outcome after LT for PFIC1 is lined by extrahepatic and hepatic graft complications putatively associated with the FIC1-deficient intestine.17 We have included 121 patients with PFIC1 in this systematic review and indeed found a high prevalence of post-LT complications: diarrhea was reported in 49.6% and allograft steatosis occurred in 50.4%. Over the last years, the impact of living-related versus DD transplantation on the occurrence of diarrhea and steatosis after LT was subject to speculation. Our analyses show that neither the type of liver graft, underlying genotype, age at LT, and year of LT nor steroid administration influenced the occurrence of steatosis. Remarkably, patients presenting with diarrhea after LT were at significant risk of developing graft steatosis (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3C). This allows us to identify patients at risk and increases the relevance of timely interventions to interrupt the enterohepatic circulation, as we have identified only 8.2% (n = 10/121) patients who have received SBD after LT. Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 100% for SBD to ameliorate graft steatosis and an overall efficacy of 94.9% for SBD to improve diarrhea.

This emphasizes first the role of the FIC1-deficient enterohepatic circulation underlying this phenomenon and second, as described in earlier reports23–27 that SBD post-LT seems an efficient treatment to protect the graft from injury. Total external biliary diversion or total internal diversion to the colon seems to be the preferable solution. As already discussed by Van Wessel and colleagues, it remains an invasive procedure, and alternative possibilities to interrupt the EHC do already exist.12 The recent development of IBATi presents a novel tool to decrease serum bile acids and consequently influence diarrhea. IBATi was shown to be efficient in controlling post-LT diarrhea.28 An alternative explanation for diarrhea might be pancreatitis and/or pancreatic insufficiency, both associated with PFIC1, but in this context, data were too limited for further analyses.

PFIC2 is considered to be cured by LT as BSEP is expressed exclusively in hepatocytes.29 Our systematic literature search identified 252 children with PFIC2. Recurrence of BSEP deficiency after LT caused by the development of anti-BSEP antibodies is known to be a complication, which is difficult to manage. We found AIBD to be reported in 16.3% of patients at a median of 36.5 months (IQR 49.25) after LT. Interestingly, only patients harboring BSEP2 and BSEP3 genotypes were at significant risk of developing AIBD (p < 0.0001). This underlines the assumption that patients without proper BSEP expression at the bile canaliculus’s surface have a “BSEP naïve” immune system. Conceivably, antibodies against BSEP might be formed after LT.30,31 One would expect a certain trigger for this to happen. Indeed, various preceding events of graft hepatocyte injury were reported. Yet, how bile canalicular BSEP is presented to the immune system is not fully understood. With this knowledge at hand, genotype-specific prediction of post-LT outcome can be assumed. Once AIBD has occurred, management is cumbersome. In our cohort, 21 cases were treated with rituximab, mostly in combination with i.v. Igs and/or plasmapheresis. Meta-analysis showed an efficacy of 81.1% [95% CI: 47.5%–100%] for rituximab-based treatment regimens to improve AIBD. Whether rituximab is effective as a monotherapy needs to be further evaluated. Retransplantation because of AIBD was common in our cohort, indicating the difficulties of controlling anti-BSEP antibody production over a long-term period. Ideally, prospective studies would be required to identify early warning signs or biomarkers for the occurrence of AIBD to initiate adequate treatment in time.

We have identified and analyzed 98 patients with PFIC3 in our systematic review. Interestingly, for patients with PFIC3, no specific disease-associated complications after liver transplantations are reported. This is of high relevance for counseling. Yet, why antibody-mediated disease recurrence after LT in patients with loss of protein expression only occurs for BSEP and not for the extracellular presented proteins FIC1 and MDR3 alike remains to be elucidated.

The mutational spectrum of PFIC4 patients is different from other types of PFIC as all but 1 patient were reported to have loss of function mutations. Native liver survival was the second shortest of all subtypes included in this study, and liver damage seems substantial, with an HCC rate at the time of LT reported higher than for PFIC2 (13.8% vs. 3.6%). This is important to emphasize to have PFIC4 patients with native liver adequately screened.

Patients with less frequent, novel, and yet not fully understood types of PFIC-associated genetic mutations (eg, nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H, MYO5B, KIF12, lipolysis stimulated lipoprotein receptor, ubiquitin-specific peptidase 53, and WD repeat domain 83 opposite strand gene) were not included in this study.

This systematic review has certain limitations that are, to some extent, based on the rarity of the diseases included. First, the retrospective design of all studies included impacts on the conclusions to be drawn. Second, most studies have very limited patient numbers with often incomplete descriptions of patients, including genetic, biochemical, and histopathologic details. Anthropometric outcomes were not assessed due to missing data. Larger cohorts lack details on individual patients included, limiting detailed analyses. Third, the quality assessment of the included studies was restricted due to the wide heterogeneity of study types and the overall low hierarchical level of evidence. These limitations also highlight the imperative for prospective studies.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study shows that half of all PFIC1 patients develop diarrhea after LT. These patients are at significant risk of developing graft steatosis, and SBD seems an efficient treatment option for these patients to protect the graft from injury. PFIC2 patients with BSEP2 or BSEP3 genotypes are at significant risk of developing AIBD. Rituximab-based treatment regimens are effective in improving AIBD. The genotype of PFIC3 patients has no impact on native liver survival, and PFIC3 patients have no PFIC-specific complications following LT. PFIC4 patients have a high rate of HCC at the time of LT. Despite some limitations, the presented analyses increase the understanding of PFIC-specific problems after transplantation, ideally allowing for better care for PFIC patients.
