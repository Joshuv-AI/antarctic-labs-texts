Scaling up the production of fungal perylenequinones and investigating their biosynthesis through stable isotope labeling
Authors: Reema A Al-Qiam, Sachin Dumbare, Tyler N Graf, Warren S Vidar, Huzefa A Raja, Cedric J Pearce, Shabnam Hematian, Nicholas H Oberlies
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerylenequinones, such as hypocrellins and hypomycins, are fungal secondary metabolites with potential for pharmaceutical and industrial applications due to both their physical and biological properties. This study focused on their sustainable production. Additionally, stable isotope labeling was used to probe the biosynthesis of these compounds, demonstrating how sugars are likely incorporated into the perylenequinone scaffold.MethodsShiraia sp. (strain MSX60519; Shiraiaceae, Pleosporales) was cultivated under varying nutrient conditions to evaluate the production of perylenequinones, with sugars serving as primary carbon sources. Five metabolites were isolated (from oatmeal cultures) using environmentally friendly solvent-based techniques, and the process was further optimized to maximize yields. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS) were employed to detect, characterize, and quantify the major compounds. Furthermore, feeding experiments were performed using 13C-labeled glucose, with droplet probe mass spectrometry used to monitor stable isotope incorporation in situ.ResultsThis study yielded three key findings. First, the production of perylenequinones was significantly enhanced by supplementing fermentation media with sugars, and disaccharides significantly enhanced the production of perylenequinones compared to monosaccharides. Optimizing sugar concentrations during the fermentation further influenced the profile of secondary metabolites. Second, stable isotope labeling experiments confirmed that sugars are the primary building blocks of perylenequinones, as noted by tracing 13C-labeling into ent-shiraiachrome A (1). Finally, a green, scalable, and sustainable strategy for producing these compounds on the gram scale was developed by optimizing fermentation conditions, refining purification methods, and improving extraction efficiency.ConclusionThese findings provide critical insights into optimizing fermentation conditions for the scaled and sustainable production of perylenequinones. This approach offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly pipeline for harnessing these valuable compounds, paving the way for broader pharmaceutical and industrial applications.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02842-6.Keywords: Hypocrellins, Hypomycins, Stable isotope labeling, 13C-labeled, Carbon metabolism, Sustainable production, Green chemistry, Shiraia sp., Shiraiaceae

Introduction

Perylenequinones, natural compounds with highly conjugated structures, have found extensive applications in various fields [1–3]. In medicine, they are being explored for their potential in treating diseases, including their use as photosensitizers in photodynamic therapy [4, 5]. Approximately 85% of perylenequinones are produced by fungi, noted across 22 genera of the phylum Ascomycota [2, 6]. These molecules often exhibit intriguing activities, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, antitumor, and antiviral [1, 2, 7]. The complexity of their structures underscores the importance of advanced synthetic and analytical techniques for their study and utilization [8, 9]. Additionally, the biosynthetic pathways responsible for producing these compounds in fungi remains a topic of ongoing research. Earlier studies traced their biosynthesis via 13C-labeled acetate experiments, where the heavy atom was incorporated into the perylenequinone scaffold [10]. More recently, synthetic biology [11, 12] and heterologous expression [13, 14] approaches have been used to modulate their biosynthesis. However, additional research is warranted to better understand and manipulate cellular metabolism so as to enhance their production and improve bioengineering processes.

Hypocrellins (1–3) and hypomycins (4–6) are two subclasses of structurally similar perylenequinones (Chart 1), where the hypomycins feature an additional six-membered ring and a less extended π-system, leading to variations in redox behavior and absorption profiles [15, 16]. The photocytotoxic effects of both hypocrellins and hypomycins have been evaluated against human melanoma cells, with hypocrellins displaying EC50 values in the nanomolar range and hypomycins in the micromolar range [17]. Importantly, both compound classes exhibit significantly reduced cytotoxicity in the absence of light (dark EC50 values ~ 100 µM), indicating high phototherapeutic indices. These properties underscore their pharmacological potential in photodynamic therapy and justify continued efforts toward scalable production and chemical characterization [17].

Structures of perylenequinones (1–6)

Very little research has been reported on the biosynthesis and scale-up of the hypomycins, yet we showed recently that we can induce intramolecular cyclization of hypocrellins to hypomycins via modulating the redox homeostasis in the growing fungus (i.e., Shiraia sp.) [15]. As such, this class of perylenequinones has not been investigated extensively, particularly with respect to pharmaceutical and industrial applications. Alternatively, a few studies have focused on increasing the production of hypocrellins [18, 19]. Moreover, while total synthesis of perylenequinones has been accomplished [20–24], none have proven to be scalable [22].

Developing reliable ways to supply perylenequinones for pharmaceutical and industrial applications is still a challenge [17, 25]. Various attempts have been made to modify the growing conditions, where light plays a crucial role in stimulating the biosynthesis of these compounds [17, 26, 27]. There have been several studies that have examined enhanced production of perylenequinones in Shiraia fungi isolated from bamboo. For example, red and blue light were found to specifically induce the biosynthesis of hypocrellin A [28, 29]. Co-culturing the fungus Shiraia sp. with bacteria, such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas, also significantly boosted production [30–32]. A suite of fermentation conditions has been tested, including variations of chemical additives, environmental factors, media, and nutrients. For example, sodium nitroprusside increased hypocrellin A biosynthesis by 179%, likely via nitric oxide release [33–36], while Triton X-100 also stimulated production [37]. Additionally, it has been reported that adding branched amino acids (e.g., L-valine) doubled output [38], and L-arginine activated perylenequinone biosynthesis and transporter proteins through nitric oxide signaling [39]. Growth at higher temperature (i.e., 32 °C) upregulated key genes, increasing yield three- to five-fold [40]. Solid-state fermentation using corn and NaNO3 further optimized production in Shiraia sp. [41]. In short, many different processes have been implemented for improving the production of perylenequinones, although none of these has focused on hypomycins nor supplementing solid media with added sugars.

This paper makes three key contributions towards both optimizing and understanding of perylenequinone biosynthesis. First, we address the production challenges by demonstrating that supplementing fungal media with specific sugars, particularly disaccharides, can double perylenequinone yields, offering a scalable strategy to overcome biosynthetic bottlenecks. Second, stable isotope labeling with 13C-glucose elucidates the metabolic origins and biosynthetic pathways of these fungal perylenequinones. Finally, we streamline the purification of key metabolites, improving efficiency and supporting more sustainable practices.

Results and discussion

Impact of different sugar substrates on the production of perylenequinones

Photoactivated perylenequinones produced by plant pathogenic fungi [e.g., Shiraia sp. (strain MSX60519; Shiraiaceae, Pleosporales)] generate reactive oxygen species that damage host cells, contributing to disease [42]. Pathogenicity depends upon the ability of the fungus to assimilate host-derived carbon, nitrogen, and micronutrients [43–46], while metabolic flexibility allows the fungus to adjust to changing environmental conditions [44, 47, 48]. For example, waterborne conidial fungi exhibit varying abilities to utilize different carbon sources for their growth and metabolic activities, typically consuming simple sugars as a source of energy [49]. Like other pathogenic fungi, Shiraia sp. relies upon environmental nutrients for survival and proliferation [11], and it is known that fungi utilize sugars via glycolysis and respiration [50]. Glucose, fructose, sucrose, and maltose are commonly used sugars in the cultivation of fungi [51], since it is established that sugars, as both carbon and energy sources, influence fungal physiology and secondary metabolism [12, 52]. However, in general, the effects of specific sugars on biomass production and chemical diversity can vary widely depending on the fungal species and the biosynthetic pathways involved.

In this study, we investigated the impact of supplementing the fermentation media (vide infra) with various sugars and examined their influence on the production of five perylenequinones by Shiraia sp. (Chart 1); their structures were confirmed by analysis of 1H NMR and ECD data (Figs. S1 and S2). Comparative analysis revealed distinct patterns linking carbon sources to perylenequinones biosynthesis. These findings offer insights into optimizing fermentation conditions and demonstrate that the production of key perylenequinones (i.e., 1, 2 and 4–6) can be enhanced, up to double, through a simple and cost-effective approach.

Specifically, glucose, fructose, and xylose were added as representative monomeric sugars (i.e., monosaccharides), while sucrose and trehalose were representative dimeric sugars (i.e., disaccharides). For each condition, approximately 240 mg of sugar were added to the growth media (see Fig. S3 for a cartoon of the protocol). This amount was chosen to reflect the estimated sugar content naturally present in oatmeal, as noted by the nutrition facts on the label, thereby maintaining a constant carbon input across all sugar types, regardless of molecular weight or structure. The cultures were grown and processed, and the resulting extracts were separated by normal-phase chromatography into three main fractions, in which fraction 2 was enriched for perylenequinones (Fig. S4). Compared to the control (i.e., no added sugar; Fig. 1), all supplemented groups (except xylose) showed a significant increase in the yield of fraction 2 (Table S1 and Fig. S5).

Since all compounds except hypocrellin B (3) are natural metabolites, we quantified 1, 2 and 4–6. To demonstrate that their absolute concentrations varied under different fermentation conditions, calibration curves were constructed using a mixture of standards. The calibration range was from 0.40 to 409.60 µg/mL for compound 1 and 0.04 to 40.96 µg/mL for compounds 2 and 4–6 (Fig. S6 and Table S2). The samples were analyzed using UPLC-HRESIMS, with data collected in triplicate and peak areas extracted from UV data at 310 nm. The analytical method was validated for linearity, precision, accuracy, and sensitivity [53, 54]. The instrument response exhibited a linear relationship with analyte concentration across the tested range. The calibration curves for each compound, the linear equation, and the coefficient of determination (R2) indicated excellent linearity, with both the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) being within acceptable ranges (Table S2). The relative standard deviations (RSDs) across eleven concentration points for all five compounds were below 10%, confirming the precision of this method.

The absolute amounts of each compound, as well as the total perylenequinones per flask, were significantly higher under sucrose- and trehalose-enriched conditions, with levels approximately doubling relative to the untreated control (Fig. 1 and Table S3). Glucose and fructose supplementation led to more modest increases of ~ 0.3- and 0.5-fold in the total amount of perylenequinones, respectively. Specifically, based on three biological replicates, the amount of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) in mg/g was 13.02 ± 1.75 mg/g with sucrose and 12.28 ± 0.94 mg/g with trehalose, compared to 8.90 ± 1.78 mg/g and 9.56 ± 1.74 mg/g for glucose and fructose, respectively. In contrast, levels were lower in the xylose (6.14 ± 1.02 mg/g) and control (7.22 ± 0.16 mg/g) groups.

The superior effect of the disaccharides was initially surprising given that they must first be hydrolyzed into monosaccharides before further metabolism; however, several hypotheses in the literature may help explain this observation. For example, disaccharides, such as sucrose and trehalose, are known to function as osmolytes, potentially regulating their cellular uptake [55, 56]. Additionally, a recent proteomics study revealed that Shiraia bambusicola, a related species, upregulates glucoside hydrolase enzymes during host colonization, possibly favoring the breakdown of complex sugars for energy and carbon [57]. Thus, the enhanced production of perylenequinones in response to sugar supplementation, particularly disaccharides, may result from multiple contributing factors. As a central carbon source, sugar catabolism via glycolysis generates key precursors and reactants such as acetyl-CoA, NADPH, and ATP that support the perylenequinone biosynthetic processes [48, 58]. Compared to control, increased sugar availability also promotes fungal biomass accumulation [59], expanding the capacity for secondary metabolite production. Moreover, sugars can upregulate genes involved in perylenequinone biosynthesis, enhancing enzyme expression and metabolic flux through the pathway [60–63]. Together, these mechanisms likely contributed to the observed increase in perylenequinone production.

To compare solid vs. liquid media, the best previously reported yields using solid media were ~ 2.02 mg/g [64], and even the most productive liquid fermentation systems reported yields of ~ 237.92 mg/L [38]. Given that our approach (13.68 mg/g) yields ~ 260 mg of perylenequinones using only two flasks (i.e., each containing 10 g of media), solid-state fermentation demonstrates both efficiency and scalability for their production on the lab scale.

Influence of sucrose concentrations on the production of perylenequinones

Overall, sucrose supplementation led to about a 100% increase in the biosynthesis of perylenequinones compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 1). To further investigate this effect, we next examined how varying sucrose concentrations impact the production of perylenequinones.

Sucrose was added to the oatmeal fermentation media at half-, one-, two-, and four-equivalent levels relative to the estimated native sucrose content in oatmeal (~ 250 mg per 10 g, based on the product’s Nutrition Facts label), with three biological replicates per condition. All sucrose-supplemented groups showed a significant increase in the yield of the perylenequinone-enriched fraction compared to the control (Fig. S7 and Table S4). Using the same calibration curves, the absolute amounts of compounds 1, 2, and 4–6 were quantified (Fig. 2 and Table S5).

Sucrose concentration plays a critical role in regulating perylenequinone production by modulating both primary and secondary metabolism. While moderate increases in sucrose enhance carbon availability and boost metabolite biosynthesis, excessive levels can cause osmotic stress and trigger carbon catabolite repression [65–67]. Production effectively doubled with one-fold supplementation and plateaued at higher concentrations. This identifies one-fold as the optimal level for maximizing yield without adverse effects and highlights sucrose supplementation as a simple strategy for efficient scale-up. For the experiments that follow, the cultures were initiated in the presence of sucrose (i.e., YESS instead of YESD).

Stable isotope labeling (SIL) for metabolic tracing

To gain deeper insight into how carbon sources regulate perylenequinone biosynthesis, we employed stable isotope labeling (SIL) combined with high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and NMR spectroscopy to trace the incorporation of labeled carbon precursors into secondary metabolites (Fig. S8). Isotope labeling is a powerful tool in natural products research, widely used to elucidate biosynthetic pathways by tracking the fate of isotopically labeled precursors within biological systems [68, 69]. By isolating and analyzing the labeled compounds, one can reveal metabolic pathways, enzymatic transformations, and precursor utilization patterns [70–75].

Our results demonstrated that sugar supplementation effectively doubled the production of perylenequinones, highlighting the metabolic flexibility of fungal biosynthetic pathways. This raised a key question: are the sugars merely supporting the fungal growth, or are they directly incorporated into the final products? Given that perylenequinones are biosynthesized via the polyketide synthase (PKS) pathway, the availability of key precursors, particularly acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, is critical to their production [10, 12], and enhanced flux through glycolysis can increase precursor availability. For perylenequinones, this supports the formation of toralactone, a key intermediate on the route to hypocrellins and hypomycins (Fig. 3A).

A proposed biosynthetic pathway of hypocrellins, starting from 13C-labeled glucose and linking glycolysis of sugar and the PKS pathway. B Stacked LC-MS chromatograms of the fungal extract (strain MSX60519) grown in control culture with glucose (Black) and grown in the same media but enriched with 13C-labeled glucose (Maroon), showing no change in retention times in both conditions. C Stacked mass spectra of the same samples shown in panel B. D Zoom in on the 13C-labeled sample shown in maroon in panel C, with the number of incorporated 13C-atoms noted

To assess carbon incorporation, cultures supplemented with 13C-labeled glucose were analyzed using the droplet probe, a mass spectrometry-based surface sampling technique that enables in situ chemical analysis with spatial and temporal resolution [76, 77]. This method is particularly suited for filamentous fungi, allowing direct analysis of metabolite distribution across distinct morphological regions with minimal preparation. Using the droplet probe, we detected 13C-labeled perylenequinones, indicating incorporation of sugar-derived carbon into the polyketide-derived fungal metabolites (Fig. S9).

Building on this, we further validated these findings by extracting the fungal culture plates, followed by LC-MS analysis to observe fragmentation patterns and quantify isotope incorporation. The chromatographic retention times of key perylenequinones for the labeled and unlabeled cultures were identical (Fig. 3B), as expected and noted by others [73]. In control samples, the m/z corresponding to [M + H]+ was 547.1581 for the major peak (i.e., compound 1), while in labeled cultures, a series of peaks indicated incorporation of multiple 13C-atoms (Fig. 3C). By using the mass spectral data for the protonated molecular ion of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) as a reference, it was possible to determine the number of 13C-labeled carbons incorporated, which indicated a range of 8–30 13C-atoms involved in the biosynthesis of 1 (Fig. 3D). MZmine® was used to quantify the number of heavy isotopes in each observed ion (Fig. S10). The highest ion intensity was related to ent-shiraiachrome A (1) with 25 incorporated 13C-atoms, followed by incorporation of 24 and 26 heavy atoms (~ 30% of the labeled population). Given the molecular formula of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) (C30H26O10), this level of labeling suggests that nearly all carbon atoms, particularly the 26 carbon atoms of the perylenequinone core, can be derived from sugar sources, a finding that aligns nicely with the biosynthetic pathway first proposed in the 1960s [52, 78].

These experiments indicate that glucose provides most of the building blocks (i.e., acetyl- and malonyl-CoA) for the perylenequinone core. Importantly, there are other carbon sources in the culture that contribute to the different ions and their isotopic profiles (i.e., 15–30 labeled carbon incorporated). However, there were some ions that indicated the incorporation of 27–30 13C-atoms, suggesting that even the methoxy moieties were labeled. This implies that the labeled glucose molecules were likely incorporated in the biosynthesis of methionine, which is needed for the O-methyltransferase enzyme process, as previously reported [14, 79–81].

To map the specific locations of label incorporation, we compared fragmentation patterns between labeled and unlabeled 1. The molecule yielded five major fragments (Fig. S11 and Table S6). Notably, fragments corresponding to acetate loss (Fragments 4 and 5) exhibited a 2-unit mass shift in labeled samples, confirming 13C-incorporation in those positions (Figs. S12-18). This analysis also revealed selective enrichment in polyketide-derived regions, particularly those originating from malonyl-CoA. The detailed fragmentation data allowed us to distinguish genuine isotopic incorporation from potential adduct formation, providing strong evidence for the integration of sugar-derived carbon into the perylenequinone scaffold. Further analysis of the fragmentation patterns of different ions suggests that most, if not all, carbon atoms in the backbone of compound 1 can be labeled. Notably, the data indicates incorporation of glucose-derived carbon via two distinct routes, either as two-carbon acetate units via PKS biosynthesis, or as single-carbon methyl groups through the methionine pathway (i.e., methoxy substituents). This dual incorporation route accounts for the observed distribution of both an odd and even number of 13C-atoms (Fig S19).

To further validate these findings, we purified ent-shiraiachrome A (1) via HPLC (Fig. S20) and acquired 1H and 13C-NMR spectra. Comparison between the labeled and unlabeled samples revealed clear increases in signal intensity at all the carbon positions in the labeled compound, along with widespread splitting due to coupling between 13C-labeled atoms (Table 1). Each carbon resonance in the labeled sample was split by at least one adjacent 13C-neighbor, a pattern only observed in 13C-enriched molecules, confirming the direct incorporation of the 13C-atoms into 1 (Fig. S21). Complementary 1H-NMR data showed no unexpected chemical shift perturbations, indicating that the core structure of 1 remained unchanged, apart from the introduced 13C-atoms (Fig. S22). Together, these results provide strong evidence that sugar-derived carbons are incorporated into perylenequinones, serving as a biosynthetic precursor to core building blocks rather than functioning solely as a general nutrient or energy source.

In total, we established a comprehensive workflow to track heavy isotope incorporation, identify labeled intermediates, and quantify metabolic flux within fungal cultures. This confirmed that labeled sugars contributed directly to acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA pools and provided a quantitative measure of biosynthetic efficiency. These findings reinforce the notion that optimizing carbon source availability can serve as a strategic approach to enhance secondary metabolite production in fungi [47, 82]. The observed labeling pattern aligns with fungal biosynthetic pathways that utilize sugar-derived intermediates, reinforcing the idea that carbon from the sugar is incorporated into the perylenequinones scaffold [14, 52, 82]. This work provides new insights into the metabolic origins of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) and lays the groundwork for further studies on the enzymatic mechanisms driving their biosynthesis.

Improving the purification of ent-shiraiachrome A (1)

To improve the yield and purity of perylenequinones, we refined both extraction and purification protocols. We explored a range of solvents and extraction strategies to identify effective methods for maximizing their isolation and recovery. Additionally, we evaluated several purification approaches, including chromatography and precipitation, to enhance both efficiency and purity. These improvements are pivotal for scaling production in a cost-effective and sustainable manner. Specifically, we incorporated a greener extraction approach to reduce environmental impact and streamlined the purification pipeline by minimizing preparative HPLC cycles, significantly improving overall speed and cost (Fig. 4). Previously these compounds were extracted and purified using a process that relied heavily on halogenated solvents, particularly CHCl3 [16, 17]; other research groups have also used halogenated solvents for similar extractions [28]. In the updated protocol, CHCl3 and MeOH were eliminated, being replaced with acetone for the initial extraction step, followed by reconstituting/partitioning with ethyl acetate and water to remove water-soluble inorganic components (Fig. S23 and Table S7). The defatting step still employed a 1:1 mixture of acetonitrile and hexanes. While hexanes, a mixture of six-carbon alkanes, is not considered a green solvent due to its toxicity and poor biodegradability [83], its selective ability to remove lipophilic pigments was advantageous in this specific context. We have noted this limitation in line with solvent selection principles and are exploring greener alternatives (e.g., n-heptane or 2-methyltetrahydrofuran) in future valorization studies. Notably, all solvents (with the exception of hexanes) used in the revised method are recommended by the CHEM21 green chemistry guide [84, 85]. To compare the two procedures, the yield of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) per fermentation flask was quantified across five biological replicates. The original method yielded 108.5 ± 7.3 mg of 1, while the greener procedure yielded 95.3 ± 12.8 mg. An unpaired t-test showed no statistically significant difference between the methods, indicating comparable efficiency in the recovery of 1.

Streamlined procedure for the isolation and purification of ent-shiraiachrome A (1), showing the green extraction steps and the resuspension-centrifugation technique to precipitate > 95% pure compound 1

Previously, approximately 50 mg of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) and 1–3 mg of compounds 2–6 were purified from a single fermentation flask (10 g oatmeal), and this required two rounds of HPLC, each run taking ~ 45 min [16, 17]. To improve accessibility of these compounds for downstream applications, we developed a simpler and more efficient purification method based on precipitation and centrifugation (Fig. 4). This approach is particularly well-suited for perylenequinones, as ent-shiraiachrome A (1) is the major metabolite. Rapid precipitation of 1 yielded ~ 100–110 mg/flask, significantly improving purification efficiency (Fig. S24). Furthermore, this method enriched the supernatant with the minor components (i.e., 2–6), streamlining their subsequent isolation and purification by HPLC.

Scaling up the semisynthesis of hypocrellin B (3)

A compound of interest to our research team (manuscript under review) that can be derived from 1 and/or 2 via semisynthesis is hypocrellin B (3). This compound exhibits a wide range of biological activities, and recent studies have focused on enhancing its chemical and biological properties to improve photodynamic efficacy [7, 86]. Building on our efforts to scale fermentation and improve extraction and isolation procedures, we generated a larger pool of 1. We observed here and in a previous study [17] that rice cultures predominantly produce compounds 1 and 2, whereas oatmeal cultures yield a larger suite of compounds, including 4–6. Therefore, we transitioned to rice cultures in this phase to simplify purification efforts. Herin, instead of starting with pure 1, we utilized a mixture of ent-shiraiachrome A (1) and hypocrellin (2), specifically, fraction 2 from normal phase chromatography (Fig. 4), as either compound can undergo dehydration to form 3. As proof of concept, 10 flasks of Shiraia sp. (strain MSX60519) were cultured and processed as described above, yielding ~ 2.5 g of extract. Normal-phase chromatography enriched the perylenequinones to give ~ 1.2 g of fraction 2, which was subjected to a dehydration reaction. After workup, this yielded ~ 1.1 g of crude hypocrellin B (3), which was purified using a centrifugation-precipitation protocol (Fig. S25A) to afford > 95% pure 3 (700 mg). Purity was validated by stacked 1H NMR spectra, confirming > 95% purity after two reconstitution and centrifugation cycles (Fig. S25B-E).

Conclusions

This study advances the sustainable production of perylenequinones, a promising class of fungal secondary metabolites with pharmaceutical and industrial relevance. By supplementing fermentation media with sugars, we significantly enhanced the overall yield of perylenequinones and gained insights into the metabolic processes underlying their biosynthesis. Through a comprehensive analytical workflow combining stable isotope labeling, droplet probe-MS, LC-MS, and NMR, we traced carbon flux from glucose to perylenequinones, confirming glucose as a primary carbon source and revealing how nutrient availability regulates secondary metabolism. Finally, we present optimized, scalable, and greener extraction and purification methods, overcoming a key production bottleneck. Together, these findings lay the groundwork for future efforts to harness the full potential of perylenequinones in sustainable biotechnological and pharmaceutical applications.

Materials and methods

General experimental procedures

1D and 2D NMR data were collected using an ECA-500 spectrometer operating at 500 MHz or an ECS-400 spectrometer operating at 400 MHz that is equipped with a high-sensitivity Royal probe and a 24-slot autosampler (both from JEOL Ltd.). Residual solvent signals for CDCl3 were utilized for referencing [i.e., δH = 7.260 and δC = 77.160]. ECD spectra data were collected using an Olis DSM 17 ECD spectrophotometer (Olis, Inc.). UPLC-HRESIMS data were collected via an LTQ-Orbitrap XL mass spectrometry system (Thermo Finnigan, San Jose, CA, USA) connected to a Waters Acquity UPLC system, which used a BEH Shield RP18 column (Waters, 1.7 µm; 50 mm × 2.1 mm) heated to 40 °C. The mobile phase comprised a linear gradient of CH3CN/H2O with 0.1% HCOOH starting at 15% CH3CN to 100% over 10 min at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. MS data were collected from m/z 150 to 2000 in positive mode. The same UPLC system (Waters Corp.), utilizing the same column, was used to measure the purity of the isolated compounds, with data collected and analyzed using Empower 3 software. For the in situ detection of the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in fungal cultures, the droplet probe (i.e., droplet-LMJ-SSP) [77] was coupled with a Waters Acquity ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system attached to a Thermo Q Exactive Plus via procedures described previously [76, 87]. Microextractions were performed using a droplet of 1:1 MeOH: H2O. Three different spots were extracted in triplicate by delivering 5 µL of the solvent from a syringe to the surface of the culture, then aspirating it back after ~ 2 s of interaction to be injected into the UPLC-MS system. The UPLC separations were performed using the BEH C18 column, as described above, with the same solvents and gradients, but over 8 min. The mobile phase was held for another 1.5 min at 100% CH3CN before going back to the starting conditions. A Varian Prostar HPLC system, equipped with ProStar 210 pumps and a Prostar 335 photodiode array detector, was used to conduct all analytical and preparative HPLC experiments, with data collected and analyzed using Galaxie Chromatography Workstation software (version 1.9.3.2, Varian Inc.). Flash chromatography was performed on a Teledyne ISCO CombiFlash Rf 200 using Silica Gold columns (from Teledyne ISCO) and monitored by UV and evaporative light-scattering detectors. All solvents used for purification and analysis were of HPLC grade and were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Media and fermentations

Shiraia sp. (strain MSX60519; Shiraiaceae, Pleosporales) was previously identified and maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco) [17]. In the present study, we used malt extract agar (MEA; Difco) to maintain the culture, as the culture seems to grow optimally on this medium. To start a seed culture, an agar plug from the leading edge of the MEA colony was aseptically transferred into a sterile tube containing 10 mL of YESD medium (20 g soy peptone, 20 g dextrose, 5 g yeast extract, 1 L nano-pure water). Cultures were incubated for 14 days at ~ 23 °C on an orbital shaker at 100 rpm. These seed cultures were used to inoculate all downstream fermentations; oatmeal was used to study the effect of different sugars on the production of hypocrellins (i.e., 1 and 2), and hypomycins (i.e., 4–6), while rice media was used for scaling up the production of 3.

Enrichment of the fermentation media with different sugars: For sugar enrichment studies, solid-state fermentations were performed in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks using 10 g of old-fashioned oatmeal (Quaker) hydrated with 17 mL of nano-pure water. To assess the impact of various sugars (glucose, fructose, xylose, sucrose, and trehalose) on metabolite production, we added ~ 240 mg of each sugar solution to the YESD starting culture and the solid culture on oatmeal (Fig. S3). Initially, stock solutions of 100,000 ppm of each sugar were created. Then, 1 mL of each of these was added to the YESD solution, and the fungus was grown for 2 weeks with shaking. Then, the oatmeal was inoculated with this culture. In addition, 1.4 mL of the stock solution of each sugar were added. This sample was then incubated at room temperature under continuous white LED light (Ustellar LED strip Lights; 24 W) in a custom lightbox (33 × 24 × 15 cm), which was described previously [17], for 14 days.

For concentration-dependent studies, a sucrose stock solution (100,000 ppm) was serially diluted to achieve final concentrations of 12,500, 25,000, 50,000, and 100,000 ppm. Corresponding volumes were added to both the 10 mL YESD (0.12, 0.35, 0.70, and 1.40 mL, respectively) and the 17 mL hydration volume used for oatmeal media (0.25, 0.50, 1.00, and 2.00 mL, respectively). These cultures were similarly incubated under LED Light for 14 days.

Scaling up the production of 3: For scale-up, the strain was grown in YESS liquid medium (20 g soy peptone, 20 g sucrose, 5 g yeast extract, 1 L nano-pure water) under the same incubation conditions as described for YESD. After 14 days, the resulting biomass was transferred to a rice-based solid media and incubated for an additional 14 days in the same lightbox to enhance production of compounds 1 and 2, which were converted to 3 (vide infra).

Plates for droplet probe experiments: To prepare samples for the droplet probe experiments, strain MSX60519 was cultured on peptone, yeast, glucose agar (PYG; 1.25 g peptone, 1.25 g yeast extract, 5 g glucose, 18 g agar, 1 L nano-pure water). Triplicate plates were inoculated with plugs from the actively growing edge of an MEA culture. A parallel set was prepared using PYG medium in which glucose was replaced with D-glucose-13C6 (CAS Number 110187-42-3). All plates were incubated for 14 days under constant LED illumination at room temperature.

Extraction, fractionation, and isolation

General green extraction protocol: An extraction protocol free of chlorinated solvents was developed, while retaining efficiency comparable to the previously reported method [17]. For each solid-state culture (on rice or oatmeal), the entire contents of the flask were chopped with a spatula in the presence of 60 mL of acetone and a small amount of Celite 545. The mixtures were shaken overnight (~ 16 h) at ambient temperature (~ 23 °C) and 100 rpm. The samples were filtered under vacuum, and the remaining solids were washed with additional acetone until the eluent was colorless. The combined filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure to remove acetone. The dried filtrate was partitioned with 90 mL of ethyl acetate and 150 mL of deionized water by stirring for 30 min, followed by partitioning in a separatory funnel. The organic (upper) phase was collected and evaporated to dryness. The residue was reconstituted in 100 mL of acetonitrile (CH3CN) and 100 mL of hexanes, shaken vigorously, and again partitioned using a separatory funnel. The CH3CN phase was collected and evaporated to yield the extract. Each sugar supplementation condition (described above) was extracted independently, and the extract masses are reported in Tables S1 and S4. Extracts were dissolved in a small amount of chloroform and adsorbed onto Celite 545 for flash chromatography. Separation was performed on a normal-phase silica column using a gradient elution of hexanes-acetone-methanol (flow rate: 18 mL/min; 75 column volumes over 25 min). The second fraction from each chromatographic run was used for compound quantification.

Scaling up the production of 3: Cultures grown in rice flasks (n = 10) were combined for each of two separate experiments—one focused on precipitating compound 1 and the other on scaling up the semisynthesis of hypocrellin B (3). The extraction protocol described above was followed with proportionally increased reagent volumes: 400 mL of acetone for the initial chopping, followed by multiple washes (~ 200 mL) until the red pigmentation was removed. Liquid-liquid partitioning was conducted using 500 mL of ethyl acetate and 700 mL of water. The final 1:1 CH3CN: hexanes partition was performed with ~ 300 mL of each solvent. This process yielded approximately 2–2.5 g of extract per experiment.

Extraction from petri plate: After droplet probe analysis, agar plates were extracted by adding ~ 5 mL of methanol directly to each plate. The agar contents were then transferred to a beaker and further extracted with ~ 20 mL of chloroform by overnight shaking at room temperature. The extract was filtered under vacuum and partitioned twice with deionized water to remove hydrophilic impurities. The organic phase was collected for further analysis.

Quantification of perylenequinones

Preparation of standards and calibration curves: Stock solutions of compounds 1, 2 and 4–6 were prepared in 1:1 methanol: dioxane at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Serial dilutions were performed to generate working standards across a wide concentration range. For compound 1, the working concentrations were: 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, 6.4, 12.8, 25.6, 51.2, 102.4, 204.8, and 409.6 µg/mL. For compounds 2, and 4–6, the concentrations were: 0.04, 0.08, 0.16, 0.32, 0.64, 1.28, 2.56, 5.12, 10.24, 20.48, and 40.96 µg/mL. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting the peak area (monitored at 310 nm) versus the concentration of each standard. Each concentration was analyzed in triplicate, and linear regression analysis yielded coefficients of determination (R2) exceeding 0.99 for all five compounds (Fig. S6 and Table S2).

Sample Preparation: Extracts from fungal cultures (prepared as described above) were dissolved in methanol to yield stock solutions at 12.5 mg/mL. Each extract was then serially diluted to 0.35 mg/mL, 0.0875 mg/mL, and 0.0218 mg/mL. All dilutions were analyzed in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Instrumentation and chromatographic conditions: Analyses were conducted using the same UPLC and mass spectrometry conditions noted above. UV detection was performed at 310 nm, as a unique wavelength for compounds 1, 2, and 4–6. Metabolite identities were confirmed by comparing retention times, exact masses, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with those of authenticated standards. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Quantification of secondary metabolites: Quantification was performed by comparing the peak areas using UV data at 310 nm of the sample extracts to those of the calibration standards. Peak areas from sample chromatograms were compared to those of calibration standards using the equations derived from the respective linear regression curves. Metabolite concentrations were calculated as micrograms per milligram of the perylenequinone-rich fraction and subsequently multiplied by the extract mass to obtain the total yield in milligrams per extract. Additionally, results were expressed as micrograms of metabolite per milligram of dry fungal weight, then multiplied by the extracted mass to get the total amounts in milligrams per extract.

Validation of the analytical method: The analytical method was validated for linearity, precision, accuracy, and sensitivity. Linearity was verified through the R2 values of the calibration curves (all > 0.99). Precision was assessed by repeated injections (n = 6) of standard solutions, with relative standard deviations (RSD) reported in Table S2. Accuracy was evaluated through recovery experiments, wherein known quantities of standards were spiked into fungal extracts, and recovered amounts were calculated. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were defined as signal-to-noise ratios of 3:1 and 10:1, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA), employing one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess differences among conditions [53].

Sample extraction via droplet probe: Droplet probe analysis was employed to examine fungal cultures grown on PYG agar plates as described previously [76]. The probe system utilized in-house software for operation and was specifically optimized for natural product surface analysis. Fisher Optima LC-MS grade solvents (1:1 MeOH:H2O) were used as the extraction medium. PYG plates were positioned on a clean glass surface beneath the probe. A total of 5 µL of solvent was aspirated into the syringe, and approximately 4 µL was dispensed onto the sample surface at a flow rate of 2 µL/s. The droplet was subsequently re-aspirated into the syringe. This dispense/withdraw cycle was repeated twice at the same location to enrich the micro-extraction. Repeated solvent cycling enhanced analyte-droplet interaction time and ensured efficient surface extraction, consistent with prior applications of the droplet probe technique.

Resuspension/centrifugation process to purify ent-shiraiachrome A (1)

To streamline and scale up the purification of compound 1 while eliminating the need for preparative HPLC, a resuspension/centrifugation protocol was developed (Fig. 4). Approximately 200 mg of the perylenequinone rich fraction was suspended in 300 µL of HPLC-grade acetonitrile and centrifuged. The resulting pellet was subjected to a second resuspension step using an additional 300 µL of acetonitrile, followed by centrifugation. The final precipitate was treated with 300 µL of ethyl acetate and 1500 µL of hexanes, vortexed, and centrifuged once more. The resulting solid was collected and analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy to assess purity.

Semisynthesis of hypocrellin B (3)

Hypocrellin B (3) was semi-synthesized from a perylenequinone rich mixture of compounds 1 and 2 obtained from fraction 2 (~ 1.2 g) via a base-catalyzed dehydration reaction, as previously reported [88]. Briefly, the mixture was treated with 40 mL of 3.0% aqueous KOH and allowed to react at room temperature (~ 23 °C) for 7 h. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched with 40 mL of 10.0% aqueous HCl to adjust the pH to neutral. The resulting mixture was extracted twice with 200 mL of ethyl acetate. The combined organic layers were dried and concentrated to yield 1.1 g of product, containing approximately 75% hypocrellin B (3) as determined by 1H NMR analysis. The crude mixture was further purified by normal phase chromatography followed by the resuspension/centrifugation method described above (Fig. S25).
