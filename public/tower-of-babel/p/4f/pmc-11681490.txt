Revealing the Novel Role of Purinergic Receptor P2X4 in Phagocytic Uptake After Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Daylin Gamiotea‐Turro, Chunxia G Cronin, Sanjeev Kumar Yadav, Arun Kumar Yadawa, Mary‐Katherine Cormier, Bruce T Liang, Rajkumar Verma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPurinergic receptor P2X4 (P2X4R), highly expressed on microglia and macrophages, is activated by ATP released from damaged cells and linked to poststroke inflammation. Previous studies showed that short‐term P2X4R inhibition reduces inflammation and promotes long term recovery, but the mechanism underlying P2X4R and inflammation remains unclear. We hypothesized that P2X4R absence or pharmacological blockade can enhance macrophage phagocytic function by alleviating excessive inflammation after stroke.Methods and ResultsWe divided P2X4R knockout and littermate control mice into 2 groups either naive or mice subjected to ischemic stroke surgery. Additionally, the regular WT mice subjected to ischemic stroke were treated with 5‐(3‐Bromophenyl)‐1,3‐dihydro‐2H‐Benzofuro[3,2‐e]‐1,4‐diazepin‐2‐one BD (a P2X4R inhibitor) or vehicle. We isolated phagocytic cells from mice in each group and assayed phagocytic activity by quantifying uptake of fluorescent beads and bioparticles using flow cytometry or confocal microscopy and by measuring protein expression related to phagocytosis. Short‐term inhibition of P2X4R with with 5‐(3‐Bromophenyl)‐1,3‐dihydro‐2H‐Benzofuro[3,2‐e]‐1,4‐diazepin‐2‐one treatment upregulated ANXA1 (annexinA1). P2X4R absence prevented ATP‐induced decline in phagocytic uptake in macrophages. Microglia or macrophages derived from P2X4R knockout mice showed significantly increased phagocytic activity compared with microglia/macrophages taken from littermate control mice. Cell surface expression of CD36, a scavenger receptor protein, increased after stroke, and was higher in P2X4R knockout mice.ConclusionsThis study suggests that blockade or absence of P2X4R increases phagocytic uptake of damaged tissue following ischemic stroke. Taken together with previous reports detailing how P2X4R inhibition is protective following stroke, our results demonstrate P2X4R may mediate long‐term resolution after ischemic stroke by enhancing phagocytic clearance.Keywords: ischemic stroke, macrophages, microglia, P2X4 receptor, phagocytosisSubject Categories: Basic Science Research, Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke

Ischemic stroke is a leading cause of disability and the fifth leading cause of death in the United States, highlighting the need for new, effective treatment strategies beyond the acute phase. 1 Despite the efficacy of current treatments like thrombolytic therapy and mechanical thrombectomy, many patients remain untreated (>90%), necessitating research into alternative therapies targeting later stages of stroke recovery. 2 Ischemic stroke induces a neuroinflammatory response through the release of ATP from damaged cells, activating microglia and macrophages via purinergic receptor P2X4 (P2X4R). 3 This activation not only exacerbates tissue damage by promoting proinflammatory cytokine release but also leads to blood–brain barrier breakdown and peripheral immune cell infiltration. 4 Excessive and prolonged P2X4R stimulation can hinder the phagocytic process essential for clearing damaged cells and preventing further ATP release, which is crucial for recovery. 3 , 5 We have previously shown that short‐term pharmacological blockage of P2X4R following ischemic stroke shows acute neuroprotection and neurorehabilitation and that these effects were mediated by a reduction in inflammation and immune cell infiltration. 3 We also found that this same short‐term blockage of P2X4R resulted in long‐term neuroprotection and neurorehabilitation, but the mechanism underlying long‐term impacts has not been elucidated. Here, we hypothesized that blocking or knocking out P2X4R will improve phagocytic clearance by reducing excessive acute inflammation thus, aiding in long‐term neuroprotection and recovery postischemic stroke. Therefore, investigated P2X4R‐mediated phagocytic signaling and phagocytosis following ischemic stroke.

METHODS

Data Availability

Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The animal study protocols were approved by the University of Connecticut's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to National Institutes of Health and Stroke Therapy Academic Industry Roundtable guidelines. We used a total of 64 P2X4 knockout (KO) and littermate (WT) mice for phagocytosis uptake experiments both in vivo and in vitro, including both sexes (Table S1). Mice in these groups were then divided into 1 of 2 groups: a group that received stroke and a group that did not receive stroke (naïve). Mice subjected to stroke underwent 60 minutes of middle cerebral artery occlusion followed by 1 to 30 days of reperfusion. Similarly, we used 12 regular WT mice, subjected them to middle cerebral artery occlusion as described, and treated them with 5‐(3‐Bromophenyl)‐1,3‐dihydro‐2H‐Benzofuro[3,2‐e]‐1,4‐diazepin‐2‐one (5‐BDBD; 1 mg/kg PO daily×3 days starting after 4 hour of middle cerebral artery occlusion onset) and let them survive for 3 and 30 days (Table S1). Primary bone marrow‐derived macrophages, microglia, or peritoneal macrophages isolated from naïve mice (WT or P2X4R KO) as well as infiltrating monocytes retrieved from ipsilateral brain tissue of poststroke mice (WT and P2X4R KO); these cells were used for phagocytic assays using Fluoresbrite Yellow Green beads or pHrodo Red Zymosan A BioParticles. Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy were used to assess phagocytic uptake and bead internalization.

RESULTS

In mice subjected to ischemic stroke, we previously showed that short‐term treatment with 5‐BDBD for 3 days enhanced long‐term recovery (30 days), reduced acute inflammation, and aided inflammation resolution, thereby improving functional recovery. 3 We found that 5‐BDBD treatment significantly (P<0.05 versus Vehicle) increased expression of ANXA1 (annexin A1), an anti‐inflammatory and phagocytic marker protein, at 30 days poststroke compared with the vehicle treated mice (Figure S1). Motivated by these observations, we then investigated P2X4R's impact on the release of inflammatory cytokines and phagocytic uptake in P2X4R KO and WT mice subjected to both stroke and no stroke (naïve). We found that lipopolysaccharides+IFN‐γ (interferon gamma)‐primed peritoneal macrophages (PMs) taken from naïve P2X4R KO mice showed significantly (P<0.05 versus WT) reduced IL‐1β (interleukin‐1beta) secretion when exposed to ATP (50μM) compared with WT mice, (Figure S2). To assay phagocytosis, we exposed lipopolysaccharides + IFN‐γ‐primed PMs to varying ATP concentrations (0 to 500 μmol/L) followed by fluorescent beads and performed flow cytometry to evaluate uptake of the beads. PMs derived from P2X4R KO mice showed significantly (P<0.05 versus WT) higher phagocytic capacity compared with WT (Figure 1A). Consistently, P2X4R KO PMs also showed significantly increased myelin uptake relative to WT (Figure 1B), indicating P2X4R's absence boosts phagocytosis at baseline in the absence of extracellular ATP as well as in the presence of successively higher ATP concentrations up to 500 μmol/L.

In the context of ischemic stroke, acute brain inflammation is driven by resident microglia and infiltrated peripheral monocytes/macrophages, which are also responsible for phagocytosis. Thus, we further assessed the phagocytic function of primary bone marrow‐derived monocytes and microglial cells. Both these monocytes and microglial cells from naïve (ie, no stroke) KO mice exhibited significantly higher phagocytic activity compared with WT as measured by increased Yellow Green bead uptake (Figure 1C through 1E), corroborating our results in PMs. We then analyzed infiltrating monocytes from the ipsilateral brain tissue of P2X4R KO and WT mice subjected to stroke by a 60 minutes middle cerebral artery occlusion and 1 to 7 days of reperfusion. Relative to WT mice, monocytes isolated from P2X4R KO brain tissue showed increased phagocytic activity and increased CD36 expression following stroke (Figure 2). Note that in Figure 2, Ly6Chi monocytes are the major infiltrating cells, which may later convert into Ly6Clow in the brain and participate in the phagocytic process. 6

Given a practical limitation that our flow analysis sometimes cannot differentiate between internalized and surface‐bound particles, which can yield inaccurate measurements of phagocytosis. To overcome this, we also employed pHrodo bioparticles and used microscopy techniques on live cells, rather than performing flow, to distinguish surface‐bound from internalized particles and validate our observations. pHrodo bioparticles fluoresce in acidic environments (pH 4–5) like those contained within endosomes and lysosomes but do not fluoresce outside of cells, which enables differentiation between internalized and surface‐bound bioparticles. Using these bioparticles, we confirmed that PMs from P2X4R KO mice (subjected to 3 days of stroke) had increased phagocytic activity as revealed by live imaging demonstrating an increase in the number of particles ingested per cell and an increase in the number of cells participating in phagocytosis (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

In the aftermath of ischemic stroke, the brain is subjected to a cascade of cellular destruction, where dead and dying cells release ATP, triggering a proinflammatory response. 3 , 4 This response is mediated through the activation of microglia and macrophages by P2X4R. 3 Overactivation of P2X4R exacerbates tissue damage and may disrupt the crucial cleanup process of phagocytosis. Our study explores the hypothesis that inhibiting or nullifying P2X4R could steer cellular mechanisms towards improved phagocytic clearance, thereby facilitating recovery after stroke. We reveal that the cellular impact of ischemic stroke can be mediated by P2X4R. 3 We previously found that treatment with P2X4R inhibitor 5‐BDBD 3 poststroke shows both acute and long‐term neuroprotective effects in a mouse model of ischemic stroke. Here, we extended this previous work by characterizing processes underlying the effects of short‐term P2X4R inhibition on long‐term neuroprotection following stroke. We demonstrated that inhibition of P2X4R with 5‐BDBD increased expression of ANXA1 in mice subjected to stroke. ANXA1 is associated with resolution, repair, and phagocytic activity, 7 the latter of which could play an important role in decreasing inflammation and offering neuroprotection and neurorehabilitation poststroke. Motivated by change in expression of ANXA1 after 5‐BDBD treatment, we then investigated if P2X4R deletion impacted phagocytic activity. Indeed, peritoneal macrophages isolated from naïve P2X4R KO mice (mice not subjected to stroke) exhibited increased phagocytic activity compared with WT counterparts, maintaining robust phagocytic activity even in the presence of supraphysiological levels of ATP, a condition that often occurs after acute ischemic stroke. 8 Consistent with this observation, phagocytosis was elevated in primary bone‐marrow‐derived monocytes and microglial cells in naive P2X4R KO mice compared with WT. In mice that were subjected to stroke, phagocytosis was higher in flow‐sorted, brain‐infiltrated monocytes taken from P2X4R KO mice compared with WT mice, indicating that P2X4R's impacts on phagocytosis were retained after stroke. Notably, poststroke, P2X4R KO mice also demonstrated elevated CD36 expression on the surface of infiltrating monocytes, indicating an enhanced phagocytic capacity during the recovery phase. The cell surface expression of the CD36 receptor plays a reparative role in stroke and other ischemic injuries by promoting phagocytosis. 9 , 10 It is possible that P2X4R stimulation following stroke reduces cell surface translocation of the CD36 receptor, thus hampering the phagocytic capacity of myeloid cells; however, this requires further investigation.

Although it is well established that the phagocytosis process is essential for creating an environment conducive to neuronal regeneration and functional recovery by removing cellular debris, it is important to note that phagocytosis can also induce mitochondrial distress and elevate reactive oxygen species levels. 11 This oxidative stress can potentially exacerbate neuronal damage and hinder recovery. Despite this, the overall benefits of phagocytosis in poststroke recovery generally outweigh these risks. The clearance of harmful debris and dead cells helps to reduce secondary damage and inflammation, thereby supporting the healing process and improving outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, the absence of P2X4R results in enhanced phagocytic uptake in brain microglia and macrophages both in the absence of and following stroke, supporting a novel mechanism of action by which P2X4R mediates damage following ischemic stroke. Our findings also indicate that P2X4R serves as a negative regulator of phagocytic uptake, probably by reducing the cell surface expression of the scavenger protein CD36.
