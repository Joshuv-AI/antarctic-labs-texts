Child and adolescent mortality in the WHO European Region: Concerning trends requiring urgent action
Authors: Alice Hucko, Ralf Weigel, Fakhriddin Nizamov, Michelle Black
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The WHO European Region is experiencing concerning setbacks in child and adolescent mortality, highlighting the need for urgent, coordinated action.Using estimates from the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME), we analyzed mortality trends from 2000 to 2022 across neonatal, under-five, 5-to-14-year-old, and 15-to-19 age groups. Our findings reveal stagnation or increases in mortality rates across multiple countries over the last five years of this period, with the highest burden in Central Asia and Eastern Europe. Several high-income countries also show worrisome plateaus or increases. Alarmingly, adolescent mortality rates are rising in 22 countries, causing the regional median to increase for the first time in decades.These trends reflect widening inequities, disproportionately affecting migrant children and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Contributing factors likely encompass health challenges faced by migrant populations, worsening adolescent mental health exacerbated by COVID-19, negative social media influences, economic decline, and armed conflict.Urgent action is needed to strengthen maternal, neonatal, and child health services and tackle preventable causes of mortality, including inadequate perinatal care, infectious diseases, and injuries. Equity-focused policies, improved health systems, a comprehensive approach to strengthening health systems, and cross-border collaboration are critical. The upcoming WHO/UNICEF 2026–2030 regional child and adolescent health and well-being strategy presents a vital opportunity to address these concerning trends.

Introduction

Infant, child, and adolescent health is at a critical juncture. In the WHO European Region, emerging mortality trends signal stalled progress and deepening inequities-issues that are compounded by the absence of a focused narrative on child and adolescent health from international agencies [1] and countries [2]. As we witness troubling reversals in child survival outcomes, it is clear that a unified, strategic focus is needed. In this commentary, developed to inform the forthcoming WHO/UNICEF Regional Strategy for Child and Adolescent Health and Well-being, we firstly highlight these emerging mortality trends for infants, children and adolescents across the WHO European Region and the challenging multifaceted causes behind them. Secondly, we draw attention to the stark and increasing inequities in child mortality between and within countries. Finally, we outline recommendations for research and policy urgently calling for a renewed commitment to protect and advance the health and survival of children and adolescents across the region.

Trends in neonatal, child and adolescent mortality

Using estimates from the United Nations Interagency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UNIGME), we analyzed mortality rates (deaths per 1000 live births) across the 53 member states of the WHO European Region from 2000 to 2022. These rates were compared with the median mortality rate for the WHO European Region during this period.

For the most recent five-year period of data (2018–2022), we categorized trends in mortality rates into three groups: increase, plateau, or decline. A plateau was defined as a reduction of less than 5 % in mortality rates over this period, while a decline was defined as a decrease of 5 % or more. Any rise in mortality rates, irrespective of magnitude, was classified as an increase. This methodological approach was adopted to ensure alignment with previous reports in the WHO European Region [1]. Given the wide variation in mortality rates across the WHO European Region, countries with consistently higher mortality- and particularly those both above the median and showing a plateau or increase-represent priority areas for action. Identifying these countries helps to contextualize disparities, draw attention to where the bulk of excess mortality lies, and informs where policy action is needed. Therefore, our analysis highlights countries with mortality rates above the regional median, as well as those experiencing a plateau or an increase in mortality rates during the 2018–2022 period. These findings are presented graphically to underscore disparities and trends within the region. San Marino was excluded from the analysis due to its low population, resulting in a small number of children at risk of mortality, which may lead to significant fluctuations in overall mortality rates.

Neonatal mortality rates have plateaued in 14 countries within the region (see Fig. 1, supplementary file). Even more alarmingly, in Slovakia, Albania and Kazakhstan-where mortality rates are also higher than the regional median-there has been a slow reversal of previous gains over the past five years, with mortality rates beginning to rise again. We are also witnessing recent increases in high-income countries such as Cyprus, Switzerland and Luxembourg.

The data also shows that neonatal deaths are not evenly distributed across the WHO European Region- 75 % of newborn deaths in 2022 occurred in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, with 40 % concentrated in Central Asia alone. Furthermore, 42 % of all neonatal deaths in the region occurred in just four countries: Türkiye, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, and Tajikistan [3].

Under-five mortality rates

While global under-five mortality rates continue to decline [4,5], analysis at a regional level reveals a more nuanced picture. In the WHO European Region, under-five mortality rates have plateaued in 11 countries over the past five years (see Fig. 2, supplementary file). Among these, six countries- Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, France, Poland, Slovakia, and Switzerland-have mortality rates exceeding the regional median. Additionally, two countries- Albania and Cyprus-have experienced increases in under-five mortality rates during this period. Concerningly, Romania and Ukraine are also beginning to see increases in mortality rates over the last three years of data (2020–2022).Fig. 2Under-five mortality rates in the WHO European region from 2000 to 2022Fig. 2

As with neonatal mortality, there are significant subregional differences in under-five mortality. More than three-quarters (77.6 %) of child deaths occurred in Eastern Europe and Central Asia and 40.7 % in Central Asia [3] alone. Notably, over half (52.6 %) of deaths in under-five year-olds occurred in the neonatal period.

5–14 Year mortality rates

In contrast to children under the age of five, the 5–14 years age group receives limited attention, with few resources allocated specifically to this age cohort [6]. Although mortality rates are generally lower among children over 5 years old, disparities across the WHO European Region highlight substantial opportunities to further reduce mortality within this age group. Previous studies have demonstrated that the leading causes of death among children aged 5–14 years are largely preventable or manageable with high-quality healthcare [7].

UNIGME estimates reveal that mortality rates among children aged 5 to 14 have plateaued in a total of eight countries over the past five years (see Fig. 3, supplementary file). Of these, three countries- Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Bosnia and Herzegovina-have mortality rates above the WHO European Region median. Adolescent (15–19 Year) mortality rates

Much of the recent investment in child health has been focused on reducing neonatal and under-five mortality, largely in line with the Sustainable Development Goals [8]. Adolescent mortality, however-like the 5-to-14 year age group-receives less attention. While overall adolescent mortality rates are lower than those of younger children, concerning trends are emerging here, too.

Although the WHO European Region's median mortality rate for 15 to 19-year-olds has steadily declined over the past decades (see Fig. 4, supplementary file), we are now witnessing a troubling reversal, with the median adolescent mortality rate increasing for the first time in 20 years. The stark increases in adolescent mortality in Ukraine, Armenia and Russia serve as a powerful reminder of the devastating societal impacts of geopolitical crises. However, this worrisome trend is not confined to conflict-affected countries. Across the region, 22 countries show incremental increases in adolescent mortality rates, including 13 with rates already exceeding the regional median. Additionally, nine countries have seen their adolescent mortality rates plateau, three of which- Georgia, Türkiye, and Uzbekistan-also have rates above the regional median.

The data also highlights marked gender disparities in survival. Boys and young men aged 15–24 years face a mortality risk 2.7 times higher than their female counterparts. In Eastern Europe and Central Asia, this gender gap widens further, with males being 3.2 times more likely to die during this age period [9].

Inequities between and within countries

While substantial progress has been made in many countries throughout the region, others are falling further behind. Two decades ago, countries with the highest neonatal mortality rates had rates 6 times higher than the European median and 13 times higher than the countries with the lowest mortality rates. Today, these disparities in neonatal mortality have widened significantly, with mortality rates now 10 times and 28 times higher, respectively. This widening gap underscores the persistent and growing inequities in child survival across the region, with the highest burden of child and adolescent deaths concentrated in the Eastern European and Central Asian subregions.

These inequities are not confined to differences between countries: in-country disparities are also prevalent. In countries with the highest under-five mortality rates, children in the lowest wealth quintiles are twice as likely to die before age five than children in the most affluent quintiles [3].

Emerging data also reveals stark differences in child mortality in high-income countries specifically in relation to race, ethnic background and socioeconomic status. A recent report on infant mortality in England demonstrated significantly higher infant mortality rates in the most deprived areas of the country compared to the least deprived [10]. These findings resonate with the outcomes of further studies in England and Sweden, where socioeconomic conditions and access to healthcare were critical determinants of preventable child mortality [11]. Furthermore, inequity in child mortality is often shaped by parental migration background [12]. Studies demonstrate that pregnant migrant women have a higher risk of poor perinatal outcomes, including preterm delivery, low birth weight, stillbirths and congenital anomalies-all of which are associated with increased mortality [13,14]. Wallace et al. found that children of immigrants in France experienced significantly higher infant mortality compared to native-born children, reinforcing the persistence of longstanding inequalities in health outcomes across Europe [15].

In countries affected by conflict, the inequality gap widens even further. Children and adolescents in conflict-affected areas face up to eight times the likelihood of not surviving to adulthood compared to those in more stable regions [2]. These numbers underline the critical role that socio-political stability plays in child survival outcomes.

Challenging and multi-faceted causes

It remains unclear whether these emerging trends mark a temporary shift or an inflection point signaling a potential reversal in progress. The macro-level causes behind these trends are likely to be multifaceted, affecting both high- and low-income countries. Factors such as the impact of migration and health challenges faced by migrant populations, the unmet needs of adolescents’ mental health-made worse by factors such as the COVID-9 pandemic-, and possible negative effects of social media as well as worsening socioeconomic conditions across the region are all likely contributors.

Events over the past two years highlight the importance of social and environmental determinants for child health and well-being. The global COVID-19 pandemic, the escalation of the war in Ukraine and subsequent refugee outflow, and the global economic downturn, have had a devastating impact on children and families in Europe and Central Asia. As a result, an estimated 35–40 million children are living below the national poverty line, one million children do not receive all recommended vaccines and over five million children are refugees, according to a recent UNICEF report. The same report states that four out of five children in the region are breathing polluted air and concludes that disadvantaged and excluded children can be found in every part of the region and every county, resulting in poor child survival outcomes [16].

At the same time, much of child and adolescent mortality is likely to be preventable. Ensuring high-quality maternal, antenatal, and perinatal care-supported by robust monitoring and surveillance systems-remains one of the most effective strategies for reducing child mortality. As demonstrated by the abovementioned data, the burden of child deaths is highest in the first weeks of life. Improving maternal and neonatal healthcare can therefore play a critical role in reducing stillbirths and neonatal deaths. Congenital anomalies remain a leading cause of child mortality, highlighting the need for preventive measures, early detection and management. In all age groups, expanding immunization programs to close gaps in vaccination coverage remains a critical strategy in preventing infectious disease-related deaths. Enhancing preventive measures and improving emergency and trauma care for both accidental and non-accidental injuries represent another key area for intervention.

However, effective policy responses [17] demand a more nuanced understanding of the underlying factors driving child and adolescent health trends’. Particular focus should be given to increasing mortality rates and plateaus in countries with mortality rates above the WHO regional median, as these indicate potential for improvement. However, while plateaus in countries with rates below the regional median may suggest limited room for further reduction, it is important to recognize that these averages can mask inequities within countries. Such plateaus may, in fact, signal growing inequities at the subnational level.

Significant gaps in knowledge remain concerning the key drivers of changing child and adolescent mortality trends in the region, including reasons for gender-based differences in survival and a comprehensive understanding of the equity-based differences in mortality rates in many high-resource countries.

Limitations

This commentary presents a regional overview of mortality trends based on publicly available UN IGME estimates. While we aimed to identify patterns and emerging signals, it is important to note that these estimates are subject to limitations. Firstly, we have not accounted for uncertainty around estimates. In countries with small populations or incomplete vital registration systems, mortality rates can fluctuate due to stochastic variation, and estimates may be derived from modeling based on surveys or censuses. As such, apparent increases or plateaus in mortality rates, especially where absolute changes are small, should be interpreted with caution. Our analysis does not include confidence or uncertainty intervals for individual country estimates, as a full secondary analysis was beyond the scope of this commentary. Secondly, our analysis focused on determining increases, declines, or plateaus in mortality data over the period 2018–2022 and does not account for longer term trends prior to 2018 or for annual fluctuations in mortality rates. Longer term trend analysis or analysis by annual rate of change may lead to a different categorization of countries. Finally, it is important to note that in conflict-affected countries such as Ukraine and Russia, the observed mortality increases are based on modeled estimates rather than direct civil registration data and are subject to uncertainty related to assumptions around the age distribution of conflict-related deaths. Whilst acknowledging these limitations, our approach was designed to highlight emerging trends and potential stagnation in a timely and policy-relevant manner as the region prepares for the forthcoming Child and Adolescent Health and Well-being strategy (2026–2030).

Recommendations for research and policy

Taken together, described trends, suggested causes, and observed inequities call for renewed action in addressing child and adolescent mortality.

Recommendations for research include further secondary analysis of trends across the region, analysis of the causes, and research which focuses on equity, all of which would provide timely evidence for policy makers. Specifically, a robust secondary analysis of UNIGME estimates across the WHO European Region, accounting for uncertainty, to determine trends in mortality rates over time is needed. Analysis of reliable region- and country-specific data on the leading causes of death in children would improve understanding of causes of mortality and inequity in mortality and inform effective action in healthcare and across the wider determinants. Additionally, in-country analysis of inequity in mortality rates by socioeconomic and ethnic factors as well as cross-country comparisons of mortality rates by deprivation or income may provide insights for cross-country learning.

National policies must be underpinned by high quality disaggregated data to inform action on reducing mortality and inequity in mortality. Countries need equity-focused and gender-specific interventions to adequately address the needs of the most vulnerable subgroups of the population.

Robust health information management systems are needed to monitor access and utilization of services and care quality, providing the foundation for evidence-based planning and policy decisions. Additionally, establishing mortality surveillance mechanisms, including cause-specific monitoring, is essential for identifying gaps and enabling access to high-quality healthcare services. Maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response systems can further strengthen accountability and improve the quality of care.

Countries should invest in maternal and child health, not only as a life-saving measure but also as a catalyst for long-term economic growth and societal well-being. Evidence suggests that every dollar invested can generate up to $20 in economic returns by reducing healthcare costs, improving workforce productivity, and fostering healthier, more resilient communities [18].

A comprehensive approach to strengthening health systems-including governance, workforce capacity, resource allocation, and community engagement-is essential for ensuring sustained improvements in child survival.

Additionally, and in light of the growing number of displaced populations, countries must promote cross-border dialogue and coordinated efforts to acknowledge, assess, and mitigate the impact of migration and conflict on mortality rates.

Lastly, it is time for a unified child and adolescent health strategy for the region, one that ensures comprehensive, integrated care with a particular emphasis on vulnerable and overlooked groups such as economically disadvantaged families and migrant children. The planned 2026–2030 strategy presents a vital opportunity to address existing gaps and strengthen health systems throughout the region [19].

Conclusion

While regional averages continue to show a gradual decline in overall child mortality, the growing disparities between and within countries, recent conflict-related increases, and signals of plateauing or rising mortality in some settings point to a concerning erosion of earlier gains with broad social and economic implications. Despite previous successes, the declining focus on child and adolescent health interventions threatens to reverse decades of improvement in survival outcomes. The stagnation and increase in mortality rates among infants, children and adolescents highlight a weakening of the region's child health systems and signal worsening health inequities. The significant disparities across countries, sub-regions, and even within subpopulations in individual countries underscore the need for targeted public health interventions and strategic resource allocation to address these inequities.

Every preventable death represents both personal tragedy and societal loss. Without urgent action and sustained commitment, we risk seeing these trends worsen over the next decade. Now is the time to intervene, with clear health strategies, better data through strengthened monitoring systems, and targeted policies that address the root causes of inequity and stagnation in neonatal, child and adolescent mortality.
