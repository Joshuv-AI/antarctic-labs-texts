Man with chest pain and neck swelling after bronchoscopy
Authors: Theresa Henson, Oleg Epelbaum
Published: 2023

PATIENT PRESENTATION

A 50‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain and neck swelling. Earlier in the day, he had undergone ambulatory bronchoscopy with transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) and ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) of mediastinal lymph nodes. The procedure was performed on positive pressure ventilation (PPV). After an uneventful bronchoscopy and an unremarkable post‐procedure chest x‐ray (CXR) (Figure 1), the patient was discharged. Upon forceful coughing, he suddenly became uncomfortable and noticed neck asymmetry. Vital signs were normal. On examination, neck asymmetry was no longer apparent, but there was crepitus along the right neck. New CXR followed by chest computed tomography (CT) revealed the cause of his symptoms (Figures 2 and 3).

Initial post‐bronchoscopy chest radiograph showing no immediate complications of the procedure.

Repeat chest radiograph performed in the emergency department showing air in the soft tissues of the neck region (white arrows). Air is also seen outlining the aortic knob (red arrows) as well as the trachea and right mainstem bronchus (blue arrows). These findings are consistent with the presence of pneumomediastinum complicated by subcutaneous emphysema. The sliver of air along the left heart border suggests concurrent pneumopericardium (black arrows).

Coronal reconstruction from chest computed tomography set to lung window demonstrating mediastinal air outlining the great vessels (white arrows) along with subcutaneous emphysema of the neck (red arrows). Computed tomography also confirmed the presence of pneumopericardium (black arrow).

DIAGNOSIS: POST‐BRONCHOSCOPIC PNEUMOMEDIASTINUM

Endoscopic procedures are a well‐documented cause of iatrogenic pneumomediastinum (PM). EBUS‐TBNA is a standard bronchoscopic modality wherein central airways are punctured with a needle to sample lymph nodes located beyond the lumen. Despite traversion of the airway wall, EBUS‐TBNA is exceedingly safe. 1 Concomitant TBLB, on the other hand, not uncommonly results in the more familiar complication of pneumothorax, 2 but this was not observed on the immediate post‐bronchoscopy CXR and would not have led to PM. PM was delayed in this case, and thus unlikely to have resulted from PPV. More likely, the patient's forceful coughing transiently exposed a central airway defect created during EBUS‐TBNA, which allowed air to escape into the mediastinum and then dissect into the subcutaneous tissues of the neck. The patient was managed symptomatically, as is the norm for PM in the absence of tension physiology.
