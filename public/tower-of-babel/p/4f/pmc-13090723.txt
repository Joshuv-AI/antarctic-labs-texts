Carbohydrate polymer-based biomaterials for light-triggered Photo-Immunotherapy: Design strategies, immune mechanisms, and translational outlook
Authors: Ayeskanta Mohanty, Sudhanya Nayak, Adityanarayan Mohapatra, Yong-Yeon Jeong, Chang-Moon Lee, In-Kyu Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in biomaterials have highlighted the immense potential of carbohydrate polymers (CHPs) as biocompatible, multifunctional platforms for cancer therapy. Indeed, the structural diversity, tunable chemistry, and inherent biological affinity of these polymers enable the precise design of systems for immune modulation. Moreover, light- and laser-driven strategies have emerged as powerful tools for achieving spatiotemporal control over therapeutic activation. Therefore, integrating CHPs with photothermal or photodynamic nanocomponents enables these hybrid systems to induce localized tumor ablation, trigger immunogenic cell death, and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment. These synergistic phototherapy–immunotherapy platforms eradicate primary tumors while promoting systemic antitumor immunity and immune memory formation. Therefore, this review aims to summarize recent advances in CHP-based biomaterials for light-mediated immune activation, focusing on mechanisms, design strategies, and therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, challenges in tumor-specific targeting, deep-tissue light penetration, and clinical translation are critically discussed. Finally, future perspectives are proposed for the development of next-generation, smart, light-responsive carbohydrate nanomedicines to enable precision cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most complex challenges in modern science and is characterized by several hallmarks, including uncontrolled cell proliferation, evasion of growth-inhibitory signals, programmed cell death (PCD), angiogenesis, and tissue invasion and metastasis [1]. Despite advances in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, the nonspecific delivery of anticancer drugs remains a major challenge [2]. This issue reduces treatment efficacy and causes significant off-target side effects. Although traditional cytotoxic therapies offer some benefits, these treatments often fail to produce lasting results as cancer cells evade growth-inhibitory signals, resist PCD, and foster tumor-promoting microenvironments [3]. In response to these challenges, nanomedicine has emerged as a promising platform for targeted delivery, controlled release, and reduced systemic toxicity [4]. Nanoparticles (NPs), with sizes of 1-100 nm, high surface-area-to-volume ratios [5], and tunable surface chemistry, have become central to this paradigm. While the enhanced permeability and retention effect enables passive NP accumulation in tumor tissues, the efficacy of this effect varies across tumor types and patients, highlighting the need for active targeting strategies to improve delivery [6,7].

Nanosystems such as metal NPs, dendrimers, polymeric NPs, liposomes, and protein-drug conjugates facilitate the encapsulation of diverse therapeutic agents, including hydrophobic drugs, proteins, nucleic acids, and imaging agents, improving solubility, stability, and biodistribution. Additionally, incorporating stimuli-responsive elements allows these carriers to release the encapsulated cargo in response to internal (e.g., pH, redox) [8] or external (e.g., light, heat, and ultrasound) triggers, enhancing therapeutic precision [9]. Although NP-based cancer therapies have advanced, the clinical application of these treatments remains limited by several challenges. Inorganic NPs, including gold, iron oxide, and quantum dots, often raise concerns about long-term toxicity [10], poor biodegradability, and slow clearance [11]. Conversely, organic NPs, such as liposomes and polymer-based systems, often face premature drug release [12], limited drug-loading capacity [13], and instability under physiological conditions [14]. Moreover, all NP types commonly face immune system detection, nonspecific distribution, and poor tumor penetration due to the complex, heterogeneous tumor microenvironment (TME) [5]. In contrast, carbohydrate polymers (CHPs), naturally derived polysaccharides, and sugar-based macromolecules have attracted significant attention for their exceptional biological functionality [15]. These polymers, chitosan (CS), starch, cellulose, hyaluronic acid (HA), dextran, alginate, and pectin (PEC), can be engineered into diverse delivery systems, including NPs, micelles, hydrogels [16], and polymer-drug conjugates [9]. The structural modifiability of CHPs enables functionalization with ligands or responsive linkers, enabling interaction with tumor-associated receptors such as cellular differentiation 44 (CD44) [17] or an asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) [18], thereby enhancing tumor-specific accumulation [19]. Furthermore, CHPs provide not only passive transport but also intrinsic therapeutic effects; for example, β-glucans function as immune adjuvants by activating dendritic cells (DCs) and enhancing immune surveillance. The natural origin, degradability, and low immunogenicity make DCs suitable for repeated administration without triggering adverse immune responses [20]. This functional versatility enables carbohydrate-based nanocarriers to act as precision-targeted delivery platforms and active modulators of tumor biology. While numerous reviews discuss light-activated nanosystems, most focus on photosensitizers or general nanomaterials rather than the unique contributions of CHPs. This functional versatility enables carbohydrate-based nanocarriers to act as precision-targeted delivery platforms and active modulators of tumor biology, a perspective that is less explored in prior reviews. Therefore, this review aims to highlight the multifaceted role of CHPs in cancer therapy and trace the evolution of light-triggered drug-release systems, emphasizing the expanding applications of these systems in immunomodulation. By integrating insights into structural advantages, therapeutic strategies, and translational opportunities, this review offers a comprehensive view of how these systems synergize to reprogram the TME. Furthermore, this review highlights the emerging paradigm of light-triggered immunomodulation, providing a critical assessment of current advances and a perspective on the potential of this technique to guide next-generation targeted and immune-modulated oncology. Advantages of carbohydrate polymers

Carbohydrate-based nanomaterials have emerged as a transformative platform in cancer therapy, bridging biocompatible materials science and precision nanomedicine. Derived from natural polysaccharides or chemically modified carbohydrate moieties, these nanocarriers possess attributes that uniquely enable them to overcome longstanding challenges in oncology, including nonspecific drug distribution, systemic toxicity, and multidrug resistance [21]. Polysaccharides, including CS, HA, alginate, dextran, and cyclodextrins, are abundant, cost-effective, and biocompatible, making them highly suitable for large-scale clinical applications. Moreover, the structural backbones of polysaccharides, which are enriched with hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino moieties, provide versatile sites for chemical modification, enabling conjugation with therapeutic agents, targeting ligands, and stimuli-responsive components. These materials can be processed into diverse nanostructures, including NPs, micelles, hydrogels, nanogels, and polymer-drug conjugates, each tailored for specific therapeutic applications [22].

The inherent biodegradability of CHPs, which decompose into sugars, including glucose and uronic acid derivatives, minimizes long-term toxicity and eliminates concerns about bioaccumulation commonly associated with synthetic nanomaterials [23]. These materials actively enhance targeting efficiency. Meanwhile, tumor-specific delivery occurs through multiple mechanisms, including ligand-receptor interactions [24], metabolic targeting [25], and lectin-mediated binding [26]. For instance, HA exhibits high affinity for CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed on many cancer cells and cancer stem cells, thereby enabling selective delivery and improved therapeutic uptake [27].

Building on these advantages, hybrid CHP-based nanocarriers have expanded the functional landscape of nanomedicine. These systems integrate polysaccharides with functional materials, including synthetic polymers, proteins, lipids, and inorganic NPs, combining the biocompatibility of carbohydrates with enhanced drug-loading, stability, and

multifunctionality of other platforms [28]. In comparison, liposomal systems are clinically validated and offer a well-defined architecture, efficient encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic agents, and scalable manufacturing processes. Nevertheless, liposomes may experience premature drug leakage, stability challenges during storage, and rapid clearance in the absence of appropriate surface modifications [29]. Synthetic polymers provide precise molecular weight control, tunable degradation kinetics, and reproducible large-scale fabrication, all of which are advantageous for regulatory compliance. However, concerns related to long-term biocompatibility, accumulation, and potential cytotoxicity remain considerations for clinical translation. Overall, CHPs provide a biologically interactive and immunologically favorable platform for photo-immune activation, whereas liposomes and synthetic polymers offer superior structural control and manufacturing consistency. Therefore, therapeutic objectives, safety requirements, and translational feasibility should be employed to guide the optimal material selection. Core-shell architectures are particularly common, featuring inorganic cores, such as mesoporous silica or gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), coated with polysaccharide shells to improve targeting and biocompatibility while maintaining payload efficiency [30]. For example, AuNPs functionalized with dextran or pullulan exhibit strong photothermal efficacy while maintaining biological safety [31]. Protein-polysaccharide hybrids, including albumin-carboxymethylcellulose composites, enable effective delivery of hydrophobic drugs via receptor-mediated transport and controlled release [32]. Lipid-polymer hybrids incorporating polysaccharide shells offer improved colloidal stability and responsiveness to tumor-specific stimuli such as acidic pH or redox gradients [33].

These multifunctional hybrids support the co-delivery of chemotherapeutics and gene therapies, broadening the therapeutic index. CS-based polyplexes have effectively delivered siRNA targeting platelet-derived growth factor-D (PDGF-D) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-beta (PDGFR-β) in breast cancer models, resulting in reduced gene expression and tumor volume, along with increased apoptosis [34]. Similarly, hydroxyethyl starch (HES)-based systems co-loaded with doxorubicin (DOX) and chlorin e6 (Ce6), or with cholesterol-modified polymers encapsulating fucoxanthin and Twist siRNA, exhibit combined chemotherapeutic and gene-silencing effects [35]. Other strategies employ enzyme-prodrug systems, including acetylated dextran conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, to activate cytotoxic agents under acidic tumor conditions and induce cell apoptosis in vitro. These innovations aid in overcoming drug resistance and enable synergistic therapeutic action [36].

In addition to therapeutic applications, hybrid carbohydrate systems are being engineered for theranostics, integrating diagnostics and treatment within a single platform. For instance, dextran-coated iron oxide NPs facilitate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided drug delivery. Immune-targeting applications are also advancing, with mannan or pullulan-modified nanocarriers co-delivering tumor antigens and immunomodulatory genes to DCs for cancer vaccination [37]. Functional enhancements, including stimuli-responsive drug release, dual-targeting via ligand conjugation, and prolonged circulation through hydrophilic stealth coatings such as HA, collectively improve pharmacokinetics and tumor accumulation. Since many of these polysaccharide components are approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or designated as generally recognized as safe (GRAS), the clinical translation of hybrid carbohydrate-based nanocarriers appears highly promising. Recent studies highlight the potential of hybrid carbohydrate-based nanocarriers to overcome therapeutic resistance, shrink tumors, activate immune responses, and prevent relapse, positioning these components as next-generation nanoplatforms for precision and personalized oncology [38]. Light-triggered phototherapy with CHPs

Lights are powerful tools in modern medicine, offering precise, minimally invasive approaches for diagnosis and therapy. This review focuses on light-responsive CHP systems because light offers a level of external control that is difficult to achieve with other stimuli. Moreover, treatment accuracy and safety are critical in cancer therapy. Unlike internal triggers such as pH, redox gradients, or enzymes, which can vary between tumor types and even among patients, light can be precisely regulated in terms of wavelength, intensity, duration, and irradiation site, this allows therapeutic activation to occur only when and where needed. The capacity to deliver controlled energy at defined wavelengths has enabled applications ranging from surgical ablation and tissue repair to photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and laser-assisted drug delivery. These systems use external light, typically in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum (650-950 nm), to noninvasively penetrate tissue and activate therapeutic payloads at the target site [39]. PDT uses light-activated photosensitizers to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce tumor cell death, whereas PTT employs light-responsive agents to generate localized heat, thereby inducing cytotoxic effects. PDT uses light-activated photosensitizer (PS) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce tumor cell death, whereas PTT employs photothermal agent (PTA) agents to generate localized heat, thereby inducing cytotoxic effects. However, both modalities are limited by the poor aqueous solubility, tumor accumulation, and rapid systemic clearance of "naked" therapeutic agents. To address this, carbohydrate-based nanoplatforms have been developed as versatile carriers, embedding photosensitive nanomaterials or light-cleavable chemical linkers that harness photothermal conversion, photochemical cleavage, or ROS generation to release drugs with precise spatial and temporal control [40]. CHPs, such as dextran, CS and HA, are not just passive carriers but also structural necessities that help prevent aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). Hydrophobic agents like Chlorin e6 (Ce6) or IR780 tend to undergo π-π stacking in physiological fluids, which dissipate their excited-state energy as non-radiative decay rather than producing ROS or heat. By providing a hydrophilic, sterically hindered scaffold, CHPs maintain these agents in a monodisperse state, thereby maximizing their loading capacity and ensuring high quantum yields that are unachievable with the free drugs alone. Beyond structural stabilization, the "inert" nature of the polymer backbone serves as a critical "safety lock" for precise spatiotemporal activation. Free PS and PTA molecules distribute non-selectively throughout the body, often leading to severe systemic photosensitivity and off-target thermal damage. In contrast, CHPs systems enable smart and controlled drug delivery, where therapeutic activation occurs in response to tumor-specific biological signals. For instance, HA-based nanocarriers remain stable during circulation but are activated when degraded by hyaluronidase (HAase), which is highly expressed in the TME. Similarly, chitosan-based systems respond to the acidic pH of tumors, where polymer protonation triggers drug release. This controlled activation ensures that light-induced therapeutic effects are mainly localized at the tumor site, reducing damage to healthy tissues and improving treatment precision. Compared with conventional CHPs, laser-triggered systems provide superior precision, reducing collateral damage while maximizing local therapeutic concentrations. The incorporation of CHPs improves biocompatibility and targeting, while broadening therapeutic potential through immune modulation, specifically by countering immunosuppressive TMEs. Recent innovations demonstrate that localized immune stimulation using laser-triggered carbohydrate systems can overcome immune evasion and synergize with existing immunotherapies [25]. By outlining the structural advantages, therapeutic strategies, and translational potential of these systems, we highlight how this synergy can advance precision and immune-modulated oncology.

NIR light-induced PTT leverages CHPs as biocompatible carriers and functional linkers for photothermal agents. Under NIR irradiation (NIR-I: 650-950 nm; NIR-II: 1000-1350 nm), nanoplatforms incorporating chromophores such as IR780, indocyanine green (ICG), CuS NPs, and gold nanorods absorb photons and release localized heat (≥ 42 °C) to denature proteins, disrupt membranes, and induce apoptosis or necrosis. CHPs stabilize photothermal dyes, enhance tumor targeting, and serve as photosensitive linkers that soften or degrade under NIR-induced heating, enabling controlled drug release. While NIR-I PTT is limited by shallow tissue penetration (≤ 1 cm) and rapid photobleaching, the NIR-II region (1000-1700 nm) offers deeper penetration, reduced light scattering, and improved signal-to-background contrast. Conversely, to overcome the constraints of uneven heat distribution in PTT, PDT provides a complementary cytotoxic mechanism through ROS generation. Meanwhile, combining PTT and PDT within a single nanoplatform can leverage their synergy: PTT increases tumor perfusion and oxygenation to enhance type II PDT, while PDT provides ROS-mediated cytotoxicity in regions inadequately heated by PTT. Collectively, CHP nanoplatforms leverage complementary PTT/PDT mechanisms to enhance therapeutic efficacy through spatiotemporal control [34]. However, the advantages of light activation must be discussed alongside the associated challenges. One major limitation is the depth of tissue penetration, especially at visible wavelengths, which restricts the treatment of deeply located tumors. Although NIR improves penetration, the overall depth remains limited. Light scattering and absorption by biological tissues can reduce therapeutic efficiency, and excessive irradiation may cause local overheating or unintended damage to the surrounding tissues. Therefore, we systematically discussed CHPs used in laser-triggered advanced strategies for enhanced drug delivery, highlighting the mechanistic advantages of laser activation in cancer therapy.

Chitosan

CS-based NPs are a new class of nanomaterials derived from CS, a naturally occurring, positively charged polysaccharide. CS is produced by partially deacetylating chitin, which is abundant in crustacean exoskeletons and fungal cell walls [41]. These NPs are valued in biomedical research for the associated properties of biodegradability, biocompatibility, and low toxicity. These properties make these nanomaterials suitable and safe for applications such as drug delivery, tissue engineering, and cancer treatment. The positive surface charge and strong mucoadhesive properties of CS NPs enable adhesion to biological membranes, facilitating controlled and targeted drug delivery [42]. For example, CS-coated bismuth sulfide (BiNS) NPs with a rhamnolipid core provide dual protection in the gastrointestinal tract: the CS shell protects BiNS (a photothermal agent) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) from premature degradation, while β-glycosidase-triggered degradation in the TME enhances uptake. Meanwhile, PTT combined with chemotherapy improves tumor penetration and reduces systemic toxicity [43]. CS serves a key function in pH-sensitive hydrogels, where carboxymethylated CS forms pH-labile Schiff base linkages that enable TME-triggered disassembly [44]. In dual-sensitive CS hydrogels, the pH-responsive component is the Schiff base (imine, –C=N–) linkage formed between carboxymethyl CS and aldehyde crosslinkers. While stable at physiological pH (7.4), this bond undergoes acid-catalyzed hydrolysis in the mildly acidic TME (pH 6.5) or endosomes (pH 5-6), leading to hydrogel disassembly and controlled drug release. Thus, the imine linkage acts as the pH-sensitive molecular switch. Another example involves CS-Codium fragile polysaccharide (CFP)-ICG NPs, which are formed by combining CS with CFP and the NIR dye ICG (Fig. 3). Through electrostatic complexation between positively charged CS and negatively charged CFP, stable NPs (CC-ICG) are formed, providing colloidal stability and promoting cellular uptake. This charge-driven nanocomplexation enables measurable ICG encapsulation (5.3%, 107.8 μg per 4.5 mg of NPs) and markedly enhances intracellular fluorescence compared with free ICG, demonstrating improved loading efficiency and delivery. The system further provides controllable activation, with ICG remaining inert until exposed to 808 nm NIR irradiation at 1.5 W/cm2 for 5 min, which was identified as the optimal condition for both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Under these conditions, CC-ICG-generated rapid, dose and power-dependent temperature elevations (63-68 °C), inducing apoptotic and necrotic tumor cell death and complete tumor remission in vivo, whereas NPs without NIR exposure produced no cytotoxic effects. Thus, CS confers dual precision, i.e., structural control via electrostatic assembly and temporal control via externally regulated NIR activation, transforming a conventional photosensitizer into a tumor-localized, on-demand therapeutic system that minimizes off-target toxicity while maximizing photothermal efficacy [45]. CS provides exceptional muco-adhesion, electrostatic stability for negatively charged therapeutic cargo, and inherent immunomodulatory properties that stimulate DC maturation. Starch

Starch and the associated derivatives are increasingly used in cancer therapy for their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, serving as nanocarriers for chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and DOX to enhance tumor-specific delivery and reduce systemic side effects [6]. Starch-based nanomaterials, particularly those that are laser-responsive, offer great promise for cancer therapy. A study developed a starch-derived photothermal nanoplatform by encapsulating iodine in acetylated starch NPs for colorectal cancer therapy (Fig. 4). Starch exhibits structurally encoded advantages in loading capacity, controllable delivery, and precise activation of photothermal agents due to the V-type helical architecture of amylose, which forms hydrophobic cavities that encapsulate iodine and stabilize polyiodide species with high efficiency [iodine drug loading content (DLC) 19.6%, encapsulation efficiency (EE) 83%]. Acetylation further enhances hydrophobic interactions, enabling co-loading of the IDO1 inhibitor NLG919 while maintaining NP stability. The iodine-starch charge transfer complex confers broad NIR absorbance, allowing efficient photothermal conversion under 808 nm irradiation at a power density of 2 W/cm2 for 10 min, reaching 50-52 °C, an optimal mild hyperthermia range sufficient to induce ICD while minimizing collateral damage. Importantly, starch enables precise thermal regulation through reversible thermochromic behavior, wherein NIR-induced heating partially unfolds the amylose helix, releasing iodine and reducing absorbance, while cooling restores the helical structure and color, thereby providing intrinsic temperature feedback and reducing the risk of overheating. Simultaneously, NIR exposure accelerates NLG919 release (2 W/cm2, 10 min), confirming photothermal-triggered drug delivery [46]. In contrast, HES based systems represent a more modular advancement in starch chemistry. Chemical hydroxyethyl substitution disrupts native crystallinity and enhances aqueous stability, enabling the formation of amphiphilic block copolymers such as HES-polycaprolactone (PCL) that self-assemble into stable core-shell NPs. Unlike the intrinsic chromogenicity of iodine-starch complexes, HES-based platforms rely on externally incorporated photothermal dyes such as ICG, typically achieving balanced loading (5% for both ICG and co-loaded immunomodulators) with high encapsulation efficiency (>90% for ICG). Importantly, the polyester/PCL segment introduces ester linkages that undergo accelerated hydrolysis under mildly acidic conditions (pH 5-6), providing endogenous pH-responsive release in addition to exogenous NIR triggering. This dual responsiveness contrasts with the starch–iodine construct, in which release is primarily thermally driven and is not intrinsically pH-cleavable [47].

Critically, while native or acetylated starch excels in high photothermal payload integration and reversible temperature precision without reliance on synthetic chromophores, starch suffers from limited mechanical robustness [48], rapid enzymatic degradation in vivo [49], modest loading efficiency for hydrophobic drugs [50], and absence of inherent targeting ligands [51]. HES based systems, by comparison, offer improved colloidal stability, tunable degradation kinetics, and more predictable systemic performance, yet depend on external dyes for photothermal activity and may dilute the structural simplicity that makes starch appealing. Thus, the progression from starch to HES reflects a broader design trade-off in carbohydrate nanomedicine: balancing intrinsic supramolecular photothermal functionality against engineered modular responsiveness. Optimizing future platforms will likely require integrating the high loading and chromogenic precision of starch helicity with the architectural stability and dual-trigger control characteristic of HES-derived hybrids to achieve clinically translatable, immune-compatible phototherapeutic systems.

Heparin

Heparin, a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan traditionally used as an anticoagulant, has been developed into NPs that serve as promising platforms for combining PTT and PDT in cancer immunotherapy, leveraging the biocompatibility, negative charge, and drug-conjugation capacity of heparin [52]. In oncology, heparin provides therapeutic benefits in addition to anticoagulation through several mechanisms: (1) inhibiting angiogenesis by sequestering pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor-2 [53], which would otherwise be released from the extracellular matrix (ECM) during heparan sulfate degradation; (2) suppressing metastasis by interfering with P-/L-selectin-mediated cancer cell adhesion to blood vessels and platelets [54,55]; (3) blocking heparanase to prevent ECM degradation that promotes tumor dissemination and growth factor release [56]; (4) enhancing immune responses by increasing natural killer cell infiltration and activity, countering heparanase-induced immune suppression [57]. These mechanisms enhance the synergy between PDT and PTT, where localized light activation generates cytotoxic ROS (PDT) or hyperthermia (PTT) to destroy tumors, while heparin-mediated pathways limit metastasis and remodel the TME [9]. In the reported low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) modified ICG liposomal platform (LMWH-ICG-Lip), ICG was efficiently encapsulated within cationic liposomes (entrapment efficiency: 65-68%) [58]. In comparison, LMWH was electrostatically adsorbed onto the liposomal surface with a high binding efficiency (88%), thereby improving colloidal stability and prolonging systemic circulation. The nanoscale size (143 nm) enabled passive tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, leading to significantly enhanced intratumoral fluorescence (12.6-fold higher than free ICG at 12 h), which directly translates into higher localized photothermal conversion upon irradiation. Under 808 nm NIR exposure at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 for 5 min, the LMWH-CG-Lip system rapidly increased the tumor temperature to 55 °C, confirming efficient and spatially controlled photothermal activation. Importantly, this delivery system allowed temporal control of irradiation, ensuring that activation occurred preferentially at the tumor site rather than systemically. In addition to delivery, LMWH alone contributed to antimetastatic functionality by blocking platelet tumor cell adhesion and reducing tumor cell migration and invasion, thereby mitigating the hypoxia-induced metastatic risk associated with phototherapy. Thus, heparin in this platform exemplifies a dual-functional CHP that integrates high loading capacity, prolonged circulation, tumor-selective accumulation, controllable NIR activation, and biological modulation to optimize both therapeutic precision and safety.

The major advantages of heparin include the associated intrinsic antiangiogenic and antimetastatic bioactivity, profound affinity for various growth factors, and high charge density that facilitates electrostatic drug complexation. Conversely, the primary disadvantage of heparin is the associated inherent strong anticoagulant activity, which poses a severe risk of off-target bleeding or hemorrhagic complications if the polymer is not carefully modified or localized exclusively to the TME.

Gellan gum

Gellan gum is an anionic polysaccharide of microbial origin composed of repeating tetrasaccharide blocks of glucose, glucuronic acid, and rhamnose [59]. Since gellan gum has strong hydrophilicity, the capacity for ion-mediated gel formation, and a close resemblance to natural ECM sugars, this polysaccharide is increasingly being explored as a biocompatible carbohydrate scaffold for phototherapy-based cancer therapies [60].

More specifically, the water-rich gellan gum network supports injectable formulations that solidify in situ, anchoring phototherapeutic cargos within the tumor and reducing the rapid diffusion and systemic elimination typically seen with free photothermal or photodynamic nanomaterials [61]. Razack et al. reported a psyllium-gellan injectable BNG, mesoporous silica NPs (mSiO2) with high surface area and porosity were loaded with caffeic acid (CA) and the NIR photosensitizer IR 820, then uniformly dispersed within the gellan matrix, enabling high loading efficiency, mechanical reinforcement, and controlled release compared to bare nanocomplexes. Upon 808 nm irradiation, the temperature increased at 0.3–1.25 W/cm2 from 32 °C to 53 °C within 4 min, with 1.25 W/cm2 for 4 min identified as optimal for cancer ablation. Notably, embedding the nanocomplex within the gel further amplified heat localization, reaching 58 °C under the same conditions, underscoring the ability of the matrix to confine thermal energy within the TME. Importantly, the pH-responsive swelling behavior of the hydrogel modulates CA diffusion under physiological versus tumor-mimetic conditions, reducing burst release while permitting TME-adaptive drug liberation. At physiological pH, the hydrogel maintains relatively stable swelling, limiting premature diffusion of CA and reducing burst release. In contrast, under mildly acidic conditions characteristic of the TME, increased swelling and network relaxation facilitate enhanced CA diffusion, enabling localized, tumor-adaptive drug liberation. More acidic conditions (pH 5-6), which mimic endosomal/lysosomal compartments following cellular uptake, may further accelerate release by enhancing polymer ionization and loosening the matrix. Alginate

Alginate, an anionic polysaccharide extracted from brown algae, has emerged as a versatile biopolymer for developing cancer-targeted nanomaterials in PDT and PTT. The linear chain in alginate, composed of alternating β-D-mannuronic and α-L-guluronic acid units, crosslinks in a pH-dependent manner upon exposure to divalent cations such as calcium, forming stable hydrogel networks with tunable pore sizes and degradation rates [63]. Under physiological conditions, alginate hydrogels remain structurally stable if ionic crosslinks are preserved; however, gradual ion exchange (Na + replacing Ca2+) leads to progressive network relaxation, increased porosity, and controlled erosion. This mechanism enables sustained release while maintaining initial depot integrity. Importantly, alginate lacks mammalian enzymatic degradation pathways, and the breakdown of alginate is primarily driven by ionic diffusion, contributing to predictable yet relatively slow matrix destabilization over time.

These physicochemical attributes make alginate particularly attractive for the delivery of photosensitizers. The high water content and three-dimensional porous network of alginate also enable efficient encapsulation and spatial segregation of multiple nanovectors, preventing premature interactions [64].

In the study by Lee et al., an injectable calcium-crosslinked alginate composite hydrogel was engineered to co-entrap ICG-loaded perfluorocarbon (PFOB) nanoemulsions (IPNEs) and camptothecin (CPT)-doped chitosan nanoparticles (CCNPs), thereby achieving high loading stability while preventing mutual interference between the phototherapeutic and chemotherapeutic components as depicted in Fig. 6. Upon 808 nm NIR irradiation at 6 W cm−2, the hydrogel exhibited effective photothermal responses, reaching cytotoxic temperature ranges (>45-60 °C within minutes, depending on dose), alongside enhanced photodynamic activity. Notably, the system produced singlet oxygen in a dose-dependent manner, with the composite hydrogel generating substantially higher ROS levels than free ICG due to the oxygen-dissolving capacity of PFOB. This oxygen enrichment enhanced photosensitization efficiency under defined irradiation parameters and enabled robust tumor suppression in vivo following only 60 s of laser exposure. Hydrogel stability and release behavior were tightly coupled with environmental and external stimuli. Under static conditions, particularly at 4 °C, CPT leakage remained minimal (<10%), indicating strong retention within the calcium-crosslinked network. At physiological temperature (37 °C), partial ion exchange and matrix relaxation enabled a moderate, sustained release. Under NIR irradiation, rapid photothermal heating increased matrix fluidity and transiently weakened ionic crosslinks, thereby promoting enhanced control over CPT release without causing complete structural collapse [65].

However, limitations persist, including limited intrinsic cellular uptake, dependence on ion exchange for degradation, and potential long-term structural instability in vivo as calcium ions gradually diffuse away. These considerations highlight the strong potential of alginate for localized photochemotherapy while underscoring the need for strategies that enhance mechanical persistence and active targeting in translational applications.

Hyaluronic acid

Recent advances in spatiotemporal therapy highlight HA as a valuable multifunctional material that offers effective tumor targeting and structural support for nanotherapeutics. The HA coating serves two main functions: enhancing tumor accumulation and cellular uptake via CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis, a pathway often upregulated in various cancers, such as 4T1 breast tumors [66]. Additionally, the HA coating serves as a TME-sensitive barrier that is selectively degraded by hyaluronidase (HAase), thereby enabling controlled disassembly and aggregation of the photosensitizer core [67]. In addition to targeting, HA functions as a protective shell responsive to TME. In a shikonin-loaded in the heparin-cystamine-glutathione (GSH) conjugated polymer matrix assembled onto a copper oxide nanocore and further coated with HA (SHK/HCG@CuOx/HA) as illustrated in Fig. 7, HA electrostatically coats the positively charged core, stabilizing the construct at physiological pH 7.4 while preventing premature drug leakage and aggregation [68]. Upon exposure to mildly acidic TME conditions (pH 6.5) and HAase-rich environments, HA undergoes enzymatic degradation and charge reversal, triggering structural loosening, enhanced cellular penetration, and exposure of the catalytic CuOx core. This process enables glutathione depletion and Cu + generation, which drives Fenton-like reactions to produce highly cytotoxic •OH radicals, while also inducing intracellular ROS amplification through redox cycling. A parallel strategy is employed in the CS/HA co-assembled nanocomposite encapsulating alkynyl-modified Au NPs and methylene blue (MB), the CS/HA-Alk-GNP-MB (CHAM) nanoplatform, where HA and CS co-assemble to encapsulate Au NPs and MB [69]. In this system, HA again provides CD44-mediated targeting and improved colloidal stability, yielding uniform 255 nm nanocomposites with superior dispersion compared with non-HA controls. MB loading efficiency is high, as indicated by the preserved absorption at 674 nm and strong NIR absorbance above 700-808 nm, reflecting efficient co-incorporation of both photothermal and photodynamic components. CHAM reaches 52-53 °C in vivo under 808 nm irradiation at 2.5 W cm−2 for 5 min, with a photothermal conversion efficiency of 45%, which is markedly higher than that of any associated non-alkynyl analogues. Simultaneously, MB activation under the same 808 nm conditions generates intracellular ROS, primarily singlet oxygen (1O2), and downstream oxidative species, as detected by 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH DA)-a widely used cell-permeable fluorescent probe that detects intracellular ROS, with CHAM producing significantly higher ROS levels than free MB or non-optimized controls. Therefore, laser exposure drives both thermal ablation and ROS-mediated collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential, demonstrating a tightly synchronized PTT/PDT mechanism [69].

The HA-based systems demonstrate clear advantages over passive polysaccharides: (i) intrinsic CD44 targeting enhances tumor-selective uptake; (ii) enzyme-responsive degradation enables controlled disassembly within the TME; (iii) electrostatic shielding improves systemic stability and circulation; (iv) spatiotemporal precision is achieved by coupling TME-triggered destabilization with defined laser parameters (808 or 980 nm; 1.2-2.5 W cm−2; 5 min irradiation). However, limitations remain. HA undergoes rapid in vivo turnover and displays batch-dependent variability; high-molecular-weight HA is costly to produce; fragmented low-molecular-weight HA may paradoxically promote angiogenesis or inflammatory signaling. Furthermore, although ROS amplification is substantial under irradiation, therapeutic efficacy remains dependent on the depth of external light penetration and oxygen availability, potentially limiting performance in hypoxic cores unless combined with redox-modulating CDT components, as in the CuOx system.

Cellulose

Cellulose is a promising natural polymer scaffold for PTT and PDT in cancer, due to the associated semi-crystalline nanofibrillar structure, strong mechanical properties, and abundant surface hydroxyl groups, which enable extensive chemical modifications while maintaining biosafety, water retention, and enzymatic biodegradability [70]. Compared with other polysaccharides such as dextrin or HA, the higher rigidity and thermal stability of cellulose make this polymer suitable for fabricating three-dimensional (3D) nanocarriers or implantable devices that preserve photosensitizer integrity under continuous irradiation [71]. Moreover, the rich surface chemistry of cellulose enables charge engineering, metal–ligand binding, hydrophobic modification, and antifouling hydration, all of which are beneficial for TME-responsive nanomedicine [70]. Quaternized microcrystalline cellulose (QMCC) exemplifies how surface functionalization can transform cellulose into a high-performance carrier for photosensitizers. Indeed, QMCC establishes strong electrostatic interactions with anionic ICG through tunable quaternary ammonium substitution, achieving high loading efficiency while suppressing aggregation-induced quenching. This stabilization broadens the NIR absorption window, enhancing photothermal conversion efficiency (up to 55%) and preserving singlet oxygen generation capacity (Fig. 8A). Under 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W cm−2, 5 min), the QMCC–ICG platform demonstrates rapid photothermal heating and concurrent photodynamic activation, producing synergistic PTT/PDT effects. Importantly, premature dye leakage remains below 10%, reflecting tight photosensitizer retention and controlled delivery. Intratumoral administration in vivo followed by 808 nm irradiation (1000 mW cm−2, 5 min) yields strong photoacoustic contrast and achieves near-complete tumor ablation. Collectively, cellulose functions not merely as a passive matrix but as a structural regulator of dye aggregation, optical stability, and ROS conversion under defined irradiation parameters. However, the strengths of cellulose in rigidity and thermal stability are offset by the associated intrinsic insolubility in water and most organic solvents, necessitating chemical derivatization before biomedical use. Pectin

Unlike positively charged CS, PEC is a chemically sophisticated, plant-derived polysaccharide rich in galacturonic acid that integrates molecular recognition, ionic reactivity, and mechanical adaptability [74]. The carboxylated backbone and branched rhamnogalacturonan structures in PEC enable metal chelation, preserve hydrophilicity, and adjust charge density in response to pH changes. Moreover, PEC interacts with metals, photosensitizers, and proteins via non-covalent coordination, simultaneously acting as a stabilizer, reductant, and regulator, a combination of properties rarely found in other CHPs. Additionally, the biocompatibility, negative surface charge, and mucoadhesive characteristics of PEC enhance selective tumor retention and enzymatic degradation, making PEC an environmentally sustainable and low-toxicity matrix for light-driven nanomedicine [75,76]. Cisneros et al. reported that PEC-coated gold (Au@PEC) and silver (Ag@PEC) NPs synthesized via green chemistry approaches form 1:1 complex with riboflavin, thereby enhancing the triplet excited-state yield and amplifying ROS production under visible-light irradiation (Fig. 8B). Photodynamic activation at an irradiance of 9.6 mW cm−2 for 2 h significantly increased cytotoxicity in HeLa cells while maintaining minimal dark toxicity. Mechanistically, singlet oxygen (1O2) was the predominant ROS, with additional contributions from superoxide (O2•-) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Intracellular ROS amplification, validated by the DCFH-DA fluorescence assay, confirmed a 5.7-fold higher ROS level than in the non-irradiated NP group, indicating the induction of enhanced oxidative stress following irradiation in vitro [73].

Building on this, hybrid nanogels exploit the iron-chelating ability of PEC to generate a redox-active microenvironment under NIR irradiation. This system synergistically induced Fenton-like hydroxyl radical generation and photothermal heating, resulting in over 90% tumor inhibition in murine melanoma models. The hydrated, negatively charged PEC matrix preserved intratumoral dispersion while promoting iron cycling and ROS diffusion, highlighting the associated dual catalytic and structural roles. In this context, PEC functions not merely as a passive coating but as a dynamic interfacial regulator that modulates protein adsorption, stabilizes the photosensitizer, and fine-tunes ROS generation under precisely defined irradiation conditions. However, PEC exhibits intrinsic structural heterogeneity governed by plant source and degree of esterification, complicating reproducible formulation. Moreover, the comparatively weak mechanical properties of PEC relative to cellulose or CS further limit the application of this polysaccharide in load-bearing or implantable systems, positioning PEC as a highly tunable yet structurally softer photodynamic carrier.

Dextran

Among CHPs, dextran offers a versatile, biocompatible scaffold that is increasingly valued for the associated synergistic PTT and PDT effects. The α-1,6-glucose backbone in dextran provides abundant hydroxyl groups for chemical modifications, including oxidation, thiolation, and β-cyclodextrin grafting [77]. These functional groups provide dextran with tunable hydrophilicity, charge neutrality, and supramolecular host-guest properties [78]. Meanwhile, these tunable properties enhance aqueous solubility, reduce immune system recognition, and prolong the systemic circulation of photoactive nanocomposites compared with more rigid or cationic polysaccharides such as CS or HA. The flexible, nonionic nature of dextran prevents aggregation-induced quenching of conjugated photosensitizers and plasmonic nanostructures, preserving deep-tissue NIR absorption and sustaining singlet oxygen generation [79]. Functionalized dextran derivatives are promising multifunctional carriers for phototherapy. For example, Liang et al. developed a hybrid NP composed of carboxymethyl-CS and β-cyclodextrin, whose cavities encapsulate the photosensitizer ICG and the antibiotic chloramphenicol (Fig. 9). The polysaccharide backbone directs mitochondrial targeting and enzymatic degradation. Pullulan

Pullulan is a well-defined, chemically versatile polysaccharide composed of α-(1 → 6)-linked maltotriose units, conferring amphiphilic properties favorable for nanoscale self-assembly [82]. Unlike CS or HA, pullulan is neutrally charged, water-soluble, and has a smooth molecular conformation, reducing nonspecific protein adsorption and enhancing stability in blood circulation. The multiple hydroxyl groups in pullulan enable modification via hydrophobic acylation, dye conjugation, and receptor-targeted derivatization, making this polysaccharide an effective scaffold for stimuli-responsive phototherapeutic agents [79]. Pullulan exhibits strong affinity for ASGPRs that serves as the hepatocyte surface receptor that recognizes pullulan-based carriers and promotes their uptake into liver cells by receptor-mediated endocytosis, which are abundantly expressed on hepatocytes, offering a natural, selective targeting mechanism for liver cancer, a property rarely seen in other CHPs [83]. The potency of this design is exemplified by Babu et al., who developed phthalyl-pullulan NPs encapsulating IR780 and the immune adjuvant R848 (PIR NPs) to integrate receptor-targeted delivery with photothermal immunotherapy; in this system, hydrophobically modified pullulan self-assembles into stable NPs (150 nm) capable of efficiently loading the NIR dye IR780 (loading efficiency 76%) while maintaining excellent colloidal stability in serum [84]. Upon NIR irradiation at 808 nm with a power density of 1.5 W cm−2 for 5 min, the NPs exhibit strong photothermal conversion (44.4% efficiency), rapidly increasing the tumor temperature to 50 °C, which is sufficient to induce tumor cell ablation and trigger ICD. This photothermal activation simultaneously accelerates the release of the immunostimulatory agent R848 (80% release after irradiation versus 48% without irradiation), enabling spatiotemporally controlled therapy. Overview of various CHPs in Phototherapy

Light-induced immunomodulation via carbohydrate nanoplatforms

Laser-enhanced photoimmunotherapy (PIT) combines light-based precision with immune modulation to induce localized tumor ablation and systemic antitumor responses. This approach primarily involves two complementary phototherapeutic mechanisms, PTT and PDT, each triggered by laser irradiation at specific wavelengths. In PDT, laser-activated photosensitizers generate ROS that damage cellular components, disrupt tumor vasculature, and trigger ICD. Conversely, PTT converts light energy into localized heat, disrupting tumor cell membranes, denaturing proteins, and releasing danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Thus, combining PTT and PDT into a single nanoplatform or therapeutic session enhances immune activation by promoting antigen release, DC maturation, and T cell recruitment [100]. Additionally, laser parameters, such as wavelength, intensity, and exposure time, can be precisely adjusted to control therapeutic penetration, minimize collateral damage, and enhance immune signaling. When combined with biocompatible carriers such as CHPs, laser-enhanced photoimmunotherapy improves tumor targeting and drug retention while linking photothermal damage to immune reprogramming, thereby bridging localized tumor ablation with systemic immune protection [101].

Recently, laser-induced immunomodulation using CHP-based nanoplatforms has emerged as a promising approach in cancer immunotherapy. Unlike conventional treatments that merely destroy tumor tissue, this approach combines the precision of laser irradiation with the biological versatility of CHPs, for example, CS, HA, dextran, alginate, and pullulan, to trigger local tumor ablation and systemic immune activation. These polymers serve as biocompatible carriers for photosensitizers or photothermal agents and as natural immunostimulants that modulate macrophage and DC function as well as cytokine signaling within the TME. The laser-triggered process begins with tumor cell damage, leading to the release of tumor-associated antigens and danger signals, including CRT, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). Antigen presenting cells (APCs), particularly DCs, then recognize and engulf these molecules [102]. CHPs enhance this phase by promoting efficient antigen capture, stabilizing inflammatory mediators, and engaging pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), to boost immune activation. Activated DCs migrate to the lymph nodes and stimulate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which then travel to the tumor site. CHPs further remodel the immune microenvironment by promoting M1-type macrophage polarization, enhancing T cell infiltration, and suppressing immunosuppressive signaling. Mannose- or β-glucan-modified carbohydrates enhance immune recognition by targeting CD44 and mannose receptors on immune cells. These strategies examine how natural and chemically modified carbohydrate-based materials enhance immune activation, regulate cytokine responses, and remodel TME. Furthermore, the following sections examine how these polymers function as immune adjuvants, antigen carriers, and signaling modulators in laser-responsive nanoplatforms. Collectively, these discussions highlight how CHPs link phototherapy-induced tumor destruction to long-term, systemic immune protection.

Induction of immunogenic cell death and DAMP-mediated signaling pathways

The concept of ICD, introduced in 2005, describes a regulated form of cell death that eliminates tumor cells and triggers an adaptive immune response against tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [103]. Unlike immunologically silent apoptosis, ICD converts dying cells into sources of antigens and immune signals that reactivate the immune system of the host. Various stressors, including chemotherapeutic agents, radiation, microbial infections, and phototherapy, can induce ICD through oxidative, thermal, or metabolic stress mechanisms [104]. Different subtypes of ICD, including ferroptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy-associated death, follow distinct molecular pathways but share a common hallmark: the release of DAMPs and TAAs that activate the immune system. These DAMPs, including CRT, ATP, HMGB1, calcium-binding proteins, and heat shock proteins, serve as immunological “danger signals” that recruit and activate APCs, including DCs [105]. When comparing the effects of different CHPs on laser-induced ICD, each exhibits distinct advantages that enhance immunogenicity. For instance, collagen–alginate hybrid nanohydrogels have been engineered to enable laser-triggered immune activation. Meanwhile, ICG was incorporated as a photosensitizer, and polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) as an immune adjuvant; the pTRG formulation released poly I:C upon NIR irradiation due to heat generation [105]. In CT-26 tumor-bearing mice, pTRG combined with NIR exposure not only elevated tumor temperature but also enhanced DC activation in tumor-draining lymph nodes, and upregulated CD40, CD80, CD86, and Major Histocompatibility Complex class II (MHC II) expression, while significantly increasing serum levels of IL-6, IL-12p70, and TNF-α. In contrast, control groups (Gel, pGel, TRG) exhibited minimal immune activation (Fig. 11). Tumor-associated macrophages polarization and TLR-NF-κB signaling

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) critically shape the TME. Additionally, TAMs exhibit two major phenotypes: M1-type macrophages, which are proinflammatory and antitumorigenic, and M2-type macrophages, which are anti-inflammatory and promote tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immune suppression. In most solid tumors, TAMs predominantly adopt an M2 phenotype, driving immune evasion and therapy resistance [107]. Therefore, strategies that reprogram TAMs from an M2 to an M1 phenotype are gaining attention for the potential to enhance cancer immunotherapy. Their polarization is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including TLR (Toll-like receptor) signaling, NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells), and STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription) pathways [108]. Indeed, studies have shown that HA-based copper–carbon nanoplatforms enable CHPs to mediate this reprogramming effectively. The HA shell, a biocompatible polysaccharide, targets CD44 receptors on cancer cells and macrophages and activates TLR2/TLR4 signaling to promote M1 differentiation. Under dual laser irradiation (660/750 nm), the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle generates ROS, triggering PTT, PDT, and CDT effects that induce ICD and release DAMPs (Fig. 13). These immunogenic cues promote DC maturation, enhance cytokine secretion (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ), and favor M1 polarization, while HIF-1α suppression limits M2-associated signaling [107]. When combined with αPD-L1 therapy, the HA–CuCD nanoplatform synergistically enhances CD8+ T cell recruitment and TAM reprogramming, thereby creating a self-sustaining immune cycle. Similarly, plant-derived CHPs, such as PEC and alginate, can modulate macrophage phenotypes via TLR-mediated pathways. Under NIR irradiation, PEC-coated gold nanorods enhance M1-related cytokine secretion (IL-6, TNF-α) and suppress M2-associated markers (IL-10, ARG1) [109]. Extending this concept, cellulose- and starch-derived NPs provide robust backbones that stabilize metal NPs under irradiation and support macrophage adhesion and activation. For instance, nanocellulose–gold composites increase polarization efficiency under oxidative stress, whereas starch-coated metallic systems generate controlled ROS that synergize with cytokine signaling to reinforce M1 polarization. Overall, β-glucan–gold, PEC–gold, and alginate–iron oxide hybrids illustrate how CHP–metal nanoplatforms combine laser-induced photothermal ablation with immune modulation [110]. Activation of TLRs by DAMPs or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) triggers downstream NF-κB signaling, inducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-12, which promote M1 polarization. These cytokines can further activate STAT pathways (e.g., STAT1 for M1 polarization, STAT3/6 for M2 polarization) further regulate macrophage phenotypes and shapes adaptive immune responses. In this context, CHPs such as β-glucan, mannans, and CS derivatives act as complementary natural and synthetic immunoadjuvants that engage multiple immune receptors and strengthen these signaling cascades. [111]. For example, β-glucan, a fungal or yeast polysaccharide, signals through dectin-1 and complement receptor 3 (CR3), activating NF-κB and inducing proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-12), thereby linking innate and adaptive immunity. Mannans, rich in mannose residues, directly target CD206 and DC-SIGN on DCs, enhancing antigen uptake, MHC expression, and T helper type 1 cells (Th1)-biased immune activation [112]. However, co-delivering PS IR-780 and the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) demonstrated enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared to control bare drug groups. Under 808 nm NIR irradiation (1 W cm−2 for 5 min, <50 °C), tumor volume was reduced by about 3.4-fold in distant tumor model [113]. Importantly, CQ-mediated autophagy inhibition disrupted the tumor protective mechanism, leading to the accumulation of cellular stress signals that promoted NF-κB pathway activation. Here, controlled mild temperature activated IR-780 mediated phototherapy, generating ROS and cellular stress that further amplified NF-Κb activation when autophagy was inhibited by CQ [113]. Such activation of immune-related signaling pathways highlights the importance of additional immunomodulatory strategies. In contrast, CS derivatives, which are cationic and biodegradable, act as delivery vehicles and TLR4 agonists, stimulating DC maturation and the secretion of IL-12 and IFN-γ [114]. β-glucan triggers strong innate responses and macrophage activation; mannans selectively target DCs; CS improves antigen transport and retention at the injection site [115]. In NP systems or photoresponsive hydrogels, these polysaccharides synergize to enhance ICD, cytokine secretion, and T cell priming, forming the basis of next-generation carbohydrate-based immunoadjuvant platforms [116].

Immune checkpoint blockade and modulation of antitumor signaling pathways

Immune checkpoints are regulatory pathways that maintain immune tolerance and prevent T cell overactivation. Tumors can exploit these pathways to weaken immune surveillance, enabling the tumor cells to avoid immune-mediated destruction. Key inhibitory checkpoints include programmed death-1 (PD-1), the associated ligand PD-L1, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4). Blocking these interactions with ICIs, including anti-PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab), and anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab), reactivates exhausted T cells, restores cytokine secretion, and promotes tumor cell killing [117]. ICIs enhance the immune recognition of cancer cells by the body. Recently, Shang et al. developed a BMS-based photoimmunotherapeutic nanoplatform that efficiently delivers the small-molecule inhibitor BMS-202 to tumor sites for precise PD-L1/PD-1 blockade and synergistic antitumor activity [117]. In this system, HA was conjugated to the photosensitizer Ce6 to form HA–Ce6 micelles that encapsulate BMS-202. The HA coating enabled CD44-targeted uptake and HAase-responsive release in the TME. Under 660 nm laser irradiation, Ce6 generates ROS for photodynamic ablation, while locally released BMS-202 reactivates T cells by inhibiting PD-L1 signaling. This HA–Ce6/BMS-202 nanoplatform integrates immune checkpoint inhibition with laser-triggered PDT, inducing strong immune activation, tumor growth suppression, and metastasis prevention [117]. However, HA–polypyrrole (PPy) NPs have been engineered for synergistic photothermal–immunotherapy by incorporating JQ-1, a small molecule inhibitor of PD-L1, Bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), and Cellular Myelocytomatosis oncogene (c-Myc) axis. In the study, varying molecular weights of HA (0.6 kDa–3 MDa) were compared, with 1 MDa HA identified as the optimal weight for colloidal stability, CD44-mediated tumor targeting, and superior JQ-1 loading capacity. Under 808 nm laser irradiation at a power density of 1.5 W cm−2 for 5 min, PPy–HA/JQ-1 NPs generate localized heat till 550C, causing tumor ablation and controlled JQ-1 release. The released JQ-1 inhibits BRD4-driven c-Myc transcription and downregulates PD-L1 expression, producing a triple synergy of photothermal ablation, oncogene suppression, and immune checkpoint blockade [118]. Although anti-PD-L1 therapy is typically administered through monoclonal antibody injections, small-molecule inhibitors such as JQ-1 can also block tumor immune checkpoint signaling. In this context, CHP platforms such as the HA/PPy–JQ-1 system enable efficient small-molecule delivery, facilitating PD-L1 pathway inhibition and enhanced antitumor immune activation. This integrated mechanism promoted T cell activation and cytokine secretion, resulting in robust tumor regression and enhanced systemic immunity. Although the treatment did not significantly inhibit primary tumor growth, secondary tumor progression was suppressed in vivo in a bilateral breast cancer mouse model, demonstrating systemic immune activation and effective control of distant tumors. Recently, chemiluminescence-driven PIT strategies have been developed to overcome the limited tissue penetration of external light. For example, a chemiluminescence-powered system enabled intracellular light generation, promoting PD-L1 degradation and inducing immunogenic pyroptosis through precise subcellular trafficking, thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses in deep-seated tumors [119]. Beyond these approaches, emerging PD-L1–targeted PIT platforms offer more precise and potent immune modulation. For instance, a J-aggregate bodipy scaffold (JBDPEt-BMS) enables simultaneous PD-L1 degradation and NIR-triggered immunogenic pyroptosis, achieving tumor-selective ROS generation even under hypoxia and significantly enhancing antitumor immune responses [120].

Similar to PD-L1–targeted immunotherapy, inhibition of CTLA-4 has also been explored to enhance antitumor T-cell activation. In one strategy, the clinical PS Temoporfin (m-THPC) was modified with –COOH groups to produce a water-soluble derivative (PS2) capable of forming CS-based hydrogels [121]. In addition, CS-based hydrogels were formed via amide and/or imine bonds between CS chains, tetrafunctionalized m-THPC derivatives, and difunctionalized PEG, where the PS2–PEG–CS formulation showed the highest potential due to improved water solubility while preserving the photophysical properties of m-THPC. This system underwent light-triggered situ gelation, enabling sustained release of PS2 and allowing multiple rounds of tumor irradiation. Upon 660 nm LED irradiation, extracellular activation of PS2 induced tumor cell damage mainly through lipid peroxidation. When combined with anti-CTLA-4 checkpoint blockade, this strategy enhanced DC vaccine–stimulated cytotoxic T-cell responses, increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and suppressed secondary CT26 colon carcinoma tumors. Notably, the survival rate decreased from 67% in wild-type BALB/c mice to 0% in immunocompromised mice under a light dose of 20 J cm−2, highlighting the essential role of the immune system in the therapeutic effect [121]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying PDT-induced ICD remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation for enhance immunosurveillance and antitumor immunity.

Programmed cell death pathways and downstream signaling pathways

PCD is a tightly regulated biological process in which cells undergo controlled self-destruction through specific molecular signaling pathways to remove damaged or abnormal cells and maintain tissue homeostasis. In cancer therapy, different PCD pathways, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and necroptosis are activated through specific signaling cascades, contributing to tumor suppression and immune activation [122]. CHPs play a critical role in regulating PCD and immune signaling pathways, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity. For example, a core–shell nanoplatform composed of glycol CS and small AuNPs (AuNSw) utilizes the interaction between Au and the amine functional groups of glycol CS to assemble the NPs. Under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation (1W cm−2, 10 min), AuNSw generates localized heat via plasmon coupling, inducing apoptotic cell death. Subsequently, the elevated intracellular thiol groups (e.g., GSH) in cancer cells replace the Au–amine interactions with stronger Au–thiol bonds, triggering NP disassembly and limiting excessive photothermal damage [123]. This process promotes ICD, characterized by the release of DAMPs such as HMGB1 and Hsp70, which enhance antigen presentation and stimulate antitumor immune responses.

Pyroptosis is a highly inflammatory form of PCD that plays a critical role in enhancing ICD and antitumor immune activation. Unlike apoptosis, pyroptosis involves gasdermin-E (GSDME), and gasdermin-D (GSDMD) mediated membrane pore formation, leading to rapid cell swelling, rupture, and the release of tumor antigens and DAMPs that stimulate immune responses. By taking advantage of colon TME, HA coated cuprous oxide nanoparticles accumulate in tumors and are converted into Cu8S9 photothermal NPs by endogenous hydrogen sulfide (H2S), enabling strong NIR-II photothermal activation. Upon 1064 nm (1W cm−2) laser treatment at 46 °C for 8 min, mild photothermal hyperthermia activates caspase-3, which cleaves GSDME and converts apoptosis into GSDME-mediated pyroptosis [124]. Moreover, photothermal activation combined with Decitabine (DAC) pretreatment induces a caspase-3 dependent apoptosis to pyroptosis switch in CT26 as shown in western blot analysis in Fig. 14. This process induces membrane pore formation, leading to the release of DAMPs such as HMGB1, ATP, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) along with tumor antigens. Moreover, CHPs modulate key pathways such as NF-κB, STING, and interferon signaling through precise molecular engineering and laser activation, linking oxidative stress to innate and adaptive immune activation [126,127]. Under laser irradiation, photoresponsive CHPs generate ROS and nitric oxide (NO), triggering ICD characterized by CRT exposure, ATP secretion, and HMGB1 release. These DAMPs activate PRRs such as TLR4 and cGAS–STING, inducing phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 and subsequent type I interferon (IFN-α/β) production [128]. This signaling cascade induces DC maturation and recruits CTL to the tumor site.

Simultaneously, CHPs modulate the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is pivotal in regulating inflammation and apoptosis. For instance, polysaccharides such as CS and β-glucan stimulate NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-12), which drives M1 macrophage polarization. In contrast, alginate- and HA-based NPs suppress NF-κB activation in tumor cells, reducing chronic inflammation and promoting immune remodeling [129]. Mohapatra et al. developed an advanced HA-based, thermosensitive, NO-releasing hydrogel to overcome immune suppression and enhance STING pathway activation for synergistic cancer immunotherapy. This hydrogel, composed of HA/pluronic F127 embedded with gold nanostars (GNSs), the IDO inhibitor (NLG919), the STING agonist (DMXAA), and cysteine–NO (Cys–NO), forms an injectable matrix for localized tumor delivery [129]. Under NIR laser irradiation at 808 nm, the embedded GNSs convert light into heat, triggering controlled NO release from Cys–NO and inducing mild hyperthermia. The released NO degrades tumor collagen and disrupts the dense ECM, thereby enhancing the penetration of NLG919 and DMXAA. Sustained NLG919 release counteracts PTT-induced indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) upregulation, restoring cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activity [129]. Meanwhile, DMXAA release activates the STING (stimulator of interferon genes) pathway, inducing type I interferons (IFN-α/β) and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) that collectively promote DC maturation and antigen presentation. A single intratumoral injection effectively suppresses tumor growth and secondary metastasis while enhancing systemic antitumor immunity via coordinated immune reprogramming and TME remodeling in TNBC models. Overall, this review highlights CHPs as versatile platforms for cancer therapy under light stimuli, enabling targeted drug delivery, controlled release, and photo-induced immune activation. Clinical translation and future perspectives

For successful clinical translation, carbohydrate-based phototherapeutic systems must be designed with a clear focus on accuracy, safety, and reproducibility. While CHPs such as HA, CS, alginate, β-glucan, dextran, and pullulan are widely valued for the associated biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ease of functionalization, these inherent advantages alone do not guarantee clinical success. One of the major challenges in current oncology practice is ensuring that therapeutic agents accumulate sufficiently within tumors while minimizing systemic toxicity. Therefore, the rational design of drug carriers for tumor-specific delivery becomes a central requirement rather than a secondary consideration. In clinical settings, passive targeting through the EPR effect has shown variability among patients, limiting the associated reliability; thus, active targeting strategies are increasingly emphasized. Surface functionalization of CHPs with targeting ligands can enhance selective tumor recognition and receptor-mediated uptake. For example, HA naturally binds to CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed in many solid tumors, thereby promoting tumor accumulation and cellular internalization [98]. Similarly, folic acid-modified CS NPs improve uptake in folate receptor-positive cancers, while RGD peptides target integrins associated with tumor angiogenesis. Antibodies and aptamers further increase specificity toward tumor-associated antigens such as HER2 and EGFR. These targeting strategies increase intratumoral drug concentrations, improve therapeutic efficacy, and reduce off-target effects. HA is frequently conjugated with photosensitizers such as Ce6 or ICG to form micelles or nanogels that combine CD44-mediated targeting with laser-responsive activation. Upon irradiation, localized heat or ROS induce tumor cell death and ICD; meanwhile, the biodegradable CHP scaffold enables the simultaneous release of co-loaded agents, such as immunomodulators or checkpoint inhibitors [130]. This integrated strategy not only promotes direct tumor ablation but also supports immune activation and TME remodeling. Comparative analyses of recent CHP-based nanoplatforms indicate that dual- or multimodal systems, such as laser-based therapies combined with chemotherapy or immunotherapy, offer superior therapeutic outcomes owing to the high loading capacity, stimulus responsiveness, and inherent immunological compatibility of the polymer [131].

In addition to selective delivery, clinical translation depends on the development of photosensitizer systems that are activated specifically within the TME. Tumors possess unique biological characteristics, including acidic pH, elevated GSH levels, hypoxia, and overexpressed enzymes, which can be strategically exploited for site-specific activation. CHP-based platforms are particularly advantageous in this regard because the polymeric backbones in CHPs can be engineered to respond to thermal, redox, enzymatic, or light stimuli. In light-activated PTT, PDT, or CDT, carbohydrate matrices facilitate controlled heat generation, ROS production, and regulated drug release within tumor tissue. Overall, the clinical advancement of laser-responsive CHPs depends on integrating two key principles: precise tumor-targeted delivery and tumor-specific activation. Thus, by combining ligand-mediated biological targeting with TME-responsive and externally controlled laser activation, these systems provide a dual-selective approach that enhances therapeutic precision, reduces systemic toxicity, and aligns with current clinical priorities in personalized cancer therapy. Recent reviews of CHP-based nanoplatforms highlight that dual- or multimodal systems (e.g., laser + immune/laser + chemo) harness the capacity of the polymer to encapsulate payloads, exhibit stimuli responsiveness (e.g., thermal, redox, or enzymatic), and exhibit immune adjuvanticity [132].

In solid tumors, conventional PTT can generate high local temperatures (> 50 °C), which may damage adjacent healthy tissue and induce heat-shock responses that suppress immune activation. Conversely, CHP-based systems allow mild hyperthermia (42–45 °C) or laser-mediated thermal modulation, promoting immune stimulation rather than immunosuppression and facilitating deeper NP penetration. Temperature-responsive carbohydrate hydrogels (e.g., HA/pluronic F127) and polysaccharide-coated metal nanostructures enable precise regulation of heat generation through laser power and polymer phase transitions. Mild heating enhances intratumoral diffusion, relaxes the dense ECM, and facilitates payload delivery, especially in fibrotic tumors where NP penetration is restricted [26]. Additionally, mild hyperthermia preserves the antigenicity of dying tumor cells and promotes ICD pathways, including CRT exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 secretion, thereby enhancing DC maturation and T cell activation. Polysaccharide-stabilized gold or carbon dot systems coated with HA or CS exhibit improved tumor accumulation, controlled mild photothermal conversion, and enhanced immune activation when combined with immune modulators or checkpoint inhibitors. The polymer shell protects the photothermal core, maintains colloidal stability, and mediates temperature through its phase behavior at the tumor site. Despite these advantages, several challenges remain for the clinical translation of light-responsive CHP systems. The limited tissue penetration depth of light, particularly in the visible region, can restrict treatment efficacy for deep-seated tumors. Moreover, potential phototoxicity and overheating of surrounding healthy tissues may occur if irradiation parameters are not carefully controlled. The reliance on external irradiation equipment and precise light delivery further adds complexity in clinical settings. In addition, issues related to large-scale synthesis, long-term biosafety, stability, and reproducibility of CHP-based nanoplatforms must be carefully addressed. Future research should therefore focus on the development of NIR-responsive systems, improved light delivery strategies, and more biocompatible nanomaterials to overcome these limitations and enhance the translational potential of light-triggered therapies. Recently, activatable PTAs have also been developed to further couple photothermal therapy with immune activation. For example, a universal ER-targeting PTA scaffold (BDP-4) enables NIR-triggered photothermal stress in the endoplasmic reticulum, inducing GSDME-mediated immunogenic pyroptosis and promoting antitumor immune responses [133].

From a clinical translation perspective, CHP-based laser-responsive systems offer several advantages, including biodegradability, low immunogenicity, ease of surface modification, and the ability to integrate multiple therapeutic modalities into a single platform. However, several challenges remain for clinical use: (1) scaling, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing of polysaccharide nanoplatforms with consistent size, coating, and payload release profiles; (2) comprehensive biodistribution and clearance studies, while many CHPs are considered safe, NPs often accumulate in the Reticuloendothelial System (RES)(liver/spleen), meaning the long-term fate of NPs must be defined; (3) tumor heterogeneity and limited light penetration, deep‐seated or large solid tumors may be inaccessible to NIR lasers, making the design of polymers responsive to second- or third-window lasers critical; (4) temperature regulation and safety, mild hyperthermia requires precise control to ensure therapeutic efficacy while avoiding collateral damage; (5) regulatory pathways, combining phototherapy, immunotherapy, and polymeric nanocarriers increases regulatory complexity; thus, early proof-of-concept studies must clearly show improved patient outcomes, safety, and cost-effectiveness.

Looking ahead, future efforts may focus on developing personalized polysaccharide nanoplatforms tailored to patient‐specific tumor characteristics (e.g., ECM density, CD44 expression, immune landscape); integrating smart feedback systems, such as thermochromic polymers, imaging reporters, to monitor in situ temperature or ROS generation; enabling multi-checkpoint immunomodulation (e.g., combining STING agonists, IDO inhibitors, checkpoint inhibitors) within a single CHP nanocarrier. Moreover, employing second‐window NIR-II lasers (1000–1700 nm) with polysaccharide-based constructs could increase penetration and clinical applicability. Finally, close collaboration with clinical oncologists and regulatory agencies will be essential to translate promising preclinical findings into human trials, where CHP laser nanomedicines could serve as next-generation adjuvant therapies for refractory solid tumors.

In summary, CHP-based laser-responsive systems represent an integrated platform that combines nanomedicine, phototherapy, and immunotherapy. Indeed, by delivering targeted therapies, inducing mild hyperthermia, activating the immune system, and supporting synergistic combination treatments, these systems are emerging as one of the most promising strategies for future cancer therapy, provided that key challenges in manufacturing, safety, and clinical validation are successfully addressed.

Conclusion

CHP-based laser-responsive nanoplatforms represent a new generation of intelligent systems for precise cancer therapy. The biocompatibility, tunable structures, and functional versatility of these systems enable targeted delivery, controlled release, and immune modulation under laser irradiation. CHPs, acting as structural and bioactive materials, can interact with cellular receptors and modulate immune signaling, enhancing therapeutic specificity. By integrating photothermal, photodynamic, and immunotherapeutic mechanisms, these multifunctional systems induce ICD, DC activation, and T cell responses. Moreover, controlled mild hyperthermia (42–45 °C) enables selective tumor ablation, deeper NP penetration, and an immune-supportive microenvironment addressing key challenges of traditional phototherapy. Therefore, future progress should focus on optimizing system design for safety, reproducibility, and clinical scalability. With advances in NIR-II laser technology, feedback-controlled heating, and multimodal imaging, CHP-based systems hold strong potential to bridge nanotechnology and immunotherapy. Overall, this review highlights the promise of these systems as clinically adaptable, laser-activated biomaterials poised to transform precision and immune-driven cancer treatment.
