Comparative infectivity of EBV subtypes in tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri)
Authors: Zhenqiu Luo, Nan Shi, Fangfang Chen, Zongjian Huang, Wei Xia, Anzhou Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Epstein–Barr Virus (EBV) has a high global prevalence and is strongly associated with the pathogenesis of diverse diseases. Types 1 (T1) and 2 (T2) represent the two major EBV subtypes. However, their infectivity and pathogenic mechanisms remain unclear. We aimed to explore the differences between T1 and T2 EBV infections using the tree shrew model.MethodsT1 and T2 EBVs were purified from the supernatant of EBV-producing cells lines B95-8 and P3HR-1, respectively cell line. Eighteen tree shrews (evenly divided by sex) were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6). Each animal intravenously received 1 × 10⁸ copies of either T1 EBV, T2 EBV, or RPMI-1640 medium (vehicle control) via the femoral vein. Peripheral blood was collected at 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-inoculation (dpi) for quantitative EBV-DNA detection. peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected on 7 and 28 dpi for RNA-seq analysis, and comparisons were made with pre-infection samples to assess host transcriptional changes.ResultsEBV-DNA was detectable in the PBMCs of all six tree shrews by day 3 post-inoculation in the T1 EBV (B95-8) group, peaking at 1 × 104.34 copies/mg. The positivity rate for EBV infection declined over time, with one tree shrew remaining positive on day 28. In the T2 EBV (P3HR-1) group, only one of the six animals exhibited low levels of PBMCs EBV-DNA at 1 × 102.31 copies/mg on day 3, with no subsequent detections. Plasma EBV DNA trends were similar. Pathological examination revealed consistent LMP1 protein and EBERs expression in the spleen and lymph nodes of T1 tree shrews, with persistent plasma EBV DNA. RNA-seq revealed distinct differentially expressed genes for T1 and T2.ConclusionsT2 EBV demonstrated significantly lower infectivity than T1 EBV in the tree shrew model. T1 infection elicits inflammatory responses, whereas T2 infection induces modest transcriptional alterations.Keywords: Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), Tree shrew model, Viral pathogenesis, Subtype-Specific infectivity, Host transcriptional response

Background

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), a ubiquitous γ-herpesvirus, infects over 90% of adults globally and is associated with several human diseases, such as infectious mononucleosis, Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and gastric cancer [1, 2]. EBV is recognized as a B-lymphotropic virus because of its ability to infect and transform B cells into lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) in vitro [3]. Based on variations in the EBNA2 gene sequence, EBV is classified into two major subtypes: type 1 (T1, with a complete sequence) and type 2 (T2, lacks the EBNA2 sequence) [4]. Epidemiological studies reveal a high global prevalence of T1 EBV, whereas T2 EBV exhibits focal endemicity in Southern Africa, with an infection rate of approximately 25% among adults [5]. However, evidence indicates that the prevalence of T2 EBV infection may be more widespread than previously thought. For instance, between 1999 and 2000, approximately 20% of university students in the UK tested positive for T2 EBV serum [6]. Moreover, in Spain, 90% of adult patients with multiple sclerosis are co-infected with both T1 and T2 EBV [7]. Co-infection with T1 and T2 EBV is particularly frequent in immunosuppressed patients, and recombination between the T1 and T2 EBV genomes has been detected [8, 9].

In contrast to T1 EBV, T2 EBV exhibits a reduced capacity to infect and transform resting B cells in vitro, while demonstrating distinct T-cell tropism [10–12]. This difference could be attributed to a single amino acid change (S442D) in EBNA2 of type 2, where serine is replaced by aspartic acid compared to type 1 EBNA2. This alteration allows it to bind to the ETS-IRF composite element (EICE) and activate the LMP1 promoter, thereby promoting B cell immortalization [13]. Despite the low efficiency of T2 EBV in infecting and transforming B cells in vitro, it is frequently detected in endemic Burkitt’s lymphoma, suggesting that T2 EBV may persist in vivo through other mechanisms. EBV is a human pathogen with a limited host range because most animals are not susceptible to EBV infection. Currently, most studies of T1 and T2 EBV rely on in vitro experiments. The absence of appropriate small animal models has restricted the exploration of the characteristics of EBV infection in vivo and its pathogenic mechanisms in human diseases.

Tree shrews, a recently discovered, small experimental animal model, have been used in various human viral infection models [14, 15]. Our previous studies indicated that tree shrews were susceptible to T1 EBV produced by B95-8 cells, and persistent EBV carriage was observed in the absence of significant clinical symptoms [16]. In the present study, we infected tree shrews with T2 EBV (derived from the P3HR-1 cell line) to compare the infection characteristics of T2 and T1 EBV. Additionally, we conducted transcriptomic sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from tree shrews infected with either T1 or T2 EBV to understand the dynamic changes in biological processes during EBV infection in vivo.

Methods

EBV viral suspension Preparation

EBV was isolated from the supernatant of EBV-producing cell lines, as described previously. T1 EBV-producing B95-8 cells (ATCC: VR-1492, RRID: CVCL_1953) and T2 EBV-producing P3HR-1 cells (ATCC: HTB-62, RRID: CVCL_2676), were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 kU/L penicillin, 100 mg/L streptomycin, Gibco) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator for 14 days. After collection, the cells and medium were centrifuged at 1200×g for 30 min to remove the cell debris. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µM filter and then ultracentrifuged at 16,000×g for 90 min at 4℃. The viral pellet was resuspended in RPMI 1640 culture medium. The EBV DNA load in the suspension was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR).

Animals

In the experiment, 18 tree shrews (8 ± 2 months old, evenly divided by sex, weighing 120–150 g) were randomly divided into three groups: the Type 1 (T1) group (n = 6), which was injected via the femoral vein with 200 µL of B95-8 EBV viral suspension containing 1 × 10⁸ copies; the Type 2 (T2) group (n = 6), which received the same volume of P3HR-1 EBV viral suspension with 1 × 10⁸ copies; and the blank control group (n = 6), which was injected with 200 µL of RPMI-1640 medium as a negative control. An experimenter monitored all animals daily for signs such as diarrhea, weight, or body temperature. The animals were housed individually in the animal facility of the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University, where breeding conditions strictly adhered to our previously established standardized tree-shrew management protocol [17]. Before the experiment, all animals were marked, weighed, and acclimated to their new environment for two weeks. Blood samples were collected from the tree shrews at 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-EBV inoculation (dpi) to detect EBV copies. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No: 202109009) and strictly followed the Guidelines for the Use and Care of Experimental Animals” issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. In accordance with the 3R principle, the total number of animals and their suffering were minimized. Following ethical guidelines, animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 62 mg/kg (Hebei Jindafu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) for autopsy and collection of organs and whole blood [18].

Real-Time quantitative PCR (qPCR) for EBV copy number detection

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of blood samples or EBV suspension using the QIAamp DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and eluted in 30 µL of nuclease-free water. Total RNA was extracted from 200 µL of blood samples using the RNAprep Pure Hi-Blood Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The quantity and purity of the extracted nucleic acids were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

EBV copy numbers in the initial inoculum and subsequent blood samples were quantified using Premix Ex Taq™ (Probe qPCR) (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) with Namalwa cell line DNA as a standard, following the manufacturer’s recommended conditions. The PCR reaction used the forward primer: 5’-TCT TAG GAG CTG TCC GAG GG-3’; reverse primer: 5’-CCC AAC ACT CCA CCA CAC C-3’; and a probe targeting the EBV BamHI W region: 5’-CAC ACA CTA CAC ACA CCC ACC CGT CTC-3’. A Bio-Rad CFX 96 detection system was used to analyze triplicate samples. Viral loads were determined based on standard curves with a PCR amplification efficiency (E) of 95–105% and a correlation coefficient (R²) >0.995 [19].

EBV detection in tree shrew tissues via EBER in situ hybridization

EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) was used to detect EBV in tree shrew tissues. A highly sensitive digoxigenin-labeled oligonucleotide probe was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, dewaxed tissue sections were digested with pepsin at 37℃ for 30 min to expose the RNA. The pre-hybridization solution was applied for 4 h to reduce nonspecific binding, followed by overnight hybridization with a mix of digoxigenin-labelled EBER-1 and EBER-2 probes. An SABC system (BOSTER, Wuhan, China) was used for colorimetric detection. Mayer’s hematoxylin was used to counterstain the nuclei, and the sections were mounted. B95-8 cell sections served as positive controls.

Immunohistochemical detection of target protein expression in tree shrew spleen

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on 4 μm thick sections of tree shrew spleen tissue using the SP-HRP kit (SP-9000, ZSGB Biotechnology, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were dewaxed and incubated in 0.5% H2O2 for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed by placing the sections in 5 mM citrate buffer (Ph = 6) and heating at high pressure. The sections were blocked with serum for 20 min at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight with the primary antibody (LMP1 1:200; Abcam ab78113, RRID: AB_1566182) and subsequently incubated with the secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Signals were developed using the DAB detection system and the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin.

RNA-seq and data analysis

At the end of the experiment, 1.5-2 ml of whole blood was collected. PBMCs were isolated according to the method described in our previous study [20], and the total RNA of tree shrew PBMCs was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies). RNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity number (RIN) values were determined using an Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Only samples with RNA integrity scores of >8 were used. Library construction and sequencing were performed using Beijing NoVo Gene Library. Raw data were subjected to quality control, including error rate and GC content analyses, to generate clean reads. These reads were aligned to the tree shrew reference genome (http://www.treeshrewdb.org/) using HISAT2, with sequences showing less than 0.9 identity or 0.85 coverage being filtered out. featureCounts (1.5.0) was used to count the reads mapped to each gene and calculate the FPKM values. Gene‑level differential expression was performed in DESeq2 (v1.20.0) using the raw count matrix from featureCounts. Library size and composition biases were accounted for by DESeq2’s median‑of‑ratios normalization. Wald test P values were adjusted for multiple testing across all genes within each contrast using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (reported as padj). Genes with |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and padj < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. For pathway analysis, clusterProfiler (v3.8.1) was used with BH correction (pAdjustMethod = “BH”), and enriched KEGG terms were reported at adjusted P < 0.05. The RNA‑seq experiment included n = 3 biological replicates per group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0. All tests were two‑sided. For two‑group comparisons, an unpaired Student’s t‑test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one‑way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post‑hoc correction was applied. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Detection of EBV-DNA load in tree shrews infected with T1 or T2 EBV

To investigate the differences in infection efficiency and pattern between T2 EBV and T1 EBV infected tree shrews, we inoculated tree shrews via the femoral vein with 1 × 10⁸ copies of T1 EBV or T2 EBV (n = 6 per group). Blood samples were collected at 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-infection (dpi). On day 3 dpi with T1 EBV, EBV DNA was detected in the PBMCs of all six tree shrews, with an average load of 1 × 104.03 copies/mg DNA and a maximum load of 1 × 104.34copies/mg. On 7 dpi, the average EBV-DNA load decreased to 1 × 102.91 copies/mg DNA, ranging from 102.24 to 103.37 copies/mg DNA. The positivity detection rate gradually declined at 14 dpi (detected positively in four out of six tree shrews), and only one tree shrew remained positive at 28 dpi, with a viral load of 1 × 102.60 copies/mg. In the T2 EBV group, only one of the six tree shrews showed detectable EBV DNA in the PBMCs at 3 dpi, with a load of 1 × 102.31 copies/mg, and no subsequent positive detections were observed (Fig. 1A-B). Similar trends were noted in plasma samples (Fig. 1C-D). To further confirm the infection, the animals were euthanized at 28 dpi, and the major organs were collected for analysis. In the three T1 EBV-infected tree shrews examined, EBV-DNA was detected in the spleen and lymph nodes, with an average load of 1 × 103.96 copies/g DNA in the spleen and 1 × 102.66 copies/g DNA in the lymph nodes. Additionally, EBV DNA was not detected in the T2 EBV-inoculated group (Fig. 1E-F). EBV DNA was not detected in the control group at any time point. Throughout the experiment, no acute complications or deaths related to EBV infection occurred in any of the three shrews.

Histopathological detection of EBV infection in tree shrew tissues

LMP1 protein- and EBER-positive cells were detected in the spleen and lymph nodes of T1 EBV-infected tree shrews using immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridization (ISH). These cells were predominantly located in the follicular marginal zones and rarely in the germinal centers (Fig. 2). EBER-positive signals were observed only in tree shrews from the T1 group with persistent viremia. Neither EBER-positive cells nor the expression of EBV-associated genes or proteins was detected in the residual tree-shrew tissues of the T2-EBV group. Likewise, neither LMP1-positive nor EBER-positive cells were observed in any of the tissues examined in the blank control group.

Transcriptomic characteristics of different EBV types in tree shrews

To elucidate the biological consequences of infection with different EBV subtypes, bulk RNA sequencing was performed on PBMCs obtained at 0, 7, and 28 dpi. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering demonstrated that T2 EBV-infected animals clustered with the control group data, whereas T1 EBV-infected tree shrews formed an independent branch (Fig. 3A), indicating that both infection duration and viral subtype markedly influenced host transcriptional profiles. The UpSet plot summarizes the overlapping differentially expressed genes DEGs (|log2FoldChange|>1 and P < 0.05) across different time points (Fig. 3B). The number of overlapping DEGs in the T1 EBV group during the infection period (7 and 28 dpi) was 214, which was much higher than the 17 overlapping DEGs in the T2 EBV group, indicating a more extensive and significant regulatory effect of T1 EBV on host gene expression. Additionally, only 16 DEGs were common to both subtypes at 7 dpi, highlighting their divergent infection dynamics. DESeq2-based differential expression analysis (Fig. 3C) identified 168 DEGs (73 upregulated and 95 downregulated) in the T2-EBV group at 7 dpi, and 133 DEGs (86 upregulated and 47 downregulated) at 28 dpi. In contrast, T1 EBV infection elicited 442 DEGs (359 upregulated and 83 downregulated) at 7 dpi, and 844 DEGs (613 upregulated and 231 downregulated) at 28 dpi.

Thus, T1 EBV predominantly induced gene upregulation, whereas T2 EBV infection showed more gene downregulation, suggesting that T1 EBV may promote viral replication and persistent infection by activating multiple signaling pathways and immune responses in host cells. The amplitude of host transcriptional changes induced by T2 EBV infection was relatively small. It failed to widely activate host signaling pathways such as T1 EBV. The significantly smaller number of DEGs in the T2 EBV group compared to the T1 EBV group may imply different in vivo roles for T2 EBV and a more robust host response to T1 EBV infection.

Thus, T1 EBV predominantly induces gene up-regulation, whereas T2 EBV is associated with a repressive transcriptional program. These findings imply that T1 EBV may facilitate viral replication and persistence by activating multiple host signaling cascades, whereas the attenuated transcriptional perturbation caused by T2 EBV suggests a distinct in vivo role and comparatively stronger host restriction. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further confirmed these differences (Fig. 3). DEGs from T1-EBV-infected animals were significantly enriched in inflammation-related pathways, including TNF, Toll-like receptor and NF-κB signalling. In contrast, no pathway reached statistical significance in the T2-EBV group at 28 dpi, and only a limited set of regulator-associated pathways were enriched at 7 dpi.

Discussion

The mechanisms of infection and pathogenesis associated with EBV remain poorly understood. The pronounced species specificity of EBV is a major obstacle to in vivo investigations [21]. Our previous study established that type-1 EBV (derived from B95-8 cells) infects tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri), producing persistent viral carriage in the absence of overt clinical signs, thereby positioning the tree shrew as a candidate small animal model for EBV infection [16]. In this study, we compared the in vivo infection efficiencies and characteristics of different EBV subtypes. We found that EBV-DNA was only transiently detected in the PBMC of animals (T2-1), but EBERs-ISH-positive cells were not found in any tissue. These findings imply that T2 EBV may establish a short-lived infection that is eliminated by innate and/or adaptive immune responses. In contrast, T1 EBV showed higher viral loads in PBMC three days post-inoculation, with a 100% detection rate within one week and persistence for at least 28 days. The EBV DNA load and detection rate in the tissues showed significantly greater viral DNA burdens and positivity rates in the spleen and lymph nodes of type-1-infected shrews than in their type-2 counterparts. The positivity rate of the three T1 group tree shrews detected was 100%, suggesting that these tissues may be the main latent and replication sites for EBV. Consistently, LMP1 and EBER transcripts were readily detected in these tissues, supporting their roles as the principal sites of latent infection and viral replication. Conversely, type-2 EBV yielded no detectable LMP1 or EBER signals, underscoring its reduced infectivity and inability to establish latency. The detection rates in PBMC and key immune organs suggest that T2 EBV has a lower infection efficiency in tree shrews than T1 EBV. Similar to a rabbit model study where P3HR-1 EBV showed a lower infection rate (17%) compared to B95-8 EBV (91%) in rabbits [22]. Notably, tree-shrew complement receptor 2 (CR2), the primary EBV entry receptor in B cells, shares 100% identity at critical residues with the human orthologue, far exceeding the 50% identity observed in rabbits [16]. The literature indicates that the rabbit model has differences in EBV antibody titers compared to human responses [19] and shows the absence of EBNA1 protein in infected rabbits [23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that tree shrews may be a more suitable animal model than rabbits for EBV infection research. Although humanized mouse models can show EBNA2-deleted EBV isolates, P3HR-1 causes Hodgkin-like lymphomas and diffuse large B cell lymphomas with T2 and Wp-restricted latency types in humanized mice; however, they have limitations such as incomplete immune microenvironments and an inability to replicate the natural EBV infection process. Therefore, the development of small animal models that more closely mimic human EBV infection remains an urgent priority. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo analyses are required to determine how EBV T1 and T2 differentially evade immunity, maintain latency, and drive oncogenesis, thereby identifying novel therapeutic targets.

One possible explanation for the reduced infection efficiency of tree shrews inoculated with T2 EBV is that P3HR-1 EBV lacks EBNA2, and its characteristics may not fully represent all T2 EBV. In contrast, B95-8 EBV, which retains intact EBNA2, is more competent at sustaining latency and promoting cellular proliferation in vivo [24, 25]. In our RNA-seq analysis, we identified NR4A1 (log2FoldChange = 1.9, padj < 0.01) as a differentially expressed gene when comparing tree shrew PBMCs on day 7 post-infection with the two viral subtypes. This gene was upregulated in T1 EBV infection, which attracted our particular interest. Previous studies have shown that NR4A1 is a key regulatory factor that contributes to T-cell dysfunction. Its upregulation promotes T-cell apoptosis, allowing EBV-infected cells to evade immune surveillance and thereby facilitating the maintenance of latent infection and immune tolerance [26, 27]. Marked differences in T-cell immune responses have been observed between T1 and T2 EBV infections, which may help explain the distinct biological behaviors of these two subtypes. In addition, some studies have reported that EBNA2 exerts anti-apoptotic functions through its interaction with NR4A1, thereby providing survival advantages to infected cells [28]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that during latent infection with type 1 EBV, the synergistic effect of EBNA2 and NR4A1 may contribute to the regulation of cell survival. In contrast, in type 2 EBV infection, the absence of EBNA2 may lead to stronger T-cell–mediated cytotoxicity and enhanced pro-apoptotic activity, which could be an important reason why type 2 EBV struggles to establish persistent infection. This hypothesis offers a novel perspective for explaining the subtype-specific differences in the capacity of EBV to sustain infection. Compared with T1 EBV infection, although T2 EBV displays a lower infection efficiency against tree shrews, the model remains useful for characterizing the in vivo behavior of EBNA2-deficient viruses. The rapid clearance of T2 EBV in vivo may trigger a distinct host immune cascade that could inform future prophylactic and therapeutic interventions.

This study provides a new animal model for comparing T1 and T2 EBV infections in vitro. However, there are some limitations that need to be addressed. First, EBV derived from P3HR-1 lacks the EBNA2 gene and may not fully represent all types 2 EBV. Using additional type 2 EBV strains for multiple verifications can more comprehensively assess the characteristics and behaviors of EBV2 types and compensate for the possible limitations of a single cell line, thereby increasing the reliability and universality of the research results. Future investigations will need to incorporate additional T2 EBV cell lines, such as AG876, to rigorously validate these findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of T1–T2 EBV differences. In addition, because of the reproductive cycle and cost constraints of the experimental animals, the sample size of tree shrews included in this study was relatively limited, and larger cohorts will be required to increase the statistical power. Secondly, because tree shrews are not conventional laboratory animals, species-specific serological reagents are not available. Therefore, it is necessary to develop specific detection tools for viral infections in tree shrews. In addition, the study relied solely on in vivo data and lacked verification of the EBV infection mechanism at the cellular level in vitro. Complementary in vitro experiments, for example, with cultured tree-shrew peripheral PBMCs, are necessary to delineate the viral entry, latency, and immune evasion mechanisms. Finally, although differences in infection between type 1 and type 2 EBV in tree shrews were observed, the molecular determinants underlying the divergent latent programs and immune escape strategies of EBV types 1 and 2 remain to be elucidated. Addressing these issues will refine the tree-shrew model, including optimizing animal models, conducting comprehensive monitoring, and exploring the infection characteristics of EBV, which will advance our understanding of EBV pathogenesis.

Conclusions

This study represents the first application of the tree-shrew model to a comparative analysis of T1 and T2 EBV. The results demonstrated that T1 EBV has a stronger infection ability and persistence in tree shrews. Higher viral DNA loads were detected in peripheral blood, and durable infection was established in lymphoid organs, including the spleen and lymph nodes. In contrast, T2 EBV exhibited limited infectivity. T2 EBV has a lower infection efficiency, with its viral load dropping to the detection limit, and fails to establish a persistent infection. The changes in induced host gene expression were also limited. Collectively, these findings indicate that T2 EBV infections have a lower infection rate than T1 EBV infections.
