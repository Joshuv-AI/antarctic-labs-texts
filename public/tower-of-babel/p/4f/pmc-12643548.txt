Racial and ethnic differences in treatment and survival for hepatocellular carcinoma patients undergoing systemic therapy
Authors: Kelsey S Lau-Min, Angela C Tramontano, Franklin Iheanacho, Thomas Adam Abrams, Christopher R Manz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are substantial historical racial and ethnic differences in treatment survival for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) that may have changed with approval of 9 novel systemic HCC therapies between 2017 and 2022.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients from the Flatiron Health Research Database who received systemic therapy between 2011 and 2023. We identified receipt of systemic therapy, including novel systemic therapies receiving US Food and Drug Adminstration approval ≥2017, and determined overall survival (OS) from start of first-line systemic therapy until death. Multivariable models adjusted for sociodemographic and clinical factors.ResultsThe study included 4198 patients who were 53.0% White, 11.8% Black, and 5.2% Asian, and 11.2% were Hispanic. Asian patients were more likely than White patients to receive novel systemic therapies (odds ratio [OR] = 1.78, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.12 to 2.83), while Black and Hispanic patients had similar receipt of treatment. Asian and Hispanic patients had median OS of 10.3 and 10.5 months compared with 8.0 and 8.3 months for White and non-Hispanic patients. Asian (HR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.65 to 0.96) and Hispanic patients (HR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.62 to 0.83) had better OS than White and non-Hispanic patients, respectively, which did not substantially change with stepwise adjustment. Racial differences in survival worsened over time. Receipt of novel therapy was associated with improved survival.ConclusionsAsian and Hispanic patients had the best survival, and Asian patients were more likely to receive novel therapies. As adjustment for many social and clinical factors had minimal impact on these findings, additional research is needed to understand these survival differences.

Introduction

The incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has risen steadily since the 1990s and, after a brief dip from the effects of hepatitis C treatment, is expected to continue to rise through 2040 as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease drives further growth of HCC.1,2 There are considerable racial and ethnic disparities in HCC incidence, with Black (age-standardized incidence rate 8.2 per 100 000), Asian (8.1) and Hispanic (8.9) patients having much higher incidence than non-Hispanic White patients (4.3).1 Furthermore, a 2022 meta-analysis of studies evaluating disparities in HCC survival found that, compared with White patients, Hispanic patients had comparable survival, while Black patients had worse survival and Asian patients had better survival.3

Racial and ethnic differences in HCC survival may be driven by a combination of factors, including etiology of underlying liver disease, later stage of disease at diagnosis, delays in starting treatment, and differences in receipt of liver transplantation and other curative intent procedures.3-5 Historically, survival differences were not related to receipt of systemic therapy because patients with HCC had only 1 systemic treatment option—sorafenib, approved in 2007 with a 2% response rate and poor survival.6 However, between 2017 and 2022, 9 new systemic therapies were approved, with the combinations of atezolizumab/bevacizumab and durvalumab/tremelimumab showing superior survival to sorafenib, leading to their incorporation as preferred first-line systemic therapies for HCC in 2020 and 2022, respectively.7-9 Yet, novel cancer therapies are not equally accessible after approval, with prior work demonstrating that Black patients were 39% less likely to receive 10 novel non-HCC cancer therapies approved in 2015-2016.10 Furthermore, the effectiveness of novel therapies may vary on the basis of underlying liver disease etiology; given that Asian, Black, and Hispanic patients are more likely than White patients to have hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, respectively, novel therapies may change survival patterns disproportionately across race and ethnicity.11,12

Few studies have examined differences in the receipt of new systemic HCC therapies. One study, which examined patients with HCC treated with first-line systemic therapy in 2017 and 2018 in the National Cancer Database, found that, compared with White and Asian patients, Black and Hispanic patients were 29% and 37% less likely to receive immunotherapy compared with “chemotherapy” (which included novel therapies such as regorafenib) and found no racial or ethnic differences in survival.13 However, the study did not accurately identify all novel therapies and was conducted prior to approval of current first-line systemic therapies for HCC. We recently conducted a study evaluating patterns of HCC survival and treatment using SEER-Medicare claims data through 2020 and found persistent racial and ethnic differences in overall survival but no racial or ethnic differences in receipt of novel therapy (Iheanacho Act F, Abrams TA, Wright AA, Manz CR. Racial, ethnic and rural disparities in hepatocellular carcinoma treatment and survival: A SEER-Medicare analysis. Unpublished data, under review). While more up-to-date than the National Cancer Database study, this study still had limited to no follow-up after approval of current first-line therapies and was restricted to elderly patients with traditional Medicare.

Our prior work demonstrated substantial changes in HCC treatment patterns between 2017 and 2023.14,15 Given these changes and historical differences in HCC treatment and survival, additional research is needed to evaluate racial and ethnic differences in treatment and survival in a real-world population of patients of all ages and insurance types. Thus, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of a nationwide electronic health record (EHR)–derived oncology database to evaluate racial and ethnic differences in treatment and survival among patients with HCC who received systemic therapy between 2011 and 2023.

Methods

Data source and patient population

This study used data from the Flatiron Health Research Database, which is derived from de-identified EHR data from approximately 280 oncology practices at approximately 800 cancer clinics throughout the United States which we described in detail previously.15,16 We included adult patients with both an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) code and clinician-documented confirmation of HCC who were treated with systemic therapy between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2023. Patients were required to have at least 2 clinic encounters on separate days to be included in the study. We included data from the Flatiron Health dataset through a cutoff date of December 31, 2023.

Exposures

Our analyses included exposure variables of EHR-documented race, which were categorized in the database as White, Black/African American, Asian, and Other Race/Unknown, and EHR-documented ethnicity, which we categorized as Hispanic/Latino, Not Hispanic/Latino, and Unknown.

Outcomes

We evaluated several treatment and survival-related outcomes. Treatment-related outcomes included (1) total lines of systemic therapy received, (2) receipt of second-line systemic therapy (yes/no), (3) receipt of novel (non-sorafenib) systemic therapy (yes/no), and (4) receipt of locoregional therapy (LRT) after systemic therapy initiation (yes/no). Systemic therapies included all FDA-approved drugs or drug combinations for HCC during the study period, and LRT included ablation, stereotactic radiotherapy, surgical resection, and transarterial chemoembolization and radioembolization.

We also evaluated overall survival (OS), which we defined as the time from first-line systemic therapy initiation to death, which was ascertained in the database using a combination of EHR, US Social Security Death Index, and obituary data. Patients were censored at the earlier of the date of death or the last date of clinical activity (eg, encounter, labs, imaging).

Potential covariates

We evaluated multiple sociodemographic and clinical factors as potential covariates in the relationship between race or ethnicity and HCC-related outcomes. These variables included age, sex, insurance status, socioeconomic status (SES, defined using the Yost index calculated at the census block group level17), Charlson comorbidity index (excluding cancer)18, etiology of any concomitant liver disease, history of hepatic decompensation (ie, ascites, encephalopathy, gastrointestinal bleeding, varices), history of autoimmune disease, year of first-line systemic therapy initiation, albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade, alpha fetoprotein (AFP), and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status. Receipt of LRT prior to systemic therapy initiation was included as an additional covariate in models evaluating overall survival. We anchored all variables to within 90 days of first-line systemic therapy initiation with the exception of Charlson comorbidity index, which was calculated using ICD-9/10 codes documented within 1 year of the index date.

Statistical analyses

We summarized baseline characteristics for the study cohort using descriptive statistics and compared them by race or ethnicity using χ2 tests. We compared systemic therapy–related outcomes by race or ethnicity using Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables (ie, total lines of systemic therapy) and χ2 tests for binary variables (ie, receipt of second-line systemic therapy, novel systemic therapy, and LRT after systemic therapy initiation). Finally, we used Kaplan-Meier methods to estimate OS and evaluated differences by race or ethnicity using log-rank tests.

We then conducted stepwise multivariable analyses to evaluate the relative contribution of social factors, clinical characteristics, laboratory values, and ECOG performance status to the observed associations between race or ethnicity and our study outcomes (Supplementary Methods). We evaluated the associations of these variables with our study outcomes using logistic generalized estimating equation models for binary outcome variables and Cox regression models for OS. All models were clustered on physician practice. To assess for the impact of receipt of novel therapies on observed survival differences and changes in racial/ethnic survival differences over time, we also ran fully adjusted OS models that included receipt of novel therapy and an interaction between race and ethnicity with time period (Supplementary Methods).

We imputed missing data using the fully conditional method for 5 variables: Charlson comorbidity index, SES, ALBI grade, AFP, and ECOG performance status, which had 4%, 11%, 12%, 25%, and 25% missingness, respectively. Charlson comorbidity index, SES, and ECOG performance status were imputed using ordinal logistic regression, whereas ALBI and AFP were imputed using predictive mean matching. All other covariates were included as predictors. We created 10 imputed datasets, ran our multivariable regression models on each imputed dataset, and pooled the results using PROC MIANALYZE. For sensitivity analyses, we also ran all models using the nonimputed dataset containing patients with no missing data (n = 2395).

Statistical significance was defined as P < .05 on 2-sided tests. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. This study was approved by the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute/Harvard Cancer Center Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent.

Results

Patient population

The imputed dataset included 4198 patients, of whom 2223 (53.0%) were White, 496 (11.8%) were Black/African American, 220 (5.2%) were Asian, and 471 (11.2%) were Hispanic (Table 1, Table S1). Compared with White patients, Black patients were younger (median 64 vs 67 years old), of lower SES (39.7% vs 17.5% in the lowest Yost index quintile), and more likely to have hepatitis C (73.6% vs 43.0%), whereas Asian patients were more likely to be commercially insured (35.5% vs 29.5%), of higher SES (25.9% vs 12.5% in the highest Yost index quintile), and more likely to have hepatitis B (45.0% vs 4.2%). Compared with non-Hispanic patients, Hispanic/Latino patients were of lower SES (35.5% vs 19.7% in the lowest Yost index quintile), more likely to have a history of heavy alcohol use (44.8% vs 36.2%), and less likely to have ALBI grade 1 disease (16.1% vs 26.5%).

Racial differences in HCC treatment

In unadjusted analyses, there were no significant differences in total lines of systemic therapy received, receipt of second-line therapy, receipt of novel systemic therapy, or receipt of LRT after systemic therapy among White, Black, and Asian patients (Table 2). A similar percentage of patients in each racial group received novel systemic therapies as they become more readily available over time (Figure 1). In fully adjusted analyses, Asian patients were 35% more likely than White patients to have received LRT after systemic therapy initiation (odds ratio = 1.35, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.01 to 1.79) and 78% more likely than White patients to have received novel systemic therapy (odds ratio = 1.78, 95% CI = 1.12 to 2.83), whereas there were no significant differences in treatment outcomes between White and Black/African patients (Figure 2, A and B, Tables S2 and S3). In sensitivity analyses using the smaller, nonimputed dataset, results were similar in direction and magnitude but no longer reached statistical significance for the receipt of LRT after systemic therapy initiation (Figure S1, A and B, Tables S4 and S5).

Racial differences in overall survival

Median OS was 8.0, 8.9, and 10.3 months for White, Black, and Asian patients, respectively (P = .04, Figure 3, A). In fully adjusted models, Asian patients experienced significantly longer overall survival compared with White patients (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.65 to 0.96), whereas Black/African American patients had similar OS to White patients in unadjusted and adjusted models (Figure 2, A and B, Table S6). These findings were similar with the stepwise addition of social factors, clinical characteristics, ALBI, AFP, and ECOG performance status to the regression models. Findings were similar in analysis of the non-imputed dataset but did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1, A and B, Table S7). When added to the fully adjusted model, receipt of novel therapy was associated with 37% better OS (HR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.56 to 0.70) but did not substantially change estimates of racial differences in survival (Table S8). Racial differences in survival did not change over time (Table S9).

Ethnic differences in HCC treatment

We noted statistically significant differences in total lines of systemic therapy and receipt of second-line and novel systemic therapies between Hispanic/Latino patients and non-Hispanic/Latino patients, but the numerical differences between groups were not clinically meaningful (Table 2). A similar percentage of patients in both ethnic groups received novel systemic therapies over time (Figure 1). There were no significant differences in treatment outcomes between groups on fully adjusted models using both the imputed and nonimputed datasets (Figure 2, C, Figure S1, Tables S2 and S5).

Ethnic differences in overall survival

Median OS was 10.5 versus 8.3 months for Hispanic versus non-Hispanic patients, respectively (P < .01, Figure 3, B). In fully adjusted models, Hispanic/Latino patients had 28% better OS (HR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.62 to 0.83) than non-Hispanic/Latino patients (Figure 2, C, Table S6). These findings were similar with the stepwise addition of social factors, clinical characteristics, ALBI, AFP, and ECOG performance status to the regression models but were attenuated in the analysis of the nonimputed dataset (Figure S1, C, Table S7). Ethnic differences in survival did not change over time or with inclusion of receipt of novel therapy (Tables S8 and S10).

Discussion

In this study of patients with HCC undergoing systemic therapy between 2011 and 2023, Asian and Hispanic patients experienced 32% and 26% better survival compared with White and non-Hispanic patients, respectively. In both cases, the survival advantages were observed in unadjusted analyses and exhibited minimal changes when adjusted for social and clinical factors, including liver function, AFP, and ECOG performance status. During a period of significant change in systemic treatment patterns for HCC, Asian patients had 78% greater odds than White and Black patients to receive novel systemic therapies, but receipt of novel therapies did not explain survival differences.

This study updates and contributes to the growing literature describing racial and ethnic differences in HCC treatment and survival. A previous meta-analysis of studies published through 2020 evaluating disparities among all patients with HCC, regardless of treatment, found that Asian patients had survival that was 19% and 27% better than White and Black patients, respectively.3 More recently, our prior work evaluating survival differences among older patients in the SEER-Medicare database with a diagnosis of HCC between 2014 and 2019 and treatment through 2020 showed similar results among all patients regardless of treatment but found no racial disparities in survival among patients treated with systemic therapy. Yet the current study shows a survival advantage for Asian and Hispanic patients receiving systemic therapy in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. The differing results between the 2 studies may reflect differences in study populations, especially age or insurance status. The SEER-Medicare study included only patients aged 65 years and older with traditional Medicare insurance, while the current study included adult patients of all ages and types of insurance in the Flatiron Health network. Age and insurance status may intersect with race and ethnicity in unmeasured ways (eg, racial differences in the generosity of insurance plans among those covered by commercial insurance) that contribute to the survival differences observed in this study.

Among patients receiving systemic therapy, this study raises questions about the common hypothesis that racial survival differences are related to unmeasured differences in liver function and tumor burden. This analysis incorporated ALBI and AFP, proxies for liver function and tumor burden, and found minimal changes in survival differences in models that included versus excluded these variables. At least among patients with disease burden sufficient to require systemic therapy and liver function adequate to allow it, ALBI and AFP do not explain the observed survival disparities. Nonetheless, these factors may still play a role in upstream treatment that affect survival (eg, receipt of LRT prior to systemic therapy, which was associated with better survival) and are important to include in studies of HCC treatment. It also remains possible that ALBI and AFP are insufficient proxies for disease burden and liver function in a cancer where precise location of tumor within the liver (eg, involving the portal vein) is prognostically and clinically important yet not captured in this or other population-level databases, and thus there could remain unmeasured racial differences in tumor burden and liver function that explain the survival differences. Similarly, reducing receipt of LRT to binary variables before and after receipt of systemic therapy may mask racial differences in intensity of treatment with LRT that could contribute to observed differences in survival, but accurate assessment of LRT was not feasible in this study.

Prior research evaluating first-line systemic therapy for HCC in 2017 and 2018 found that, compared with White patients, Asian patients had similar and Black patients had 29% lower likelihood of receiving immunotherapy, but the study was conducted early in the novel therapy era, during a period of considerable flux in treatment patterns and before approval of current first-line therapies.13,15,19 In contrast, our current study found no differences in receipt of novel therapies for Black compared with White patients but found that Asian patients had 78% higher odds of receiving novel therapies. Our analysis adjusted for common contraindicated comorbidities to some novel therapies but likely underdetected important contraindications unique to novel therapies (especially autoimmune disease); yet, unmeasured differences in contraindications are unlikely to explain the large difference in receipt of novel therapies. Notably, while receipt of novel therapies was associated with 37% better OS, inclusion of receipt of novel therapies in survival models did not change racial and ethnic survival differences, suggesting that receipt of these treatments does not drive the observed survival differences.

Finally, we also found that Hispanic patients had 28% better survival than non-Hispanic patients in this study. This is a larger difference than the 8% better survival associated with Hispanic ethnicity in the aforementioned meta-analysis of patients regardless of treatment status.13 As with the evaluation of racial differences in survival outcomes, inclusion of social and clinical factors resulted in minimal change in the estimates of survival differences by ethnicity. This “Hispanic paradox,” whereby Hispanic patients face socioeconomic disadvantage yet experience better health outcomes than non-Hispanic patients, has been long observed in healthcare and more recently in patients with HCC.3,20,21 The drivers of this survival benefit are unclear and worth continued investigation.

Overall, our 2 recent studies on racial and ethnic differences in HCC treatment and survival tell an illuminating story. Our SEER-Medicare analysis suggested that upstream factors such as access to gastroenterology specialists, early diagnosis, and local therapy play an important role in driving differences in survival for all patients with HCC, while treatment and survival outcomes are similar once patients start systemic therapy. Yet the current study finds persistent survival differences even among patients receiving systemic therapy. The strengths of the underlying datasets hint at explanations: Medicare claims–based analyses can comprehensively account for all care received, regardless of provider, while Flatiron data may miss treatment or comorbidities that are not captured in the underlying electronic medical record but includes laboratory data and encompasses patients of all ages and insurance types. Thus, studies that include patients of all ages and insurance types, and all care that patients receive are necessary to understand the totality of HCC care and to fully appreciate the extent and drivers of differences in HCC survival outcomes. For now, comprehensive databases are lacking and cross-database comparisons are necessary to evaluate HCC outcomes.

This study has several limitations. It uses a database with data abstracted from an EHR and may incompletely capture care that occurs outside of the EHR, which could have the effect of over- or underestimating differences in survival or treatment. Yet the database abstracts care documented in notes to capture some care outside of the EHR, and missingness would need to be systemically racially and ethnically biased to affect study results. In our adjusted models, we used ALBI and AFP close to the start of systemic therapy as proxies for liver function and tumor burden, but these measures incompletely capture important variation in liver function and tumor burden over time. Nonetheless, they provide more insight into the contribution of these important clinical features than is available in commonly used registry datasets.

In summary, we found that Asian and Hispanic patients with HCC receiving systemic therapy had better survival than patients of other races and ethnicities, and Asian patients were more likely to receive novel therapies. As adjustment for many social and clinical factors had minimal impact on these findings, additional research is needed to understand these survival differences and ensure that all patients have an equal opportunity to benefit from advances in HCC treatment.
