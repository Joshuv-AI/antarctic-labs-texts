Pulmonary Hypertension Molecular Switch: HIF Signaling Pathway
Authors: Yinhui Sun, Rongzhen Ding, Xianli Su, Li Qin, Lihuai Wang, Jian Yi, Ruicheng Hu, Shuying You, Zhenfei Tan, Aiguo Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a multifaceted disorder characterized by the crucial involvement of activated hypoxia‐inducible factors (HIFs). Recent investigations have unveiled HIFs' ability to orchestrate a wide array of signaling cascades, encompassing voltage‐gated potassium channels, non‐selective cation channels (NSCCs), Ca2+/PKC/MAPKs, PI3K/Akt, RhoA/ROCK, Notch, VEGF, and miRNA pathways. The interplay of these signaling cascades drives critical events, namely hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) and hypoxic pulmonary vascular remodeling (HPVR), which collectively contribute to the pathogenesis of PH. This review summarizes the role of HIF signaling in hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, pulmonary vascular remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, inflammation, non‐coding RNA regulation, and emerging HIF‐targeted therapies.

Background

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a heterogeneous and progressive disease characterized by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, ultimately leading to right ventricular failure and death [1, 2]. Due to its insidious onset, typical clinical symptoms, complex etiology, and high mortality rate, PH has been vividly described as the “malignant tumor of the cardiovascular system” [3]. Currently, PH is clinically classified into five major categories, with hypoxia being a significant contributing factor [4]. Hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vascular responses exhibit a biological paradox: acute hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is a protective and reversible response that improves ventilation‐perfusion matching, whereas chronic hypoxia‐associated pulmonary vascular remodeling is a maladaptive structural process that increases pulmonary vascular resistance and drives PH progression [5]. Although these two processes share common oxygen‐sensing mechanisms and downstream signaling modules, including HIF activation, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, ion‐channel remodeling, calcium signaling, endothelial dysfunction, metabolic reprogramming, and inflammatory communication, their phenotypic outcomes are clearly distinct.

HIFs were identified in the early 1990s during studies of hypoxia‐responsive regulation of the erythropoietin gene. They are heterodimeric transcription factors composed of an oxygen‐sensitive HIFα subunit and a constitutively expressed HIFβ subunit, also known as aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) [6] (Figure 1). The HIFα family comprises HIF‐1α, HIF‐2α, and HIF‐3α. HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α share a conserved structural organization that includes a basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) domain, two Per‐ARNT‐Sim (PAS) domains, an oxygen‐dependent degradation (ODD) domain, and C‐terminal transactivation domains. These domains mediate DNA binding, dimerization with HIFβ, oxygen‐dependent protein stability, and transcriptional activation, respectively [7, 8]. Under normoxic conditions, prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins (PHD1, PHD2, and PHD3) hydroxylate HIFα in an oxygen‐, Fe2+‐, and 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent manner, enabling recognition by von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) protein and subsequent proteasomal degradation [9, 10]. Hypoxia inhibits PHD activity, resulting in HIFα stabilization, nuclear translocation, dimerization with HIFβ, and binding to hypoxia‐responsive elements (HREs) in target genes to activate hypoxia‐responsive transcription [11, 12, 13]. Among the HIF isoforms, HIF‐1α is widely induced in response to hypoxia, HIF‐2α shows more tissue‐specific expression patterns and distinct biological functions, and HIF‐3α undergoes alternative splicing and is generally regarded as a negative regulator [14, 15]. The HIFβ subunit is constitutively expressed and largely insensitive to oxygen tension.

These findings have expanded our understanding of HIF‐mediated adaptation to hypoxia and its involvement in diverse physiological and pathological processes.

HIF and Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV), also known as the von Euler–Liljestrand mechanism, is a physiological response to alveolar hypoxia that improves ventilation‐perfusion matching and preserves oxygenation by redirecting blood flow toward better‐ventilated lung regions [16]. Nevertheless, sustained hypoxia may transform this adaptive response into PH. HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α are activated in both HPV and PH, highlighting a paradigm of mechanistic overlap but phenotypic divergence: these two conditions share core oxygen‐sensing pathways, including HIF signaling and ROS‐mediated pathways, but chronic PH additionally involves endothelial heterogeneity, vascular remodeling, and microenvironmental reprogramming [17, 18]. Thus, HIF signaling may serve as a molecular link between acute HPV and chronic PH, while the final biological outcomes depend on hypoxic duration, spatial distribution, and cellular context [19].

HIF Orchestrates Ion‐Channel Remodeling in HPV and Links Acute Vasoconstriction to Chronic Vascular Remodeling

In the acute stage of HPV, HIF‐related signaling intersects with oxygen‐sensitive ion‐channel regulation in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). Hypoxia inhibits voltage‐gated potassium channels, particularly Kv1.5 and Kv2.1, leading to membrane depolarization, activation of l‐type voltage‐dependent calcium channels (l‐VDCCs), and increased extracellular Ca2+ influx [20]. In parallel, hypoxia enhances the activity or expression of transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) channels, including TRPC1 and TRPC6, which further amplify receptor‐operated and store‐operated Ca2+ entry [21]. The Na+/H+ exchanger isoform 1 (NHE1) also contributes to this response by increasing intracellular pH and favoring PASMC contraction and proliferation [22]. Collectively, inhibition of Kv channels, activation of TRPC channels, stimulation of NHE1, and enhanced intracellular Ca2+ accumulation form a functional module that links oxygen sensing to PASMC contraction. This module is highly relevant to HPV because it rapidly increases pulmonary vascular tone in response to alveolar hypoxia [23, 24].

However, when hypoxic signaling is sustained, the same molecular module is progressively converted from a vasomotor program into a remodeling program. HIF‐1α can induce or stabilize the expression of genes involved in glycolysis, ion transport, Ca2+ handling, proliferation, and survival [25, 26]. In PASMCs, chronic HIF‐1α activation promotes a shift from a contractile phenotype toward a synthetic, proliferative, and apoptosis‐resistant phenotype.

RhoA/ROCK signaling further reinforces this transition by increasing Ca2+ sensitization and by upregulating HIF‐1α‐dependent TRPC1/TRPC6 expression, thereby enhancing store‐operated and receptor‐operated Ca2+ entry. This creates a feed‐forward loop in which hypoxia activates HIF and RhoA/ROCK signaling, HIF increases TRPC‐mediated Ca2+ influx, and sustained Ca2+ elevation promotes both vasoconstriction and structural remodeling. Thus, the HIF–ion channel–Ca2+ axis represents a point of mechanistic continuity between HPV and PH, whereas the persistence and cellular stabilization of this axis determine the transition from reversible vasoconstriction to chronic remodeling.

Recent single‐cell RNA sequencing and multi‐omics studies provide important support for this distinction. In acute HPV, the dominant cellular event is functional contraction of PASMCs, coordinated with endothelial oxygen sensing and vasoactive mediator release [27]. In chronic PH, however, single‐cell studies reveal widespread remodeling across multiple vascular and immune cell populations. Single‐cell analyses of PH lungs and pulmonary arteries have revealed altered macrophage, monocyte, fibroblast, and myofibroblast states. In particular, macrophage‐derived signals may promote PASMC phenotypic switching, while endothelial–mural cell communication through pathways such as NDRG1, PTGF‐β/SMAD, and inflammatory ligand–receptor interactions may stabilize the remodeled vascular wall [28, 29].

Taken together, HIF activation provides a mechanistic bridge between HPV and PH, but the biological outcome depends on context. In acute regional hypoxia, HIF‐related oxygen‐sensing pathways and ion‐channel responses contribute to adaptive vasoconstriction and ventilation/perfusion matching. In chronic hypoxia‐induced PH, persistent activation of these core modules drives transcriptional reprogramming, metabolic adaptation, endothelial heterogeneity, PASMC phenotypic switching, pericyte activation, inflammatory communication, and extracellular matrix remodeling. This explains the apparent paradox of shared mechanisms but divergent phenotypes: HPV is primarily a reversible vasomotor response, whereas PH is a chronic multicellular remodeling disease.

HIF Integrates Multiple Vasoconstrictive Signals to Drive the Phenotypic Manifestation of HPV

HIF integrates multiple vasoconstrictive pathways to drive HPV. Studies have demonstrated that hypoxia‐induced mitogenic factor (HIMF/FIZZ1/RELMα) acts as a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor, with HIF‐1α functioning as a key downstream transcription factor in HIMF‐induced vascular remodeling and PH pathogenesis [30]. HIMF stimulates intracellular Ca2+ release in PASMCs in a Ca2+‐dependent manner through the phospholipase C‐inositol trisphosphate (PLC‐IP3) pathway, and Gαq/11 protein‐coupled receptors and ryanodine receptors contribute to the increase in [Ca2+]i [31]. However, contradictory findings exist regarding the role of the PLC‐IP3 pathway, as some studies have reported inconsistent Ca2+ response patterns across different vascular beds. The proposed mechanism involving Gαq/11‐coupled receptors and ryanodine receptors requires further validation, given limitations in current experimental models that often utilize non‐physiological agonist concentrations [32].

Further research has revealed that hypoxia induces HIF‐1α expression, downregulates Kv channel subunits, increases TRPC and NHE1 expression and NHE activity, and elevates pHi [23, 24]. The sustained increase in [Ca2+]i may originate from changes in resting membrane potential caused by decreased Kv1.5 expression. Hypoxia persistently activates Rho kinase, enhancing vasoconstriction and HIF‐1α expression, ultimately leading to sustained contraction, promoting pulmonary vascular remodeling and PH [1]. While HIF‐1α has been implicated in hypoxia‐induced proliferation inhibition via l‐VDCC regulation, this interpretation carries uncertainty due to: (1) most studies employing acute hypoxia models whereas PH develops chronically; (2) potential off‐target effects of pharmacological inhibitors used in key experiments [25]; and (3) variability in l‐VDCC isoform expression across species.

Endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) is another key player in the development of HIF‐mediated hypoxic PH. ET‐1 binds to receptors on PASMCs, increases the Ca2+ sensitivity of contractile proteins, upregulates HIF‐1α, and forms a positive feedback loop that enhances HIF activation. ET‐1 increases HIF‐1α protein expression by activating calcineurin and dephosphorylating RACK1, thereby enhancing HIF activity and promoting the secretion of mediators such as ET‐1 by PASMCs, which contribute to PH progression [33]. The ET‐1/HIF‐1α positive feedback hypothesis, though mechanistically plausible, presents several methodological concerns: many supporting studies rely on cultured PASMCs without accounting for endothelial modulation in vivo; calcineurin‐RACK1 experiments typically use supraphysiological ET‐1 doses; and the purported feedback loop lacks direct evidence from conditional knockout models.

Moreover, the role of the ROS/Kv/HIF axis in the development of hypoxic PH has garnered significant attention. Research has shown that regulating cellular redox status and ROS levels affects the intensity of HPV, suggesting that ROS may be involved in hypoxic signal transduction [34]. ROS can activate l‐VDCCs and intracellular Ca2+ channels and inhibit Kv channels. Hypoxia can also increase HIF‐1α stability and transcriptional activity through ROS‐induced Ref‐1 and YAP activation [35]. Antioxidants (such as NAC) improve HPV in animal models, but have not shown consistent benefit in PH clinical trials [36], which may be related to the spatiotemporal specificity (acute vs. chronic) of ROS action.

The ROS/Kv/HIF axis hypothesis, although supported by substantial evidence, faces interpretation challenges. ROS measurements in hypoxia studies show poor reproducibility due to methodological variability. Moreover, the proposed Ref‐1/YAP activation pathway remains controversial, with some groups failing to replicate these findings [37]. In addition, most supporting evidence derives from acute hypoxia experiments, whereas chronic PH may involve distinct mechanisms. Notably, antioxidant trials in PH have yielded mixed results, suggesting more complex redox regulation than current models propose.

HIF Counterbalances HPV by Activating Vasodilatory Signaling

HIF also activates counter‐regulatory vasodilatory signals that restrain HPV. Nitric oxide (NO), as a natural vasodilator, exhibits significant physiological/pathological duality in its regulatory mechanisms. Early studies (e.g., Hu R et al., 2002) observed a positive correlation between HIF‐1α and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression in rat models [38]. Regarding functional differentiation of NOS isoforms, current evidence indicates that endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) maintains basal NO levels [39], while iNOS becomes overactivated during hypoxia. Notably, the “pathological role” hypothesis of iNOS primarily derives from in vitro cell studies (e.g., Martin Lee's team [40, 41]), most of which employed 48‐h continuous hypoxia conditions, a scenario markedly different from clinical intermittent hypoxia. The downregulation of HIF‐α expression by NO may be an important mechanism for inhibiting HPV. These findings establish the crucial role of HIF‐1α in inducing human iNOS gene expression in response to cytokine stimulation. Therefore, the net effect of HIF‐regulated NO signaling may depend on the NOS isoform, cellular source, inflammatory context, and disease stage.

Although HIF‐1α‐mediated iNOS transcription has been widely reported [40, 41, 42], critical controversies persist: Discrepancies exist among HIF‐1α knockout models from different laboratories due to variations in Cre recombinase activity, such as the use of Tie2‐Cre by C Guignabert et al. [43] vs. VE‐cadherin‐Cre by Toullec A et al. [44]. Cell‐specific issues also remain, as alveolar epithelial cells and endothelial cells (ECs) demonstrate differences in HIF‐1α response thresholds (data from single‐cell sequencing studies [45]. Therefore, although the transcriptional activation of iNOS has been widely confirmed, its net effect in intermittent hypoxia (such as OSAHS) remains questionable. We suggest prioritizing research on the dynamic balance between eNOS and iNOS (rather than focusing on a single target), as this may mitigate the risk of immune deficiency caused by long‐term inhibition of iNOS.

Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), also referred to as B‐type natriuretic peptide, is a biologically active hormone primarily synthesized and secreted by the ventricular myocardium [46]. Regarding the relationship between BNP and PH, it is currently believed that BNP is closely related to hypoxia. Research has shown that BNP can dilate pulmonary blood vessels and reduce pulmonary arterial pressure, thereby alleviating right ventricular hypertrophy and PH caused by hypoxia [47]. Researchers investigating the association between plasma N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) and PH in individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) discovered that COPD patients with mild PH exhibited substantially higher levels of BNP compared to those with COPD alone. As part of the body's protective responses to hypoxia, NT‐proBNP expression in lung tissue increases during hypoxia, thereby increasing the permeability of pulmonary vascular smooth muscle to calcium ions, increasing calcium influx, causing pulmonary vasoconstriction, and leading to an increase in pulmonary arterial pressure. Therefore, BNP is an effective non‐traumatic parameter for assessing pulmonary arterial pressure levels [48].

Another study confirmed that exercise preconditioning can provide protection to ischemic brain tissue through HIF‐1α, while HIF‐1α cooperates with other factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and erythropoietin (EPO), to increase BNP expression, causing vasodilation, thereby increasing ischemic blood flow and then alleviating injuries caused by ischemia [49].

Studies have shown that elevated intracellular calcium levels activate Ca2+ calmodulin‐dependent myosin light chain kinase, resulting in enhanced cellular tension. In the context of PASMCs, HIF‐1α has been observed to decrease vascular tension by attenuating the phosphorylation of myosin light chain (MLC). These findings suggest that the primary function of HIF‐1α lies in modulating vascular tone rather than influencing vascular remodeling processes [50] (Figure 2).

Collectively, these findings suggest that HIF‐mediated signaling in HPV is not unidirectional but rather bidirectional and context‐dependent: the same oxygen‐sensing network can amplify vasoconstrictive responses while simultaneously engaging compensatory vasodilatory pathways. This mechanistic overlap, coupled with phenotypic divergence, provides the conceptual basis for distinguishing acute HPV from chronic HPVR.

Under hypoxic conditions, HIFs are stabilized and activated in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells. Activated HIFs transcriptionally regulate the expression of various ion channels and transporters, such as transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) channels, l‐type voltage‐dependent calcium channels (l‐VDCCs), neuropilin‐1 (NRP1), and potassium (K+) channels, leading to an increase in intracellular Ca2+ concentration and resulting in PASMC contraction. Furthermore, HIFs induce the expression of contraction‐related factors, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) and endothelin‐1 (ET‐1), further exacerbating HPV. Concurrently, HIFs upregulate the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), inducing vasodilation and thereby inhibiting the progression of pulmonary hypertension to a certain extent. In conclusion, HIFs play a pivotal role in HPV by transcriptionally regulating multiple vasoconstrictor and vasodilator factors, making them a crucial entry point for investigating the pathogenesis of pulmonary.

HIF and Hypoxic Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling

Chronic hypoxia induces hypoxic pulmonary vascular remodeling (HPVR), a pathological process characterized by pulmonary arterial wall thickening, smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, endothelial dysfunction, neomuscularization of distal vessels, and elastic lamina remodeling (Figure 3). HPVR is a core structural hallmark of hypoxia‐induced PH and reflects the transition from acute adaptive vasoconstriction to sustained vascular remodeling. HIF regulates multiple pathways involved in this process, including MLCK, p‐MLC [51], PI3K/AKT/mTOR [52], VEGF [53], miRNA [54], and NF‐κB signaling [55], thereby promoting vascular cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and inflammation, while disrupting apoptotic balance. In experimental models, HIF inhibition can attenuate arterial wall thickening, vascular muscularization, and right ventricular hypertrophy, supporting its central role in hypoxia‐driven PH progression [56, 57].

HIF signaling in hypoxic pulmonary vascular remodeling. Hypoxia‐induced ROS stabilizes HIF‐1α and activates multiple signaling pathways in pulmonary endothelial cells, PASMCs, macrophages, pericytes, and the extracellular matrix. HIF‐related signaling regulates VEGF/VEGFR, AKT/ERK–FOXM1, SDF‐1–mediated inflammatory pathways, and TGF‐β/PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascades. These processes promote endothelial proliferation, PASMC activation, inflammatory cell recruitment, and distal artery neomuscularization.

The Role of HIF in the Balance of Proliferation and Death of PASMCs Induced by Hypoxia

Emerging evidence suggests that dysregulated PASMC proliferation and imbalanced cell death are pivotal drivers of HPVR. Abnormal cell proliferation has long been recognized as an important mechanism of PH, but cell death accompanied by inflammation dysregulation, cell dysfunction, and tissue damage also plays a crucial role in the progression and pathogenesis of PH. A key feature of PH pathology is the dysregulated proliferation of pulmonary vascular cells, primarily PASMCs and ECs, with HIFs implicated as central but context‐dependent regulators. Early studies demonstrated that mice deficient in HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α exhibit attenuated hypoxia‐induced vascular remodeling [58, 59]. In recent years, researchers have conducted in‐depth studies on PASMC proliferation and HIF‐regulated signaling pathways. In vivo observations suggest HIF‐2α predominantly drives EC proliferation [60], whereas HIF‐1α promotes PASMC hyperplasia [61], with EC proliferation purportedly preceding PASMC expansion under hypoxia [62]. However, this temporal and spatial dichotomy lacks universal validation, as some studies have reported concurrent proliferation of both cell types. It may be that HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α regulate largely distinct gene sets during chronic hypoxia [63], suggesting model‐specific variability (e.g., chronic vs. intermittent hypoxia). Studies have shown that the precise molecular cascades linking HIF isoforms to distinct proliferative responses remain unclear. For instance, HIF‐2α's role in ECs may involve nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT) [64], whereas HIF‐1α's PASMC‐specific effects could stem from metabolic reprogramming [65]. In addition, upstream regulation and pathway crosstalk also play an important role. Recent work implicates PI3K/AKT/mTOR as a critical upstream activator of HIF‐1α in PASMCs, with PDGF‐induced proliferation dependent on this axis [66]. Yet, several knowledge gaps persist: Isoform selectivity remains unclear, as whether PI3K/AKT/mTOR similarly regulates HIF‐2α in ECs has been underexplored, despite evidence of mTORC2‐HIF‐2α crosstalk in cancer models [67]. Currently, although interventions targeting HIF or regulating HIF signaling pathways can inhibit PASMC proliferation, most of them are limited to in vitro studies and animal experiments, and their clinical efficacy needs further verification. In summary, identifying the convergence points among multiple signaling pathways for targeted intervention is the key to inhibiting PASMC proliferation and preventing or reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling.

In recent years, in addition to classical apoptosis, some new types of programmed cell death (PCD) modes such as pyroptosis have been found to be involved in the HPVR process and play an important role in the development of PH [68]. Hypoxia can reduce apoptosis in PASMCs by regulating mitochondrial apoptosis or proliferation signaling pathways, which is a protective response to prevent vascular wall thickening [69]. Yao et al. found that the feedback loop between HIF‐1α and 15‐lipoxygenase (15‐LO)/15‐Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (15‐HETE) can significantly enhance the anti‐apoptotic effect of PASMCs induced by hypoxia, which may become a new target for PH treatment [70]. Evidence suggests that hypoxia causes increased proliferation and decreased apoptosis of PASMCs by regulating gene expression, leading to changes in pulmonary vascular structure and triggering PH [71].

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved cellular self‐degradation process that regulates the balance between cell survival and death. Studies indicate that the HIF‐1α/miR‐26a‐5p/PFKFB3/ULK1/2 pathway promotes hypoxia‐induced vascular remodeling in PH by modulating autophagy [72], while the PI3K p85α/HIF‐1α axis further accelerates disease progression by enhancing fatty acid uptake and hyperactivating mitophagy [73]. However, contrasting evidence suggests that Maresin‐1 enhances PINK1‐dependent mitophagy via the ALXR/HSP90α axis, effectively clearing damaged mitochondria and maintaining cardiomyocyte energy homeostasis [74]. Similarly, 1,8‐cineole has been shown to restore Connexin43 expression and improve right ventricular function through AMPK/ULK1‐mediated selective autophagy [75]. This apparent paradox may stem from the spatiotemporal specificity and cell‐type‐dependent regulation of autophagy: in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), it manifests as pro‐proliferative non‐selective autophagy hyperactivation, whereas in cardiomyocytes, it serves a protective role in mitochondrial quality control. Recent single‐cell RNA sequencing studies further reveal significant heterogeneity in autophagy‐related gene expression across PH cell populations, providing new insights into this dual functionality [76]. Future research should focus on deciphering autophagy signatures in distinct cell subpopulations, developing cell‐specific autophagy modulation strategies, and exploring stage‐selective interventions to achieve breakthroughs in PH therapy.

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of regulated cell death, with gasdermin D identified as a key executioner in 2015, and the activation of NACHT, LRR and PYD domains‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and caspase‐1/11 are its key events. Studies have shown that HIF‐1α overexpression upregulates key pyroptosis‐related proteins, including NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, GSDMD, and GSDME [77]. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream effector caspase‐1/11 serves as a critical driver of pyroptosis. In hypoxia‐induced PH models, knockout of NLRP3 or caspase‐1 significantly alleviates pulmonary vascular resistance [78, 79]. HIF plays a central role in regulating pyroptosis and the pathogenesis of PH, primarily through the PHD2/HIF‐1α signaling pathway. Xi J et al. demonstrated that astragaloside IV suppresses PHD2, thereby stabilizing HIF‐1α and reducing NLRP3/caspase‐1‐mediated pyroptosis and fibrosis in PASMCs, leading to improved hemodynamic parameters [68]. Additionally, another study revealed that G protein‐coupled receptor 146 (GPR146) induces pulmonary arterial endothelial cell (PAEC) pyroptosis via the NLRP3/caspase‐1 axis, contributing to endothelial injury and PH progression [80]. These findings highlight HIF‐1α as a hypoxia‐responsive hub that orchestrates pyroptosis‐related molecular events through multiple targets, driving PH.

Ferroptosis, an iron‐dependent form of non‐apoptotic programmed cell death (PCD) discovered in 2012, has been implicated in PH. Studies reveal that SLC7A11 inhibits ferroptosis and promotes proliferation in PH, thereby restoring the balance between cell death and proliferation in PASMCs [81]. Kazmirczak F et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis is activated in PH lung tissues and hypoxia‐exposed PASMCs, while the iron chelator deferoxamine suppresses ferroptosis signaling and alleviates PH progression. Conversely, overexpression of key ferroptosis regulators such as acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (ACSL4) exacerbates HPVR, underscoring the pivotal role of ferroptosis in PH pathogenesis [82]. Growing evidence supports the hypothesis that ferroptosis contributes to arterial hypertension progression. However, only a limited number of studies have explored the involvement of the HIF signaling pathway in PH. Research indicates that elevated MIR210HG levels promote the transition of PASMCs from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype under hypoxia by activating an autophagy‐dependent ferroptosis pathway. Notably, HIF‐2α overexpression in PASMCs under hypoxia significantly reverses the phenotypic changes induced by MIR210HG knockdown [83].

In summary, new types of PCD such as autophagy, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis are involved in regulating the HPVR process, and interventions targeting these cell death pathways may provide new ideas for PH prevention and treatment. However, current research on these HIF‐involved PCD modes in PH is still in its infancy, and their mechanisms of action, interrelationships, and clinical translational value need further in‐depth exploration.

HIF Signaling in Hypoxia‐Induced Vascular Neomuscularization

Recent attention has focused on the role of HIF in hypoxia‐induced vascular neomuscularization. The pathogenesis of PH is significantly influenced by the thickening of the pulmonary artery wall and the appearance of newly formed muscle in distal pulmonary vessels [84]. HIF participates in this process by regulating multiple angiogenesis‐related genes and signaling pathways. A systematic study by Zhang et al. revealed that smooth muscle cell (SMC)‐specific Becn1 knockout induced apoptosis, reversing hypoxia‐ and/or Sugen 5416‐mediated distal muscularization and PH. Further investigations demonstrated that chronic hypoxia‐induced activation of the HIFα/PDGF‐B axis in ECs is essential for the non‐cell‐autonomous, Beclin1‐mediated survival of pathological distal arterial SMCs [85].

Another study identified increased fibrotic remodeling in human alveolar capillary dysplasia with misalignment of pulmonary veins (ACDMPV) lungs. Using blastocyst complementation with embryonic stem cells carrying the S52F Foxf1 mutation, chimeric mice were generated, revealing that Foxf1 WT/S52F embryonic stem cells preferentially differentiate into pulmonary myofibroblasts [86]. Although various cell types can differentiate into myofibroblasts, fibroblast‐to‐myofibroblast differentiation (FMD) is considered the primary source. Mechanistically, TGF‐β1 promotes Brg1 redistribution from distal intergenic sites of stem cell genes to promoter regions of myofibroblast‐associated genes—a process blocked by PFI‐3 [87]. These findings provide insights into the epigenetic regulation of resident vascular progenitor cell differentiation.

Using single‐cell RNA sequencing combined with lineage tracing, Hu et al. demonstrated that under steady‐state conditions, cells are abundantly present around distal non‐muscular vessels, contributing to ~50% of newly formed ACTA2+ (α‐smooth muscle actin‐positive) cells around these typically non‐muscular vessels. During reverse remodeling, GLI1+ lineage‐derived cells were extensively eliminated alongside muscularization reversal. Partial ablation of GLI1+ cells markedly prevented hypoxia‐induced vascular remodeling and attenuated increases in right ventricular systolic pressure and hypertrophy [88]. Valentina Biasin et al. further identified, via cell‐type‐specific next‐generation sequencing, that myofibroblasts synthesize pro‐fibrotic proteins (e.g., collagen I and fibronectin) in response to elevated α‐smooth muscle actin levels [89]. Saito et al. found that the histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) inhibitor Tubastatin A suppresses TGF‐β1‐induced collagen expression by inhibiting the PI3K‐Akt‐HIF‐1α‐VEGF pathway [90], suggesting HIF plays an indirect role in fibroblast transformation.

Additionally, signaling pathways such as interleukin‐33 (IL‐33)/ST2 receptor [91] and Ras homolog family member A (RhoA)/Rho‐associated protein kinase (ROCK) participate in angiogenesis by modulating the HIF/VEGF pathway [92]. HIF‐1α in smooth muscle contributes significantly to the pulmonary vascular remodeling induced by chronic hypoxia [93]. NO, a type of reactive nitrogen species, generally stabilizes HIF‐1α and stimulates secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) from ECs and macrophages, thereby inducing angiogenesis and muscle regeneration [94]. HIF‐1 promotes angiogenesis in adult organisms through multiple mechanisms, including transcriptional activation of angiogenic genes and their receptors [95, 96]. The regulation of chemokines like stromal cell‐derived factor‐1α (SDF‐1α), sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P), and their receptors (CXCR4 and S1PR) facilitates endothelial progenitor cell recruitment to hypoxic sites [97].

Collectively, these findings underscore HIF's pivotal role in coordinating vascular myogenic processes and highlight the importance of HIF signaling in hypoxia‐associated PH development (Table 1).

HIF Upregulates Inflammatory Mediators to Promote Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling

Inflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of PH, participating in triggering and maintaining pulmonary vascular remodeling. In PH patients and animal models, inflammation has been found to be an early consequence of hypoxia exposure [108]. Studies in chronic hypoxic rat models have shown that mast cell accumulation, increased inflammatory cell infiltration, and circulating monocyte aggregation promote the process of pulmonary vascular remodeling [109]. HIF‐1α also plays a key role in controlling pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways. Fitzpatrick SF et al. found that hypoxic conditions enhance the stability of HIF‐1α, enabling its interaction with the p65 subunit of nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB). This interaction amplifies NF‐κB's transcriptional activity and stimulates the production of inflammatory mediators, including interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), IL‐8, and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) [105]. These inflammatory factors stimulate the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells and induce extracellular matrix deposition, leading to progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling [110]. At the same time, the recruited inflammatory cells themselves can also secrete various cytokines and chemokines, forming a vicious cycle and exacerbating the progression of PH [111].

New research reveals that HIMF, along with its human counterpart RELMβ, markedly upregulates VEGF‐A, Ca2+, and IL‐6 expression via pathways mediated by HIF‐1α and NF‐κB. These two factors are key mediators in hypoxia‐induced inflammatory responses and the pathogenesis of PH [22, 30]. Mizuno et al. used a rat model of PH induced by a combination of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) tyrosine kinase inhibitor Sugen 5416 and Th2 immune system activation (Su‐ovalbumin, Su‐OVA) to identify 22 factors crucial for PH formation. The study found that the expression of HIF‐1α, VEGF, and IL‐6 in lung tissue was significantly increased after immunization with ovalbumin (OVA) alone or Su‐OVA treatment. Notably, the expression level of IL‐6 protein in lung tissue was positively correlated with mean pulmonary arterial pressure, suggesting that the elevation of HIF‐1α and IL‐6 in lung tissue caused by Th2 inflammation may be a key component of PH pathogenesis [102, 103]. HIF‐1 is a key downstream signaling molecule of HIMF in the formation of PH. The pro‐inflammatory microenvironment generated in the pulmonary vascular system involves several known HIF target genes, including NF‐κB, SDF‐1, and VEGF [112, 113]. Taken together, the evidence indicates that HIF's influence on the progression of hypoxia‐induced PH extends beyond its direct actions on ECs and pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). It may also exacerbate the condition by cultivating a pro‐inflammatory milieu.

The Role of HIF and Non‐Coding RNAs in HPVR

High‐throughput RNA sequencing of isogenic cell lines with silenced HIF subunits has revealed that HIF regulates a broad spectrum of RNAs, including non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) [59, 114] (Table 2). Increasing evidence shows that miRNAs are closely involved in hypoxia‐induced PASMC proliferation and pulmonary hypertension. For example, downregulation of miR‐103/107 under hypoxia removes its inhibitory effect on HIF‐1β, leading to excessive PASMC proliferation and vascular remodeling [117]. Similarly, hypoxia suppresses miR‐206, thereby activating the HIF‐1α/Fhl‐1 pathway and promoting PH progression [118]. Although HIF is central to hypoxic adaptation, it is insufficient alone to drive the full cellular response, and miRNAs interact with both HIF‐dependent and HIF‐independent pathways to form a complex regulatory network [120, 124]. More than 90 miRNAs have been reported to change under hypoxia, some of which are direct HIF targets. For instance, miR‐210 is induced by HIF and stabilizes HIF‐1α by targeting GPD1L [127], while miR‐424 stabilizes HIFα subunits in endothelial cells by targeting CUL2 [129]. Conversely, hypoxia suppresses miRNAs such as miR‐199a‐5p, which normally target HIFα transcripts, thereby enhancing HIF signaling [130].

Several miRNAs also regulate PH through the PI3K/Akt/HIF‐1α axis. miR‐21 increases P‐Akt and HIF‐1α activity, and a positive feedback loop between miR‐21 and HIF‐1 has been reported, in which HIF‐1 enhances miR‐21 transcription, while miR‐21 promotes HIF‐1α expression via the PTEN/Akt pathway [122, 123]. Various studies have indicated that miRNAs play an important role in regulating HIF‐induced PASMC proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, and angiogenesis in PH [132]. Among these miRNAs, miR‐103/107 and miR‐206 have been further implicated in hypoxia‐induced PASMC proliferation and vascular remodeling in PH [117]. Specifically, miR‐103/107 targets HIF‐1β, and its downregulation under hypoxic conditions leads to loss of inhibitory control over HIF‐1β, resulting in excessive PASMC proliferation and pathological vascular remodeling in PH. Similarly, hypoxia suppresses miR‐206, which normally modulates the HIF‐1α/Fhl‐1 pathway, further exacerbating PH progression [118]. While miR‐103/107 inhibitors show therapeutic potential by restoring HIF‐1β regulation, their clinical translation faces challenges in delivery efficiency and tissue specificity. Likewise, miR‐206‐based interventions, though effective in animal models, require optimization of human homology and dosing strategies before therapeutic application. These findings highlight the interconnected regulatory roles of miR‐103/107 and miR‐206 in hypoxic PH while underscoring key translational hurdles that must be addressed. Furthermore, MicroRNA‐143‐5p can promote the phenotypic transformation, proliferation, and migration of PASMCs under hypoxic conditions by directly targeting HIF‐1α [131]. MicroRNA‐150 alleviates hypoxia‐induced excessive proliferation and migration of PASMCs by reducing HIF‐1α expression [119]. The discovery and reporting of key miRNAs regulating HIF in PASMCs make them promising candidates for new strategies in the treatment of PH. In addition, to date, only a few miRNAs have been shown to control the expression of HIF‐2α and HIF‐3α, but they have not been validated in PH [128, 133].

Over the past decade, lncRNAs have also emerged as key regulators of PH and HIF signaling [134, 135]. lncRNAs, defined as non‐coding RNAs longer than 200 nucleotides, regulate gene expression, proliferation, differentiation, endothelial dysfunction, and smooth muscle cell remodeling [136]. Transcriptomic studies have shown that MALAT1 expression is elevated in the lung tissues and pulmonary artery PASMCs of PH patients. Notably, silencing MALAT1 expression has been found to alleviate PH symptoms in rat models, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target [137, 138]. MEG3 is a tumor‐suppressive lncRNA that is downregulated in various cancers. However, MEG3 is highly expressed in the plasma and PASMCs of PH patients, inducing apoptosis resistance in PASMCs through activating the p53 pathway, and knockdown of MEG3 can alleviate PH in rats [121, 139]. PAXIP1‐AS1 is increased in PH and promotes PASMC proliferation and migration by activating the IL‐6/STAT3 pathway, whereas TUG1 appears to exert a protective role [140].

Importantly, lncRNAs and HIFs regulate each other in PH. Some lncRNAs can indirectly affect the progression of PH by regulating HIF expression and activity. For example, lncRNA H19 may interfere with vascular remodeling in hypoxia‐induced pulmonary hypertension by upregulating the expression of HIF‐1α and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in ECs [54]. On the other hand, hypoxia/HIF‐related signaling may regulate PH‐associated lncRNAs. lncRNA‐MEG3 is upregulated in hypoxia‐induced PH and promotes pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation by sponging miR‐328‐3p and increasing IGF1R expression. Moreover, lncRNA H19 promotes angiogenesis in mouse pulmonary artery endothelial cells via the HIF‐1α/VEGF pathway, supporting a role for hypoxia/HIF‐related lncRNA regulation in PH progression [54, 121]. HIF‐1α can also induce the expression of PAXIP1‐AS1 mRNA and MIAT [141]. These findings indicate that the lncRNA–HIF network plays an important role in PH pathogenesis. Although targets such as MALAT1 have shown therapeutic potential in animal models, clinical translation remains limited by insufficient human safety data, high costs of modified antisense oligonucleotides, and possible off‐target effects. Moreover, the dual roles of MEG3, the vascular homeostasis risks of targeting the H19/HIF‐1α/VEGF axis, and the unconfirmed efficacy of HIF inhibitors such as PT2977 in PH suggest that lncRNA‐based therapies may need to be developed independently or selectively from direct HIF inhibition. Future studies should further clarify key lncRNA–HIF signaling networks and evaluate their potential as early diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for precision PH treatment.

Therapeutic Implications: Opportunities and Barriers of Targeting HIF‐Centered Hypoxic Signaling

Traditionally, treatment approaches for PH have mainly centered on regulating crucial elements within five molecular pathways linked to the disorder: voltage‐gated l‐type calcium channels, nitric oxide (NO), cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), endothelin (ET), and prostacyclin (PGI2). However, these treatments are largely palliative, aimed at alleviating symptoms through oxygen support, NO inhalation, calcium channel blockers, phosphodiesterase inhibitors (PDEIs), ET receptor antagonists, and prostaglandins. They rarely reverse disease progression or restore right ventricular (RV) function. Considering the multifaceted involvement of the hypoxia‐inducible factor signaling pathway in the onset and advancement of PH, scientists have recently focused on developing therapeutic agents that target this pathway. It is evident that the HIF regulatory pathway is an intricate network encompassing numerous signaling cascades and interconnected mechanisms, each representing a potential target or step for targeted intervention (Figure 4).

Current HIF‐targeted or HIF‐modulating strategies in PH can be broadly divided into several mechanistic categories. The first approach involves the direct suppression of HIF‐α accumulation or HIF transcriptional activity. In 2008, Zhang et al. used a high‐throughput HIF activity screening assay to identify several clinically used compounds from the Johns Hopkins Drug Library that suppress HIF activity [142]. Among these candidates, digoxin, a cardiac glycoside long used for the treatment of heart failure because of its positive inotropic effects, was shown in animal experiments to inhibit HIF‐1α protein accumulation and HIF‐1 activity in vivo [143]. Topotecan (TPT), a topoisomerase inhibitor commonly used in cancer therapy, has also been reported to inhibit HIF‐1α and attenuate hypoxia‐induced upregulation of transient receptor potential channels (TRPCs) and Ca2+ influx in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) [26]. By limiting Ca2+‐dependent PASMC proliferation, TPT ameliorated hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH. In addition, celastramycin has been shown to reduce the protein levels of HIF‐1α (involved in altered aerobic metabolism) and NF‐κB (which promotes proinflammatory signaling) in PH‐PASMCs, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine secretion [144]. These findings support the therapeutic potential of HIF‐1α inhibition in limiting PASMC proliferation, metabolic remodeling, and inflammation. However, given the pleiotropic pharmacological actions of these agents, it remains difficult to determine whether their protective effects in PH models are mediated exclusively through HIF inhibition.

A second strategy involves disruption of HIF complex formation rather than reduction of HIF‐α protein abundance. Acriflavine, a topical antiseptic, inhibits HIF activity without decreasing HIF‐1α protein levels; instead, it prevents HIF transcriptional activity by blocking HIF subunit dimerization [145]. Both digoxin and acriflavine improved outcomes in rodent PH models, suggesting that suppression of excessive HIF signaling may attenuate pulmonary vascular remodeling. However, the translation of these findings into clinical PH therapy is limited by differences in drug metabolism, physiological responses, and disease complexity between experimental animals and humans. Moreover, systemic inhibition of HIF signaling may influence multiple physiological processes beyond the pulmonary circulation, including erythropoiesis, angiogenesis, mitochondrial metabolism, immune responses, and tissue adaptation to hypoxia.

A third therapeutic strategy is modulation of the canonical prolyl hydroxylase domain protein–von Hippel–Lindau–HIF axis. Because prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins (PHDs) require oxygen and iron as cofactors, iron availability can indirectly regulate HIF stability. Consequently, iron supplementation has been considered an indirect approach for modulating the HIF system, since PHD activity is highly sensitive to iron status [146, 147]. Conversely, HIF‐prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors (HIF‐PHIs) and other PHD‐modulating compounds may stabilize HIF‐α proteins by limiting PHD‐mediated hydroxylation and subsequent VHL‐dependent degradation. In this context, caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) has been reported to inhibit HIF‐PHD activity and delay VHL‐dependent HIF‐1α degradation [148, 149]. PHDs may also contribute to hypoxic pulmonary vascular remodeling (HPVR) in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) by regulating HIF‐1α gene expression. These observations highlight the therapeutic relevance of the PHD–VHL–HIF oxygen‐sensing pathway. However, they also illustrate a central dilemma in HIF‐targeted therapy: both inhibition and stabilization of HIF signaling may exert protective or detrimental effects depending on disease stage, cell type, PH subtype, and the balance between adaptive and maladaptive hypoxic responses. Sustained HIF‐1α activation in pulmonary vascular cells may promote PASMC proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, inflammation, and vascular remodeling, whereas HIF activation in other contexts may contribute to adaptive responses to hypoxia. Therefore, therapeutic modulation of this axis requires precise control rather than indiscriminate activation or inhibition.

Additional approaches focus on upstream regulators or downstream effectors of HIF signaling. Jiang et al. reported that prostaglandin E1 (PGE1), a regulator of HIF‐1α, markedly reduced pulmonary arterial pressure, improved RV function, suppressed PASMC apoptosis, and promoted mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) homing in a hypoxia‐induced rat model of PH [150]. However, its clinical use still requires clarification of optimal dosage, long‐term safety, and delivery route. Several natural products and traditional Chinese medicine extracts, including CAPE, dioscin, and Huaizhong No. 1 Rehmannia glutinosa, have also been reported to inhibit HIF signaling in animal models [151]. Although these compounds provide useful pharmacological tools for exploring HIF‐related pathways, their clinical translation is challenging because of compound complexity, difficulties in quality control and standardization, uncertain pharmacokinetics, and the need for large‐scale clinical trials to confirm safety and efficacy.

Recent studies have further expanded the range of HIF‐related therapeutic targets. Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) appears to exert protective effects in hypoxia‐induced PH. Prolonged hypoxia increases 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE), which stabilizes HIF‐1α, promotes dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1) phosphorylation, facilitates mitochondrial fission, and ultimately enhances PASMC proliferation. ALDH2 may therefore protect smooth muscle cells against PH progression by counteracting this 4‐HNE–HIF‐1α–Drp1 axis [107]. Similarly, phenethylamine has been reported to exert anti‐PH effects through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α pathway by reducing oxidative stress, apoptosis imbalance, and immune dysregulation [152, 153]. The ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS) and PHD‐mediated HIF regulation have also emerged as important therapeutic targets in PH and HPVR [154]. In addition, HIF‐regulated nucleic acid‐based and peptide‐based strategies, including small interfering RNA (siRNA), antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), blocking peptides, and microRNA‐based approaches, have been linked to pulmonary vascular reactivity and remodeling. For example, Zhang R et al. found that miRNA let‐7b inhibited ACE2 expression through the HIF‐1α–let‐7b–ACE2 axis, thereby contributing to PH pathogenesis by stimulating PASMC proliferation and migration [155]. These findings suggest that modulation of HIF‐associated regulatory RNAs and downstream signaling networks may provide new opportunities for reversing pathological pulmonary vascular remodeling. However, these approaches face major translational barriers, including efficient and cell‐specific delivery, potential immune activation, off‐target effects, long‐term stability, and uncertain safety in humans.

Despite encouraging preclinical evidence, the clinical translation of HIF‐centered therapies for PH remains limited by the context‐dependent and bidirectional nature of HIF signaling. HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α may play distinct or even opposing roles in endothelial cells, PASMCs, fibroblasts, inflammatory cells, and cardiomyocytes, and their effects may vary across disease stage, hypoxic exposure, and pulmonary vascular vs. RV compartments. This complexity is compounded by the heterogeneity of PH subtypes, which differ in HIF activation patterns, pathological mechanisms, and therapeutic vulnerability, thereby limiting the predictive value of hypoxia‐induced animal models for human disease. In addition, systemic manipulation of HIF raises safety concerns, as this pathway regulates erythropoiesis, angiogenesis, metabolism, inflammation, and tumor biology, while many candidate agents such as digoxin, topotecan, acriflavine, CAPE, and celastramycin exhibit pleiotropic effects that complicate target specificity and dose optimization. Further translational barriers include the lack of robust biomarkers, insufficient cell‐specific delivery, and the limited clinical relevance of current experimental models. Therefore, future development should emphasize precision approaches, including isoform‐selective modulators, targeted delivery systems, biomarker‐guided patient selection, and validation in human‐derived and clinically relevant models.
