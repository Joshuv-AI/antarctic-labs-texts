Prevalence and risk factors for Schistosoma haematobium infestation in WASH-based communities of Ogun Central, Nigeria
Authors: Oluwaseyi T Taiwo, Sammy O Sam-Wobo, Kehinde O Ademolu, Adewale O Talabi, Adewale M Taiwo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeglected tropical diseases (NTDs) remain a major public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa, where inadequate sanitation and unsafe water contribute to ongoing transmission. This study examined the prevalence and associated risk factors of Schistosoma haematobium among school-aged children in 20 WASH-focused communities across four Local Government Areas in Ogun Central, Nigeria, between March 2021 and March 2023. Urine and stool samples were collected from 1019 pupils and analyzed using standard parasitological techniques, while structured questionnaires captured demographic information, WASH indicators, and attitudes toward anthelmintic treatment. The overall prevalence of S. haematobium was 2%. Significant associations were observed between infection and water sources (p < 0.05), toilet facilities (p = 0.045), and hematuria (p < 0.001). Age was also identified as a significant demographic factor influencing infection. These findings highlight the persistent vulnerability of children in WASH-dependent communities to urinary schistosomiasis and emphasize the need for integrated control strategies that combine improved sanitation, safe water supply, health education, and regular mass drug administration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-025-08569-y.Keywords: Neglected tropical diseases, Hematuria, WASH indicators, School children, Mass drug administration

Introduction

Schistosoma haematobium is a parasitic worm that causes urogenital schistosomiasis, and it is a major public health issue in many low- and middle-income countries. This parasite is found in 53 countries, mainly in Africa and the Middle East, affecting more than 110 million people worldwide (Olveda et al. 2013; Barakat 2013). In Africa, countries such as Nigeria, Egypt, Sudan, Malawi, Ghana, and Tanzania bear a heavy burden, with the spread of the disease fueled by inadequate sanitation, limited access to clean water, and frequent human contact with contaminated water through everyday activities (Bukenya and Nsungwa 1994; Traore et al. 1998).

Despite global efforts to control schistosomiasis, transmission has worsened due to water resource projects, population growth, and migration trends (Chitsulo et al. 2000). However, nations such as Morocco, Brazil, and China have made notable progress in reducing the disease’s impact through large-scale preventive treatments and improved water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices. Yet, in most African countries where the disease is common, implementing sustainable control measures remains a significant challenge (WHO 2018).

The primary symptom of an S. haematobium infection is blood in the urine, which is often used to diagnose the condition in endemic areas, especially among school-aged children, who are the main focus of mass drug distribution (CDC 2018). While global death estimates vary, schistosomiasis is believed to cause between 24,000 and 200,000 deaths annually, with Africa contributing the largest share of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost to the illness (WHO 2017, 2018; Global Health 2017). To effectively address this issue, it is crucial not only to provide regular deworming with praziquantel but also to increase community awareness, improve sanitation practices, and focus on vulnerable groups such as preschool children and pregnant women (WHO 2013; Olveda et al. 2013).

This study presents new epidemiological insights into S. haematobium within WASH-focused communities in Ogun Central, Nigeria. This region has seen minimal local evaluations since preventive treatments were widely introduced. In contrast to earlier research, our work combines parasitological data with specific WASH metrics, such as water sources, sanitation facilities, and hygiene practices. This approach enhances understanding of the environmental and behavioral risk factors involved. Moreover, we explore issues such as treatment fatigue and community perceptions of anthelmintics—topics often overlooked in standard prevalence studies.

Urogenital schistosomiasis, caused by S. haematobium, continues to affect millions worldwide, with Nigeria facing one of the heaviest burdens in sub-Saharan Africa (WHO 2022). Despite ongoing national mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns, localized transmission remains a challenge, particularly in regions with high water exposure and inadequate sanitation (Colley et al. 2014). Current control efforts frequently overlook localized WASH inequalities and the shifting risk factors associated with urban growth and water infrastructure improvements.

This study aims to fill this knowledge gap by providing location-specific data to guide targeted intervention strategies and enhance the effectiveness of control initiatives in endemic areas. The main objective was to assess the prevalence of S. haematobium infection and identify the related environmental and behavioral risk factors among school-aged children in WASH-oriented communities in Ogun Central, Nigeria. Specifically, we sought to analyze how various factors—such as water sources, sanitation types, hygiene habits, and treatment practices—affect infection rates. This information is crucial for developing more tailored public health interventions.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Ogun State, located in southwestern Nigeria, covers an area of 16,980.55 km2 and lies between longitudes 2°45′ E–3°55′ E and latitudes 7°01′ N–7°18′ N. It comprises 20 Local Government Areas (LGAs) across three senatorial districts. Known for its industrial growth (second only to that of Lagos), it has a strong metal-processing sector and benefits from well-developed road and rail infrastructure linking it to Lagos ports. The state had a population of 3,751,140 in 2006, which was projected to grow to 6,379,500 by 2022 (NPC 2022). Figure 1 shows the map of the study area and the sampling sites.

Map of the study area in Abeokuta metropolis showing the sampling sites

Abeokuta South LGA

Abeokuta South LGA, with its headquarters in Ake, covers 71 km2 with a population of 250,278 in 2006, projected to increase to 425,700 by 2022. It is mainly inhabited by the Egba Ake people and is home to the Alake of Egbaland. The population practices Christianity, Islam, and traditional religions. The area has approximately 35 km of tarred roads, residential estates in Asero and Old Owode Road, and is culturally rich in crafts such as wood carving, pottery, and tie-dye. The LGA is divided into 15 wards.

Abeokuta North LGA

Abeokuta North, with its headquarters at Akomoje, covers 808 km² and had a population of 201,329 in 2006. While over 90% of the area is rural, more than 75% of residents live in urban zones. The LGA is bordered by several others and hosts the Oyan Dam, a crucial water source associated with high urinary schistosomiasis prevalence (Akinwale et al. 2008). Predominantly populated by the Gbagura, Oke-Ona, Oke-Ogun, and Owu people, it is known for farming—especially cocoa, cassava, and vegetables—and artisan trades. The LGA has 77 primary schools, 14 secondary schools, and 16 political wards.

Obafemi-Owode LGA

Obafemi-Owode LGA, with its headquarters at Owode Egba, spans 1410 km² with a population of 228,851 in 2006, projected to increase to 399,800 by 2022. The LGA borders both Ogun and Lagos States and comprises over 1200 towns. Its population, mainly Egba-speaking Yoruba people, is largely engaged in farming and fishing, with Ofada rice being a notable local crop. The area has several healthcare facilities, key markets such as Siun and Ogunmakin, and a network of state and federal roads. It is divided into 12 wards.

Odeda LGA

Odeda LGA, with its headquarters in Odeda town, lies along the Abeokuta–Ibadan road, about 20 km from Abeokuta. It covers 1560 km² and had a population of 109,449 in 2006. Located near the Oyo State border, Odeda includes 20 peri-urban areas and 860 villages and hamlets. The LGA is predominantly inhabited by the Egba people, with Christianity and Islam as the main religions. Residents are mostly traders and farmers, producing crops such as cocoa and plantain in large quantities.

Sample collection

Urine samples were collected from children whose parents or guardians had given informed consent to participate in the study. Each child received a clean, universal sample bottle for urine collection, in line with WHO guidelines (WHO 2002). The bottles were pre-labeled with unique identification numbers, ensuring they matched each child’s demographic information before distribution. All samples were collected during school hours and then transported to the laboratory for microscopic analysis to detect S. haematobium eggs.

Laboratory analysis

The diagnosis of urinary schistosomiasis was carried out using the polycarbonate urine filtration method, a technique endorsed by WHO (2018) as the standard. A 10 mL urine sample was drawn into a syringe and connected to a filter holder containing a polycarbonate membrane. The urine was then gently pushed through the filter, capturing any S. haematobium eggs. The filter was carefully extracted with sterile forceps and placed on a glass microscope slide. A drop of Lugol’s iodine was added and left for about 15 s to stain the eggs. The slide was then examined under a light microscope at ×40 magnification. Schistosome eggs appeared orange and were identified based on their shape and structure (Ephraim et al. 2014; Afifi et al. 2016).

Questionnaire administration

A total of 1019 pre-tested and structured questionnaires were administered to pupils who, along with their parents, provided informed consent to participate. Demographic information was collected to assess participants’ knowledge of S. haematobium, awareness of drug side effects, and behavioral indicators such as anthelmintic fatigue. The questionnaire also evaluated knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to water, sanitation, and hygiene (Taiwo et al. 2017).

Data analysis

Data obtained from the urine sample analysis and questionnaires were analyzed using SPSS for Windows (Version 23). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) and inferential statistics (chi-square test) were employed to interpret the results.

Children who drank both sachet water and rainwater recorded the highest prevalence (6%). Pupils who consumed untreated water (3%) had a higher prevalence than those who treated their water. Footwear use was also included among hygiene-related risk factors.

The highest prevalence was observed among respondents using pit latrines (3%) and practising bush defecation. Respondents without access to handwashing facilities had a higher prevalence (3%) than those with access (0%). Pupils who washed their hands after defecation (2%) and before eating (2%) showed higher prevalence than those who did not wash their hands (0%).

Prevalence of S. haematobium by river/pond visits and purpose of visit showed similar levels (2%) among respondents who never visited. The highest prevalence was observed among pupils who visited rivers/ponds for recreation or fetching water (7%).

Pupils who engaged in geophagy (soil eating) had a prevalence of 4%, compared to 1% among those who did not. Lower prevalence was observed in respondents who wore footwear (2%) compared to those who did not (0%).

Prevalence was significantly higher among respondents who had experienced blood in urine (13%) than those who had not (0%) (χ2 = 0.000; p < 0.05).

Discussion

Schistosoma haematobium was found to have an overall prevalence of 2% in the study area. The highest prevalence was observed in Abeokuta North and South Local Government Areas (LGAs), each at 4%, while Odeda LGA recorded ~ 1% and Obafemi-Owode LGA had no detected cases. The higher prevalence in Abeokuta North and South is plausibly linked to their proximity to the Ogun River, a major water source for domestic use, agriculture, and recreation, which increases residents’ exposure to potentially infected water (Nnamdi Opara et al. 2021; Ezeh et al. 2019). This is consistent with broader evidence that communities near rivers or streams tend to experience higher transmission.

Compared with previous studies in Ogun State, the observed prevalence is lower than earlier reports. Ekpo et al. (2008) and Ude et al. (2009) found higher prevalence, while Okonko et al. (2009) reported lower values, consistent with the current findings. Recent reviews also indicate that prevalence in some formerly hyperendemic areas has declined, partly due to environmental changes and control measures such as mass drug administration (MDA) with praziquantel (Ezeh et al. 2019; Usman et al. 2023).

By demographic characteristics, pupils in Primary 3 (approx. age 8–9), followed by those in Primary 5 and Primary 1, recorded the highest infection rates. This is likely due to greater exposure to unsafe water through swimming, bathing, or water fetching, common among younger school children (Nnamdi Opara et al. 2021). Males had a slightly higher prevalence (3%) than females (2%), although this difference was not statistically significant. This contrasts with earlier findings in Abeokuta (Sam-Wobo et al. 2013), where females were more affected, suggesting that gender roles in water contact may differ between communities. Other reviews also note that boys often have higher exposure due to swimming and fishing activities (Ezeh et al. 2019).

Water source was a significant risk factor

Respondents who accessed rainwater or boreholes had the highest prevalence (5%), followed by sachet water users (4%). This suggests that even “safe” water can be compromised during collection, storage, or distribution (Nnamdi Opara et al. 2021). Water treatment was statistically significant, with untreated water users showing higher prevalence (3%), underscoring the importance of point-of-use treatment in endemic settings.

Sanitation influenced infection risk

Pupils using pit latrines or practising open defecation recorded higher prevalence (~3%) compared with water closet users (~2%). This supports evidence that poor sanitation contributes to water contamination with schistosome eggs, maintaining local transmission (Sam-Wobo et al. 2017).

Hygiene practices were also relevant

Households without handwashing facilities recorded higher prevalence (3%), while those with functional facilities had no cases. Though not statistically significant, this trend aligns with evidence linking improved hygiene to reduced schistosomiasis and other helminth infections (Nnamdi Opara et al. 2021).

Clinical symptoms were strongly associated with infection

Pupils reporting haematuria had ~13% prevalence compared with ~1% among those without, confirming haematuria as a reliable diagnostic indicator (Anosike et al. 2001; Ojo et al. 2021).

Deworming status correlated significantly

Respondents who had not been dewormed recently had higher prevalence (~6%) than those who had (~2%), consistent with studies showing that MDA reduces prevalence and intensity but reinfection persists where unsafe water contact remains (Sam-Wobo et al. 2017; Mwandawiro et al. 2013). Overall, the transmission of S. haematobium in this area remains influenced by environmental exposure (water source, sanitation), behavioral and demographic factors (age, gender, hygiene), and programmatic interventions (MDA participation). Sustained control efforts integrating drug treatment, health education, and water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions are essential.

Conclusion

Schistosoma haematobium infection was found to have a prevalence of 2% in the study area. Hematuria was strongly associated with infection, with significantly higher prevalence among children reporting blood in urine. The highest prevalence was observed among 5-year-old pupils, with a slight male predominance.

The study showed that frequent exposure to contaminated water, particularly through washing and recreational activities, increased infection risk. To reduce the burden, regular hematuria screening in schools, improved access to safe water and sanitation, community health education, consistent MDA, and better access to healthcare services are recommended.

Limitations of the study included the cross-sectional design, reliance on microscopy and self-reported data, and narrow geographic coverage, which may have led to an underestimation of prevalence. The lack of infection intensity measurements and limited environmental evaluation also restricted a complete understanding of transmission dynamics and associated risk factors.
