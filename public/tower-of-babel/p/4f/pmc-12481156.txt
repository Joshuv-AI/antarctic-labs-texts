Cognitive Function 1 Year After COVID Infection
Authors: Joviane Daher, Ziad Koberssy, Jared C Durieux, Ornina Atieh, Jhony Baissary, Marc Abboud, Grace A McComsey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile emerging evidence suggests a potential link between COVID-19 and cognitive impairment, there is a lack of prospective longitudinal research that objectively assesses cognitive outcomes after SARS-CoV-2 infection. This study aims to evaluate changes in cognitive function following COVID-19 in a group of individuals with baseline pre-infectious cognitive assessments.MethodsIn this cohort study, cognitive function was objectively measured using the computerized Cognivue Clarity® device. All participants who had available Cognivue® testing were followed with a second Cognivue® assessment ∼1 year later. Based on whether they contracted COVID-19 during this period, participants were categorized into 2 groups according to COVID status.ResultsWe enrolled 110 participants with a median age of 45 years, 35% females and 46% white; 55 (50%) participants experienced a documented COVID-19 infection during the follow-up period (COVID + group), and the rest remained free of COVID infection (COVID- group). COVID- and COVID + groups were balanced for demographics and duration of follow-up. In the COVID + group, only memory scores changed during follow-up (+3.9; P = .03). The COVID- group showed improvements in the overall Cognivue® score (+2; P = .03), as well as in visuospatial (+1.9; P = .04), executive function (+2.2; P = .02), and naming language (+2.2; P = .01) scores. No statistically significant differences were observed in the overall cognitive score or its subdomains between the 2 groups.ConclusionsIn a 45-year-old average population, no decrease in cognitive function was observed 1 year after COVID-19 infection.Keywords: cognitive function, cognivue clarity®, COVID-19, long-COVID

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has resulted in at least 750 million documented cases worldwide, leading to over 7 million deaths [1]. During acute infection, the spike protein (S) of SARS-CoV-2 binds to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors present on various cells, including neurons, glial cells, and neuroepithelial cells, leading to neurological symptoms that range from mild manifestations such as headache, fatigue, hyposmia, and hypogeusia to severe conditions including encephalopathy, meningitis, seizures, and cerebrovascular accidents [2, 3]. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that COVID-19 is not limited to the acute infection phase; instead, it has left around 60 million people globally and 18% of adults in the USA with a new or persistent cluster of chronic and disabling symptoms known as Long COVID [4, 5]. Various definitions and terminologies have emerged for long COVID, all of which include symptoms of cognitive impairment, highlighting the potential long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 on cognition [4, 6–8].

The exact pathophysiology of long COVID remains unclear, with several implicated mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction leading to oxidative stress, vascular complications, gut microbiome disruption, viral persistence, and an ongoing inflammatory state [9, 10]. In the central nervous system (CNS), SARS-CoV-2 exhibits endothelial tropism, leading to endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and impaired cerebral perfusion [11]. Immune dysregulation and the formation of autoantibodies may also contribute to cerebral microvascular compromise and sustained neuroinflammation [12].

Although growing evidence suggests that individuals hospitalized with COVID-19 may experience persistent cognitive impairment for months following discharge [13, 14], and a small longitudinal case-control study has demonstrated both cognitive deficits and brain changes on imaging post-infection [15], there remains a need for prospective cohort studies with less severely ill patients to better understand the cognitive impacts of SARS-CoV-2. Moreover, the current literature is limited by methodological challenges, including the use of self-reported symptoms, subjective scales, the lack of pre-infection data, and retrospective designs. As a result, actual evidence lacks objective measurement of longitudinal change in cognitive function after COVID-19 infection.

In addition to its direct impact on cognition, COVID-19 adversely affects metabolic health through mechanisms such as inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress [16, 17]. In turn, these metabolic changes are known to influence cognitive function [18, 19]. Thus, COVID-19 can impact cognition directly and indirectly through compromised metabolic health.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study to evaluate the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on cognitive function using the Cognivue Clarity® device as an objective, Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared cognitive assessment tool, in comparison to a control group with no SARS-COV-2 infection.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

This is a prospective cohort study conducted at the University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center (UHCMC), Cleveland, Ohio, USA. Our study's primary exposure of interest was COVID-19 infection during follow-up period (COVID-positive = COVID+), confirmed through either a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), positive serology (nucleocapsid antibody, or spike antibody if unimmunized), or any FDA-approved SARS-CoV-2 antigen test. The non-exposed control group (COVID-negative = COVID-) consisted of patients with no history of upper respiratory illness or symptoms suggestive of COVID-19 during the follow-up period and no documented COVID-19 infection. The inclusion criteria consisted of adults with a documented pre-pandemic cognitive function assessment using the Cognivue Clarity® device as part of prior studies conducted at the Metabolic Research Center at UHCMC. We excluded individuals diagnosed with dementia or uncontrolled neuropsychiatric disorder. Participants underwent a follow-up cognitive function assessment using the same device. For COVID-positive participants, this follow-up assessment was conducted no earlier than 90 days post-infection, allowing for recovery and minimizing the short-term effects of the illness. A total of 110 participants met the eligibility criteria for inclusion. All enrolled participants completed a baseline clinical assessment and provided a detailed medical and COVID-19 history. Blood samples were also collected to measure specific metabolic markers.

Ethical Considerations

Before any evaluation, participants signed a written informed consent approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center.

Study Measurements

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

Well-trained healthcare professionals applied standardized questionnaires to collect comprehensive data on participants on their first visit, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history.

Metabolic Biomarkers

All blood samples were collected after participants were instructed to fast for at least 12 hours before blood collection and sent to a CLIA-certified laboratory for metabolic measurements, including glucose, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol, and non-high-density lipoprotein (non-HDL).

Cognitive Function With Cognivue Clarity®

We assessed cognition using the Cognivue Clarity® FDA-approved device (Figure 1). This is a 10-minute, computerized, self-administered test delivered on a laptop-like device with only a screen and CogniWheel that facilitates providing answers (https://www.cognivue.com/videos/). The test includes 3 sub-batteries and 10 subtests divided into: (1) Two adaptive measures (Adaptive Motor Control, Visual Salience) that assess the speed of manipulating the wheel and the ability to determine visual stimuli adjusting for people with motor or visual impairments and will not contribute to the final score. (2) Four discrimination tests (Letter, Word, Shape, Motion). (3) Four memory tests (Letter, Word, Shape, Motion).

The Cognivue Clarity® Device.

Upon test completion, a report is generated that includes scores for 6 cognitive subdomains (visuospatial, executive function/attention, naming/language, memory, delayed recall, and abstraction), along with an overall average Cognivue® score (Figure 2). Reaction time and processing speed are also reported on the second page of the report. Sub-domains and overall average scores are presented on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better performance. Patients scoring ≤50 are at higher risk for impairment while those scoring ≥75 are at no risk [20, 21].

Example of a Cognivue Clarity® participant's report highlighting the overall and the different subdomains scores.

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of the study participants were described using descriptive statistics including mean +/− standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency (n) and percentage (%) for categorical variables. Differences between groups were computed using an independent t-test or its non-parametric analogue, the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher's exact for categorical variables. Correlated measures within each participant were assessed using a paired t-test. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and P-values less than alpha <.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of Study Participants

We enrolled 110 participants; 55 remained COVID-free (COVID-), and 55 had documented COVID infection during follow-up (COVID+). The mean age among the COVID + group was 45.1 ± 15 years, and 38.2% were females, the average BMI was 29.2 ± 6.3 kg/m2, and the median number of days between the 2 visits was 400 days (IQR: 364, 554). Overall, 11 participants (6 in COVID + group) had excessive alcohol use, and 22 (11 in the COVID + group) had a history of illicit drug use, most commonly cannabis.

Overall, a history of controlled depression was documented in 36 participants (15 in COVID + group) and 27 had a history of anxiety (13 in COVID + group). Study groups were balanced (P > .05) based on sex, age, and the number of days between the 2 visits. The average Cognivue® score in the COVID + was 78 ± 15 compared to 74.8 ± 15.2 for their COVID- counterparts (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort study, we evaluated changes in cognitive function and metabolic markers among individuals after COVID-19 infection compared to a matched group who never had COVID-19 infection, using for the first time the Cognivue Clarity®, an FDA-approved computerized cognitive assessment tool.

We demonstrated a modest but significant improvement in the overall Cognivue® score in the COVID- group but not in the COVID + group between the 2 study time points. Additionally, the COVID-negative group showed a significant increase in visuospatial, executive function, and naming/language scores from baseline to follow-up, whereas the COVID + group showed improvement in the memory score only. More importantly, there was no significant difference in the average Cognivue® score or any of the subdomains scores when comparing the 2 groups over time. This suggests that cognitive function was not negatively affected following SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Although emerging evidence has described persistent neurocognitive symptoms such as memory loss, reduced attention, and brain fog following COVID-19, our study did not support these findings when objectively assessing cognitive function [22, 23]. This contrasts with the results of a meta-analysis by Ceban et al [24], which reported that 22% of patients had cognitive impairment 12 weeks or more after infection. Similarly, an extensive community-based observational study conducted in the United Kingdom involving 112 964 participants reported that COVID-19 infection was associated with greater declines in global cognitive scores, particularly in memory, reasoning, and executive function [25]. These effects were more pronounced in individuals infected with the original virus or the alpha variant, those with unresolved symptoms after 12 weeks, and those admitted to intensive care. The cognitive decline ranged from the equivalent of a 3-point IQ loss in patients with mild disease who felt recovered to a 6-point loss in those with lingering symptoms and up to a 9-point loss in patients who required ICU admission. Notably, participants who reported subjective symptoms, such as brain fog and memory issues, experienced only minor impairments in specific cognitive tasks, suggesting that objective evaluations are crucial for accurately assessing the cognitive impact of COVID-19.

In this study, we utilized Cognivue® to assess changes in cognitive function before and after infection. In contrast, other studies have used traditional tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and patient-reported symptom scales and questionnaires [24, 26, 27]. These conventional tools are validated and widely used but they can introduce subjectivity and interrater variability, thereby increasing the risk of administrator bias [28]. In contrast, Cognivue® is an automated, computerized assessment that offers a more objective evaluation and eliminates the need for manual scoring or trained personnel [29]. Its performance has been validated and shown to exceed that of established tools such as the St. Louis University Mental Status (SLUMS) exam and MoCA [21, 30, 31]. Furthermore, conventional assessments can be influenced by factors such as language, educational background, and cultural differences [32, 33]. Potential test–retest bias is also present with conventional assessment tools [34]. Cognivue® minimizes such bias, which was further mitigated in this study by the relatively long interval (400 days on average) between assessments [21].

A possible factor supporting our findings is the time between the infection and follow-up assessment in the COVID-positive group (≥90 days), which may have allowed sufficient time for recovery from mild post-infectious symptoms. This aligns our results with multiple studies that have examined the evolution of symptoms over time. For instance, a prospective study found that cognitive function improved at both the 3-month and 6-month follow ups after the post-acute assessment, with no significant difference between these 2 time points, indicating a plateau [14]. Similarly, Blazhenets et al [35] observed a reduction in brain hypermetabolism, as measured by 18F-FDG PET scans, along with improved cognitive function 6 months after the subacute phase. A prospective study by Del Brutto et al [36] demonstrated that although a significant cognitive decline was observed six months after infection, this effect was reversed after 1 year of follow-up. Moreover, a geographically diverse prospective study by Wisk et al [37] investigating health-related quality of life at 1 year after COVID infection, demonstrated meaningful recovery in physical symptoms including cognitive function as early as 3 months after infection.

Additionally, metabolic syndrome is implicated in cognitive impairment through neuro-inflammation and vascular dysfunction, the same mechanisms involved in post-COVID neurological symptoms [38, 39]. In our cohort, however, we found no significant changes in metabolic markers over time, nor were there any significant differences between the exposed and control groups. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with poor metabolic profiles have a higher risk of developing cognitive dysfunction compared to those with ideal metabolic profiles [19, 40]. Therefore, the metabolic stability of our participants throughout the study period may support the observed absence of cognitive decline.

The strength of our study lies in the availability of pre-pandemic Cognivue® assessments, as well as a matched contemporary COVID-negative control group, which accounts for natural variability in cognitive performance over time. Additionally, we accounted for metabolic changes as a potential confounder by measuring variations in metabolic markers. Finally, we utilized the Cognivue Clarity® device to assess cognitive function objectively and precisely, thereby reducing subjectivity and biases that arise from traditional neurocognitive assessments. However, it is also important to acknowledge limitations, such as the need for a more comprehensive analysis in future research with a larger sample size, including data on participants’ symptoms, disease severity, comorbidities, vaccination status, Long COVID status, and inflammatory and neuronal injury markers. We also acknowledge that our controls were not tested for antibodies or T-cell immunity to definitively rule out asymptomatic infection. However, since the severity of the acute COVID-19 illness is a significant risk factor for long COVID, asymptomatic infection are unlikely, though not impossible, to cause long COVID [41]. Moreover, our study lacks cognitive assessments during acute infection and at multiple follow-up points, limiting our understanding of cognitive outcomes over time.

CONCLUSION

In a 45-year-old average population, no decrease in cognitive function was observed over an average 1-year follow-up period. While certain subdomains changed modestly over time in both groups, no significant differences were observed between the groups. Further longitudinal studies using larger samples and longer follow-up durations are needed to determine the long-term cognitive consequences of COVID-19.
