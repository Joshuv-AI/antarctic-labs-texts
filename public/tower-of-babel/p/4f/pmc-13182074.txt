Evaluating clinicopathological pattern and management of colorectal cancer for adult patients presenting at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital: a 10-year retrospective review
Authors: Thokozani Masina, Linda Chokotho, Patrick Noah, Wakisa Mulwafu, Leo Masamba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer mortality and the third prevalent cancer globally. In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the incidence is increasing. This retrospective cross-sectional study evaluated clinicopathological pattern and management of CRC for adult patients presenting at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH), Malawi.MethodsThe review of oncology and surgical register books identified patients diagnosed with CRC who presented between 2012 and 2022. The variables assessed were age, sex, HIV status, symptoms, family history, diagnostic approach, tumour site, histological diagnosis, treatment modality, and length of hospital stay (LOHS).ResultsThere were 192 patients identified with CRC. The median age was 45 years [interquartile range (IQR) = 43.5–60], with slight male predominance at 100 (52.08%). There were 23 (11.98%) patients with HIV infection and all were on treatment. Abdominal pain and rectal bleeding were the predominant symptoms occuring in 100 (52.08%) and 78 (40.63%) respectively. The mean duration of signs and symptoms was 7.6 months (SD = 4.6). Over half of the patients, 113 (58.85%) were in clinical stage IV. The majority were diagnosed at laparotomy 116 (60.42%), followed by colonoscopy 19 (9.90%). Out of the 192 participants, 110 (57.29%) had histology reports confirming CRC, while 82 (42.71%) had no histology reports. All the available histology reports showed adenocarcinoma. Surgery was the main treatment for 155 (80.73%) patients while chemotherapy was offered to 81 (42.19%) patients.ConclusionCRC at QECH is predominantly diagnosed in young patients at advanced stages. Screening younger age groups could improve with early diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most frequent cancer in the world and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1–3]. The global incidence according to global cancer statistics for 2020 for both the colon and rectum was approximately 1.9 million cases, representing 9.8% of all cancer cases [4]. The incidence of CRC in Africa is relatively low compared to high income countries (HICs). A systematic review and meta-analysis done by Arhin et al., on age standardised incidence rates (ASIR) and epidemiology of CRC in Africa reported an overall ASIR of 5.25 per 100 000 population [5]. A systematic analysis done by Graham et al., estimating the incidence of CRC in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), reported a crude incidence of 4.04 per 100 000 population for both sex groups [6]. The clinicopathological pattern of CRC; such as age at presentation, clinical features, tumour anatomical site, clinical stage, histological diagnosis and its subtypes, varies among different population demographics including race [7]. The pattern predicts the overall cancer behavior and potential treatment outcomes. For instance, young age at diagnosis, advanced clinical staging and unfavorable histological features, may be associated with a higher likelihood for poor treatment outcomes [7].

The majority of the CRC patients in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are diagnosed at an advanced clinical stage. This could be due to lack of early diagnostic measures such as screening methods, lack of disease awareness, poor health seeking behavior and shortage of skilled health care workers [8, 9]. Furthermore, there are limited diagnostic resources and treatment options. Some of these factors explain why LMICs account for up to 70% of all CRC related mortality [3, 10].

There has been no study done in Malawi to explain the clinicopathological pattern and the treatment offered to CRC patients. Anecdotal evidence shows an increase in number of cases of CRC at QECH. This study aims to fill the existing gap by evaluating the clinicopathological pattern, and management of CRC at QECH. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing effective strategies to improve early diagnosis and treatment, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in Malawi and similar LMICs settings.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study reviewed data from Oncology and Surgical wards at QECH, for adult patients for a period of 10 years from 1st January, 2012 to 31st December, 2022. QECH, one of the 4 tertiary hospitals serves approximately 5 million people and has a bed capacity of 1350 beds [11]. It offers chemotherapy, general surgery, diagnostic colonoscopy, CT scan, barium contrast studies, and other basic imaging, and laboratory tests. Histopathology services are outsourced privately, and MRI scan and radiotherapy are currently unavailable.

All patients aged 18 years and above with suspected or confirmed diagnosis of CRC seen at QECH from 1st January 2012 to 31st December 2022 were included in the study. Paper case files meeting the inclusion criteria were retrieved from the registry offices. The files were screened to ensure eligibility, then listed by patient’s name to avoid duplication before de-identifying them with unique codes to ensure confidentiality.

A data collection form was developed to capture the data from the case notes. The tool was in both paper and digital formats. The digital format was aided by Redcap™ software version 12.4.18. The tool was piloted using four random case files and was improved afterwards. Data collection was aided by a trained research assistant. The variables captured were; demographic details, characteristics of clinicopathological pattern and treatment modalities. The electronic data captured on Redcap was stored in a password protected computer and the case notes were only handled by the investigators who used a private secured room, and stored in a locked cabinet. The data was exported to the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for cleaning and then was exported to STATA™ software package version 16 for analysis [12].

Descriptive statistical analysis was done. Frequencies and percentages were computed for categorical variables. Mean and standard deviation; median and interquartile range were computed for continuous variables. Normally distributed numerical data, was analysed using either Student T-test or ANOVA, depending on whether the comparison involved two groups or more than two groups, respectively. If the numerical data was not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparisons involving more than two groups were used. For categorical data, the analysis was done using either the Fisher exact test or the Chi-square test, depending on the context. All the missing variables were accounted for in the analysis.

Results

Clinico-demographic characteristics of CRC at QECH

There were 192 cases of CRC seen during the study period. The age range was 21–87 years with a median age of 45 years, [IQR (43.5–60)]. There was slight male predominance at 52.08% (100/192). There were 11.98% (23/192) patients who were HIV positive and on treatment while 46.35% (89/192) were HIV negative. HIV status was unknown in 41.15% (79/192). The overall mean duration for signs and symptoms before presenting to the hospital was 7.6 months SD (4.6). Of the 192 cases with, abdominal pain followed by rectal bleeding being the commonly presenting symptoms at 52.08% (100/192) and 40.63% (78/192) respectively. 101 had details on length of hospital stay LOHS. Of these, the median LOHS was 16 days [IQR (7–30)]. One participant reported positive family history of CRC. However, majority (82%) of the cases had missing recording on family history status. Clinicopathological pattern of CRC at QECH

More than half of the participants were in stage IV category of clinical staging at 58.85% (113/192). Rectum was the commonest tumour anatomical site at 52.6% (101/192). Most of the tumours were located on the left side at 73.4% (141/192). There were 42.71% (82/192) of patients with missing histology reports in their case notes. All the available 110 histology reports showed adenocarcinoma as the histological diagnosis. In terms of tumour differentiation, 81.82% (90/110) of the adenocarcinomas were ‘not otherwise specified’ followed by 9.09% (10/110) being moderately differentiated. Treatment of CRC at QECH

Surgery was the main treatment modality offered to 80.73% (155/192) of the participants. Approximately 2/3 of them, 60.00% (93/155) were offered non-definitive surgical procedures with 26.45% (41/155) being offered colostomy alone. Among definitive surgical procedures offered, hemicolectomies accounted for 30.32% (47/155) followed by anterior resection plus colostomy at 6.45% (10/155). In terms of urgency of surgical intervention, elective surgery was the commonest approach done for 72.90% (113/155) of the participants.

Chemotherapy was offered to 42.19% (81/192) of the participants. Among them, 53.09% (43/81) had palliative chemotherapy, 44.44% (36/81) had adjuvant chemotherapy, and 2.47% (2/81) were offered neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Discussion

Participant’s characteristics

The majority of the participants in this study were under 50 years with a median age at diagnosis of 45 years aligning with similar findings in Africa and Asia. Studies by Chalya et al., in Tanzania and Deo et al., in India reported median ages of 46 and 47.7 years respectively [8, 13]. On the contrary, a study done in Sweden by Pasquali et al., reported a median age at diagnosis of 72 years [14]. The younger age of CRC patients at QECH may be linked to factors such as unfavorable lifestyle due to urbanisation, rapid socioeconomic development and genetic factors as proposed in other studies [7]. In regard to sex, the preponderance towards male sex observed in this study, though borderline, concurs with other studies that CRC is relatively common in males [13, 15, 16]. Both of these findings could help in planning disease awareness and targeted screening.

Family history of CRC was documented for only one patient highlighting a gap in history taking at QECH. Documenting this information could help identify hereditary CRC, aiding in risk prediction and guiding screening for relatives particularly in FAP or HNPCC cases [17, 18].

Clinical signs and symptoms of CRC and their duration

CRC symptoms are not specific to the disease making awareness of the ‘alarm’ features like those in the UK’s NICE guidelines essential for timely referral [19, 20].

In this study, the commonest signs and symptoms in order of presentation were abdominal pain, rectal bleeding and anaemia. Similar findings were observed in Kenya [21], India [13] and Tanzania [22]. Abdominal pain often a non-specific symptom, is likely to be treated symptomatically, particularly in primary and secondary care facilities in Malawi, where non-doctor cadres work with limited diagnostic investigations [23].

An audit done at a tertiary centre in India by Patil et al., reported a mean duration of symptoms of 4 months prior to presentation [24] whereas in our study, the mean was nearly double. Although reasons for the late presentation in our case were not explored, factors may be similar to those identified by Chadza et al., in a Malawian study on cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment delay [25]. The factors included ; limited disease awareness, long distance to a health facility, high cost of transportation and delays within the healthcare system itself.

Clinical stage of CRC

Late-stage CRC at diagnosis is known to be associated with poor treatment response and survival rates [26]. In this study, more than 80% of the participants had stage III or IV with nearly 60% in stage IV. Similar findings were reported in studies done in Nigeria and Tanzania [22, 27]. Another study done in Uganda by Wismayer et al., reported up to 60% of the participants presenting with advanced stage of disease [28]. Late diagnosis or late presentation may be due to a lack of disease awareness, lack of trained health care workers and lack of diagnostic and treatment infrastructure [29]. The predominance of younger age at diagnosis in this study, may also explain the higher rates of late-stage of disease at presentation, consistent with US studies showing that those diagnosed under 50 have a higher risk of late-stage presentation [26, 30]. Unlike standardised protocols using MRI or endoscopic ultrasound for staging rectal cancer [31], at QECH, there was no documented use of MRI despite its availability during the study period. This highlights a gap in protocol adherence.

Tumour anatomical site and histology

Surgical approaches, neoadjuvant therapy, and staging protocols differ between colon and rectal cancers due to anatomical, embryological and metastatic variations [32–35]. These differences necessitate individualised treatment [32, 33].

In this study, rectal cancer accounted for slightly more than half of the participants, similar to findings from India, Ghana and Uganda, while a study in Algeria reported a predominance of colon cancer at 62% [13, 16, 28, 36, 37].

In this study, majority of tumors were left sided, involving the descending colon, sigmoid colon, rectosigmoid junction and rectum. This finding aligns with a study done in Rwanda by Baldwin et al., [38]. It is reported that left sided tumours have better prognosis as compared to right sided tumours [39, 40], however, this study did not evaluate prognosis and outcomes by tumour location.

All the CRC patients with histology reports were diagnosed with adenocarcinoma. The basis of diagnosis in those with missing histology reports could be on clinical suspicion or mere failure to attach or document the details of the histology report by the attending physician despite having it. Missing histology reports could be because the histology processing is done privately with the guardians often retaining the reports. Improving filing and documention of results in the case files is essential to ensure data accuracy.

Diagnostic methods for CRC

There was lack of standardised approach in working up patients, with some elective patients undergoing laparotomy for biopsy, instead of standard diagnostic colonoscopy [41]. Laparotomy was the most common diagnostic method, similar to findings from the Tanzanian study where 37% of patients underwent laparotomy for CRC related abdominal signs [8]. QECH has a colonoscopy facility, but the lengthy waiting list coupled with shortage of trained personnel hinders timely access.

Barium enema was the least common diagnostic modality in our study. A study done in Bangladesh by Gafur MA., reported the sensitivity and specificity of barium enema at 88.9% and 86% respectively [42]. Though not superior to colonoscopy, its use as an initial investigation in clinically suspicious colorectal cancer should be considered.

Treatment of CRC

CRC treatment requires a multidisciplinary approach. Surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy are some of the vital components of the comprehensive CRC care package [22, 29]. At QECH, the majority of cases were offered surgery but the percentage of those offered surgery in our study is relatively lower than what was reported in a Tanzanian study by Chalya et al., [8].

Non-definitive surgeries accounted for 60%, demonstrating low resection rates. On the other hand, elective surgeries were more common than emergency surgeries which accounted for only 30%. This finding highlights a missed opportunity to do a proper work-up of the elective patients including doing diagnostic colonoscopies, MRI and CT scan to improve management.

Chemotherapy has a vital role in management of CRC [31, 43], administered for neoadjuvant, adjuvant or palliative purposes. In this study, chemotherapy was offered to just above 40% of the participants, primarily for palliative ( just over half) and adjuvant (nearly 45%) intentions. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was almost obsolete. This contrasts with the Rwandan study by Baldwin et al. where neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy rates were higher [38]. Unavailability of radiotherapy at QECH and the whole of Malawi during the study period might have contributed to low resection rates especially for locally advanced rectal cancer where neoadjuvant chemoradiation is indicated [22].

The treatment of colon and rectal cancers at QECH, has showed discrepancies from standard NCCN guidelines [31, 43]. For instance, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy or radiotherapy for rectal cancer, was unavailable. Additionally, neoadjuvant chemotherapy for colon cancer was rarely used despite advanced disease stages. Locally unresectable tumours managed with non-definitive surgery and palliation would have potentially been converted to resectable status with standard systemic therapy.

Study strengths and limitations

This study is the first of its kind to be conducted at QECH and in Malawi; evaluating the clinicopathological pattern and management of CRC. Therefore, it provides baseline data that can be used for comparative analysis in future studies. Furthermore, it provides a foundational understanding of the issue, setting the stage for future research and interventions. Though its findings are not generalizable to Malawi, the findings will help lobby for resources to improve CRC care for Malawian patients. It will also improve the practice by highlighting the need to follow standard guidelines thereby preventing the heterogeneity and diversion from standard protocol in management of CRC as found in this study. The findings of this study align with those from other studies done in other LMICs and elsewhere, indicating that despite the challenges in management of CRC at QECH, the results remain comparable to those reported in other centres.

The study’s retrospective design might have contributed to the missing or incomplete data limiting its ability to accurately characterize the disease. For example, 82/192 of the participants had no histology reports, leading to incomplete assessments of tumor type and other critical histopathological features. The other limitations to our study were recall and selection bias making it vulnerable to inaccuracy of the data captured and non-representative sampling respectively. Additionally, the 10-year timeframe may not reflect on recent knowledge about colorectal cancer. Furthermore, being a single centre study, its findings cannot be generalized to the whole Malawi as clinicopathological pattern and management of colorectal cancer may vary across different regions.

Conclusion

This study has showed that CRC at QECH is characterized by a younger age at diagnosis, a predominance of rectal tumours, late-clinical stage of disease; and management often involving elective, non-definitive surgery. Additionally, most patients are not being treated with chemotherapy. The study has also demonstrated a lack of adherence to standardised treatment guidelines leading to variability in CRC management. A prospective multicenter study should be conducted to study the epidemiology, clinicopathological pattern and management of CRC in Malawi in order to provide a broader understanding of the disease within the country.
