From guidelines to reality: how do physicians really manage sarcoidosis?
Authors: Ezgi Erdem Türe, Berna Akıncı Özyürek, Emire Pınar Seyfettin Çelik, Ceyda Anar, Nilgün Yılmaz Demirci, Funda Coşkun, Oğuzhan Okutan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines exist for the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of sarcoidosis; however, considerable variation in clinical practice remains. This study aimed to evaluate the approaches of pulmonologists in Turkey to the management of patients with sarcoidosis through a survey.MethodsA web-based survey consisting of 26 questions regarding diagnostic and therapeutic approaches and follow-up strategies in patients with sarcoidosis was sent to the email groups of specialists. The study was designed as a multicenter project and targeted only pulmonology physicians. The responses provided by the participating physicians were analyzed statistically.ResultsNinety-seven pulmonologists participated in the survey. Stage I–II sarcoidosis was most frequently encountered, and endobronchial ultrasound was the preferred diagnostic modality across all stages. Routine evaluations commonly included laboratory tests, thoracic imaging, and pulmonary function testing. Systemic therapy was rarely initiated in stage I–II disease but was routinely used in stage III–IV disease, with glucocorticoids as first-line treatment and methotrexate as the most common second-line agent. Antifibrotic therapy was considered by approximately one-third of physicians for stage IV sarcoidosis. Follow-up intervals were generally 3–6 months for untreated patients and 3 months for treated patients.ConclusionsThere are individual and institutional variations in the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of sarcoidosis in Turkey, particularly in diagnostic methods and treatment duration. Considering the well-recognized environmental and racial differences in disease presentation, our findings highlight the need for a national diagnostic and treatment guideline for sarcoidosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04226-x.Keywords: Sarcoidosis, Diagnosis, Management, Treatment, Follow-up, Survey

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem granulomatous disease of unknown cause. Sarcoidosis shows wide variation in incidence and prevalence worldwide. The incidence of sarcoidosis is 0.3–11.5 per 100,000 per year [1]. Although pulmonary and thoracic lymph node involvement is most common, sarcoidosis may also affect any organ system [2–4].

The diagnosis of sarcoidosis typically relies on histopathological confirmation supported by compatible clinical and radiological findings [4]. Tissue biopsy provides diagnostic confirmation; however, it is essential to exclude other granulomatous diseases [2, 5–7]. Laboratory tests are not sufficiently sensitive or specific to confirm a diagnosis, but they are conducted to identify any evidence of extrapulmonary involvement [2, 8]. Sarcoidosis is generally a benign condition, and most patients do not require treatment. However, some patients develop a chronic and progressive disease that can lead to life-threatening complications and multi-organ involvement [4].

Sarcoidosis also demonstrates considerable ethnic and regional variability. In Turkey, available epidemiological data suggest an incidence of approximately 4 per 100,000 per year [9]. Despite advances in diagnostic approaches, no universally accepted or definitive standard exists for establishing the diagnosis of sarcoidosis. In clinical practice, diagnostic decisions are largely based on the individual patient’s clinical presentation, with the selection and prioritization of diagnostic investigations varying according to disease stage and organ involvement. Consequently, both the diagnostic pathway and the extent of evaluation are tailored to the patient’s clinical findings [4, 5, 7]. Similarly, treatment strategies are individualized, with therapeutic decisions guided by the patient’s clinical course rather than a uniform algorithm. The indication for treatment, along with the selection of therapeutic agents, dosing, and treatment duration, is determined by the type and severity of organ involvement and the presence of disease progression, underscoring the need for a personalized management strategy [5–7, 10, 11].

Despite the availability of clinical guidelines, considerable variation persists in the diagnosis, management, and follow-up of sarcoidosis in real-world practice. This study aimed to assess real-world diagnostic, therapeutic, and follow-up practices for sarcoidosis among pulmonologists in Turkey through a nationwide survey, and to evaluate the consistency of these practices with current clinical guidelines.

Materials and methods

The survey was developed specifically for this study, which based on current clinical guidelines and consisted of 26 items covering patient volume, disease stage distribution, diagnostic approaches, diagnostic and follow-up investigations, treatment practices, and follow-up intervals. Some questions required participants to select a single response, whereas others allowed multiple responses. A preliminary version of the questionnaire was pilot-tested with eight clinicians, after which minor revisions were made to improve clarity and content validity. The final survey questions are presented in supplementary file 1.

The finalized survey was distributed to pulmonologists via the email groups of relevant professional societies using their personal email addresses. Specialist physicians were invited to complete the questionnaire by reflecting their own clinical attitudes and practices. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to survey administration. Participants’ names, surnames, and institutional affiliations were not collected to ensure anonymity.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 0091; date: 21 March 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages. Chi-square (χ2) tests of independence assessed associations between categorical variables, and Kruskal–Wallis tests compared distributions of ordinal data, with effect sizes reported. Participants were grouped according to institutions (university vs. non-university hospital), years of clinical experience (≤ 10 vs. > 10 years), and annual sarcoidosis patient volume (≤ 20 vs. > 20 patients per year). Diagnostic approaches, investigations, treatment strategies, and follow-up practices were compared across these groups. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) 22.0 software package.

Results

The survey was completed by 97 pulmonologists from 30 cities, with the highest participation from Ankara (n = 24) and Istanbul (n = 21). The majority of participants (27.8%) had over 25 years of professional experience, followed by those with 1–5 years of experience (20.6%). Most participants worked in university hospitals (41.2%) and reported evaluating 1–20 sarcoidosis patients annually (73.2%). Systemic therapy was routinely initiated by 8.2% (n = 8) of physicians in patients with stage I–II sarcoidosis and by 81.4% (n = 76) in those with stage III–IV disease. All physicians who initiated systemic therapy in stage I–II disease reported prescribing glucocorticoids. Among the 76 physicians who routinely treated stage III–IV disease, 65 prescribed glucocorticoids alone, 10 prescribed a combination of glucocorticoids and methotrexate, and one reported using immunosuppressive therapy alone. Glucocorticoids were the predominant first-line systemic therapy, with methylprednisolone being the most frequently preferred agent. As first-line treatment, 87.6% (n = 85) of participants reported using methylprednisolone, 9.3% (n = 9) prednisolone, and 3.1% (n = 3) deflazacort. Methylprednisolone doses ranged from 20 to 80 mg/day, most commonly 32 mg and 40 mg/day, while prednisolone doses were most frequently 40 mg/day (range: 4–80 mg/day) and deflazacort was prescribed at 20–40 mg/day.

The reported duration of first-line systemic steroid therapy was 3 months in 19.6% (n = 19), 6 months in 40.2% (n = 39), and 12 months in 39.2% (n = 38) of cases. For second-line treatment, 81.4% (n = 79) of physicians indicated a preference for methotrexate. Antifibrotic therapy was considered by 35.1% (n = 34) of participants for patients with stage IV sarcoidosis. The reported treatment approaches are summarized in Table 5.

Discussion

In this nationwide survey, we evaluated current diagnostic, therapeutic, and follow-up practices for sarcoidosis among pulmonologists in Turkey and identified substantial variability among physicians. The majority of respondents were from tertiary and academic centers and sarcoidosis care in Turkey is largely concentrated in specialized referral centers. Therefore, the study reflects real-world decision-making in centers most experienced with sarcoidosis rather than general outpatient practice. The most prominent areas of heterogeneity were diagnostic strategies, baseline and follow-up assessments, and the treatment duration. Physician-focused studies on sarcoidosis are limited in the literature, and most existing research primarily evaluates specific organ involvement. To the best of our knowledge, no physician-focused study has comprehensively evaluated the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up strategies of sarcoidosis.

There is no universally accepted standards for verifying diagnostic criteria; therefore, the diagnosis of sarcoidosis can never be regarded as completely certain. While the demonstration of non-caseating granulomas on biopsy is necessary for diagnosis, it is not sufficient on its own, and supportive radiological findings are also required [2, 4, 5, 7, 10]. Current guidelines recommend EBUS-guided lymph node sampling as the initial diagnostic procedure. When parenchymal lesions predominate, transbronchial lung biopsy is suggested to confirm the diagnosis. VATS lung biopsy is not routinely recommended; however, it may be considered as a last-resort diagnostic option [5–7]. In the present survey, consistent with national data from Turkey and current guideline recommendations, pulmonologists most frequently encountered stage I and II sarcoidosis, and approximately 69–74% of participants reported routinely pursuing histopathological confirmation across all disease stages. EBUS was the most commonly preferred diagnostic method irrespective of stage, followed by bronchoscopy with BAL. Surgical lung biopsy was reported more frequently in advanced-stage disease compared with other parenchymal biopsy techniques. Previous studies from Turkey similarly report that patients are most often diagnosed at stages I–II and that histopathological confirmation remains the preferred approach for establishing the diagnosis [12, 13]. In another study from Turkey, EBUS was the most commonly utilized diagnostic procedure, whereas reported rates of VATS biopsy varied considerably, reaching up to 19% [14]. International data also demonstrate notable variability. In the United Kingdom, clinical–radiological diagnosis has been reported in approximately 36% of cases, with EBUS-TBNA as the most frequently used invasive modality, followed by transbronchial biopsy and BAL; mediastinoscopy was performed in approximately 12% of patients [15]. In Iceland, histopathological confirmation was achieved in 54% of cases, most commonly through lung and/or transbronchial biopsies. Mediastinoscopy was performed more frequently than in our cohort, whereas surgical biopsy rates were comparable to those in our study [16]. In a nationwide study from Korea, surgical lung biopsy was performed in 25.6% of patients following EBUS, a rate notably higher than that observed in our study [17]. These findings suggest adherence to guideline based diagnostic strategies indicate a high level of clinical awareness; however, despite general concordance, some differences are present. However, the higher than expected use of VATS in advanced stages may reflect an aggressive diagnostic strategy aimed at obtaining a rapid and definitive diagnosis to inform subsequent follow-up and treatment decisions in Turkey and other countries.

Current guidelines recommend routine baseline investigations in patients with suspected sarcoidosis full blood count, biochemical testing (including urea and electrolytes, liver function tests, and calcium), serum ACE measurement, ECG, chest X-ray and also baseline cardiac, ophthalmologic, and hepatic assessments are suggested, even in asymptomatic patients. Routine Holter monitoring and echocardiography are not recommended in the absence of cardiac symptoms or ECG abnormalities. Advanced cardiac imaging is suggested in patients with clinical risk factors, with cardiac MRI recommended as the preferred initial modality [5, 6, 8, 18]. Additional investigations, such as 24-hour urinary calcium measurement, HRCT in stage IV disease and abdominal ultrasonography are recommended selectively based on clinical context and diagnostic or prognostic relevance [3, 6]. PET-CT may be useful for assessing disease activity, detecting occult involvement, and evaluating disease extent and treatment response [19], while functional assessments, including forced vital capacity (FVC), DLCO, and the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), may be useful for monitoring functional impairment [5, 6]. In our study, most participants routinely performed baseline evaluations strongly recommended by current guidelines, including core laboratory and respiratory assessments. In contrast, a population-based study conducted in Denmark reported that the most frequently performed investigations were chest radiography (84%), thoracic computed tomography (59%), and spirometry (60%), while extended pulmonary function testing was performed in only 28% of patients and PET-CT was rarely utilized (3%) [20]. Data from Iceland similarly demonstrated partial implementation of guideline-recommended assessments: pulmonary function test data were available in only 57.7% of patients, serum calcium levels were measured in 29%, and ACE levels in just 8.6% of cases [16]. In Turkey, the reported prevalence of ocular involvement ranges widely from 11% to 83%, which likely explains the high rate of referral for ophthalmologic evaluation observed in clinical practice [12].

Despite these strengths, several gaps were identified. Routine ECG was performed in only approximately half of patients, and the frequency of routine cardiac and hepatologic evaluations was lower than expected. Overall awareness of extrapulmonary organ involvement appeared limited. Even in the presence of cardiac symptoms, ECG utilization rates were insufficient, and advanced cardiac investigations, including transthoracic echocardiography, Holter monitoring, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, were underutilized. Investigations aimed at excluding alternative diagnoses or supporting the diagnosis, such as sputum acid-fast bacilli testing, autoimmune antibody panels, interferon-gamma release assays, immunoglobulin levels, and purified protein derivative testing, were generally not requested. Overall, while guideline recommended core assessments were largely implemented, there was substantial variability among clinicians in the use of conditionally recommended investigations. This heterogeneity likely reflects individualized clinical decision making based on patient presentation, as well as differences in institutional resources, particularly access to advanced cardiac imaging modalities such as cardiac MRI and PET. The lower frequency of echocardiography requests in university hospitals may be attributable to longer appointment waiting times.

Spontaneous remission occurred in approximately half of the cases, whereas the remaining patients followed a chronic and progressive course, necessitating systemic therapy. Current guidelines recommend initiating treatment when there is a risk of fatal outcomes, permanent organ damage, or significant impairment in quality of life, supporting a stage-adapted and risk-based therapeutic strategy [4–7, 10, 21]. In our study, pulmonologists generally avoided routine systemic therapy in patients with stage I–II disease, while approximately 80% preferred systemic treatment in patients with stage III–IV disease. This pattern reflects a cautious, evidence-aligned approach in early-stage disease, prioritizing the avoidance of unnecessary treatment-related adverse effects, while highlighting the recognition of higher morbidity risks in advanced stages.

Glucocorticoids are the first-line therapy for sarcoidosis, with dosing tailored to disease severity and clinical presentation. An initial prednisolone dose of approximately 10 mg/day is recommended for chronic, slowly progressive disease, whereas higher doses of 20–40 mg/day are advised for acute or rapidly progressive disease. Treatment duration is usually 6–12 months but may be extended up to 24 months when clinically indicated. Methotrexate and azathioprine are recommended as second-line agents in corticosteroid-refractory cases or when steroid dose reduction is not feasible, while cyclophosphamide and rituximab are reserved for more refractory disease [5–7, 10, 11].

International data demonstrate substantial variability in treatment practices. In the United States, 60% of treated patients received steroids alone, 13% nonsteroidal immunosuppressives alone, and 27% combination therapy, with nonsteroidal agents used in 40% overall [22]. In Japan, oral corticosteroids were prescribed in approximately 25% of patients, with other systemic therapies used in fewer than 3% [23]. In the United Kingdom, 39% of patients received oral corticosteroids at presentation, while 42% received no pharmacological treatment [15]. In Iceland, oral corticosteroids were used in 72.6% of cases, followed by methotrexate (24.8%) [16]. Korean nationwide data report higher treatment rates, with nearly 60% of patients receiving systemic steroids, typically initiated at higher doses (prednisolone equivalent dose of 30.0 mg/day) and with a median treatment duration of 11.5 months [17]. In our study, glucocorticoids, particularly methylprednisolone, were the main treatment and most physicians favored a treatment duration of 6–12 months, in line with guideline recommendations. High dose steroid regimens were frequently preferred, possibly reflecting established treatment habits and concerns regarding insufficient therapeutic response. Increased availability of evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of low dose glucocorticoid regimens may help shift clinical practice toward more conservative dosing strategies. Consistent with literature, methotrexate was the most commonly preferred second-line agent among participants.

Fibrotic sarcoidosis develops in approximately 20% of patients with sarcoidosis [24], and its pattern of progression is heterogeneous. Current recommendations support the initiation of antifibrotic therapy in patients who meet criteria for progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) [25]. However, the presence of fibrotic changes alone does not equate to PPF, as the diagnosis requires objective evidence of disease progression during follow-up. In our study, only about one-third of participants reported that they would consider antifibrotic therapy for patients with stage 4 disease. The low rate of antifibrotic use may be related to the fact that the PPF criteria for interstitial lung diseases were defined in the 2022 Official ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT Clinical Practice Guideline and have not yet been sufficiently reflected in daily clinical practice for sarcoidosis.

Current guidelines recommend follow-up every 3–6 months during the first two years for patients who are not receiving treatment [26], while a similar 3–6-month interval is commonly preferred for patients undergoing therapy [11]. All patients undergoing monitoring or treatment are recommended to undergo regular lung function testing, liver function assessment, and chest X-ray as part of routine care. In contrast, serial chest CT scans, 24-hour urinary calcium measurements, and serum ACE levels are not recommended for routine follow-up [6]. In our study, there was a high level of concordance with guideline recommendations regarding both follow-up intervals and the investigations performed during follow-up. Among untreated patients, clinicians were almost evenly divided between 3- and 6-month follow-up intervals. In contrast, nearly all clinicians favored closer follow-up at 3-month intervals for patients receiving treatment. Because of the symptoms and organ dysfunction in patients undergoing treatment, clinicians have found it appropriate to follow up this group more frequently.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were obtained from a single country, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare systems. Second, the number of participants was relatively small, as sarcoidosis patients are predominantly managed in referral centers, which restricts access to a broader physician population. Third, only a limited proportion of participants reported evaluating more than 20 sarcoidosis patients annually, and a substantial number of respondents had more than 25 years of professional experience. Consequently, differences related to individual clinical experience, particularly across varying patient volumes and levels of professional seniority, could not be fully delineated.

Conclusion

Real-world clinical practice in Turkey demonstrated a high degree of concordance with current guideline recommendations, particularly regarding the use of EBUS for diagnosis, stage-adapted treatment decisions, and follow-up intervals. However, notable variability was observed in extrapulmonary screening practices, systemic corticosteroid dosing strategies, and consideration of antifibrotic therapy. Further standardization through national guidance may improve consistency in sarcoidosis management. Similar studies conducted worldwide may help identify variability in physicians’ practice patterns and better delineate areas of uncertainty within existing guidelines, thereby contributing to their refinement and future development.
