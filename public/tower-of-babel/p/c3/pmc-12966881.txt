Respiratory Particle Emissions in Tracheostomized Outpatients: Comparable to Healthy Volunteers
Authors: Petra Nikuri, Anna Tuhkuri Matvejeff, Ahmed Geneid, Teemu Kinnari, Enni Sanmark, Anthony Maalouf, Petra Pietarinen, Saku Sinkkonen, Ville A Vartiainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)Humans continuously emit respiratory particles during routine activities (breathing, speaking, coughing), and these can carry pathogens. Particle size and site of origin determine suspension time, deposition, and transmission risk. Tracheostomy makes the airflow bypass the upper airway partly or in full. Its effect on aerosol generation is unclear; therefore, we compared emissions between tracheostomized and healthy volunteers during tidal breathing, deep breathing, speaking, and coughing, and examined differences across activities within the tracheostomized group.MethodsAerosol emissions of 10 tracheostomized, uninfected volunteers and 16 healthy volunteers were measured with a portable aerosol spectrometer. Tracheostomized participants were measured with and without a heat moisture exchanger. A Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare aerosol emissions between tracheostomized participants and healthy controls. Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used to compare paired measurements within the same individuals in the tracheostomized group.ResultsTracheostomy, whether occluded or not occluded, does not produce significantly more aerosol than healthy participants. Most detected particles were < 1 μm (≈90% of measurements), with medium particles ≈50% and large particles uncommon.ConclusionIn stable outpatients, aerosol emissions did not differ between the three groups across common respiratory activities, suggesting the exhalation route is not a major determinant of emission under these conditions. The impact of tracheostomy care procedures, such as suctioning, was not evaluated in this study.Level of Evidence2.Keywords: aerosol emissions, respiratory aerosols, tracheostomy

Introduction

Humans produce respiratory particles constantly through all respiratory activities such as breathing, speaking, coughing, and sneezing. These particles are generated in the respiratory tract and can carry pathogens. The origin and size distribution of respiratory particles are important factors in determining transmission risk. The size of the particles influences their aerodynamic behavior, including how long they remain suspended in air, where they deposit in the respiratory system, and their potential to carry infectious pathogens [1, 2]. In general, respiratory particles typically span a wide size range, from less than 1 μm to over 100 μm in diameter, and the size distribution is known to vary depending on the activity. Most particles are smaller than 10 μm, with normal breathing producing primarily small particles (< 1 μm) originating mainly deep in the respiratory tract [3, 4]. In contrast, speaking, coughing, and sneezing generate also medium (1–5 μm) and large (> 5 μm) particles generated in the laryngeal and oral area [5, 6].

It is understood that changes in the structure or function of the respiratory tract can alter the distribution of emitted particles. For example, studies have shown that opening the small airways, (bronchioles), for example, during deep breathing or the use of bronchodilators, can affect particle emissions from the lungs [4, 7, 8, 9]. However, there is currently no evidence on how tracheostomy affects particle emissions during common respiratory maneuvers in real patients. A study by Tran et al. [10] used numerical simulations to investigate airflow and particle deposition in the tracheostomized airway, showing changes in airflow dynamics but not providing specific particle emission values.

Investigating emissions from tracheostomized patients also provides an opportunity to study the effect of upper airway on the particle generation as it anatomically and physiologically alters the respiratory tract. In tracheostomy surgery a stoma is created through the front of the neck into the trachea, allowing a tracheostomy tube to be inserted. This enables the air to bypass the upper airways—the nose, mouth, pharynx, and larynx—and enter and exit the lungs directly from the trachea [11, 12, 13]. Most often, the tracheostomy tube is covered with a heat and moisture exchanger (HME), which replicates the filtering and moisturizing effects of upper airways. However, patients can also breathe via an open tracheostomy tube, for example during tracheostomy care such as suctioning and cleaning, or during coughing [11, 14, 15].

Currently, both the tracheostomy surgery and associated care activities are classified as aerosol‐generating procedures [16]. They are considered to carry a high risk of infection transmission due to the potential for aerosolizing virus‐laden secretions [17, 18, 19]. However, despite growing awareness of the role of aerosols in disease transmission, little is known about how tracheostomy affects the number and size distribution of exhaled particles. In this study, we aim to compare the aerosol emissions between tracheostomized volunteers and healthy controls during four different activities: tidal breathing, deep breathing, speaking, and coughing. Additionally, we aim to compare aerosol emissions in tracheostomized individuals with and without an HME during the four different respiratory activities.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This was a prospective study. Study was approved by Ethical committee of Helsinki University hospital (HUS/1701/2020), and research permit was obtained by the Internal review board. A total of 30 participants were recruited for this study. Ten participants had a tracheostomy and 20 were healthy volunteers [19]. From the healthy volunteer group, four participants were excluded from the final analysis due to data corruption.

The measurements were conducted between January and July 2023 at Helsinki University Hospital Ear‐, Nose, and Throat Department. The inclusion criterion for the tracheostomy group was having a tracheostomy, with the additional requirement that the participant's condition allowed them to move and voluntarily participate in the measurements, including the capability of speaking. For the healthy control group, the sole inclusion criterion was being healthy at the time of the measurements. An acute or chronic respiratory disease was the only exclusion criterion for both groups. All tracheostomized participants had laryngeal speech function; no patients with voice prostheses were included. Written informed consent was obtained before the measurements. In addition, we collected information about the participant's overall health and details regarding the tracheostomy, such as the cannula size and the date of the last cannula change (Table S1).

Setup and Instruments

The complete setup and measurement protocol have been described in a previous article [20]. Briefly, all measurements were conducted in an operating room with laminar flow and an air change rate of 30 air changes per hour. Participants were positioned on a surgical bed in a semirecumbent Fowler position. Particle concentrations (particles/L) and particle size distributions (0.253–35.15 μm) were measured using a Grimm 11D Portable Aerosol Spectrometer (GRIMM Aerosol Technik GmbH & Co. KG, Ainring, Germany). The spectrometer was placed at a height of 120 and 40 cm diagonally from the participant's face (Figure 1).

The experimental setup during measurements. Participants followed instructions from a laptop screen during the measurement. The optical particle sizer (in red) was placed 40 cm diagonally from each participant's face.

Protocol

The measurement protocol consisted of four tasks: tidal breathing, deep breathing, speaking, and coughing. The duration of each measurement was 1 min, with 1‐min breaks between (Table 1). For continuous activities (tidal breathing, deep breathing, and speaking), the reported particle emissions represent the mean concentration measured over the 1‐min sampling period. For coughing, which is noncontinuous, participants were instructed to cough three times in succession, repeated three times within the 1‐min period, and the reported value is also the mean concentration over the entire sampling duration, reflecting cumulative exposure potential rather than peak emission per cough. Instructions for the participants were provided via a slideshow. The data collection was initiated after the participant indicated readiness with a hand gesture (thumbs up). To mimic a real clinical situation, participants were not specifically instructed to keep their mouths open or closed during the measurements. Background aerosol levels in the operation room were measured individually for each participant including both healthy and tracheostomized participants prior to the start of the official participant measurements, typically 5–10 min in advance. Additionally, before initiating each respiratory maneuver, the real‐time readout of the particle counter was closely monitored. The background particle concentrations across participants ranged from 0 to 1.48 particles/L.

All tracheostomized patients were offered an unused speaking valve (TrachPhone 7704, Atos Medical, Malmö, Sweden) in the beginning of the measurement. Then, the protocol was performed twice, under two airway conditions: (A) the tracheostomy tube occluded with a speaking valve combined with a heat and moisture exchanger (HME + SV), and (B) the tracheostomy not occluded (Open). When the tracheostomy is occluded with HME + SV, the inhalation flows through the tracheotomy tube, but the expiratory air flows also through the upper airway, hence partially resembling normal exhalation in nontracheostomized individuals. However, when the tracheostomy is open, the exhaled air mainly bypasses the upper airway and exits directly through the tracheostoma [18, 19] (Table 2). When speaking, the participants pressed the lid to use the speaking valve (HME + SV) or occluded the open tracheostomy tube with a finger, to direct the airflow through the vocal cords and upper airways, hence resembling airflow in the nontracheostomized situation. Participants were asked to try the speaking techniques with both HME + SV and open before the measurement.

Statistical Analysis

Particles were classified into three size categories: Small (< 1 μm), medium (1–5 μm), and large (> 5 μm). A Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare aerosol emissions between healthy participants and tracheostomized participants under the four different activities (e.g., deep breathing vs. speaking) and particle size categories (small, medium, and large). The comparison was conducted separately between healthy and HME + SV and between healthy and Open.

A Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used to compare paired measurements within the same individuals in the tracheostomized group. This included: Open versus HME + SV comparisons for total and size‐specific particle emissions, and breathing condition comparisons within individuals (e.g., tidal breathing vs. coughing).

To summarize the emission data, we calculated the mean aerosol particle concentration for each participant per activity. The mean was used to represent typical emission levels for each participant, reducing variability and allowing for easier comparison across conditions and participants.

Post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted using a two‐sample comparison (n = 10 vs. n = 16, α = 0.05, power = 0.8), which indicated that the study was powered to detect large differences (Cohen's d ≈ 1.1).

Results

Of the 10 tracheostomized volunteers, six (60%) were female and four (40%) were male. The median age was 68.5 years (range 31–84). The 16 healthy volunteers consisted of nine (56%) females and seven (44%) males, with a median age of 32.5 years (range 24–82).

Small (< 1 μm) particles were detected in 90% of the measurements in tracheostomized participants, medium (1–5 μm) particles in 48.75% and large (> 5 μm) particles in 0.05%. For the healthy volunteers, small particles were detected in 86.94% of measurements, medium particles in 50%, and large particles in 4.69%.

We observed no statistically significant differences in aerosol emissions when comparing healthy controls to tracheostomized patients (both HME + SV and Open). Discussion

We investigated aerosol emissions in tracheostomized individuals across four different respiratory activities (tidal breathing, deep breathing, speaking, and coughing), compared emissions between tracheostomy occluded (HME + SV) and open conditions, and contrasted the results with those from healthy individuals without tracheostomy. We found no statistically significant differences in aerosol emissions between HME + SV and open, across different respiratory activities. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between tracheostomized and healthy participants.

While tracheostomy care and airway procedures are well‐documented aerosol‐generating procedures [17, 18], our findings suggest that typical respiratory activities in tracheostomized individuals, outside of active interventions like suctioning, may not present a higher transmission risk than those of non‐tracheostomized individuals. This suggests that tracheostomy itself does not inherently increase aerosol generation.

Our findings further suggest that the route of expiratory airflow, whether through the natural upper airway (HME + SV or control) or directly via the tracheostomy (Open), may not substantially impact the quantity or size distribution of aerosols generated during common respiratory activities. This challenges the assumption that bypassing the upper airway would inherently change aerosol emission patterns, given the upper airway's known role as a filter and humidifier [18, 19].

Respiratory particle generation is thought to result primarily from two main mechanisms: fluid film, filament, or bubble breakage/bursting (FFBB) and turbulent aerosolization. FFBB predominantly occurs in the smaller airways and upper respiratory tract, while turbulent aerosolization can occur throughout the respiratory tract, including both the larger bronchi and larynx as well as smaller airways. The upper respiratory tract has been shown to act as a natural filter during exhalation, allowing only small particles to pass into the exhaled air [21, 22].

Moreover, most of the particles detected in our study were small (< 1 μm), consistent with prior research [3, 4, 23]. The lack of significant difference between the three groups in breathing and coughing may therefore indicate that particle generation is more closely tied to lower airway mechanics, such as the reopening of collapsed bronchioles, than to the route of exhalation [9] since in these groups, the same patients breathed either through the upper respiratory tract or a tracheostomy tube. This is further supported by our finding that activities known to open small airways, such as deep breathing, did not produce notably different results between HME + SV and Open. Additionally, the large interindividual variability in aerosol emissions reported in the literature, often spanning orders of magnitude, may have masked smaller effects in our dataset.

Our study is the first to report airway particle generation in tracheostomized patients. One of the strengths of the study is that we adhered to a standardized protocol, providing clear instructions on a screen for each phase, including normal breathing, deep breathing, speaking, and coughing. This approach ensured that the results were consistent and reproducible across participants. Furthermore, the inclusion of stable outpatients without acute respiratory infections helped to establish a consistent baseline for particle emission levels, making it easier to identify differences caused by the specific respiratory activities. However, this choice does limit the broader applicability of our findings, as individuals with respiratory conditions might display different emission patterns. Additionally, while we carefully controlled the testing environment, real‐life conditions may differ.

Contrary to prior studies that report higher aerosol emissions during activities such as coughing and speaking compared to quiet breathing [5, 6], our data did not show statistically significant differences in particle emission across the four activities within the cohorts. This may reflect the relatively small sample size (10 tracheostomized participants and 16 healthy participants) and interindividual variability in aerosol production. However, given the complexities of aerosol measurement in controlled environments, the small sample sizes are inevitable. Furthermore, the sample size used in our study is consistent with other similar aerosol studies, where participant numbers typically range from 10 to 25 [24, 25, 26, 27]. Airflow dynamics—including pressure, turbulence, and velocity—may also differ from normal breathing, further impacting particle formation and dispersion. Additionally, our measurement setup—which relied on ambient particle collection near the participant rather than direct exhalation into a funnel—may have resulted in under‐sampling of brief or forceful events like coughing due to particle dispersion and room airflow. Differences in vocalization loudness or cough intensity were not standardized or measured, which may also explain the lack of clear trends.

Our study did not compare different HMEs or other humidification systems, which are commonly used in tracheostomized patients and may influence aerosol behavior [12, 14]. However, a previous study in 12 stable tracheostomy patients tested five different HMEs and found no significant differences in particle concentrations across devices, suggesting that the choice of HME may not substantially affect emissions [28].

Furthermore, our results suggest that the role of the upper respiratory tract in particle generation, at least in the particle emissions measured in our study, may be smaller than previously thought. While we did not evaluate the impact of tracheostomy care procedures such as suctioning, routine activities like coughing did not produce greater aerosol emissions compared to the healthy control group, suggesting that these activities may not pose as significant a risk as initially assumed. In mild SARS‐CoV‐2 infections, viral replication is often concentrated in the upper respiratory tract, particularly the nasopharynx. As such, bypassing the nasopharynx in tracheostomized individuals may plausibly reduce the viral load in exhaled aerosols [29]. Taken together, this raises the hypothesis that, in stable outpatients, tracheostomized individuals may pose a lower environmental infection risk than non‐tracheostomized patients under similar conditions. However, this inference is speculative and should be further tested.

Conclusion

This study found no significant differences in aerosol emissions between tracheostomized individuals with tracheostomy occluded and not occluded nor between tracheostomized and healthy individuals across a range of respiratory activities. These findings suggest that the route of exhalation may not substantially alter aerosol emission during common respiratory maneuvers in stable outpatients. However, more studies are needed to confirm these findings and to assess their implications for infection control and clinical care.
