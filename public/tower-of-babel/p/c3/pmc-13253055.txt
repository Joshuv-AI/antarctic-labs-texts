Understanding donor behaviour following a deferral for low haemoglobin: A cross‐sectional survey study
Authors: Amanda Thijsen, Marijke Welvaert, Peter Bentley, Joanna Speedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesWhile deferral due to low haemoglobin (Hb) is known to affect donor return, how donors react to and manage low Hb is less well understood. This paper aims to (1) describe donor activities to manage low Hb, and (2) examine donor knowledge, recalled emotions, satisfaction and potential concerns, and to determine their association with doctor visit and actual donor return.Materials and MethodsAn online survey was completed by 360 donors (mean age = 51.0 ± 16.7 years; 78.1% female) on average 13 days after a low Hb deferral (females <115 g/L; males <125 g/L). Comparative analyses (gender, age, Hb level and donation experience) and logistic regression analyses were conducted.ResultsMost donors reported visiting (51%) or making an appointment to see (17%) a doctor. Donors reported a moderate understanding of their Hb result and recalled feeling Disappointed and Valued rather much at the time of deferral. Donors reported high intentions to return to donate, with little self‐reported effect of the low Hb result. Significant determinants for a doctor visit were feeling Grateful (odds ratio [OR]: 1.27, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05–1.54), Worried (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.71) and Disappointed (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.64–0.96). Significant determinants of return within 12 months were feeling Sadness (OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.41), Valued (OR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.63–0.94) and donation intentions (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.57).ConclusionTo improve donor retention or doctor follow‐up, targeting emotional responses may help, but further research is needed to understand what other factors affect donor return following a low Hb result.

INTRODUCTION

To ensure a stable blood supply, it is important for blood collection agencies to investigate ways to minimize the effects of low haemoglobin (Hb) deferrals on donor return while safeguarding donor health. Being deferred for a low Hb level is one of the most common reasons for temporary deferral from blood, plasma and platelet donation, with women and repeat donors being more at risk [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. These deferrals are also a known deterrent for continued blood donation [1, 6, 7, 8]. In an Australian study, donors were 69% less likely to have returned and donated less frequently in the 3 years following a low Hb deferral compared to no deferral of any type [1]. Similar findings have been reported in other settings [9, 10, 11].

While the negative effects on donor return rates have been documented, there has been less attention given to donor experiences of being deferred for low Hb. In their review of the literature, Davison et al. [6] suggested that donors are less likely to return due to negative emotions experienced at the time of deferral [12, 13], disruption of their routine donation habit [13], confusion regarding their eligibility to donate [3, 13] and concerns about subsequent deferrals [7]. These suggestions, however, were based on studies either small in sample size [13], focused on examining one aspect such as emotions [12] or were given as possible explanations for trends observed in the data [3, 7]. In addition, as a low Hb level may indicate a health issue, some blood collection agencies advise donors to see a doctor for further investigation [14], which may result in additional costs, inconvenience and potential angst to the donor. Further research on donor experiences following a low Hb deferral is warranted to identify potentially modifiable factors affecting donor return and inform intervention design.

Therefore, the aim of this survey was to examine donors' experiences of receiving a low Hb deferral of 6 months. It also aimed to assess donors' perceptions of and self‐reported medical follow‐up in response to the recommendation to consult a doctor for low Hb, and the potential effects on donor return. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study setting

This study was conducted at Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (Lifeblood). To ensure that the donor's Hb level is at a safe minimum threshold to donate, Hb levels are checked using a capillary sample from a finger prick test immediately prior to the donation (in a limited number of centres a venous sample is first line for the Hb screening test). If their Hb level is below the acceptable range for donation (see Table 1 for reference ranges), a venous sample is taken to confirm the result. Donors with a Hb result below 115 g/L for females and 125 g/L for males are given a hardcopy letter with their Hb result from the donor centre staff and referred to a doctor (i.e., medical practitioner), and are deferred for 6 months. In addition, all donors with a Hb level lower than 100 g/L will speak with a Lifeblood medical officer while they are onsite, who then conducts a brief assessment and conveys the urgency of their referral [15].

Study design and procedure

Using a cross‐sectional online survey, data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted by Australian Red Cross Lifeblood. The online survey was emailed to eligible donors (n = 1608) from October to November 2023 on a weekly basis, with a reminder email sent after 3 days to non‐responders. Donors were considered eligible to participate in this study if they had attended a whole‐blood or plasma donation appointment in the last 8–14 days, had a low Hb level at their appointment (females <115 g/L; males <125 g/L) and had a valid email address. Donors were excluded if they had attempted to donate for therapeutic or autologous reasons, were a Lifeblood staff member, had been placed off‐service (i.e., the donor has temporarily opted out of communications) or had been contacted for research in the last 3 months. Routinely collected data were extracted for all participants to capture demographic characteristics (age, gender), donation characteristics (donation count by phlebotomy type, Hb status) and subsequent donations within the next 12 months (next attendance date). Ethical approval to conduct the study was obtained from Australian Red Cross Lifeblood Human Research Ethics Committee (2023#27).

Survey instrument

The questionnaire (see Supporting Information) was developed using insights from existing literature [3, 6, 7, 12, 13] and Lifeblood communications to address our research objectives. First, we asked respondents about their satisfaction with their overall experience of donating blood and with the level of information Lifeblood provides on Hb and iron. Second, we asked respondents about their knowledge of their Hb result and iron in general. Third, we asked respondents to rate how they felt at the time they were told their Hb level was too low to donate. Fourth, we asked donors to select what activities they have undertaken since their last donation appointment to manage their low Hb, as well as intentions to visit a doctor to discuss their Hb level and reasons for not wanting to visit a doctor. Finally, we asked respondents about their intentions to donate blood in the next 12 months, the potential influence of their Hb result on their donation intentions and to rate their level of concern about being refused to donate, having a low Hb level or feeling judged if not eligible to donate at their next donation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp, 2023, Stata Statistical Software: Release 18, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). For descriptive analysis, sample characteristics and survey items were summarized by medians (interquartile range) for continuous variables and by percentages for categorical variables. A variable ‘doctor visit’ was created using dichotomous coding based on their responses when asked about their post‐donation activities (1 if the respondent had selected either ‘Visited your general practitioner to discuss your eligibility to donate blood’, ‘Visited your general practitioner to discuss your haemoglobin level’ or ‘Made an appointment to see my general practitioner’; and 0 if other). Univariate differences were examined using t‐tests, chi‐square goodness‐of‐fit tests and one‐way analyses of variances for normally distributed data and Kruskal–Wallis H tests and Mann–Whitney U tests for skewed data. For comparative analyses, continuous variables age, donation count and Hb level were converted to categorical variables (age group: 18–44 years, 45–83 years; donor status: novice [0–2 donations], experienced [3–10 donations], very experienced [11–466 donations]; Hb type: very low [<100 g/L], low [100–124 g/L]) to assess differences in responses to survey items. The categorization of age was informed by childbearing age (<45 years), which is associated with increased risk of iron deficiency [16].

To determine whether post‐donation activities, knowledge, emotions, satisfaction and concerns or intentions were associated with likelihood of a doctor visit, we performed separate univariate logistic regression analyses where the survey items were the independent variables and doctor visit (Yes/No) as the dependent variable, including age (continuous) and Hb level (continuous) as covariates. Similarly, we performed separate logistic regression analyses for donor return within 12 months (Yes/No), including sex, donation count (continuous), and Hb level (continuous) as covariates. Only variables significantly associated with return or doctor visit were included as covariates in the logistic regression analyses. Statistical significance was defined a priori as p < 0.05. Results are presented as percentages, or mean (±standard deviation) or mean differences (∆).

RESULTS

A total of 360 donors (22% response rate) completed the survey on average 13 days after their donation appointment (range: 8–28 days). An overview of the characteristics of the sample is provided in Table 2 (details of Hb levels stratified by gender are shown in Table S1). Most of the respondents were female (78%), with an average age of 51 (±16.7) years. The participants were quite experienced, with more than half of them (54%) having donated more than 10 times and an average count of 18 (±27.2) donations. A small number of participants (6%) had a Hb result of <100 g/L. Half of respondents (50%) returned to attempt to donate again within 12 months. Among those who returned, there was a greater proportion of males (χ 2(1) = 6.85, p < 0.01) with a higher Hb level (t(358) = −4.51, p < 0.01) and a greater donation count (U = −5.58, p < 0.01) than the non‐returned group. There was no evidence of differences in age between those who returned and those who did not (p = 0.30).

Deferral experience

Respondents had on average a moderate understanding of their Hb result and the implications on their eligibility to donate blood (2.51 ± 0.75). When asked specifically about their eligibility to donate, 64% indicated correctly that they were unable to donate for 6 months and 31% indicated that they were unable to donate again until they had been cleared by their doctor. Only a small number of respondents incorrectly indicated that they could still donate (n = 10; 2.8%) or were unsure of their eligibility (n = 6; 1.7%). Donors had a good understanding of why their Hb levels were tested (3.89 ± 1.05), how blood donation affects Hb levels in the body (3.36 ± 1.20) and what actions are needed to manage their low Hb levels (3.35 ± 1.24). Donors understood to a lesser extent the difference between Hb and iron (2.95 ± 1.29). The most common emotions recalled when advised of a low Hb result and the need for deferral were Disappointment and Valued. On average, respondents were very satisfied with their overall experience of donating blood (8.86 ± 1.90), as well as the level of information Lifeblood provides about blood donation and how it affects Hb levels and iron health (8.58 ± 1.98).

Potential differences in donor experience were examined by sex, age group, donor experience and Hb level (see Tables S2–S5). Younger donors reported a greater understanding of why Lifeblood tested both Hb and ferritin compared with older donors (∆ = 0.25). Women recalled greater feelings of Sadness than men (∆ = 0.49). Older donors recalled feeling more Valued (∆ = 0.36) and Grateful (∆ = 0.60) than younger donors. Very experienced donors recalled feeling more Valued (∆ = 0.54) and less Sad (∆ = −0.52) than novice donors. Women reported greater satisfaction with their overall donation experience than men (∆ = 0.77).

Low Hb management activities

Almost half of the respondents (45%) reported attempting to learn more about Hb after their appointment (see Table 3), with the most common source of information being the internet (41.1%), followed by the Lifeblood website (12.8%) and the Lifeblood call centre (2.5%). Most respondents (50.8%) reported having visited a doctor to either discuss their Hb level (28.3%) or their eligibility to donate blood (2.5%), or both (20.0%). Further, 16.9% of respondents reported making an appointment to see a doctor. Among those without an appointment or visit, 33.6% (n = 36) had no intentions to visit a doctor. Reasons for not wanting to visit a doctor included not feeling the need to (55.6%), preferring to manage their low Hb themselves (30.6%), cost (30.6%), convenience (25.0%) and busy with other commitments (19.4%).

Further investigations into group differences (see also Tables S2–S5) showed that respondents who reported searching for information were on average 5 years younger (t(358) = 3.02, p < 0.01), appeared to have a slightly lower Hb level (∆ = 1.4, t(358) = 1.97, p = 0.05) and made on average eight less donations (U = 2.41, p = 0.02) than those who had not reported searching for information. Those who had or were planning to see their doctor were on average 7 years older (t(358) = −4.05, p < 0.01) and appeared to have a slightly lower Hb result (∆ = 1.7) (t(358) = 2.25, p = 0.03) than those who did not. Of those with a Hb <100 g/L, 18 (90%) had seen a doctor.

Intentions to return

Intentions to donate blood or plasma in the next 12 months was on average high among respondents (6.34 ± 1.34). Overall, the low Hb test result had little influence on the donors' intentions to donate in the future (0.83 ± 1.68). Donors were somewhat concerned about having a low Hb level (1.64 ± 1.15) and being refused to donate blood (1.43 ± 1.24) at their next attendance but were less concerned about feeling judged if deemed not eligible to donate (0.44 ± 1.00).

Examining group differences (see Tables S2–S5 for details), donors with a very low Hb result reported lower intentions to return than donors with a low Hb result (∆ = –0.63). Younger donors and novice donors felt more discouraged by their low Hb result to continue donating than older donors (∆ = –0.74) and very experienced donors (∆ = –0.80), respectively. Further, younger donors were more concerned about being refused to donate blood (∆ = 0.44) and feeling judged if not eligible to donate (∆ = 0.22) than older donors. In addition, novice donors reported feeling more concerned about being refused at their next donation than experienced (∆ = 0.47) and very experienced donors (∆ = 0.57).

Factors associated with doctor visit and donor return

We explored whether deferral experience, management activities and donation intentions were associated with doctor visit and actual donor return within 12 months (see Table 4). The likelihood of a doctor visit increased with greater recalled feelings of Grateful (odds ratio [OR]: 1.27, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05–1.54) and Worried (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.71) and lower feelings of Disappointment (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.64–0.96). The likelihood of return within 12 months increased with greater recalled feelings of Sadness (OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.41) and higher donation intentions (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.57) while greater feelings of Valued (OR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.63–0.94) decreased the likelihood of return.

DISCUSSION

This study provides insights into how donors react to and manage a low Hb result, and how these responses affect donor return. While half of respondents reported visiting a doctor within 4 weeks after a low Hb result, one‐third did not seek medical advice. Donors generally reported a good understanding of Hb and the implications on their eligibility to donate, and almost half had looked for more information, although few used Lifeblood resources. The most strongly recalled emotions at deferral were Disappointment and feeling Valued. Although donors also expressed some concern about having a low Hb result and being refused to donate again, most reported high intentions to return, with little self‐reported influence of the Hb result. This shows that low Hb deferral does not uniformly deter donors from continuing to donate and suggests that donor experience and interpretation may influence donor retention.

One of the key findings of this study is that donation intentions do not appear to be affected by the low Hb result. Respondents reported high intentions to return, little influence of the Hb result and only minimal concerns of a subsequent low Hb deferral. Further, donor concerns regarding low Hb or potential subsequent deferrals were not associated with actual donor return. This aligns with Hillgrove et al. [13], who found that return was influenced by convenience and opportunity to donate rather than the Hb result. They found that older people with less self‐assessed time commitments were more likely to return following a low Hb deferral than younger people with more carer's responsibility. Further research is needed to clarify reasons for non‐return to inform targeted interventions to encourage return.

To safeguard donor health, donors are strongly advised to seek medical care to investigate and manage underlying causes of low Hb. Half of the respondents reported visiting a doctor, consistent with other studies [14, 17, 18]. A greater proportion of donors with very low Hb levels had visited a doctor, which suggests that the additional telephone call with a medical officer may encourage doctor visits. This will, however, need to be confirmed in future studies, as this cannot explicitly be inferred from our data. Only 10% of our sample had no intentions to visit a doctor, with most not feeling the need to and preferring to manage their low Hb level themselves. These donors may have had prior investigations relating to low Hb. These potentially modifiable barriers could be addressed by clearly communicating a rationale for medical follow‐up. Additional research is needed to identify motivators for seeking medical care and to inform intervention design.

The emotional implications of a deferral have been noted in other studies [12, 13]. In this study, donors most commonly recalled feeling Disappointed but Valued following a low Hb deferral. This finding suggests that disappointment may persist beyond the donation centre, in contrast to other research which reported transient negative emotional responses to deferral [12, 13]. Emotions were the only significant determinants of doctor visit, with Grateful and Worried being positive determinants and Disappointment a negative determinant of a self‐reported doctor visit. This aligns with broader literature showing that concern increases healthcare‐seeking behaviour [19, 20]. Further, greater feelings of Sadness and lesser feelings of Valued increased the likelihood of a donor returning to donate again. A potential explanation for this effect may be that some donors felt sad due to their inability to donate at the time [13], as they had strong motivations to donate and this motivation, in turn, encouraged them to donate again. Further, donors may have felt valued through the provision of valuable knowledge about their health [13], which may have resulted from taking a break from donation to help their bodies recover. More research is needed to further understand why donors experience certain emotions at the time of deferral and how they affect their return behaviour following a low Hb deferral.

It is important to consider the study limitations when interpreting these results. First, our sample participants were quite experienced, which is reflective of the increased exposure to risk of a low Hb deferral over time. Novice donors, however, may have different experiences regarding low Hb deferrals and may require a separate investigation. Second, this study relied on self‐reported data, which may be affected by recall bias. Respondents may have inaccurately remembered or misreported how they reacted to and managed their low Hb level, potentially affecting the reliability of the data. Third, this study may have been affected by response bias, as donors who chose to participate may differ from those who did not. For example, responders may have had stronger opinions on their low Hb result and may have been more compliant with the recommendations given by Lifeblood, potentially affecting our results. Finally, in lieu of available validated survey items relating to low Hb deferral experience, the questionnaire items were designed by the study team with subject‐matter expertise in blood donor health and survey design using insights from the literature. Our questionnaire, however, has not been validated, and careful interpretation of our findings is advised.

In conclusion, doctor consultation, Hb knowledge, information‐seeking behaviour, donation satisfaction and concerns about low Hb or subsequent deferral had limited influence on donor return. Those wishing to develop interventions to improve donor retention or uptake of doctor visits to discuss Hb might target their emotional responses, but more research is needed to understand whether other factors, such as convenience and perceived time commitment, affect donor return.
