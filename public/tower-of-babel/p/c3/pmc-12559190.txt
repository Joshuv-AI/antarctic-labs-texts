Study on urban passenger transport carbon reduction pathways based on system dynamics
Authors: Ranqi Liu, Ning Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPassenger transport carbon emission control is recognized as a critical component in achieving the dual-carbon goals amid accelerating urbanization. A system dynamics model incorporating population and economic factors, energy consumption, traffic operation, and carbon emission control has been developed. Through boundary identification and validation of variable relationships, a simulation model has been established. Scenario simulations revealed that, among single-policy interventions, public transit optimization and energy technology upgrades demonstrated greater effectiveness than travel demand management. Dual-policy combinations significantly improved emission reduction efficiency, with effectiveness ranked as follows: public transport and energy technology policies (11.32%), public transport and travel demand policies (10.58%), and energy technology and travel demand policies (8.94%). The findings suggest that single measures are insufficient to meet emission reduction targets. Instead, a multidimensional policy synergy—integrating technological upgrades, structural optimization, and behavioral guidance—is essential in providing a systematic carbon reduction pathway for urban transport development.Keywords: Passenger transport, System dynamics, Scenario analysis, Emission reduction pathwaySubject terms: Climate-change mitigation, Energy conservation, Sustainability, Governance

Introduction

“Carbon Peak” and “Carbon Neutrality” often referred to as China’s “Dual Carbon Goals”, is a key national strategy aiming to peak carbon dioxide emissions (Carbon Dioxide Emissions Peak, or Carbon Peak) by 2030 and achieve Carbon Neutrality (net-zero CO₂ emissions) by 2060. This commitment represents China’s significant contribution to global climate action. With the continuous advancement of global urbanization, urban residents’ travel demands have grown increasingly diverse, personalized, and frequent. As a critical infrastructure supporting urban operations and daily life, the urban transportation system is recognized as a key indicator in evaluating sustainable urban development. However, traditional transportation models are characterized by structural imbalances, severe traffic congestion, and low energy efficiency, thereby contributing to the continuous growth of carbon emissions from transportation systems and placing significant pressure on urban ecosystems and residents’ quality of life1,2. Studies indicate that the transportation sector accounts for approximately 24% of global carbon emissions, with urban passenger transport identified as one of the major contributors3. In China, in particular, the rapid increase in vehicle ownership and the persistent rise in urban population density have resulted in an annual increase of approximately 1.7% in transportation-related carbon emissions4,5. This trend has been even more pronounced in developing countries, thereby posing a significant challenge to the attainment of the goals of “carbon peaking” and “carbon neutrality”6.

Against this backdrop, achieving a low-carbon transition in urban passenger transportation systems and exploring sustainable emission reduction pathways have emerged as central concerns for researchers and policymakers globally. Mainstream research has primarily concentrated on three dimensions: technological innovation, structural optimization, and behavioral regulation. The adoption and promotion of new energy technologies are recognized as key technological approaches to enhancing emission reduction efficiency7,8. Adjustments in the transportation structure—such as increasing the share of public transportation and optimizing the layout of rail transit—constitute the structural foundation for systemic emission reduction9. Meanwhile, encouraging green travel behavior—such as cycling, walking, or using public transit—is viewed as a vital social mechanism for achieving urban carbon reduction goals10,11.

However, the urban transportation system is inherently complex, marked by multivariable interactions and robust feedback loops. As a result, single-policy measures are often insufficient to achieve intended outcomes. Consequently, systemic modeling approaches have garnered increasing scholarly attention. Among them, the System Dynamics (SD) model is extensively employed in the study of urban transport carbon emissions, given its capability in analyzing complex feedback systems, simulating policy effects, and forecasting scenarios12–14. For example, Ercan et al.15 evaluated the carbon reduction potential of the U.S. public transportation system using an SD model and demonstrated that increased public transit usage significantly reduces per-trip carbon emissions. Liu et al.16 developed an SD model based on Beijing’s transportation system to simulate energy consumption trends under different policy scenarios, thereby offering a quantitative foundation for policy optimization. Wen et al.17 integrated the SD model with urban economic growth models, establishing a coupling framework between urban transportation and industrial structure, thus revealing the dynamic mechanisms through which transportation development influences urban carbon emissions.

In addition, several studies have incorporated emerging tools such as the LEAP model (Long-range Energy Alternatives Planning System), agent-based modeling, and machine learning to facilitate more precise simulation of urban transportation systems. For instance, Al-Osaimi et al.18 simulated a scenario in a Middle Eastern country in which public transport share was increased to 30%, resulting in a 70% reduction in energy consumption and a 50% reduction in carbon emissions. Norouzian-Maleki et al.19 combined System Dynamics and Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to enhance the evaluation framework for urban transportation policy performance. Shamsapour et al.20 conducted an empirical assessment of the potential carbon reductions associated with substituting natural gas for gasoline in Tehran’s multi-modal transport system.

Meanwhile, a growing body of research has examined micro-level drivers and pathways influencing carbon emissions, including urban morphology, road network structure, and resident behavior21–23. For example, Chen et al.24 highlighted that road density and the spatial match between housing and employment significantly influence transportation carbon intensity. Chester25, using a life cycle assessment approach, evaluated the long-term carbon implications of high-speed rail construction and emphasized emissions from infrastructure, which are often overlooked. He et al.26 analyzed the interaction between air pollution and carbon emissions from transportation in Chinese cities and suggested that environmental regulation contributes synergistically to carbon mitigation efforts.

Innovations

The growing complexity of urban travel demand and the accelerating structural transformation of transportation systems pose increasingly complex, systemic challenges for controlling carbon emissions from urban passenger transport. Although substantial progress has been made in identifying policy tools, exploring technical pathways, and conducting scenario analysis, much of the current research remains predominantly macro-level in focus. Limited scholarly attention has been given to the dynamic coupling mechanisms among various subsystems—such as population and economy, energy consumption, road structure, traffic operations, and carbon governance—within the urban transportation system. Few studies have undertaken integrated, cross-sectoral modeling efforts at the city level27–29.

Therefore, this study seeks to develop a System Dynamics-based simulation model for reducing carbon emissions from urban passenger transport by incorporating five key subsystems. Taking Beijing as a case study and utilizing statistical data from 2010 to 2024, the model integrates transportation operation data, energy structure information, and carbon governance parameters. Single-policy and dual-policy scenarios are employed to evaluate the dynamic effects of various policy configurations on carbon emissions within the urban passenger transport system. The findings are anticipated to generate actionable policy recommendations for low-carbon transportation, providing theoretical support and decision-making references for China’s “transportation power” strategy and dual-carbon goals.

Urban passenger transport carbon emission system dynamics model

System boundary

The definition of the system boundary served as a fundamental basis for constructing the System Dynamics (SD) model, determining the inclusion and exclusion of variables within the model framework30,31. As a highly complex and dynamic system, the SD model for urban passenger transport carbon emissions is typically composed of multiple interrelated subsystems. In this study, the system comprises five functional subsystems: population and economy, energy consumption, urban road infrastructure, traffic operation, and carbon emissions. The interactions among these subsystems constitute the core conceptual basis for the evolution of urban carbon reduction pathways within transportation systems32.

Stock and flow diagram of the system

After completing the causal loop analysis of the urban passenger transport carbon reduction pathways, a system dynamics model was constructed using Vensim PLE software. Based on the qualitative causal relationships among variables, a stock and flow diagram was developed. By assigning initial values or defining calculation formulas for each variable, the model enabled dynamic simulation and predictive analysis35.

Relying on the structural characteristics of the study subject, the model was divided into five functional modules: the Population and Economy Subsystem, Energy Consumption Subsystem, Urban Road Subsystem, Traffic Operation Subsystem, and Carbon Emission Control Subsystem. Modeling and simulation analysis for each of these subsystems are presented in the subsequent Sects36. –37.

The population and economy subsystem is centered on two core stock variables: urban population and urban GDP. A bidirectional coupling structure is formed. The population component dynamically adjusts through birth rate, death rate, and migration rate, the latter of which is influenced by per capita GDP. Economic growth is driven by the combined output of the primary, secondary, and tertiary industries38. The relationships between all variables are shown in Fig. 3.

Population and Economy Subsystem.

The traffic operation subsystem visually illustrates the dynamic relationships between urban passenger transport demand and operational components through a compact structure. The core modules include three major transport categories—urban rail transit, public buses, and private cars. Blue arrows indicate feedback relationships among transport demand, volume allocation, and energy consumption indicators. The relationships between all variables are shown in Fig. 4.

Traffic Operation Subsystem.

This subsystem begins with emission coefficients for new energy vehicles, gasoline vehicles, and electric locomotives. Blue arrows direct the flow toward the core variable—incremental CO2 emissions—and link with total CO2 emissions, emission reductions, and pollution levels to form a dynamic balancing structure. Emission sources on the left and the governance module on the right are linked in a closed loop through “carbon governance efficiency” and “governance investment,” demonstrating a “pollution–accumulation–mitigation” system feedback cycle. The relationships between all variables are shown in Fig. 5.

Carbon Emission Control Subsystem.

The urban road subsystem is structured around urban road length as the core stock variable. Arrows indicate its interactions with urban GDP, fixed asset investment, and rail transit development. Key drivers include the impact of per capita GDP growth on increasing the share of fixed asset investment, which in turn promotes expansion in urban infrastructure and rail mileage. Additionally, per capita travel frequency and per capita road length form a demand feedback loop, while passenger capacity and unit transport volume constitute the subsystem for transport efficiency. The energy consumption subsystem adopts a hub-and-spoke structure to clearly represent energy flow relationships. Centered on “energy consumption volume,” branches extend to different transport modes—public buses, taxis, private cars, and subways. Each transport mode’s specific consumption of diesel, electricity, and other energy types is dynamically linked to the corresponding energy price systems, which ultimately converge to determine the total energy cost39. Model validation

Following the construction of the stock-and-flow diagram, a comprehensive model validation was conducted in accordance with standard system dynamics modeling procedures, prior to entering the simulation phase. This step was essential to ensure both the structural soundness and the feasibility of simulation.

The widely adopted validation methods in system dynamics include structure consistency testing, time step sensitivity analysis, and historical data fitting. In this study, all three methods were applied to systematically evaluate the urban passenger transport carbon emission system model. The model successfully passed all validation procedures, confirming its reliability for simulation analysis.

Applicability test

The system dynamics model for urban passenger transport emissions was fully developed using Vensim PLE software. A comprehensive review was conducted of all internal variables, feedback loops, and their logical relationships, with particular attention given to the consistency of equation logic and unit dimensions. In addition, the “Check Model” tool was used to verify the model’s unit consistency and formula completeness. The system returned the result “Model is OK”, indicating that the model passed the applicability test and demonstrated sound structural integrity and feasibility for simulation.

Time step sensitivity test

The time step sensitivity test was conducted to evaluate the model’s stability and reliability under different simulation time intervals. This test involved setting various time steps and examining whether the model’s output remained consistent across different levels of temporal discretization, thereby assessing the model’s sensitivity to time step size42.

In this study, the variable Urban GDP was selected as the reference indicator. The simulation time step was set to 0.25, 0.5, and 1, respectively. The model was run under each condition, and the output results were recorded. A comparison of the simulation output curves revealed only minimal deviations between the results, indicating that the model exhibited excellent consistency and stability throughout the simulations.

These findings suggest that the developed system dynamics model for urban passenger transport emissions possesses strong robustness with respect to time discretization and demonstrates reliable dynamic simulation capabilities.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis in system dynamics serves as a critical tool for assessing the reliability and robustness of a model. It is intended to evaluate how variations in key parameters influence model outputs. Sensitivity testing not only helps determine the model’s adaptability under different scenarios, but also provides valuable insights for subsequent policy simulations, thereby enhancing the model’s explanatory and predictive capabilities in real-world applications.

In addition, this process can guide model calibration and data collection by identifying and prioritizing key variables that exert significant influence on system behavior. In this study, urban GDP was selected as the reference variable for sensitivity analysis43. Energy cost ratio variable is used to conduct the sensitivity test. Urban passenger transport carbon reduction scenario simulation

Baseline configuration

Within the research framework of urban passenger transport carbon reduction pathways, the previously constructed system dynamics model was used as the foundation for scenario simulation. The simulation period was set from 2010 to 2040, with a time step of one year, in order to analyze the effects of different policy interventions on carbon reduction pathways over time.

The simulation was conducted in phases. First, a baseline scenario was simulated without introducing any policy adjustments, keeping all initial system conditions constant. This served as a reference point for evaluating subsequent policy interventions. Then, multiple policy variables were introduced sequentially to simulate single-policy interventions and dual-policy combinations. The goal was to systematically evaluate differences in emission reduction effectiveness and system responses under various configurations, and to explore the potential impacts of different policy mixes on optimizing urban passenger transport carbon reduction pathways.

Carbon emissions by transport mode

Simulation analyses were conducted for the carbon emissions of public buses, taxis, and rail transit in Fig. 11. The results showed that emissions from both public buses and taxis followed a declining trend, while emissions from rail transit exhibited a gradual increase. These trends were primarily driven by sustained population growth, expanded transport demand, and icreased per capita travel frequency—all of which contributed to the expansion of urban passenger transport capacity.

Simulation of Carbon Emissions by Transport Mode.

During the early simulation stage, traditional fossil fuel-powered transport modes remained dominant, particularly diesel- and gasoline-fueled buses and taxis, which still held a substantial share of the overall transport structure. This dominance resulted in persistently high carbon emissions. Additionally, the underdevelopment of urban road and rail transit infrastructure, along with the relatively low penetration of clean energy vehicles, further intensified emission pressures.

As the system evolved, carbon emissions from buses and taxis steadily declined, while emissions from rail transit reached a peak. This phase marked a critical turning point in the transport system’s transformation from high-emission to high-efficiency, low-carbon operation. This shift was closely associated with policy interventions and structural optimization—including an increase in the adoption of new energy vehicles, expansion of rail transit mileage, reallocation of fixed asset investment toward green transport, and continuous improvements in vehicle energy efficiency technologies.

Furthermore, adjustments in the energy pricing mechanism and enhancements in carbon governance efficiency significantly contributed to emission reductions. The widespread adoption of rail transit, electric buses, the accelerated phase-out of fossil-fuel vehicles, and the integration of intelligent transportation systems jointly drove improvements in energy structure and efficiency across the passenger transport sector.

This ongoing trend not only supports the sustainable development of urban transport systems, but also lays a solid foundation for achieving the strategic goals of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality. Single-policy scenarios and simulation analysis

Dual-policy combination design and simulation analysis

In actual urban governance and transportation management, policies are often not implemented in isolation but are promoted in combination to achieve more comprehensive and impactful regulatory effects. Building on the previous analysis of single-policy effects, this study further introduced dual-policy combination scenarios to explore the integrated impact of different policy combinations on urban passenger transport carbon reduction pathways. Specifically, three types of dual-policy combinations were designed: (1) Public transport + energy technology policy linkage; (2) Public transport + travel behavior intervention; (3) Energy technology + travel behavior integration.

These composite policy scenarios were simulated and comparatively analyzed to investigate their emission reduction mechanisms within the system. The simulation results of the relevant variables are presented in Fig. 15, highlighting the impacts and dynamic responses of each policy combination on the carbon reduction pathway of urban passenger transport.

Simulation of Dual-Policy Combination Scenarios.

As shown in the simulation results, the three dual-policy combinations exhibited notable differences in emission reduction performance. Specifically, the effectiveness of carbon reduction from strongest to weakest was as follows: (1) Public transport + energy technology (11.32%); (2) Public transport + travel behavior (10.58%); (3) Energy technology + travel behavior (8.94%).

These results indicate that policy combinations differ in both their mechanisms of action and regulatory strength. Among them, the synergy between public transport and energy technology policies yielded the most significant emission reduction benefit.

Moreover, compared with single-policy interventions, dual-policy combinations demonstrated more substantial improvements in the carbon reduction pathway for urban passenger transport. This suggests that under multi-factor interactions, different policy variables can achieve stronger complementarity and linkage, producing a synergistic effect that enhances the overall carbon efficiency of the system.

In conclusion, based on simulation findings, dual-policy combinations with synergistic effects should be prioritized in practical urban traffic management. Compared to single interventions, combination policies clearly offer greater practicality and regulatory effectiveness in supporting the achievement of urban passenger transport carbon reduction goals.

Conclusions

This study first defined the system structure and boundaries involved in the carbon reduction pathway of urban passenger transport. It was established that the system consists of five core subsystems: population and economy, energy consumption, urban road infrastructure, transport operations, and carbon emission control. On this basis, the internal logical relationships among these subsystems were mapped, and a causal loop diagram and corresponding stock-and-flow diagram were systematically constructed to provide the structural foundation for the model.

Following the principles of system dynamics modeling, the functional dependencies among key variables were clarified, leading to the development of a system dynamics model for simulating the carbon reduction pathway of urban passenger transport. To validate the model’s accuracy and applicability, historical data were employed for back-testing. The results demonstrated a high degree of fit, indicating that the model could reliably reflect the actual operational characteristics and evolutionary trends of the system.

Subsequently, the model was used to explore the influencing mechanisms and policy effects related to the carbon reduction pathway. Based on the trend of urban passenger transport emissions from 2010 to 2024, initial model conditions were set to support the simulation of single and combined policy scenarios. Key findings are summarized as follows:

(1) Baseline Scenario Analysis. In the baseline scenario, carbon emissions from urban passenger transportation exhibit varying trends across different modes of transportation. This step established a foundation for the subsequent policy scenarios.

(2) Single-Policy Impact Analysis. The simulation of three standalone policy types—public transport development, energy technology upgrades, and travel behavior guidance—demonstrated that each policy contributed to carbon reduction to varying extents. Among them, public transport and energy technology policies showed comparatively greater effectiveness.

(3) Dual-Policy Synergy Assessment. Further analysis of policy combinations revealed that dual-policy implementation resulted in significantly better emission reduction outcomes than individual policies. Specifically, the public transport + energy technology combination yielded the most substantial reduction, followed by the public transport + travel behavior combination, while the energy technology + travel behavior pairing had a comparatively weaker effect. These results indicate that policy synergies can generate multidimensional complementarities and additive effects, thereby enhancing the overall system efficiency for low-carbon governance.

In conclusion, the findings underscore that the carbon reduction pathway of urban passenger transport is influenced by multiple interwoven factors. Relying on a single policy strategy is insufficient to achieve optimal reduction outcomes. Therefore, future policy formulation and urban transport management should adopt an integrated approach, coordinating multiple policy instruments to drive a green and low-carbon transformation of the urban transport system.

Future research directions

Multi-scale and multi-system coupling studies: Dynamic coupling mechanisms between the urban passenger transport system and broader systems such as urban energy, land use planning, and regional economic models should be explored. For instance, the impacts of transport carbon reduction policies on the structure of urban electricity demand (i.e., affordable clean energy) or spatial planning equity (i.e., sustainable cities and communities) could be analyzed. Through this, the potential for cross-sectoral coordinated emission reductions and the identification of systemic risks may be revealed.

Refined simulation and equity assessment of policy portfolios: Building upon existing policy scenario simulations, the cost-benefit ratios and social equity impacts of different policy combinations should be quantified. Research should incorporate disparities in travel accessibility across income groups and regions (i.e., reducing inequalities), evaluating whether combinations such as “public transportation + behavioral guidance” can ensure the mobility rights of vulnerable populations while achieving emission reductions—thereby preventing the exacerbation of social stratification during the green transition.

Dynamic optimization of technological pathways and the impact of disruptive innovation: Model integration of emerging technologies—such as autonomous driving, shared mobility platforms, and hydrogen-based transport—should be further enhanced to simulate their large-scale penetration pathways under various policy environments. Special attention should be given to how technological leaps may affect infrastructure investment (i.e., industry innovation and infrastructure) and disrupt traditional transport sectors, in order to support the design of forward-looking policy frameworks.
