Spinal extradural arachnoid cysts (SEACs) are rare, CSF-filled lesions that arise from congenital or acquired defects in the spinal dura mater, leading to the herniation of arachnoid membrane and subsequent cyst formation.1 These cysts are typically solitary, sporadic, and located posteriorly in the spinal canal, while multiple cysts are extremely rare.2 These cysts most commonly occur in the thoracic spine and are often asymptomatic; however, when symptomatic, they present with progressive myelopathy due to spinal cord compression.3 The pathophysiology of SEACs remains incompletely understood, but proposed mechanisms include congenital dural weakness, trauma, or inflammatory processes.4

SEACs account for approximately 1% of all spinal space-occupying lesions, with a predilection for adolescents and young adults.5 Clinical manifestations vary depending on the location and size of the cyst but typically include back pain, radiculopathy, and progressive lower limb weakness or sensory deficits, with back pain and radiculopathy being the most commonly reported symptoms.1,2 MRI is the diagnostic modality of choice, revealing CSF-isointense lesions without contrast enhancement, often associated with spinal cord displacement or flattening with or without bone remodeling.2 Surgical intervention, including laminectomy or laminoplasty and dural defect repair with or without total cyst excision, is the definitive treatment for symptomatic cases and is associated with favorable neurological outcomes.3,5,6

Here, we present the case of a 13-year-old female with progressive myelopathy secondary to multiple thoracic SEACs, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, radiological and histopathological findings, and the role of timely surgical intervention in preventing irreversible neurological damage. This case underscores the importance of considering SEACs in the differential diagnosis of progressive spinal cord compression, particularly in pediatric populations.

Illustrative Case

A 13-year-old female presented with a 2-year history of progressive lower limb weakness, gait disturbance, and intermittent midthoracic back pain. Over the 6 months prior to admission, her symptoms worsened, manifesting as ascending numbness in both legs, frequent tripping due to foot drop, and new-onset urinary hesitancy. Neurological examination revealed spastic paraparesis with Medical Research Council grade 4/5 strength in the iliopsoas and tibialis anterior muscles, hyperreflexia in the patellar and Achilles tendons, sustained ankle clonus, and diminished vibration sensation up to the T6 dermatome. These findings localized the pathology to the midthoracic spinal cord, predominantly affecting the posterior columns.

MRI studies (Figs. 1 and 2) demonstrated multiple dorsally located cystic lesions extending from C7 to L2, causing significant spinal cord compression. The most severe compression was observed at T4–7, where the cord was flattened to < 2 mm in anteroposterior diameter and displaced anteriorly against the vertebral bodies. The cysts exhibited CSF signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, with no contrast enhancement or evidence of hemosiderin deposition. Given the progressive myelopathic symptoms and radiographic confirmation of cord compression, surgical intervention was indicated to prevent irreversible neurological damage. Intraoperatively, a T4–7 laminotomy was performed, revealing multiple epidural cysts compressing the thoracic cord. The largest cyst (4.5 cm in maximum diameter) (Fig. 3) was adherent to the dura; on dissection, a small dural defect was identified and primarily repaired using 6-0 Prolene sutures, followed by reinforcement with fibrin sealant. The repair was confirmed to be watertight with no evidence of CSF leakage on Valsalva maneuver. Subsequently, laminoplasty was performed, followed by standard layered closure. Histopathological examination of the excised cyst wall (Fig. 4) demonstrated hypocellular laminated fibrous tissue composed of parallel collagen bundles interspersed with flattened mesenchymal cells. The absence of ependymal lining, arachnoid granulations, or gliotic changes supported the diagnosis of a developmental meningeal cyst, with no features suggestive of a neoplastic or inflammatory process. Postoperatively, the patient showed significant neurological improvement over an 8-week rehabilitation period. Proximal lower limb strength recovered to 5−/5, with only mild residual weakness (4+/5) in foot dorsiflexors. Spasticity resolved sufficiently to discontinue baclofen, and sensory deficits normalized completely. Urodynamic studies confirmed restoration of normal bladder function. Follow-up MRI at 3 months demonstrated complete cyst resection and reexpansion of the spinal cord, although faint T2 hyperintensity at T5–6 indicated residual gliotic changes from chronic compression.

The residual small cysts in the cervical, lower thoracic, and lumbar spine demonstrated no interval change in size. Given the absence of radiological or clinical evidence of neurological compression or compromise, a conservative management approach (watchful waiting) was recommended. Additionally, the patient was referred to an immunology center for evaluation of potential underlying connective tissue disorders; however, diagnostic workup returned negative results.

Discussion

SEACs are rare but clinically significant lesions that can lead to progressive myelopathy, particularly in pediatric and young adult populations. The present case of a 13-year-old female with multiple thoracic SEACs underscores several key diagnostic, pathophysiological, and therapeutic considerations that align with and expand on the current literature.

Observations

SEACs are thought to arise from congenital or acquired dural defects, allowing arachnoid membrane herniation and subsequent cyst formation.1,2 The congenital theory is supported by the predominance of SEACs in younger patients, as in our case, while acquired cases may follow trauma, inflammation, or iatrogenic dural injury like lumbar tap or injection of spinal anesthetic materials or contrast dyes within the subarachnoid space.4,7 The absence of ependymal lining or inflammation in our patient’s histopathological findings favors a developmental origin, consistent with prior reports.5 Notably, the presence of multiple cysts—a rare finding—raises questions about underlying dural fragility or multifocal arachnoid outpouchings, as suggested by Hamamcioglu et al.8

The diagnosis of SEACs is complex due to their rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation, as illustrated by our current case. Patients often present with nonspecific symptoms such as back pain and lower limb numbness that mimic more common musculoskeletal or inflammatory disorders, leading to delayed diagnosis. The literature notes that 68% of SEAC cases initially experience diagnostic delays due to symptom overlap, with pediatric cases frequently enduring prolonged diagnostic delays exceeding 12 months.9,10 Although MRI is the imaging modality of choice, it has limitations: standard sequences may fail to reveal multifocal cyst septations or the dural defects communicating with the subarachnoid space, which are only detected intraoperatively in a significant proportion of cases.10,11 Advanced MRI sequences like FIESTA or CISS and CT myelography can improve the detection of these features but are not always definitive.9–11

Differential diagnosis is challenging, requiring exclusion of congenital cysts (neurenteric, dermoid), traumatic pseudomeningoceles, inflammatory arachnoiditis, degenerative synovial or Tarlov cysts, and neoplastic cystic lesions such as schwannomas or metastases.9,10 The multifocal presentation seen here is particularly rare, with fewer than 10 such cases reported since 2000, complicating clinical and radiological interpretation and mimicking other pathologies like transverse myelitis.12,13 This underscores the need for comprehensive whole-spine MRI and dynamic imaging techniques to identify occult dural defects and cyst communications. CT myelography, although less commonly used today, may demonstrate communication between the cyst and subarachnoid space, aiding surgical planning.14,15

Histopathological confirmation remains essential due to a documented 12% discordance rate between imaging and final diagnosis in cystic spinal lesions; in this case, the hypocellular fibrous cyst wall without ependymal lining excluded malignant mimics and supported the diagnosis of SEAC.10,11 Emerging diagnostic strategies such as 3D phase-contrast MRI to detect CSF flow through dural defects and multidisciplinary collaboration between neuroradiologists and spinal surgeons have been shown to reduce time to treatment and improve diagnostic accuracy.9,13 Collectively, these findings highlight the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for SEAC in progressive pediatric myelopathy and integrating advanced imaging, histopathological analysis, and coordinated clinical evaluation to overcome the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity.16,17

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention is guided by the progression of symptoms and radiological evidence of spinal cord compression.3 In the present case, the patient underwent laminotomy with cyst excision and primary dural repair—a well-established strategy associated with favorable neurological outcomes in 70%–90% of cases.5,6 Laminoplasty, performed concurrently, may further mitigate postoperative spinal instability, particularly in pediatric populations, by preserving posterior spinal elements.2 Intraoperative identification and meticulous repair of the dural defect are critical to prevent cyst recurrence, as emphasized by Brugières et al.18

Notably, the surgical approach may vary based on cyst characteristics. Funao et al.3 compared outcomes in 12 patients with SEACs and found no significant difference in neurological recovery between total cyst resection and simple dural defect closure; however, the latter was associated with reduced invasiveness and a lower risk of thoracolumbar kyphotic deformity. Conversely, Garg et al.19 highlighted that while extradural cysts (like those in our case) typically require excision and dural repair, intradural cysts may be managed with fenestration alone. The patient’s rapid postoperative improvement aligns with existing evidence that younger age and shorter symptom duration correlate with superior recovery.1,19

Follow-up MRI in our case revealed residual T2 hyperintensity at T5–6, reflecting chronic gliosis from prolonged compression. Similar findings were reported by Wenger et al., who noted that persistent signal changes do not always correlate with clinical deficits.20 Long-term surveillance is recommended, as delayed recurrence—although rare—has been documented.21

Novel Insights and Unanswered Questions

This case highlights the rarity of multifocal SEACs and their potential for rapid neurological decline. The role of genetic predisposition or connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan syndrome) in such cases warrants exploration. Additionally, advances in minimally invasive techniques (e.g., endoscopic cyst fenestration) may offer alternatives to open surgery, although evidence remains limited.

Lessons

SEACs, although rare, require high clinical suspicion in patients with progressive myelopathy. Early MRI diagnosis and surgical intervention are pivotal to preventing irreversible deficits. Our case reinforces the importance of histopathological confirmation, meticulous dural repair, and postoperative surveillance. Future research should focus on molecular mechanisms underlying cyst formation and optimizing surgical approaches for pediatric populations.
