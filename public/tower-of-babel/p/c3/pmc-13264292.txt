Introduction

Aging is a natural process encompassing both biological and psychological changes (Ashapkin et al., 2017). Older adults are defined as individuals aged 60 years or older (Fang et al., 2020). According to WHO estimates, 727 million older adults were living worldwide in 2020, a figure projected to exceed 1.5 billion by 2050 (Michel et al., 2021). Aging represents a critical phase in human life, making it essential to address the issues and challenges associated with this stage (Ashapkin et al., 2017). Due to declining birth rates, improved healthcare, increased life expectancy, and reduced mortality rates, aging has become a significant global phenomenon (Mensah et al., 2023).

Physiological and mechanical body functions decline during aging, increasing the prevalence of debilitating chronic physical and mental illnesses (Radak et al., 2019). Various studies have reported a high prevalence of suicide attempts in the older adults (Chan et al., 2011; Fu et al., 2024; Scocco and De Leo, 2002). Factors such as the loss of a spouse, friends, or peers, children leaving the family, proximity to death, and loss of physical abilities and social status contribute to stronger thoughts and actions related to suicide in older adults (Crestani et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Makara-Studzińska et al., 2021; Valera Ortín and Lucerón Lucas-Torres, 2021). Attempting suicide can have significant social, economic, and psychological consequences at the community level (Klonsky et al., 2016; Turecki et al., 2019).

According to WHO statistics, over 49,000 people died by suicide in 2023 (Organization, 2025). It is estimated that of every 100 instances of suicidal ideation, 20 result in suicide attempts (Stone et al., 2023). Suicide is often driven by despair, frequently attributed to mental disorders (depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and autism spectrum disorders), physical conditions (chronic fatigue syndrome), romantic failure, alcohol addiction, or substance abuse (Shanahan and Copeland, 2021). Stressful factors such as financial difficulties or interpersonal relationship problems also play a role (O'Connor et al., 2020). It is estimated that 10 to 20 million non-fatal suicide attempts occur annually (Stone et al., 2023). Suicide involves a multi-stage process, including suicidal ideation, planning, attempts, and actions to end one's life (O'Connor et al., 2020).

Studies have highlighted that suicide attempts differ from suicidal ideation (Klonsky et al., 2021; Klonsky et al., 2016; Weiss et al., 2022), as many thoughts of suicide do not result in actual attempts (Klonsky et al., 2021). Studies categorize non-fatal suicidal thoughts and behaviors into three types: suicidal ideation (thoughts of ending one's life); suicide plans (specific methods for dying); and suicide attempts (self-harmful actions with the intent to die) (Nock et al., 2008; Rens et al., 2023). Suicide attempts and suicidal behavior involve non-lethal actions intended to cause self-harm (De Leo et al., 2021).

Several primary studies have examined the prevalence of suicide attempts among older adults (Chan et al., 2011; Fu et al., 2024; Scocco and De Leo, 2002). However, these studies have primarily focused on limited geographical areas and small sample sizes. Additionally, none of these studies have evaluated the influence of potential factors such as the study year, sample size, age, and study quality. They have also failed to report the prevalence rates across different continents, given the shifting demographic structures worldwide. Significant discrepancies in reported prevalence rates further highlight the need for a comprehensive study.

Therefore, the present study is unique based on PICo (Population, Interest, Context) (P: older adults, I: Suicide attempt is of particular importance especially in the older adults, and C: Suicide attempt in the older adults leads to many economic, social, and psychological problems). To our knowledge, no such study has been conducted. Therefore, the present study aimed to estimate the global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults using a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

This study was carried out as a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis)PRISMA(2020 protocol (http://www.prisma-statement.org/), comprising the stages of identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion (Page et al., 2021). This study was registered with the Vice-Chancellor for Research and Technology at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences and received approval from its ethics committee.

Identification

To investigate the research question, "What is the global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults?" we searched several international databases, including Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WoS). Our search utilized the keywords “Epidemiology,” "Prevalence," "Aged," and “Suicide.” These keywords were verified using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase and were combined with ORAND operators. The search period extended from the inception of these databases up to February 2026, aiming to gather all potentially relevant studies.

Inclusion criteria

This study included all available full-text articles reporting the prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults. These articles encompassed primary studies and observational studies, with no restrictions on publication year, sample size, study location, or type of suicide attempt. In this study, participants aged 60 and older were considered older adult (Michel et al., 2021). Early studies measured suicide attempts in the older adults based on self-report measures.

Exclusion criteria

Articles not relevant to the aim of present study, interventional studies, conference presentations, and letters to the editor, qualitative studies, theses, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, animal studies, and duplicate articles from different databases were excluded. If the full text of an article was unavailable, we emailed the corresponding author three times. Articles without a response within a week were removed. Similarly, articles lacking sufficient data (specifically, the prevalence of suicide attempts in the older adult) were also excluded. Studies related to suicidal thoughts were excluded.

Study screening

Initially, one researcher (M.K.) applied the specified search strategy to identify relevant studies within the databases. All retrieved articles from various databases were organized using EndNote X9, from which duplicates were removed. Next, three researchers (M.K., M.G and A.J.) independently reviewed the articles to exclude those that did not meet the inclusion criteria or fell under the exclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, the matter was discussed with a third reviewer (E.E.) to reach a consensus. In the following stage, the titles and abstracts of the studies were carefully reviewed without considering details about the journal or author, leading to the exclusion of irrelevant studies. As noted, gray literature was also examined, as it played a vital role in ensuring the comprehensiveness of the research by allowing the inclusion of unpublished studies and other lesser-known research. This approach helped ensure that all relevant investigations were covered. Finally, all selected articles that met the inclusion criteria underwent a qualitative evaluation to determine their eligibility for inclusion in the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Qualitative evaluation

The qualitative evaluation of the studies included in the meta-analysis was performed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist (Munn et al., 2015). This checklist consists of nine questions that assess the sample frame, participant details, sample size, study subjects, the context of the research, data analysis, valid methods for identifying conditions, measurement accuracy, statistical analysis, and the adequacy of response rates. For scoring, a response of "Yes" was given if the criterion was met, "No" if it was not met, and "NA" if it was not reported. Scores ranged from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 9, reflecting compliance with the JBI criteria: 1–3 points indicate low quality, 4–6 points indicate medium quality, and 7–9 points indicate high quality (Rajati et al., 2022). Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using a pre-designed checklist comprising the following items; name of the first author, year of publication, country and continent where the study was conducted, sample size, age, study type, prevalence percentage, study quality.

Statistical analysis

The primary indicator examined in this study was the percentage of suicide attempts among older adults. To synthesize the results from various studies, we used the prevalence percentage or relative frequency reported in each study. We assessed heterogeneity among the studies using the I² statistic, where I² < 50% indicates low heterogeneity and I² > 50% indicates high heterogeneity. Due to the high heterogeneity observed (I² > 50%), we employed a random-effects model. This model accounts for variations across studies and offers greater generalizability under high heterogeneity conditions compared to the fixed-effects model. A random effects model, also called a variance components model, is a statistical model in which the model parameters are random variables. It is a type of hierarchical model that assumes that the data being analyzed are drawn from a hierarchy of different populations whose differences are related to that hierarchy (Yang et al., 2021). We conducted a sensitivity analysis to identify the source of this heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test and Egger's regression. Additionally, we conducted meta-regression analysis to explore the relationship between the global prevalence of suicide attempts among older adults and factors such as sample size, publication year, age, and JBI score. Subgroup analysis was performed by continent. All statistical analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (v.2), with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Summary of article inclusion in the meta-analysis

Using search strategies across various databases, a total of 10,785 studies were identified. Of these, 6215 duplicate and overlapping studies were removed in EndNote. From the remaining 4570 studies, 4447 articles were excluded after reviewing their titles and abstracts due to irrelevance to the study objective. Subsequently, out of the 123 remaining studies, 102 articles were excluded after full-text review for failure to meet the inclusion criteria or for meeting exclusion criteria (28 articles no primary outcome, 36 articles irrelevant studies, 18 articles study design, 13 articles overlapping data, and 7 articles language). As a result, 21 articles meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the meta-analysis. The flowchart of the PRISMA 2020 process is presented in Fig. 1 (Fig. 1).

PRISMA 2020 search flow diagram.

General characteristics of the studies

In this study, we focused exclusively on suicide attempts. We examined their frequency and ensured that the included studies exclusively documented suicide attempts.

The earliest study included in the analysis was conducted in 2000, while the most recent study was from 2024. The total sample size across the 21 studies included in the meta-analysis was 99,197 individuals. The largest sample size was reported by Chin et al., with 67,835 participants (De Leo et al., 2021), and the smallest by Shah et al., with 63 participants (Page et al., 2021). All studies included in the analysis were rated as having either medium or high quality according to the JBI checklist. The characteristics and data of the studies included in the systematic review and meta-analysis are presented in Table 1 (Table 1).

Meta-analysis of global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults

The I² index for the global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults indicated substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 98.62%). Therefore, the data were analyzed using the random effects model (Table 2). Based on the results of the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation and Egger's regression, no evidence of publication bias was observed at the 0.1 significance level (P Begg and Mazumdar = 0.879 and P Egger's regression = 0.532) (Fig. 2). After combining the results of the studies included in the meta-analysis, the estimated global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults was 5.6% (95% CI: 4.0–7.9%) based on the random effects model. The black square represents the percentage in each study, and the horizontal line extending from it shows the 95% confidence interval. The diamond shape indicates the global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults (Fig. 3). The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that removing any single study did not significantly alter the overall outcome (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The global prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults was estimated through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Based on data from the 21 articles included in the meta-analysis with a total sample size of over 99,000 individuals worldwide, the prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults was estimated at 5.6%. The highest prevalence was reported by Magagna et al. at 38.4% (Magagna et al., 2012), while the lowest was observed by Mokhtari at 0.21% (Mokhtari et al., 2019). The highest qualitative assessment score, based on the JBI checklist, was reported by Liao et al. equal to 3.1% (Liao et al., 2018).

The meta-analysis conducted by Dong et al. estimated the prevalence of suicide among older adults in China equal to 12% (Dong et al., 2014b). Key differences between Dong et al.'s study and the present meta-analysis include the number of studies included (11 articles vs. 21 articles), sample size (11,000 individuals vs. 99,000 individuals), study location (only studies in China vs. studies around the world), search period (up to 2014 vs. up to 2024), and the exclusion of databases such as Scopus and WoS in Dong et al.'s analysis. Furthermore, Dong et al.'s study did not examine the influence of potential factors such as publication year, sample size, age, and study quality.

The systematic review conducted by Simon et al. reported the prevalence of suicide among older adults in China between 2.2% and 41.2% (Simon et al., 2013). Differences between the findings of the present study and Simon et al.'s study include the number of articles included (8 articles vs. 21 articles), sample size (not reported vs. 99,000 individuals), study location (only studies in China vs. studies around the world), search period (up to 2013 vs. up to 2024), and the exclusion of Scopus and WoS databases in Simon et al.'s review. Additionally, Simon et al.'s study was a systematic review, which did not assess the impact of potential factors such as publication year, sample size, age, and study quality.

The population-based meta-analysis by Andriessen et al. estimated the prevalence of suicide attempts at 4.3% (Andriessen et al., 2017). Similarly, the meta-analysis by Liu et al. reported the prevalence of suicide attempts among children as 0.75 per million individuals (Liu et al., 2022). Comparing these studies with the present one reveals that the prevalence of suicide attempts in older adults is higher than in other age groups. Furthermore, the meta-regression results (Fig. 8) indicated a rising trend in suicide attempt prevalence with increase of age among older adults. Although suicide occurs across all age groups, the likelihood is higher in older adults compared to others. Significant contributing factors in this age group include depression, suicidal thoughts, isolation, loneliness, financial difficulties, chronic physical illnesses, and cognitive or physical disabilities (Liu et al., 2022). Research works have suggested that physical illnesses and fear of them, grief, feelings of rejection, guilt, chronic pain, hopelessness, helplessness, and particularly the loss of a spouse or child, are risk factors for suicide attempts among older adults (Liu et al., 2022; Makara-Studzińska et al., 2021).

In recent years, economic challenges and the outbreak of the Covid-19 pandemic in 2019 may have contributed to the rise in suicide attempts (Klonsky et al., 2021; Shekelle et al., 2024). The death of loved ones, feelings of loneliness, and various economic and social issues are significant risk factors for suicide attempts (Makara-Studzińska et al., 2021; Nie et al., 2020a). Furthermore, as people age, physiological and sometimes pathological changes can lead to physical, mental, and social problems. Social isolation, resulting from physical and mental difficulties, financial strain, the loss of friends and family, and a pervasive sense of loneliness, can affect the elderly (Sheperis et al., 2023). These factors often manifest with increasing age and may be associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors.

Given the variation in demographic structure, culture, race, genetics, economic and social conditions, stress, anxiety, depression, and medication access across countries and regions, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on continents. The highest percentage of suicide attempts among older adults was observed in Europe at 17.1%, while the lowest was in Australia at 1.1%. However, the very wide confidence interval (3.8–51.9%) in studies conducted in continental Europe, as well as the high level of heterogeneity (I2 = 98.55%), and the limited number of studies conducted in subgroups, make us accept the results of subgroup analysis with caution.

According to the meta-regression results, the prevalence of suicide attempts has shown an upward trend in recent years, potentially due to factors such as increasing economic challenges, loneliness, and chronic illnesses. This concerning trend highlights the urgent need for preventive measures for older adults, including identifying individuals with suicidal thoughts, preventing isolation and loneliness, promoting active aging, and implementing supportive interventions (Makara-Studzińska et al., 2021). Meta-regression analysis revealed that the prevalence of suicide among the elderly has increased over the years, as indicated by the publication dates of the studies. This trend may be attributed to several factors, including increased life expectancy and a growing elderly population, greater social isolation and single living, reduced support networks, a rise in chronic illnesses and physical disabilities, and in some countries, economic pressures and financial insecurity. Furthermore, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic's influence on this issue warrants further investigation.

The relatively high prevalence of suicide attempts among older adults, as identified in this systematic review and meta-analysis, underscores the need for further investigation and intervention regarding this social issue. Suicide is one of the most critical challenges in old age, leading to significant health consequences, including injury and increased economic costs. Thinking about death and attempting suicide have emotional, social, and economic consequences. For example, a person may be so preoccupied with these negative thoughts that they are unable to continue their daily lives. Many suicides occur suddenly and at critical moments and can leave the person with serious or permanent complications, such as organ failure or brain damage. Grief, anger, depression, and guilt are also common in those who have attempted suicide. Protective factors such as support from family and friends, religion, culture, moral beliefs, social participation, access to mental health services and care, and a satisfying social life can reduce the risk of suicide (Zhang et al., 2018b). Suicide attempts are more likely to occur in specific situations such as low socioeconomic status and education level, social stress, grief, mental disorders, feelings of hopelessness, physical and sexual abuse, chronic illnesses, pain, and access to suicide tools, which requires preventive measures (Zhang et al., 2017b). Suicide attempts in older adults can be mitigated by identifying at-risk individuals, providing appropriate education, implementing active aging programs, reducing substance use, alleviating chronic pain, and improving overall health and quality of life (Zeppegno et al., 2018).

This particular study focused exclusively on suicide attempts, meaning that other related behaviors or outcomes were not included in the analysis. When examining the data, the researchers carefully counted the number of suicide attempts that occurred. The studies included in this review specifically documented and recorded instances of suicide attempts. Our primary objective in undertaking this systematic review and meta-analysis was to establish how frequently suicide occurs within the elderly population. It's important to note that the existing studies we analyzed had a limited way of counting these events; they generally treated any number of suicide attempts, whether it was just one or as many as ten, as simply "suicide attempts" without further differentiation.

Some limitations of this research include the low number of studies conducted in the Americas and Australia, as well as the absence of studies from Africa. More studies with larger sample sizes are needed in countries on these continents to better understand the prevalence of suicide attempts among older adults in these regions. Other limitations of the present study include the potential inability to access all relevant articles and unpublished reports, as well as inconsistencies in the methodologies used across studies. Additionally, the significant heterogeneity observed in the studies (over 98%) necessitated a subgroup analysis based on continents, which slightly reduced heterogeneity but still showed high variability within subgroups. This may stem from differences in sample size, demographic characteristics, and study methodologies. However, due to the high heterogeneity between the studies, the results should be accepted with caution. Future systematic reviews and meta-analyses are recommended to address other social issues affecting older adults, as well as the prevalence of factors associated with suicide, while overcoming the limitations mentioned in the present study. It is also recommended that preliminary studies be conducted to examine the effects of factors such as economic conditions, educational status, and other related variables on suicide attempts, in order to better clarify their impact and enable more detailed subgroup analyses.

Conclusion

The findings of this study, taking into account its limitations, indicate a high prevalence of suicide attempts among the elderly, which has been increasing with age due to meta-regression in recent years. Given the high rate of suicide attempts, especially in Europe, and that attempts increase with age, doctors should regularly check older adults (65+) for suicide risk. This is especially important for those 75+, with long-term illnesses, or feeling lonely. A simple first check followed by a more thorough assessment can work well. Public health efforts should focus on reducing loneliness, providing support after hospital stays, and including mental health checks for chronic conditions. These steps offer practical ways to help older adults at risk.
