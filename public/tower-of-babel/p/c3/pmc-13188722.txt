Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) for severe cat bite infections with negative aerobic culture: a single-center retrospective study in a rabies vaccination center

Introduction

Cat bites account for 10%-15% of all animal bite injuries globally, with an infection rate significantly higher than that of dog bites [1]. Typical “small and deep” puncture wounds can quickly close to form an anaerobic microenvironment, which is conducive to the proliferation of anaerobic pathogens and easily leads to severe soft tissue infections or even osteomyelitis [2]. Pasteurella multocida, Bacteroides spp., and Fusobacterium spp. are the most common pathogenic bacteria in cat bite infections, but traditional aerobic bacterial culture can only detect aerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria, and the detection rate of strict anaerobic bacteria is extremely low, which is prone to false-negative results and thus delays targeted treatment [3]. Delayed diagnosis and inappropriate antibiotic use are the main causes of severe complications and medical disputes in cat bite infections [4].

Clinical diagnosis and management of cat bite infections adhere to international evidence-based recommendations, including the clinical practice guidelines for skin and soft tissue infections issued by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), which emphasize early standardized wound care, rational selection of antimicrobials covering anaerobic bacteria, and timely recognition of polymicrobial infection in severe cases [5].

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) technology can directly detect all nucleic acid sequences in clinical samples without prior culture and can quickly and accurately identify anaerobic bacteria, rare pathogenic bacteria and mixed infectious pathogens, providing a new technical means for the diagnosis of culture-negative infectious diseases [6]. This technology has been widely used in the diagnosis of severe infectious diseases in emergency departments, and its clinical application value has been confirmed [7]. Changping District Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine Hospital is the designated rabies vaccination and treatment center in Changping District, Beijing, receiving 30–40 animal bite patients daily with an annual volume of approximately 3,000 cases. In recent years, the proportion of cat bite infection patients with negative traditional aerobic bacterial cultures admitted to our emergency department has been relatively high, which brings great challenges to clinical diagnosis and treatment. To optimize the clinical diagnosis and treatment protocol of such infections, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 17 patients with cat bite infections (13 mild, 4 severe) and negative traditional aerobic cultures and explored their etiological characteristics and the potential diagnostic value of mNGS in a single tested severe case, thus providing preliminary practical guidance for the emergency management of cat bite infections in primary hospitals.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Clinical data of patients with cat bite infections admitted to the Emergency Department of our hospital from May 2025 to January 2026 were collected retrospectively.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Definite cat bite history with deep puncture wounds (depth > 0.5 cm); (2) Classified as mild or severe infection based on clinical and laboratory criteria: severe infection was defined as the presence of fever (body temperature ≥ 38.5 °C), severe local swelling, severe pain, limited activity, and significantly elevated infection indicators (white blood cell count > 12 × 10⁹/L or neutrophil ratio > 80%, C-reactive protein > 50 mg/L), in accordance with the IDSA clinical practice guidelines for skin and soft tissue infections; mild infection was defined as local swelling/pain without systemic symptoms and slightly elevated or normal infection indicators; (3) Negative wound aerobic bacterial culture results; (4) Having signed the Informed Consent Form for Diagnosis and Treatment of Dog and Cat Bite Infections (or equivalent risk communication completed if the case occurred before the implementation of the document); (5) Complete clinical data.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Complicated with infections in other sites; (2) History of immunodeficiency (such as diabetes, long-term use of immunosuppressants, etc.); (3) Having received standardized debridement and antibiotic treatment in other hospitals after being bitten.

Finally, 17 patients were included, including 7 males and 10 females; aged 22–65 years, with an average of 41.3 ± 10.5 years; 13 mild cases and 4 severe cases; bite sites: 15 cases in the hands (10 cases in fingers, 3 cases in palms, 2 cases in dorsum of hands) and 2 cases in the forearms; time from injury to treatment: 4–72 h, with an average of 28.6 ± 15.3 h.

Study methods

Data collection

Clinical data were collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including general demographic characteristics (gender, age), bite-related conditions (bite site, wound depth, time from injury to treatment), clinical manifestations, laboratory examinations (blood routine, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin), etiological test results (aerobic bacterial culture, mNGS), treatment protocols (debridement method, antibiotic use), and prognosis (treatment cycle, recovery status). All patients underwent 3 months of regular follow-up after discharge to assess the development of severe complications (e.g., osteomyelitis).

Etiological detection

All patients underwent aerobic bacterial culture of wound secretions or tissue samples after admission (using Columbia blood agar medium, incubated at 35 °C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h; bacterial identification was performed using the VITEK 2 Compact automated microbial identification system). Negative culture results were confirmed at 72 h.

For the 4 severe patients who showed no improvement after 3 days of conventional treatment (debridement + empirical antibiotic therapy), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was recommended for etiological detection. Only 1 patient consented to external testing at Beijing You’an Hospital after signing the informed consent form, while the other 3 patients declined mNGS testing due to economic constraints. mNGS library preparation, sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis were performed by the certified clinical laboratory of Beijing You’an Hospital affiliated with Capital Medical University. The entire workflow was completed within 24 h (detection cost: approximately 1500 RMB per case). mNGS was performed on wound secretion samples collected from the patient, following the standard clinical protocols of the certified laboratory at Beijing You’an Hospital.

Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were subjected to strict quality control to remove low-quality and adapter sequences using Trimmomatic (version 0.39). Human host sequences were removed by alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.2).

Clean reads were aligned to the NCBI RefSeq microbial database (release 220; bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites) for species annotation using Kraken2 (version 2.1.2). Microbial abundance was estimated using Bracken (version 2.6).

Pathogen identification was determined based on comprehensive criteria:

Treatment methods

All patients underwent emergency debridement immediately after admission: wounds were repeatedly irrigated with hydrogen peroxide combined with normal saline to remove necrotic tissue and foreign bodies; through-and-through drainage or direct drainage was selected according to wound conditions, and dressings were changed daily after surgery. Initial empirical antibiotic treatment consisted of intravenous infusion of cefoperazone (2 g, once every 12 h) combined with ornidazole (0.5 g, once every 12 h). For the 4 severe patients with no improvement after initial treatment, antibiotic regimens were adjusted with reference to the mNGS results to intravenous infusion of meropenem (1 g, once every 8 h) combined with metronidazole (0.5 g, once every 12 h). This empirical regimen of cefoperazone combined with ornidazole is the first-line therapy for skin and soft tissue infections in our hospital, based on local pathogen susceptibility data and clinical practice experience in primary medical institutions.

Results

Etiological detection results

All 17 patients had negative aerobic bacterial culture results after 72 h of incubation. In the single severe patient who underwent mNGS testing, multiple anaerobic or facultative anaerobic pathogens were identified (no viral, fungal or parasitic pathogens detected). Table 1 summarizes the mNGS-detected pathogens and their relative abundances in the single severe case, with clinical relevance descriptions based on published literature and clinical microbiological consensus. Treatment and prognosis

Among the 17 patients, 13 mild patients gradually improved after initial empirical antibiotic treatment, with an average treatment cycle of 18.7 ± 3.1 days, and all were cured and discharged without severe complications.

The initial empirical treatment was ineffective in all 4 severe patients; among them, 1 patient underwent mNGS testing and had antibiotic regimens adjusted according to the results, while the other 3 had antibiotic regimens adjusted with reference to the mNGS results of the tested patient. After adjustment, infections in all 4 severe patients were effectively controlled, with an average treatment cycle of (14.5 ± 2.3) days; the shorter treatment cycle in severe patients is an observational finding only, with no statistical analysis performed due to the small sample size. All severe patients were cured and discharged after treatment, and no severe complications (e.g., osteomyelitis, soft tissue necrosis) were observed during the 3-month follow-up period.

Discussion

Pathogenic characteristics of cat bite infections with negative aerobic culture

The etiological characteristics of cat bite infections are significantly specific and are closely related to the “small and deep” anatomical characteristics of cat bite wounds [8]. The puncture wounds caused by cat teeth are narrow and deep, which can quickly close after injury, leading to poor oxygen exchange in the wound and the formation of a stable anaerobic microenvironment, acting as an important condition for the proliferation of anaerobic bacteria [9]. In this study, aerobic culture was negative in all 17 cat bite infection cases; in the single severe patient tested by mNGS, multiple anaerobic and facultative anaerobic pathogens were identified, with Bacteroides pyogenes as the dominant species, which is consistent with the polymicrobial anaerobic infection characteristics of cat bite infections reported in previous studies [10].

Traditional aerobic bacterial culture is the most commonly used etiological detection method in primary hospitals, but it has obvious limitations in the diagnosis of cat bite infections [11]. On the one hand, aerobic culture cannot provide the anaerobic growth environment required for the survival of strict anaerobic bacteria, resulting in the failure to detect anaerobic pathogens; on the other hand, deep cat bite wounds are difficult to sample, and the collected samples are easily contaminated by superficial aerobic bacteria, which further reduces the detection rate of pathogenic bacteria. The 100% negative rate of aerobic culture in this study reflects the limited ability of conventional aerobic culture to detect pathogens in cat bite infections, which may lead to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed targeted treatment in clinical practice. Notably, anaerobic culture was not performed in this study due to the lack of relevant experimental conditions in our primary hospital, so a direct comparison of the diagnostic yield between aerobic culture and anaerobic culture cannot be made.

Potential clinical value of mNGS in severe culture-negative cat bite infections

mNGS technology breaks through the limitations of traditional culture-dependent detection methods and has shown potential value as a supplementary diagnostic tool for culture-negative infectious diseases [12]. This technology does not require prior culture and can directly sequence all nucleic acids in the sample, so it can detect all types of pathogens, including strict anaerobic bacteria, rare bacteria and fungi, at the same time and has the advantages of rapidity (24-hour result reporting, highly consistent with emergency rapid diagnosis demand), comprehensiveness and accuracy [13].

In this study, mNGS detected multiple anaerobic and facultative anaerobic pathogens in a single severe patient with negative aerobic culture, clarifying the polymicrobial infection spectrum of the patient and providing a clear basis for the adjustment of antibiotic regimens. The 4 severe patients all achieved good therapeutic effects after adjusting the antibiotic regimens with reference to the mNGS results, which suggests the potential clinical guiding value of mNGS for the management of severe culture-negative cat bite infections. However, it should be emphasized that only one patient underwent mNGS testing in this study due to economic factors of patients and the limited accessibility of mNGS in primary hospitals, so no definitive conclusions on the diagnostic value of mNGS can be drawn from these findings. For severe cat bite infection patients who are unresponsive to empirical antibiotic treatment, mNGS may help avoid the blind use of antibiotics and facilitate the implementation of precise treatment, but its clinical application still needs to be verified by large-sample studies.

Clinical management recommendations for cat bite infections in regional rabies vaccination and treatment centers

Based on the results of this study and the clinical experience of our hospital as a regional rabies vaccination and treatment center with an annual volume of 3000 animal bite cases [14], we propose preliminary clinical management recommendations for cat bite infections (limited to the clinical practice of primary hospitals): (1) Strengthen early standardized debridement: For all cat bite wounds, especially deep puncture wounds, thorough debridement should be performed within 24 h. The wound should be repeatedly irrigated with hydrogen peroxide combined with normal saline for at least 15 min to ensure the removal of necrotic tissue and foreign bodies, and multiple debridements may be necessary for severe wounds. (2) Optimize empirical antibiotic treatment: Due to the high negative rate of aerobic culture in cat bite infections, clinicians may consider prioritizing empirical antibiotics with anaerobic coverage, avoiding the sole use of agents targeting only aerobic bacteria. Based on local antimicrobial susceptibility data and clinical practice in primary care settings, cefoperazone combined with ornidazole or amoxicillin-clavulanate may be considered as first-line empirical options. (3) Rational application of mNGS testing: For severe cat bite infection patients with an inadequate response to empirical antibiotic therapy, mNGS testing may be considered as early as feasible if institutional conditions allow and with informed patient consent. Antibiotic regimens may then be adjusted in combination with mNGS results and the patient’s actual clinical status; standardized informed consent should be completed to strengthen physician–patient risk communication regarding the cost and clinical reference value of mNGS testing. (4) Establish a standardized referral and risk management system: Regional rabies vaccination and treatment centers should establish standardized referral procedures with primary medical institutions, strengthen the training of primary medical staff on the diagnosis and treatment of cat bite infections, and improve their ability to perform early debridement and risk assessment.

For patients with deep puncture wounds or delayed treatment after cat bite, close clinical monitoring is recommended, and timely referral is required if the condition worsens to reduce the risk of severe complications and medical disputes.

Study limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be fully acknowledged. First, this is a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size (17 patients in total). Only one patient underwent mNGS testing due to the lack of on-site mNGS equipment in our hospital, the long distance to the designated testing institution, and the relatively high detection cost, which may affect the generalizability of the results, and the conclusions need to be validated by multicenter, large-sample prospective studies. This limitation will be effectively addressed in our subsequent prospective randomized controlled studies with larger sample sizes (planned to include more than 100 cases), which will further explore the application value of mNGS in the etiological diagnosis of various surgical infectious diseases in primary hospitals. In addition, anaerobic culture was not performed in this study because our primary hospital does not have the experimental conditions for anaerobic culture, which limits the study’s ability to confirm that mNGS detects pathogens missed by conventional culture methods. Second, the cost-effectiveness of mNGS testing compared with traditional culture was not analyzed in this study, and the clinical application and promotion of mNGS in primary hospitals need further discussion due to the high detection cost. Third, no statistical analysis was performed on differences in treatment cycles due to the small sample size, and the observed shorter treatment cycle in severe patients cannot be attributed to the clinical effect of mNGS. Future collaboration with commercial mNGS platforms will help standardize detection procedures, expand sample sizes, reduce detection costs in primary hospitals, and lay a foundation for the accumulation of high-quality clinical data in subsequent related research.

Conclusion

In this small single-center retrospective study, aerobic culture was negative in all 17 cat bite infection cases (13 mild, 4 severe). In the single tested severe patient, mNGS identified multiple anaerobic and facultative anaerobic pathogens and facilitated targeted antibiotic adjustment, suggesting that mNGS may act as a potential supplementary diagnostic tool for severe culture-negative cat bite infections in emergency settings of primary hospitals. Given the very limited sample size, only one mNGS test performed, and the lack of anaerobic culture as a comparator, no broad conclusions or recommendations for the routine clinical use of mNGS can be made.

Regional rabies vaccination and treatment centers should establish standardized debridement procedures, optimize empirical antibiotic regimens covering anaerobic bacteria, strengthen physician–patient risk communication, and improve the standardized referral system to reduce the risk of severe complications and medical disputes in cat bite infections. Future multicenter, large-sample prospective studies with anaerobic culture as a comparator are needed to further verify the diagnostic value, clinical utility, and cost-effectiveness of mNGS for cat bite infections.
