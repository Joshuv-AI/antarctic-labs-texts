Modeling how penalty sequences influence decisive scoring in elite judo
Authors: Mei-Yin Su, Tzai-Hung Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Penalty sequences are a central component of tactical decision making in elite judo. Although prior research has examined the frequency and consequences of shido penalties, limited attention has been given to how an opponent’s shido relates to the timing of a decisive outcome. This study aimed to estimate the interval from an opponent’s shido to a subsequent ippon and to examine whether the covariates associated with this interval differed between the opponent’s first-shido and second-shido phases. Bout-level data from the Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 Olympic Games and the 2021–2024 World Judo Championships were analyzed using Weibull accelerated failure time models. The results revealed clear phase-dependent patterns. Following the opponent’s first shido, time to ippon was significantly prolonged by Golden Score entry and increased with the winner’s pre-penalty shido count, while ranking differential showed a marginal delaying effect and heavier weight categories tended to resolve faster. In contrast, after the opponent’s second shido, Golden Score remained the dominant predictor of longer time to ippon, and the winner’s pre-penalty shido count and weight category retained statistically significant associations, whereas ranking differential and other bout characteristics were not significant. Sensitivity analyses reinforced this asymmetry: the first-shido model exhibited broader dependence on contextual covariates, whereas the second-shido model was comparatively concentrated in a smaller set of drivers. These findings indicate that the correlates of shido-to-ippon timing differ across penalty phases, with later phases operating under more constrained structural conditions. The proposed phase-aware time-to-event framework provides a quantitative template for penalty-conditioned scoring tempo analysis in elite judo.

Introduction

Judo is a dynamic and multifaceted combat sport in which success depends on the coordinated interaction of technical execution, tactical adaptability, and psychological resilience. Like other rule-governed combat sports such as wrestling, boxing, and taekwondo, judo contests unfold through rapid alternations of initiative and are shaped by both scoring actions and officiated interventions. These structural features make judo a useful analytical setting for examining how rule-enforced states influence the timing and resolution of decisive outcomes.

The most definitive outcome in judo is the ippon, a full-point score that immediately ends the contest and reflects the successful execution of a throw, hold, or submission technique. Although ippon is produced through technical action, it emerges within a shifting tactical environment shaped by initiative changes, gripping exchanges, and the regulatory structure of the sport. Within this environment, penalties, particularly shido, serve as formal responses to behaviors such as non-combativity, false attacks, or grip avoidance. A growing body of research has shown that shido plays a decisive tactical role in elite judo: early penalties can place athletes at a disadvantage, disrupt offensive initiative, and force tactical adjustments, thereby influencing the progression and outcome of the bout1–5.

Despite this growing literature, relatively little attention has been given to the temporal relationship between penalties and decisive scoring events such as ippon. Existing studies have largely examined the frequency, tactical meaning, or outcome consequences of penalties, but have less often treated shido as the starting point of a time-dependent scoring process. This is an important limitation, because the tactical significance of a shido depends not only on its occurrence, but also on what happens afterward: whether the bout accelerates toward decisive closure, slows under increased regulatory pressure, or shifts into a more constrained pacing regime. Examining the interval from an opponent’s shido to a subsequent ippon therefore provides a more direct way to assess how penalty-defined states reorganize bout tempo and decision making under pressure6,7.

Ippon provides a particularly informative endpoint for this purpose. Unlike match termination through cumulative penalties or the expiration of regulation time, ippon reflects deliberate technical success and offers a clearer lens on the tactical mechanisms shaping bout resolution. Focusing on the time from an opponent’s shido to ippon allows the analysis to isolate how penalty-defined changes in advantage and momentum relate to the pace of decisive outcomes, while reducing confounding forms of procedural closure.

Therefore, this study had two specific goals: (1) to estimate the interval from an opponent’s shido to a subsequent ippon in elite judo, and (2) to determine whether the covariates associated with this interval differed between the opponent’s first-shido and second-shido phases. We hypothesized that shido functions as a state transition that reorganizes bout tempo rather than merely signaling aggressive or passive behavior, and that the observable correlates of shido-to-ippon timing would become fewer and more concentrated in the second-shido phase. We further hypothesized that entering the penalty-defined interval with a pre-shido waza-ari advantage would be associated with a shorter time to ippon.

Data and methods

Data source

Match-level data were obtained from the official Judo Results Books published by the International Judo Federation (IJF). To ensure a comprehensive analysis of elite-level tactical trends across the complete Olympic cycle, the dataset was expanded to include the 2020 Tokyo and 2024 Paris Olympic Games, as well as the World Judo Championships from 2021 to 2024. The compiled dataset contains athlete identifiers, world ranking points, gender, weight category, scoring outcomes, and precise penalty timelines. Mixed team event bouts were excluded because team-based formats involve tactical structures that differ from individual matches.

For the present analysis, analytic samples were defined using an inclusive hierarchical approach to avoid survivorship bias. Model 1 (First Shido Phase) included all bouts in which the opponent received at least one shido penalty (N = 1,797). The outcome time was defined as the interval from the first shido to ippon or censoring. Crucially, this sample includes bouts that may have subsequently progressed to a second or third penalty, thereby capturing the full spectrum of behaviors immediately following the first infraction. Model 2 (Second Shido Phase) analyzed the specific subset of bouts in which the opponent received at least two shido penalties (N = 1,061), with the outcome time defined as the interval from the second shido to ippon or censoring. This inclusive structure ensures that the first-phase analysis remains robust and representative of all penalized scenarios, correcting for the selection bias often present in mutually exclusive sampling designs.

Statistical analysis

A time-to-event modeling framework was used to examine the timing of ippon following an opponent’s penalty. Bout durations, particularly those that continue into Golden Score, tend to be right-skewed, so we compared log-normal, Weibull, and log-logistic Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) models using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The Weibull specification provided the lowest or comparable AIC values in both penalty phases, and the estimated coefficients were highly consistent across the three distributions. For this reason, the Weibull AFT model was selected as the primary analytical specification.

In the Weibull AFT model, the logarithm of the event time is expressed as a linear function of covariates:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{log}\left({T}_{i}\right)={x}_{i}^{T}\beta\:+{\sigma\:u}_{i}\:,$$\end{document}

where Ti is the time from the opponent’s shido penalty to the occurrence of ippon or censoring in bout i. The vector xi​ contains the covariates, β is the vector of regression coefficients, and ui​ follows a Type-I extreme value (Gumbel) distribution. The parameter σ represents the scale of the error distribution and determines the dispersion of event times on the log-time scale. The Weibull shape parameter is equal to 1/σ, so smaller values of σ correspond to a more sharply increasing hazard function. Under the parameterization, exp(β) represents a time ratio, with values below 1 indicating shorter time to ippon.

Definition of outcome and censoring

The outcome variable Ti was defined separately for the two analytical conditions:

Model 1: Ti represents the time from the opponent’s first shido to ippon. Bouts ending without ippon (for example, waza-ari accumulation) were treated as right-censored at bout termination.

Model 2: Ti represents the time from the opponent’s second shido to ippon. As above, non-ippon outcomes were coded as right-censored.

All models were estimated using maximum likelihood methods. Exponentiated coefficients, exp(β), were interpreted as time ratios, where values below 1 indicate shorter time to ippon.

Covariates

The primary independent variable was the Winner’s pre-penalty shido count, defined as the cumulative number of shido penalties (0, 1, or 2) held by the eventual winner immediately before the opponent’s phase-defining shido was issued. For Model 1, this value was measured at the time of the opponent’s first shido; for Model 2, it was measured at the time of the opponent’s second shido. This variable captures the winner’s disciplinary state at the start of the modeled interval and is interpreted as a bout-state correlate of subsequent shido-to-ippon timing, rather than a direct psychological measurement of “pressure.”

We incorporated a set of covariates to adjust for other bout-level characteristics. Ranking Differential (winner’s ranking minus the opponent’s) represented relative competitive ability. Gender and Weight Category were included to account for established differences in pacing and penalty patterns across divisions. To control for the expanded dataset structure, Event Type (Olympics vs. World Championships) was included to capture potential differences in officiating trends or competitive intensity. An interaction term between Winner’s Shido Count and Event Type was specified to examine whether the tactical impact of penalties varied significantly between these two major tournament levels.

Additionally, we controlled for Pre-shido Waza-ari, defined as a binary indicator of whether the eventual winner had scored a waza-ari prior to the onset of the penalty phase, to distinguish the effects of technical leads from penalty pressure. Finally, an indicator for Golden Score entry was included to account for the structural extension of bout duration inherent to overtime scenarios. Only a small proportion of shido-to-ippon intervals crossed from regulation time into Golden Score, so Golden Score operates as a bout-level characteristic of the interval rather than a within-interval shift. This fixed specification provides a clear and parsimonious adjustment for phase-related variation in pacing.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate model robustness and quantify the influence of each covariate, sensitivity analyses were conducted by refitting each AFT model after removing one covariate at a time and recording the corresponding change in AIC (ΔAIC) and the associated reduction in log-likelihood. Larger increases in AIC indicate greater contribution of the removed variable… ΔAIC < 2 was interpreted as negligible, whereas ΔAIC > 5 was considered substantial. Coefficient stability and changes in 95% confidence intervals were also examined across reduced models to assess the consistency of parameter estimates.

Results

Model adequacy was evaluated using Cox–Snell residuals and Q–Q plots under the Weibull AFT specification. As shown in Fig. 1, the Cox–Snell curves for both penalty phases followed the 45-degree reference line closely, indicating good overall fit. The Q–Q plots of standardized log-times (Fig. 2) further confirmed the suitability of the Weibull distribution, with empirical residuals aligning well with theoretical quantiles across the majority of the data range. These diagnostic patterns support the validity of the Weibull AFT model for capturing the time-dependent nature of ippon outcomes.

Table 1 presents the estimates for the first-shido phase (N = 1,797). The winner’s pre-penalty shido count was positively associated with time to ippon (Coef = 0.27, TR = 1.32, p < .001). This finding indicates that when the eventual winner entered the first-shido–defined interval already carrying more shidos, the subsequent shido-to-ippon interval tended to be longer. This pattern is consistent with risk-management pacing under elevated disqualification risk. Golden Score entry was the strongest predictor of extended duration (Coef = 0.80, TR = 2.23, p < .001). Ranking differential showed a small positive association with time to ippon (p = .024), suggesting slightly longer resolution when the winner was higher ranked. Weight category was significantly negative (p = .003), indicating that heavier divisions tended to resolve faster after the first shido. Pre-shido waza-ari, gender, event type, and the interaction between winner’s pre-penalty shido count and event type were not statistically significant in the full model.

Table 2 reports results for the second-shido phase (N = 1,061). Golden Score remained the dominant predictor of longer time to ippon (Coef = 0.81, TR = 2.24, p < .001). The winner’s pre-penalty shido count also remained significantly associated with longer shido-to-ippon intervals (Coef = 0.21, TR = 1.23, p = .004). This suggests that the winner’s disciplinary state at the start of the second-shido–defined interval remains informative of subsequent shido-to-ippon timing. Notably, in this phase, ranking differential is not statistically significant (p = .533), whereas weight category remains significant (p = .03), indicating that division-level dynamics continue to shape resolution speed even under higher-penalty conditions. Figures 3 and 4 visualize these phase-specific patterns by showing the estimated coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals for Models 1 and 2, respectively, highlighting the broader set of nonzero effects in the first-shido phase and the more concentrated profile in the second-shido phase.

Sensitivity analyses (Tables 3 and 4) further quantify the contrast between phases. For Model 1, removing Golden Score produced the largest deterioration in fit (ΔAIC = 162.6), followed by removing the winner’s pre-penalty shido count (ΔAIC = 60.49), with smaller but nontrivial contributions from weight category and ranking differential. For Model 2, Golden Score remained the principal driver (ΔAIC = 55.95), while the winner’s pre-penalty shido count and weight category contributed more modestly, and ranking differential exhibited negligible influence. Overall, the first-shido phase shows broader dependence on contextual covariates, whereas the second-shido phase is dominated by a smaller set of structural and bout-state correlates, especially Golden Score.

Discussion

This study had two specific goals: first, to estimate the interval from an opponent’s shido to a subsequent ippon in elite judo; and second, to determine whether the covariates associated with this interval differed between the opponent’s first-shido and second-shido phases. Using a phase-aware time-to-event framework, the findings support a clear phase-dependent pattern. Following the opponent’s first shido, shido-to-ippon timing was associated with a broader set of contextual correlates, whereas after the opponent’s second shido, the pattern became more concentrated and was dominated by a smaller set of bout-state and structural factors, most notably Golden Score. By anchoring event time to penalty occurrence, the analysis moves beyond static outcomes and provides a temporal perspective on how penalty-defined states relate to decisive scoring tempo3,8.

A key finding is the positive association between the winner’s pre-penalty shido count and longer shido-to-ippon intervals. As defined in the Methods section, this variable captures the winner’s disciplinary state at the start of the modeled interval and is informative of subsequent pacing and resolution timing1. A plausible interpretation is risk management under elevated disqualification risk, whereby athletes may reduce attack frequency to avoid further infractions2. However, alternative explanations such as officiating stringency or match-style heterogeneity cannot be ruled out in bout-level models. Accordingly, this effect is best interpreted as a robust bout-state correlate rather than definitive evidence of a one-way psychological mechanism.

Separating the first- and second-shido phases clarifies how tactical degrees of freedom compress as penalty pressure escalates. After the first shido, several covariates exhibit detectable associations with shido-to-ippon timing, including ranking differential and weight category, alongside Golden Score and the winner’s pre-penalty shido count. After the second shido, however, ranking differential is no longer statistically significant (p = .533), while weight category remains significant (p = .03), and Golden Score continues to exert the largest effect. The persistence of weight-related effects aligns with prior research indicating that division-level cost structures and time-motion characteristics fundamentally shape exchange dynamics9,10. Conceptually, the second-shido phase appears to function as a more constrained late-game environment in which structural conditions dominate. The pronounced influence of Golden Score in this phase reflects the increasing trend of matches extending into overtime in modern elite judo11.

Pre-shido waza-ari was not significantly associated with time to ippon in either phase. This finding is consistent with the possibility that a scoring lead does not reliably predict faster bout closure once a penalty has been issued, potentially reflecting strategic time management or lead protection behaviors2,12. However, as with the interpretation of the winner’s pre-penalty shido count, this should be framed cautiously: the absence of association does not identify a specific behavioral mechanism, but indicates that within the observed elite context, having a waza-ari advantage at the moment of a shido does not reliably predict the remaining time until an ippon occurs.

An important practical implication of the Phase 2 results is that the second-shido regime may function as a conditional “equalizer” for lower-ranked athletes. Once a bout progresses into a high-penalty state, status signals proxied by ranking differential lose explanatory power for shido-to-ippon timing, whereas structural conditions, particularly Golden Score, remain dominant. This suggests that, conditional on reaching Phase 2, the higher-ranked athlete’s advantage in controlling the timing window toward decisive resolution is structurally compressed9,11. For coaching and match planning, this reframes a viable underdog strategy: prioritize entering and surviving the late-game high-penalty state, where structural constraints, including the heightened likelihood of overtime, govern bout tempo more than pre-bout status advantage7.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis focuses on ippon as the event of interest, while other match-ending mechanisms were treated as right-censored. In judo, however, non-ippon endings (for example, wins by waza-ari accumulation, decision outcomes, or penalty-based disqualification) may be strategically and mechanically distinct from ippon pathways7. This raises the possibility of informative censoring and competing event processes, which can bias time-to-event estimates if the censoring mechanism is related to the same tactical factors that govern ippon timing. Future studies should consider competing-risks frameworks (cause-specific or subdistribution approaches) or multi-state event-history models that jointly represent transitions from penalty states to multiple terminal outcomes (ippon, non-ippon win, hansoku-make, and time expiration). Second, several explanatory variables would benefit from time-varying or state-dependent definitions. Penalty accumulation should be measured at or before the phase of interest to reduce post hoc contamination. Third, unobserved moment-to-moment determinants such as fatigue, grip dominance, refereeing tendencies, and micro-tactical sequences were not available in the bout-level dataset; integrating video-derived features or sensor-based workload proxies would substantially strengthen mechanism-level inference. Finally, the sample reflects the highest competitive tier; strategic responses to penalties may differ in lower-level competitions where risk management is less institutionalized.

Conclusion

This study addressed two specific goals. First, it showed that the interval from an opponent’s shido to a subsequent ippon can be meaningfully modeled as a time-dependent process in elite judo. Second, it demonstrated that the covariates associated with this interval differ across penalty phases: the first-shido phase is associated with a broader set of observable contextual factors, whereas the second-shido phase is characterized by a more constrained explanatory structure. These findings support interpreting shido as a state transition that reorganizes bout tempo rather than as a uniform signal of aggression or passivity. From a practical perspective, this phase-aware view suggests that performance analysis and training design should move beyond counting penalties and instead focus on decision making and pacing control under high-penalty conditions. Although future work incorporating alternative match-ending mechanisms and richer state representations would strengthen mechanism-level inference, the present study provides a transferable framework for penalty-conditioned tempo analysis in elite judo and other rule-governed combat sports.
