Validation of Orthopedic Surgery Residency Database for Use Within Plastic Surgery
Authors: Isabella Zorra, Tyler Miller, Allison Raymundo, Catherine Xu, Benjamin Smith, Freddy P. Jacome, Quinn Lawery, Christina Langone, Julia F. Corcoran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Matched plastic surgery (PS) residency applicants report higher mean numbers of abstracts, presentations, and publications than unmatched peers. However, the National Resident Matching Program aggregates these metrics, limiting insight into program-specific trends. Building on an orthopedic surgery model that uses Python scripts interfaced with the Elsevier Scopus Application Programming Interface, this study aims to create a comprehensive database of PS resident research output and demographics.Methods:Eighty-eight active integrated PS residency programs were identified via the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education. A total of 1170 residents (postgraduate years 1–6) were identified through official program websites and social media and validated with the National Resident Matching Program data. Resident demographics (degree type, sex, medical school) were verified using the Residency Explorer (RE) tool. A Python script was used to collect bibliometric data, including publication count, authorship role, journal name, impact factor, and citation count. Manual verification was followed to confirm authorship.Results:The script identified 38,226 publications, of which 14,385 were verified and attributed to 1055 residents. This methodology captured 90% of residents within multiple queries. Degree type data matched RE data in 80% of programs, and gender data matched RE data in 62% of programs.Conclusions:This study demonstrates the feasibility of using automated data mining and public sources to construct a robust, validated database of PS research output. The methodology offers scalability to other specialties and lays the groundwork for predictive tools to support applicants in selecting programs that align with their academic profiles, as well as guiding away-rotation and signaling decisions.

Takeaways

Question: How can plastic surgery residency applicants better understand program-specific research expectations and identify programs that align with their academic profiles?

Findings: Using automated data mining tools and manual validation, we created a comprehensive database of 1170 integrated plastic surgery residents across 88 programs (active from 2018 to 2023), capturing 14,385 verified publications. Our methods accurately identified 90% of resident research output and provided detailed bibliometric and demographic data.

Meaning: Applicants can use this validated database to identify programs that best match their research productivity and background, aiding more informed decisions regarding away rotations, program signaling, and residency applications.

INTRODUCTION

Background

Plastic surgery (PS) has increasingly become one of the most competitive specialties in medicine since the recognition of the integrated PS residency program pathway in 1995.1 This competitiveness is in part due to the increased number of applicants per available position in the residency match, with 1.64 applicants per residency position, second only to neurosurgery at 1.72 in the 2024 match.1–5 With this increase in competitiveness, PS residency applicants have been compelled to find ways to distinguish themselves from other applicants. One of these strategies is to increase research productivity, with many students choosing to complete a dedicated research year to increase their chances of matching.6,7 Along with other competitive specialties, including neurosurgery, dermatology, orthopedic surgery, and otolaryngology, PS residency applicants are known within medical education to have some of the highest research productivity in preparation for the match process.3 In all the aforementioned specialties, matched candidates had a higher mean number of abstracts, presentations, and publications among US doctor of medicine (MD) seniors than unmatched candidates.2,8–10 For PS, this average was 34.7 in matched candidates versus 26.3 in unmatched candidates.2 This only fuels the desire for students to enhance their research productivity, termed the “research arms race” as a means to better their odds of matching and make their application stand out among the many other stellar candidates in their residency match cohort.6,11

With the recent conversion of the United States Medical Licensing Examination Step 1 from a numerical score to pass/fail in 2022, there has been renewed interest in the role that research productivity plays in the residency match process. Previously, Step 1 was a major factor in gauging the competitiveness of a PS applicant.8,12,13 Without this metric to differentiate applicants, program directors have increasingly moved to placing increased emphasis on other aspects of the application, such as United States Medical Licensing Examination Step 2 scores, letters of recommendation, and research. A recent study by Boyd et al14 shows that increased research involvement, away rotations, and letters of recommendation, are the top 3 most important factors that program directors now evaluate after the conversion of Step 1 to pass/fail.14 From the applicant side, there is even more pressure to strengthen their research engagement and to consider a dedicated research year than in years prior.15

PS is not the only specialty affected by the changing landscape of the match process. Other comparably competitive specialties, such as neurosurgery, dermatology, orthopedic surgery, and otolaryngology, have undergone similar transitions. Our colleagues in orthopedic surgery have produced an article titled “Where Do Gender Disparities in Research Productivity Begin? A Study of Orthopedic Surgery Applicants and Residents” to further detail research productivity and matching in orthopedic surgery.16 The research methodology in this study was quite comprehensive, compiling data on 2723 orthopedic surgery residents and 17,695 publications to create a comprehensive database of matched orthopedic surgery residents.

Given the similarity between the PS and orthopedic surgery residency application process, our aim in the current study was to produce a similar database for the PS residency cohort from 2018 to 2023. We also aimed to validate the collection process used by our orthopedic surgery colleagues, including accessing residency website and social media data as well as the process for data mining publication data using their novel Python script.16–18 Similar Python-based tools have been developed for use in the fields of scientometrics and bibliometrics to assist in process automation and decreasing the risk of errors inherent in more manual processes.19,20 Although traditionally used in the field of informatics, these Python tools have seldom been used within the field of medical residency applications to assist applicants and programs to determine trends for matched candidates.21 Our novel automated Python tool enabled the development of an extensive database, including publication data, cumulative and chronological data that allow for year-over-year comparisons, Alpha Omega Alpha status, medical school, sex, and degree type (international medical graduate [IMG], doctor of osteopathic medicine [DO], doctor of medicine [MD], MD/doctor of philosophy [PhD]) for PS residents between 2018 and 2023.

This database will help PS residency applicants as well as PS programs and residency directors navigate the evolving match process and allow applicants to apply to programs with greater confidence. We also intend for this database to assist in creating a decision-making tool and algorithm, found at www.residencyhub.org, to aid aspiring plastic surgeons in their quest for matching into the specialty.22

METHODS

Program and Resident Data Collection

A list of the 88 integrated PS residency programs accredited during our study period between 2018 and 2023 was compiled using the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education website.23 The University of Nebraska was excluded, as its integrated residency program was not active during the study period. Two former programs, Case Western Reserve University and the University of Tennessee Health Science Center, were excluded due to loss of accreditation in 2021.24,25 The program list was validated against the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) databases for accuracy.26,27

A comprehensive list of current PS residents from postgraduate years (PGYs) 1–6 was obtained from publicly available residency program websites and official social media pages. Data collection occurred between June and August 2024, capturing residents who entered training between 2018 and 2023. Resident program data validation was performed by comparing the total number of residents per program in our database to NRMP match data from 2018 to 2023.26,28–32

Other resident data collected included medical degree type, sex, and medical school attended. Degree types were classified as MD for graduates of US allopathic medical schools, MD/PhD for those who also completed a PhD, DO for graduates of US osteopathic medical schools, and IMG for those who obtained their medical degree outside the United States. Degree type and gender data were validated using the Residency Explorer (RE) website, which provides aggregate program data from 2016 to 2024.33

Bibliometric Data Collection

Resident publication data were systematically collected using the Elsevier Scopus Application Programming Interfaces (APIs), a tool designed to streamline text and data mining processes located within the Elsevier Developer Portal, providing noncommercial users free access to text data. This tool is designed to be used with programming languages such as Python, JavaScript Object Notation, and Extensible Markup Language. We used the novel Python script developed by our orthopedic surgery colleagues to query data for our PS database using the Abstract Retrieval, Scopus Search, and Author Search APIs.34 This allowed us to automate the generation of citation data, metadata, and abstract data that were exported as a Comma-Separated Values file for our use.

The initial search with our Python script used a combination of resident name, medical school, and residency program. If no results were found, a second query was performed to search all PS-related publications between 2011 and 2024. Residents with no results after these queries underwent manual verification using sources such as ResearchGate, PubMed, and LinkedIn. If no publications were found after these steps, residents were classified as having zero publications. Of note, NRMP bibliometric data could not be used for validation of our publication data because, at this time, the data aggregate abstracts, posters, and publications into published research values.26

The data we extracted included the total number of publications per resident and individual H-index scores. Additional metadata collected included source type (eg, journals, books, letters), citation counts, journal name, and impact factor. Authorship details, such as first authorship status, total number of authors, author order, and author affiliations, were also recorded.

Once publications were identified, they were manually screened to ensure they belonged to the correct resident. Screening criteria included matching the resident’s medical school name, years attended, publication date, journal name, and research subject matter to confirm authorship. Publications that did not align with these parameters were excluded. The final step in data collection involved manually reviewing and removing any duplicate erratum listings to ensure accuracy in publication counts and metadata.

To distinguish preresidency from in-residency publications, a cutoff of September 1 in the first year of residency was used, under the assumption that most research conducted in medical school would have been completed before this date. Another Python script was used to manually calculate preresidency H-index by filtering out all publications from September 1 of the residency start year onward.

RESULTS

To compare our degree type data with RE data, MD and MD/PhD residents were combined and compared against the number of MDs reported in RE, whereas our total number of IMGs was compared against RE’s combined US-citizen and non-US-citizen IMG categories. Of the 88 programs in our study, 60 had RE data available for comparison. Our degree distribution (MD, DO, IMG) exactly matched RE data for 48 programs, was within 1% for 5 programs, and differed by 2%–9% for 7 programs. For gender identification, our database matched RE data exactly for 37 of the 60 programs available and differed by 1%–5% for 14 programs, by 6%–10% for 7 programs, and by 11%–15% for 2 programs. Notably, none of the 60 programs with RE data reported residents identifying as “another gender,” though that category did exist as an option.

Our final dataset contained 1170 residents, excluding 6 students on dedicated research years, whereas the NRMP match lists from 2018 to 2023 included 1108 residents. Our initial query of all 1170 residents in our database using our data mining Python tool returned 28,039 publications with bibliometric data attributed to individuals with the same names as the residents in our database. Our preliminary screening results yielded bibliometric data on 1001 residents as having produced 13,965 publications. The remaining 169 residents underwent subsequent queries, which yielded 10,227 publications that were screened, with 489 attributed to 54 residents. In total, our queries yielded 38,266 publications that were screened and attributed to 1055 residents who produced 14,385 publications once duplicates and errata were removed. We were unable to find publication data for 115 residents in our database (Figs. 1–4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we validated the data collection methodology used by our orthopedic surgery colleagues by applying similar techniques to PS residency programs. By combining publicly available program websites and social media data with automated data mining tools such as Python and Elsevier’s API, we successfully constructed a comprehensive database of PS resident research output and quality.

Our validation process identified a discrepancy of 56 additional residents in our database compared with NRMP-published data, with our dataset containing 1170 residents. Several plausible factors could explain this difference. First, PS residents commonly take optional or mandated research years during training. A 2022 study found that 14% of PS residents take at least 1 dedicated research year.35 Residents who take time away for research would delay their clinical progression, resulting in them appearing on rosters for subsequent training years rather than aligning neatly with their original match cohort. Thus, residents who matched between 2018 and 2023 but then paused clinical duties temporarily would inflate the apparent number of active residents relative to NRMP match data.

Second, new program accreditation and expansion of existing residency programs during the past 6 years play a significant role.36,37 Between 2018 and 2023, the number of integrated PS positions increased from 168 to 207, a 23% rise, whereas the number of accredited programs grew by a net of 11, with 13 new programs and 2 closures.4 Additionally, as programs expand, they may add residency positions outside the match or backfill PGY-2 or PGY-3 positions with transfers from other surgical specialties, including general surgery, orthopedic surgery, and otolaryngology.38 As programs gain new residency slots or add positions in response to workforce demands or institutional funding changes, resident cohorts would increase in size beyond what was initially reflected in match reports. These changes would not retroactively adjust NRMP match data but would be captured in our current database of active trainees.

Third, attrition, although low, must be considered. A study examining integrated PS programs reported an attrition rate of approximately 0.85%.38 In cases where residents leave and programs subsequently refill vacant positions with off-cycle entrants or outside transfers, the composition of the resident body would no longer match the original NRMP records. Finally, minor discrepancies could arise from the dynamic nature of program websites and social media pages, which may occasionally lag behind real-time changes in resident status or fail to immediately reflect leaves of absence, dual-degree pursuits (such as residents concurrently pursuing MBAs or MPHs), or unanticipated interruptions such as medical leaves.

Importantly, our PGY-1 cohort size exactly matched NRMP data, validating the accuracy of our methods at the entry point into residency. The discrepancies primarily appeared in more senior PGY levels, consistent with expected fluctuations over the duration of training.

Our analysis of degree type and gender data also revealed some variation when compared with the RE database. This discrepancy is likely attributable in part to differences in the observation window, as our dataset focused on residents between 2018 and 2023, whereas RE aggregates data from 2016 to 2024 during the period from which the data were retrieved. Additionally, our dataset includes a broader, more real-time pool of residents, capturing those currently in training, including residents in backfilled or off-cycle positions, whereas RE relies on static, aggregated institutional reporting. Interestingly, we found that across the programs for which RE data were available, our dataset showed 6% fewer female residents. This finding is surprising given national trends. Over the last decade, women have comprised an increasingly greater proportion of PS applicants, and now there are nearly equal numbers of female and male residents.36,37 One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that backfilled or transfer positions filled outside the typical match process may disproportionately go to male applicants, particularly those transferring from traditionally male-dominated specialties such as general surgery or orthopedic surgery.39 This could skew the gender distribution in our observed dataset. Despite this, our degree-type data remained highly consistent with RE, matching in 80% of programs. This suggests that degree type has remained relatively stable among matched applicants over time, whereas gender representation may be subject to greater fluctuation due to evolving applicant trends and differential access to nontraditional entry pathways.

Overall, although minor discrepancies were expected given the fluid nature of residency training pathways, our findings strongly support the validity and comprehensiveness of our data collection methods. We are confident that our data collection methods have yielded the most comprehensive database of PS residents and their publication records to date. This dataset has the potential to serve as a valuable resource for future applicants by informing an algorithm or machine learning model designed to identify applicant characteristics associated with successful matches at various integrated programs. This tool could be particularly beneficial for applicants without access to strong PS mentorship at their home institution or for those seeking guidance on program signaling in the match process. By providing insights into the characteristics of successfully matched applicants, this database may help prospective residents make more informed decisions about where to apply and how to strengthen their applications.

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights into the characteristics of PS residency programs and their residents, several limitations must be considered. One important limitation is that the data collection process relied heavily on publicly available program websites and official social media pages, which may not always present a complete, current, or fully accurate representation of resident rosters, demographics, or research output. Programs vary widely in how often they update their online information, and discrepancies between actual and posted resident lists could introduce minor inaccuracies into our database.

Another consideration is the inherent limitation of bibliometric data obtained through the Elsevier Scopus API. Scopus may not index all relevant journals, particularly smaller specialty journals or newer open-access publications. As a result, some resident publications may have been missed, leading to an underestimation of research productivity. Conversely, despite rigorous manual screening, there remains a small possibility of misattributing publications to the wrong individual, particularly for residents with common names or incomplete online profiles.

In addition, although we validated resident demographics (sex and degree type) against the RE database, differences in observation windows between our dataset (2018–2023) and RE’s aggregation (2016–2024) at the time of retrieval introduce potential comparability challenges. Residents’ self-identification of sex and degree pathways may also evolve over time and may not always be fully captured in publicly accessible records.

We also acknowledge that dynamic factors influencing residency rosters such as research year deferrals, lateral transfers from other specialties, program expansions, leaves of absence, and attrition—could not be systematically quantified in our analysis. Although we have qualitatively discussed their impact in our discussion, precise tracking of these events across programs would require a level of internal program data access that is beyond the scope of our methodology.

Finally, our study focused exclusively on integrated PS residency programs. Independent pathway residents, who enter PS training after completing general surgery or another prerequisite residency, were not included. Thus, the database and tools developed from this study are specifically tailored to applicants pursuing the integrated match.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates the feasibility and validity of using publicly available program websites and social media platforms, combined with automated data mining tools, to construct the most comprehensive and real-time database of integrated PS resident research output to date. By adapting and validating a methodology previously applied in orthopedic surgery, we successfully captured bibliometric and demographic data across all PGY levels, both before and during residency training. Discrepancies between our resident counts and NRMP match data can be explained by factors such as research year deferrals, lateral transfers from other specialties, and postmatch cohort expansion, elements not reflected in static national datasets but captured through our real-time approach.

In addition to validating this methodology, our database offers meaningful insight into publication trends among successfully matched applicants and reveals important patterns in gender representation and degree type across programs. Interestingly, we found that 10% of matched applicants between 2018 and 2023 did not have verifiable publications.

This validated dataset now serves as the foundation for a decision-making tool and algorithm we are developing, which may be accessed at www.residencyhub.org, to assist future applicants, particularly those without access to strong home program mentorship, in identifying programs aligned with their academic and research profiles.22 Though our tool is geared toward demystifying the residency match process for applicants, this resource also has applications for programs and residency directors by promoting greater transparency and equity and informing future policy, recruitment, and diversity efforts across the specialty.

Looking forward, this approach has broad scalability, with potential applications in other competitive specialties such as dermatology, neurosurgery, and otolaryngology. Future iterations of this work will focus on refining predictive models with additional applicant-level data and further developing decision-support tools for both applicants and training programs navigating an increasingly data-driven residency landscape.
