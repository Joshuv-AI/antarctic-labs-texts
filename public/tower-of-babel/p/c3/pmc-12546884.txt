Weight loss remains a challenge for idiopathic intracranial hypertension patients: A retrospective case series from Kenya
Authors: Zian Muikamba, Jaskirat Sokhi, Jasmit Shah, Sheila Marco, Juzar Hooker, Dilraj Sokhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a neurological condition characterized by elevated intracranial CSF pressure without demonstratable evident causes. The mechanisms leading to a particular predilection for women of childbearing age who are overweight and obese or who have recent rapid weight changes remain imprecisely described. If undiagnosed, it can progress to disabling irreversible visual loss. Weight loss is useful in addition to medical therapy in treating the condition. There is a rise of obesity in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) but there is little published on IIH.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study at our tertiary regional referral neurology centre in Nairobi, Kenya. Data were extracted from medical files of patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for IIH aged >18 years who were diagnosed or managed at AKUHN from January 2011 to December 2022.ResultsWe identified 152 patients: female-to-male ratio was 9:1, mean age 34.2 years. Mean body mass index (BMI) was 31.2 kgs/m2. Almost a third (32.2 %) had compromised vision at presentation in at least one eye. None had other medical comorbidities to precipitate IIH. H2O. 80.2 % had brain MRI, of which 78.4 % had ancillary IIH findings on imaging. Median cerebrospinal fluid opening pressure was 31 cm. Treatment was acetazolamide alone in 44 %, topiramate alone in 15 %, and combined in 30 %. 9.8 % were referred for neurosurgical evaluation, mainly due to refractory symptoms or intolerance/contraindications to medical therapy. Only 49.3 % had symptom improvement at one year. 52.6 % did not have a formal documented weight loss plan. Mean BMI at one year was 30.9 kg/m2.ConclusionOur study describes the first cohort of IIH patients from the East Africa region. Demographics are concordant with those reported internationally. However, our patients failed to lose weight, and emphasizes the need to formally incorporate weight loss plans (diet and exercise) in IIH care.Keywords: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a rare headache and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics disorder, diagnosed using the modified Dandy criteria which incorporates symptoms, signs of raised intracranial pressure, absence of localizing neurological findings on clinical examination, CSF pressure and exclusion of secondary causes of headaches [1,2]. The disorder disproportionately affects obese women, in their childbearing years, and has increased in prevalence in Western countries due to the increased prevalence of obesity [3]. Delayed diagnosis and treatment can lead to irreversible visual loss. Long term recurring headaches leads to reduced quality of life, with loss of economic and social productivity [4]. Firstline medical therapy is acetazolamide, and regular follow up is essential to for evaluation of reduction in papilledema and progress in weight loss [1]. Those with contraindications, or who have refractory disease, to medical therapy may require CSF diversion interventions including shunting and optic nerve sheath fenestration [5].

There is very little published data on IIH from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Studies looking at headaches or general neurological conditions from North [6], West [7] and East [8] Africa total less than 20 patients, and mostly show outcomes of compromised vision due to delayed diagnosis. At our regional tertiary neurology referral centre with internationally accredited diagnostic facilities, we have observed a constant stream of IIH over the years. To bridge the knowledge gap of IIH in SSA, we sought to describe the cases referred to us from within the East Africa region.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of medical charts for patients diagnosed with IIH above the age of 18 years, presenting to our hospital between 1st January 2011 to 31st December 2022. We applied the modified Dandy criteria as the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

All eligible patient files from the study period were retrieved and reviewed. A total of 152 files met the inclusion criteria and data extracted from physical and electronically stored medical records. We used a standard data abstraction tool on REDCap® to capture demographic details (age, weight, and height), relevant past medical history, recent or current medication use, presenting symptoms, baseline ophthalmological and neuroimaging findings, lumbar puncture opening pressures and cerebrospinal fluid analysis results, investigations for secondary causes, and follow-up records. No patient identifiers were captured.

Ethical considerations

The project was approved by the Aga Khan University Institutional Ethics Review Committee (Ref: 2022/ISERC-56) and the National Committee for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI, Ref No: 313854).

Data analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages whereas continuous data were presented as median, mean and standard deviations. Continuous data were evaluated with general normality tests.

Discussion

Our cohort's demographic and phenotypic profile is very similar to that described in the literature [9,10]. However, interestingly, over half of the total cases occurred after the onset of the COVID19 pandemic in 2020, which could be due to the early lockdown in the country causing increasing rates of obesity [11]. There was a small proportion of IIH patients with asymmetric or no papilloedema, which have been described as part of an expanding spectrum of the disease [10,12].

Comprehensive neuro-ophthalmology assessments in terms of visual acuity, papilloedema grading, colour vision measurement and formal visual field testing at baseline were altogether suboptimal, (between 24.3 and 64.5 % of patients did not have these done). Well-defined pathways, increased awareness amongst healthcare workers and specialised headache centres have been proposed to address shortcomings and variability in assessing IIH patients [13]. However, unstructured eye care health systems in SSA [14], a lack of specialised personnel including neuro-ophthalmologists (there are none in Kenya) and relative unavailability of diagnostic equipment [15] would make such aspirations difficult in resource-limited settings such as ours.

In terms of investigations, almost all patients (87.5 %) had appropriate neuro-imaging with the vast majority being MRI scans in keeping with guidelines [5]. A sixth (16.6 %) of patients did not have records of CSF opening pressures (OP), most for which we could not find the paper records to confirm. We still included them in the study as they fulfilled all other criteria for IIH, and were managed as such in the follow-up clinics. There has been debate on whether CSF OP are absolutely necessary to make the diagnosis [16], and in our settings patients in general are usually reluctant to undergo LP [17]. About a tenth (10.4 %) had OP <20 cm H2O; LP procedures are very operator-dependent and therefore opening pressures can be variable and sometimes unreliable [18]; there is some suggestion as well the IIH can exist with normal CSF OP [19] and therefore we continued to include these patients in our study.

In about a fifth of patients (20.4 %) there were new co-morbidities identified as potential secondary causes for IIH. We found more cases of dysthyroid states and positive connective tissue disorder tests when compared to the literature for secondary IIH, where hypertension, anaemia, chronic kidney disease [20] and polycystic ovarian syndrome [21] are more frequently documented.

There were some challenges faced with treatment in our cohort. Whilst the majority were appropriately treated with acetazolamide [2], with or without other migraine preventatives such as topiramate, a proportion of patients (15.1 %) were on topiramate monotherapy alone, and therefore undertreated as topiramate is no longer deemed first-line treatment [5,22]. The biggest challenge, however, was weight loss for our cohort. Over half of the patients did not get referred to formal weight loss strategies and/or exercise programmes, and the median BMI hardly shifted at last follow-up (median 9.1 months), even if the treatment regimen included topiramate, which can cause weight loss. Weight loss is a crucial factor in curing patients of IIH, and guidelines suggest realistic targets of 1-2 kg weight loss per month, with a target of losing 15–20 % of body mass over 6–12 months [3]. Challenges with weight loss in IIH have been documented and are multi-factorial, including facilitation by the healthcare provider [23], and may also relevant to our cohort but more research would be required to ascertain these barriers relevant to our setting. None of our patients were referred for the more recently indicated weight-loss interventions for IIH such as bariatric surgery [24] and glucagon-like-peptide-1 receptor agonists [25], which could be viable options for the future if made more accessible.

Over a third of the cohort [38.2 % (58/152)] were lost to follow-up, which is not unusual in our setting or indeed our own hospital's wider outpatient services. Those who did attend follow-up clinics benefited in terms of improved headache control, improvement in their WHO visual impairment classification (16.8 %) and their papilloedema grade (39.8 %). Some patients continued with headache despite polytherapy with migraine preventative measures, which is known to occur in IIH patients [5] and there is an expanding role of calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibody therapy in these cases, which none of our patients were on.

Our study had several limitations. It was a hospital-based study with reliance on paper hospital records, all of which invoke selection, recall and misclassification bias. As a retrospective study we cannot define causality or incidence; prospective community-based studies would address these limitations.

Conclusion

Our study is the largest cohort of IIH described from the region, and highlights a potential growing problem as obesity rises in SSA. Despite our resource limitations, as a regional referral centre our patients underwent reasonable clinical, laboratory and radiological assessments which revealed some interesting results that could be highlighting nuances of the disease in our setting. We have described infrastructural challenges throughout the patient care pathway, which cut across those we have previously described in other neurological diseases [26,27]. Our study provides opportunity to reflect on how to address these challenges in both a broader sense – systems of healthcare, including perhaps advocating for domestic IIH clinical care pathway which includes formal weight loss programmes – as well as specific to IIH, such as opening up access to more novel therapies to improve our patients' care in the region.
