Salivary glucose, salivary interleukin-18, glycemic control, and periodontal status in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Suteera Techatanawat, Pirayaporn Puangmaliwan, Bhornsawan Thanathornwong, Kongthawat Chairatvit, Bishwa Prakash Bhattarai, Weerapan Khovidhunkit, Siribang-on Piboonniyom Khovidhunkit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the relationships among salivary glucose, IL-18, glycemic control, and periodontal status in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Seventy-five individuals were categorized according to glycemic status: systemically healthy (controls, n = 25), T2DM with controlled glycemic status (CDM, HbA1C ≤ 7%, n = 21), and T2DM with uncontrolled glycemic status (UCDM, HbA1C > 7%, n = 29). Periodontal parameters, including periodontal probing depth (PPD) were recorded. Unstimulated whole saliva samples were analyzed for glucose and IL-18 using enzymatic and ELISA assays, respectively. Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression were used to assess relationships among variables. Salivary glucose and IL-18 levels were significantly elevated in CDM and UCDM groups compared to controls. Salivary glucose levels were associated with glycated hemoglobin (β = 0.429, P < 0.001) and fasting plasma glucose (β = 0.365, P = 0.001) independent of age, sex, PPD, and salivary flow rate. Salivary glucose and IL-18 levels were associated with each other (β = 0.282, P = 0.018) independent of age, sex, PPD, and salivary flow rate. Salivary glucose may serve as a glycemic biomarker, independent of periodontal status. Salivary glucose and IL-18 levels were significantly elevated in patients with T2DM and correlated with each other.Keywords: Salivary glucose, Salivary IL-18, Diabetes mellitusSubject terms: Biomarkers, Endocrinology, Medical research

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and periodontal disease have been recognized as chronic inflammatory diseases, and the results of some studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between these conditions1–3. Both diseases share common pathways, including oxidative stress pathways, the advanced glycation end products (AGE)/receptor for AGE (RAGE) pathway, and the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK)/RANK ligand (RANKL)/osteoprotegerin pathway1. Thus, the effect of glycemic control on periodontal status should be considered in periodontal disease research involving patients with T2DM.

Salivary glucose has been proposed as a biomarker of glycemic status based on multiple studies indicating a relationship with blood glucose4–6. However, some studies have revealed contradictory results, possibly because of differences in study settings, saliva collection methods, saliva sampling approaches (stimulated or unstimulated), and saliva collection times (fasting or postprandial)7,8.

Interleukin (IL)-18, initially identified as an interferon-gamma-inducing factor, is a proinflammatory cytokine in the IL-1 family. IL-18 plays vital roles in enhancing T- and natural killer cell maturation and activating the production of various chemokines, cytokines, and adhesion molecules9,10. Elevated serum IL-18 levels have been linked to the pathogenesis of T2DM11. Elevated salivary IL-18 levels are reportedly related to periodontal inflammation among individuals with chronic periodontitis12. In a previous study, we revealed that salivary IL-18 could serve as a marker of glycemic status owing to its association with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) independent of age, sex, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and periodontal status13. Surprisingly, we found that the levels of IL-18 in serum and saliva were not significantly correlated with each other.

An experimental study by Esposito et al. showed that acute hyperglycemia could increase circulating levels of IL-1814. Thus, we previously hypothesized that high blood glucose levels could contribute to elevated salivary glucose levels in the oral cavity, leading to elevated salivary IL-18 levels13. However, our previous study was hindered by a lack of measurements for salivary glucose levels, which might constitute a link between salivary IL-18 and blood glucose levels13. Additionally, our previous study population primarily comprised patients with T2DM who displayed glycemic control (glycated hemoglobin ≤ 7%); such patients might not represent all individuals with T2DM. Thus, there was a need for further investigation of the relationship between salivary IL-18 levels and glycemic status in patients with T2DM. In the present study, we aimed to investigate salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels among individuals with different glycemic statuses. Next, we sought to examine the relationship between salivary levels of glucose and IL-18, then analyze the associations of these markers with clinical parameters of periodontal diseases.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

This cross-sectional study included 75 individuals: 25 systemically healthy controls, 21 patients with controlled diabetes mellitus (CDM; HbA1C ≤ 7%), and 29 patients with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (UCDM; HbA1C > 7%). Participants in the control group were significantly younger than participants in the CDM and UCDM groups. They also had significantly lower body mass index, FPG, HbA1C, and systolic blood pressure values (Table 1). Participants in the UCDM group had significantly higher HbA1C levels than participants in the CDM group, but there was no significant difference in disease duration between the groups. There were no significant differences in sex distribution or diastolic blood pressure among the three groups (Table 1).

Salivary glucose levels

Salivary glucose levels were significantly higher in the CDM and UCDM groups than in the control group, but there was no significant difference between the CDM group and the UCDM group (Table 1; Fig. 2). Salivary glucose levels were detectable but below 1 µM, which was the detection limit, in seven of 25 participants (28%) in the control group. All salivary glucose levels in the CDM and the UCDM groups were above the detection limit.

Salivary glucose levels (A) and salivary IL-18 levels (B) in the control, CDM, and UCDM groups. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.001.

Correlations and associations of salivary glucose with clinical and periodontal parameters

We analyzed correlations of salivary glucose levels with clinical parameters using the Pearson correlation method. Salivary glucose levels were significantly correlated with both FPG (r = 0.400, P < 0.001) and HbA1C (r = 0.468, P < 0.001). However, salivary glucose levels were not correlated with periodontal parameters (Table 2). In addition, when the participants were categorized according to periodontal severity, no significant correlation was observed between the stage of periodontal disease and salivary glucose levels (r = 0.108, P = 0.345) (Table 2).

Salivary IL-18 levels

Participants in the CDM and UCDM groups had significantly higher salivary IL-18 levels than participants in the control group. No significant difference in IL-18 levels was identified between the CDM group and the UCDM group (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Correlations of salivary IL-18 levels with clinical and periodontal parameters

Salivary IL-18 levels were not correlated with any clinical or periodontal parameters except age (r = 0.273, P = 0.018) (Table 2). Older participants tended to have higher salivary IL-18 levels.

Correlations and associations of salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels

A significant correlation was observed between salivary IL-18 levels and salivary glucose levels (r = 0.300, P = 0.009) (Table 2; Fig. 3). After partial correlation analysis controlling for age and sex, the significant positive correlation between the two markers remained (r = 0.256, P = 0.027).

Considering the positive correlation between salivary IL-18 levels and salivary glucose levels, we hypothesized that salivary glucose could affect salivary IL-18 levels; thus, we used multiple regression analysis to test this hypothesis. When salivary glucose (instead of HbA1C) was utilized as an independent variable in the prediction of salivary IL-18 level, salivary IL-18 was significantly associated with age (β = 0.241, P = 0.048) independent of sex, salivary glucose, PPD, and salivary flow rate. In addition, salivary IL-18 was significantly associated with salivary glucose (β = 0.282, P = 0.018) independent of age, sex, PPD, and salivary flow rate (Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigated salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels among patients with T2DM who had different glycemic statuses. It also evaluated the relationships of these markers with clinical and periodontal parameters.

The present study showed that fasting salivary glucose levels from unstimulated whole saliva samples reflect blood glucose levels in terms of both FPG and HbA1C. Significantly elevated salivary glucose levels were observed in patients with T2DM (Fig. 2A), and the levels were significantly correlated with both FPG and HbA1C (Table 2). These results are consistent with a meta-analysis by Mascarenhas et al., which demonstrated a significant correlation between salivary glucose levels and blood glucose or HbA1C values15. Furthermore, our results showed statistically significant associations of salivary glucose levels with HbA1C (β = 0.429, P < 0.001) and FPG (β = 0.365, P = 0.001) independent of age, sex, PPD, number of teeth, and salivary flow rate (Tables 3 and 4). Similar to our results, a previous study showed significantly higher salivary glucose levels in patients with T2DM than in non-diabetic individuals, independent of periodontal disease status16. These findings imply that periodontal inflammation does not interfere with salivary glucose levels. Overall, the present study confirms the value of using salivary glucose as a glycemic biomarker in patients with T2DM, independent of periodontal status.

Salivary IL-18 levels were significantly higher in patients with T2DM than in healthy controls (Fig. 2B). These results suggest a relationship between elevated salivary IL-18 levels and T2DM, consistent with previous findings. Maksymenko et al. reported that among children with healthy gums, individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) had significantly higher salivary IL-18 levels than individuals who were systemically healthy. They also noted that elevated salivary IL-18 levels were associated with the presence of diabetes mellitus (DM)17. Maftei et al. found higher salivary IL-18 levels in patients with T2DM than in healthy individuals. They also demonstrated the utility of salivary IL-18 in predicting HbA1C values after dental extraction procedures, when used in combination with other salivary markers (e.g., osteoprotegerin, RANKL, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, matrix metalloproteinase-9, and oxidative stress markers)18. Furthermore, elevated circulating levels of IL-18 have been linked to the pathogenesis of T2DM and metabolic syndrome11,19.

The present study showed that salivary IL-18 levels were not significantly correlated with periodontal parameters (Table 2). This result is contrary to the findings of some previous studies, which showed that increased IL-18 levels in oral fluid were associated with both DM and periodontal inflammation. Toma et al. reported that IL-18 levels in gingival crevicular fluid were higher among individuals with T1DM who did not show clinical signs of gingivitis than among individuals in the control group, independent of gingivitis severity. Additionally, they demonstrated that IL-18 levels proportionally increased with the severity of gingival inflammation20. Chithralekha et al. reported significantly higher salivary IL-18 levels in patients with periodontitis who had DM than in patients who had periodontitis alone or in systemically healthy controls without periodontitis. They suggested that IL-18 levels are associated with both DM and periodontitis21. Similarly, Arunachalam et al. detected significantly higher salivary IL-18 levels among patients with both periodontitis and DM than among people with DM alone and among healthy individuals with and without periodontitis22. Notably, these studies categorized participants according to both periodontal and glycemic statuses.

Nevertheless, some of these studies did not consider the variability in HbA1C levels among participants. Thus, discrepancies between the present study and previous studies may be owing to differences in study populations and analytical protocols. Whereas previous studies categorized participants according to periodontal status, the present study mainly divided participants according to glycemic status, rather than periodontal status. Future studies with dual stratification by both glycemic and periodontal status should be considered to better distinguish their respective contributions.

Our results indicated an association between elevated salivary glucose levels and elevated salivary IL-18 levels in patients with T2DM (Table 5). Hyperglycemia has been suggested to cause acidification of the oral environment and alter the oral microbiome, thereby contributing to the development of various oral diseases23,24. Additionally, high glucose-induced activation of the NLRP-3 inflammasome can activate caspase-1, which is essential for IL-18 production25. It remains unclear whether elevated salivary glucose levels directly or indirectly influence salivary IL-18 levels. Due to the cross-sectional study design, the present findings cannot be used to infer the causal relationship between salivary glucose and salivary IL-18. Further in vivo studies with real-time measurements of oral glucose and salivary IL-18 levels, along with assessment of caspase-1 and the NLRP-3 inflammasome, are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

A major strength of this study is that, to the best of our knowledge, it is the first to evaluate salivary glucose and IL-18 levels in patients with controlled and uncontrolled T2DM. Nevertheless, this study had some limitations. First, the median age of the control group was significantly less than that of the diabetic groups which might influence the levels of salivary IL-18 in different age groups. Salivary IL-18 levels are reported to be increased in elderly healthy individuals compared to younger healthy individuals26. Hence, if further study is to be conducted, considering age-matched group and a larger sample size with dual stratification by glycemic and periodontal status should be advocated. Second, saliva samples in this study were collected while participants were in a fasting state. Thus, the results cannot be generalized to individuals in a non-fasting state. Finally, owing to the cross-sectional study design, a causal relationship between salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels could not be established in this study. Additional longitudinal studies should be performed to confirm our results.

Conclusion

Salivary glucose was associated with glycemic status, independent of periodontal status. Elevated salivary IL-18 levels were observed in patients with T2DM; these levels were correlated with salivary glucose levels. Future studies should focus on mechanistic explanations of the relationship between salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels.

Methods

This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (IRB No.100/57), the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Dentistry/Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (COA.No.MU-DT/PY-IRB 2011/012.3103) and the Institutional Human Research Ethics Committee of Srinakharinwirot University (SWUEC/F-005/2565) which was in full compliance with International Guidelines for Human Research Protection including the Helsinki Declaration, the Belmont Report, CIOMS Guideline, and the International Conference on Harmonization in Good Clinical Practice. All participants understood the study protocol and provided written informed consent to take part in the study.

Study population

Participants were categorized into three groups—a systemically healthy group (control), a controlled diabetes mellitus (CDM) group, and an uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (UCDM) group—using the HbA1C level to define controlled and uncontrolled glycemic statuses. Study participants with HbA1C levels ≤ 7% formed the CDM group; participants with HbA1C levels > 7% comprised the UCDM group (Fig. 4).

Schematic workflow of the study.

Fifty patients diagnosed with T2DM, in accordance with the American Diabetes Association 2022 guidelines for T2DM27, were recruited from the Outpatient Clinic of the Endocrinology and Metabolism Section of the Department of Medicine, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. Twenty-one and 29 patients were categorized into the CDM and UCDM groups, respectively. All patients with T2DM were receiving oral diabetic medications and/or insulin. Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of T2DM and at least 10 remaining teeth. Exclusion criteria were receipt of antibiotics or periodontal treatment within the previous 3 months; a history of salivary gland pathology or radiotherapy to the head and neck area; pregnancy; active breastfeeding; or smoking. The control group comprised 25 systemically healthy individuals with at least 10 remaining teeth who had visited the hospital for a medical check-up. Exclusion criteria for the control group were identical to exclusion criteria for the CDM and UCDM groups.

Clinical data and laboratory examinations

Demographic data and medical history were obtained for all participants using a questionnaire and electronic medical records. Blood and saliva samples were collected on the same day after an 8-hour fasting period. A 6-mL venous blood sample was collected from each participant’s antecubital vein and sent to the hospital laboratory for evaluations of HbA1C and FPG levels. HbA1C was determined using the Alinity c Hemoglobin A1c Reagent Kit (Abbott GmbH & Co., KG, Germany). Additionally, FPG levels were evaluated using the Alinity c Glucose Reagent Kit (Abbott GmbH & Co.) based on the hexokinase/glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase method.

Unstimulated whole saliva samples were collected between 8 a.m. and 11 a.m. using the spit method. First, participants were asked to rinse the oral cavity with water and avoid speaking. A 5-mL saliva sample was collected from each participant using a 50-mL centrifuge tube (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA, USA) and placed on ice. The salivary flow rate (mL/min) was determined during saliva collection. After saliva sample collection had been completed, a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) was added to the saliva sample. All saliva samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C; aliquoted supernatants were stored at -80 °C in 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes (Biologix Group, Lenexa, KS, USA) for further analysis.

A single trained investigator (PP) performed periodontal examinations of all teeth. Plaque indices were determined using the method published by Silness and Loe28. Periodontal probing depth (PPD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and bleeding on probing (BOP) status were determined at six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, mid-buccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, mid-lingual, and distolingual) using a UNC-15 periodontal probe. The mean PPD and CAL values were calculated as the sum of the maximum PPD and CAL per tooth, respectively, divided by the participant’s total number of teeth. The percentage of sites with BOP was determined (% BOP). The periodontal diagnosis categorizing participants with or without periodontitis was performed according to the guidelines from the World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions29.

Determination of salivary glucose levels and salivary IL-18 levels

Salivary glucose levels were determined using the EnzyChrom™ glucose assay kit (Bioassay Systems, Hayward, CA, USA, EBGL-100), which utilized the glucose oxidase reaction to generate a colored product. Glucose standards were prepared at concentrations of 0–30 µM. Twenty microliters of standard solutions or undiluted saliva samples were added to a black 96-well plate (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Eighty microliters of working reagent—assay buffer, enzyme mix, and dye—were subsequently added to each well, and the mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance at λex/em = 530/585 nm was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The glucose concentrations (µM) of the samples were analyzed using a standard curve generated by Gen5™version 2.0 data analysis software (Agilent BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The detection limit of the EnzyChrom™ glucose assay kit was 1 µM.

Salivary IL-18 levels were determined with a human IL-18 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom, ab215539), using the sandwich ELISA principle. Briefly, saliva samples were thawed at room temperature and diluted 30-fold with the sample diluent solution included in the kit. Standard IL-18 solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0–4,000 pg/mL. Fifty microliters of standard solutions or samples were added to each well. Fifty microliters of the antibody cocktail included in the kit were added to each well, and the mixtures were incubated at room temperature with shaking at 400 rpm for 1 h. Excess antibody solution was discarded from each well, and the wells were washed with wash buffer (3 × 350 µL). One hundred microliters of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethyl benzidine substrate solution were added to each well and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, 100 µL of stop solution were added into each well, the plate was shaken for 1 min, and the absorbance values of the standards and samples at 450 nm were measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Epoch™, Agilent). Standard curves were plotted with Gen5™version 2.0 data analysis software (Agilent BioTek) which accompanied the microplate reader. The concentrations of IL-18 in the saliva samples were expressed in units of pg/mL, and the assay sensitivity was 8.3 pg/mL.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using statistical power analysis software (G*Power 3.1, Bonn, Germany). The power calculation indicated that a sample size of 75 would provide > 85% power with an effect size of 0.4, assuming a significance level of 5%. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous data were subjected to normality assessment using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Differences among groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Post hoc analyses were performed with the Games–Howell test or Tukey test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to determine correlations among the salivary glucose level, the salivary IL-18 level, glycemic status, and periodontal parameters. The associations of salivary glucose and salivary IL-18 levels with glycemic status and periodontal parameters were evaluated by multiple linear regression analysis. Age, sex, HbA1C, PPD, and salivary flow rate were included as independent variables in the model. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in all analyses.
