Dynamic land-cover maps in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area at 30 m spatial resolution, 2000-2022
Authors: Yue Li, Qiang Liu, Shuang Chen, Lingfeng Lv, Xinpei Han, Run Jia, Lingchen Bu, Xiaotong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a world-class urban agglomeration, the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) has experienced substantial land-cover restructuring driven by urbanization, necessitating high temporal resolution monitoring to capture dynamic surface processes. However, traditional land-cover products cannot capture intra-annual dynamics effectively due to their limited update cycles. Therefore, this study generates a publicly accessible dynamic land-cover dataset for the GBA spanning 2000–2022, with a 30-meter spatial resolution and a 15-day temporal resolution. This dataset achieves an overall accuracy of 97.46%, an average accuracy of 94.5%, an F1 score of 95.19%, and a kappa coefficient of 96.78%. The validation results indicated that this dataset significantly enhances the detection of short-term land cover transitions and facilitates the characterization of critical environmental patterns. We anticipate it will serve as a baseline for monitoring agricultural growing cycles, tracking vegetation phenological shifts, and identifying disturbances in forests and croplands caused by natural hazards or anthropogenic activities.

Background & Summary

As cartographic representations of terrestrial surface characteristics1, land-cover maps visually reflect the synergistic effects of anthropogenic interventions and natural drivers on landscapes2. These datasets enable tracking of spatio-temporal land transitions, including agricultural expansion, urbanization, and conservation zoning, while supporting quantitative evaluations of ecosystem service capacities and habitat integrity3,4. Furthermore, such maps exhibit intrinsic linkages to global climate change5–7, the surface carbon cycle8–10, and biodiversity11–13. Consequently, time-series land-cover classification data are fundamental for advancing sustainable development goals.

Frequent and detailed earth observation data14 offer a spatially extensive and temporally dense source, providing an efficient foundation for land use and land cover monitoring15. Large-scale interpretation of such data mainly relies on automatic classification algorithms16, which can be broadly categorized into supervised and unsupervised classification methods. Unsupervised classification primarily employs clustering algorithms (e.g., ISODATA, maximum likelihood classification, K-Means17), which enable pattern recognition without labeled training data. In contrast, supervised classification depends on pre-labeled datasets and is commonly implemented via machine learning approaches, such as decision trees, artificial neural networks (ANN), support vector machines (SVM), and random forests (RF)18–21. Recent advances center on AI-based frameworks22,23, where deep learning architectures, including Stacked Autoencoders (SAE), Deep Belief Networks (DBN), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)24–28, have demonstrated transformative capabilities in remote sensing image analysis. Consequently, AI-based supervised classification has demonstrated remarkable potential in processing earth observation data and has become integral to contemporary land-cover mapping workflows29–33.

The existing mainstream land-cover products (as detailed in Table 1) use learning-based models to generate large-scale annual maps: SVM-driven frameworks (FROM-GLC34, GlobalLand 3035), RF-based systems (GLASS-GLC36, GLC_FCS3037, CLCD38), CNN-powered solutions (Dynamic World39, Esri Land Cover40). Although these products are effective in capturing inter-annual land-cover changes, they are inadequate for fully representing the continuous land-cover change process and may miss short-term changes. Consequently, annual maps inherently limit persistent monitoring precision. A second constraint involves coarse spatial resolution in major global datasets, including the MCD12Q1 500 m resolution dataset41,42, the ESA Climate Change Initiative 300 m dataset43, and the CGLS-LC100 dataset44,45. Many important land changes are difficult or impossible to observe at a spatial resolution greater than 100 m and annual temporal resolution39,46, such as incipient settlements and small-scale agriculture39, initial deforestation, and wetland-grassland conversion. Thus, there is an urgent need for moderate-resolution products (10–30 m) with greater temporal frequency39. Lastly, the incomplete spatial coverage poses a significant challenge. Many products perform well in inland areas where clear-sky remote sensing images are usually available. As one of China’s premier economic hubs, the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) has experienced accelerated urban-industrial transformation, driving profound land-cover modifications47. Rapid demographic shifts and environmental pressures further necessitate precise, high-frequency mapping to track dynamic transitions. Consequently, high spatio-temporal resolution land-cover products are critical for ensuring long-term monitoring consistency. However, the GBA is a subtropical cloudy area, and persistent cloud cover impedes spatially complete land-cover generation via conventional methods. While existing products cover GBA (e.g., the FROM-GLC34, Globeland3035, GLC-FCS3037, ESA WorldCover48, and CLCD38), most offer only intermittent snapshots (single/multi-year intervals), limiting continuous temporal analysis of dynamic land changes. Though the CLCD provides a continuous annual land-cover continuity, it focuses primarily on land mapping. For coastal regions like the GBA, persistent shoreline discontinuities in complex estuaries have been observed.

Thus, creating a long-term land-cover product for the GBA that incorporates regional characteristics over the past two decades would significantly advance urban monitoring capabilities. This study developed a TTT_FCN model for land-cover classification implemented in PyTorch49. The generated dynamic land-cover maps feature a spatial resolution of 30 meters and span 2000–2022. These maps consist of an annual series totaling 24 maps, which classify land cover into nine categories: Cropland, Forest, Shrubland, Grassland, Water, Snow/Ice, Barren, Impervious, and Wetland.

Methods

Study area

The Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) spans southern China’s coastal region, incorporating nine municipalities in Guangdong Province along with the special administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macao (see Fig. 1). This economically vital region (111.35–115.40°E, 21.56–24.40°N) experiences a subtropical monsoon climate and features diverse topography, including the Pearl River Delta plains and mountainous northern terrain. The GBA’s unique geographical configuration presents three distinctive characteristics: a natural amphitheater formed by surrounding mountain ranges, an extensive river network converging at the Pearl River estuary, and a highly developed coastal zone with numerous deep-water ports50. These geographical advantages have fostered the region’s emergence as China’s premier economic hub.Fig. 1Geographical location and satellite image of the GBA.

Geographical location and satellite image of the GBA.

Recent decades have witnessed remarkable spatial transformations across the GBA, driven by rapid industrialization and urban growth51,52. The landscape has undergone substantial modifications, including conversion of agricultural land, coastal reclamation projects, and expansion of transportation networks. These transformations mirror broader regional development trends, reflecting both evolving land-use patterns characteristic of fast-growing metropolitan regions and socio-economic changes manifested through urban expansion, agricultural practice shifts, and sustained infrastructural development

Basic data

The 30 m global daily seamless data cube (SDC)53–55 is generated by combining multi-sensor observations from Landsat 5, 7, 8, and 9 satellites with Terra MODIS (MOD09GA) data. Following a processing chain including gridding and reprojection, Landsat cloud masking, Landsat cross-sensor calibration, MODIS harmonization to Landsat bandpass, and a unified gap-filling plus spatio-temporal fusion process, the resulting dataset contains six spectral bands: Blue, Green, Red, NIR, SWIR1, and SWIR254.

Compared to other Landsat-based surface reflectance datasets, the SDC exhibits superior capability and consistency in monitoring land changes across both spatial and temporal dimensions, with complete coverage for all surface pixels, including shallow marine areas. The dataset provides global coverage over 23 years (2000–2022). Validation results indicate high accuracy of the SDC reconstruction. Available in the UTM Military Grid Reference System (MGRS) format, the dataset is accessible at https://data-starcloud.pcl.ac.cn/aiforearth, with a tiling size of 109.83 × 109.83 km (equivalent to 3661 × 3661 Landsat pixels)54. The quality of the generated SDC dataset is evaluated at 425 global test sites by comparing it with actual Landsat surface reflectance values, yielding a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.014 (with valid surface reflectance range: 0–1). A cross-comparison of the SDC with HLS products over 22 MGRS tiles shows a mean absolute deviation (MAD) of 0.017 for L30 (the Landsat 8-based 30 m HLS product) and 0.021 for S30 (the Sentinel-2-based 30 m HLS product)54. Furthermore, using SDC for global land-cover classification demonstrated significant accuracy improvements (2.4%–11.3%) over Landsat composite or interpolated datasets. These results position the SDC as a competitive, analysis-ready surface reflectance dataset for global environmental monitoring.

The China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD38) is an annual land-cover dataset for China, accessible via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. It provides 30-meter resolution land-cover data covering dynamics from 1990 to 2023, encompassing 9 land-cover types (Cropland, Forest, Shrubland, Grassland, Water, Snow/Ice, Barren, Impervious, and Wetland). Generated from 335,709 Landsat images using the random forests classifier, it achieves 79.31% overall accuracy (5,463 visually interpreted samples). Additionally, an evaluation involving 5,131 independent test samples indicates that the CLCD exhibits superior performance compared to other datasets, including MCD12Q1, ESA_CCI, FROM_GLC, and GlobeLand30. Therefore, we use the annual product CLCD to determine the stable area and extract the primary sample points. Then, after further selection of these sample points, they are used for the subsequent time-series classification.

Step 2: Training and Test Samples Generation

This study chose stable sample pixels to compose the training and test datasets. Stable pixels were initially identified as stable land-type areas by using multi-temporal CLCD maps covering the study area56. Stratified sampling was adopted to guarantee a sufficient number of primary sample points. Ultimately, following a method delineated in representative samples57, reliable training samples were automatically amassed across diverse land types. We used standardized time-series indices data to remove uncertain pixels from the primary sample set (examples of sample data processed with different spectral indices are shown in Fig. 3). Notably, the time series data had a wide uncertainty range in the original sample collection, which may be due to clouds. To reduce this uncertainty, we calculated the time series average (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of each index and removed samples that deviated from the average curve. That is to say, uncertain sample points were excluded, and only samples for each class within the (μ ± σ) range were preserved (demarcated by the red boundaries in Fig. 3). This filtering preserved only statistically representative samples within each class, thereby enhancing the robustness of subsequent classification models.Fig. 3Remove unreliable samples from spectral indices.

Remove unreliable samples from spectral indices.

Though indices thresholds simplify the sample filtering process, which may not fully address differences between crop types and local water conditions. This approach could potentially exclude some valid cropland samples or keep unrepresentative ones. However, the GBA (area: [55900 km2]) is a relatively small-scale area where agricultural modes are relatively simple and water body dynamics show consistent seasonal patterns. Therefore, the method works well for the GBA. If this method was to be applied to larger-scale areas, it would require more sophisticated filtering approaches which consider crop-specific phenological profiles and localized hydrological conditions.

After filtering the samples, we obtained 188,831 sample points, distributed as follows: Cropland (11,656), Forest (100,082), Shrubland (407), Grassland (1,485), Water (21,847), Barren (2,974), and Impervious (50,380). Following the extraction of spectral-temporal features from these sample points, the final dataset has a three-dimensional structure (188,831 × 48 × 10), which corresponds to the number of sample points, the time steps, and the spectral-temporal features, respectively. So, we utilized 10 bands (comprising 6 spectral bands and 4 indices), yielding a total of 480 input features (48-periods × 10 bands) for the TTT_FCN model.

Step 3: Classification by the TTT_FCN Model

We introduce a TTT_FCN classification model, wherein 70% of the samples obtained in the previous step are utilized for training the model and optimizing its hyperparameters. The remaining 30% of the samples serve to assess the model’s accuracy metrics. Then, the hyperparameters are applied to the SDC55 reflectance images to generate 24-scene/annual dynamic classification maps.

Hyperparameter optimization

Prior to model training, we use Optuna to determine the most suitable hyperparameters for the model. Optuna58 is an efficient, automated hyperparameter optimization tool that is compatible with major machine learning libraries, such as scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, and PyTorch. Its core principles include sampling algorithms and pruning algorithms. The sampling algorithm generates new parameters based on historical trial results, gradually approaching regions that enhance the objective function, and ultimately determines the optimal hyperparameter combination. During model training, some hyperparameter configurations may perform inadequately in the early stages and are unlikely to yield better results. The pruning algorithm terminates these trials early (similar to early stopping) to save computational resources.

Figure 4 displays the hyperparameter optimization results for the TTT_FCN model, illustrating the correlation between parameter configurations and their respective loss values. The connected line segments represent different trials, with increasingly red hues indicating lower loss values. The optimal configuration, marked by the deepest red lines, consists of 128 hidden dimensions, a learning rate of 0.001, and a batch size of 64.Fig. 4The hyperparameter tuning results of the TTT_FCN model by Optuna.

The hyperparameter tuning results of the TTT_FCN model by Optuna.

The TTT_FCN model

To capture both temporal and spectral dependencies, we employ a Test-Time Training layer for Fully Convolutional Networks (TTT_FCN) to extract spatio-temporal features. Its encoder-decoder architecture achieves multi-scale feature fusion while dynamically updating hidden states to enhance expressive ability. The network comprises four stacked convolutional blocks, each performing two convolutions followed by a pooling operation (the overall network structure of the TTT_FCN model is shown in Fig. 5). Crucially, we integrate the Test-Time Training (TTT) layer59 within the training process. This layer replaces conventional RNN hidden states with learnable parameters (Conn1D: 1-dimensional convolutional layer) and compresses context via input token gradient descent. Unlike attention-based models, the TTT layer enables linear-complexity architectures through expressive memory, outperforming Transformers and Mamba (Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces) in long-context processing59.Fig. 5The TTT_FCN model network.

The TTT_FCN model network.

Step 4: Accuracy Assessment

We used the Python module sklearn. metrics to calculate overall accuracy (OA), average accuracy (AA), F1 score (F1), and kappa coefficients (Kappa) from the confusion matrix of all categories. Additionally, we applied SHAP60 to explain the contribution of each input feature, summarizing feature importance and providing a global model explanation.

Technical Validation

Since the dynamic GBA map61,62 is built based on validated and released input data, the main purpose of the current validation procedure is to ensure the map’s accuracy through three aspects: First, verifying the accuracy of different time-series classification methods using the same test set. Second, a comparison between the dynamic map and traditional land-cover products through visual interpretation samples. Third, individual sample points are selected to observe their type changes during 2020–2021.

Monitoring of land-cover temporal changes

Conventional land-cover products that only provide initial and final states fail to capture transitional dynamics. As illustrated in Figs. 10, 11, land often undergoes multiple intermediate conversions between different cover types. The processes remain obscured when only endpoints are documented. High temporal-resolution monitoring is essential to reveal these dynamic transitions and prevent information loss from coarse temporal sampling. This is particularly evident at the pixel scale, where intra-annual land-cover variability becomes most pronounced. High temporal resolution can more precisely reveal the subtle changes that occur over time, documenting the frequency and timing of the transition from one type of land to another.

Importance of input features

Deep learning models are frequently regarded as “black boxes”, which complicates the interpretation of their predictive logic. Therefore, we employed SHAP60 (SHapley Additive exPlanation) importance analysis to reveal the importance of input features. The fundamental principle of SHAP60 is to ascertain the marginal contribution of features to a model’s output, thereby facilitating the understanding of its behaviour. SHAP reveals the clue of predictive logic by assigning each feature a contribution value (positive or negative), revealing its impact on the model’s predictions and enhancing the interpretability of complex deep learning models.

Figure 12 shows the SHAP importance analysis of spatio-temporal features. Each row in the SHAP graph represents a feature, and the ranking represents the importance of the feature. The upper horizontal axis represents the Mean Shapley Value. The larger the Mean Shapley Value, the higher the importance of the feature to the model. The horizontal axis below represents the Shapley Value Contribution. One point represents one sample. The darker the color, the greater the Shapley Value. A Shapley value greater than 0 indicates a positive impact, while a Shapley value less than 0 indicates a negative impact. For the feature NDVI, the sample distribution is relatively dispersed, indicating that this feature has the greatest influence on the classification results. NDBI and NDWI are important factors affecting land-cover classification in the TTT_FCN classification. Usage Notes

Rapid urbanization and industrialization have resulted in frequent land-cover alterations within the Greater Bay Area (GBA). High spatio-temporal resolution data can facilitate timely monitoring of these changes and support rational urban development planning. This study developed a TTT_FCN model to produce high-frequency land-cover maps for the GBA from 2000 to 2022, with a spatial resolution of 30 meters and an annual production of 24 maps. Each geospatial image is stored in GeoTIFF format, georeferenced to WGS 84/UTM zone 49 N (EPSG:32649), and assigned a unique land-cover label61. These files use unsigned 8-bit integer (uint8) format with LZW compression. The database captures both abrupt and gradual land-cover changes, including various types such as forest, cropland, and impervious surfaces, each playing distinct roles in carbon uptake, storage, and release. Long-term land-cover mapping enhances understanding of carbon cycle mechanisms in the GBA and provides a scientific foundation for climate change mitigation.
