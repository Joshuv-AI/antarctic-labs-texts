NF-κB/AP-1-mediated expression of the porcine cathelicidin proPMAP-37 suppresses inflammation by neutralizing LPS and inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization
Authors: Qingqing Fu, Kunhong Xie, Bing Yu, Zhiqing Huang, Yuheng Luo, Ping Zheng, Xiangbing Mao, Jie Yu, Junqiu Luo, Hui Yan, Jun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCathelicidins are short cationic peptides secreted in pro-peptide, characterized by a conserved N-terminal cathelin domain and a diverse C-terminal mature peptide. While the antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties of mature cathelicidins are well-characterized, whether their precursor forms (pro-forms) possess biological activity remains a subject of debate. Amid the global challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and the mandate to reduce antibiotic use in livestock, cathelicidins represent promising alternatives for managing bacterial infection in agriculture. However, high production costs hamper their large-scale application. Therefore, elucidating the regulation of expression of porcine myeloid antimicrobial peptide 37 (PMAP-37), investigating the function of its pro-form (proPMAP-37), and developing a cost-effective production strategy are of significant importance for both basic immunology in porcine systems and agricultural applications.ResultsThis study demonstrated that porcine myeloid antimicrobial peptide 37 (PMAP-37) exhibited widespread tissue distribution in disease-susceptible Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) pigs, whereas its constitutive expression level was significantly higher in disease-resistant Tibetan pigs (TB). Furthermore, Escherichia coli K88 (E. coli K88) upregulated PMAP-37 expression in the intestine of DLY pigs. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the induction of PMAP-37 mediated by E. coli K88 depends on NF-κB and AP-1 transcription factors. Importantly, proPMAP-37 (the precursor of PMAP-37) was successfully expressed in a heterologous system and purified to homogeneity. Functional characterization demonstrated negligible hemolytic activity and cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells. Although no direct bactericidal effect was observed in vitro, proPMAP-37 functioned as an immunomodulator. It exhibited high affinity with Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and inhibited pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization, thereby reducing the expression of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).ConclusionsThis comprehensive study provides novel insights into the immunological functions of porcine cathelicidins and promotes the industrial development of proPMAP-37 as a novel anti-inflammatory agent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05710-6.Keywords: Cathelicidins, NF-κB/AP-1, proPMAP-37, Anti-inflammatory

Background

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K88 remains a primary pathogen of diarrhea after weaning piglets, causing serious economic losses to the global porcine industry [1]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major structural component of the E. coli outer membrane, can trigger a strong inflammatory response characterized by the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines [2]. The use of in-feed antibiotics has been widely implemented as an effective strategy to control ETEC infections [3]. The global challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become a critical threat to livestock health and sustainable agricultural production, prompting an urgent search for alternatives to conventional antibiotics [4].

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), as evolutionarily conserved components of the innate immune system, have emerged as promising candidates due to their dual antimicrobial and immunomodulatory roles [5]. As an ancient defense weapon of the innate immune system, AMPs are dynamically induced by invasive pathogens and contribute to controlling pathogenic infections [6]. AMPs exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [7]. AMPs primarily act by disrupting microbial membranes and interfering with key cellular processes, including DNA replication and protein synthesis [8, 9]. Beyond direct antimicrobial activity, AMPs play a key role in regulating immune responses, including orchestrating immune cell recruitment, enhancing phagocytosis and intracellular killing, regulating inflammatory responses, modulating programmed cell death, and shaping adaptive immune activation [10]. Consequently, AMPs are now widely recognized not merely as natural antimicrobials but as integral host defense peptides (HDPs) [11].

Cathelicidins, a prominent family of AMPs, are short cationic peptides [12]. Cathelicidins have been discovered in nearly all vertebrates, including mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish [13–17]. As a natural defense mechanism against various pathogens, Cathelicidins are produced by various immune system cells and epithelial cells in the skin and mucosal surfaces [18]. Structurally, these peptides are synthesized as precursors, consisting of a conserved N-terminal cathelin-like domain and a highly variable C-terminal mature peptide responsible for biological activity [19]. The function of the precursors remains controversial. The initial hypothesis was that its role was to keep the antimicrobial domain in an inactive state [20]. However, accumulating evidence challenges this paradigm that precursors are biologically inert [21, 22]. Notably, hCAP18 (the human LL-37 precursor) exhibits Gram-negative antibacterial potency comparable to its mature counterpart [23]. These findings suggest that cathelicidin precursors may exert unique biological functions.

Porcine myeloid antimicrobial peptide 37 (PMAP-37), a key cathelicidin in porcine, was initially cloned from porcine bone marrow and encoded a 167-residue polypeptide [24]. The pre-pro-region of this polypeptide is highly similar to the cathelin domain derived from porcine, cattle, and rabbit [24]. Followed by a unique and cationic 37-residue sequence termed PMAP-37, which is a single amphipathic α-helix similar to the human cathelicidin LL-37 [24, 25]. PMAP-37 was renowned for its potent broad-spectrum bactericidal activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [24, 26]. Recent research indicates that PMAP-37 is a multifunctional peptide with antiviral and anti-inflammatory activities [25]. These findings position PMAP-37 as an important component of porcine innate immunity, with the potential to defend against bacterial and viral infections. Currently, its expression profiles and transcriptional regulation remain incompletely defined, and the function of its precursor, proPMAP-37, is uncharacterized. Moreover, limited scalability and high production costs hinder its translational potential. This study attempted to elucidate expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms of PMAP-37, establish an efficient method for producing high-purity recombinant proPMAP-37 using the E. coli expression system, and subsequently investigate its antibacterial activity and immunomodulatory properties.

Materials and methods

Strains and cells

E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3) (Novagen, 71397), Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli K88 (E. coli K88, CVCC225), Salmonella Typhimurium (S. typhimurium, ATCC14028), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus ATCC25923), Streptococcus suis (S. suis, CVCC4350), Intestinal Porcine Epithelial Cell line-J2 (IPEC-J2, DSMZ ACC701), Mouse mononuclear macrophage leukemia Cell line (RAW 264.7, ATCC TIB-71) and Human Embryonic Kidney 293T Cell line (HEK293T, ATCC ACS-4500) were maintained by our laboratory.

Cell culture

IPEC-J2 cells were cultured in cell culture dishes in DMEM-F12 (Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco, USA), and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA). RAW264.7 cells and HEK293T cells were cultured in cell culture dishes in DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco, USA), and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Phylogenetic tree and multiple sequence alignment

Multiple PMAP-37 coding sequences (CDS) were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and aligned using ClustalW. Phylogenetic trees were constructed via the neighbor-joining method with the Kimura 2-parameter model in MEGA 11, with nodal support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates. Multiple sequence homology analyses were performed using ClustalX 2.1.

Animals

Twelve 21-day-old Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) were purchased from Sichuan Meishan Wanjiaohao Porcine Breeding Co., Ltd. (Meishan, China). All porcine were randomly divided into two groups: a control group (CON) and an E. coli K88-challenged group (K88). Following a 4-day basal diet adaptation period (formulated per NRC 2012; Table 1), the CON group received 100 mL LB culture orally via orogastric tube once daily for 3 consecutive days, while the K88 group received equivalent volumes of E. coli K88 suspension (1 × 109 CFU/mL in LB culture). The animal was euthanized 24 h after the last oral gavage administration of E. coli K88. Six 28-day-old TB pigs were purchased from Luding County Yiho Agricultural Ecological Development Co., Ltd. (Luding, China). At the end of the experiment, all porcines were deeply anesthetized via intravenous injection of Zoletil® 50 (a combination of Tiletamine 125 mg and Zolazepam 125 mg; Virbac, France) at a dosage of 5 mg/kg body weight. Upon confirmation of a complete loss of consciousness and lack of pedal reflex, the animals were euthanized via exsanguination. This method was selected to ensure a rapid and humane euthanasia. Subsequently, porcine tissues were collected and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for qRT-PCR analysis.

IPEC-J2 cells were treated with E. coli K88 or Toll-like receptors (TLRs) activators

To explore the regulatory mechanisms of PMAP 37, IPEC-J2 cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates and grown to 80% confluence at 37℃ in a CO2 incubator (5% v/v). Subsequently, cells were exposed to E. coli K88 (104 CFUs/mL, 105 CFUs/mL 106 CFUs/mL, 107 CFUs/mL) for 2 h or treated with different TLRs activators [ TLR1/2 agonist Pam3CSK4 ( invivoGen: tlrl-pms; 12 h; 1 µg/mL), TLR4 agonist LPS (Sigma-Aldrich: L2880; 3 h; 0.1 µg/mL), TLR5 agonist Entolimod (TargetMol: 951628-22-1; 6 h; 1 µg/mL), and TLR9 agonist dsDNA-Ec (invivoGen: tlrl-ecdna; 6 h; 1 µg/mL) ], or treated with inhibitors [NF-κB inhibitors SC75741 (MedChemExpress: HY-10496; 4 h, 10 µM), AP-1 inhibitors T-5224 (MedChemExpress: HY-12270; 2 h, 10 µM)]. After treatment, the cells were gently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Subsequently, RNAiso Plus (Takara, China) was added to lyse the cells immediately, and then the cells were collected for quantitative real-time PCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted using standardized protocols (Promega, Madison, WI). The firefly luciferase coding sequence was subcloned downstream of wild-type or mutant PMAP-37 promoter regions, followed by co-transfection with pcDNA3.1- RelA/c-Fos/c-Jun expression vectors and pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control plasmid into HEK293T cells. After 48 h, following cell lysis, the firefly luciferase activity was measured by a GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega, Madison, WI).

The expression and purification of fusion proPMAP-37

The CDS of PMAP-37 was cloned into the NcoI and XhoI sites of pET-32a (+) to generate an N-terminal His-tagged fusion construct. This plasmid was expressed in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3). After transformation, E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3) cells were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 28 °C for 8 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in ice-cold TieChui™ Lysis Buffer (ACE Biotechnology, China), and lysed. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 9,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Fusion proPMAP-37 was purified from the supernatant via Ni NTA Magarose Bead affinity chromatography (Smart Lifesciences, China). The magnetic bead was washed with binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and eluted with 250 mM imidazole (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Fractions containing the recombinant protein were identified by SDS-PAGE analysis using 12.5% resolving gel.

Fusion proPMAP-37 cleavage via enterokinase

The His-tagged proPMAP-37 fusion protein was displaced into Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM imidazole, pH8.0) by Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (3 kDa MWCO, Merck Millipore, USA). Proteolytic cleavage was performed using enterokinase (Yeasen Biotech, China) at 25 °C for 24 h. The digested product was applied to Ni NTA Magarose Beads (Smart-Lifesciences, China) to remove residual His-tagged fusion partners and enterokinase. LPS was removed from proPMAP-37 using ToxinEraser™ Endotoxin Removal Resin (Genscript, China). Finally, proPMAP-37 was concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (3 kDa MWCO, Merck Millipore, USA). The concentration of recombinant proPMAP-37 was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Identification of proPMAP-37

Amino acid sequences of the target protein were identified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Separated proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE analysis using 12.5% resolving gel. Gel bands corresponding to target proteins were excised and submitted to Sangon Biotech for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Cell viability

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) following standard protocols. Briefly, 5 × 104 IPEC-J2 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates (Corning, USA) and grown to 80% confluence at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator (5% v/v). After treatment with proPMAP-37 (5, 10, 20, 40 µM) for 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with 100 µL fresh medium containing 10 µL Cell Counting Kit-8 solution. The plates were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C, and cell viability was determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Devices, USA).

Hemolytic activity

Hemolytic activity of recombinant proPMAP-37 was evaluated using porcine erythrocytes isolated from heparinized blood. After differential centrifugation (300 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), red blood cells (RBCs) were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and resuspended to 4% (v/v) in PBS. Aliquots (100 µL) of RBC suspension were treated with proPMAP-37 at concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM at 37 °C for 1 h. Hemolysis was quantified by measuring hemoglobin release at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Devices, USA). PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 were employed as the negative and positive controls, respectively.

Antimicrobial assay

The antimicrobial activity of proPMAP-37 in vitro was determined by minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay. Mid-log phase bacteria were diluted to 1.0 × 105 CFUs/mL with Mueller-Hinton broth. Specifically, 100 µL of bacterial suspensions were treated with 100 µL of proPMAP-37 (final concentration with 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) in 96-well plates at 37 °C for 18 h. MIC was determined as the lowest concentration showing complete absence of visible growth.

LPS binding

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich: L2880; biological source: E. coli O55:B5; < 3% protein) binding of proPMAP-37 was quantified by BODIPY-TR cadaverine displacement. Briefly, LPS (50 µg/mL) and BODIPY-TR cadaverine (5 µg/mL) were pre-complexed in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 4 h in the dark. proPMAP-37 (5, 10, 20, 40 µM) was incubated with the LPS-probe complex for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescent (excitation/emission: 580/620 nm) was measured using a SpectraMax M2 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). LPS attachment to RAW264.7

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells/well and grown to 80% confluence (37 °C, 5% CO₂). 1 µg/mL FITC-LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was incubated with varying concentrations of proPMAP-37 (0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 µM) at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. This mixture of LPS and proPMAP-37 was then added to the RAW264.7 cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After the incubation, RAW264.7 cells were washed twice with PBS to eliminate any unbound LPS. After washing, cells were gently pipetted with PBS. FITC-LPS binding was quantified via the BD FACSVerse™ instrument (BD Biosciences, CA) and analyzed in FlowJo 10.8.1 software.

Macrophage polarization

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells/well and grown to 50% confluence (37 °C, 5% CO₂). For M1-like polarization, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 1000 ng/mL LPS (Sigma, USA) for 12 h. proPMAP-37 (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 µM) pre-treatment was usually performed 12 h before. Next, RAW 264.7 cells were gently pipetted and collected with PBS, centrifuged (500 ×g, 4 °C, 3 min), then washed twice with PBS. Washed cells were stained with APC/Cy7 anti-mouse CD86 (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min at RT (dark, per manufacturer protocol). After PBS washing, cells were resuspended in PBS. Samples were assessed by the BD FACSVerse™ instrument (BD Biosciences, USA) and analyzed in FlowJo 10 software.

Total RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from tissues and cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and 1.0 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, China) in a 20 µL reaction volume. qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcript abundances were normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression values were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method [27]. Primer sequences are provided in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups used an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test after verifying parametric assumptions. Multiple groups were compared with one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Results

Nucleotide sequence analysis of the PMAP-37

The overall topology of the neighbor-joining tree (Fig. 1A) delineates distinct evolutionary lineages. Specifically, the sequence of Sus scrofa (wild pig) PMAP-37 gene exhibits close similarity to the corresponding sequences in three cetacean species, including Neophocaena asiaeorientalis asiaeorientalis (porpoise), Lipotes vexillifer (Yangtze River dolphin or baiji), and Balaenoptera musculus (blue whale). Sus scrofa clusters together with three cetacean species to form a major clade. The clustering pattern reveals that Sus scrofa is a sister taxon to the cetacean subclade. This basal branching is highly reliable, as it is supported by a maximal bootstrap value of 100%, with the internal nodes of the cetacean species also exhibiting 100% bootstrap support. In contrast, the marsupial Phascolarctos cinereus (koala) branches at the root with a long branch length, demonstrating profound evolutionary divergence and serving as a robust outgroup. Nucleotide sequence analysis among these related species (Fig. 1B) demonstrated that the 5’ region, encoding the signal peptide and the cathelin domain, features minimal nucleotide substitutions. Conversely, the 3’ region encoding the mature peptide displays extensive variation, marked by frequent nucleotide substitutions and prominent insertion-deletion events.

PMAP-37 is upregulated by E. coli K88 and its Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs)

We further explore whether the expression of PMAP-37 is associated with immune defense against bacterial challenge. In DLY pigs, E. coli K88 significantly upregulated relative expression of PMAP-37 in jejunum and ileum (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B). Consistent with in vivo findings, E. coli K88 significantly upregulated relative expression of PMAP-37 in IPEC-J2 cells (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4A). As PAMPs from E. coli K88 activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs) signaling to induce downstream innate immune responses, we examined whether specific TLR pathways induce PMAP-37 expression. Our findings demonstrated that agonistic ligands of TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, and TLR9 significantly upregulated relative expression of PMAP-37 in IPEC-J2 cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B).

E. coli K88 induced PMAP − 37 by NF-kB/AP-1

To elucidate the pathway through which PMAP-37 is induced by TLRs stimulation, we characterized the PMAP-37 promoter region using the JASPAR bioinformatics platform (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), focusing on transcription factors NF-κB (RelA) and AP-1 (c-Fos/c-Jun). A systematic scan of the 2.0 kilobase (kb) sequence upstream of the PMAP-37 transcriptional start site (TSS) predicted consensus binding motifs for NF-κB (RelA) and AP-1 (c-Fos/c-Jun) (Fig. 4C). Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that ectopic overexpression of the transcription factor RelA, c-Jun, and c-Fos both significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity (P < 0.01, Fig. 4D-F), whereas mutations in their respective binding motifs could not enhance fluorescence intensity (P > 0.05, Fig. 4D-F). We then investigated the contribution of NF-κB and AP-1 to E. coli K88-induced PMAP-37 upregulation. SC75741 (NF-κB inhibitor) (P < 0.01; Fig. 4G) or T5224 (AP-1 inhibitor) (P < 0.01; Fig. 4H) significantly attenuated relative expression of PMAP-37 in IPEC-J2 cells challenged with E. coli K88. Thus, NF-κB and AP-1 are essential transcriptional regulators of PMAP-37 during E. coli K88 challenge.

Heterologous expression of proPMAP-37 in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3)

To characterize the functional properties of the protein encoded by PMAP-37, we expressed proPMAP-37 using a pET32a (+) prokaryotic system in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3) competent cells. SDS-PAGE analysis of the whole-cell lysate revealed a prominent induced band at an apparent molecular weight of ~ 33 kDa, consistent with the theoretical size (Fig. 5A). The soluble fraction was subsequently subjected to Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. Following extensive washing with 20 mM imidazole to remove non-specifically bound host proteins, the target recombinant proPMAP-37 was successfully eluted using 250 mM imidazole. Analysis of these specific elution fractions via SDS-PAGE (Fig. 5B) demonstrated a highly purified single band at ~ 33 kDa, confirming the efficacy of the affinity purification step. Subsequently, the fusion protein was subjected to specific enzymatic cleavage to release the fusion flag, which exhibited an apparent molecular weight of ~ 18 kDa, and proPMAP-37 exhibited an apparent molecular weight of ~ 15 kDa (Fig. 5C). The final purified proPMAP-37 exhibited an apparent molecular weight of ~ 15 kDa on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 5D). The yield of recombinant proPMAP-37 consistently reached 12.62 mg/L. To definitively verify the protein identity at the molecular level, the purified band was analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The resulting mass spectrum (Fig. 5E) yielded the exact peptide sequence ‘IGQKIKDLSEFFQS’, which perfectly matches the theoretical sequence of the proPMAP-37. Other peptide sequences are provided in the supplementary materials (Fig. S2).

Cytotoxicity and hemolytic activity

IPEC-J2 cells were used to investigate the cytotoxicity of proPMAP-37, and cell viability was evaluated using a CCK‑8 assay following treatment with proPMAP-37 (5, 10, 20, 40 µM) for 24 h. The results demonstrated that proPMAP‑37 exhibited no cytotoxic effects on cell viability (P > 0.05; Fig. 6A). Subsequently, porcine erythrocytes, isolated from fresh blood, were incubated with proPMAP‑37 (5, 10, 20, 40 µM) for 1 h. Compared to the potent hemolytic agent Triton X‑100, proPMAP‑37 displayed no significant hemolytic activity (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Over the past decades, research on antibacterial peptides (AMPs) and their potential has attracted considerable interest in agriculture [28]. As multifunctional peptides of the host immune system, cathelicidins are strictly regulated to achieve precise spatiotemporal induction in response to microbial challenges. PMAP-37 is a key cathelicidin in porcine. The expression dynamics of PMAP-37 and the biological activity of its precursor, proPMAP-37, remain largely unexplored. In this study, we bridge this gap by elucidating the systemic expression and regulatory mechanisms of PMAP-37, alongside establishing a recombinant system for soluble proPMAP-37 production in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3), followed by characterizing the antimicrobial activity and immunomodulatory properties of recombinant proPMAP-37 in vitro. This provides a theoretical basis and technical support for the application of proPMAP-37 in agriculture.

Cathelicidins constitute a ubiquitous family of AMPs in vertebrates, exhibiting broad tissue expression [29, 30]. In this study, we confirmed systemic PMAP-37 expression in DLY pigs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and intestinal segments (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and colon), highlighting its potential role in porcine innate immunity. We further identified differential expression levels of PMAP-37 between disease-susceptible DLY pigs and disease-resistant TB pigs, observing elevated levels in the lung, jejunum, cecum, and colon of Tibetan pigs. A similar breed-dependent expression trend has been reported by Ma et al. [31], who demonstrated that the expression levels of PMAP-37 were significantly higher in the majority of tissues in indigenous Min pigs compared to commercial Landrace pigs. The elevated basal expression of AMPs serves as a molecular correlate of the superior innate disease resistance observed in these indigenous breeds [32, 33].

The inducible nature of AMPs during infection is well established [6, 34]. To investigate the responsiveness of PMAP-37 to bacterial infection, we assessed its expression following E. coli K88 challenge. Our findings revealed PMAP-37 expression was significantly upregulated in the E. coli K88-challenged group, consistent with previous reports demonstrating dynamic induction of cathelicidins during infection [35, 36]. TLRs recognize microbial pathogens and trigger direct antimicrobial activity [37–39], which may be linked to the induction of AMPs [40–42]. Consistent with this, we demonstrated that TLRs activation (TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, TLR9) upregulated PMAP-37 expression. TLRs initiate downstream kinase cascades that activate transcription factors NF-κB (RelA) and AP-1 (c-Fos/c-Jun), driving immune modulator production [43]. Previous studies have shown that NF-κB is critical for the inducible expression of AMPs, such as β-defensins and LL-37 [44–46]. AP-1 also contributes to TLR4-mediated hBD-2 expression [47]. Given the presence of NF-κB and AP-1 binding sites within the PMAP-37 promoter, we investigated their roles in the transcriptional activation of PMAP-37. We confirmed E. coli K88 induced PMAP-37 expression through NF-κB and AP-1. This result aligns with observations in human studies, where NF-κB and AP-1 drive hBD-2 expression in Caco-2 cells challenged with E. coli Nissle 1917 [48], suggesting an evolutionarily conserved mechanism for cathelicidins deployment at host-microbe interfaces.

PMAP-37 is synthesized as a pre-pro-peptide, consisting of an N-terminal signal peptide (residues 1–29), a conserved cathelin domain (30–130), and a C-terminal mature peptide (131–167). The pro-forms of cathelicidins, considered storage forms, require proteolytic processing to become biologically functional [23]. However, certain pro-form peptides, such as proBac7 and CAP18, have been shown to retain biological activity, prompting us to investigate the biological activity of proPMAP-37 [21, 22]. Currently, characterization and production of PMAP-37 rely on chemical synthesis [49, 50]. However, its high cost typically precludes large-scale applications. Advances in molecular biology and recombinant DNA technology have facilitated genetic engineering as an effective alternative for antimicrobial peptide expression, offering streamlined processes, operational flexibility, cost efficiency, and high yields [51]. Various heterologous expression systems have been developed for large-scale peptide production, with E. coli serving as the predominant microbial host platform [52]. In this study, we successfully produced a fusion protein with a molecular weight of approximately 33 kDa using Escherichia coli Rosetta™ (DE3). Subsequently, we successfully obtained proPMAP-37 with a molecular weight of approximately 15 kDa through affinity chromatography and in vitro cleavage with enterokinase.

To characterize the biological properties of proPMAP-37, we evaluated its direct antimicrobial activity using MIC assays. In this study, although the concentration of 40 µM proPMAP-37 was ten times higher than the reported MIC value of PMAP-37, this concentration did not show direct bactericidal activity against E. coli K88 and S. typhimurium [24]. Most AMPs exploit their inherent cationic charge to selectively target bacteria through electrostatic interactions with anionic surface components, leading to membrane permeabilization and lethal disruption of membrane integrity [53]. Thus, the lack of direct bacterial inhibitory activity of proPMAP-37 may be due to the cathelin-like domain altering the charge of PMAP-37. In addition, pro-fragment promotes charge balance, helping to reduce the cytotoxicity [54]. In this study, proPMAP-37 showed no significant hemolysis and cytotoxicity against mammalian cells.

Endotoxin, known as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), is a structural component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria and is a potent stimulator of the inflammatory cascade, which may progress to sepsis and septic shock [55–57]. In general, endotoxin shock occurs following antibiotic treatment for bacterial infections, as antibiotics eliminate bacteria without neutralizing LPS released from lysed bacterial membranes [58]. Consequently, neutralizing LPS is crucial to prevent or mitigate the endotoxin shock caused by Gram-negative bacterial infections [59]. Prior studies have identified that cathelicidins neutralize LPS [60–62]. CAP11 and CAP18 neutralize LPS, thereby conferring protection in murine endotoxic shock models [63]. Recent research indicates PMAP-37 can neutralize LPS, thereby suppressing LPS-induced NO production in RAW 264.7 macrophages [25]. The mechanism by which cathelicidins neutralize LPS is not fully elucidated, but the most likely mechanism is that they bind LPS, thereby inhibiting the interaction of LPS with LPS-binding protein (LBP) [64]. Here, we demonstrate that proPMAP-37 binds LPS, resulting in reduced LPS attachment to RAW 264.7 macrophages. LBP mediates the transport of LPS to CD14; the LPS-CD14 complex activates downstream inflammatory cascades involving MyD88 and NF-κB, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [65]. In support of this mechanism, we also found that proPMAP-37 suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α by binding LPS. In addition to neutralizing LPS, cathelicidins exhibit anti-inflammatory activity via the intracellular signaling pathway [66, 67]. Given that the macrophages are key orchestrators of the inflammatory response [68], we investigated whether proPMAP-37 can directly regulate macrophage polarization in a manner independent of LPS binding. In this study, we confirmed that proPMAP-37 directly inhibited M1 polarization, thereby decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α). Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) is a membrane-bound protein on the surface of cells that recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [69]. TLR4 signaling is mediated by the adaptor protein myeloid differentiation-associated protein 88 (MyD88), which involves the activation of a series of downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, such as the MAPK and NF-κB pathways [70]. Studies have shown that cathelicidins not only inhibit TLR4 activation by neutralizing LPS but also regulate downstream signaling molecules of TLR4 [12]. Mouse CRAMP reduces MyD88 synthesis and inhibits the phosphorylation of p38 and ERK in this cascade [71]. Cath-MH, isolated from the skin of the Microhyla heymonsivogt frog, inhibits MAPK signaling [72]. Furthermore, Chicken CATH-2 suppresses APEC-induced inflammation via the NF-κB/NLRP3/MAPK pathway [66]. Based on these findings, it is highly possible that proPMAP-37 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by modulating similar TLR4-dependent downstream pathways. Although the direct intracellular molecular targets of proPMAP-37 were not fully elucidated in the present study, systematically deciphering these signaling pathways will be the primary focus of our upcoming research.

Crucially, while mature cathelicidins are typically potent antimicrobials, they often exhibit severe toxicity toward expression hosts [24]. Notably, no studies have reported the successful heterologous expression of PMAP-37 in E. coli, a limitation likely driven by the antimicrobial activity against E. coli. In contrast, our study demonstrates that the pro-region effectively mitigates this toxicity. By mitigating detrimental selection pressure and cellular damage to the expression system, proPMAP-37 facilitated a stable and high-yield production in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3), achieving a quantitative yield of 12.62 mg/L in our laboratory-scale setup. Combined with the feasibility of high-density fermentation in industrial production, this precursor form exhibits excellent scalable, low-cost industrial production potential. Furthermore, despite its low cytotoxicity, proPMAP-37 retains robust extracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-binding and immunomodulatory capacities. Collectively, these findings offer a cost-effective strategy for the industrial scale-up of antimicrobial peptides and establish a solid foundation for proPMAP-37 as an innovative immunomodulatory agent.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a systematic characterization of PMAP-37, elucidating its regulatory mechanisms and the potential applications of its precursor in agricultural production through biosynthesis. We confirmed that PMAP-37 is a critical component in the porcine innate immune system, showing constitutively higher expression in disease-resistant Tibetan pigs compared to DLY pigs and specific induction via the NF-κB and AP-1 signaling pathways during E. coli K88 challenge. Furthermore, the development of a high-yield, recombinant production system for proPMAP-37 in E. coli Rosetta™ (DE3) addresses a significant bottleneck in the scalable synthesis of antimicrobial peptides. Our functional validation demonstrates that proPMAP-37 acts as a potent anti-inflammatory agent by neutralizing LPS and inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization. These results offer a cost-effective strategy for managing inflammation in livestock.
