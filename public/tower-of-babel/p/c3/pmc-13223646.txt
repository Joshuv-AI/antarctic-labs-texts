Family perspective: raising a child with NEC and the need for multidisciplinary care
Authors: David Russell
Published: 2026

The steady pulse of monitors and pumps fills the room while I hear the clinical team assembling out in the hall. This all too familiar scenario briefly takes me back to our days in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), where my son spent his first 7 months. Yet on this day, this is the backdrop as we prepare for his third surgery to place a g-tube in the last 7 days. We are here because at 9 years old, he weighs only 38 pounds (17.2 kg), one of many enduring remnants of his battle with necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) as an infant. Surgery to place a g-tube is fairly straightforward, but nothing about his abdomen is straightforward. Even so, three surgeries, fixed-loop bowel obstruction, and nearly 4 weeks in the hospital just for a g-tube far exceeded what we had anticipated.

My son is among the children fortunate enough to survive NEC, so being here at all is a blessing that demands a heavy dose of perspective and a reality check against the alternative that far too many families face. Nevertheless, this disease has affected his life in profound ways that often are not considered or imagined during those long NICU months when the only hope and singular focus as a parent is that your child makes it to the next day. Since being just a few weeks old, NEC and the effects of multiple laparotomies and bowel resections necessary to save his life in the NICU have been intertwined with his story and an ever-present, insidious force every step of the way. The consequences extend far beyond NICU discharge, manifesting in both subtle and unmistakable ways that challenge growth, development, and quality of life.

The most serious of these consequences had been his chronically poor weight gain. Nutrition and hydration were persistent battles. Like many NEC survivors, he remained consistently well below the first percentile on growth charts, and the long-term growth trajectory was consistently discouraging. His earlier experiences with gastrointestinal pain and constipation as a result of surgical NEC created a deep-seated oral aversion that shaped a negative relationship with food. Before the g-tube, his refusal to drink worsened his constipation, and standard recommendations such as MiraLAX proved futile without adequate hydration. The vicious cycle of low intake, dehydration, constipation, increased pain, and then further reduced intake continued for years. Multiple severe occurrences resulted in emergency department visits.

Despite the initial challenges with placement and the related hospitalizations, the g-tube was transformative. It immediately improved his weight and growth, but equally important, it eliminated his chronic constipation once we could hydrate him consistently. Over time, this shift fundamentally changed his relationship with food. The removal of the pressure of consuming sufficient calories alleviated the stress of mealtime because the nutritional intake necessary for health and growth could instead simply be provided overnight by tube. The minimized anxiety allowed his experience with food to be positively reframed as something other than painful. Now as a teenager, he no longer requires the g-tube for nutrition, though it is still crucial for hydration and medication administration.

Another persistent but less anticipated consequence has been weakened abdominal muscles and limited core strength. The early laparotomies in the NICU left significant scarring and tissue injury that, in hindsight, deserved far more attention. The long-term impacts of his core weakness remain significant challenges and barriers. These issues compounded with chronic malnutrition, causing gross motor delays that were evident in missed milestones such as sitting, crawling, and walking. He received multiple therapies beginning at NICU discharge onward. When speech therapy began working on oral feeding as a toddler, his core weakness was so pronounced that the therapist had to build a seating support system within his highchair just to hold him properly upright. The goal was to teach him to manipulate food, chew, and swallow with correct alignment, but the weakness of his core prevented him from even having appropriate eating posture to avoid choking. Each therapy discipline (speech, occupational, and physical) had specific goals like these for him to attain, and attaining those goals was the measure of success. Feed himself—goal accomplished. Play with toys while sitting—goal accomplished. Crawl, walk, and so on. Each of the therapists was diligent in working towards meeting these goals in the limited time available each week, but this approach of addressing the exact tasks that he struggled with meant the underlying cause of most of the deficits—poor core support—often went unaddressed. For him, as with anyone recovering from significant abdominal surgery, the muscles themselves needed a reset, the nerve endings were severed, he highly guarded his belly so scar tissue built up and the problem further snowballed. He learned to sit, stand, and walk crooked, in the manner causing the least abdominal pain. So, as his core eventually did strengthen, these compensations became deeply ingrained.

Although his therapists communicated with each other, they were not connected to his medical team. Surgical follow-up from the NEC surgeries occurred in the NICU, long before transitioning to outpatient providers. Effective long-term management depends on coordinated care, but the system is not always structured to encourage this level of collaboration between the various providers and subspecialists involved—many employed by separate and distinct health organizations or hospital systems. It is critical to the success of long-term care for providers, especially pediatric surgery, to recognize that while lifesaving, NEC-related surgical interventions initiate a cascade of events that demand monitoring and follow-up beyond the NICU and operating room. The abdominal incisions that compromise core strength led to musculoskeletal issues and motor delays that extend well into adolescence if not addressed in their early stages. Counsel from surgical teams for families about these risks early on can prepare parents for growth and nutritional challenges, psychological and body image impacts, and strategies for scar healing and skin rejuvenation that will boost quality of life. Inpatient teams can facilitate and advocate for seamless transitions to outpatient care and a multidisciplinary framework as goals shift from acute care to optimization of long-term quality of life.

Our experience is not unique among families with NEC survivors. Through my involvement with the NEC Society, a nonprofit dedicated to NEC, I have seen how much our experience mirrors that of many families navigating the lasting effects of NEC. For our family and many others, NEC is a life-altering diagnosis that does not end at discharge. While the physical and emotional imprint of NEC continues to affect my son now as a teenager, he is thriving in ways that once seemed impossible. He has undergone 10 surgeries directly related to prematurity or NEC. Each one offered lifesaving treatment, but each one also left a mark. Multidisciplinary healthcare teams working collaboratively are uniquely positioned to guide families towards the best outcomes. Focusing only on the immediate crisis or on just one element of the problem is not enough; rather, communicating early and honestly with families about potential long-term complications and how to maximize quality of life by addressing core underlying deficits can turn survival into flourishing, where kids like my son can meet their potential and feel they truly belong.
