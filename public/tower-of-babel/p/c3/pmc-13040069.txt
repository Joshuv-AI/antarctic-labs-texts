An interpretable Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration ensemble approach for predicting rock fragmentation in mine blasting
Authors: Chunping Lin, Xiaolei Sun, Junfeng Mai, Bo Liu, Peng Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRock fragmentation size (X50) is a critical process indicator in mining production, directly influencing the efficiency and cost of downstream operations such as crushing, transportation, and mineral processing. Due to the coupled effects of geological conditions and blast design parameters, X50 exhibits strong nonlinearity and interactive characteristics, making it difficult for traditional empirical or single data-driven models to simultaneously achieve high accuracy, robustness, and interpretability. This study introduces a novel ensemble method termed Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration (CSGC), which integrates six base machine learning models (including KNN, RandomForest (RF), SVR, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), XGBoost, and MLP) using stratified out-of-fold predictions, MAPE-friendly convex optimization, and guarded isotonic calibration. Based on a dataset of 91 field samples from 8 open-pit mines, models were evaluated using an 80/20 train–test split and 5-fold out-of-fold validation. The proposed CSGC framework is compared against individual models and classical stacking. Results show that CSGC achieves superior performance on the test set (R2 = 0.928, RMSE = 0.039), showing better predictive accuracy than the best individual model(XGBoost: R2 = 0.896, RMSE = 0.047). Global and local interpretability analyses via SHAP and LIME identify elastic modulus (E), stemming-to-burden ratio (T/B), bench height-to-burden ratio (H/B), in-situ block size (Xa), and powder factor (Pf) as the dominant factors driving X50, with notable nonlinear and interactive effects across value ranges. The study improves prediction accuracy and interpretability for fragmentation modeling that supports blast optimization and operational control in mining engineering.Keywords: Rock fragmentation prediction, Ensemble learning, Interpretable machine learning, SHAP analysis, Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration (CSGC)Subject terms: Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Rock fragmentation refers to the size distribution of rock fragments formed after blasting, a critical indicator in mining engineering as it directly impacts the efficiency and cost of subsequent operations such as loading, transportation, and crushing1,2. Achieving an optimal fragment size distribution can significantly reduce grinding energy consumption, improve mineral processing recovery rates, and enhance overall mining profitability. Under complex geological conditions and highly variable blasting scenarios, the coupled effects of rock mass structure, explosive properties, and blast design parameters lead to high variability and unpredictability in fragmentation outcomes3. Therefore, developing a high-precision, highly adaptive, and physically consistent fragmentation prediction model is essential for gaining deeper insights into rock-breaking mechanisms under varying conditions and for optimizing blast design strategies.

Empirical models, particularly those based on the Kuznetsov equation and the Kuz–Ram framework, have long been widely used to guide blast design and fragmentation control. Their applicability has been improved through continuous parameter adjustments and scenario-specific calibration across multiple mining sites4–7. For instance, Engin et al.8 integrated digital image processing with the Kuz–Ram model to optimize fragmentation control in bench blasting. Gheibie et al.7 modified Kuz–Ram model parameters for a specific copper mine, proposing an improved fragmentation prediction method. Additionally, Morin and Ficarazzo9 developed a Monte Carlo simulation tool based on the Kuz–Ram model to predict fragment size distribution and assess blast performance uncertainty. While these traditional approaches have enhanced prediction accuracy to some extent, Moomivand and Vandyousefi10 further incorporated variables such as rock mass properties, borehole parameters, and powder factor to develop new empirical models for improved precision. However, empirical models remain limited in parameter coverage, cross-scenario generalization, and their ability to capture nonlinear relationships, often leading to prediction biases under complex conditions and necessitating site-specific recalibration11,12.

Despite progress in identifying the factors governing blast fragmentation through empirical formulas and field measurements, these approaches remain constrained in modern mining practice13. In particular, field tests are costly and difficult to standardize across mines and blasting conditions, which often leads to limited and heterogeneous datasets; meanwhile, classical empirical equations (e.g., Kuznetsov and Kuz–Ram) struggle to represent the nonlinear, multivariable coupling inherent in fragmentation and commonly require site-specific recalibration14. In addition, manual screening and image-based assessment are typically subjective and delayed, making them unsuitable for real-time, full-scale characterization of fragment size distributions. These limitations motivate the adoption of more flexible data-driven modeling strategies.

Accordingly, a growing body of work has explored intelligent prediction models such as support vector machines and neural networks for blast fragmentation forecasting15–22. Subsequent studies have further improved predictive performance by combining learners (e.g., SVR, ANN, GBM, XGBoost) with hyperparameter tuning and metaheuristic optimizers (e.g., grid search, Bayesian optimization, FFA, GWO, PSO, JSO)17,23–31. Nevertheless, several challenges remain: (i) many models are developed for specific sites or narrow operating ranges, limiting generalizability under geologically heterogeneous conditions32; (ii) common “black-box” architectures or fixed-form ensembles provide limited capacity to adaptively capture context-dependent marginal effects; and (iii) interpretability is often insufficient, hindering transparent assessment of feature influence and prediction credibility. Hence, there is a need for fragmentation models that jointly deliver high accuracy, robustness, interpretability, and adaptive learning. Moreover, blast fragmentation datasets are often small and noisy due to the high cost of field measurements, making model training prone to overfitting and performance instability; however, this small-sample challenge is rarely discussed explicitly in existing studies.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes an intelligent modeling approach based on Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration (CSGC), designed to achieve high-precision predictions in complex engineering data environments. This method integrates six structurally complementary base learners (LGBM, XGBoost, RF, KNN, SVR, and MLP) and designs a weight learning mechanism at the ensemble layer based on out-of-fold (OOF) predictions33. It incorporates a MAPE-friendly convex optimization objective and a guarded calibration strategy to dynamically balance the contributions of each base learner. Specifically, the method first generates OOF prediction features using bin-assisted stratified internal folds. It then employs a regularized framework to learn non-negative, normalized weights and performs shrinkage blending with the best single model. Finally, bias correction and guarded isotonic regression are applied to calibrate predictions, significantly enhancing the model’s generalization ability and robustness. Concurrently, this study employs two model interpretability techniques, i.e., SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), to analyze the marginal contributions and influence paths of input features under different blasting conditions from both global and local perspectives. These analyses help identify influential variables and potential nonlinear interaction patterns within the sampled ranges. Unlike many existing studies that rely on unconstrained stacking or metaheuristic tuning mainly for accuracy gains, CSGC introduces reliability-oriented safeguards tailored to small and heterogeneous engineering datasets. Specifically, the ensemble weights are learned as non-negative, normalized (convex) coefficients on stratified OOF predictions, and the final output is further shrunk toward the best single model, which reduces overfitting risk and improves stability. In addition, the guarded calibration module is activated only when OOF self-validation indicates net improvement, preventing calibration-induced degradation. Through these innovative methods, this study aims to improve predictive performance on the present data while offering interpretable insights that may assist parameter screening and decision support in blasting practice; broader validation is still needed to confirm generalizability across sites and operating regimes.

Dataset

Dataset construction

The dataset employed in this study comprises 91 sets of blasting data collected from eight open-pit mines across diverse geographical regions, including Spain, Turkey, Indonesia, and India. The lithology of the rock masses varies widely, encompassing breccia, dolomite, dacite, marl, andesite, limestone, mica schist, and sandstone. In all recorded blasting operations, ammonium nitrate–fuel oil (ANFO) was uniformly used as the explosive, characterized by a moisture content of less than 0.5% and a density ranging from 0.80 to 1.00 g/cm3. The mean fragment size (X50, in meters) of the blasted rock was determined using digital image analysis techniques34–36.

As summarized in Table 1, the dataset includes seven input variables and one output variable, each representing key factors influencing energy transmission and rock fragmentation mechanisms during blasting. The input parameters consist of blast geometry design variables, such as spacing-to-burden ratio (S/B), bench height-to-burden ratio (H/B), burden-to-hole diameter ratio (B/D), and stemming length-to-burden ratio (T/B), along with powder factor (Pf), in-situ block size (Xa), and rock stiffness property (elastic modulus, E). Notably, the elastic modulus E exhibits a broad range of 9.57–60.00 GPa, reflecting diverse geological conditions from soft to medium-hard rock formations. Meanwhile, parameters such as S/B and T/B play critical roles in regulating blast-induced energy distribution and fragmentation extent, illustrating the geometric coupling mechanism between energy release and rock breakage. The output variable, X50, represents the mean blast fragment size (in meters) extracted via digital image analysis, with values ranging from 0.12 to 0.96 m and a mean of 0.3146 m. This range adequately covers various fragmentation levels, from coarse to fine fragments. Despite the broad lithological and geographical coverage, the dataset size is relatively limited given the number of variables considered, which may constrain the representativeness of rare blasting regimes and increase statistical uncertainty. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept on the available multi-mine dataset, and further validation using larger datasets is needed to confirm generalizability across sites.

Methods

Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration

The Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration (CSGC) method is proposed for predictive modeling. This approach employs a two-layer ensemble architecture to systematically combine the predictive capabilities of multiple base learners. It incorporates an L2 regularization shrinkage mechanism and cross-validation-based calibration to enhance generalization performance robustly. The overall framework comprises three core stages: Out-of-Fold (OOF) prediction generation and base model selection, weight learning and hybrid optimization, and prediction calibration with dynamic weighted fusion (Fig. 4).

Stacking regression

In this study, stacking regression is implemented as a benchmark ensemble method. This approach systematically combines the predictive capabilities of multiple base learners through a two-layer architecture, thereby enhancing the overall model’s predictive accuracy and robustness. The framework primarily consists of two stages: base model training with out-of-fold (OOF) feature generation and meta-learner-based prediction integration.

Machine learning base models

To construct a highly diverse and complementary ensemble learning framework, this study selects six representative machine learning regression models as base learners. These include three simple linear baselines, namely ordinary linear regression (OLR), Lasso, and Bayesian Ridge, an instance-based algorithm (KNN), ensemble tree models (RF, LGBM, XGBoost), a kernel-based method (SVR), and a feedforward neural network (MLP). This selection covers a broad spectrum of mainstream modeling paradigms. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a unified framework based on cooperative game theory, providing both global and local interpretability for machine learning models. It quantitatively assesses the contribution of each input feature to the prediction of an individual sample38. The core idea of SHAP originates from the Shapley value concept, which aims to fairly allocate the total prediction output among features by evaluating their marginal contributions across all possible feature subsets. Among the ensemble models, CSGC outperforms all individual base models, demonstrating the effectiveness of the ensemble strategy in enhancing both model stability and predictive accuracy. On the test set, CSGC achieves the best overall performance, with an R² of 0.928, RMSE of 0.039, MAE of 0.032, and a Bias close to zero, indicating no significant systematic deviation and uniformly convergent errors. Specifically, the CSGC method learns non-negative convex weights (summing to 1) at the Out-of-Fold (OOF) level through a MAPE-friendly optimization objective, enabling linear weighted fusion of base learner outputs. This is combined with the best single model via a blending mechanism, supplemented by bias calibration and guarded isotonic regression. Without introducing a secondary meta-learner, this process adaptively determines the fusion ratio based on the data, balancing the bias–variance trade-off and ensuring methodological unbiasedness.

As further observed in the error distribution plot in Fig. 6, CSGC maintains small deviations and error fluctuations across all X50 ranges, with predicted points closely clustered along the diagonal. This error pattern indicates that the model effectively captures both global and local data characteristics, demonstrating strong robustness and generalization, particularly in medium to high fragment size ranges. These attributes underscore its practical value for optimizing blasting design in engineering applications.

In summary, the CSGC model exhibits superior predictive accuracy and stability on the dataset constructed in this study, outperforming not only all individual base learners but also the Stacking ensemble method. Its core advantage lies in the data-driven dynamic convex weight optimization and stratified validation mechanism, which enables flexible adaptation to complex response patterns under varying input conditions. This provides a refined, scalable, and computationally efficient modeling approach for intelligent prediction of blast fragment size.

These results suggest that the contribution of the proposed weighting strategy should be interpreted cautiously. Model interpretation

SHAP interpretation of the CSGC model

To elucidate the mechanism of the CSGC model in predicting blast fragment size (X50), this study employs SHAP for interpretability analysis. Given that the ensemble comprises multiple base learners, the interpretation is conducted at two levels: first, at the base model level, to evaluate the feature importance and directional effects within each model; second, at the ensemble output level, where SHAP values are computed based on the weighted overall predictions to characterize global importance, marginal effects, and potential interactions. This systematic approach reveals the marginal contributions and response patterns of input variables to the prediction outcomes.

Figure 7 presents a comprehensive visualization of SHAP results for the three top-performing base learners in the CSGC model (i.e., XGBoost, LGBM, and RF) including combined beeswarm plots and mean absolute SHAP value bar charts. The beeswarm plots illustrate the positive and negative influences of each input variable on the predicted blast fragment size across different samples, along with their distribution characteristics, while the bar charts quantify the global importance of each variable. The results indicate that the elastic modulus (E) consistently dominates across all base models, with mean absolute SHAP values of 0.063, 0.059, and 0.051 in XGBoost, LGBM, and RF, respectively, indicating that elastic modulus (E) is the primary driver of X50 prediction. in the current dataset and modeling framework. This data-driven ranking is broadly consistent with engineering intuition regarding rock stiffness. This is followed by the stemming length-to-burden ratio (T/B) and bench height-to-burden ratio (H/B), both of which exhibit stable high importance across the models. Furthermore, the beeswarm plots of XGBoost and LightGBM show broader distributions and more pronounced polarities in SHAP values, particularly for E and T/B, indicating stronger responsiveness to varying rock mass conditions and blast designs, and an enhanced ability to capture nonlinear relationships in fragment size evolution. In contrast, the SHAP distributions for RF are more concentrated, with relatively conservative feature interactions, yet the model maintains strong predictive performance through multi-dimensional combinations. This feature importance pattern aligns with the generalization performance ranking of the models in Fig. 7, further validating the effectiveness of the ensemble strategy in leveraging complementary information.

To further clarify the fusion logic and weight allocation mechanism of the CSGC model, Fig. 8 illustrates the weight configuration of base learners, a comprehensive SHAP analysis of the ensemble output, and SHAP decision plots for all samples. As shown in Fig. 8a, XGBoost contributes most significantly to the ensemble, with a mean SHAP value of 0.0906, substantially higher than those of LightGBM and RF, confirming its dominant role in the framework. In contrast, RF is assigned a weight of zero and effectively excluded from the ensemble due to redundant prediction pathways, demonstrating the sparse selection capability of the CSGC approach.

The SHAP analysis in Fig. 8b reveals that the elastic modulus (E) exerts bidirectional influences in most samples. Low values of E (blue) generally correspond to negative SHAP values, significantly reducing the final prediction, while high values (red) strongly drive the blast fragment size upward. This behavior is broadly consistent with engineering expectations and the learned patterns of the model. However, the SHAP-based direction and magnitude of these effects are data-dependent and may vary with the sample distribution and feature interactions. higher rock stiffness enhances resistance to fragmentation, leading to larger fragment sizes, whereas lower stiffness promotes finer fragmentation. Meanwhile, the stemming length-to-burden ratio (T/B) exhibits an overall positive contribution, with increasing values generally elevating the predicted fragment size, possibly reflecting the adverse effect of excessive stemming on energy confinement.

Figure 8c presents the SHAP decision plot for the CSGC model, illustrating how the model accumulates contributions from different features, starting from the baseline output, to approach the final prediction for each task. In samples with higher predicted values (X50 > 0.6 m), E typically provides a positive contribution. When E is low, its contribution is mostly negative, and the model pathways are relatively concentrated, indicating strong robustness and consistency. Conversely, in the low to medium fragment size range (X50 < 0.2 m), the model pathways are more dispersed, suggesting that the ensemble strategy dynamically adjusts weights according to different rock mass combinations, demonstrating strong adaptability to heterogeneity and personalized modeling capability. These differentiated decision pathways further confirm that the CSGC mechanism not only optimizes base learner selection but also explores nonlinear interactions and contextual dependencies at the feature level, providing an interpretable and adaptive ensemble paradigm for complex blast fragment evolution processes.

Interpretation of variable effects

To further elucidate the predictive response mechanisms of the CSGC model under varying rock mass and blasting parameter conditions, particularly the nonlinear moderating effect of the key variable, elastic modulus (E), on blast fragment size prediction, this study conducted explanatory analyses on individual samples based on SHAP dependence plots and local interpretation methods. Figure 9 presents the SHAP dependence plots for each input feature of the CSGC model, illustrating the marginal influence trends of feature value variations on SHAP values and the final predictive output.

SHAP dependence plots for input features in the CSGC model.

As observed in Fig. 9, most input variables exhibit significant nonlinear effects on the model’s predictions. The elastic modulus (E), as the most influential dominant variable, demonstrates a highly nonlinear piecewise response pattern. As E increases from the low range (approximately 10–17 GPa) to moderate levels (around 30–34 GPa), the SHAP values remain within a significantly negative interval, indicating that softer or highly jointed rock masses are more prone to fragmentation refinement, leading to a notable reduction in fragment size. When E exceeds a critical threshold (> 45 GPa), the SHAP values shift to significantly positive, suggesting that rock stiffness becomes the dominant mechanism controlling coarser fragmentation. For E > 60 GPa, the rate of increase in SHAP values slows, implying diminishing marginal returns in predictive gain under extremely high stiffness conditions, potentially due to reduced energy matching efficiency.

The stemming length to burden ratio (T/B) also displays non-monotonic behavior: in the low range (T/B < 0.9), it generally contributes negatively; within the conventional range of 1.0–1.75, SHAP values transition from slightly negative to marginally positive, overall showing a significantly positive trend. This indicates that excessively increasing the stemming ratio constrains explosive gas expansion, consequently increasing the proportion of coarse fragments. When T/B > 3.0, SHAP values remain distinctly positive without decline, suggesting that over-stemming may persistently enhance the energy confinement effect.

The bench height to burden ratio (H/B) exhibits an overall negative regulatory effect. Its SHAP values remain negative after H/B > 2.5, albeit with small magnitude, and continue to be slightly negative even in the H/B > 4.0 range. Physically, this reflects that higher benches, with stronger free face constraints, promote fragmentation refinement, though the marginal effect is limited.

In contrast, the contributions of powder factor (Pf) and in-situ block size (Xa) are more dispersed and exhibit counter-intuitive characteristics. Within the low-to-medium Pf range (0.27–0.55 kg/m³), SHAP values are near zero, with an overall slight positive tendency within a generally negative correlation. When Pf > 0.7 kg/m³, the contribution becomes significantly negative, confirming the role of sufficient energy in promoting fragmentation refinement, while also revealing the engineering reality of insufficient energy leading to coarser fragmentation in the current dataset. For Xa, SHAP values are mostly negative when the inherent block size is < 0.6 m, but become positive for sizes > 1.5 m, indicating that the influence direction of inherent structure size varies in the transition zone due to complex interactions between joint spacing and blasting parameters.

The marginal contributions of the spacing to burden ratio (S/B) and the burden to diameter ratio (B/D) are generally low. Only extreme values of S/B reach − 0.022, confirming their secondary roles within the current blast design parameter ranges.

To further investigate the response mechanisms of the CSGC model across different rock stiffness intervals, representative samples with low (9.57 GPa), medium (16.9 GPa), and high (50 GPa) elastic modulus values were selected. Their SHAP waterfall plots and LIME bar explanation charts are shown in Fig. 10, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the marginal contribution characteristics of input variables in individual prediction tasks. Figure 10a displays the prediction pathway for the low-stiffness sample (E = 9.57 GPa). In the SHAP waterfall plot, T/B contributes + 0.084, acting as the sole significantly positive factor among all variables, leading the model to substantially increase the prediction to 0.356. The contribution of E itself is + 0.023, indicating a weak positive effect on the predicted fragment size in this particular sample. This result appears counter to the conventional expectation that low-stiffness rock tends to fracture more easily. A possible explanation is that, under this sample’s blasting design conditions, the influence of geometric parameters may outweigh the favorable effect of low stiffness on breakage, resulting in a net tendency toward coarser fragmentation. LIME analysis further verifies that T/B > 1.40 contributes + 0.0577, the highest positive value globally, while E ≤ 15.00 GPa also contributes + 0.0351. This indicates that in the low-stiffness range, the model relies more on geometric parameters than rock properties for prediction. Meanwhile, H/B (-0.035) and Pf (-0.0039) serve as negative factors, partially offsetting the enhancing effect of T/B, suggesting that bench confinement and explosive energy play secondary regulatory roles under these conditions.

Local explanation of representative samples under varying elastic modulus (E) conditions: SHAP waterfall plots (left) and LIME bar plots (right) for (a) high, (b) medium, and (c) low stiffness scenarios.

Figure 10b illustrates the intermediate sample with medium stiffness (E = 16.9 GPa). Here, the SHAP contribution of E turns significantly negative (-0.058), becoming the primary factor driving the model to reduce the predicted value to 0.168. This indicates that within the medium stiffness range, the rock’s resistance to fragmentation becomes apparent, significantly restraining fragment coarsening. H/B (-0.029) and Xa (-0.023) also show strong negative contributions, indicating that bench geometry and inherent structure size jointly suppress the formation of coarse fragments. T/B (-0.019) is also negative, reflecting insufficient energy concentration under conventional stemming ratios, thereby enhancing fragmentation. LIME results are highly consistent: the range 15.00 < E ≤ 16.90 contributes − 0.0406, ranking highest among negative factors; Pf and Xa contribute − 0.024 and − 0.015, respectively, validating the stable interpretability of the CSGC model under medium-stiffness conditions.

Figure 10c presents the explanation results for the high-stiffness sample (E = 50 GPa). In the SHAP waterfall plot, E contributes + 0.038, resuming its role as a positive dominant factor and driving the predicted value back to 0.269. This demonstrates that the anti-fragmentation property of high-stiffness rock masses becomes the core mechanism for coarser fragmentation. However, the negative contributions of T/B (-0.029), Xa (-0.020), and H/B (-0.016) significantly attenuate the enhancing effect of E, revealing that even in hard rock, proper blast design (low stemming ratio, high bench ratio) can still promote fragmentation refinement. LIME analysis further confirms that E > 45.00 contributes + 0.0607, acting as the decisive positive factor. Both methods show high consistency regarding the positive influence of E, but differ in the contribution intensity of geometric parameters like H/B, reflecting the varying sensitivities of different interpreters to feature interactions.

In summary, the response mechanism of the CSGC model to input variables exhibits significant differences across elastic modulus intervals: in the low-stiffness stage, predictions are primarily dominated by charge structure (T/B), with rock attributes showing even a weak positive drive; in the medium-stiffness stage, rock stiffness (E) becomes the core negative factor constraining fragment size; in the high-stiffness stage, E resumes positive dominance, but the negative moderating effects of geometric constraint parameters (T/B, H/B) become prominent, shifting the model toward multi-factor coupled control. This self-adaptive switching of response logic, dependent on rock mass conditions, verifies that the CSGC model, through base learner complementarity and sparse selection, achieves segmented accurate capturing of the blast fragmentation evolution mechanism.

Conclusions

This study addresses the complex engineering problem of predicting rock blast fragmentation by proposing an integrated regression framework that combines ensemble learning with interpretability analysis. The CSGC (Convex Stacking with Guarded Calibration) method is developed to systematically leverage the complementary strengths of three base learners, including XGBoost, LGBM, and RF, while SHAP and LIME are employed to interpret the decision-making process of the model. The main findings are summarized as follows:

The proposed CSGC model optimizes sparse weighting at the meta-learning level, achieving an optimal combination of the three base learners. XGBoost attains the highest weight and dominates the ensemble output, whereas RF is assigned a zero weight and excluded due to redundant contributions, demonstrating the model’s capability of dynamic selection. Compared to individual models, the ensemble approach achieves superior prediction accuracy (R² = 0.928), lower errors (RMSE = 0.039, MAE = 0.032), and reduced bias (Bias = -0.014) on the test set, significantly enhancing generalization and robustness under heterogeneous rock mass conditions.

SHAP-based interpretability analysis reveals that within the current dataset and model, the elastic modulus (E) is the most influential variable in predicting X50, followed by geometric design parameters such as the stemming-to-burden ratio (T/B) and bench height-to-burden ratio (H/B). This study is constrained by a relatively small dataset, which may limit the stability of model estimation and the generalizability of the findings. Although the adopted validation strategy was designed to improve robustness, the possibility of site-level information leakage and optimistic performance estimation cannot be fully excluded. Future work should incorporate larger datasets covering more mining sites and operational conditions, together with stricter evaluation protocols, to further verify the robustness and transferability of the proposed framework.
