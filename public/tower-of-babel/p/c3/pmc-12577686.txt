Mechanism-based peroxiredoxin 3 inhibitors exploit a covalent warhead for cancer therapy
Authors: Kimberly J. Nelson, Terrence L. Smalley, Terri Messier, Rajesh Gumpena, Uma Gandhi, Stephanie Milczarek, Aida Habibovic, Hattie Hoffman, Victoria Gibson, Robert J. Hondal, W. Todd Lowther, Brian Cunniff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Covalent inhibitors that are approved and marketed drugs exploit a wide array of warheads and reactions with amino acid side chain–based nucleophiles. Thiostrepton (TS) inhibits the peroxidase activity of the mitochondrial antioxidant protein peroxiredoxin 3 by forming a covalent crosslink between the two active site cysteine residues. Peroxiredoxin 3 inactivation increases reactive oxygen species levels, induces cancer cell death in preclinical models, and shows promise in an ongoing clinical trial for malignant mesothelioma using direct pleural infusion. We report the identification of the minimal fragment of TS that contains tandem dehydro-alanine (DHA) moieties and maintains anticancer activity while losing interactions with three alternative targets of intact TS. Biochemical, kinetic, cellular, and structural studies demonstrate that this fragment is a mechanism-based peroxiredoxin inhibitor. These findings represent a promising start toward a pro-oxidant approach for cancer therapy. Moreover, the data support that the DHA moiety should be added to the covalent warhead arsenal.

INTRODUCTION

The development of covalent inhibitors has reached a zenith with ~30% of all marketed drugs exploiting this mechanism despite historical concerns for irreversibility and potential toxicity (1). Mechanism-based inhibitors exploit binding to the active site. An analysis of the recent patent landscape illustrates the tremendous success of targeted covalent inhibitors (TCIs) that react with noncatalytic cysteine (Cys) residues within kinases and growth factor receptors for cancer therapy and viral proteins (e.g., Bruton tyrosine kinase, fibroblast growth factor receptor, epidermal growth factor receptor, Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 main protease) (2, 3). Both classes of compounds rely upon one or more electrophilic reacting groups within a “warhead” to form a permanent or semi-permanent crosslink to the protein of interest leading to either functional inactivation or blocking of a protein-protein interaction. Cys residues can be targeted by an extensive array of warheads [e.g., α,β-unsaturated amides, α-substituted acrylamides, alkenyl- and alkynl-substituted heteroarenes, and compounds that can undergo nucleophilic aromatic SNAr-based substitution; comprehensively reviewed in (4)]. The more reactive seleno-Cys (Sec) found in some enzymes (e.g., eukaryotic thioredoxin reductases) can also react with these types of warheads. In some situations, reactions with other side chains can also be exploited (Lys, Ser/Thr/Tyr, His, Glu/Asp, and Trp). The study presented here identifies a warhead that can be used to selectively target two Cys resides within the active site of mitochondrial, antioxidant enzyme peroxiredoxin 3 (PRX3) for cancer therapy.

PRX3 is responsible for metabolizing ~90% of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS), specifically hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (5). The GEPIA2 database of matched pairs of patient samples (tumor versus normal) illustrates how PRX3 transcript levels are elevated in mesothelioma, ovarian cancer and many other cancer types (6). Other attractive features of PRX3 as a molecular target include: (i) no mutations in the PRX3 gene known to support resistance development; (ii) PRX3 knockout mice are viable and reach maturity (7, 8); (iii) partial knockdown of PRX3 via shRNA slows mesothelioma tumor cell proliferation (9); and (iv) siRNA knockdown of PRX3 expression slows the growth of SKOV3 ovarian cancer cells, in vitro and in vivo, and enhances sensitivity to cisplatin-induced apoptosis (10).

Inhibition of PRX3 by the thiopeptide antibiotic thiostrepton (TS) leads to an increase in mROS and cell death in cell culture and animal models of malignant mesothelioma (9, 11). The observed efficacy of TS against 403 tumor cells lines derived from a wide array of human tissues supports TS as candidate for further development (12). A formulation of TS has enabled delivery via a pleural catheter, and a phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trial for the treatment of effusions arising from metastatic solid tumors including malignant mesothelioma (NCT05278975) are either in progress or recruiting (13, 14). However, the chemical complexity and insolubility of TS has prevented its intravenous delivery to treat other locations and cancer types. Several other mechanisms have also been proposed for the pharmacological activity of TS: interaction with the forkhead box protein M1 (FOXM1) transcription factor (15), inhibition of the 20/26S proteasome (16), and binding to the large subunit of bacterial and mitochondrial ribosomes (17, 18). Thus, there is a need to determine the structural and chemical components of TS that mediate its interactions with PRX3 and other cellular targets.

TS contains two macrocyclic rings, named rings A and B historically, and five reactive groups that could potentially form Cys adducts: three dehydro-alanines (DHA), one dehydro-threonine (DHT; also called dehydrobutyrine), and one thiazoline (THZ) (Fig. 1A, green boxes). DHA1 and DHA2 are present in tandem at the C-terminus of the molecule. The attachment of a biotin probe to DHA2 enabled the pulldown of FOXM1, the 26S proteosome subunits, and the L11 mitochondrial ribosome protein (15). The alkylation of all Cys residues within FOXM1 did not block binding with the TS affinity probe, supporting the notion that a covalent Cys adduct with FOXM1 does not occur. Structural evidence shows that TS binds directly to the bacterial ribosome through rings A and B (18). Moreover, alkylation and arylation of TS at both DHA1/2 did not affect antibacterial/plasmodial activity (19, 20). Together, these observations support that the A/B rings are the primary determinants for antimicrobial activity (21). An intact A/B ring system is also necessary to inhibit the chymotrypsin- and caspase-like activity of the 20S proteosome (19). In stark contrast, the inhibition of PRX3 by TS occurs through a cysteine-dependent mechanism (11).

TS inhibits PRX3 by irreversibly crosslinking the two catalytic Cys residues (Fig. 1B) (9, 11). During normal catalysis these residues facilitate the reduction of hydrogen peroxide by forming a Cys sulfenic acid intermediate on the peroxidatic Cys residue (CysP108-SOH) in the fully folded (FF) active site. The formation of the subsequent intermolecular disulfide bond with the resolving Cys (CysR229) requires the structure around both Cys residues to locally unfold. This disulfide is reduced by thioredoxin 2 (Trx2). Reaction with TS, however, results in a nonreducible crosslink between these residues (CysP108-S–TS–S-CysR229). Because these residues are located on separate subunits of an obligate homodimer, TS adduction results in a permanent ~45-kDa dimer, which can easily be monitored using a reducing, SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel (9, 11). The specificity of TS for PRX3 over the cytoplasmic isoforms, PRX1 and PRX2, is derived from the unique biochemical and oligomeric properties of PRX3, the pH of the mitochondrial compartment, and the resistance of PRX3 to hyperoxidation (11, 22, 23). Together, these observations fit with the current model for TS adduction where PRX3 collapses to dimer with substoichiometric disulfide formation, resulting in a population of partially reduced dimer that can react with thiostrepton (Fig. 1B). During oxidative stress or inhibition of the reducing system, there is a higher proportion of the partially reduced dimer and increased PRX3-TS crosslink formation (11). The minimal fragment of TS required to react with PRX3 and the order of Cys adduction are not known.

Here, we report the molecular mechanism of PRX3 adduction to the minimal warhead of TS that contains tandem DHA groups, leading to an inhibition of the peroxidase activity, increase in ROS and cell death. The binding mode of the compound promotes reaction at DHA1 first, which contrasts with many studies demonstrating the preference of nucleophiles to react at DHA2. This fragment also does not react with a model small-molecule thiolate, does not inhibit the Sec-containing enzymes TrxR1 and TrxR2, does not block the activity of the known alternative targets of intact TS, and has lost antimicrobial activity. These studies lay a strong foundation for the further development of PRX3 inhibitors as a pro-oxidant therapy for cancer. Moreover, the results described here suggest that adding the DHA moiety to the covalent warhead toolbox is warranted.

RESULTS

Identification of minimal TS fragment required to crosslink and inhibit PRX3

To determine which reactive groups in TS are essential for crosslinking recombinant PRX3 (rPRX3), we used procedures adapted from Nicolaou et al. (24, 25) to synthesize a variety of different fragments containing the DHA, DHT, and THZ moieties (Fig. 1A). Only one of these fragment groups showed reactivity with the engineered dimer of recombinant PRX3 (S139E/A142E; PRX3-EE). This PRX3 construct was previously reported to react with TS more quickly and completely than the wild-type, dodecameric protein (11). WF-097 (Fig. 1C), which contains both DHA1 and DHA2, was able to crosslink PRX3 same as intact TS (Fig. 1D) (9, 11). Derivatives that contain only DHA1 (WF-196), DHA2 (WF-160), or no DHA groups (WF-292) were not able to crosslink PRX3. Similar to TS, CysP108 and CysR229 were both required for WF-097 to crosslink PRX3 (fig. S1A). Although WF-196 and WF-160 did not form crosslinks with PRX3, mass spectrometry indicated that they both formed adducts with PRX3, although WF-196 formed more adducts than WF-160 (fig. S1B). Therefore, both Cys residues and DHA moieties are required for PRX3 crosslinking. More detailed studies were required to dissect the order of Cys residue addition and the site of adduction.

Mechanism of PRX3 covalent adduction by WF-097

The adduction of either catalytic cysteine residue by TS or WF-097 would be expected to inactivate PRX3 peroxidase activity; however, direct evidence that adduction blocks activity has not been reported. Prereduced rPRX3-EE was incubated with WF-097 at pH 8.0. At various times, the peroxidase activity (fig. S2A) was measured by diluting the PRX3–WF-097 mixture into a solution containing Escherichia coli thioredoxin reductase (eTR), E. coli Trx2 (EcTrx2), reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), and H2O2 at pH 7.0 to measure peroxidase activity (Fig. 2A, assay A). WF-097 directly inhibited the peroxidase activity of PRX3 in a time-dependent manner. The loss in activity coincides with the rate of crosslink formation, as determined by SDS-PAGE (~0.04 min−1) (Fig. 2B, assay B). No inhibition of activity was observed if reduced PRX3 was either preincubated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or when WF-097 was added directly to the pH 7.0 assay buffer without preincubation (fig. S2, B and C). These data support that WF-097 inhibits PRX3 through formation of an adduct on one or both catalytic Cys residues and does not inhibit the EcTrx2/TR system.

WF-097 was also reacted with two rPRX3-EE variants to measure single adduct formation with either CysP108 (CysR mutated; C229S) or CysR229 (CysP mutated; C108S). The reactions were quenched at different time points by the addition of 0.2% formic acid, and the appearance of mono adduct was determined by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (Fig. 2C, assay C). The rate of covalent adduction of WF-097 with CysP108 (~0.04 min−1) also correlated with loss in peroxidase activity. The rate of reaction with CysR229 was substantially slower (0.007 min−1) (Fig. 2D). Thus, the reaction of WF-097 with CysP precedes the reaction with CysR. The adduction of CysR229 alone was much slower than the rate of crosslink formation, suggesting that once the adduct forms with CysP, the reaction with CysR proceeds rapidly.

To determine the site of addition on WF-097, single DHA-containing compounds (Fig. 1C) with the two PRX3 Cys variants described above were evaluated. WF-292, a bis-Ala compound, served as the negative control. The rate of adduction (Fig. 2E) was ~22- to 83-fold faster when CysP108 attacked DHA1 (WF-196) versus DHA2 (WF-160). Reaction with CysR229 was ~10-fold lower with DHA1 and was not detectible with DHA2 in this context.

We sought to further verify the site of adduction using x-ray crystallography. Since attempts to crystallize PRX3 cross-linked with WF-097 were unsuccessful, a dimeric, single Cys-containing variant of PRX1 (C71S/C83S/C173S) that had been successfully crystallized in several studies was used (26, 27). As described previously, wild-type PRX1 is predominantly decameric and does not react efficiently with intact TS (11). By using the engineered PRX1 dimer and performing the reaction at mitochondrial pH, adducts with WF-097 were efficiently formed. The 2.30 Å crystal structure (Fig. 2F and table S1) showed that the CysP residue (Cys51 in PRX1) was crosslinked to DHA1 in WF-097. The compound extended across the active site interface between the two PRX subunits and positioned DHA2 where it could be attacked by the CysR residue. In summary, the biochemical, kinetic, and structural data support that the CysP residue attacks DHA1 first, followed by the formation of the CysR-DHA2 adduct, crosslinking the PRX subunits and inactivating the enzyme.

TS-based fragments preserve cytotoxicity, ROS production, and PRX3 crosslinking

The cytotoxic activity of TS versus WF-097, WF-160, WF-196, and WF-292 was tested against the human malignant mesothelioma H-MESO-1 cell line (HM) following 48 hours of incubation (Fig. 3A). WF-097 exhibited a median effective concentration (EC50) value of 4.0 ± 0.4 μM, a value ~4-fold higher than TS (0.91 ± 0.03 μM). In contrast, compounds with substitutions to one or both DHA moieties had no cytotoxic activity. Given the lower potency of WF-097, a small portfolio of additional analogs was synthesized to identify a compound with comparable activity to TS. One such compound, WF-242 (Fig. 3B), which contains an aliphatic appendage and modifications to the six-membered ring, exhibited an EC50 value of 0.86 ± 0.06 μM. The amount of PRX3 crosslinking observed for TS, WF-097, and WF-242 in HM cells (Fig. 3C) increased with dose. Compounds missing a DHA group were not able to crosslink PRX3. The crosslinking of the entire PRX3 population was not required for cell death. The level of ROS production for these compounds also increased with dose and required PRX3 crosslinking (Fig. 3D), consistent with the loss in PRX3 peroxidase activity (Fig. 2D).

Given the differences in potency of the fragment-based compounds, we determined the time dependence of the EC50 values (Fig. 3E). HM cells were exposed to compound for the indicated time, after which compound was removed by washing the cells with fresh media. For all samples, cell viability was measured after 48 hours. While the EC50 values of WF-242 and TS were similar after 24 and 48 hours of treatment, WF-242 was more potent than TS at 3- and 6-hour exposures. WF-097 was less potent than either TS or WF-242 at all time points. This suggests that WF-242 induces cell death more rapidly than either TS or WF-097. Next, the amount of PRX3 crosslink was measured in HM cells at various times after treatment with 5 μM TS or WF-242 (Fig. 3F). WF-242 showed significantly more PRX3 crosslink at 3 and 6 hours than TS, indicating that the increased potency at early time points is associated with faster PRX3 crosslinking in cells.

To evaluate the PRX specificity of the compounds, the amount of crosslinking between PRX subunits was determined upon treating cells with 1 μM compound using antibodies specific for PRX1, PRX2, and PRX3 to probe HM cell lysates following a 24-hour treatment (Fig. 3G). TS showed a significantly higher degree of crosslinking with PRX3 than PRX1 or PRX2, agreeing with our published report (11). WF-242 also exhibited a five- to sixfold increase in crosslinking for PRX3 compared to PRX1 or PRX2. The efficacy of the compounds was also tested in an array of cancer cell lines (table S2). Despite having differences in tissue origin, background mutations, and chemo- and radiation resistance, WF-097 and WF-242 were cytotoxic to the cells at a level comparable to intact TS. Moreover, the immortalized nontumorigenic mesothelial cells (LP9) were approximately two- to threefold less sensitive to both TS and WF-242 than the mesothelioma cell lines (H-MESO-1 and H2373) (fig. S3). Together, these results support that the TS-based fragments recapitulate the broad cancer cell cytotoxicity, preference for PRX crosslinking, and ROS production of intact TS.

TS-based fragments do not react with an activated small-molecule thiol, Trx reductases, and other known TS targets

Given the reactive nature of Cys-targeted warheads, it is important to verify that the warhead does not readily cross react with other thiols and the more reactive Sec in proteins. A common approach to assess this parameter in electrophilic fragment screening is to determine whether the compound reacts with 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoate (TNB−), which contains a fully deprotonated thiolate (4, 28). TS, WF-097, and WF-242 did not react with TNB− (Fig. 4A) when incubated for 22 hours at 37°C, whereas the positive control iodoacetamide (IAM) reacted completely. Thus, both DHA moieties are not highly reactive with a free thiolate.

The PRX3 catalytic cycle relies upon the Trx2-thioredoxin reductase 2 system (Trx2-TR2; also known as TXN2-TXNR2) (Fig. 1B). Mitochondrial TR2 and the cytosolic TR1 both contain Sec within their active sites. To determine whether the tandem DHA warhead reacts with Sec, the activity of TR1 and TR2 was assessed using recombinant proteins, produced using semisynthetic methods to incorporate Sec. Auranofin and IAM were used as positive controls. A cellular TR1/2 assay using the addition of exogenous seleno-cystine to cell lysates was also performed (29, 30). TS and WF-242 (50 μM) did not inhibit either recombinant human TR1 (hTR1) or mouse TR2 (mTR2) (Fig. 4B) under the same conditions that gave 100% inhibition for PRX3 (Fig. 2D). A modest inhibition of global TR1/TR2 did occur in cellular lysates for TS and WF-242 (1 to 2.5 μM) (Fig. 4C), but the interpretation of this result is complicated by the presence of other proteins in the lysate that could be substrates for either NADPH or the TR/Trx systems. Based on these data, the inhibition of TR1 or TR2 is unlikely to contribute significantly to TS- or WF-242–mediated cell death.

The ability of TS, WF-097, and WF-242 to inhibit the chymotrypsin-like activity of the proteasome was tested both in vitro using purified 20S proteasome and in HM cells (Fig. 4D). The MG132 proteasome inhibitor eliminated activity completely in both assays. TS decreased the activity of both the purified 20S proteasome and the cellular proteasome by >70%, consistent with previous reports (19, 31). In contrast, WF-097 and WF-242 were not inhibitory in either assay, even when used at a 10-fold higher concentration (200 μM) than TS in the purified 20S proteasome assay.

To measure how TS, WF-097, and WF-242 affect the DNA binding activity of FOXM1, a nontagged version of the DNA binding domain of FOXM1 (residues 222 to 360, FOXM1-DBD) was purified. A previously published fluorescence polarization assay was used with minor buffer modifications to measure the binding of FOXM1-DBD to a fluorescently labeled 16–base pair (bp) double-stranded DNA fragment containing a repeat of the FOXM1 recognition motif (see Materials and Methods for details) (32). In this assay, the KD (dissociation constant) of the untagged FOXM1-DBD (0.23 ± 0.06 μM; fig. S4) was comparable to the KD value reported for the GST-tagged FOXM1-DBD fusion construct (0.22 ± 0.03 μM) (32). The addition of a zwitterionic detergent, NDSB-256, was required to solubilize TS and resulted in a slight increase in the KD for FOXM1 (0.62 ± 0.07 μM). As previously reported, preincubation of FOXM1-DBD with 200 μM TS resulted in a decrease in FOXM1-DNA binding (Fig. 4E) (15, 32). No inhibition of DNA binding was observed for either WF-097 or WF-242 at the same concentration.

The antimicrobial activity of TS, WF-097, and WF-242 was tested against Enterococcus hirae [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), #10541], the organism used to measure TS potency according to United States Pharmacopeia reference protocol #81 (33). Cultures of E. hirae were treated with DMSO control or various concentrations of TS to determine the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) required to block all growth. The MIC for TS was 0.045 μM (Fig. 4F), a value similar to those published for TS against several species of Enterococcus (20, 21). In contrast, no inhibition of growth was seen with 20 μM WF-097 or WF-242. In summary, the TS-based fragments contain a tandem DHA warhead that retains the ability to crosslink key Cys residues of PRX3 and to kill cancer cells but have lost the ability to inhibit the other likely targets of TS. Moreover, WF-242 represents a promising start toward the development of compounds that inhibit PRX3 for cancer therapy.

DISCUSSION

The literature is replete with examples of the overexpression and metabolic importance of the human peroxiredoxins, PRX1-6, in cancer cells from a variety of tissues (34–36). The up-regulation of the PRXs occurs to manage the aberrant, high levels of ROS. Inhibiting PRX activity represents a pro-oxidant strategy to kill cancer cells, as these cells typically cannot compensate for a further increase in ROS. The mitochondrial isoform, PRX3, has been the focus of our team’s work, with the identification of TS as a selective inhibitor and the ability to increase mROS levels, to initiate cell death and to kill malignant mesothelioma cells in cellular and mouse xenograft models (9, 37). The unique biochemical and structural features, mitochondrial location, and specificity of TS for PRX3 indicate that targeting this specific PRX isoform is both desirable and feasible (11, 22, 23).

The recent development of a formulation to solubilize and deliver TS via a catheter has allowed for clinical trials to assess efficacy in humans against HM and other cancers with pleural effusions (13, 14). While this strategy is promising for these patients, the insolubility and chemical complexity of TS currently limits its intravenous use to treat other cancer types and locations. The goal of this study was to move toward the development of a small-molecule PRX3 inhibitor by identifying the minimal fragment of TS that would recapitulate PRX3 inhibition and cytotoxic activity against cancer cells. A key secondary goal was to decouple the anticancer activity of TS from its ability to inhibit the FOXM1 transcription factor DNA binding and proteasome activity and to function as an antibiotic (15–18, 38).

This study demonstrated that the two tandem DHA moieties (DHA1 and DHA2) of TS (Fig. 1A) react with the active site Cys residues of PRX3 to form a permanent covalent crosslink and to inactivate the peroxidase activity. Kinetic and mass spectrometry studies, using a panel of PRX3 Cys variants and analogs with DHA1 and DHA2 removed, determined the order of addition (Fig. 2, D and E). The CP108 residue reacts with DHA1 first, and structural studies confirmed the CysP-DHA1 adduct (Fig. 2F). Given that the CysP residue is located at the bottom of the active site pocket (Fig. 1B), it makes sense that only the DHA1 residue would be able to react. DHA2 is then positioned outward toward solvent to react with the CR residue to form the second crosslink.

It is important to point out here that this enzyme-catalyzed reaction at DHA1 contrasts with the literature that has focused on trying to improve the solubility of TS by a variety of chemical modifications. While modification at DHA2 is more likely to occur because of its activation by the adjacent thiazole moiety, catalysts are still required. For example, Rh-catalyzed arylation, photoredox-catalyzed modifications, Diels-Alder–facilitated cycloadditions, and light-initiated 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions with tetrazoles can be used to modify DHA2 (15, 20, 39–41). A cobalt(III)-catalyzed C-H amidation reaction is the only study found that modifies DHA1 (42). These observations and the lack of reaction of TNB− with WF-097 and WF-242 (Fig. 4A) support that DHA1 and DHA2 are not intrinsically reactive. The placement of the DHA1 moiety into the correct position within the PRX3 active site, however, enables reaction with the CP residue, which is expected to have a lower pKa (where Ka is the acid dissociation constant) value based on measurements with homologous proteins (43, 44).

The TS-based fragments, WF-097 and WF-242, have comparable cytotoxicity to intact TS in HM cells at 48 hours (Fig. 3A). These minimal fragments require both DHA1 and DHA2 to crosslink PRX3 in HM cells (Fig. 3C). Crosslinking of PRX3 is also required to induce the production of ROS (Fig. 3D). WF-242 reaches maximal potency within 3 to 6 hours of exposure while TS requires 24 to 48 hours (Fig. 3E). WF-242 forms the crosslink faster than intact TS (Fig. 3F), consistent with its cytotoxicity with shorter exposure. WF-242 also prefers to react with PRX3 in HM cells (Fig. 3G). One of the most important findings of this study was the identification of the minimal fragment of TS that would kill HM cells but not inhibit the known alternative targets of TS (15–18, 38). Moreover, WF-097 and WF-242 do not react with an activated thiol, Sec-containing proteins; inhibit proteasome activity; inhibit FOXM1 transcription factor DNA binding; and do not have antimicrobial activity (Fig. 4). Together, these data support the concept that the WF-242 scaffold could be modified to further enhance crosslinking efficiency and specificity for PRX3.

The detailed molecular mechanism determined for the reaction of TS, WF-097, and WF-242 with PRX3 contrasts with the available literature on other PRX inhibitors. A summary of known PRX inhibitors, the type of interaction with the PRX molecule, and the available experimental evidence is summarized in table S2. Most of these compounds react with human PRX1 and/or PRX2. The only other PRX3 inhibitor identified to date is spilanthol endoperoxide, but the mechanistic details have not been determined (45). Conoidin A can crosslink the active site cysteines of Ancylostoma ceylanicum PRX1 and adduct the CysP of Toxoplasma gondii PRX2; this compound has not been shown to interact with PRX3 (46, 47). Even in cases where data are present for the interaction of these compounds with PRX1 or PRX2, there are notable differences in the mechanistic details. Many inhibitors (Adenanthin, Parvifoline AA, SK053, Frenolicin B, Celastrol and Ainsliadimer A) have been identified that can form a covalent adduct with the CysR residue in PRX1 and/or PRX2 (48–53). This observation is consistent from a Cys accessibility point of view; the CysR residue is typically more solvent exposed than the CysP residue, which is present within an activating and protected environment (Fig. 1B) (54). 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid, an analog of Celastrol, was shown to interact with the CysP residue of PRX1/2 (55). The discrepancy between these studies is unresolved. BNP7787 can adduct both the CysP and CysR residues of PRX1 and PRX4 (56); however, it is a disulfide containing compound such as oxidized glutathione, so any adduction would be easily reduced by cellular reductants including Trx, glutaredoxins, free Cys, and glutathione. Several noncovalent inhibitors (Piercidin A, H7, AI-44, and JDA-202) have also been identified for PRX1 using a variety of cell-based, analytical methods and modeling, but these remain to be validated in mechanistic detail (57–60). Thus, the TS-based, small-molecule fragment identified and characterized here represent the first-in-class, mechanism-based inhibitors for any peroxiredoxin.

In summary, the minimal fragment of TS can function as a PRX3 inhibitor to increase ROS and to initiate cancer cell death. This minimal fragment does not block the activity of the alternative targets of intact TS. Detailed mechanistic studies have determined the order and site of addition, which contrast with all known PRX inhibitors. Moreover, future modifications to the scaffold could further enhance reactivity and specificity. WF-242 represents a promising start toward the development of tandem, DHA-containing small-molecule inhibitor to target PRX3 for the treatment of cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Expression and purification of PRX3 variants

An engineered, dimeric form of His-tagged PRX3 (PRX3-EE) was expressed from the pET15b vector, purified from BL21 Gold (DE3) E. coli cells, and the His-tag was removed, as previously described (9). Individual cysteine mutations to the engineered PRX3 dimer (PRX3-S139E/A142E/CP108S and PRX3-S139E/A142E/CR229S) were generated by GenScript using the same expression vector. The purification protocol was the same for the CP108S variant, but the CR229S variant required the addition of 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to all chromatography, dialysis, and storage buffers. Protein aliquots for this protein and the others described below were flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use.

Purification of thioredoxin reducing system proteins

eTrx2 (the trxC gene product) and eTR were expressed and purified as previously described (11, 61). mTR2 was expressed from the pET15b vector in BL21 Gold (DE3) cells that were grown at 37°C in two 12 liter of fermenters until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.8 and induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl 1-thio-β-d-galactopyranoside at 18°C for 18 to 20 hours (62). Note that this form mTR2 contains a Cys instead of a seleno-Cys in the active site; nonetheless, this form was sufficiently active for the assays described below. The cells were lysed in 100 ml of affinity buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.9) and 500 mM KCl] supplemented with 1 to 2 mg of flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), 10% glycerol, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 0.1 mM benzamidine using an Emulsiflex C5 homogenizer (Avestin Inc.). The mTR2-containing fractions, eluted from a HisPur cobalt affinity resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), were pooled, and 1 mg of HRV-3C protease was added for every for every 15 mg of eTR2 and incubated overnight at 4°C during dialysis into buffer [20 mM Hepes and 100 mM NaCl (pH 7.5)]. The protein was further purified by further purified by Q-Sepharose FF and Superdex 200 columns (both GE Healthcare). The final storage buffer was 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) and 100 mM NaCl. The codon-optimized human Trx2 gene (residues 84 to 271) was subcloned into the pET15b vector containing a cleavable His-tag. The protein was purified, and the His-tag removed as described for mTR2 above except that the Q-Sepharose column was omitted.

Purification of DNA binding domain of FOXM1

The DNA binding domain of FoxM1C (FoxM1-DBD; residues 222 to 360) containing an N-terminal 6His-tag and an intervening HRV-3C cleavage site was expressed using the pET28b vector within BL21 Gold (DE3) cells. Cells were grown at 37°C in a 12 liters of fermenter until the OD600 = 0.8 and induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl 1-thio-β-d-galactopyranoside at 18°C for 18 to 20 hours. The same purification strategy and removal of the His-tag was used as for mTR2 above, but no FAD was added.

Multiple turnover PRX3 crosslinking assay

PRX3 crosslinking in fig. S1 was measured in the multiturnover assay described by Nelson et al. (63) with 100 μM PRX3-EE, 5 μM E. coli TRX2, 0.5 μM E. coli TR, and a NADPH regenerating system composed of 3.2 mM glucose 6-phosphate, glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (3.2 U/ml), and 0.4 mM NADPH. A modified version of this reaction (Fig. 1D) used the mammalian mitochondrial reducing system in which the E. coli proteins were replaced with 50 μM hTRX2 and 0.5 μM mTR2. Samples were incubated at 37°C with either 0.20 mM compound or an equivalent volume of DMSO (≤1% final). Assay components were pulsed with successive additions of 50 μM H2O2 to induce turnover of PRX3 and incubated at 37°C for 10 to 15 min between H2O2 additions. These pulses were used to minimize the hyperoxidation of the PRX protein. Samples were stored at −20°C until analyzed. Samples were diluted with 5X SDS loading buffer containing 100 mM DTT and heated to 100°C for 10 min, and the proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and stained for total protein using GelCode Blue (Life Technologies).

Inhibition of PRX3 peroxidase activity (assay A)

PRX3-EE was reduced with 10 mM DTT for 30 min at room temperature, and excess DTT was removed by buffer exchange into 50 mM tris (pH 8.0) [note that high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade water was used to minimize PRX3 oxidation] using a BioGel P6 spin column (Bio-Rad). PRX3-EE (20 μM) was incubated with 50 μM WF-097 at pH 8.0 at 37°C for the indicated times. The mixture was then diluted 20-fold into a solution containing eTrx2, eTR, NADPH, and H2O2 at pH 7.0 to measure the peroxidase activity at 25°C [final assay: PRX3 (1 μM), WF-097 (5 μM), eTrx2 (5 μM), eTR (0.2 μM), NADPH (150 μM), H2O2 (100 μM), and 50 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7) (n = 3)]. The background signal in the absence of PRX3 was subtracted from all samples. The results were fit to a one phase association nonlinear regression model with GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 software.

Single turnover PRX3 crosslinking assay (assay B)

Reduced PRX3-EE (20 μM) was incubated with 50 μM WF-097 at 37°C in 50 mM tris (pH 8.0) (note that HPLC grade water was used to minimize PRX3 oxidation). At each time point (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 90, 120, 180, and 240 min), a fraction (8 μl) of the reaction was quenched by the addition of 8 μl of 5x SDS loading buffer containing 200 mM NEM. Samples were left at room temperature for 10 min and then stored on ice. When the time course was complete, samples were reduced by the addition of 58 mM DTT and heated to 100°C for 10 min, and the proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and stained for total protein using GelCode Blue (Life Technologies). The intensity of the nonreducible PRX dimer band was quantified with ImageJ2 version 2.14.0 and divided by the summed intensity of all PRX bands within each lane to determine the % crosslink. Results are presented as means ± SD from three independent replicates. Data were plotted and fit to a one phase association nonlinear regression model with GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 software.

Single turnover assay to measure the rate of single adduct formation (assay C)

PRX3-EE C229S and PRX3-EE-C108S were prereduced with 100 mM DTT for 30 min at room temperature, and excess DTT was removed by buffer exchange with a 100 μl of BioGel P6 column equilibrated in 25 mM ammonium acetate (pH 8.3) made with HPLC grade water to minimize oxidation of PRX3. Reduced PRX3 (20 μM) was incubated with each analog (200 μM) at 37°C. At each time point, a fraction (8 μl) of the reaction was quenched by the addition of 4 μl of 0.2% formic acid. Each sample was then mixed 1:1 with a sinapinic acid matrix solution [sinapinic acid (20 mg/ml), 50% acetonitrile, and 0.1% formic acid in water]. Samples were analyzed on a Bruker Autoflex MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer in positive ion and linear acquisition mode. The intensity of the monomer SH peak and the analog monoadduct peak were determined and corrected for background signal at the adduct peak in the DMSO control. The fraction of single analog adduct was determined by dividing the intensity of the adduct peak by the summed intensity of the SH and adduct peaks. Results are presented as mean values ± SD from a minimum of three independent replicates. The results were fit to a one phase association nonlinear regression model with GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 software.

Structure determination of the PRX1-WF-097 monoadduct by x-ray crystallography

PRX1-C71S/C83S/C173S (PRX1-C3S) was expressed and purified as previously described and stored with 1 mM DTT (26). This PRX1 variant has all Cys residues mutated to Ala except for CysP52. The protein sample was desalted to remove the DTT using BioGel P6 column and then mixed with a 50 mM stock solution of WF-097 in DMSO to give the following final concentrations: 100 μM PRX1-C71S/C83E/C173S, 200 μM WF-097, 20 mM Hepes (pH 8), 100 mM NaCl, and 0.4% DMSO. The compound was allowed to react for 5 hours at 23°C, followed by an additional 18 hours at 4°C. Excess compound was removed by running the protein back over a Superdex 75 (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with the same buffer. MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry analysis of the final sample indicated that >95% of the protein had reacted with WF-097 to form a monoadduct. Crystals of the Prx1-C3S-WF97 complex were grown by mixing the complex (16.6 mg/ml) with 0.1 M Hepes (pH 7.3), 1.8 M ammonium sulfate, 2% (v/v) polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 1000 using the sitting drop method (2:1 volume ratio) at 291 K. The crystal was cryo-protected with mother liquor supplemented with 30% ethylene glycol at room temperature and flash-frozen directly using a 100 K nitrogen stream. Data were collected at 100 K on a rotating anode home x-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Micromax 007 X-ray source with VariMax-HF Confocal Optic System coupled to Pilatus3M detector) operated at 40 kV and 20 mA. The data were integrated and scaled with HKL-3000. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using previously published coordinates of PRX1 [Protein Data Bank (PDB) code 7LJ1] as search model (64). The search model was prepared by removing all nonprotein molecules and the active site residues. The model of the complex was manually rebuilt in COOT and refined with REFMAC (65, 66). Model validation was performed with PROCHECK and MOLPROBITY (67, 68). Details of data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in table S1. Figures were prepared with PyMOL (Shrödinger).

Cells and cell culture

The malignant mesothelioma cell lines H-MESO-1 (HM) and H2373 and normal immortalized nontumorigenic mesothelial cells (LP9) were cultured as previously described (9, 69). The STR profile of the line was validated by the Vermont Integrative Genomics Resource DNA Facility, supported by University of Vermont Cancer Center, Lake Champlain Cancer Research Organization, and the UVM Larner College of Medicine. The STR profiles matched previously annotated DNA fingerprints (69).

Cell viability assays

Cells were plated in 96-well plates (Corning, Kennebunk, ME) at a density of 2500 cells per well. The following day, cells were treated with test compounds diluted from a 20 mM stock in DMSO into complete media followed by incubation for 48 hours. Postincubation cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Corning cellgro, Manassas, VA), fixed with 3.0% formaldehyde (Fisher BioReagents, Fair Lawn, NJ) in PBS, and stained for 30 min with 0.1% crystal violet (Acros Organics, Fair Lawn, NJ) in water. Crystal violet stain was removed, and the plates were washed with H2O and allowed to dry. To quantify cell viability, plates were imaged using the Lionheart Plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT) and/or analyzed by absorbance at 540 nm (crystal violet dye dissolved in 100% methanol) using the Synergy HTX plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT). To determine the EC50 of test compounds the data were plotted using a four-parameter nonlinear regression model using GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Immunoblotting

Cells were plated in six-well plates at a density of 200,000 cells per well. After 24 hours, cells were treated with the indicated compounds as described in the main text. Cell lysates were harvested at indicated time points using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer [50 mM tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS, in deionized (DI) water] for reducing samples to be analyzed by reducing SDS-PAGE. Protein concentrations were determined via Bradford Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). Lysates (15 μg protein per well) were resolved by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions on 4 to 12% gradient Bis-Tris Midi gel (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) at constant 200 V for 50 m. The gel was transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane at constant 1 A for 50 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) diluted in 1× tris-buffered saline with 1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for a minimum of 1 hour, and incubated with PRX1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), PRX2 (1:1000; AbFrontier), or PRX3 (1:1000; AbFrontier), in 5% BSA TBS-T at 4°C overnight. The membrane was washed with 1× TBS-T for 1 hour, incubated with appropriate secondary antibody for 1 hour, and washed again with 1X TBS-T for 1 hour. Membranes were incubated with ECL Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) and visualized using a GE Amersham Imager chemiluminescent detection system. Blots were washed with 1× TBS-T and reprobed with actin (Invitrogen) to verify equal protein loading. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ (NIH). Results are presented as mean values ± SD from a minimum of five independent replicates. Data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey HSD post hoc correction using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1.

Measurement of H2O2 production in cells

The ROS-Glo-H2O2 assay was conducted per the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega). Briefly, H-MESO-1 cells were plated at 2500 cells per well in a 96-well tissue culture plate in complete media. The following day, the indicated concentrations of TS or WF-fragments were added to the cells. After 1 hour of incubation, the H2O2 substrate was added to each well. After 6 hours of incubation the ROS-Glo detection reagent was added to each well and incubated for 20 min. Equal volumes were transferred to a white 96-well plate, and chemiluminescence was read on at BioTek Synergy plate reader. Results are presented as mean values ± SD from a minimum of three independent replicates. Data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey HSD post hoc correction using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1.

TNB− reactivity assay

TNB− was prepared by treating 25 μM 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic (DTNB; Ellman’s Reagent) with 200 μM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) in 20 mM Hepes (pH 8.0) and 100 mM NaCl for 10 min at room temperature. This treatment yielded a 50 μM stock solution of TNB−. Each compound (200 μM) was added to the TNB− solution and incubated for 22 hours at 37°C. To measure the remaining free TNB− in solution, 200 μl of each solution was diluted with 200 μl of the same buffer and the absorbance read at 412 nm (e = 14,150 M−1 cm−1).

Recombinant thioredoxin reductase inhibition assay

Sec-containing hTR1 and mTR2 were prepared using a semisynthetic method, in which a C-terminal intein-fusion was ligated with a tripeptide containing Sec (29). hTR1 or mTR2 (50 nM) was reduced with 2 μM NADPH in 50 mM tris (pH 8) and BSA (0.1 mg/ml). Samples were incubated with DMSO, 50 μM TS, 50 μM WF-097, or 50 μM WF-097 for 1 hour at pH 8.0 and 37°C. The mixture was then diluted 20-fold into a solution containing NADPH and DTNB at pH 7.0 at 25°C. The rate of reduction of DTNB was measured as an increase at 415, nm and the concentration of TNB− was calculated using an ε415 = 14,140 M−1 cm−1. The final assay contained: TR1 or TR2 (5 nM), TS, WF-097 or WF-242 (2.5 μM), NADPH (200 μM), and DTNB (1 mM). The background signal in the absence of TR was subtracted from all samples. Values within each set were normalized to the maximum slope within that set. Significant differences (n = 3) were determined by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. ****P value < 0.0001 and **P value < 0.01.

Cellular thioredoxin reductase inhibition assay

H-MESO-1 cells were treated for 24 hours with either DMSO, 3 μM auranofin, or 1 to 2.5 μM conc compound (TS, WF-097, and WF-242), and cell lysates prepared on ice as previously reported (30). The assay (200 μl) contained 10 to 30 μg of protein, 100 to 200 μM seleno-cystine, 200 μM NADPH in 100 mM potassium phosphate, and 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.0) buffer. The consumption of NADPH was monitored at 340 nm for 2 min. For each sample, the NADPH consumption in the absence of seleno-cystine substrate was measured, and this value was subtracted from the experimental sample. Three replicates were performed for each treatment, and the specific activity was calculated as (nanomolar NADPH reduced/min/microgram of cellular protein).

Purified 20S proteasome assay

Human 20S proteasome (1.3 nM; Boston Biochem) was incubated with DMSO, 10 μM MG132 (positive control for proteasome inhibition), 20 μM TS, 200 μM WF-097, or 200 μM WF-242 at 37°C. After 1 hour, the analog/proteasome mix was incubated with substrate and luciferase detection reagent, and the proteosome activity was measured using the Proteasome Glo Chymotrypsin-Like Assay Kit (Promega). Background (blank) values were averaged and subtracted from each reading, and the luminescence of each sample was normalized to the luminescence of the untreated control. Results are presented as mean values ± SD from nine independent replicates. Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1.

Cellular proteasome assay

HMESO cells were trypsinized, washed once with complete supplemented media and once with PBS, and then resuspended in complete supplemented media to eliminate interference of trypsin activity in the assay. HMESO cells were plated at a density of 2500 cells per well in 96-well plate and left to adhere and grow for 24 hours. Cells were treated in triplicate as blank (no cells with media and vehicle DMSO), no treatment (cells with media and vehicle DMSO), or 10 μM each of MG132, TS, WF-097, WF-242, or WF-292. At 5 to 6 hours posttreatment, Promega Proteasome-Glo Assay Chymotrypsin-Like Cell-Based Assay (catalog no. G8660) was performed, and luminescence was read on Biotek plate reader. Background (blank) values were averaged and subtracted from each reading. Results are presented as mean values ± SD from six independent replicates. Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with a Tukey HSD post hoc correction using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1.

Inhibition of FOXM1 DNA binding by fluorescence polarization

To measure the KD of FOXM1 binding, serial dilutions of FoxM1-DBD (0.14 to 38 μM) were incubated for 20 min at ambient temperature with 20 nM fluorescein-labeled dsDNA (16 bp) containing repeats of the FOXM1 recognition sequence (5′-AAACAAACAAACAATC-3′) and 60 nM nonspecific unlabeled dsDNA. Fluorescence polarization (FP) was measured with λex = 470 nm and λem = 530 nm in FP buffer [20 mM MES (pH 6.5), 0.2 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 2% glycerol, and 0.2 mM DTT] with and without 62.5 mM NDSB-256. Polarization values (PObs) were corrected for the polarization with DNA alone (P0). Values were fit to a one site specific binding model in GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 (fig. S4). To measure inhibition of FOXM1 DNA binding, a 20 mM stock of TS, WF-097, or WF-242 was made in DMSO and diluted to 200 μM into FP buffer containing 0.47 μM FOXM1-DBD (final 1% DMSO). After 30-min preincubation with inhibitor, 20 nM fluorescent DNA was added, and FP was measured as described above. All values were compared to a 1% DMSO control, and the FP of DNA incubated with each compound did not differ from the FP of DNA alone. Results are presented as mean values ± SD from a minimum of none independent replicates. Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with a Tukey HSD post hoc correction using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1.

Antimicrobial activity

To determine the MIC of intact TS with E. hirae (ATCC, #10541), a frozen stock of E. hirae was streaked onto a plate containing brain heart infusion (BHI) growth media [brain extract (7.8 g/liter), dextrose (2.0 g/liter), disodium phosphate (2.5 g/liter), heart extract (9.7 g/liter), proteose peptone (10 g/liter), sodium chloride (5 g/liter) supplemented with 0.01% (v/v) polysorbate 80, dextrose (10 g/ml), and agar (10 g/liter). The plated E. hirae was used to inoculate 10 ml of media 41 [described in USP37 <81>: pancreatic digest of casein (9 g/liter), yeast extract (5 g/liter), dextrose (20 g/liter), sodium citrate (10 g/liter), monobasic potassium phosphate (1 g/liter), and dibasic potassium phosphate (1 g/liter)] and grown overnight at 37°C (33). The overnight growth was diluted into growth media to make a single solution of E. hirae with a final optical density at 600 nm = 0.02 arbitrary units (AU). A 20 mM stock solution of TS in DMSO was made and used to make multiple dilutions of TS in DMSO. Each TS dilution (0.1 ml) was then added to a test tube containing 10 ml of the diluted E. hirae and grown at 37°C overnight while shaking at 180 rpm. The next morning, each tube was visually inspected to determine whether the cells grew. Final concentrations tested were: 0.120, 0.090, 0.075, 0.060, 0.045, 0.030, and 0.015 μM TS. Complete growth (same as DMSO-negative control) was observed at TS concentrations ≤0.030 μM, and no growth was observed with all TS concentrations ≥0.045 μM (same as 20 μM TS-positive control). Three replicate cultures were made on three separate days (n = 9). No intermediate growth was observed, and all replicates for each treatment concentration showed the same growth profile. For WF-097 and WF-242, 20 mM stock in DMSO was added to the dilute E. hirae (20 μM final concentration of each analog) (n = 9). No growth inhibition was observed for any analog. All treated samples either grew to the same density as the DMSO control or did not grow at all.

High-resolution mass spectrometry of WF-compounds

The exact mass of each compound was measured by LC-MS using an Accela Open UPLC coupled to a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap LTQ XL high-resolution mass spectrometer. A 20 mM stock of each compound was made in DMSO and diluted to 0.2 mM in chloroform. Separations were achieved by injecting 2-μl sample onto a Thermo Hypersil GOLD C18 column (250 mm by 2.6 mm, 5-μm particle size) at ambient temperature with buffer A [0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water] and buffer B [acetonitrile with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid] at a flow rate of 200 μl/min. Compounds were separated by washing for 4 min with 30% B, followed by a gradient elution of 30 to 98% (v/v) B over 16 min, followed by a 3-min wash at 98% B. The gradient was then decreased to 30% B over 3 min and held at 30% B for 5 min for a total run time of 31 min. Eluant was introduced to the mass spectrometer via positive electrospray ionization with the following settings: sheath gas of 60, source voltage of 5.0 kV, capillary temperature of 275°C, capillary voltage of 35 V, and tube lens held at 110 V. The mass spectrometer was operated using Xcalibur v1.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in full scan mode (100 to 1400 mass/charge ratio range) at high resolution (30,000).
