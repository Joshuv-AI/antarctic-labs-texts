Fermented palm kernel meal modulates gut barrier and inflammatory gene via short-chain fatty acid production in broiler chickens
Authors: Dimas Fakhruddin, Nida Adzilah Auliani, Erlambang Ajidarma, Ken Dwiba Amarakamini, Ahmad Alfarobi Jauharul Ilmi, Mochammad Fahmi Habibi, Chusnul Hanim, Muhlisin Muhlisin, Asih Kurniawati, Muhsin Al Anas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of dietary inclusion of fermented palm kernel meal (FPKM), produced using Limosilactobacillus fermentum BN21, on growth performance, jejunal histomorphology, cecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, and the expression of genes related to intestinal barrier function and inflammation in broiler chickens. A total of 320-day-old male broilers were randomly allocated to five dietary treatments containing 0, 25, 50, 75, or 100 g/kg FPKM. Each treatment consisted of eight replicate pens with eight birds per pen, and the pen was considered the experimental unit. Experimental diets were fed from day 11 to 35, covering the grower (days 11–21) and finisher (days 22–35) phases. Dietary inclusion of FPKM up to 75 g/kg did not impair growth performance, whereas 100 g/kg FPKM significantly reduced weight gain and increased feed conversion ratio (P< 0.05). The villus height-to-crypt depth (VH:CD) ratio was significantly higher in the 25 and 50 g/kg FPKM groups, although villus height and crypt depth were unchanged. Cecal concentrations of acetate, propionate, butyrate, and total SCFAs increased linearly with FPKM levels (P< 0.05). Supplementation with FPKM also downregulated claudin-1 (CLDN-1) and upregulated zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (P< 0.05). Expression of interleukin-18 (IL-18) decreased, whereas interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) were upregulated (P< 0.001). These findings indicate that dietary inclusion of 50 g/kg FPKM fermented by L. fermentum BN21 improves intestinal health by enhancing SCFA production, intestinal morphology, and immune-related gene expression without compromising growth performance in broilers.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Palm kernel meal, Fermentation, Gut health, Sustainable feed

Introduction

The global demand for animal-based protein continues to increase alongside population growth. Poultry meat consumption is projected to rise by 15 % by 2032, accounting for approximately 41 % of total global meat consumption and surpassing pork, beef, and lamb (FAO, 2023). In Indonesia, broiler meat production reached 3.84 million metric tonnes in 2024, highlighting its strategic role as a major protein source (BPS, 2024a). This growing demand intensifies the need for efficient and affordable feed resources, as feed represents the largest cost component in poultry production (Makkar et al., 2014). However, rising feed costs and heavy dependence on imported ingredients remain major constraints for feed formulation. In 2024 alone, Indonesia imported approximately 2.68 million metric tonnes of soybeans, valued at around USD 1.4 billion, thereby increasing production costs and exposing the feed industry to supply risks (BPS, 2024b). Soybean meals remain the primary protein source in poultry diets; however, its high price and strong dependence on imports create economic challenges for feed formulation. Consequently, locally available by-products such as palm kernel meal (PKM) are being explored as complementary ingredients to partially reduce dependence on imported feedstuffs (Blair et al., 2025; Dairo and Fasuyi, 2008; Ojewola and Ozuo, 2006).

Palm kernel meal, a by-product of the palm oil industry, represents a promising locally available alternative feed ingredient. Indonesia, the world’s leading palm oil producer, generates substantial quantities of PKM, with annual production estimated at 6.3–6.6 million metric tonnes (USDA, 2025). This abundant supply highlights its potential as a cost-effective feed resource, particularly in tropical regions. Palm kernel meal contains moderate crude protein (approximately 14–22 %) but relatively high fiber levels, which are poorly digested by poultry due to the lack of endogenous fiber degrading enzymes (Alimon, 2003). Therefore, strategies to improve its nutritional quality are required.

Solid-state fermentation (SSF) has been widely applied to enhance PKM nutritive value by modifying structural carbohydrate components and improving nutrient availability (Alshelmani et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2015; Saenphoom et al., 2011). Solid-state fermentation plays an important role in improving the nutritional value of PKM through fiber reduction and crude protein enrichment. In addition, PKM contains ß-mannan polysaccharides, which function as prebiotic components (Azizi et al., 2021).

Lactic acid bacteria have been reported to modify structural carbohydrate components in PKM while improving amino acid digestibility through the production of hydrolytic enzymes such as cellulase, hemicellulase, mannanase, and protease. These enzymes are capable of hydrolyzing complex non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) and partially hydrolyze complex proteins, thereby releasing entrapped nutrients and increasing protein availability for digestion and absorption (Hakim et al., 2020; Seftiadi et al., 2020). In particular, Limosilactobacillus fermentum has demonstrated fiber-degrading activity and probiotic potential, contributing to improved substrate quality and the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), which play key roles in intestinal morphology, microbial balance, and immune regulation (Gill et al., 2018; Latue et al., 2025; Niccolai et al., 2019).

Most previous studies have employed Bacillus spp. Aspergillus spp., Saccharomyces cerevisiae or conventional Lactobacillus strains for PKM fermentation. However, the application of L. fermentum strains remains limited, particularly in poultry nutrition. Compared with these commonly used microorganisms, L. fermentum BN21 possesses hydrolytic enzyme activity and probiotic properties, suggesting a dual role in improving both nutrient availability and gut health (Mirnawati et al., 2025; Ong et al., 2024a, Ong et al., 2024b; Seftiadi et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2024).

Although PKM fermentation for poultry feed has been widely investigated, the use of Limosilactobacillus fermentum remains limited despite its fiber-degrading and probiotic potential. Most previous studies have focused primarily on improvements in chemical composition, whereas information regarding its effects on intestinal health, including gut morphology, SCFA production, and barrier- and inflammation-related gene expression, is still scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of FPKM produced using L. fermentum BN21 on growth performance, intestinal histomorphology, cecal SCFA production, and genes associated with intestinal barrier function and inflammation in broiler chickens, thereby assessing its potential as a locally available alternative feed ingredient.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Universitas Gadjah Mada, Indonesia, under approval number 94/EC-FKH/Int./2024.

Preparation of fermented palm kernel meal

Palm kernel meal used in this study was obtained from PT. Wilmar Nabati Tbk, Indonesia. The PKM used as a fermentation substrate was finely ground and subjected to proximate analysis to determine dry matter (DM, AOAC 930.15), ash (AOAC 942.05), crude protein (CP; AOAC 984.13), ether extract (EE; AOAC 920.39), and crude fiber (CF; AOAC 978.10) following the procedures described by AOAC (2005). Limosilactobacillus fermentum BN21 (10⁹ CFU/mL), used as the fermenting microorganism, was obtained from the Laboratory of Nutritional Biochemistry, Department of Animal Nutrition and Feed Science, Faculty of Animal Science, Universitas Gadjah Mada.

The strain was cultured on sterile de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar and incubated at 37°C for 24 h (Memmert IN55, 53 L, Schwabach, Germany). For the fermentation, L. fermentum (10⁷ CFU/mL) was inoculated into the PKM substrate. The inoculated substrate was thoroughly mixed and adjusted to 40 % moisture content using sterile distilled water. The mixture was evenly distributed into 40 × 60 cm polyethylene bags, sealed tightly, and incubated at 28°C for 6 d under anaerobic conditions. After completion of fermentation, the substrate was dried in oven (Faithful DGF-4A, China) at 55°C for 24 h. The dried FPKM was ground, screened, and stored at room temperature until incorporated into experimental diets.

Animals and housing

A total of 320 one-day-old male broiler chicks (New Lohmann Indian River strain, MB 202 Platinum) were obtained from a commercial hatchery (PT. Widodo Makmur Unggas Tbk, Yogyakarta, Indonesia). All chicks had been vaccinated against Newcastle Disease (ND) and Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD) prior to the study. The management of broilers primarily followed the recommendations of the Indian River broiler management handbook (Aviagen, 2018).

During the first 10 days (brooding phase), birds were housed in a brooder facility under standard management conditions. On day 11, chicks were individually weighed (initial body weight: 350 ± 10 g) and randomly allocated to 40 floor pens (1 m × 0.75 m) following a completely randomized design. Each pen housed eight birds, resulting in a stocking density of approximately 10.7 birds/m2. Pens were equipped with ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experimental period.

Throughout the experiment, birds were reared in a semi-open house constructed with solid concrete walls and upper ventilation openings. Natural ventilation was supplemented with mechanical exhaust fans to enhance air circulation and facilitate heat dissipation under tropical environment conditions. Ambient temperature was maintained at 32–34°C during the first week and gradually reduced to 24–26°C by day 21, following commercial recommendations. Relative humidity ranged from 60–70 %. A continuous lighting program of 23L:1D was applied during the first 7 days, followed by 20L:4D thereafter.

Experimental design and diet

The study was conducted using a completely randomized design with five dietary treatment groups. Each treatment consisted of eight replicate pens with eight birds per pen, and the pen was considered the experimental unit. The experimental diets were formulated to contain 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100 g/kg fermented palm kernel meal (FPKM), corresponding to 0 %, 2.5 %, 5 %, 7.5 %, and 10 % inclusion levels, respectively. For clarity, the treatments are designated as CTRL, FPKM2.5, FPKM5, FPKM7.5, and FPKM10 throughout the manuscript.

During the starter phase (1–10 d), birds were fed a commercial diet obtained from PT. Japfa Comfeed Indonesia Tbk, containing 12 % moisture, 5 % ether extract, 1.1 % calcium (Ca), 0.5 % phosphorus (P), 1.20 % lysine, 0.45 % methionine, 0.80 % methionine + cystine, 0.19 % tryptophan, and 0.75 % threonine. Experimental diets were provided in two phases: grower (11–21 d) and finisher (22–35 d). The grower and finisher diets were formulated according to the nutritional recommendations of Aviagen (2022), as presented in Table 1, Table 2, respectively. Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period.

Growth performance measurement

Growth performance was evaluated based on feed intake (FI), body weight (BW), body weight gain (BWG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), mortality, and performance index (PI). The FI was recorded from day 11 to day 35 by calculating the difference between the feed offered and feed refusals for each pen. Birds were weighed collectively per pen at the beginning and end of the experimental period to determine final BW and BWG. The FCR was calculated as the ratio of total FI to total BWG and was adjusted for mortality. Mortality was recorded daily to determine livability percentage. Sample collection

At the end of the feeding trial (d 35), two birds per replicate pen with body weights closest to the pen median were selected following a 2-h fasting period. A total of 80 birds were sampled for tissue collection. Birds were individually weighed and euthanized by decapitation, followed by severing of the jugular vein to ensure complete exsanguination. Jejunal tissue samples were collected 6 cm distal to Meckel’s diverticulum. A 2-cm segment was immediately fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for histomorphological analysis. Another 2-cm was placed into RNAse-free microtubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80°C for gene expression analysis. Additionally, cecal digesta samples (approximately 2 g per bird) were collected aseptically using sterile instruments, transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in a cooler at –20°C until SCFA analysis.

Jejunal histomorphology analysis

Jejunal histomorphological analysis was performed following a modified method based on Maesaroh et al. (2022) and Ali et al. (2025). Jejunal segments (approximately 2-cm in length) were excised at a location 6 cm distal to Meckel’s diverticulum. Tissues were gently flushed with physiological saline solution to remove digesta and immediately fixed in Bouin’s solution. Following fixation, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (35, 50, 70, and 95 %), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Tissue sections were cut at a thickness of 4 µm using rotary microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (40 × magnification) equipped with a digital camera (Optilab Advance, Miconos, Indonesia). Digital images were analyzed using Image Raster software (version 4.0.5).

Morphometric parameters were measured according to Latue et al. (2025), including villus height (VH; measured from the tip of the villus to the villus-crypt junction), villus width (VW; measured at one-third and two-thirds of the villus height), crypt depth (CD; measured from the base of the villus to the submucosa), and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD). All measurements were performed using a calibrated scale bar of 100-µm scale bar within the Image Raster software. For each treatment, a total of 48 well-oriented villi were measured to ensure accuracy and representativeness of the morphometric evaluation.

Short chain fatty acids analysis

The analysis of SCFA profiles in cecal digesta were analyzed according to García-Villalba et al. (2012), with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.5 g of cecal digesta was mixed with 1.5 mL of 2.5 % metaphosphoric acid solution. The mixture was cooled on ice for 30 min and homogenized using a vortex mixer (Dlab MX-S). Sample were then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected and used for SCFA quantification via gas chromatography (Agilent 780A, Wilmington, NC, USA). The SCFAs analyzed included acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid.

Gene expression analysis using quantitative real-time PCR

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from approximately 30 mg of jejunal tissue using the Quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Maestrogen Inc., Hsinchu City, Taiwan). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan, Cat. No. FSQ-301), containing reverse transcriptase enzyme and necessary reagents for reverse transcription.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Thunderbird® SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan; Cat. No. QPX-201). Each 20 µL reaction contained 2 µL diluted cDNA, 6 pmol of each forward and reverse primer, 10 µL SYBR qPCR Mix, with nuclease-free water added to reach the final volume.

Target genes analyzed included tight junction genes including claudin-1 (CLDN-1) and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), pro-inflammatory genes tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-18 (IL-18), and anti-inflammatory gene interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-13 (IL-13). Primer sequences are listed in Table 3. Thermal cycling consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melt curve analysis was performed to verify specificity. Each treatment group included eight biological replicates (n = 8), and all reaction were run in technical duplicates. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with β-actin as the reference gene (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Expression levels were expressed relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with dietary FPKM level as the fixed effect, and the pen was considered the experimental unit for all parameters. When significant effects were detected (P < 0.05), means were separated using Tukey’s post hoc test. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear, quadratic, and cubic) were applied to evaluate dose-response relationships across increasing dietary FPKM levels. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Birds fed 25 g/kg FPKM generally showed higher BW, BWG, ADG, and IP compared with higher inclusion levels. The lowest FCR was observed in the FPKM2.5 group and FCR increased at higher levels. Polynomial contrast indicated significant linear responses for BW, BWG, ADG, FCR, and IP (P < 0.05). In addition, quadratic effects were detected for ADG and FCR (P < 0.05), whereas cubic responses were not detected (P > 0.05).

Across the entire experimental period, dietary FPKM levels significantly influenced BW, BWG, ADG, FCR, and IP (P < 0.05), whereas FI remained unchanged (P > 0.05). Birds fed 25 g/kg FPKM achieved the best overall performance, whereas the FPKM10 group showed reduced growth and poorest feed efficiency. Quadratic effects were detected for BW, BWG, and ADG (P < 0.05), whereas a significant cubic response was observed for IP only (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Palm kernel meal has long been used as an alternative feed ingredient in poultry production (Blair et al., 2025; Sundu et al., 2006). Fermentation has been reported to enhance its nutritional value and nutrient utilization through microbial modification of structural carbohydrate components, which may improve nutrient availability and digestibility (Alshelmani et al., 2016). In particular, fermentation with lactic acid bacteria such as Limosilactobacillus fermentum BN21 produces hydrolytic enzymes, including mannanase, cellulase, and β-glucosidase, which break down complex fiber components into more digestible forms (Krabi et al., 2015; Seftiadi et al., 2020).

In the present study, birds fed 100 g/kg FPKM exhibited significantly lower BW compared with the control group. This reduction was likely associated with decreased nutrient digestibility, as the fiber content increased proportionally with dietary FPKM level. It should be noted that crude fiber, as determined in proximate analysis, does not fully represent total NSP. Therefore, the actual structural carbohydrate fraction in PKM may be underestimated. The PKM used in this study was characterized by a high concentration of high structural carbohydrate content, ranging from 60-74.3 %, consisting mainly of β-mannan (78 %), xyloglucan (6 %), and cellulose (12 %) (Dong et al., 2022; Ong et al., 2024a). High structural carbohydrate content may limit nutrient accessibility and reduce digestibility, particularly at elevated inclusion levels, thereby accelerating passage rate and limiting enzymatic hydrolysis and nutrient absorption at the intestinal villi (Alshelmani et al., 2016; Yaophakdee et al., 2018). This is consistent with Aya et al. (2013), who reported that inclusion of 100 g/kg PKM reduced nutrient digestibility in broiler starter diets. Although conducted during the starter phase, their findings indicate that excessive PKM levels may impair nutrient utilization due to high fiber content. Similarly, Lawal et al. (2010) observed lower digestibility at 100 g/kg PKM inclusion unless cellulase was supplemented.

In contrast, FPKM supplementation did not affect FI across treatments. This finding slightly differs from Mateos et al. (2012), who suggested that increasing dietary fiber may reduce FI. In the present study, the palatability of FPKM-based diets may have been maintained by fermentation-derived metabolites that enhance feed aroma, texture, and nutrient availability (Senanayake et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023). Consistently, Walugembe et al. (2014) reported that inclusion of high-fiber ingredients at 60–120 g/kg did not compromise growth performance in broilers. Therefore, it is not unexpected that FI remained stable even at higher FPKM inclusion levels.

However, FCR increased significantly in birds fed 100 g/kg FPKM, indicating reduced feed efficiency. Because all experimental diets were formulated to be iso-nitrogenous and supplemented with synthetic lysine and methionine to meet nutrient requirements, this response was unlikely related to essential amino acid deficiencies. Instead, it may be attributed to residual structural carbohydrate components that may not have been fully modified during fermentation, which can encapsulate nutrients, increase digesta viscosity, and reduce nutrient digestibility and energy utilization in the gastrointestinal tract (Jha and Mishra, 2021).

Intestinal villi constitute the primary site of nutrient absorption. Therefore, greater villus height increases the absorptive surface area, whereas deeper crypts reflect higher epithelial turnover and greater energy demands for tissue renewal (Saenphoom et al., 2013). In the present study, dietary FPKM did not significantly affect VH, VW, or CD, suggesting that moderate inclusion of FPKM did not compromise intestinal structural integrity. This limited response may be associated with the adaptive capacity of older birds, in which gastrointestinal development, digestive system activity, and microbial capability to degrade NSP are already well established, thereby reducing the sensitivity of intestinal morphology to dietary fiber variations (Yaophakdee et al., 2018).

Previous findings regarding PKM and intestinal structure have been inconsistent. Rahim et al. (2007) reported reduced villus height with increasing PKM inclusion, whereas Zulkifli et al. (2009) observed enhanced villus development at higher dietary levels. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in bird age, processing methods, and the fermentability of fiber sources. Fermentable fibers are converted by intestinal microbiota into SCFAs, which serve as important energy substrates for epithelial cells and contribute to mucosal protection and regeneration (Yang et al., 2024).

Although crypt depth was not statistically different, a tendency toward shallower crypts was observed in birds fed FPKM. Reduced crypt depth may indicate lower epithelial turnover and diminished tissue repair requirements, reflecting a more stable intestinal environment. This improvement may be linked to the enhanced anti-inflammatory status observed in the present study, as evidenced by increased expression of IL-10 and IL-13 together with reduced IL-18 expression, which collectively may minimize inflammatory stress on the mucosa. In addition, increased SCFA availability provides readily oxidizable energy for epithelial cells, supporting maintenance of mucosal integrity. Collectively, these adaptations, including shallower crypts, maintained villus structure, and an improved VH:CD ratio suggest enhanced absorptive efficiency and intestinal health (Ghiselli et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).

Interestingly, the VH:CD ratio increased significantly with dietary FPKM inclusion, indicating improved intestinal structure. A higher VH:CD ratio reflects elongated villi combined with relatively shallower crypts, which suggests an expanded absorptive surface area and reduced energy expenditure for epithelial turnover. Such morphological adaptations are commonly associated with enhanced nutrient utilization and growth efficiency in broilers (Abdel-Raheem et al., 2012; Awad et al., 2009). The improvement in jejunal morphology may be associated with the fermentation process of PKM, which can alter structural carbohydrate components and improve substrate availability, thereby influencing intestinal microbial activity (Sundu and Dingle, 2003). In addition, fermented substrates can modulate the intestinal microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, leading to greater production of SCFA (Cao et al., 2013).

Among these, butyrate serves as the primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells and supports cellular proliferation, differentiation, and mucosal development (Guilloteau et al., 2010; Ratajczak et al., 2019). Increased SCFA availability may therefore supply additional metabolic energy to enterocytes, facilitating villus growth while limiting excessive crypt hyperplasia. Collectively, these effects contribute to improved mucosal integrity and absorptive capacity. Similar improvements in intestinal morphology have been reported in broilers fed Lactobacillus fermented feeds, which enhanced villus development, reduced epithelial apoptosis, and strengthened mucosal health (Li et al., 2024; Missotten et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2019).

The improvement in intestinal morphology observed in the present study appears closely associated with enhanced microbial fermentation, as evidenced by increased cecal SCFA production in birds fed FPKM. Short-chain fatty acids are the primary end-products of microbial fermentation activity and play key roles in gut physiology and host metabolism (Koh et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023; Louis and Flint, 2017; ). Dietary FPKM supplementation elevated acetic, propionic, and butyric acid concentrations, indicating greater fermentative activity suggesting enhanced microbial fermentation of undigested dietary substrates.

Collectively, these metabolites supply energy to epithelial cells, promote epithelial development, and exert anti-inflammatory effects, particularly butyrate, which serves as the main energy source for enterocytes and a regulator of intestinal barrier integrity (Corrêa-Oliveira et al, 2016; Feng et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2020). The higher total SCFA concentrations observed in FPKM-fed birds therefore suggest improved microbial fermentation efficiency, which may partly explain the enhanced intestinal morphology and tight-junction gene modulation detected in this study. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that fermentation of PKM enhances substrate availability and promotes SCFA production (Alshelmani et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2019).

Short-chain fatty acids play central roles in intestinal physiology, particularly in maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and regulating local immune responses (Koh et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023). The increased SCFA concentrations observed in birds fed FPKM may partly explain the changes in tight-junction gene expression detected in this study. Tight junctions consist of transmembrane proteins such as occludin (OCLN), claudins (CLDN), tricellulin, and junctional adhesion molecules (JAM), together with intracellular plaque proteins including zonula occludens (ZO) and cingulin (Fukuoka and Yoshimoto, 2018; Suzuki, 2020). Tight-junction assembly and function are regulated by multiple factors, including cytokines, growth factors, and nutrients (Slifer and Blikslager., 2020).

In the present study, CLDN-1 expression decreased progressively with increasing dietary FPKM, with the lowest values observed in the FPKM10 group. Although CLDN-1 is an integral component of tight junction strands, its reduction does not necessarily indicate impaired barrier integrity, as tight-junction proteins are dynamically regulated and may undergo compositional remodeling (Paradis et al., 2021). In contrast, ZO-1 expression increased significantly in all FPKM-fed groups and was numerically highest at the highest inclusion level. As a scaffold protein linking transmembrane junctional proteins to the actin cytoskeleton, ZO-1 plays a central role in maintaining epithelial stability and barrier function (Slifer and Blikslager, 2020).

The concurrent decrease in CLDN-1 and increase in ZO-1 may therefore reflect adaptive tight-junction remodelling rather than barrier disruption. This pattern suggests functional reinforcement of the tight-junction complex, as ZO-1 acts as a principal scaffolding protein that anchors claudins and occludin to the cytoskeleton, thereby strengthening junctional assembly and enhancing structural integrity of the epithelial barrier. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that probiotic or fermented-feed supplementation enhances tight-junction organization and upregulates ZO-1 expression (Chen et al., 2015; Suzuki, 2020). Similarly, supplementation with Limosilactobacillus fermentum has been shown to increase ZO-1 expression and improve intestinal barrier function (Archer et al., 2021).

Cytokine expression is tightly regulated by nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), which controls the transcription of several pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α. Short-chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, have been shown to suppress NF-κB activation and thereby exert anti-inflammatory effects (Corrêa-Oliveira et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023; Tedelind et al., 2007; Yao et al., 2020). Despite the increased SCFA concentrations observed in the present study, TNF-α expression remained unchanged among treatments. This stable expression may reflect the healthy physiological status of the birds and the absence of overt inflammatory challenge, as sampling was conducted at the end of the rearing period under normal husbandry conditions. Under such baseline conditions, pro-inflammatory cytokines are typically expressed at low levels, limiting the magnitude of further suppression. Therefore, the lack of significant differences in TNF-α likely indicates maintenance of immune homeostasis rather than a lack of biological activity of FPKM. Similar findings have been reported in broilers fed fermented feed ingredients under non-challenged conditions (Archer et al., 2021; Vinolo et al., 2011).

Importantly, IL-18 expression decreased significantly with increasing dietary FPKM. This effect is likely linked to higher SCFA, particularly butyrate, associated with enhanced microbial fermentation activity, which modulates immune signaling and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Consistent with this interpretation, Madani et al. (2024) reported that diets enriched in dietary fiber increased cecal butyrate concentrations and concomitantly down-regulated IL-18 expression in broilers. Likewise, Li et al. (2021) demonstrated that butyrate inhibits the production of several pro-inflammatory cytokines. These results indicate that FPKM may attenuate intestinal inflammation partly through SCFA-mediated regulation of tight-junction and cytokine signaling.

Although detergent fiber fractions such as neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) provide a more comprehensive characterization of structural carbohydrates, the present study followed proximate analysis according to AOAC procedures, and crude fiber was reported accordingly. Future studies incorporating detailed fiber fraction analysis would help clarify the mechanistic contribution of structural carbohydrates from FPKM to intestinal fermentation and immune modulation.

Practically, dietary inclusion of FPKM at 50 g/kg appears feasible for commercial broiler feed formulation, particularly in tropical regions where palm oil by-products are abundant and readily available. At this level, FPKM can partially replace conventional ingredients without compromising growth performance while supporting intestinal health. Such substitution may contribute to reduced feed costs and improved utilization of locally sourced resources, thereby enhancing the sustainability and economic efficiency of broiler production systems in palm oil-producing countries.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that fermented palm kernel meal can be included at 50 g/kg in broiler diets to support intestinal health without compromising growth performance. Higher inclusion levels are not recommended due to reduced nutrient utilization. Therefore, FPKM represent a promising functional and sustainable feed ingredient for poultry production in palm oil-producing regions.
