Development and validation of a machine learning model for predicting 6-month mortality in patients with infective endocarditis
Authors: Xiujuan Wu, Yue Han, Weili Liu, Song Lin, Jie Yuan, Yannan Ma, Chuanyu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning (ML)-based model for predicting 6-month all-cause mortality in patients diagnosed with infective endocarditis (IE), using retrospective data from a single tertiary care center. Additionally, key prognostic features were identified through model interpretability analysis.MethodsA cohort of 444 patients with IE was retrospectively assessed and randomly divided into a training set (n = 310) and a test set (n = 134). Following feature selection, a Cox proportional hazards model and four ML-based survival models: Random Survival Forest, Extremely Randomized Survival Trees (EST), eXtreme Gradient Boosting, and Support Vector Machine were established. Model performance was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. The model demonstrating optimal performance underwent interpretability analysis using Shapley additive explanations and an exploratory online tool was developed to demonstrate its functionality.ResultsNine predictors were selected for model construction: hemoglobin concentration, presence of severe valvular regurgitation, occurrence of septic shock, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, serum albumin level, intracerebral hemorrhage, neutrophil percentage, history of coronary heart disease, and receipt of surgical intervention. The EST model demonstrated the highest predictive performance in the test set (C-index: 0.852), with the smallest discrepancy in performance between the training and test datasets, indicating favorable generalizability. Time-dependent AUCs at 30, 90, and 180 days were 0.957, 0.925, and 0.855, respectively. Surgical intervention was modeled as a protective factor, associated with lower mortality risk, while intracerebral hemorrhage, higher NYHA class, severe valvular regurgitation, and septic shock were associated with increased risk.ConclusionsThe EST model demonstrated a high predictive accuracy for 6-month mortality in patients with IE, outperforming both conventional and other ML models. Its robust performance and interpretability suggest that, following rigorous external and multicenter validation, it may serve as a hypothesis-generating tool for risk stratification. It is not yet validated for direct clinical application and should currently be considered exploratory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-026-01559-w.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a severe infectious disease resulting from the invasion of the endocardium, cardiac valves, or the endothelium of adjacent large vessels by pathogens such as bacteria and fungi. The global incidence of IE has continued to increase, and the in-hospital mortality rate has remained high at 15–30% despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, surgical techniques, and intensive care support in recent years, highlighting the complexity and challenges associated with its clinical management [1, 2]. The prognosis of patients with IE shows substantial heterogeneity and is influenced by multiple factors, including host characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities, immune status), pathogen-related features, complications (e.g., heart failure, embolic events, septic shock), and treatment strategies [3, 4]. Along with traditional high-risk factors like Staphylococcus aureus infection, heart failure, cerebrovascular events, and renal insufficiency, emerging challenges including drug-resistant phenotypes, prosthetic valve infections, and delays in surgical intervention further compromise patient outcomes [5, 6]. The considerable clinical and pathological heterogeneity of IE complicates accurate mortality risk assessment at the individual level, emphasizing the need for early identification of high-risk patients and personalized intervention strategies to support clinical decision-making and improve prognosis.

The development of reliable mortality risk prediction models represents a key step toward advancing precision medicine in IE. Standard prognostic assessment tools, such as the EUROScore II and STS score, are predominantly derived from historical surgical cohorts using traditional logistic regression methodologies [7, 8]. These models are based on a limited number of structured variables (e.g., demographic characteristics, and indices of cardiac and renal function) and, although clinically utilized, exhibit notable limitations. Their predictive capabilities are generally confined to modeling linear associations, with a restricted capacity to capture complex interactions and nonlinear relationships among variables. Consequently, performance during external validation across diverse populations and clinical centers is frequently suboptimal, with reported area under the curve (AUC) values typically ranging between 0.65 and 0.72 [9, 10]. Additionally, the restricted variable scope in these models fails to account for evolving pathogen profiles, the growing burden of comorbidities in geriatric populations, and novel clinical features such as transcatheter interventions, thereby limiting both the timeliness and predictive accuracy of these tools.

As a fundamental branch of artificial intelligence, machine learning (ML) enables the identification of patterns within complex datasets without reliance on strong a priori model assumptions, and has demonstrated superior performance across multiple medical domains, including disease diagnosis, image analysis, and prognostic prediction [11]. In mortality risk prediction for IE, ML algorithms such as Random Survival Forest (RSF), Extremely Randomized Survival Trees (EST), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and support vector machine (SVM) offer the potential to address limitations inherent in traditional statistical models. These algorithms can integrate multi-source, multi-type variables to enhance the identification of patients at elevated risk [12, 13]. Although initial studies have applied individual ML models, such as neural networks and RSF, for prognostic assessment in IE, yielding encouraging results, important limitations remain [14–16]. These include the absence of systematic algorithmic comparisons, limited model interpretability, and insufficient validation of generalizability in region-specific populations, such as those in Henan Province, China.

Given the extended risk of mortality associated with complications of IE, such as heart failure and embolic events, this study selected 6-month all-cause mortality as the primary endpoint to more comprehensively capture both acute and intermediate-phase risk, thereby addressing the limitations of in-hospital mortality metrics. A single-center retrospective cohort was utilized to construct a high-performance prediction model by integrating multidimensional clinical variables with multiple ML algorithms. To enhance model interpretability, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were used to identify the most influential predictive factors. The resulting tool offers a precise, reliable, and clinically interpretable approach for risk stratification and personalized management, providing data-driven support for clinical decision-making in patients with IE.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics

A total of 444 patients diagnosed with IE were enrolled in the study. Patients were randomly allocated to a training set (n = 310) and a test set (n = 134) in a 7:3 ratio using the random number table method in R software. The training set was used for model development, while the test set was reserved for evaluating generalization performance.

Baseline characteristics, including demographic data, clinical symptoms and signs, laboratory parameters, predisposing factors, echocardiographic findings, blood culture results, and complications did not differ significantly between the two groups, except for the variable “Cough” (p = 0.010). As this variable was not included in the final model, it is unlikely to have introduced meaningful bias. Prognostic factor screening

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted on the training set and identified 25 variables that were significantly associated with patient prognosis (p < 0.05), as detailed in Additional file 8: Table S1.

To reduce the risk of overfitting and to isolate the most critical predictors, feature selection was performed using LASSO-Cox regression. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined through tenfold cross-validation. As the value of λ increased, the regression coefficients of less influential variables were progressively shrunk to zero, thereby excluding them from the model. At λ = 0.0236, corresponding to the minimum cross-validation error, 15 predictors were retained. A more parsimonious model comprising nine clinically relevant variables was selected using the one-standard-error rule (λ = 0.0598): hemoglobin, severe valve regurgitation, septic shock, NYHA classification, albumin, intracranial hemorrhage, neutrophil percentage, coronary artery disease, and surgical treatment. The event-per-predictor (EPP) ratio was calculated based on the 51 mortality events in the training set. The initial candidate set contained 69 variables (EPP = 0.74). Univariate Cox regression screening retained 25 variables with p < 0.05 (EPP = 2.04). The final model selected by LASSO-Cox regression contained 9 predictors, resulting in an EPP ratio of 5.67.

To empirically validate the robustness of our two-step selection strategy, we conducted two complementary sensitivity analyses. First, to examine whether univariate screening inadvertently excluded variables with weak marginal but strong multivariate effects, we applied LASSO-Cox regression directly to all 69 candidate variables without prior filtering, using identical tenfold cross-validation and the λ.1se criterion. As shown in Additional file 8: Table S2, this full-variable LASSO selected the identical nine predictors as our two-step approach, with near-identical coefficient estimates (absolute differences: 0.00000–0.00116; mean = 0.00016). This provides strong empirical evidence that our two-step strategy did not discard important multivariate prognostic signals and that the initial univariate screening did not alter the core prognostic drivers identified. Second, to quantify the stability of the nine selected predictors under sampling variability, we performed 1000 bootstrap resamples of the training set. In each iteration, LASSO-Cox regression (λ.1se criterion) was reapplied, and the selection frequency—defined as the percentage of bootstrap samples in which a variable retained a non-zero coefficient—was recorded for each predictor. As presented in Additional file 8: Table S3, all nine final predictors demonstrated high selection stability: surgical intervention (100%; 1000/1000), NYHA classification (98.5%), septic shock (94.8%), albumin (78.0%), intracerebral hemorrhage (72.2%), neutrophil percentage (58.1%), hemoglobin (55.0%), coronary heart disease (50.1%), and severe valvular regurgitation (48.4%). These results confirm that the final feature set is highly robust to sampling variability and not driven by idiosyncrasies of a single train–test split. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses confirm that our parsimonious nine-predictor model is both empirically robust and clinically coherent, and that the two-step selection strategy, while conservative, did not introduce substantive selection bias.

Assessment of distributional consistency for core features

To ensure the validity of subsequent model evaluation, the distributional consistency of the nine selected predictors between the training and test sets was quantitatively assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test and Wasserstein distance. The results indicated high distributional similarity across all features. All p values from the KS test exceeded 0.05, indicating no statistically significant differences, and all features achieved consistency scores greater than 0.85. This high level of distributional equivalence confirms the representativeness of the data partition and supports unbiased model performance evaluation between the training and test sets. Detailed results are presented in Additional file 8: Table S4.

To visually assess the distributions of the nine key predictors across the training and test sets, a feature distribution comparison plot was generated (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The kernel density estimation curves indicated substantial overlaps and closely aligned distribution shapes for all variables, further confirming high distributional consistency between the two datasets. These visual observations support the quantitative findings from the KS test and Wasserstein distance analysis, validating the appropriateness of the data partitioning strategy and ensuring fair conditions for comparing model performance.

Predictive model construction and performance assessment

A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model and four ML-based survival models—RSF, EST, XGBoost, and SVM–were constructed for comparative performance assessment using the nine selected predictors.

To assess risk stratification capability, patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score generated by each model. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted for both the training and test sets (Fig. 2). All five models demonstrated significant discriminatory power in both datasets (log-rank test p < 0.05). Although all models achieved strong performance on the training set (p < 0.001), the Cox and EST models retained the highest statistical significance on the test set (p < 0.001), indicating superior generalizability. The RSF, XGBoost, and SVM models, though remaining significant, exhibited reduced performance in the test set (p ≈ 0.001), suggesting a mild degree of overfitting. Given the relatively low event rate, we further explored a more stringent risk threshold (the 75th percentile of predicted risk scores) specifically for the optimal EST model. Compared to the median split, this 75th-percentile threshold defined a smaller, more extreme high-risk subgroup (n = 34 vs. n = 67) with a markedly worse prognosis (e.g., a substantially lower 180-day survival probability). The survival separation remained highly significant (log-rank p < 0.001; Additional file 2: Fig. S2), confirming the model’s ability to robustly identify patients at the greatest risk.

Model discrimination was further assessed using the C-index. As presented in Fig. 3, all models achieved excellent C-indices on the training set (> 0.92). Online platform for EST survival prediction

The EST-based online prediction platform has been successfully implemented and is currently operating with stable performance (accessible at: https://estpredictor.pythonanywhere.com/). This fully functional system features a responsive user interface and accepts nine key clinical parameters, including albumin, hemoglobin, history of coronary heart disease, intracerebral hemorrhage, and NYHA classification. Automated data validation and medical plausibility checks have been incorporated to ensure input integrity. Upon submission, the platform generates immediate structured outputs, including individualized risk scores, survival probability curves, and visualizations for risk stratification. This platform is deployed as a proof-of-concept research tool only to facilitate model interrogation and hypothesis generation; it is strictly exploratory and must not be used for clinical decision-making, patient counseling, or treatment guidance.

Discussion

In the context of the expanding application of ML in medical prognosis, a 6-month mortality prediction model for patients with IE was developed and validated using a Cox proportional hazards model alongside four ML algorithms. Among these, the EST model demonstrated the strongest performance, achieving a C-index of 0.852, high generalizability (ΔC-index = 0.086), and consistently elevated time-dependent AUC values at 30, 90, and 180 days (0.957, 0.925, and 0.855, respectively). Furthermore, the model’s high discriminatory performance was complemented by excellent calibration on the test set in the short-to-medium term, mitigating concerns about optimism from the high training-set performance. The observed tendency to overestimate risk at 180 days aligns with the expected increase in prognostic uncertainty over longer time horizons. The robustness of the model may be attributed to the inherent randomization mechanisms of the algorithm, which enable effective modeling of complex variable interactions while reducing the risk of overfitting [17].

Notably, conventional surgical risk scores such as EuroSCORE II and STS-IE tend to underestimate mortality in IE, primarily due to the limited inclusion of IE patients in their development cohorts and the exclusion of IE-specific prognostic variables [5, 9, 10]. Although dedicated IE-specific scoring systems such as APORTEI, RISK-E, AEPEI, and ANCLA include more clinically relevant features, their applicability remains largely confined to short-term surgical outcomes [5, 18–21]. As a result, their use in non-surgical patients and in medium- to long-term risk stratification is limited. It is important to note that these established scores and our model have fundamentally different objectives. EuroSCORE II and STS-IE are designed to predict perioperative or short-term (e.g., in-hospital) mortality specifically in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. In contrast, our model aims to predict 6-month all-cause mortality in a broader population of patients diagnosed with IE, encompassing both surgical and non-surgical management pathways. This distinction is crucial when interpreting the comparative performance and intended clinical applications of these tools.

Through SHAP interpretability analysis, the model identified predictors with strong clinical coherence. Most notably, surgical intervention was modeled as the variable most strongly associated with lower predicted risk, correlating with substantially lower observed 6-month mortality (6.0% vs. 49.5%) [22–24]. Crucially, this association captured by the model reflects real-world clinical pathways and inherent treatment selection bias; the ‘surgery’ variable encapsulates not only potential therapeutic benefit but also the fact that patients selected for surgery typically possess greater physiological reserve and fewer contraindications. The sensitivity analysis excluding surgery demonstrated that the model retained a C-index of 0.804 (p = 0.22 vs. full model) and that the remaining predictors (e.g., NYHA class, intracerebral hemorrhage, coronary heart disease) continued to drive risk stratification in a clinically plausible manner (Additional file 6: Fig. S6; Additional file 8: Table S5). This reinforces that the model captures fundamental prognostic determinants of IE beyond the selection bias inherent in surgical decision-making. Nevertheless, the association with surgery should not be interpreted in isolation as evidence of direct therapeutic benefit, as it unavoidably incorporates immortal-time bias and confounding by indication. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as characterizing the prognostic profile of patients who undergo surgery rather than as an isolated measure of surgical efficacy. However, the timing of surgical intervention, particularly in the presence of concurrent intracranial hemorrhage or septic shock, remains a significant clinical challenge. Conservative management in such cases may elevate risk in otherwise eligible surgical candidates, highlighting the necessity of individualized treatment strategies [25, 26]. In these complex scenarios, the model may contribute to dynamic risk assessment; however, clinical decision-making should continue to be guided by a comprehensive assessment of patient-specific factors.

Uncertainty quantification further supported the EST model's robustness. The lower bound of the 95% CI for the C-index (0.759) remained above 0.75, and the lower bounds for the 30-day and 90-day AUCs (0.922 and 0.863, respectively) remained substantially above 0.80, indicating that the model's superior performance is not attributable to chance or sampling variability.

Cerebral hemorrhage and septic shock were reaffirmed as major determinants of mortality risk. Within the cohort, cerebral hemorrhage was associated with a mortality rate of 52.6%, consistent with its severe neurologic consequences. Septic shock was linked to mortality exceeding 50%, substantially higher than that observed in typical cases of IE, which generally range from 6 to 25%, and frequently serves as a contraindication to surgical intervention [27–31]. Although early surgery has been associated with improved outcomes, even in the presence of multi-organ dysfunction, further investigation is warranted regarding the potential role of mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to surgical management in hemodynamically unstable patients [27, 28, 32–35].

Advanced NYHA classification (Class III/IV), hypoalbuminemia, and anemia were identified as significant predictors, collectively reflecting a high-risk “malnutrition–inflammation–anemia” profile known to exacerbate disease progression and increase cardiac burden [36–45]. The inclusion of neutrophil percentage further reinforced this pathophysiological axis, serving as a direct indicator of systemic inflammatory burden. These findings underscore the potential value of early nutritional and hematinic support as part of a comprehensive management strategy for IE.

This study has several limitations. First and foremost, the model was developed and validated using data from a single center. All validation performed is internal; no external validation has been conducted. The absence of external validation in an independent, geographically distinct cohort is a major limitation that precludes claims of broad generalizability. While our internal validation (train–test split) and the use of regularization techniques suggest reasonable robustness, the model’s performance must be confirmed in external, multicenter populations before clinical application can be considered. Second, the accompanying online prediction tool is an exploratory implementation. We strongly caution that its outputs should be considered hypothesis-generating only and must not guide clinical decisions. This tool lacks prospective external validation, regulatory approval, and the infrastructure required for bedside use; its primary purpose is to facilitate model interrogation and external validation efforts by other research groups. Third, while the model demonstrated satisfactory internal calibration in the short-to-medium term, a tendency to overestimate risk was noted at 180 days. Furthermore, calibration performance in external populations remains to be validated. Fourth, a key methodological limitation is the relatively low number of mortality events relative to the number of candidate predictors during the initial modeling stages, with an initial event-per-predictor ratio of 0.74. The relatively limited number of mortality events (51 in the training set) in relation to the number of predictors resulted in a final event-per-predictor ratio of 5.67, which is below the common heuristic of 10 and necessitates cautious interpretation regarding model stability. This limitation was mitigated by the use of strong regularization (LASSO), rigorous internal validation, and the consistency demonstrated in our sensitivity analysis. Although our two-step feature selection strategy was empirically validated through full-variable LASSO sensitivity analysis (Additional file 8: Table S2) and bootstrap stability assessment (Additional file 8: Table S3), no such strategy is entirely free of theoretical selection bias. However, the convergent evidence—identical predictor selection with near-identical coefficients, high bootstrap selection frequencies (> 48% for all nine predictors), and strong clinical coherence—substantially mitigates this concern. Fifth, certain potentially relevant variables such as pathogen drug resistance profiles and specific embolic foci were not included, which may limit the predictive comprehensiveness of the model. Sixth, the use of the model in informing specific surgical timing decisions has not yet been validated and requires further prospective investigation. Seventh, the variable ‘surgical intervention’ was modeled as a baseline covariate, which does not account for its time-dependent nature and may introduce immortal time bias. Although our sensitivity analysis (excluding surgery) confirmed that the model’s predictive performance remains substantial (C-index = 0.804) and its interpretability clinically coherent without this variable (Additional file 6: Fig. S6), and that the performance difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.22; Additional file 8: Table S5), the treatment of surgery as a baseline covariate may still introduce residual immortal-time bias that we cannot fully eliminate in this retrospective design. Future studies using time-dependent covariate modeling are warranted to more precisely quantify the effect of surgical timing on survival. Eighth, the predictive performance of the model naturally attenuates over time, as evidenced by the decrease in AUC at 180 days. This underscores that its highest clinical utility lies in short-to-medium-term (e.g., 30–90 day) risk assessment rather than long-term prognosis.

Future studies should prioritize prospective multicenter external validation to confirm the model’s generalizability across different healthcare settings and patient populations, and to rigorously assess its clinical impact. Additionally, exploration of dynamic prediction models that integrate evolving clinical parameters, along with evaluation of clinical utility through decision curve analysis, represents important directions for continued research.

Conclusions

An ML model was developed and internally validated to predict 6-month mortality in patients with IE. As this is a single-center, internally validated model, its findings require confirmation through rigorous external validation in diverse, multicenter cohorts before any consideration for clinical application. Among the assessed models, the EST algorithm demonstrated superior performance, outperforming both traditional and other ML-based approaches, with a C-index of 0.852 and strong generalizability. Key predictors identified, such as surgical intervention (protective), intracerebral hemorrhage, septic shock, and NYHA classification, were consistent with established clinical understanding. The resulting model is interpretable and clinically coherent, and has been deployed as an online exploratory research tool. This tool is not approved or validated for clinical use and is intended solely for hypothesis generation and academic review. Future external and multicenter validation is required to assess whether the model may ultimately have utility in risk stratification; at present, no claims of clinical applicability can be made.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

In this retrospective study, patients were initially identified through the electronic medical record system at Fuwai Central China Cardiovascular Hospital between January 1, 2018, and June 30, 2025, using diagnosis codes associated with IE. Following a review of the electronic medical records, individuals were included if a first-time diagnosis was confirmed and the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria were fulfilled [46]. Patients were excluded if medical records were incomplete, if they were transferred to another hospital, or if a concurrent malignant tumor was present.

Data were comprehensively extracted using a standardized form. Collected data encompassed demographic characteristics, clinical history, symptoms and signs, laboratory and echocardiographic findings, past medical history, diagnostic data, treatment details (including antimicrobial and surgical interventions), and in-hospital complications. The primary endpoint, all-cause mortality, was determined through structured telephone follow-up. These follow-ups were conducted using a pre-designed questionnaire, which recorded the general condition of the patient, recurrence of IE, and any complications. For patients who had died, the date and cause of death were also ascertained.

During the 6-month follow-up period, a total of 74 patients (16.6%) died, with 51 events (16.5%) in the training set and 23 events (17.2%) in the test set.

Data preprocessing and dataset splitting

A standardized preprocessing pipeline was applied to the dataset to ensure analytical robustness. A completeness validation was conducted, with particular attention given to the integrity of survival data. Missing values in continuous and categorical variables were imputed using the median and mode, respectively. Features were subsequently prepared for ML by labeling binary variables and applying integer encoding to ordinal categories. To ensure data accuracy, independent reviews were carried out by two cardiologists.

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%), with proportions of positive events maintained consistently across both subsets. Univariate analyses of baseline variables were performed separately on the training and test sets to assess variable distributions and evaluate the balance achieved through the data split. All variables, including surgical intervention, were treated as fixed baseline covariates measured at or shortly after diagnosis.

Feature variable selection

Feature selection was conducted in two sequential phases to enhance model robustness and to manage the high dimensionality of the initial candidate predictor set (n = 69) relative to the number of events in the training set (n = 51). Given the low event number relative to candidate predictors, we implemented a conservative two-step feature selection strategy: (1) Univariate Cox screening as a noise filter. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to the training set to assess the association between individual predictors and survival outcomes. Variables demonstrating statistical significance at p < 0.05 were retained for the subsequent phase. This univariate screening served exclusively as a conservative noise filter to exclude variables with extremely weak marginal signals and to improve computational stability for LASSO regularization. It was not used as a definitive selector of prognostic factors. (2) LASSO-Cox regression with tenfold cross-validation. In the second phase, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) was used within the Cox regression framework to address multicollinearity and reduce the risk of overfitting. LASSO applies an L₁ regularization penalty, which shrinks the coefficients of redundant or noisy variables to zero, thereby enabling automatic variable selection. The optimal tuning parameter (λ) was determined using tenfold cross-validation, and the final model was selected according to the λ.1se criterion, which identifies the most parsimonious model within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error. The final predictor set was determined exclusively by this LASSO-Cox regression, not by the preceding univariate screening step. We acknowledge that univariate screening carries a theoretical risk of excluding predictors that exert their influence only after adjusting for other covariates. To empirically assess this risk, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by applying LASSO-Cox directly to the full set of 69 candidate variables without prior univariate filtering, using identical tenfold cross-validation and the λ.1se criterion. Results are presented in Results 2.2 and Additional file 2: Table S2.

Given the limited number of mortality events relative to the initial pool of candidate predictors, this two-step feature selection strategy combined with strong regularization (using the λ.1se criterion) was employed to mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhance model stability.

Assessment of feature distributional consistency between training and test sets

Following variable selection, the dataset was restricted to the retained core features for both the training and test sets. A comprehensive assessment of distributional consistency was subsequently conducted to validate the integrity of the data partition and to minimize potential systematic bias. This assessment incorporated several complementary methods. The KS test was used to assess the similarity between cumulative distribution functions. The Wasserstein distance was used to quantify distributional divergence based on the minimal transformation required to align the distributions. Additionally, descriptive statistics and standardized mean differences were calculated to measure intergroup variability. Visualizations, including overlaid density plots and comparative bar charts, were generated to support intuitive comparisons of feature distributions across the two sets.

Development of survival prediction models

Five survival analysis models were constructed and compared using the training set, based on the selected variables and with 6-month survival defined as the endpoint. The first model consisted of a Cox proportional hazards model fitted via partial likelihood estimation, without hyperparameter tuning. The second model involved an RSF, with hyperparameters optimized through five-fold cross-validation and grid search, utilizing data formats compatible with the scikit-survival library. The third model used the EST algorithm, following a similar optimization strategy to the RSF, including random grid search, and providing intrinsic feature importance metrics. The fourth model was developed using an XGBoost Survival framework, with key parameters tuned via five-fold cross-validation and random grid search. The fifth model incorporated a Survival SVM, implemented through the Fast Survival SVM algorithm, with feature standardization and hyperparameter optimization performed via five-fold cross-validation. All stochastic procedures were controlled using a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility.

All machine learning models (RSF, EST, XGBoost, SVM) were trained with hyperparameter optimization via five-fold cross-validation and grid search. This approach, combined with the preceding LASSO-based feature selection (“Predictive model construction and performance assessment” section), was implemented to rigorously control the risk of overfitting and improve the generalizability of the models.

Model assessment, comparison, and interpretability analysis

The predictive performance of the five survival models was assessed and compared using multiple complementary methods. To quantify the statistical uncertainty of model discrimination, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the C-index and time-dependent AUC were estimated using bootstrap resampling on the test set. Specifically, the test set was resampled with replacement for 1000 iterations, and the performance metrics were recalculated for each bootstrap sample. The 95% CIs were derived using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. For ROC curves, 95% confidence bands were generated using the same bootstrap procedure and are displayed as shaded regions in Additional file 4: Fig. S4. Kaplan–Meier analysis was first used to visually assess stratification capability. Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-risk groups based on median risk scores derived from the training set, and survival curves were plotted for both training and test sets. Given the relatively low event rate, we also explored a more stringent threshold (the 75th percentile of risk scores) to better define a clinically relevant high-risk subgroup for the optimal model. The concordance index (C-index) was then calculated as the primary measure of discrimination, separately for the training and test sets, using the scikit-survival library to appropriately manage right-censored data. The difference between the training and test set C-indices was used to quantify model generalization, with smaller discrepancies indicating reduced overfitting. Time-dependent AUC analysis was subsequently conducted to assess discriminatory performance across 151 time points spanning from 30 to 180 days. At each time point t, patients with survival times ≤ t were classified as events, and corresponding AUC values were computed. The optimal model was selected based on overall performance in the test set, primarily informed by the C-index and further supported by the stability observed in time-dependent AUC values.

Model calibration, the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, was assessed for the optimal model on the test set. Calibration plots were generated at 30, 90, and 180 days by grouping patients into deciles of predicted risk and comparing the mean predicted risk with the observed Kaplan–Meier event probability within each group. Quantitative calibration was evaluated using the Brier score, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and by estimating the calibration intercept (α) and slope (β) from a linear regression of observed on predicted probabilities across risk deciles.

To provide a rigorous assessment of the optimal model’s discriminative ability at fixed operational points, we calculated standard classification metrics and constructed confusion matrices at three clinically relevant time points (30, 90, and 180 days). A common binary classification threshold was determined by maximizing Youden’s index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) on the training set predictions at the primary 180-day endpoint. This single threshold was then applied to the risk scores from both the training and test sets at all three time points (30, 90, and 180 days) to generate binary classifications. Sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy, and F1-score for both datasets are reported. The corresponding confusion matrices are provided. Model interpretability was analyzed through feature importance ranking, variable effect analysis, and implementation of the SHAP framework. Feature importance was visually ranked to indicate the relative contribution of each variable to model predictions. Variable effect analysis was conducted by systematically varying the value of each predictor while holding all other variables constant, thereby isolating its individual effect and indicating potential nonlinear associations with survival probability. Survival probabilities were derived from risk scores using the logistic transformation: Survival probability = 1/(1 + exp (Standardized Relative Risk)) and were visualized using box plots for categorical variables and scatter plots with trend lines for continuous variables. SHAP analysis was then used to quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to the model output, using a background dataset of up to 100 samples, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of the direction and magnitude of each variable’s influence on survival predictions.

The reported training set C-index for the developed models is the mean (± standard deviation) obtained from fivefold cross-validation. For benchmarking with EuroSCORE II, both scores were calculated and compared exclusively on the surgical patient subset (n = 102) of the test set.

Online prediction system implementation

To support technical deployment demonstration of the validated EST model, a web-based prediction platform was developed and deployed. The system was implemented using a Flask backend framework in Python 3.9, with the scikit-survival library used to load the pre-trained model and expose a RESTful API for risk prediction. The frontend was designed with a responsive layout to ensure optimal usability across various device types.

For production deployment, a Gunicorn and Nginx stack was configured to support multi-process handling and reverse proxy functionality. Security was enhanced through the implementation of HTTPS encryption and cross-origin resource sharing controls. Docker containerization was used to improve the scalability and maintainability of the system. The platform accepts nine key clinical parameters, including albumin level, hemoglobin level, history of coronary heart disease, intracerebral hemorrhage, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, and incorporates comprehensive data validation procedures to ensure input integrity prior to generating predictions.

Statistical analysis

Traditional statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0. The KS test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median with interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)] and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentage [n (%)], with group comparisons performed using Pearson’s chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on sample size and expected frequency distributions.

For survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival rates and generate survival curves, with differences between groups assessed using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors for IE mortality were initially screened through univariate Cox regression analysis. Subsequently, variable selection was performed using LASSO-Cox regression to identify the most relevant predictors.

ML prediction models, including RSF, EST, XGBoost, and SVM survival models, were developed using R version 4.4.1 and Python version 3.10. Key analytical packages included: lifelines and scikit-survival for survival analysis; scikit-learn and XGBoost for ML modeling; SHAP for interpretability analysis; pandas and numpy for data preprocessing; and matplotlib, seaborn, and plotly for data visualization. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
