Ventricular Septal Defect as a Bail-out Shunt in Prosthetic Tricuspid Valve Obstruction
Authors: Hadi Fowaz, Salim Adawi, Amir Aker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prosthetic valve thrombosis or obstruction is a feared complication of mechanical valve replacement. The risk is highest in the tricuspid position, with an estimated incidence of 0.7%–1.2% per patient-year, largely attributable to the low-pressure, low-flow environment of the right heart. Clinical presentation is usually acute, with symptoms of right-sided heart failure and cardiogenic shock necessitating urgent intervention. We present an unusual case of a chronic, completely stuck prosthetic tricuspid valve discovered incidentally with no hemodynamic compromise. The patient’s hemodynamic stability was sustained by a preexisting ventricular septal defect, which provided an alternative pathway for systemic blood flow. This case highlights the critical interplay between complex coexisting congenital anomalies in patients with prosthetic mechanical valves.

INTRODUCTION

The management of mechanical valves in patients with congenital heart disease poses a significant clinical challenge due to the variable presentations and potentially severe complications.

We present a complex congenital heart disease case of a patient with a stuck bileaflet prosthetic tricuspid valve.

CASE REPORT

A 39-year-old woman with a history of complex congenital heart disease (CHD) presented with symptomatic atrial flutter. Her underlying anatomy was a variant of single ventricle physiology, characterized by hypoplastic left heart syndrome, double-outlet right ventricle (RV), a large ventricular septal defect (VSD), an atrial septal defect (ASD), and a persistent left superior vena cava. Consequently, her RV functioned as the systemic ventricle. Our patient’s surgical history was extensive: (1) A left modified Blalock–Taussig shunt was performed in the neonatal period; (2) A fenestrated Fontan procedure during childhood; (3) Tricuspid valve replacement with a bileaflet mechanical prosthesis during adulthood. Postoperatively, warfarin therapy was initiated with a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 3.0–3.5. During follow-up, the patient exhibited labile INR values, which were complicated by three episodes of transient ischemic attack (TIA).

At presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable. As a rhythm control strategy was elected, a precardioversion transesophageal echocardiogram was performed due to the history of TIA and suboptimal anticoagulation, showing a completely immobile, stuck prosthetic tricuspid valve, consistent with chronic obstruction without evidence of thrombus [Figure 1a-c]. Color-flow Doppler demonstrated a significant left-to-right shunt across a large VSD, which directly supplied the systemic RV [Figure 1d and e]. This shunt was responsible for maintaining systemic cardiac output, explaining the patient’s hemodynamic stability. A prominent ASD was also noted.

Electrical cardioversion was performed successfully, restoring sinus rhythm. In a multidisciplinary evaluation, we concluded that the valve obstruction was likely chronic and well-compensated. Given the high surgical risk associated with reoperative intervention and the protective effect of the VSD, a conservative approach was adopted. The management plan focused on strict optimization of warfarin therapy. The patient was discharged in a stable condition and remained asymptomatic at her 1-year follow-up.

DISCUSSION

This case provides two pivotal insights. First, it reinforces the high risk of prosthetic valve obstruction in the tricuspid position, particularly in patients with CHD and challenging anticoagulation management.[1] Second, and more uniquely, it illustrates a paradoxical protective mechanism, where a congenital defect mitigates a life-threatening acquired complication. The etiology of the stuck valve in this case is likely multifactorial. While thrombosis is common, the chronicity and completeness of the obstruction, in the setting of underlying anticoagulation, suggest significant pannus formation.[2]

The most remarkable aspect of this case is the patient’s clinical stability. In a typical anatomy, complete tricuspid valve obstruction would lead to a dramatic reduction in RV preload, causing low cardiac output and shock. However, in this patient, the large VSD allowed oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to shunt left-to-right, directly supplying the systemic RV and bypassing the obstructed tricuspid valve. This unique pathway acted as a natural “bail-out” shunt, preventing circulatory collapse.

This hemodynamic stability allowed for a nonurgent, conservative strategy focusing on rhythm control and anticoagulation. This case aligns with guidelines suggesting a higher INR target (3.0–3.5) for mechanical valves in the tricuspid position, though achieving stable anticoagulation remains a significant challenge in this population.[34] This case prompts a reconsideration of valve choice in complex CHD. While mechanical valves offer durability, their thrombotic and pannus formation risk may be substantially high despite underlying anticoagulation. Bioprosthetic valves, though more prone to degeneration, may be preferable in high-risk cases.[5] This patient population is underrepresented in clinical trials, underscoring the importance of individualized decision-making.[6]

CONCLUSION

We describe a unique case of a chronically stuck mechanical tricuspid valve without hemodynamic compromise due to the protective hemodynamic effects of a coexisting VSD. This case serves as a striking example of the complex pathophysiology in adults with complex CHD and mechanical valves. It emphasizes the critical importance of a comprehensive hemodynamic assessment and supports a tailored approach to anticoagulation management and prosthetic valve selection for this growing patient population.
