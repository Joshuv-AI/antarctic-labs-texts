Uncovering hidden spermatogenesis: INPP1 and GTSF1 as non-invasive predictors of sperm retrieval in non-obstructive azoospermia
Authors: Emad Babakhanzadeh, Ali Khodadadian, Forouzan Esmaeili Dahaj, Faezeh Hakimi, Majid Nazari, Mahya Mozhdeh, Serajoddin Vahidi Mehrgardi, Mohammad Hassan Jafari, Mohammadreza Dehghani, Soudeh Ghafouri-Fard, Nasrin Ghasemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aetiology of non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) remains poorly understood, highlighting the need for non-invasive molecular biomarkers to predict the outcome of sperm retrieval. In this study, we combined integrative bioinformatic analysis with experimental validation to investigate two regulatory RNA networks involving INPP1 and GTSF1 in NOA. Analysis of seven GEO transcriptome datasets identified INPP1 and GTSF1 as consistently downregulated genes in testicular tissue of NOA patients. Upstream regulatory elements were predicted using miRNA–circRNA interaction databases, leading to the selection of miR-27a and hsa_circ_0057497 for INPP1 and miR-146a-5p and hsa_circ_0026774 for GTSF1. The expression levels of these six markers were quantified in plasma samples from 60 men with NOA (stratified by both histopathological subtypes — SCOS, MA, HYPO — and sperm retrieval status: NOA⁺ vs. NOA-) and 40 fertile controls. INPP1 and GTSF1 were significantly reduced in NOA patients; in particular, INPP1 showed the lowest expression in SCOS patients, while GTSF1 was most downregulated in the MA and HYPO subgroups. The corresponding miRNAs were upregulated, and circRNAs showed inverse patterns. ROC analysis showed excellent diagnostic performance in distinguishing NOA⁺ from NOA-: AUCs were 0.956 for hsa_circ_0057497, 0.970 for hsa_circ_0026774 and ≥ 0.90 for the combined biomarker model. These results suggest that the INPP1 and GTSF1 RNA axes are perturbed in NOA and reflect both sperm retrieval potential and histological severity.,This study provides a basis for non-invasive molecular diagnostics and more personalized treatment strategies for male infertility.Keywords: Non-obstructive azoospermia, INPP1, GTSF1, miR-27a, miR-146a-5p, hsa_circ_0026774, hsa_circ_0057497Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Genetics, Medical research, Molecular biology, Urology

Introduction

Infertility is a major health problem worldwide, and male factors are responsible in almost half of all cases. Azoospermia – the complete absence of sperm in the ejaculate – is one of the most serious causes of male infertility, affecting ~ 1% of all men and up to 10–15% of infertile men1. Azoospermia is divided into obstructive (OA), which is due to physical blockages in the reproductive tract, and non-obstructive (NOA), which is due to intrinsic testicular failure and impaired spermatogenesis2. NOA accounts for about 60% of azoospermia cases and is idiopathic in about 75% of patients, i.e. the underlying molecular cause remains unknown3. Current treatment strategies for NOA, such as microdissection testicular sperm extraction (micro-TESE) in combination with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), have limited success rates (usually below ~ 50%)4. This limited success is largely due to the lack of reliable molecular biomarkers to predict which NOA patients have residual foci of spermatogenesis and are likely to undergo sperm retrieval. In addition, the heterogeneous histology of NOA testes – which often contain isolated foci of spermatogenesis amidst otherwise Sertoli cell-only or arrested tubules – makes preoperative prediction of sperm retrieval difficult5. An accurate, non-invasive prognostic biomarker for NOA could therefore spare patients the physical and emotional burden of unnecessary surgery and improve success rates through better patient selection.

A better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying NOA is essential for the development of such biomarkers and new therapies. Spermatogenesis is an extraordinarily complex process involving thousands of genes and coordinated cell–cell interactions; disruptions in hormonal signaling, gene expression and apoptotic regulation can all contribute to spermatogenesis failure6. In recent years, non-coding RNAs – particularly circular RNAs (circRNAs) – have emerged as critical regulators of gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. CircRNAs are covalently closed RNA loops formed by back-splicing of pre-mRNAs that lack 5′-3′ ends and are highly stable in cells and body fluids. An important function of circRNAs is to act as “sponges” for microRNAs (miRNAs) by sequestering and inhibiting specific miRNAs and thereby modulating the expression of the miRNAs’ target genes7. Such circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axes form intricate networks that play a role in many biological processes, including spermatogenesis. Dysregulation of these networks can disrupt the homeostasis of gene expression and contribute to impaired sperm production and male infertility. Indeed, recent studies have begun to identify circRNAs that are abnormally expressed in the testes or sperm of infertile men. For example, certain circRNA/miRNA pairs have been linked to spermatogenesis failure: hsa_circ_0000116 can act as a sponge for miR-449 and inhibits spermatogenesis by increasing the expression of genes related to autophagy8. hsa_circRNA_402130 can sponge miRNAs of the let-7 family and thus impair stemness in NOA9. These results emphasize the potential importance of circRNA-regulated metabolic pathways in the pathogenesis of NOA.

Against this background, the present study aimed to identify novel circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axes associated with NOA and evaluate their potential as biomarkers of sperm retrieval outcomes. In an initial analysis of public transcriptome data, we found that numerous genes involved in spermatogenesis were consistently downregulated in NOA patients. Two genes in particular – inositol polyphosphate 1-phosphatase (INPP1) and gametocyte-specific factor 1 (GTSF1) – were consistently underexpressed in testicular tissue from men with NOA. GTSF1 is a testis-specific protein that is thought to be required for germ cell development, as suggested by its role in piRNA-mediated transposon silencing and the sterility of male GTSF1 knockout mice10. In contrast, INPP1 is a broadly expressed enzyme involved in phosphatidylinositol signaling. While its role in spermatogenesis is not well understood, other members of the inositol phosphatase family (such as INPP4B and INPP5B) are known to be important for testicular function and germ cell maintenance11,12.

We hypothesized that the downregulation of INPP1 and GTSF1 in NOA may be due in part to dysregulation of upstream non-coding RNAs – specifically overactive miRNAs and/or loss of their regulatory circRNAs. Through an integrative bioinformatic analysis, we identified two putative competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks of interest: hsa_circ_0057497 – miR-27a-5p – INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774 – miR-146a-5p – GTSF1. In these axes, miR-27a-5p and miR-146a-5p are predicted to target INPP1 and GTSF1 mRNAs, respectively, while circRNAs hsa_circ_0057497 and hsa_circ_0026774 sponge these miRNAs, thereby cancelling their suppressive effects on the target genes. In the present study, we combined analyses of patient samples and in silico data to (a) confirm that these circRNA/miRNA/mRNA axes are indeed dysregulated in men with NOA, and (b) determine whether their expression patterns correlate with histological subtypes of NOA or with successful sperm retrieval after micro-TESE. By elucidating these regulatory pathways, the aim is to expand the basic knowledge of the molecular aetiology of NOA and to propose new, non-invasive biomarkers that could improve the diagnosis and treatment of male infertility.

Materials and methods

Selection of transcriptomic datasets

A comprehensive search of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was performed to identify transcriptomic datasets containing profiles of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in testicular tissue from patients with NOA. To ensure data reliability and reduce potential bias, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (a) studies based exclusively on human samples; (b) availability of microarray-based gene expression data from both infertile patients and healthy controls; and (c) at least three biological samples per group. Of the approximately 35 datasets related to male infertility, 7 met these criteria and were included in the final analysis (Table 1).

Identification of differentially expressed genes and functional enrichment

Differential expression analysis was performed independently for each GEO dataset using the GEO2R online tool, which applies the limma package for platform-appropriate background correction and normalization. To avoid batch effects caused by platform heterogeneity, raw data from different datasets were not merged or jointly normalized. Instead, DEGs were identified separately within each dataset using an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.01 and |log2 fold change| > 2. Subsequently, overlapping downregulated genes across datasets were identified using Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 1). Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), were performed for the common DEGs using the web-based Enrichr platform to gain insights into the biological processes and pathways that may be involved.

Selection of hsa_circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774/ miR-146a-5p/GTSF1

As shown in Fig. 1, INPP1 and GTSF1 were found to be the most important downregulated genes in NOA. To investigate the upstream post-transcriptional regulation of these genes, we utilised multiple microRNA target prediction platforms, including miRTarBase, TargetScan, miRBase and miRDB. Integrative analysis identified miR-27a as a strong candidate for the regulation of INPP1, while hsa-miR-146a-5p was predicted as a target for GTSF1. Circular RNA databases such as circBank, circBase, CircInteractome and circAtlaswere then queried to identify circRNAs that could serve as ceRNAs for these miRNAs. This search revealed hsa_circ_0057497 as a putative sponge for miR-27a and hsa_circ_0026774 as a potential regulator of hsa-miR-146a-5p. Taken together, these results suggest two novel ceRNA axes — hsa_circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774/hsa-miR-146a-5p/GTSF1 — that may contribute to the molecular aetiology of NOA and warrant further functional validation.

Participants

A total of 60 men diagnosed with NOA were recruited from the Abortion Research Centre at the Reproductive Sciences Institute in Yazd. All participants underwent bilateral micro-TESE as part of assisted reproductive treatment for sperm retrieval for ICSI. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran (approval number: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1403.331). All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Before surgery, all patients underwent a comprehensive clinical examination, including standard karyotype analysis, screening for Y chromosome microdeletion and serum hormone tests for follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH) and testosterone. Participants with a history of cryptorchidism, previous TESE, hormone therapy or secondary infertility were excluded. In addition, individuals with chromosomal abnormalities, Y chromosome microdeletions or cystic fibrosis were not included in the study.

During micro-TESE, testicular tissue samples were taken for histological analysis. The samples were fixed in Bouin’s solution and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). A pathologist specializing in male infertility classified the NOA cases into three histopathological categories: Sertoli cell-only syndrome (SCOS; n = 20), hypospermatogenesis (HYPO; n = 20) and maturation arrest (MA; n = 20). In addition, peripheral blood samples (~ 3 ml) were taken from each patient for subsequent molecular analyses. A control group of 40 fertile men — partners of women with confirmed infertility due to the female factor — was also included for comparison purposes. Based on the results of the micro-TESE, the NOA patients were categorized into two subgroups: NOA+ (successful sperm retrieval; SCOS: n = 3, HYPO: n = 16, MA: n = 10) and NOA− (no sperm retrieval; n = 31), as summarized in Table 2.

RNA extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and RT-qPCR

After blood collection and plasma separation, total RNA was extracted from the plasma samples using the TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s manual protocol. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was assessed by gel electrophoresis. For RT-qPCR analysis, miRNAs were first polyadenylated and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using standard commercial kits following the manufacturer’s protocols. The expression of hsa_circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774/hsa-miR-146a-5p/GTSF1 was quantified by real-time PCR (Rotor-Gene Q, Qiagen). Specific primers were designed for each target: for INPP1 (forward 5′-ACTTCAAGACGCTGGCTGAT-3′; reverse 5′- TGCCACCTTGTTACCATTGA-3′), for hsa_circ_0057497 (forward 5′- AGGTTTCATTTGGCAACAGG-3′; reverse 5′- TCAGCCTCCTGAGTAGTTGG-3′), and for hsa-miR-27a-5p (forward 5′- AGGGCTTAGCTGCTTGTGAGCA − 3′; reverse 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′), for GTSF1 (forward 5′-CAGGTTCCTCGAGCTGAAAT-3′; reverse 5′-GAGTTGTGGCCCCAGACAAAT-3′), for hsa_circ_0026774 (forward 5′-CTACGATACCACGAGCATAT-3′; reverse 5′- GACTAGTCCGCGAGACACAT-3′) and for hsa-miR-146a-5p (forward 5′- TGAGAACTGAATTCCATGGGTT − 3′; reverse 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′). U6 small nuclear RNA and B2M (β-2-microglobulin) were used as internal reference controls (primers for U6: forward 5′- CTGGGCTACGGCAGCCCA-3′, reverse 5′-AACGATTCACGCATCTGCGT-3′; primers for B2M: forward 5′- AAGATGAGTATGCCTGCCGTG-3′, reverse 5′-CTGCTTACATGTCTCTCGATCCCAC-3′). Relative expression values were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test to assess differences from the control groups. For comparisons between two independent groups (NOA + vs. NOA−), an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the biomarker candidates, the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve was analyzed. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the ability of each biomarker to discriminate between NOA + and NOA− subgroups. Limited assistance from ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used for language polishing during the manuscript preparation. The scientific content was fully developed and verified by the authors.

Results

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Analysis of the integrated GEO datasets (Table 1) identified a subset of DEGs that were consistently downregulated in NOA. Among them, GTSF1 and INPP1 were selected for further analyses due to their recurrent dysregulation in several data sets (Fig. 1). To complement the functional analyses, we first examined the tissue-specific expression patterns of these genes using transcriptome data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) and the GTEx projects (Fig. 2A and B). GTSF1 showed a strongly testis-restricted expression profile with minimal to no expression in other tissues, supporting its classification as a testis-specific gene. Conversely, INPP1 showed widespread expression in various tissues, including brain, esophagus, pancreas and thyroid, while still maintaining a relatively high expression level in the testis, suggesting a possible, but not exclusive, role in testicular physiology.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to further investigate the biological relevance of the downregulated DEGs (Figs. 2C–E). In the Biological Process category (Fig. 2C), the enriched terms were mainly associated with spermatid development, axoneme assembly and microtubule bundle formation, highlighting the potential involvement of these genes in spermatogenesis and cilia-associated structures. In the cellular components domain (Fig. 2D), the downregulated genes were significantly enriched in components critical for sperm structure and function, including the sperm flagellum, motile cilium, acrosomal vesicle, and PAR polarity complex. These components are crucial for sperm motility and fertilization potential. Finally, analysis of the KEGG pathways (Fig. 2E) revealed that these DEGs are predominantly involved in lysosome and endocytosis pathways — two essential processes of cellular recycling, membrane transport and degradation. These pathways are functionally important for maintaining testicular homeostasis and supporting germ cell maturation.

Gene expression analysis of hsa_circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774/ miR-146a-5p/GTSF1 in NOA subtypes

To experimentally validate the results of our bioinformatic analyses, we examined the expression levels of INPP1, GTSF1, their corresponding miRNA regulators (miR-27a and hsa-miR-146a-5p) and the associated circular RNAs (hsa_circ_0057497 and hsa_circ_0026774) in plasma samples from patients with different NOA subtypes (HYPO, MA, SCOS) and healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 3A, INPP1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in the SCOS group (****p < 0.0001), whereas no significant downregulation was observed in the HYPO or MA group. Conversely, GTSF1 expression (Fig. 3B) was significantly downregulated in both HYPO and MA subtypes (****p < 0.0001), but remained unchanged in SCOS patients. These results suggest subtype-specific regulation of these genes, with INPP1 possibly being more relevant for pure Sertoli cell syndrome and dysregulation of GTSF1 being associated with impaired spermatogenesis in HYPO and MA stages.

Consistent with these patterns, miR-27a, the predicted upstream regulator of INPP1, was significantly upregulated in the SCOS group (Fig. 3C), while hsa-miR-146a-5p, which targets GTSF1, showed substantial overexpression in both HYPO and MA (Fig. 3D) (****p < 0.0001), consistent with the observed inverse expression profiles of their target genes. As for circRNAs, hsa_circ_0057497, which is associated with miR-27a, showed significant reduction in MA and SCOS groups (Fig. 3E), and hsa_circ_0026774, which is predicted to modulate hsa-miR-146a-5p, was strongly downregulated in HYPO and MA, with partial recovery in SCOS (Fig. 3F). These patterns suggest that dysregulation of circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axes may contribute to molecular heterogeneity between NOA subtypes. Taken together, these data support the existence of distinct but overlapping non-coding RNA regulatory circuits involving INPP1/miR-27a/hsa_circ_0057497 and GTSF1/hsa-miR-146a-5p/hsa_circ_0026774, each potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of specific NOA subtypes.

Elevated expression of INPP1, GTSF1, and associated circRNAs correlates with successful sperm retrieval in NOA patients

To determine whether the expression levels of candidate genes and regulatory non-coding RNAs can predict the outcome of sperm retrieval, we compared NOA patients with successful (NOA⁺) versus unsuccessful (NOA-) micro-TESE results. As shown in Fig. 4A–B, mRNA levels of INPP1 and GTSF1 were significantly higher in the NOA⁺ group compared to NOA patients (p < 0.0001), suggesting that increased expression of these genes may indicate preserved spermatogenic potential and a favorable outcome in sperm retrieval. Consistent with this, hsa_circ_0057497 and hsa_circ_0026774, the predicted circRNA regulators of INPP1 and GTSF1, also showed significantly higher expression in NOA⁺ patients (Fig. 4E–F). This observation supports the involvement of these circRNAs in the maintenance of gene expression through miRNA sponge activity and emphasizes their potential as positive predictors of micro-TESE success. In contrast, the expression levels of miR-27a and hsa-miR-146a-5p— - targeting INPP1 and GTSF1, respectively — were significantly increased in the NOA group (Fig. 4C–D), consistent with their proposed inhibitory roles in the respective regulatory axes. Taken together, these results suggest that higher expression of INPP1, GTSF1 and their associated circRNAs is associated with positive sperm retrieval outcomes, while upregulation of their inhibitory miRNAs is associated with testicular failure. These molecules could therefore serve as promising biomarkers for the stratification of NOA patients prior to surgical intervention.

Diagnostic performance of INPP1, GTSF1, and their regulatory ncRNAs in predicting sperm retrieval outcomes

To determine the diagnostic utility of the candidate molecular components, we performed ROC curve analyses to assess their ability to discriminate between NOA⁺ and NOA patients (Fig. 5). In panel A, hsa_circ_0057497 showed the highest diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.956 (95% CI: 0.915–0.997), indicating excellent discriminatory power. MiR-27a also showed high diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.900, 95% CI: 0.828–0.984). In contrast, INPP1 showed a rather moderate predictive value (AUC = 0.798, 95% CI: 0.687–0.909). These results suggest that both circ_0057497 and miR-27a are promising non-invasive biomarkers for the prediction of micro-TESE outcomes in NOA patients. In Panel B, hsa_circ_0026774 achieved the best diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.970 (95% CI: 0.939–1.000). miR-146a-5p also showed robust accuracy (AUC = 0.913, 95% CI: 0.840–0.987), while GTSF1 exhibited good predictive power (AUC = 0.851, 95% CI: 0.757–0.946). These results support the diagnostic relevance of these regulatory molecules in distinguishing NOA cases based on sperm recovery potential.

Discussion

This study identified two novel non-coding RNA regulatory axes – circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and circ_0026774/miR-146a-5p/GTSF1 – that appear to play an important role in NOA. Our main findings can be summarized as follows:1 Both INPP1 and GTSF1 mRNA levels are significantly downregulated in NOA patients, but with different subtype specificity – INPP1 is most strongly suppressed in men with SCOS, while GTSF1 is most strongly suppressed in HYPO and MA subtypes2. The predicted miRNA regulators of these genes show an inverse pattern: miR-27a-5p (targeting INPP1) is significantly overexpressed specifically in SCOS patients, and miR-146a-5p (targeting GTSF1) is highly overexpressed in the HYPO and MA groups. This reciprocal relationship strongly suggests that the downregulation of INPP1 and GTSF1 in NOA is at least partially due to excessive miRNA-mediated inhibition3. Upstream circular RNAs that could modulate these miRNAs are themselves dysregulated in NOA: hsa_circ_0057497 (a putative sponge of miR-27a) is markedly downregulated (particularly in MA and SCOS), and hsa_circ_0026774 (putative sponge of miR-146a-5p) is downregulated in HYPO and MA. Loss of these circRNAs would release miR-27a and miR-146a-5p from sequestration, allowing them to more strongly repress INPP1 and GTSF1, respectively4. Importantly, we found that higher expression of INPP1, GTSF1 and their associated circRNAs correlates with better clinical outcomes in NOA: Patients whose sperm were successfully retrieved (NOA⁺) had significantly higher levels of INPP1, GTSF1, circ_0057497 and circ_0026774 than patients with failed retrieval (NOA-). Conversely, inhibitory miR-27a and miR-146a-5p were dramatically increased in the NOA- group compared to NOA⁺. This contrasting expression pattern underscores a plausible causal chain: dysregulation of these circRNA–miRNA–mRNA circuits may impair spermatogenesis and thus reduce the likelihood of finding sperm in a biopsy. Overall, these results support a model in which the hsa_circ_0057497 → miR-27a → INPP1 and hsa_circ_0026774 → miR-146a-5p → GTSF1 signaling pathways contribute to the molecular pathophysiology of NOA and may serve as indicators of spermatogenic “health” in the testis. We note that the number of patients with SCOS who achieved successful sperm retrieval (SCOS-NOA⁺) in this study was small (n = 3), which limits statistical power; therefore, findings specific to the SCOS subgroup should be interpreted with caution.

These findings expand the current understanding of the aetiology of NOA in several ways. First, they emphasize that NOA is not a unitary condition but comprises different molecular subtypes. Previous gene expression studies suggested such heterogeneity: Omolaoye et al. (2022) performed a meta-analysis of testicular transcriptomes and identified a set of 25 genes that are consistently downregulated in NOA13. Remarkably, INPP1 and GTSF1 were among these top candidates, emphasizing their robustness as markers of spermatogenic failure. Our results not only confirm the dysregulation of INPP1 and GTSF1 in an independent study, but also show that the downregulation of each gene is restricted to specific histological patterns (SCOS for INPP1 versus MA/HYPO for GTSF1). This suggests that different mechanisms may underlie the different NOA subtypes – for example, Sertoli cell-only syndrome may be related to defects in Sertoli/germ cell interaction or support (reflected in loss of INPP1), whereas maturation arrest is associated with failure of germ cell-intrinsic factors such as GTSF1. Second, by integrating miRNA and circRNA data, we provide a plausible regulatory explanation for how these genes are dysregulated. Previous research had identified miR-27a-3p and miR-146a as biologically relevant in other contexts of male reproduction. For example, Norioun et al. (2020) reported that miR-27a-3p is overexpressed in testicular biopsies from NOA patients compared to OA controls and that this miRNA can directly downregulate the KDM3A gene, a histone demethylase essential for post-meiotic chromatin remodeling in spermatids14. Our finding of upregulation of miR-27a in SCOS is consistent with Norioun’s report and suggests that miR-27a may have multiple deleterious targets in spermatogenesis. Similarly, miR-146a is known to be involved in spermatogonial differentiation: In mouse models, Mir146 is highly expressed in undifferentiated spermatogonia and must be dramatically downregulated for these cells to differentiate in response to retinoic acid15. Huszar et al. (2013) showed that miR-146a directly inhibits Med1 and KIT; overexpression of miR-146 in spermatogonia blocks their differentiation, while inhibition of miR-146 promotes differentiation15. These data from animal studies are consistent with our observation that miR-146a-5p is abnormally high in NOA patients with maturation arrest. It is suggested that elevated miR-146a in these patients may suppress retinoic acid signaling or other differentiation-promoting pathways and thus contribute to the arrest of germ cell development. By locating miR-27a and miR-146a in regulatory axes with circRNAs and target genes, our study links these broader concepts to specific molecular findings in human NOA.

Our results also confirm and build on the emerging evidence that circRNAs play an important role in male infertility. Several recent studies have investigated circRNA expression in the testes or seminal plasma of NOA patients. For example, Ji et al. (2021) investigated circRNAs in seminal plasma as potential predictors for the outcome of micro-TESE16. They identified two circRNAs (derived from the PDE3B and ZHX3 genes) that were significantly upregulated in NOA patients in whom sperm retrieval was successful compared to those in whom it failed. This is consistent with our finding that circ_0057497 and circ_0026774 levels are higher in NOA⁺ patients than in NOA patients, supporting the idea that the abundance of circRNA in body fluids may reflect the presence of active spermatogenesis. On the other hand, Shi et al. (2023) reported an interesting counterexample: they found that circMGLL was elevated in testicular tissue of NOA men and that higher circMGLL expression was associated with a lower probability of sperm retrieval17. In their study, circMGLL acted as a potential negative biomarker and was included alongside clinical variables in a nomogram to predict outcomes. In contrast, the circ_0057497 and circ_0026774 we identified likely sponge miRNAs (miR-27a, miR-146a-5p) that inhibit spermatogenesis; therefore, higher levels of these circRNAs protect key genes (such as INPP1, GTSF1 or others such as KDM3A) from miRNA repression, which correlates with better spermatogenesis. This emphasizes that circRNAs can have different effects in NOA depending on their specific interactions – some circRNAs can act in pro-spermatogenic networks, while others hinder them. It also emphasizes the value of a network-based approach (involving circRNA, miRNA and mRNA) to understand the role of each molecule in context, rather than looking at each biomarker in isolation.

The comparison of our results with earlier studies also reveals methodological differences and progress. Much previous work on NOA biomarkers relied on biopsy samples of testicular tissue and focused on either coding genes or single circRNAs. In contrast, in our study we analyzed circulating (plasma) nucleic acids, which is less invasive and more clinically practical for diagnostics. Interestingly, despite analyzing the plasma, we were able to detect clear signals indicative of testicular pathology, probably because these circRNAs and miRNAs can be released from the testis into the bloodstream (possibly via exosomes). This approach builds on the “liquid biopsy” paradigm in male infertility, where it is hoped to detect testis-specific biomarkers in blood or semen. In addition, we included several comparison groups (different histological subtypes and a control group of fertile men), whereas in many studies only a binary comparison between “NOA vs. OA” or “healthy” was performed. Including NOA subclassifications in our analysis allowed us to identify more nuanced patterns that would be lost in a mass comparison, for example, GTSF1 would not appear significantly altered if all NOAs were lumped together. This finer stratification could explain why certain biomarkers appeared inconsistent across studies; differences in the proportional representation of NOA subtypes or sample sources (testicular tissue vs. blood) could lead to seemingly contradictory results. By addressing these design gaps, using integrative in silico screening, validation in a well-characterized study and analysis of relevant subgroups, our study provides a more detailed and reliable picture of molecular changes in NOA.

In the present study, we detected circulating nucleic acids rather than intracellular proteins. Although INPP1 and GTSF1 encode intracellular, testis-enriched proteins, their transcripts can be released into the bloodstream under physiological and pathophysiological conditions. Testis-derived RNAs, including mRNAs, miRNAs, and circular RNAs, may enter the circulation through mechanisms such as apoptosis, cell turnover, or secretion via extracellular vesicles and exosomes, consistent with the concept of liquid biopsy18–20. Therefore, detection of INPP1- and GTSF1-related transcripts in plasma does not imply active protein secretion but rather reflects underlying testicular gene expression.

Having established the agreement and discrepancies of our results with the literature, we can propose mechanistic interpretations for the observed regulatory axes. Our data and previous studies suggest that GTSF1 plays a critical role in maintaining germ cell survival and genomic integrity. GTSF1 is known to be involved in the PIWI-interacting RNA (piRNA) pathway, which silences transposable elements in the germline10,21. In mice, Gtsf1 knockout leads to derepression of LINE-1 retrotransposons and causes massive apoptotic death of germ cells shortly after birth22. Consistently, we found that GTSF1 mRNA is almost absent in patients with maturation arrest, a phenotype characterized by failure of spermatocytes to develop into spermatids, which is often associated with increased germ cell apoptosis. It is plausible that insufficient GTSF1 in these individuals allows for transposon activation or other genetic instability that triggers meiotic arrest and cell death, reflecting the mouse knockout phenotype.

The upregulation of miR-146a-5p in the same patients provides a plausible cause for GTSF1 insufficiency. MiR-146a has been reported to be expressed in human testis and has known pro-apoptotic and anti-differentiation effects23,24. We hypothesize that excess miR-146a-5p in the testis could directly suppress GTSF1 translation and potentially also create a more stem-like, undifferentiated environment by modulating other targets such as Med1 and KIT in the RA pathway. The end result would be a combination of lack of entry into meiosis and increased death of germ cells, consistent with the histology of maturation arrest. Meanwhile, the circRNA hsa_circ_0026774 is likely derived from a host gene or locus relevant to the testis. By acting as a molecular “sponge” for miR-146a-5p, circ_0026774 would normally contribute to fine-tuning the activity of this miRNA. In NOA patients, we observed that circ_0026774 levels are very low in HYPO and MA, implying that inhibition of miR-146a-5p is weakened, allowing miR-146a-5p to accumulate and exert stronger effects on GTSF1 and possibly other differentiation genes. This loss of circ_0026774 can thus be viewed as a permissive event that exacerbates the inhibitory milieu for germ cell maturation.

INPP1 is an enzyme of the inositol-phospholipid signaling pathway, which is of fundamental importance for many cellular processes such as cell proliferation, metabolism and apoptosis25. While INPP1 itself has not yet been studied in detail in spermatogenesis, other inositol phosphatases provide evidence of its importance. For example, INPP4B knockout mice show reduced testis size, fewer mature sperm and increased germ cell apoptosis, suggesting that inositol signaling affects germ cell survival and differentiation26. Similarly, INPP5B-deficient mice exhibit defects in Sertoli cell adhesion structures, leading to premature loss of developing germ cells12. These studies suggest that appropriate regulation of phosphoinositide metabolism is required for the maintenance of Sertoli cell germ cell architecture and support. By analogy, downregulation of INPP1 could contribute to Sertoli cell-only syndrome by disrupting signaling pathways required by Sertoli cells for germ cell maintenance. The fact that INPP1 was selectively decreased in SCOS patients in our study – and that these patients had the most extreme phenotype of missing germ cells – supports the notion that INPP1 insufficiency may be related to an environment that is unable to support spermatogenesis.

MiR-27a is an influential miRNA that plays a role in the control of the cell cycle and differentiation in various tissues27,28. In the context of spermatogenesis, it has been shown that miR-27a not only targets KDM3A, but also represses TNP1 and PRM1 via the KDM3A pathway14. Our data add INPP1 to the list of miR-27a targets. We can speculate that in some patients, excessive miR-27a activity could simultaneously hit multiple targets critical for the later stages of spermatogenesis – switching off both epigenetic programming (via KDM3A/TNP1/PRM1) and metabolic/supportive signaling (via INPP1). Such a multi-track blockade would lead to a complete failure of germ cell development, which manifests as SCOS. The circRNA hsa_circ_0057497 appears to be a protective factor in this equation. We found that circ_0057497 is drastically reduced in SCOS (and to a lesser extent in MA), implying that SCOS patients lack this miR-27a “sponge”. Under normal conditions, circ_0057497 could serve to restrict miR-27a activity in testicular cells and prevent it from over-suppressing targets such as INPP1. Its deficiency in SCOS would remove this restriction and cause miR-27a to more strongly suppress INPP1. It is noteworthy that circ_0057497 was also significantly reduced in the MA group. This may indicate that circ_0057497 has other roles or targets beyond INPP1 – possibly miR-27a also has other relevant targets in MA, or circ_0057497 may intercept other miRNAs. In comparison, conventional clinical predictors such as FSH levels and testicular volume generally yield AUC values of approximately 0.60–0.70 in the literature29, whereas the circRNA biomarkers identified in this study achieved substantially higher AUC values (e.g. AUC = 0.95–0.97 for circ_0057497 and circ_0026774), highlighting their superior predictive performance.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the transcriptomic datasets analyzed in this study were generated using different platforms, including both microarray and RNA-sequencing technologies. Because raw data were not merged, formal batch-effect correction and cross-platform normalization were not performed. Instead, we adopted a consensus-DEG strategy by identifying overlapping genes that were consistently downregulated across independently analyzed datasets, an approach commonly used when raw data integration is not feasible. Nevertheless, platform heterogeneity may influence the results and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Second, this study did not include an independent external validation study; therefore, the reported high AUC values should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in future studies. Finally, further functional assays, e.g. luciferase reporter assays for miR-27a/ has-circ_0057497 and miR-146a-5p, hsa_circ_0026774 levels in cell models, are needed to decipher the exact interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, our results elucidate the molecular heterogeneity of NOA and suggest two novel ceRNA axes — circ_0057497/miR-27a/INPP1 and circ_0026774/miR-146a/GTSF1 — that underlie distinct histopathological subtypes and each have a significant impact on whether sperm production is maintained or collapses. These networks hold promise as non-invasive biomarkers and provide mechanistic insights into the cellular perturbations affecting human spermatogenesis. By integrating computational analyses with clinical and molecular data, this study lays the foundation for biomarker-based management of NOA and advances our understanding of its complex aetiology.
