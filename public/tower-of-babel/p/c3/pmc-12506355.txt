A previously unreported triad of poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, parathyroid adenoma, and paraganglioma: random coincidence or hidden genetic etiology—a case report
Authors: Ankush Nayyar, Praneeth Potluri, Kshitij Charaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoplasms arising simultaneously from the thyroid, parathyroid, and paraganglionic tissues are exceptionally rare. To our knowledge, no previous case has documented the synchronous occurrence of poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, parathyroid adenoma, and cervical paraganglioma. This report highlights the diagnostic and embryologic significance of this rare triad.Case presentationA 79-year-old South Asian woman presented with a slowly progressive anterior neck swelling. Imaging showed a mass in the left thyroid lobe with vascular displacement. Total thyroidectomy with neck dissection was performed. Histology revealed poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma with insular architecture and high mitotic activity. Adjacent to the tumor, a parathyroid adenoma was identified. A separate encapsulated lesion near the internal jugular vein showed a Zellballen pattern consistent with paraganglioma. Immunohistochemistry confirmed all three components. There was no history of familial endocrine neoplasia.ConclusionThis case presents a unique triad of poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, parathyroid adenoma, and paraganglioma—an association not previously reported. Their coexistence in a single patient may reflect a shared embryologic origin from neural crest derivatives. Recognition of such unusual combinations can aid in accurate diagnosis, prevent misinterpretation of tumor margins, and guide appropriate surgical and follow-up decisions.

Introduction

Poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC) represents a challenging entity in thyroid oncology owing to its aggressive behavior and intermediate prognosis compared with well-differentiated thyroid carcinomas and anaplastic carcinoma [1]. With the recent inclusion of PDTC as a distinct diagnostic category in the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, its biological behavior, molecular underpinnings, and clinical management have gained renewed attention. These tumors occupy an intermediate position in the spectrum of thyroid malignancies—more aggressive than well-differentiated carcinomas, but less aggressive than undifferentiated or anaplastic forms [2, 3]. The recognition of PDTC as a separate category is justified given its distinct clinical and pathological features. The unique intersection of PDTC, parathyroid adenoma, and extra-adrenal paraganglioma represents an exceedingly rare combination in the spectrum of endocrine disorders, and to date, there exists no documented case in the medical literature that describes this specific triad. Multiple primary neoplasms in the same patient pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, influencing treatment strategies, prognosis, and long-term surveillance protocols.

Reports of synchronous thyroid and parathyroid tumors, as well as thyroid and paraganglioma tumors, exist but are limited to isolated case reports. To our knowledge, a triad involving all three tumors has not previously been described. Summarizing these rare dual presentations provides important context for understanding the uniqueness of the present case.

Clinical case

A 79-year-old South Asian female presented to the ear, nose, and throat (ENT) department with a history of gradually enlarging anterior neck swelling for 30 years, with sudden increase in size over the past 3–4 months duration. The patient also gave complaint of mild dysphagia and generalized weakness. There was/no history of dyspnea, hoarseness of voice, rapid weight loss, fever, night sweats, heat or cold intolerance, palpitations, or tremors, and no past history of neck irradiation. No familial history of endocrine or thyroid disorders was documented. There was a history of hypertension since the last 20 years, which was controlled by use of medications. The patient lived with her family, and no relevant psychosocial issues were found.

On physical examination, the patient was afebrile, with stable vital signs (blood pressure 140/85 mmHg; pulse: 78 beats per minute; respiratory rate: 16/min). On local examination, a thyroid swelling of size 7 × 8 cm2 was seen, showing diffuse enlargement predominantly on the left side (Fig. 1). The swelling was firm in consistency with irregular surface; nontender and restricted mobility was noted. The overlying skin was intact, pinchable, with no signs of inflammation. No other palpable swelling or enlarged lymph nodes were noted. Cardiorespiratory and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. There were no overt clinical signs of hypercalcemia or catecholamine excess at presentation.Fig. 1Clinical photograph showing thyroid gland swelling

Echogenic content was noted in left internal jugular vein (IJV), which appeared to be continuous with aforementioned nodule, likely IJV thrombus. The right lobe of thyroid also showed multiple nodules, the largest measuring 2.8 cm × 2 cm hyperechoic, with irregular margins and wider than taller nodule (TIRADS IV). Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was done, in which cytomorphological features were suggestive of malignancy, suspicious of papillary carcinoma of thyroid (Bethesda V). FNAC was not done at our institution, so images are not available.

Preoperative evaluation was limited to routine blood tests. Further biochemical investigations (serum calcium, parathyroid hormone, thyroid function tests, and catecholamine levels) were not pursued because the working diagnosis based on FNAC was papillary thyroid carcinoma and neither parathyroid adenoma nor paraganglioma was clinically suspected.

On the basis of clinical and radiological findings, the patient was scheduled for a total thyroidectomy with left level II–V lymph node dissection. Intraoperatively, the thyroid gland was found to be markedly enlarged and irregular, with infiltration into strap muscles and sternocleidomastoid muscle necessitating their removal along with tumor. The thyroid tissue demonstrated adherence to surrounding soft tissue, necessitating meticulous dissection. We employed advanced nerve monitoring techniques to preserve the recurrent laryngeal nerve during gland mobilization. Right superior and inferior parathyroids were identified and preserved, but parathyroids on the left side were not visualized.

During neck dissection, a well-encapsulated nodule in close proximity to the internal jugular vein was seen. The lesion was indiscreet and adherent to vascular structure. Given the suspicious nature based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative palpation, the nodule was resected en bloc along with IJV and sent separately for histopathological examination.

The entire surgical procedure was completed without intraoperative complications. The resected specimens were sent for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses.

For histopathological diagnosis, a specimen of total thyroidectomy was received along with level III and IV nodes. Right lobe of thyroid measured 7 × 6.5 × 4.5 cm3, and left lobe measured 5 × 3 × 3 cm3. Isthmus measured 2 × 2 cm2. On cut section of both lobes, a fleshy tumor was seen, involving almost the whole of the thyroid along with isthmus, replacing almost whole of the thyroid. A nodule measuring about 1.7 cm was seen near upper pole of thyroid, and a section was given from this nodule. A separate container labelled “left internal jugular vein lymph node” contained a single bit of nodular tissue measuring about 2 × 1 × 1 cm3. A tiny bit of vessel was also attached to it.

On microscopic examination of thyroid (Fig. 3A, B), a tumor composed of highly dysplastic cells was seen, composed of vaguely columnar to polygonal cells having a scant to moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm. The cells were arranged in trabeculae and insular pattern. There was evidence of brisk mitosis. The nuclei were vesicular with coarse chromatin and irregular nuclear membrane. Areas of fibrosis were also seen. Lymphovascular invasion was identified. Necrosis was not identified in our tumor. The nearest differential diagnosis was medullary carcinoma owing to large amount of collagenous stroma. IHC for calcitonin (Fig. 5A) was done, and the tumor was negative for that. IHC for thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF1) was also done, and the results came out to be positive (Fig. 5B), differentiating it from anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. In the postoperative period, serum calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were within normal limits, confirming the incidental nature of the parathyroid adenoma. Likewise, there was no biochemical evidence of catecholamine excess. This established that the paraganglioma was a nonfunctional incidental lesion.

Discussion

Poorly differentiated thyroid cancer represents an aggressive subset of thyroid malignancies. Prognostically, it occupies an intermediate position between well-differentiated and anaplastic thyroid cancers [2]. Epidemiologically, the frequency is higher in older age [4]. PDTCs are more aggressive than well-differentiated carcinomas but less aggressive than undifferentiated or anaplastic forms [2]. It accounts for only approximately 2–3% of all thyroid cancers worldwide [5]. Its clinical significance is magnified by poorer prognosis, higher recurrence rates, and a propensity for both regional and distant metastases [3, 5]. Elderly individuals, particularly those over 75 years, tend to have a poorer prognosis due to factors such as extensive local invasion, distant metastases at presentation, and decreased physiological reserves [6].

Coexistence of thyroid malignancies with parathyroid adenomas, while rare, has been documented in several case reports [7]. Removal of parathyroid disease at the time of thyroid surgery can be performed safely and avoids the need for a second procedure with its attendant risks; however, surgeons must balance this against the potential risk of hypocalcemia if a normal parathyroid gland is inadvertently excised [8]. In the present case, preoperative evaluation was limited to routine blood tests, and more specific biochemical assays (serum calcium, parathyroid hormone [PTH], thyroid function tests, and catecholamine levels) were not pursued because neither parathyroid adenoma nor paraganglioma was clinically suspected. Postoperatively, however, serum calcium and PTH levels were within institutional normal limits, and there was no clinical or biochemical evidence of catecholamine excess, thereby confirming the incidental and nonfunctional nature of both the parathyroid adenoma and the paraganglioma. The incidental discovery of a parathyroid adenoma is sometimes referred to as a parathyroid incidentaloma, defined as an unsuspected parathyroid lesion identified during imaging or surgery undertaken for other reasons [9]. Thus, our case may reasonably be regarded as a parathyroid incidentaloma.

The concurrent manifestation of parathyroid adenoma and extra-adrenal paraganglioma represents a rare clinical scenario, demanding a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic approach [10]. Parathyroid adenomas, typically benign neoplasms, lead to primary hyperparathyroidism characterized by elevated parathyroid hormone levels and subsequent hypercalcemia. On the other hand, extra-adrenal paragangliomas, arising from chromaffin cells outside the adrenal medulla, can secrete catecholamines, resulting in a spectrum of adrenergic symptoms [11]. The intersection of these two distinct endocrine disorders necessitates careful consideration of their individual and combined effects on the patient. Our case might be in rarest of the rare categories because both parathyroid adenoma and extra-adrenal paraganglioma were clinically asymptomatic and incidental findings in histopathology. Recent case reports highlight the intricate diagnostic and management dilemmas posed by the synchronous presentation of parathyroid adenoma and extra-adrenal paraganglioma, thus reinforcing the importance of meticulous clinical assessment and thorough investigation [12].

It is believed that poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma is an aggressive form of thyroid cancer derived from follicular cells. PDTC diagnosis involves assessing specific histological features [13]. The Turin proposal outlines three key criteria for classification. First, the lesion must exhibit a solid, trabecular, or insular growth pattern. Second, there should be an absence of nuclear features typically associated with papillary carcinoma. Third, the presence of at least one of the following features is required: a mitotic index of ≥ 3 per 10 high-power fields, necrosis, or convoluted nuclei [13]. These criteria help in distinguishing specific thyroid neoplasms with more aggressive behaviors, aiding in accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. In our case, trabecular pattern of growth was seen with nuclei not resembling typical papillary carcinoma-like morphology along with brisk mitosis (> 3/10 HPF). Necrosis, however, was not seen in our case. IHC was further used, and tumor cells were negative for calcitonin, which ruled out medullary carcinoma.

The occurrence of multiple endocrine neoplasms within a single patient, specifically involving the thyroid, parathyroid, and paraganglioma, represents a complex clinical scenario that necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms [14]. Multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes constitute a spectrum of inherited cancer syndromes characterized by the development of tumors in multiple endocrine glands [15]. While certain subtypes of multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) are well defined, the concurrent presentation of thyroid, parathyroid, and paraganglioma in a single individual may suggest a novel genetic etiology or a sporadic combination of events. Recent research has shed light on the genetic mutations linking thyroid cancer, parathyroid adenoma, and pheochromocytoma, particularly in the context of multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndromes. The RET proto-oncogene has been identified as a key player in the development of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and pheochromocytoma in MEN2 [16]. Germline mutations in RET are responsible for the hereditary form of these tumors, while somatic mutations are found in sporadic cases. Interestingly, mutations in the cell cycle regulator P18 have also been discovered in RET-associated MTCs and pheochromocytomas, suggesting its role as a tumor suppressor gene in these neoplasms [16]. The genetic landscape of these endocrine tumors is complex, with recent studies revealing that more than 20% of apparently sporadic cases harbor occult germline mutations characteristic of familial syndromes [17]. This finding has significant implications for patient management and genetic counseling. Furthermore, the location of tumors and the risk of malignancy have been shown to vary depending on the underlying genetic defect [17]. Interestingly our case presented with PDTC and not medullary carcinoma, as IHC was negative for calcitonin; this suggests that some unique molecular profile might be at interplay and should be evaluated. However, owing to financial constraints, we could not carry out molecular evaluation in this patient. The genetic basis of thyroid cancer, parathyroid adenoma, and pheochromocytoma is increasingly understood to be interconnected, with shared mutations and pathways contributing to their development. This knowledge has led to more targeted approaches in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategies for these endocrine neoplasms [18].

The management of PDTC is very challenging as there are no standardized guidelines owing to its low prevalence and limited available literature. Optimal management strategies for nonmetastatic PDTC include complete resection of primary disease with therapeutic neck dissection. For locoregionally advanced PDTC, complete (R0) resection without any residual disease or with microscopic residual tumor (R1) shows better survival and locoregional control over debulking or macroscopic residual (R2) tumor resection [19, 20]. Adjuvant treatment with radiotherapy after surgery for locoregionally advanced PDTC showed increased disease-specific survival compared with surgery alone [21]. Presence of distant metastasis is a poor prognostic indicator. Radioactive iodine avidity in PDTC depends on the percentage of well-differentiated component in PDTC, tumoral thyroglobulin expression, and Ki-67 index. Overall, 40–50% of PDTCs show radioactive avidity for metastatic disease [22]. Systemic chemotherapy drug or regimens have not been proven effective in treating PDTC at present.

The synchronous occurrence of poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, parathyroid adenoma, and paraganglioma in a single patient may reflect more than mere coincidence. While paragangliomas originate from neural crest-derived cells, the parathyroid glands arise from the endoderm of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches. Though historically debated, parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid are now understood to arise from an endodermal origin via the ultimobranchial body, as supported by recent lineage-tracing studies [23]. The spatial and developmental proximity of these embryologically distinct tissues within the neck may contribute to their rare synchronous neoplastic transformation.

This case holds significant educational value beyond its rarity. It highlights the importance of considering synchronous pathologies in the neck region, even when a dominant lesion is clinically apparent. The diagnostic complexity involved—spanning thyroid, parathyroid, and paraganglionic tissue—underscores the role of meticulous histopathology and immunohistochemistry in guiding accurate diagnosis. Furthermore, the triad may reflect embryological convergence of neural crest derivatives, which can inform both pathological interpretation and clinical vigilance during surgical exploration. Sharing such real-world diagnostic challenges, especially from resource-limited settings, contributes to global learning and may assist clinicians in recognizing atypical presentations in endocrine pathology.

This report has certain limitations. First, comprehensive molecular and genetic testing could not be performed, which would have provided deeper insight into potential underlying syndromic or hereditary associations. Second, preoperative biochemical evaluation for parathyroid and paraganglioma involvement (serum calcium, parathyroid hormone, thyroid function tests, and catecholamine levels) was not undertaken, as these lesions were not suspected prior to surgery. Finally, the follow-up duration is relatively short, limiting conclusions about long-term prognosis and disease course.

Conclusion

This case underscores the extraordinary coexistence of three distinct endocrine neoplasms—poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC), parathyroid adenoma, and cervical paraganglioma—in a single patient without syndromic features. The thyroid lesion displayed classic features of PDTC, including solid architecture and increased mitotic activity, while the incidental parathyroid adenoma and paraganglioma were diagnosed histologically and confirmed by immunohistochemistry. Notably, the patient had no clinical or biochemical signs of hyperparathyroidism or hereditary syndromes.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case documenting this specific triad, raising important considerations about embryologic convergence and diagnostic complexity. The case highlights the importance of thorough pathological evaluation, particularly in low-resource settings, and suggests a need for increased awareness of such rare combinations among clinicians and pathologists. Further investigation into the developmental and molecular basis of such synchronous tumors may offer new insights into endocrine tumorigenesis.
