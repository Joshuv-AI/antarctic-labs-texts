Size Dependent Uncatalyzed Sulfite Oxidation in Aqueous Microdroplets
Authors: Kedong Gong, Sandhya Sethuraman, Adriane Tam, Zifeng Tang, V. Faye McNeill, Vicki H. Grassian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aqueous aerosols and microdroplets exhibit unique chemical kinetics relative to the bulk phase, impacting air quality and climate via multiphase atmospheric processes. Here, we investigate uncatalyzed oxidation of sulfite to sulfate by O2 in aqueous microdroplets deposited on a superhydrophobic substrate, as a function of size, gas-phase composition, and temperature utilizing in situ micro-Raman spectroscopy. We show that the uncatalyzed sulfite oxidation is size-dependent across varying O2 concentrations and temperatures. Reaction rates scale with the surface-area to volume ratio (1/radius) of the microdroplet. We use a resistor-based approach to model multiphase mass transfer and reactions in the experimental system, confirming that the observed size-dependent kinetics reflect slow bulk kinetics coupled with an efficient reaction at the interfacek 2 = 9.43 × 10–3 (+2.26 × 10–3/–2.38 × 10–3) M–1 s–1, γ s,0 = 9.27 × 10–10 (+9.30 × 10–11/–1.70 × 10–10) at 298 K and 21% O2. Above a critical droplet radius, bulk kinetics dominate, but for sufficiently small atmospherically relevant sizes, rates are accelerated due to the role of the interfacial reaction. These results provide insights into chemistry in microcompartments and provide an outlook for improved representations of sulfate formation in atmospheric droplets and aerosols in large-scale atmospheric chemistry models.

Introduction

Aqueous aerosol microdroplets are unique microreactors; compared to bulk phase solutions, the air–water interface in these systems enhances reaction rates and enables redox chemistry. − This has been ascribed to enhanced mass transfer, differences in bulk and surface pH, molecular orientation, interfacial propensity, and interfacial electric field effects. − Interfacial electric field effects have been suggested to contribute to redox processes. − Examples include the spontaneous oxidation of salicylic acid and aromatic sulfones, , changes in oxidation states of interfacial iodine, and the spontaneous generation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). ,, Additionally, the spontaneous formation of H2O2 in droplets can be attributed to the chemical transformation of O3 or electronic excitation during pneumatic nebulization and electrospray ionization. , However, other studies have not observed spontaneous radical generation in droplets formed by nebulization and electrospray. The dominant factors for interfacial enhancement of specific reaction pathways continue to be poorly understood, inhibiting our ability to model these important processes.

Among these reactions, the oxidation of S­(IV) (SO3 2–, HSO3 –, and/or SO2·H2O) to form S­(VI) (SO4 2–, HSO4 –, and/or H2SO4) is fundamental for understanding air quality (haze formation) and acid rain. However, urban haze events in Asia are inadequately explained by current mechanisms including only cloudwater processes derived from bulk phase aqueous chemistry, which successfully model sulfate formation in the U.S. and Europe. The missing sulfate content scales with the aerosol water content, indicating aerosol-phase S­(IV) oxidationespecially within submicron-scale droplets or at their interfacesplays a critical role in polluted environments. Since S­(IV) oxidation pathways are highly sensitive to ionic strength and the pH of the reaction medium, as well as the role of the gas-aerosol interface, it is not possible to directly extrapolate the reaction mechanisms and kinetics for cloudwater conditions to aerosol conditions, necessitating laboratory and modeling studies in the aqueous aerosol. −

Recent studies suggest that S­(IV) oxidation is accelerated in micron-sized aqueous aerosols compared to bulk solutions. − ,− Various microdroplet-mediated S­(IV) oxidation pathways have been proposed, including uncatalyzed oxidation, − transition metal ion (TMI)-catalyzed interfacial oxidation, , and interfacial NO2-assisted oxidation. , Several mechanisms have been proposed for these reactions. Zhong et al. suggested that an H2O···SO2 intermediate facilitates the solvation of SO2 at the air–water interface. The Hoffmann group observed rapid S­(VI) formation via the uncatalyzed oxidation pathway by a direct interfacial electron transfer from S­(IV) to O2 to form sulfur radical species at the droplet surface. , Another study found that SO2–H2O complexes at the air–water interface are associated with fewer water molecules than fully dissolved SO2. , This kind of partial solvation of S­(IV) at the microdroplet surface may decrease the energy barrier of oxidation between S­(IV) and O2. These properties of the gas-aqueous interface play a significant role in the oxidation reaction. ,,,,

Among these pathways, the uncatalyzed oxidation of S­(IV) by O2 has received less attention, in part because the first-order rate constant (∼10–5 s–1) in bulk solution is too slow to be atmospherically significant, particularly at atmospherically relevant pH. , However, Hung et al. recently demonstrated that this uncatalyzed oxidation is approximately 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than that by H2O2 oxidation for 1.0 ppbv H2O2 interacting with 50 μg/m of aerosols with diameters of 1–16 μm. Chen et al. demonstrated that, at high ionic strength, typically found in atmospheric aerosols, uncatalyzed SO2 oxidation occurs rapidly in aerosols. This is even faster than Fe­(III)-catalyzed HSO3 – oxidation in bulk aqueous solutions under the same conditions, which decreases with increasing ionic strength. Additionally, Li et al. reported that S­(IV) oxidation through different oxidation pathways, including uncatalyzed oxidation, scales with droplet surface area, highlighting the significant influence of droplet size on this reaction. These findings suggest that the accurate prediction of atmospheric sulfate formation requires understanding the kinetics of uncatalyzed S­(IV) oxidation within individual aqueous aerosols as a function of size in order to simulate these processes numerically. However, until now, there has been insufficient experimental data and no modeling framework to enable representation of this process in atmospheric chemistry models to test its environmental significance. ,

In this study, we measured the oxidation of SO3 2– in microdroplets deposited on a superhydrophobic substrate using micro-Raman spectroscopy to better understand dissolved sulfite oxidation as a function of microdroplet size, gas-phase oxygen concentration, and temperature. We used an additive resistance-based model to analytically simulate multiphase mass transfer and reactions, showing that the observed size-dependent kinetics are consistent with an efficient interfacial reaction coupled with slower bulk kinetics, and we extrapolated these results to the atmospherically relevant size distribution of aerosols.

Methods

Chemicals and Materials

Sodium sulfite (98.5%) was purchased from Thermo Scientific. Sodium sulfate (≥99.0%, certified ACS), sodium chloride (≥99.0%, certified ACS), and hydrochloric acid solution (1 m) were obtained from Fisher Chemical. The quartz substrates for supporting the microdroplet were purchased from Alfa Chemistry. The Cab-O-SIL TS-720 was purchased from Inoxia Ltd. All gases, including nitrogen (medical national formulary grade), oxygen (ultra-high purity grade), and a mixture containing 21% oxygen balanced in nitrogen, were sourced from Airgas. Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ·cm–1, 4 ppb of Total-Organic-Carbon (TOC)) used for all experiments was obtained from a Milli-Q Advantage A10 Water Purification System (Millipore SAS - Molsheim, France). The superhydrophobic substrate, with a contact angle of 160.1 ± 3.4° (see Figure S1), was produced by dip-coating a quartz plate into a solution of Cab-O-SIL TS-720 suspended in acetone (20 mg/mL), followed by air drying at 200 °C on a hot plate.

Micro-Raman Spectroscopy Experiments

Bulk solutions were prepared with oxygen-free Milli-Q water (Milli-Q water purged with nitrogen) in order to reduce the potential influence of water-soluble oxygen. The 1.2 mol/kg Na2SO3 solution was prepared with an initial pH of 9.5. This pH was not further adjusted because at this pH, there is essentially one S­(IV) species, SO3 2–, presented in the bulk solution, thus limiting the number of oxidation reactions taking place in the solution and simplifying kinetic analysis (Figure S2). A high pH also reduces the potential influence of trace concentrations of TMI (i.e., catalyst species) that inevitably originate from Milli-Q water or the chemical reagents used here, according to previous studies. , Microdroplets were generated from the bulk solutions by using a nebulizer and collected on a superhydrophobic quartz substrate. The hydrophobic substrate with aerosols was transferred to a sealed environmental cell (Linkam, THMS600 Stage) connected to a humidity control system (see Figure S3). The relative humidity (RH) within the environment cell was set at ∼80 ± 3%. This was controlled by adjusting the ratio of dry and wet gases and monitored by a hygrometer (Buck, CR-4). The droplets were monitored in situ with micro-Raman spectroscopy (Horiba, LabRam HR Evolution). The spectrometer is equipped with an optical microscope (Olympus BX41), featuring 10× and 100× objectives, and a 532 nm laser. Spectra were collected at the droplet center, with kinetics largely independent of detection depth (Figure S4), using a 5 s acquisition time, 2 co-added accumulations, and a spectral resolution of ∼1 cm–1.

Results and Discussion

Size Dependent Kinetics

Figure a presents the Raman spectrum of a sulfite oxidation reaction within an aqueous microdroplet on a superhydrophobic substrate compared to a bulk solution under 21% O2 and 298 K in the environmental chamber held at 80% RH. The superhydrophobic substrate keeps the geometry of microdroplets nearly spherical and the radii of the droplets remained stable during the reaction (see Figure S14). The dominant species, sulfite (v s(SO3 2–) at 934 cm–1 and v a(SO3 2–) at 966 cm–1), was observed. , As the reaction progresses, the Raman peak for sulfate, v s(SO4 2–) at 980 cm–1 increases. This oxidation rate increases as the microdroplet size decreases. Notably, for a 1.8 μm microdroplet, the v s(SO3 2–) decreases significantly within 16 min, accompanied by a significant increase in the v s(SO4 2–) peak intensity. This observation suggests that the microdroplet environment accelerates sulfate production.

The reaction kinetics were tracked in situ for sulfite ([SO3 2–]) and sulfate ([SO4 2–]) concentrations. Figure b shows the time evolution of [SO4 2–] within both bulk solution and microdroplets with radii ranging from 1.5 to 112.0 μm, indicating that the sulfate production rate increased with decreasing microdroplet radius. A continuous increase in [SO4 2–] was observed for bulk solution and large microdroplets (≥3.7 μm), which is in accordance with the results reported by Wang et al. and Jing et al. For the 8.3 μm microdroplet with sulfite as the sulfur source and no catalyst, an inverse sulfate formation time scale (1/τsulfate) of 2.84 × 10–3 min–1 was observed, which is similar to that (2.25 × 10–3 min–1) reported by Li et al. for uncatalyzed oxidation of S­(IV) by oxygen in the 7.0-μm microdroplet. The oxidation rate constant further increased to 2.75 × 10–2 min–1 when the radius of the microdroplet decreased to 1.5 μm, which is more than 2 orders of magnitude higher compared to that in the bulk solution (1.93 × 10–4 min–1). These results suggest an enhanced uncatalyzed oxidation in the small aqueous aerosol (Figure c) and highlight the significant role of the aerosol size and interface in sulfate formation.

O2 Concentration and Temperature Dependence of Sulfate Formation

For air–water interfacial reactions, the reaction rate scales with the surface-to-volume ratio (S/V, i.e., 3/r). Figure a shows a linear relationship between 1/τsulfate and the inverse of the microdroplet radius (r) under 9% and 21% O2 conditions. 1/τsulfate increased with decreasing microdroplet size, and rates were slower under 9% O2. This finding is consistent with our expectations that O2 drives oxidation in this system. This is further demonstrated in control experiments: no oxidation was observed after replacing 21% O2 with N2 (0% O2), as depicted in Figure a. These results demonstrate that this uncatalyzed oxidation occurs at the air–water interface for droplets deposited on a superhydrophobic surface.

These experiments were also carried out at three different temperatures (298, 288, and 278 K) to study the effect of temperature on sulfite oxidation rates. Figure b shows 1/τsulfate as a function of 1/r at 278, 288, and 298 K. As seen, the sulfite oxidation rate increased with increasing temperature at a fixed RH of 80%. Although there is a fair amount of scatter in these single microdroplet measurements, these data show that increasing the temperature from 278 to 298 K increases the rate of sulfate formation by at most a factor of 2 in this size range.

Notably, the droplet pH likely decreases as Na2SO3 is fully converted to Na2SO4. The pH-dependence of 1/τsulfate in bulk solutions is well-known, and we observed this as well (Figure S15). However, we note that our analysis shows that kinetics in our system are dominated by surface processes, and the surface pH, and how it may evolve during the reaction, is not known in this study. Our experimental data for microdroplets (Figures S8–S11) show a consistent first-order linear relationship between ln­(C/C 0) and time throughout the reaction. This suggests that either (a) surface pH does not vary significantly during the reaction, or (b) the surface processes that lead to surface reaction do not vary, or vary in compensating manners, with surface pH. This observation aligns with previous studies which have shown that surface reactions can exhibit different pH dependencies compared to bulk solution phase chemistry. ,

Kinetic Modeling

We applied the additive resistance model to evaluate the relative contributions of possible processes in this multiphase kinetic system to the observed S­(IV) oxidation. After analyzing the possible limiting cases, we can eliminate some scenarios (e.g., domination by the slow particle-phase reaction) based on the observed size dependence, and others based on the anticipated rate compared to our observed rates (i.e., processes limited by the rate of collision of O2 with the surface are ruled out due to very high O2 collision rates and moderate observed reaction rates). Additionally, we eliminate scenarios limited by in-particle diffusion or gas-phase diffusion, which both have short sulfate formation time scales that are incompatible with moderate observed reaction rates, and differ from the observed size dependence (Figure ). At 298 K, the value of γp,diff (which quantifies the contribution of in-particle diffusion) ranges from 6.98 × 10–3 (at small radii, 1.5 μm), to 9.35 × 10–5 (at large radii, 112.0 μm). At 298 K, the value of γdiff (which quantifies the contribution of gas-phase diffusion) ranges from 6.17 × 10–2 (at small radii, 1.5 μm), to 8.05 × 10–4 (at large radii, 112.0 μm). Because these values are much larger than the typical values for either γs or γrxn by several orders of magnitude, including these in the overall rate calculation would be negligible, since the total resistance is calculated by taking the inverses of the decoupled processes.

Time scale analysis shows that in-particle diffusion yields high rates which are unlikely to account for the moderate oxidation observed in experiments. The lines for gas-phase diffusion and in-particle diffusion overlap, although gas-phase diffusion is even faster (time scale ∼10–7 min). The critical radius is given by the point where the time scales for the bulk-limited and surface-limited processes cross. The time scales of the slow bulk reaction, surface reaction, and particle phase diffusion are given by τbulk, τs, and τdiff, respectively. We conclude that an efficient (but not fast) interfacial reaction in parallel with a slow bulk reaction best describes the experimental system. This is consistent with recent studies of S­(IV) oxidation chemistry.

We use Monte Carlo (MC) analysis to find optimal values of k 2 and β and obtain fits from experimental data; as we know, k 2 relates to γrxn and β relates to γs,0. After generating 10,000 random samples from a joint k 2 and β distribution, we minimize the weighted sum of squares between the derived concentration profile and the experimental data, where more importance is given to data sets with more points, which typically correspond to smaller radii. To assess the confidence for the fitted parameters and provide a range for potential values, we select the top 5% of samples with the smallest weighted sum of squares, i.e., those exhibiting the best performance. This analysis shows that the system is more sensitive to β than it is to k 2. To further refine the parameter space given this relative insensitivity to k 2, we use the critical radius reported by Li et al. as the size where the dominant process transitions from surface to bulk kinetics (Figure ). For uncatalyzed oxidation at pH 9, which is consistent with our experimental conditions, they reported a critical radius of 213.0 μm (+62/–48). We used this to place bounds on k 2, as this radius should represent the size at which the formation time scales from the surface and bulk reactions are exactly equal. From this analysis, we determine β = 8.42 × 10–10 (+8.30 × 10–11/–1.55 × 10–10) at 298 K, and k 2 = 9.43 × 10–3 (+2.26 × 10–3/–2.38 × 10–3) M–1 s–1. Li et al. report a pseudo-first order rate coefficient of value 1.31 × 10–6 s–1, which is within an order of magnitude of Radojevic et al., who report a first order bulk rate coefficient of 1.51 × 10–5 s–1. Other studies, however, report that at more basic conditions (pH 7, pH 8.2–8.8), pseudo-first-order rate coefficients are 3.5 × 10–3 and 1.3 × 10–2 s–1, respectively. , While we model our system as a second order process, these are consistent with the pseudo-first order rates that we would obtain using our k 2 values, which would range from 1.41 × 10–3 to 2.34 × 10–3 s–1. Due to the differences in our experimental setups and approach to constraining k 2 based on the transition point from surface to bulk kinetics, we report k 2 as a relatively narrow range at pH 9, consistent with Brandt and Eldik’s approximation, but there is room for expanding the uncertainty in this parameter in future work.

Proposed Mechanism

According to the model results, the surface reaction is more efficient than the bulk reaction in aqueous aerosols, highlighting the importance of understanding its underlying mechanism. Recently, autoionization of water near the air–water interface to generate •OH, H•, and solvated e– (–) has been widely considered to be the triggering step for accelerated reactions in aqueous microdroplets. ,, Spontaneous H2O2 formationa well-known examplecan occur via the recombination of •OH () or HO2 • (–) and interaction between HO2 • and H• (). These radical reactions may enhance the uncatalyzed SO3 2– oxidation via H2O2 formation (). For , some H+ remains available even under high pH conditions, as H3O+ is enriched at the air–water interface whereas OH– tends to be repelled from the interface and resides beneath it. ,

The overall kinetics depend linearly on O2 concentration (Figure ), suggesting that O2 is a reactant in the rate limiting step. Based on this, we suggest that a possible candidate for the rate limiting step is O2 + e– → O2 •–. While previous studies propose OH– as the electron donor at the droplet surface, the higher charge density and stronger electron-donating ability of SO3 2– make it plausible that SO3 •– and solvated e– may also form at the interface (). The standard reduction potential of •OH/OH ranges from 1.25 to 2.10 V (calculated ΔG° ≈ 120.6–202.6 kJ/mol, pH 14 to 0), while that of SO3 •–/SO3 2– appears to be ∼0.63 V (calculated ΔG° ≈ 183.3 kJ/mol). If assuming this potential acts over 5 Å, they correspond to electric fields of 2.5–4.2 × 107 V/cm for •OH/OH and 1.3 × 107 V/cm for SO3 •–/SO3 2–. These results suggest that SO3 2– may more readily release e–. Recently, both simulation and experimental results confirmed a strong electric field at the droplet surface ranging from 1.8 to 8.0 × 107 V/cm, , which seems sufficiently strong to facilitate the release of electrons from SO3 2– or even OH–. Generated SO3 •– can be oxidized directly by O2 (), accelerating sulfite oxidation via the sulfur radical pathway (–).

To confirm the role of radicals in this interfacial process, we investigated the effects of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), methanol (MeOH), and 1-butanol on the reaction kinetics in microdroplets (Figure S17). DMSO functions as both an •OH scavenger and O-atom acceptor. 1/τsulfate decreased with increasing DMSO concentrations from 0 to 50 mmol/kg, confirming the involvement of •OH or HO2 •/O2 •– radical. Under 36–50 mmol/kg DMSO, 1/τsulfate plateaued with an overall reduction of ∼75.5%, suggesting the coexistence of both bulk and surface radical reactions, and the significant role of the radical reaction. MeOH (a scavenger for •OH and SO4 •–) and 1-butanol (a scavenger for •OH) were also used to determine the contribution of SO3 •–. Unexpectedly, 1/τsulfate with MeOH was higher than that with 1-butanol, which may be attributed to additional H2O2 formation, consistent with previous studies. , These results underscore the importance of radical chemistry in this system, particularly •OH.

We propose that both the H2O2 oxidation pathway (–) and the SO3 •–reaction mechanism (–) reported by Hung et al. , are plausible here. Both processes may be enhanced at the interface. Among them, H2O2 is more likely formed via (k 6 = 1.8 × 1010 M–1·s–1), (k 7 = 5.0–7.2 × 1010 M–1·s–1) and (k 9 = 1.2 × 1010 M–1·s–1), rather than (k 8 = 1–31 × 106 M–1·s–1) due to its slower kinetics. Sulfate ions have been shown to have a propensity to move toward the aqueous bulk, and sulfite may reside at the near-surface region (but not at the interface). A hydration layer at the interface may provide a buffer for direct surface collisions. These factors may explain the relatively low reactive uptake probabilities inferred in this study. Schematic of Proposed Sulfite Oxidation Mechanisms at the Air–Water Interface and within Aqueous Aerosols

Based on the kinetic modeling analysis and the range of microdroplet sizes utilized in the current study, our data suggest that interfacial processes are driving the reaction chemistry in soluble S­(IV) to S­(VI) oxidation. Notably, this study utilizes droplets on solid surfaces to investigate processes relevant to atmospheric aerosols. In order to investigate the possibility that interfacial effects, whether redox chemistry, , electric field effects, or others, are specific to the substrate droplet interface in our experiment, we repeated our experiment with suspended droplets via an aerosol optical tweezer (AOT). The results show similar size-dependent kinetics, consistent with our substrate-supported study (Figure S19), highlighting the role of the gas-droplet interface. The enhanced surface reaction of uncatalyzed S­(IV) oxidation at droplet surfaces remains an open issue that requires further study.

In addition to enhanced kinetics at the surface, it is also possible that the surface analog of the Henry’s law constant (H s), is significantly larger than the H value in the bulk. This pattern, observed in an MD simulation study by Vacha et al., yields concentrations of O2 at the air–water interface that are 40 times the concentrations in the bulk, and could be even larger when considering salting-out effects. A higher concentration of O2 at the surface would increase the surface reaction rate.

Environmental Implications

While the interfacial reaction clearly leads to enhanced rates in the experimentally generated particles (<5 μm in radius in this study), we can further utilize our model to extrapolate these results to the most atmospherically relevant size range for aqueous aerosols (including submicron sizes). We find that the sulfate formation time scales in atmospherically relevant particles are smaller than in larger particles, indicating that oxidation takes place more quickly, likely due to the rate enhancement from an efficient interfacial reaction (Figure a). Compared to larger particles from laboratory experiments, formation time scales are ∼1 order of magnitude smaller. The value of 1/τsulfate for a particle of radius 0.24 μm, which is the average size in a typical sulfate distribution, is 0.108 min–1. This result indicates that efficient size-dependent chemistry is likely important in the atmosphere and could account for some of the missing sources of sulfate formation. Future implementation of the modeling framework developed here into atmospheric chemistry models will be necessary to test its relative significance. ,

Interestingly, the relative contributions of the bulk and surface processes (assuming the same kinetic parameters apply for an atmospherically relevant size range as to the larger microdroplets), show that the contribution of the surface reaction to the overall reactive uptake coefficient is significantly larger for smaller particles. As shown in Figure b, the contribution of surface processes can account for nearly 100% when the aerosol radius is below 10 μm, then gradually decreases to ∼50% when the aerosol radius is 200.0 μm. This is consistent with the observations of Li et al., who noted the importance of the interfacial pathway for this system below the critical radius of 213 μm (Figure b).

Surface reactions for small particles are ∼100 times faster than those in the bulk-phase, and we show that the sulfate formation rate scales with increased temperatures and O2 concentrations across the size distribution. This underscores the critical role of size-dependent chemistry in sulfate formation, potentially enhanced by the air–water interface of the aqueous aerosol. The interfacial electric field at the aqueous aerosol surface potentially initialized this enhanced surface uncatalyzed oxidation. Overall, we show that size-dependent uncatalyzed sulfite oxidation is likely important in the atmosphere by coupling experiments with a modeling framework, and these insights will be used to improve model predictions of atmospheric sulfate formation in fine aerosols.
