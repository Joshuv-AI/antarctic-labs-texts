Periprocedural Assessment of Paravalvular Regurgitation After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Using Diastolic Delta and Videodensitometry
Authors: Maxim J P Rooijakkers, Ahmed Elkoumy, Niels A Stens, Marleen H van Wely, Geert A A Versteeg, Tsung‐Ying Tsai, Laura Rodwell, Robin H Heijmen, Patrick W Serruys, Osama Soliman, Niels van Royen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParavalvular regurgitation (PVR) is a common complication after transcatheter aortic valve replacement, posing an increased risk of heart failure and mortality. Accurate intraprocedural quantification of PVR is challenging. Both hemodynamic indices and videodensitometry can be used for intraprocedural assessment of PVR. We compared the predictive value of the isolated versus combined use of the hemodynamic index diastolic delta (DD) and videodensitometry for the incidence of relevant PVR 1 month after transcatheter aortic valve replacement.Methods and ResultsIn this prospective cohort study, patients underwent periprocedural PVR assessment by DD and videodensitometry (using left ventricular outflow tract—aortic regurgitation [LVOT‐AR]). Cardiac magnetic resonance served as reference modality for PVR assessment. Relevant PVR was defined as cardiac magnetic resonance–regurgitant fraction >20%. Fifty‐one patients were enrolled in this study. Mean age was 80.6±5.2 years and 45.1% of patients were men. Mean LVOT‐AR and cardiac magnetic resonance–regurgitant fraction were 8.2%±7.8% and 11.7%±9.6%, respectively. The correlation between DD and LVOT‐AR was weak (r=−0.36). DD and LVOT‐AR showed a comparable accuracy to predict relevant PVR (area under the curve 0.82, 95% CI: 0.69–0.95 versus area area under the time–density curve 0.80, 95% CI: 0.62–0.99). The combination of DD and LVOT‐AR improved the prediction of relevant PVR (area under the time–density curve, 0.90, 95% CI: 0.81–0.99), and resulted in an increased concordance (86.3%) and positive predictive value (75%) compared with DD alone (76.5% and 40%, respectively), or LVOT‐AR alone (82.3% and 50%, respectively).ConclusionsDD and videodensitometry are both accurate and feasible modalities for the assessment of PVR after transcatheter aortic valve replacement. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is an established minimally invasive alternative to surgery in patients with severe aortic stenosis across the spectrum of surgical risk. 1 , 2 However, paravalvular regurgitation (PVR) remains an important shortcoming of TAVR, with incidences of mild PVR up to 40%, and incidences of ≥ moderate PVR up to 10% in contemporary TAVR studies. 3 , 4 Not only ≥ moderate PVR affects morbidity and mortality, but also mild to moderate and even mild PVR are associated with increased mortality. 5 , 6 Therefore, accurate grading of PVR severity during the TAVR procedure is essential to guide additional interventions (e.g., post‐dilation) to reduce PVR and improve patient outcome.

The adoption of the minimalist TAVR approach has precluded the use of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) in procedural detection of PVR, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) has several limitations in the assessment of PVR, potentially underestimating the degree of PVR. 7 , 8 Angiographic grading using the visual Sellers' method is well feasible but is subjective and lacks diagnostic accuracy. 9 The limitations of contemporary imaging modalities highlight the need for objective modalities for the periprocedural assessment of PVR.

Recently, 2 new modalities have been introduced for the assessment of PVR during TAVR: invasive hemodynamic indices and quantitative videodensitometric aortography. Both techniques are well feasible, reproducible, inexpensive, can be used during the TAVR procedure, and have been validated against gold‐standard cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). 10 , 11 Invasive hemodynamic indices to detect PVR are easy to use, do not require additional instrumentation, and are related to clinical outcome after TAVR. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 In the APPOSE (Assessment of Paravalvular Regurgitation After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement by Hemodynamic Measurements and Cardiac Magnetic Resonance) trial, we found that diastolic delta (DD) had the highest predictive value for relevant CMR‐assessed PVR. 10 Videodensitometry can also be used to assess PVR. It provides a quantitative assessment of the regurgitant fraction (RF) from the final aortogram after valve implantation and can be used both online and offline. 11 , 17 However, it is not known whether hemodynamic indices and videodensitometry can be used interchangeably, and whether they perform equally. Also, the potential additive performance of the combined use for the prediction of relevant (CMR‐assessed) PVR has never been evaluated.

Therefore, the aims of this substudy of the APPOSE trial were (1) to compare DD with videodensitometry in the assessment of PVR during TAVR, (2) to assess the predictive value of the isolated use of DD versus videodensitometry for relevant PVR as quantified by CMR 1 month after TAVR, and (3) to assess the predictive value of the combined use of DD and videodensitometry for relevant PVR.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This study was performed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting observational studies. 18

Population and Design

The APPOSE trial was an investigator‐initiated, prospective single‐center study evaluating the accuracy of periprocedural assessment of hemodynamic indices to predict relevant PVR as quantified by CMR at 1 month after TAVR. 10 In brief, patients were eligible for the study if they underwent TAVR for severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, with main exclusion criteria a preexisting cardiac device, left ventricular ejection fraction <30%, and a serum creatinine >250 μmol/L or end‐stage renal disease. In all patients, a self‐expanding valve (Portico; Abbot Structural Heart, St. Paul, MN) was implanted, with valve sizes ranging between 23 and 29 mm. Between November 2019 and October 2021, 103 patients provided written informed consent for the APPOSE trial. A total of 77 patients were included in the primary analysis. Patients were eligible for the present substudy after successful quantification of PVR by both CMR and videodensitometry. The study protocol was approved by the local Medical Research Ethics Committee and by the institutional review board of the Radboud University Medical Center.

Hemodynamic Measurements

The TAVR procedure was performed according to routine local protocol, which has been described previously. 10 Four hemodynamic indices (DD, heart rate–adjusted DD, aortic regurgitation index [ARI], and ARI ratio) were derived from the simultaneous pressure measurements in the aorta and left ventricle. These pressure measurements were performed at baseline (i.e., before pre‐dilation), directly after implantation of the Portico valve, and (if applicable) after post‐dilation. The hemodynamic measurements at baseline and after final TAVR result were used for calculation of the hemodynamic indices. For each hemodynamic index, the averaged pressures were used of at least 3 representative cardiac cycles during sinus rhythm, or at least 5 representative cycles during atrial fibrillation. Performing these pressure measurements requires ≈2 to 3 minutes, including the positioning of the fluid‐filled pigtail catheters in the aorta and left ventricle, and recording of the pressure measurements.

The 4 hemodynamic indices were computed using the following calculations:

DD: end‐diastolic blood pressure−left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure

DD: end‐diastolic blood pressure−left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure

Heart rate‐adjusted DD: (DD/heart rate)×80

Heart rate‐adjusted DD: (DD/heart rate)×80

ARI: (DD/systolic blood pressure)×100

ARI: (DD/systolic blood pressure)×100

ARI ratio: ARI after implantation/ARI before implantation

ARI ratio: ARI after implantation/ARI before implantation

PVR Assessment Using Quantitative Videodensitometry

The quantitative videodensitometry technique was described previously. 19 In brief, time–density curves are generated in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) (region of interest) and in the aortic root where the contrast is injected (reference area). From these time–density curves, the area under the time‐density curve (AUC) is automatically computed to represent the time–density integral. The regurgitant fraction (LVOT‐AR) is calculated by dividing the AUC of the LVOT by the AUC of the aortic root. 11 The following previously validated cutoff values to classify PVR severity were used 20 : RF ≤6% indicates none/trace PVR, RF 7% to ≤17% indicates mild PVR, and RF >17% indicates ≥ moderate PVR. For videodensitometric analysis, at least 3 cardiac cycles after contrast injection are averaged, irrespective of the heart rhythm.

The anonymized Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine files of the TAVR procedure were shared with the CORRIB Core Laboratory (University of Galway, Galway, Ireland) for independent offline (retrospective) analysis of the final aortic angiograms (i.e., after post‐dilation if applicable) after implantation of the Portico valve. Quantitative videodensitometry was performed using CAAS A‐Valve version 2.1.2 (Pie Medical Imaging, Maastricht, the Netherlands) to assess PVR severity expressed as regurgitant fraction. This software has a low system requirement and demands no additional equipment. The analysis by a trained individual requires ≈2 to 4 minutes, including saving the results in a PDF, XML, or Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format.

CMR Measurements

All patients were scanned 4 to 6 weeks after TAVR on a commercially available 1.5 T CMR scanner (MAGNETOM Avanto; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Details on the CMR acquisition and analysis are described elsewhere. 10 , 21 Briefly, flow acquisitions were performed during successive end‐expiratory breath‐holds using a high velocity encoding of ≥180 cm/s to quantify the forward (i.e., high‐flow) volume, and a low velocity encoding of 75 cm/s to quantify the regurgitant (i.e., low‐flow) volume. The regurgitant fraction was calculated by dividing the regurgitant volume (low velocity encoding) by the forward volume (high velocity encoding), multiplied by 100. Image analysis was performed offline with Medis Suite MR version 4.0.24.4 (Medis Medical Imaging, Leiden, the Netherlands).

End Points

End points were (1) the correlation between hemodynamic indices and LVOT‐AR; (2) the predictive value of hemodynamic indices alone, LVOT‐AR alone, and the combination of both modalities for the occurrence of relevant PVR 1 month after TAVR as quantified by CMR; and (3) the concordance, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the individual modalities and its combination for the occurrence of relevant PVR 1 month after TAVR. Cutoffs associated with relevant PVR were ≤32 mm Hg for DD 10 and>17% for LVOT‐AR. 20 , 22

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean±SD, or number (percentage), as appropriate. Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to quantify the correlation between continuous variables (regarding the variables DD, LVOT‐AR, and CMR‐RF). The area under receiver operating characteristic curve was used to assess the predictive value of the (1) DD, (2) LVOT‐AR, and (3) combination of the DD and LVOT‐AR for relevant PVR (CMR‐RF >20%). To allow receiver operating characteristic analysis, relevant PVR was defined as CMR‐RF >20%. 23 Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess the incremental value of the combined versus isolated use of DD and LVOT‐AR for the prediction of relevant PVR. 24 All statistical tests were 2‐tailed, and a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

The (inverse) correlation between DD and LVOT‐AR was −0.36 (P=0.01) (Figure 1). The agreement (i.e., either DD ≤32 mm Hg and LVOT‐AR >17%, or DD >32 mm Hg and LVOT‐AR ≤17%) between DD and LVOT‐AR was 70.6%. Mean DD was significantly lower in the subgroup with LVOT‐AR >17% compared with the LVOT‐AR ≤17% subgroup (P=0.03) (Figure 2).

Comparison of DD and Videodensitometry With CMR

The (inverse) correlation between DD and CMR‐RF was −0.29 (P=0.04) (Figure 3A) and the correlation between LVOT‐AR and CMR‐RF was 0.52 (P<0.001) (Figure 3B). Isolated use of the DD and LVOT‐AR resulted in similar predictive values for relevant PVR (AUC DD: 0.82 [95% CI, 0.69–0.95]; AUC LVOT‐AR: 0.80 [95% CI, 0.62–0.99]). The AUC of PVR on predischarge TTE versus relevant CMR‐assessed PVR (i.e., CMR‐RF >20%) was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.72–0.95). Concordance between DD ≤32 mm Hg and CMR‐RF >20% was 76.5%, whereas concordance between LVOT‐AR >17% and CMR‐RF >20% was 82.3%. PPV and NPV of DD for relevant PVR were 40% and 91.7%, respectively (Table 3). PPV and NPV for LVOT‐AR were 50% and 88.4%, respectively.

The combination of both modalities yielded an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.81–0.99) (Figure 4).

The NRI was 1.08 (95% CI, 0.48–1.68; P<0.001) and the IDI was 0.15 (95% CI, 0.01–0.29; P=0.03) for the combination of DD and LVOT‐AR compared with DD alone. An improved AUC was observed for all hemodynamic indices after combining them with the LVOT‐AR, compared with either the AUC of the hemodynamic index alone or the AUC of the LVOT‐AR alone (Figure 4, Figure S1).

Discussion

The aims of this APPOSE substudy were to (1) assess the correlation between the DD and videodensitometry, (2) assess the predictive value of the isolated use of DD versus videodensitometry for the incidence of relevant PVR after TAVR, and (3) determine the additive value of combining the DD and videodensitometry for the prediction of relevant PVR as quantified by gold‐standard CMR.

The key findings can be summarized as follows. First, the correlation between DD and videodensitometry was significant but weak (correlation coefficient, −0.36). Second, DD and videodensitometry yielded an equal and high predictive value for relevant PVR as assessed with CMR (AUCs 0.82 and 0.80, respectively). Third, the combination of DD and videodensitometry yielded a higher AUC, NRI, IDI, concordance, and PPV for relevant PVR than the isolated use of the DD or videodensitometry. Taken together, these results highlight the potential of DD and videodensitometry for objective and accurate grading of PVR after TAVR.

Although the incidence of PVR is decreasing with newer‐generation TAVR devices and enhanced operator skills, 25 the risk of PVR is still considered an important shortcoming of TAVR. With the increasing evidence that mild to moderate or even only mild PVR is associated with adverse outcomes, in terms of both morbidity and mortality, every effort should be made to reduce the degree of PVR. 5 , 6 Accurate grading of PVR is therefore of paramount importance, especially since various countermeasures are periprocedurally available to reduce PVR. Multiple imaging modalities, such as TTE and angiography, are being used for the assessment of PVR after TAVR. TTE is widely available, inexpensive, and easy to use. However, accurate assessment of PVR by TTE can be challenging due to poor acoustic windows (e.g., due to obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or acoustic shadowing of the TAVR stent frame) and multiple and eccentric jets, potentially underestimating the degree of PVR as compared with CMR. 7 , 8 , 23

With the widely adopted minimalist TAVR approach, which uses only local anesthesia and minimal sedation, the use of transesophageal echocardiography for intraprocedural PVR assessment is becoming infeasible for most patients. Traditional angiographic assessment using the visual Sellers' method is widely available, but lacks diagnostic accuracy in quantifying PVR. 26 , 27 CMR is considered an accurate and reproducible technique in the assessment of PVR and is acknowledged as the gold standard for measurements of left ventricular volume, mass, and ejection fraction. 2 , 28 The accuracy of CMR is less influenced by the number and eccentricity of paravalvular leaks than echocardiography. However, CMR does not permit intraprocedural assessment of PVR.

Transvalvular hemodynamic indices are readily available and can be performed at various moments during TAVR. These hemodynamic indices have previously been shown to be of prognostic value for post‐TAVR survival. It was demonstrated that DD ≤18 mm Hg, 12 heart rate‐adjusted‐DD <25 mm Hg/bpm, 13 ARI <25, 14 and ARI ratio <0.6 15 were all independently associated with mortality after TAVR. In addition, ARI ratio <0.6 appeared to be the strongest predictor of 1‐year death after TAVR. 16 Also, these hemodynamic indices have been assessed for their predictive value for relevant PVR. We previously showed that DD had the strongest correlation with CMR‐RF and the highest predictive value for relevant PVR. 10

Alternatively, quantitative videodensitometry has emerged as a well‐feasible and highly reproducible method in the assessment of PVR after TAVR, which can be used both online and offline. 11 , 17 Videodensitometry has been validated in vivo and in vitro, and has robust outcome data to support its prognostic value. 29 In a previous study by Abdel‐Wahab et al., a substantial correlation (r=0.78) was found between quantitative assessment of PVR using CMR and videodensitometry. 11 In our study, we also found a significant but somewhat weaker correlation between both modalities (r=0.52). This discrepancy may be explained by the differences in type of TAVR device, CMR postprocessing software, and sample size. The average LVOT‐AR of 8.2% in our cohort is well comparable with those described for other TAVR platforms. 29 , 30 , 31

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study directly comparing hemodynamic indices with videodensitometry for the assessment of PVR after TAVR. In our study, we found a significant but rather weak (r=−0.36) correlation between DD and videodensitometry. This weak intermodality correlation and the high predictive values of both the DD and videodensitometry for relevant PVR suggest an important role for complementary use of these modalities. The predictive value for relevant PVR increased (i.e., from AUC 0.80 to AUC 0.90) after combination of both techniques, and the NRI and IDI indicated a significant improvement of the predictive value after combining both modalities. We found a slight increase in the concordance from 76.5% (DD alone) and 82.3% (videodensitometry alone) to 86.3% after combining DD with videodensitometry. In addition, we found a strong increase in the PPV from 40% (DD alone) and 50% (videodensitometry alone) to 75% after combining both modalities. The combination of both modalities was associated with only a modest decrease in the NPV (87.2%) compared with the NPVs of the individual modalities (91.7% for DD, 88.4% for videodensitometry).

Future Perspectives

The number of TAVR procedures is annually increasing. 25 With the expanded indications to also low‐risk patients, and potentially asymptomatic patients and patients with moderate aortic stenosis, the need for accurately quantifying PVR is undisputable. Hemodynamic indices and videodensitometry are 2 useful techniques that individually, but more importantly synergistically, can aid the operator in periprocedural decision making. Future studies should address the feasibility of the combined use of these modalities.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was limited, partly due to a number of patients without analyzable videodensitometry. However, with the implementation of acquisition protocols with computed tomographic guidance to optimize the angiographic projection, the analyzability of videodensitometry substantially increases to >95%. 17 , 32 Optimization of the computed tomography–guided angiographic projection includes avoidance of overlap between the contrast‐filled descending aorta with the LVOT (region of interest) and aortic root (reference area). 29 In addition, the analyzable and nonanalyzable patients have similar characteristics and CMR‐RF as shown in Table S3. Second, the time difference between angiography (used for videodensitometry) and CMR might have contributed to the difference in PVR quantification between the imaging modalities. Third, this study was solely performed with the Abbott Portico valve, precluding the direct translation of these results to other types of TAVR devices.

Conclusions

DD and videodensitometry are both accurate and feasible modalities for the intraprocedural assessment of PVR after TAVR. The combination of both techniques increases the predictive value for relevant PVR after TAVR.
