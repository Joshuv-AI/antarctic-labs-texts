Causal effects of gut microbiota on diabetic kidney disease: insights from 16S rRNA sequencing and bidirectional Mendelian randomization
Authors: Weiming Wang, Liwen Zhang, Ying Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysbiosis of the gut microbiome plays a critical role in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) development and progression. We stratified 46 type 2 DKD patients from Ruijin Hospital into early or advanced DKD groups in this cross-sectional study. Fecal samples underwent 16S rRNA sequencing. Statistical analyses (t-tests, Chi-square, GLMs) and MR were performed using SPSS and R. We found that advanced DKD patients exhibited distinct gut microbiota profiles, with LEfSe analysis showing higher Butyricimonas, Fusicatenibacter, and Barnesiella and lower Allisonella compared to early DKD, while DKD-susceptible individuals had elevated Fusobacterium and reduced Allisonella and Eubacterium. GLMs linked Barnesiella, Streptococcus, Fusobacterium to DKD susceptibility (p < 0.05), and Slackia and Eubacterium to early/resistant DKD. MR analysis demonstrated causality: Barnesiella (OR: 2.382), Butyricimonas (OR: 1.278), Desulfovibrio (OR: 2.518), and Hemophilus (OR: 1.622) worsened DKD, whereas Slackia and Allisonella protected against eGFR decline (p < 0.05). Reverse MR revealed the bidirectional effects—severe DKD increased Ruminococcus2 but suppressed Allisonella and Akkermansia (p < 0.05), underscoring microbiota-DKD interplay. Sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, weighted median, leave-one-out) confirmed the robustness and directionality of these causal effects. In conclusion, 10 key bacterial genera were causally linked to DKD progression and susceptibility, including harmful (Barnesiella, Butyricimonas, Desulfovibrio, Hemophilus, Bacteroides, Streptococcus, Ruminococcus2) and protective taxa (Slackia, Allisonella, Akkermansia). These genera serve as novel biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification, and as potential therapeutic targets for microbiota-modulating interventions to mitigate DKD progression. The bidirectional microbiota-DKD interplay further underscores the promise of targeting gut dysbiosis in DKD precision management.Keywords: Causal inference, genetic epidemiology, microbiome-targeted therapy, diabetic kidney disease biomarkers, gut microbiota

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), a major microvascular complication of diabetes, is characterized by albuminuria or declining estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [1,2]. Despite intensive glycemic control, significant inter-individual variability in DKD progression persists [3], suggesting the involvement of non-glycemic factors such as gut microbiota dysbiosis.

The gut microbiome, comprising over 100 trillion microbial cells, critically regulates host metabolism, immune function, and mucosal integrity [4]. Microbial cells are the susceptibility factors for the development of nephropathy in individuals predisposed to nephropathy, such as diabetes mellitus (DM) patients [5]. In DKD, gut microbiota may influence disease progression through altered endocrine signaling and microbial metabolite production (e.g., short-chain fatty acids, uremic toxins). Conversely, hyperglycemia and declining renal function can exacerbate dysbiosis, creating a potential feedback loop [6,7]. Although observational studies report microbial differences between DKD patients and controls, critical knowledge gaps persist: (1) whether these alterations are causal drivers or secondary consequences of DKD, and (2) which specific taxa exert bidirectional effects on disease progression. Resolving these questions requires causal inference approaches, such as Mendelian randomization (MR), to disentangle microbiome-DKD interactions beyond correlations.

We hypothesized that: (1) Specific gut microbial genera show causal associations with DKD progression phenotypes, and (2) bidirectional microbiota-kidney interactions exist, where disease severity reciprocally reshapes microbial composition. By integrating 16S rRNA sequencing with MR analysis, this study aims to identify causal microbial targets for DKD prediction and intervention.

Methods

Cross-sectional study

Participant recruitment and ethical considerations

The study was conducted with the informed consent of each participant, ensuring voluntary participation in all measurements and questionnaires. The Research Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China, granted approval for this study protocol.

From June 2019 to February 2020, we recruited 46 clinically diagnosed type 2 DKD patients from the Department of Nephrology of Ruijin Hospital. The inclusion criteria were: (i) age between 18 and 90 years; (ii) clinical diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) and DKD, based on the presence of albuminuria (urine albumin excretion ≥30 mg/day or ≥30 mg/g) or reduced eGFR (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 using the CKD-EPI formula); and (iii) absence of serious illnesses such as heart failure, liver cirrhosis, malignancy, hematological or autoimmune diseases, and gastrointestinal disease. Exclusion criteria included: (i) features suggesting an alternative cause for the kidney disease and (ii) use of antibiotics or probiotic supplements within 3 months prior to sample collection. While the sample size was limited by the single-center design and stringent inclusion criteria, this exploratory study provides preliminary data for future validation in larger cohorts.

Out of the recruited patients, 44 were included in the final analysis (see recruitment flowchart in Figure 1). These patients were categorized into early and advanced DKD groups based on their 24-h urinary protein (24-h UPro) and eGFR (early DKD group: 24-h UPro ≤ 500 mg/24 h, eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2; advanced DKD group: 24-h UPro > 500 mg/24 h or eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2). Furthermore, we defined the DKD-susceptible group as those with a duration from DM onset to advanced DKD of less than 10 years, or those whose creatinine doubled or progressed to CKD stage 5 within 1 year of follow-up. The DKD-resistant group comprised early DKD patients with a DM duration exceeding 10 years.

Flowchart illustrating the recruitment of patients with type 2 DKD based on the exclusion and inclusion criteria.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were obtained through direct interviews with nephrologists. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg at repeated examinations on different days in the hospital, a self-reported history of hypertension, or the use of antihypertensive medications [8]. Biochemical tests of blood and urine and fecal collection were performed at the time of enrollment. The eGFR was calculated using the 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine formula [9]. CKD stages were classified from 1 to 5 based on the eGFR [10].

Collection of samples and extraction of DNA

Each participant in the study was instructed to provide a fecal sample during the morning hours utilizing fecal collection containers. These containers were subsequently transported under refrigeration and preserved at −80 °C prior to further processing. Total fecal DNA was extracted employing a QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). All DNA extraction procedures were conducted under a Class II biological safety cabinet. The concentration of genomic DNA present in each fecal sample was quantified utilizing a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The integrity and sizes of the DNA were evaluated using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE). The DNA was then re-suspended in H2O and preserved at −80 °C for future use.

Amplification and sequencing of 16S rRNA gene

The V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using universal primers (341 F and 806 R) that were linked with indices and sequencing adaptors. The resulting amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina Miseq platform, yielding 300-bp paired-end reads. The total number of clean reads across all samples was 5,277,034, with an average of 119,932.6 ± 5,995.8 clean tags per sample (sequencing depth). To account for sequencing saturation and sample integrity and to standardize comparisons, the data were normalized by randomly selecting 67,914 tags per sample for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (ver. 3.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for quantitative variables and Pearson’s Chi-square test for categorical variables. Associations between genera (sequence counts) with a prevalence ≥ 10% and clinical parameters of different groups were evaluated using generalized linear models (GLMs), employing negative binomials as dictated by the distribution of the target variable. This was achieved using the R package ‘glmmTMB.’ Correlations between DKD clinical parameters and genera (sequence counts) with a prevalence ≥ 10% for 44 DKD patients were calculated using Spearman’s rank-correlation analysis with the R package ‘cor.test.’ Collinearity of the variables was tested using the variance inflation factor (VIF) in the ‘vif’ function in the R package ‘car,’ with variables exhibiting a VIF > 5 deemed collinear. Potential confounders were selected using fivefold cross-validated least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) in the R package ‘glmnet.’ Genera significantly associated with confounders based on Spearman’s correlation (p < 0.05) were evaluated using GLMs with individual variables.

Mendelian randomization (MR) study

Data source

The details of the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) incorporated in this paper are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The MiBioGen consortium (www.mibiogen.org) offers GWAS summary data for the gut microbiota and is presently the largest published source of human-related gut microbiota GWAS data [11]. The study comprised 24 cohorts with 18,340 participants, including 14,306 European individuals from 18 cohorts. This GWAS data encompasses 211 groups of bacteria, classified into five types: class, phylum, order, family, and genus. The smallest level, the genus, was the focus of this study. A total of 131 genera were identified, and bacterial genera with clinical significance in the preceding cross-sectional study were incorporated into the MR analysis. Diabetic nephropathy was characterized as an outcome when glomerular disorders in patients with DM met the ICD-10 (code: N08.3*) criterion, with summary statistics from the FinnGen biobank comprising 184,987 European individuals (3,283 cases and 181,704 controls). We also collated the aggregated data on glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in diabetics from a prior study containing 11,522 participants of European descent. Given that participants in our cross-sectional study had type 2 DKD, we extracted GWAS of DKD in type 2 diabetes (T2D) using eight complementary dichotomous and quantitative DKD phenotypes from the previous study: The principal dichotomous analysis involved 5,717 T2D subjects, 3,345 with DKD. Promising association signals were evaluated in up to 26,827 subjects with T2D (12,710 with DKD) [12].

SNPs selection and statistical analysis

The study employed the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method for conducting the MR analysis. The selection of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) involved a multi-step process designed to satisfy the three core assumptions of MR for valid causal inference: (1) Relevance: SNPs must be strongly associated with the exposure (gut microbiota abundance). (2) Independence: SNPs must be independent of confounders. (3) Exclusion restriction: SNPs must affect the outcome (DKD) only through the exposure, not via alternative pathways (no horizontal pleiotropy). Specifically, SNPs were identified based on a significance threshold of (p < 1 × 10−5) to find more potential causal associations [11,13,14]. SNPs in linkage disequilibrium were excluded by setting a threshold of (r2 < 0.01) with a clumping distance of 500 kb [15]. To address the potential pleiotropy, the PhenoScanner database was utilized for SNP identification and subsequent exclusion. Weak instrumental variables, indicated by an F-statistic less than 10, were systematically excluded from the analysis. The MR–pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) method was employed before each MR analysis to eliminate the potential outliers. In the GWAS of diabetic nephropathy from the FinnGen biobank or GFR in diabetes, it was assumed that alleles were coded on the forward strand based on knowledge of non-palindromic SNPs, thus retaining all palindromic SNPs. However, for GWAS of DKD in T2D, all palindromic SNPs were dropped due to the inability to infer the direction.

Following this rigorous screening process, the remaining SNPs were retained for subsequent analyses. To validate the findings, alternative methods including MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple model were applied in conjunction with IVW. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, while pleiotropy was evaluated through MR-Egger intercept testing. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was further executed to assess the influence of individual SNPs on the results. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) for both MR analyses and sensitivity assessments.

Taxa alterations between groups

No significant differences were found in the mean fecal microbiota community diversity indexes (i.e., α-diversity, including chao1, observed species, PD whole tree, Shannon and Simpson based on operational taxonomic unit (OTU) levels) between the advanced and early DKD groups, and between DKD-susceptible and DKD-resistant groups (Supplementary Table 2). This suggests that the richness and diversity of the gut microbiota between the compared groups do not significantly differ. Similarly, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) plots visualizing β-diversity based on both unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances showed no significant separation between the compared groups (Supplementary Figure 1; Adonis p > 0.05 for all comparisons), indicating the overall fecal microbial structure.

A supervised comparison of the microbiota between groups was conducted using the Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis, a method commonly used to identify the presence and effect size of region-specific OTUs among different groups [16,17]. A logarithmic LDA score cutoff of 2.0 was used to identify significant taxonomic differences between the advanced DKD and early DKD groups, as well as between the DKD-resistant and susceptible groups. The results suggested a notable difference in fecal microbiota between the groups based on the LEfSe analysis. At the genus level, the relative abundances of Butyricimonas, Fusicatenibacter, and Barnesiella were higher, while the relative abundance of Allisonella was lower in the advanced DKD patients compared to the early DKD patients (LDA score (log10) > 2, Figure 2(a), (c)). In patients susceptible to DKD, the relative abundance of Fusobacterium was higher, while the relative abundances of Allisonella and Eubacterium were lower compared to patients resistant to DKD (LDA score (log10) > 2, Figure 2(b), (d)).

GLMs were employed to discern the genera exhibiting significant differences between the two compared groups, with age and gender serving as control variables. The fecal samples from the advanced and early groups demonstrated associations with the genera Olsenella, Barnesiella, Butyricimonas, Granulicatella, Desulfovibrio, Eggerthella, Slackia, Saccharibacteria genera incertae sedis, Eubacterium, Flavonifractor, Acidaminococcus, Phascolarctobacterium, and Pseudomonas (p < 0.05, Table 2). This suggests a potential link between these genera and advanced DKD. In addition, the fecal samples from the DKD-susceptible and DKD-resistant groups showed associations with the genera Barnesiella, Gemella, Streptococcus, Holdemanella, Fusobacterium, Sphingomonas, Hemophilus, Eggerthella, Eubacterium, and Akkermansia (p < 0.05, Table 2), indicating a possible correlation between these genera and susceptibility to DKD. These findings provide valuable insights into the microbial landscape associated with DKD progression and susceptibility.

Correlations between fecal microbiota and DKD clinical characteristics

Our study established associations between fecal microbiota (at the genus level, with a prevalence of ≥ 10%) and various clinical parameters of DKD, including age (both chronological and age of DM onset), disease duration (span of DM, DKD, and the period from DM to DKD), glycemic control (FBG and HbA1c), and DKD severity (eGFR, CKD stage, and 24-h UPro). The majority of the identified genera in fecal microbiota exhibited a negative correlation with age (for instance, Anaerofustis with age and age of DM onset, p < 0.01) (Figure 3). Conversely, most identified genera in fecal microbiota showed a positive correlation with a longer duration from DM to renal injury (e.g., Eubacterium with duration from DM to DKD, p < 0.01), poorer glycemic control (Anaerostipes with HbA1c, p < 0.01; Senegalimassilia with FBG, p < 0.05), increased proteinuria (Butyricicoccus with 24-h UPro, p < 0.05), and improved renal function (Eubacterium with eGFR, p < 0.05) (Figure 3).

Among the 32 significantly associated taxa identified through Spearman analysis in DKD patients with clinical characteristics (p < 0.05) (Figure 3), the genera Barnesiella, Allisonella, and Fusobacterium were significantly different between the compared groups at an alpha cutoff of 0.05 and an effect size cutoff of 2.0 through LEfSe analysis (Figure 2(a), (b)). Through negative binomial GLMs analysis, the genera Slackia, Acidaminococcus, Eggerthella, and Eubacterium were associated with early DKD, with the latter two also associated with resistance to DKD (Table 2). The genera Streptococcus, Fusobacterium, Hemophilus, and Barnesiella were associated with susceptibility to DKD, with the last one also associated with advanced DKD (Table 2).

Following adjustment using GLMs, the genera Bradyrhizobium and Bilophila remained associated with age. Similarly, the genera Rothia and Allisonella continued to show a correlation with FBG. The genera Bacteroides and Butyricicoccus preserved their association with 24-h UPro levels. Lastly, the genus Parabacteroides maintained its relationship with eGFR (Table 3).

IVW analyses revealed an association between 14 bacterial genera and DKD phenotypes (Table 4). The IVW estimate indicates that the genera Barnesiella (Figure 4(a)), Butyricimonas (Figure 4(b)), Desulfovibrio (Figure 4(c)), Hemophilus (Figure 4(d)), Bacteroides (Figure 4(e)), Streptococcus (Figure 4(f)), Flavonifractor, and Phascolarctobacterium exert a detrimental effect on DKD (Table 4). Notably, the first seven genera were positively correlated with the onset of DKD or more severe DKD phenotypes (Table 4, p < 0.05), while the last one was negatively correlated with eGFR in T2D (Table 4, Beta ± SE: −6.813 ± 2.626, p = 0.009). Conversely, the genera Slackia (Figure 4(h)), Allisonella (Figure 4(i)), Anaerostipes, and Bilophila exhibited a protective effect on DKD (Table 4). The first three were positively correlated with GFR in diabetics (Table 4, p < 0.05), and the last genus was negatively correlated with Microalbuminuria in T2D (Table 4, Beta ± SE: −0.653 ± 0.311, p = 0.036). However, Ruminococcus1 and Fusicatenibacter displayed contradictory effects on DKD (Table 4). Scatter plots (Supplementary Figure 2) and forest plots of leave-one-out sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Figure 3) visualize these effect estimates across MR methods and SNPs, demonstrating consistent causal directions. Detailed results for all 14 associated genera, including effect sizes and statistical significance, are provided in Table 4. Cochran’s IVW Q test and MR-Egger regression intercept analysis showed no significant heterogeneity of these IVs or significant directional horizontal pleiotropy (Table 4). The analysis of MR-PRESSO did not identify any outlier of SNPs. These sensitivity analyses confirm the robustness of the observed causal relationships.

Discussion

Our study confirmed the link between gut microbiota dysbiosis and DKD progression. We used prediction models and MR to identify and confirm the causal relationship of several bacterial genera with DKD. We identified 44 bacterial genera associated with DKD clinical parameters, with 29 linked to kidney injury characteristics such as early or advanced stage, susceptibility, CKD stage, 24-h UPro, and eGFR. This suggests that gut microbiota changes dynamically from DKD onset to progression, and these 29 genera may contribute to DKD pathogenesis and exacerbation. We identified 10 of these genera causally linked to DKD, most of which have been overlooked in prior studies (Figure 4).

Our phenotypic definitions for advanced DKD (24-h UPro >500 mg/24 h or eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2) were grounded in established KDIGO guidelines. The eGFR threshold of <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 corresponds to CKD stage G3b, which KDIGO distinguishes due to its elevated risk of progression and comorbidities, while the 24-h UPro threshold of >500 mg/24 h aligns with KDIGO’s albuminuria category A3 (ACR ≥300 mg/g), indicative of severe albuminuria and high-risk DKD progression [18–20]. These criteria are widely adopted in prognostic studies to stratify DKD severity [21,22]. For the definition of DKD susceptibility (duration from the DM onset to advanced DKD <10 years), we referenced epidemiological studies linking shorter diabetes duration to accelerated renal decline [23,24]. While consensus on this specific threshold remains limited, its use facilitates comparability with cohorts demonstrating rapid progression phenotypes. We acknowledge the exploratory nature of this definition and have addressed its interpretative limitations in subgroup analyses.

Our study reveals a positive correlation between the genera Bacteroides, Barnesiella, and Butyricimonas of the Bacteroidota phylum and severe DKD phenotypes, and the results of MR analysis show that they are the cause of aggravation of DKD. Bacteroides species can degrade host glucagon-like peptide-1 activity through microbial dipeptidyl peptidase 4 [25] and produce indole, which is converted to indoxyl sulfate [26]. Barnesiella species are involved in carbohydrate fermentation and immunoregulation [27,28], and are more abundant in the DN group [29]. Butyricimonas, known for producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [30], is enriched in DKD patients’ intestines [31,32]. Desulfovibrio and Hemophilus, which once belonged to the phylum Proteobacteria, have been reclassified into the phylum Thermodesulfobacteria and Pseudomonas [33], respectively, and play a role in gut microbial dysbiosis, affecting the host’s metabolic and immune disorders, inflammation, and nutritional status [34]. Our study shows that their levels are increased in advanced and susceptible individuals and play a role in DKD exacerbation. Desulfovibrio contributes to intestinal barrier loss and promotes inflammation, while Hemophilus is known for its pathogenicity. Streptococcus and Ruminococcus2, both under the phylum Bacillota, have contrasting characteristics. Streptococcus promotes DKD progression, but not all species are pathogenic [35]. Many are part of the human microbiota. Ruminococcus2, identified in this study as being induced by proteinuria via MR, degrades resistant starch in the colon [36] and is associated with various diseases [37] as the marker of inflammtion. Slackia, Allisonella, and Akkermansia, linked with milder DKD, have a causal relationship with DKD. Slackia produces polyphenols [38] and bioactive metabolites, beneficial for renal disease therapy. Allisonella produces histamine from histidine [39], which has renoprotective effects in mouse model of hypertension [40] and severe cardiorenal damages [41], rats model of anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody-induced glomerulonephritis [42], and postoperative acute kidney injury in acute type A aortic dissection patients [43]. Akkermansia, a probiotic, improves gut barrier integrity [44] and produces butyrate [45]. In summary, as DKD worsens, beneficial microbiota decrease and harmful microbiota increase.

Crucially, our findings suggest concrete clinical utility for these microbial signatures. We highlighted that certain microbiota genera, such as Barnesiella, Butyricimonas, and Desulfovibrio, could serve as early biomarkers for DKD progression due to their causal associations with disease severity. Conversely, protective genera like Slackia, Allisonella, and Akkermansia may represent promising therapeutic targets. For instance, probiotics targeting these beneficial taxa or interventions modulating their metabolites (e.g., SCFAs) could potentially slow DKD progression. These insights align with emerging strategies for microbiota-based diagnostics and therapies, warranting further clinical validation.

Despite global studies, a consistent set of key bacteria for DKD is still unclear [46–53]. A meta-analysis encompassing 16 studies (578 DKD patients and 444 healthy controls) revealed a significant decrease in bacterial richness in DKD patients compared to healthy controls, but no significant difference in diversity indexes [48]. Notably, prior observational studies predominantly contrasted DKD patients with healthy controls, identifying depletion of Faecalibacterium and Roseburia and enrichment of pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae and Desulfovibrio [48,54]. However, such comparisons may conflate diabetes-related microbial shifts with DKD-specific alterations.

Unlike these studies, we used early-stage or resistant DKD patients as controls instead of healthy individuals. Our results showed no significant difference in the α-diversity index (richness and diversity) and β-diversity index (structure) of the fecal microbiota in DKD patients between groups. This contrasts with reports of reduced microbial richness in DKD vs. healthy cohorts [48,54], suggesting that taxon-specific dynamics—not overall diversity—drive DKD progression. For instance, while Bacteroides and Streptococcus were enriched in advanced DKD in our cohort, their causal roles were further validated via MR, addressing limitations of correlation-based observational studies. Conversely, protective genera like Allisonella and Slackia, overlooked in prior meta-analyses [48], emerged as novel biomarkers for preserved renal function (p < 0.05).

This study is the first MR analysis to investigate the genetic causality between gut microbiota and DKD. Our MR study, using the largest GWAS of gut microbiota, provides robust evidence supporting the role of specific gut microbiota alterations in DKD pathogenesis. While randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for establishing causality, their application is challenging due to the vast diversity of gut microbial species and the extended latency period from gut microbiota imbalance to DKD development [55].

Our study has several limitations. As a cross-sectional single-center study with a small cohort, the generalizability of our findings requires validation in larger, multi-center populations. Secondly, while most patients were on hypoglycemic or antihypertensive medications, prior RCTs in T2D populations (e.g., dapagliflozin/gliclazide [56]) suggest minimal short-term effects of these drugs on fecal microbiome composition. Thirdly, while 16S rRNA sequencing offers taxonomic insights, its resolution limits species-level and functional interpretations. Future integration of shotgun metagenomics and metabolomics could clarify microbial pathways and identify causal metabolites in DKD progression. Multi-omics approaches, as demonstrated, will enhance biomarker discovery and therapeutic targeting. Lastly, ethnic differences in gut microbial composition (e.g., Bacteroides dominance in Western cohorts vs. Prevotella enrichment in East Asian populations [57]) and genetic adaptations (e.g., diet-related polymorphisms) may introduce bias in trans-ethnic MR. Population-specific GWAS and multi-ethnic validation are critical to improve generalizability.

Conclusions

Our study reveals a causal relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and DKD progression and susceptibility. We identified several bacterial genera that influence DKD, suggesting potential therapeutic targets to address this condition. Additionally, we identified several bacterial genera affected by DKD, which may serve as biomarkers for DKD progression and susceptibility. Evidence of gut microbiota dysbiosis in DKD appears quite consistent across studies, although the taxa shown to be associated with DKD differ among studies worldwide. These differences may enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of DKD and could support potentially novel therapeutic options aimed at modifying the gut microbiota in DKD patients. These findings provide a foundation for microbiota-targeted interventions to slow DKD progression.
