Palmaris longus tendon and its relationship with the median nerve: a population-based morphometric magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Esin Kurtulus Ozturk, Saffet Ozturk, Sitki Safa Taflan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe palmaris longus (PL) tendon is frequently used as an autograft in reconstructive surgeries, and anatomic and morphometric differences in race, gender, and side of the tendon may have an impact on the suitability of the tendon graft. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence, variations and morphology of the PL tendon and to determine its relationship with the median nerve (MN) and the antebrachial fascia on wrist magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).MethodsThis retrospective study included 1,030 wrist MRI examinations to evaluate the presence, anatomical variations, and morphometric features of the PL tendon. The width, thickness and cross-sectional area (CSA) of the PL tendon and its relationship with the MN and antebrachial fascia were assessed on axial T1-weighted images, and the data were correlated with gender and side.ResultsThe results of the study indicated that the PL tendon was absent in 197 patients (19.1%), and the most common type was the classic anatomic variation (79.5%). Rare types were observed in 18 patients, two of whom exhibited reverse PL. There was no statistically significant difference between gender and side in terms of tendon presence (P=0.09). The median width was measured at 3.35 mm, the median thickness at 1.43 mm, and the median CSA at 3.53 mm2. PL tendon width, thickness and CSA were significantly higher in men (P<0.001) [width 3.7 mm (range, 1.2–5.9 mm), thickness 1.47 mm (range, 0.7–2.05 mm) and CSA 3.8 mm2 (range, 1.47–5.92 mm2)]. MN distance 2.6 mm (range, 1.1–4.1 mm) and antebrachial fascia thickness 1.12 mm (range, 0.8–1.5 mm) were significantly higher in men (P<0.001). Additionally, the right-sided hand exhibited statistically significantly greater PL tendon thickness and CSA (P=0.007, P<0.001, respectively) but lower antebrachial fascia thickness (P=0.010).ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first and largest MRI study reporting PL tendon morphometrics and its prevalence in the Turkish population. It is evident from the findings of this study that a physical examination alone is inadequate for evaluating the PL tendon. However, MRI has been demonstrated to be a reliable and precise modality for assessing the PL tendon, particularly in cases where tendon reconstruction surgery is indicated.

Introduction

The palmaris longus (PL) muscle, a superficial flexor muscle of the forearm, typically originates from the level of the medial epicondyle of the elbow and inserts at the wrist via the palmar aponeurosis and flexor retinaculum. The PL tendon is an extrasynovial tendon that passes over the median nerve (MN), outside the carpal tunnel (1-4). The PL tendon is generally preferred as an autologous tendon graft because it has a minor role in wrist flexion, is superficially located, has a long tendon with a short muscle body, and can be stretched both laterally and longitudinally without tearing (5-7).

The PL muscle is one of the most variable muscles in the human body. Reimann et al. were the first to report the palmaris longus absence (PLA) with a rate of 15% in 1944 (8). Subsequent studies found its absence in percentages ranging from 1.5% to 63.9% depending on ethnicity (5). The PL muscle typically has a short belly and a long tendon. Classifications were made based on variations in the number and location of the muscle belly and the number and termination type of the tendon. The muscle components can be reversed, bifid, trifid, or centrally located. The tendon may have classic, duplicate, short, very thin, or branched distal variations (1,3).

In most individuals with a PL tendon, the tendon has been shown to penetrate the antebrachial fascia and extend subcutaneously at the level of the wrist, facilitating tendon assessment via physical examination (2). The presence, absence or variation of the tendon can be assessed using Schaeffer, Mishra I and II, Thompson and Pushpakumar tests (5,9-11). However, physical examination can be challenging, especially in individuals with a high body mass index. On the other hand, radiological imaging methods are more reliable than the physical examination in assessing the presence of the PL tendon (11).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide a more objective, noninvasive anatomic examination of the PL tendon, which is widely used in reconstructions and tendon replacements. Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate the morphological and morphometric features of the PL tendon and determine its relationship with the MN in patients who underwent wrist MRI. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-971/rc).

Methods

A retrospective evaluation of all wrist MRI images of adult patients was conducted between December 2022 and December 2023 using the hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ankara Etlik City Hospital (approval No. AESH-EK1-2023-822). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Adult patients of Turkish ethnicity over the age of 18 years who had been referred to the institution due to variable clinical symptoms were eligible for inclusion in the study.

All patients were subjected to a standard wrist protocol evaluation, utilizing 1.5 T or 3.0 T magnetic resonance devices (Philips Ingenia Elition X 3.0 T, Philips Ingenia Evolution X 1.5 T Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands; GE, Signa Explorer X 1.5 T, General Electric, Milwaukee, USA). Patients were excluded from the study in the following instances: firstly, poor imaging quality; secondly, artefacts that impeded the analysis of imaging data; and thirdly, anatomic changes (congenital anomalies, tumor, trauma, infection, or history of surgery). All MRI scans that constituted part of the study were subjected to evaluation by two radiologists with experience in the field of musculoskeletal radiology (with 6 and 8 years of experience, respectively). Morphological evaluation and calculations of the distal PL tendon were performed on axial T1 sequences [repetition time/echo time (TR/TE), 510–610/10–20], and the slice thickness was 0.4 mm, field of view 100–120 mm. A comprehensive data set was collated, encompassing demographic details such as age, gender, and laterality, along with the outcomes of observations conducted on the palmaris tendon and its correlation with the MN and the antebrachial fascia.

Initially, the presence or absence of the structure was determined, followed by anatomic variations and measurements. The tendon may have classic, duplicate, short, very thin, or branched distal variations (Figures 1,2) (3). The cross-sectional image was magnified 4 times, and the width, thickness, cross-sectional area (CSA), distance to the MN, and thickness of the antebrachial fascia were measured. The CSA measurement was traced using the freehand region-of-interest tool. The position of the MN relative to the PL tendon at the level of the carpal tunnel inlet was analyzed using perpendicular projections, and classified as radial, mid and mid-radial. The distance from the medial border of the PL tendon to the medial end of the MN (PL-MN) was also measured. If the projections of the MN and PL tendons overlapped at any point, the PL-MN was not measured and was classified as mid or mid-radial. Conversely, if the medial margin of the MN did not overlie the medial border of the PL tendon, the PL-MN was measured and classified as radial (Figure 3).

Finally, gender and laterality differences were then compared in terms of tendon presence, tendon morphometric measurements, and distance to MN, as well as antebrachial fascia thickness.

Statistical analysis

The data analyses in this study were performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or the median and interquartile range (25th–75th percentiles). The normality of continuous variables in groups was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Chi-squared test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare the variables related PL tendon. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Interobserver agreement for the assessment of relevant factors was determined using weighted kappa statistics (0.41–0.60: moderate; 0.61–0.80: substantial; more than >0.80, almost perfect).

Results

A total of 1,030 patients were included in our study: 648 (62.9%) were female and 382 (37.1%) were male. Of the wrist MRIs, 641 (62.2%) were obtained from the right hand and 389 (37.8%) were taken from the left hand. The PL tendon was absent in 197 (19.1%) patients. The most common type is the classical typical, which usually passes over the retinaculum as a single tendon (79.5% of cases). Rare variations such as reverse muscle belly, duplicate tendon, and triplicate tendon were observed in 18 patients (1.8%). The most frequent position of the MN relative to the PL tendon was mid-radial (51.6%) (see Table 1 and Figure 4). The presence of the palmaris tendon did not differ statistically in terms of age (P=0.208), gender (P=0.418) or laterality (P=0.922) (see Table 2).

The median PL tendon thickness was measured at 1.43 mm (range, 0.6–2.18 mm), width at 3.35 mm (range, 1.2–5.9 mm), and CSA at 3.53 mm2 (range, 1.42–5.92 mm2). Males demonstrated a statistically significantly larger tendon width, thickness and CSA dimensions, as well as a statistically significantly greater MN distance and antebrachial fascia thickness. The distance between the PL tendon and the MN was measured as 2.4 mm (range, 1.1–4.1 mm) and was found to be statistically significantly shorter in women (P<0.001). Additionally, the right hand exhibited statistically significantly higher PL tendon thickness and tendon CSA (P=0.007, P<0.001, respectively) but lower antebrachial fascia thickness (P=0.010) (Table 3). The kappa for interobserver agreement was substantial for the following: PL tendon width [0.82; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.72], tendon thickness (0.78; 95% CI: 0.70), tendon CSA (0.75; 95% CI: 0.62), distance to the MN (0.78; 95% CI: 0.62), and antebrachial fascia thickness (0.70; 95% CI: 0.54).

Discussion

The PL, as one of the most variable muscles in the human body, is frequently used in reconstructive surgeries such as tendon replacements, facial paralysis treatment, and ptosis correction (9). Therefore, it is important to determine its existence and know its variations. The most common variation in the PL tendon is its absence, with a traditionally accepted rate of 15% (8). However, studies have reported rates ranging from 1.5% to 63.9% among different populations. PLA is less common in the black population (5). In a study by Gangata, conducted in Zimbabwe, the PLA rate was 1.5% (12). In a clinical study conducted by Ceyhan and Mavt in Turkey, the PLA rate was 63.9% in the 12–18 years age group (13). Kose et al. reported a prevalence of 26.6% in their study on the clinical examination of the PLA in the Turkish population (10). A meta-analysis evaluating PLA in cadaveric studies and physical examination studies found no statistically significant difference, but the PLA rate was lower in cadaveric studies (15.6% in physical examination and 10.3% in cadaveric studies) (11). Furthermore, a study by Holzgrefe et al., comparing the use of physical examination and ultrasound (US) for detecting PLA, found that US was the more valuable method. They reported PLA rates of 10% with US and 14% with physical examination (14). In the first and only MRI study in the literature to assess PL using MRI, Kikano et al. found the prevalence of PLA to be 34.8% in 335 Lebanese individuals (15). In our MRI study, we found the prevalence of PLA to be 19.1% in a sample of 1,030 Turkish patients. The PL tendon with smaller thickness detected in our study was more likely to be classified as PLA, based on physical examination, leading to a higher detection rate of the PL tendon.

PLA is more commonly observed in women and on the left side (5). However, in a meta-analysis by Yammine, although PLA was found more frequently in women, no statistically significant difference was detected (16). Another meta-analysis by Ioannis et al., which reviewed ten studies on PLA, identified a significant difference with a higher prevalence in women, but only two of the studies revealed that it was more common in men. However, 11 other studies found no statistically significant difference between genders (5). Kose et al. found that PLA was statistically significantly more prevalent in women in the Turkish population (10). Similarly, Fidan et al. found PLA more frequently in women in their US study (17). In our study, PLA was seen more frequently in women, in line with many previous studies, but no statistically significant difference was found between genders.

Excluding PLA, variations are reported to occur at a rate of 5–9%. Anatomical variations other than reversed PL are generally of no clinical significance. However, studies have shown that reversed PL can cause compression syndrome by exerting pressure on the median or ulnar nerves (3,18). In an MRI study of PL conducted by Kikano et al., only two variations (0.6%) were reported, both of which were reversed PL (15). We observed that the variation rate outside the PLA was 1.8%, two (0.2%) of which were reversed PL.

The PL tendon passes over the MN at the level of the carpal tunnel but does not enter the carpal tunnel and is closely associated with the transverse carpal ligament. Therefore, previous studies have suggested a potential association with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) (4,19-21). In a study by Boltuch et al., the prevalence of PL in patients with CTS was found to be 6.4% (4). Keese et al. reported a prevalence of 2.8% in patients with CTS and 22.2% in the control group (20). Given its association with CTS and its importance in endoscopic CTS interventions, it is necessary to determine the presence of the PL tendon and its relationship with the MN. In our study, the PL tendon was most frequently positioned mid-radially relative to the MN (51.6% of cases).

Knowing the location of the MN related to the PL tendon is so crucial for surgical planning. During the surgical approach for CTS, the wrist is in supination and surgeons usually choose the ulnar side of the PL tendon in order to avoid the trunk of the MN. The rationale for choosing the ulnar side of the PL tendon is based on the theory that the MN lies on the radial side of the PL tendon (22,23). In our study, all cases were in the hand pronation position with all MNs located in a middle or radial location. We therefore suggest that the position of the MN may change depending on the position of the hand movement. A safer surgical area can be achieved with the wrist in pronation than with the wrist in supination.

A meta-analysis conducted by Yammine and Erić evaluated 20 studies and found that the PL width was 4.0±1.7 mm. Of these studies, seven evaluated the relationship between PL width and gender and found no significant correlation (6). In a cadaver study conducted by Alagöz et al. in Turkey, the PL tendon width was found to be 7.7±2.0 mm, with a mean tendon thickness of 3.6 mm (24). In their study, Fidan et al. examined patients diagnosed with PLA through physical examination using US. They detected the PL tendon in 20 patients, with an average thickness of 0.88 mm, and found no significant differences based on side (17). Kikano et al. evaluated 339 MRI examinations and found that the mean PL width was 4.24±1.2 mm, while the mean PL thickness was 2.75±0.6 mm. However, they did not assess the relationship between PL thickness and width in relation to gender or side (15). In our MRI study of 1,030 cases, we found that the PL tendon width was 3.35 mm (range, 1.2–5.9 mm) and the tendon thickness was 1.43 mm (range, 0.6–2.18 mm). Additionally, the PL tendon was found to be statistically wider and thicker in men and on the right side. Furthermore, significant statistical differences were found in width, thickness, and CSA between the sexes and sides. Interestingly, we found that the vertical distance between the PL and MN was statistically closer in women than in men, by 2.3 mm (range, 1.1–3.9 mm, P<0.001), which may be a predisposing factor for the higher prevalence of CTS in women.

In a study of CTS and PL-CSA by Joo et al., it was found that the CSA of a control group of 21 patients averaged 2.34±0.82 mm2, demonstrating a statistically significant increase in PL-CSA in patients with CTS. No significant relationship was found between CSA and sex (25). In our study, the median CSA was found to be 3.53 mm2 (range, 1.42–5.92 mm2). We also demonstrated that PL-CSA was statistically significantly higher in men and on the right side. Therefore, there may be an association between PL-CSA and CTS; however, we believe that further studies with larger populations are needed to better understand the influence of gender and side.

The PL tendon extends into the subcutaneous tissue by penetrating the antebrachial fascia at the wrist level (2). No studies have evaluated the thickness of the antebrachial fascia in relation to the PL tendon. In our study, we found that the antebrachial fascia was statistically significantly thicker in men and on the right side. The thickness, width, and CSA of the PL tendon, as well as antebrachial thickness, may be affected by the physical characteristics of men and the general prevalence of right-hand dominance in humans.

Our study had several limitations. It is retrospective, and single-centered, and it only includes a certain ethnicity. Therefore, the results cannot be fully generalized. Nevertheless, our study is important because there are few MRI studies examining PL variations and accompanying morphological factors. The physical examination findings of the patients were unavailable for comparison with MRI findings. The study did not include features related to the muscle belly and the proximal segment of the muscle. It has also not been compared with other imaging modalities.

Conclusions

Physical examination may not be sufficient in the evaluation of the PL tendon, and the use of MRI can provide a more accurate and repeatable evaluation of the PL tendon, particularly when tendon reconstruction surgery is required. It is important to establish the presence of the PL tendon and its relationship with the MN, as the PL tendon is an important structure in endoscopic CTS interventions. Furthermore, it is important to elucidate the relationships between PL tendon thickness, width, and CSA according to gender and side.
