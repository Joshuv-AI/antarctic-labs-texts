Direct-printed aligners in orthodontics: Integrating digital design, materials science, and clinical biomechanics
Authors: Yoo Jin Kim Gabriel Miranda Ki Beom Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition from thermoformed aligners (TFAs) to direct-printed aligners (DPAs) represents an important development in contemporary clear aligner therapy. The inherent limitations of TFAs, including material thinning, geometric distortion, inconsistent force delivery, and limited predictability for complex tooth movements, have driven the development of DPAs, which use shape memory polymers (SMPs) and eliminate the thermoforming process. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on the transition from TFAs to DPAs, focusing on advances in materials science, digital design, biomechanical performance, and clinical application. In vitro studies have shown that TFAs undergo substantial thickness reduction and degradation of mechanical properties after thermoforming, resulting in excessive initial forces and pronounced force decay that may compromise physiological tooth movement. In contrast, DPAs are fabricated using SMPs, which exhibit favorable viscoelastic behavior and can deliver light, continuous forces within physiological ranges with minimal force decay over clinically relevant wear periods. Additive manufacturing also enables advanced biomechanical design features, including localized thickness variation, reinforcement zones, pressure and relief areas, extended trimlines, and direct integration of auxiliaries into the aligner shell. Emerging clinical evidence and illustrative case applications suggest that these capabilities may improve torque expression, rotational control, extrusion efficiency, posterior expansion, and anchorage management while reducing reliance on bonded attachments and the number of aligners required. Although current findings suggest that DPAs may offer improved biomechanical control, predictability, and sustainability compared with TFAs, further research is needed to refine design protocols and evaluate their long-term cytotoxicity, intraoral aging behavior, and environmental impact.

INTRODUCTION

Clear aligner therapy (CAT) originated in the 1940s with Kesling’s sequential tooth-positioning appliance.1 Although innovative, this appliance lacked esthetics and comfort, and fabrication was labor-intensive. In the 1960s, Nahoum advanced this concept by introducing clear thermoplastics and vacuum-forming technology. Subsequent refinements by Ponitz,2 McNamara et al.,3 and Sheridan et al.4 helped define CAT's foundational principles; however, clinical applications remained limited to retention or minor tooth movements because fabrication was complex and manual.

CAT underwent a major transformation in the late 1990s with the adoption of computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing. Over the past two decades, advances in biomaterials and digital technologies have enabled more efficient, predictable tooth movement.5-9 Although CAT still requires improvements to optimize force systems and treatment predictability, recent advances in materials and biomechanical strategies, including staging protocols, attachment incorporation, and overcorrection, have improved clinical outcomes.10-13 Together with patient preference for esthetics and comfort, these advances have established CAT as an essential modality in modern orthodontics.

For several decades, aligners were manufactured primarily through thermoforming; however, this process introduces substantial variability and degradation in material and dimensional properties. The heating and vacuum adaptation used to fabricate thermoformed aligners (TFAs) significantly compromise the mechanical properties of polymers such as polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG), one of the most commonly used materials. One study reported an 80% reduction in the flexural modulus of PETG attributable solely to thermoforming.14 Furthermore, thermoforming can introduce geometric inaccuracies in areas crucial for controlled force delivery. Studies have reported substantial thickness reduction of 45.3% in TFAs.15,16 Because aligner thickness is directly related to stiffness and force output,17 these discrepancies can produce inconsistent force levels and excessive initial forces,9 thereby impairing the ability of TFAs to deliver the predictable and controlled orthodontic forces needed for physiological tooth movement.

The inherent geometric limitations of TFAs may partly explain the reported challenges in CAT predictability. Early studies on TFAs reported an overall tooth-movement accuracy of only 41%, with later improvements reaching only 50% over the following decade.10,18 These findings underscore the challenge of achieving predictability when the manufacturing process itself can compromise dimensional accuracy and material integrity.

These inherent limitations of TFAs have prompted the development of new technological solutions. In 2019, direct aligner printing marked a significant departure from traditional CAT manufacturing methods. Graphy Inc. (Seoul, Korea) introduced direct three-dimensional (3D) printing of aligners.15 This additive manufacturing approach, used to fabricate direct-printed aligners (DPAs), eliminates multiple intermediate steps required for TFAs, such as physical model printing. The DPA workflow may provide greater dimensional accuracy and improved material properties through the use of novel materials, particularly shape memory polymers (SMPs), which can deliver light, continuous forces within physiological limits and thereby address a major clinical limitation of TFAs.9,15,19 This review discusses the digital workflow, material properties, biomechanical principles, clinical applications, and remaining limitations of DPAs in contemporary orthodontic practice.

THE DIGITAL WORKFLOW FOR DIRECT PRINTING

Initial stages of DPA and TFA workflows are similar: patient scanning, manipulating digital models in CAD, and designing tooth movements to create staged model stereolithography (STL) files. For DPAs, the next step involves generating the aligner shell for direct printing (Figure 1A), which allows extensive customization, including thickness variations for specific force control (Figure 1B), as demonstrated by Grant et al.,19 and integration of auxiliary features. The ability to digitally plan and modify aligner geometry with high fidelity allows force vectors to be optimized and customized according to the biomechanical needs of each malocclusion.20 Although DPA fabrication allows open-system printing, successful implementation requires strict protocol adherence because errors in build-angle orientation can affect dimensional accuracy and surface quality.

After 3D printing, DPAs undergo critical post-processing steps that are essential for biomechanical integrity and patient safety (Figure 2). Although isopropyl alcohol (IPA) is commonly used to clean printed models in TFA workflows, it should not be applied directly to DPAs because it may compromise their mechanical properties and surface microstructure. DPA workflows use a mechanical spinner to remove uncured resin from the aligner surface (Figure 2B).21 This device applies centrifugal force to separate uncured resin from the aligner surface, eliminating the need for chemical solvents and reducing both health and environmental risks. The standard protocol involves placing the aligner with the occlusal surface facing outward and running the spinner for a single 6-minute cycle.

Curing is one of the most critical steps because the printed object is intended for direct intraoral use. Achieving high monomer conversion is essential to minimize residual, potentially cytotoxic monomers and ensure biomechanical stability. Oxygen inhibits polymerization and forms an inhibition layer. Curing in an oxygen-free environment, specifically a nitrogen atmosphere, enhances cross-linking density and significantly reduces residual monomer content. Failure to adhere to this protocol may result in visible cloudiness, insufficient cell viability, and mild cytotoxicity due to residual free monomers.22,23 With proper curing under nitrogen, the aligner transitions from a yellowish hue to a transparent, glass-like appearance (Figure 2D–F). After curing, aligners undergo ultrasonic cleaning with distilled water heated to 80°C for 1 minute to remove surface particulates (Figure 2G). IPA and other chemical solvents are contraindicated because they can compromise the mechanical integrity of the aligner.21

A final quality-verification step involves submerging the aligner in boiling water (100°C) for 1 minute (Figure 2H). A properly cured DPA maintains its optical clarity. If the aligner exhibits cloudiness, this indicates insufficient polymer cross-linking, and the aligner must be excluded from clinical use (Figure 2I).

The DPA manufacturing workflow has several proposed advantages over the TFA workflow (Table 1).6,9,14-16,24,25 In a study by Koenig et al.,6 DPAs exhibited superior precision and geometric control, whereas TFAs showed deviations of up to 2.5 mm. Although an in vitro micro-computed tomography analysis by Park et al.26 showed that DPAs may have increased cervical gaps at the aligner–tooth interface, often attributed to accumulated photopolymerization shrinkage during printing, other studies have reported superior overall dimensional fidelity for DPAs.27,28 Koenig et al.6 demonstrated that DPAs had higher overall trueness (root mean square [RMS]: 0.140 mm) and precision than thermoformed materials (RMS: 0.188–0.209 mm). Specifically, at the molar gingival margin, DPAs showed significantly lower discrepancies than TFAs, likely because the streamlined digital workflow eliminates cumulative errors associated with thermoforming on physical models. Furthermore, a more recent study by Lim et al.29 found that although DPAs had larger initial gaps in the passive state, they maintained dimensional stability under labial activations of 0.3 and 0.5 mm. Conversely, TFAs exhibited progressively larger gaps and significant fit degradation as activation increased. These findings suggest that DPAs can accommodate activations while maintaining a stable and predictable fit, whereas TFAs may show deformation-related fit degradation under stress.

The manufacturing pathways alter aligner wall dimensions between TFAs and DPAs in different magnitudes and directions. The thermoforming process of TFAs yields significant material shrinkage of 45.3%, whereas the additive manufacturing workflow for DPAs results in a post-printing material overbuild of approximately 12% relative to the nominal thickness.15,16 Additionally, the DPA workflow may improve sustainability by eliminating reliance on physical models for fabrication. This streamlined, automated process may also improve reproducibility and efficiency.30-32

MATERIAL AND FUNCTIONAL PROPERTIES

Currently, only a limited number of photopolymerizable resins have been introduced for DPA fabrication, and their material composition and mechanical behavior vary among manufacturers. Among the most widely investigated polyurethane-based systems are TC-85 and TA-28 (Graphy), both of which are specifically formulated for intraoral orthodontic applications and have regulatory approvals (CE Class IIa and FDA 510(k) Class II). TC-85 has been extensively studied in active aligner therapy and is characterized by shape-memory behavior, favorable elastic recovery, and the ability to deliver sustained orthodontic forces over time.9,15,19 In contrast, TA-28 exhibits a comparatively higher elastic modulus and greater stiffness, with a higher Young’s modulus at body temperature than TC-85 (311.10 MPa vs. 289.58 MPa). TA-28 also showed a higher ultimate tensile strength range than TC-85 (21.52–35.06 MPa vs. 14.38–24.29 MPa).24 These properties may be advantageous in clinical situations requiring greater force expression, enhanced torque control, or improved anchorage management. Material selection should therefore be guided by the planned biomechanics, magnitude of activation, desired force system, and anticipated force decay profile. In addition, the degree of conversion, post-curing protocol, dimensional accuracy, resistance to intraoral aging, and biocompatibility remain essential considerations when selecting an appropriate resin for specific clinical indications. Accordingly, material selection in DPA therapy should be biomechanically driven and tailored to the intended treatment objectives. Nevertheless, the limited number of comparative studies evaluating these materials highlights the need for further research to define optimal case selection and clinical indications.

The biocompatibility of DPA materials is critical because of prolonged oral exposure. TFAs have been associated with detectable bisphenol A (BPA) leaching, which has potential estrogenic activity.33,34 In vitro studies report no detectable BPA leaching from shape-memory photopolymers used for DPAs.22,35 Although previous studies investigating the cytotoxicity and estrogenicity of nitrogen-cured SMP aligners in gingival fibroblasts found no toxic compounds, supporting the safety of these materials when manufacturing protocols are strictly followed, a recent study by Andrew Choi et al.36 demonstrated that the surface topography, hardness, and cytocompatibility of 3D-printed aligners undergo measurable changes after intraoral use. This suggests that the material–host interaction is not static; rather, the oral environment and mechanical loading can alter the material’s surface characteristics, and the biological response may vary depending on cellular state. Further longitudinal studies are needed to investigate DPA cytocompatibility while accounting for dynamic degradation during active intraoral use and its effects on fracture resistance, surface wear, and potential microplastic generation.36

Potential adverse clinical reactions must also be considered. Incomplete polymerization or inadequate post-curing may result in residual monomer release, which has been associated with mucosal irritation, transient burning sensations, contact stomatitis, or hypersensitivity reactions in susceptible individuals.37 Although nitrogen-curing protocols have been shown to improve the degree of conversion and reduce residual monomer content, strict adherence to manufacturer-recommended post-processing procedures remains critical to minimize biological risks.23 Clinicians should also obtain a thorough medical and allergy history before initiating treatment, particularly regarding previous reactions to dental resins, methacrylates, or polyurethane-based materials. Although reported adverse events with DPAs remain limited in the literature, vigilance is warranted because of prolonged intraoral exposure and the dynamic changes that may occur during material aging and mechanical loading. Continued in vivo investigations are needed to characterize the long-term biological response to these materials under clinical conditions.

DPA resins, particularly SMPs, are composed of cross-linked polymers with hard segments that provide structural stability and soft segments that allow flexibility. When heated, soft segments temporarily permit reversible deformation. This allows the clinician or patient to warm the DPA before insertion, enabling adaptation to dental anatomy such as deep undercuts or areas with multiple attachments, without losing its programmed shape or force potential. Once placed in the oral environment, hard segments are activated by body temperature to restore the aligner’s programmed shape.15,38 This mechanism may help apply consistent forces immediately and maintain them over the prescribed wear time. This improved adaptation may allow DPAs to accommodate higher activations while maintaining proper fit, whereas TFAs may be limited by rapid force decay and deformation risks.

The unique properties of DPAs require specific clinical handling protocols to optimize efficacy. Clinicians and patients should warm DPAs before placement, particularly in cases involving complex dental anatomy or multiple attachments, to use their shape-memory properties effectively. This warming may improve aligner adaptation to the dental anatomy and reduce deformation during placement, which could otherwise contribute to force decay and suboptimal outcomes. Patients should also be instructed to adhere to the recommended wear time, typically > 22 hr/day, and to maintain appropriate oral hygiene.

BIOMECHANICS AND FORCE SYSTEMS

Optimal orthodontic tooth movement relies on light, continuous forces, generally ranging from 50 to 250 g. Forces below this range may be ineffective, whereas excessive forces may increase the risk of root resorption and periodontal damage.39 Studies have shown that TFAs can generate excessive forces that exceed physiological limits. Elkholy et al.40 observed that 0.25-mm activation of 0.5-mm-thick TFAs generated forces ranging from 301 to 531 g, whereas Gao and Wichelhaus41 reported forces as high as 810 g; these findings indicate potential challenges in achieving safe, physiological tooth movement.

By using their shape-memory properties, DPAs may help maintain force levels within physiological limits.9,19,25,42 Hertan et al.9 reported that under comparable displacements of 0.1–0.3 mm, TFAs delivered forces up to 15.04 N, whereas DPAs delivered forces ranging from 0.76 to 1.57 N. This controlled force magnitude may reduce unwanted effects on both target and adjacent teeth.

Stress reduction under constant strain is a major limitation of TFA materials. Studies have shown that thermoformed plastics exhibit substantial force decay over 15 days, with stress levels decreasing to 39.2% of their initial value within the first few hours.43

The viscoelastic behavior of DPA resins is highly sensitive to loading conditions and specimen geometry. Cremonini et al.44 provided an important perspective on these properties, reporting that under constant 0.5-mm deflection of material specimens at 37°C, TC-85 exhibited 90–100% stress relaxation. This high decay rate under static strain in a controlled material-level test raises potential concerns about force sustainability. However, these results contrast with findings from investigations using actual aligner geometries. Smith45 and Remley et al.8 evaluated TC-85 in its functional appliance form and found that it delivered lower and more consistent forces than thermoformed materials; Smith45 also observed almost no stress relaxation over a 14-day period. Whereas TFAs can lose up to 84% of their force over 2 weeks, DPAs in aligner form have shown minimal reductions or even slight increases in force retention, depending on activation. The discrepancy between these results suggests that the 3D structural integrity of the aligner shell may provide biomechanical advantages that are not evident in standard material testing. Furthermore, because TC-85 is a shape-memory polymer, its stiffness and force potential may recover each time the aligner is reinserted and thermally activated by body temperature, a dynamic property that constant-deflection models may not capture.

Consequently, whereas TFAs are generally restricted to activations of approximately 0.25 mm per stage, DPAs may accommodate a broader activation range, often exceeding 0.4 mm per stage. The lower elastic modulus and larger elastic range of TC-85 compared with PETG (4.65% vs. 3.92%) may enable it to maintain predictable forces while accommodating higher activations without permanent deformation. This biomechanical stability and efficiency may reduce the total number of aligners required for treatment, with potential benefits for clinical outcomes and environmental sustainability.46

DESIGN AND CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

The enhanced material properties,8,19 geometric precision,6 and design flexibility of DPAs may offer clinical advantages in managing complex tooth movements that are challenging with TFAs. The following cases illustrate these clinical applications; informed consent was obtained from all included patients.

Reduced dependence on attachments

Complex movements, such as rotation, torque, and extrusion, traditionally require bonded attachments to improve aligner tracking and force delivery;10 however, attachments have esthetic limitations. DPAs use customized internal geometry to enhance control and deliver physiologically acceptable forces, potentially allowing challenging movements with fewer attachments.

Variable thickness, reinforcement zones, and custom trimlines

TFAs are inherently limited by the starting plastic sheet thickness. Conversely, DPAs allow precise variation in aligner thickness during the CAD phase, enabling targeted force application and anchorage customization (Figure 1B).47 Grant et al.19 showed that increasing labial and lingual thickness improved torque control by generating stronger counter-moments and minimizing unintended forces on adjacent teeth during lingual movement.

The following case illustrates the potential for improved biomechanical torque control with DPAs. A 14-year-old male patient undergoing comprehensive orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances exhibited undesirable torque expression of the mandibular left central incisor during the finishing stage of treatment. The root was displaced into an unfavorable position within the alveolar bone housing, leading to periodontal compromise (Figure 3A and 3B). At this point in treatment, the patient and his family expressed a desire to transition from fixed appliances to clear aligners. With conventional TFAs, this scenario would pose significant limitations because previous studies have reported suboptimal accuracy and efficiency of TFAs in producing controlled torque movements.10,48 A recent cone-beam computed tomography study further reported low torque efficiency with TFAs, averaging approximately 47%, with particularly poor root-movement control.49 Given the biomechanical potential of DPAs, a favorable outcome was considered achievable through changes in aligner dimensions. Moreover, recent clinical data on DPAs reported torque accuracies approaching 68%, which were higher than those documented for TFAs.50

Figure 4A–D demonstrate the biomechanical enhancements incorporated into DPAs fabricated using TA-28 resin. First, 10° of lingual root torque was prescribed for the mandibular left central incisor in the digital setup without overcorrection, given the higher precision reported for DPAs.50 The STL models were then converted into aligner shells using a design software (Direct Aligner Design 1.0, Graphy), with several intentional biomechanical design features: 1) Extended trimline: the gingival margin around the target tooth was intentionally extended by 2 mm to increase regional stiffness and improve the efficiency of force delivery. This design is supported by recent evidence showing that trimline configuration plays a significant role in the biomechanical performance of clear aligners. A recent review showed that extended borders provide better control for demanding movements, particularly torque control.51 2) Cervically positioned 1-mm internal pressure area: a 1-mm pressure area was placed as cervically as possible to generate an appropriate counter-moment and improve precision during lingual root-torque movement. 3) Localized external thickness reinforcement: to further stiffen the aligner in the region responsible for expressing lingual root torque, the external surface over mandibular left central incisor was selectively thickened from 0.5 to 0.8 mm to enhance the local modulus of elasticity and reduce deformation under load, thereby improving the efficiency and predictability of torque expression. This design approach is supported by the in vitro findings of Grant et al.,19 which demonstrated that modifying the labial or lingual surface thickness of DPAs results in measurable changes in the magnitude and distribution of forces and moments delivered during controlled tooth movement.

A movement that would typically require root-control attachments, overcorrections, and likely refinements in a thermoformed system was achieved with two DPAs, each with 5° of activation. These coordinated digital modifications produced improvements in root positioning, as confirmed by the final superimposition and intraoral records (Figure 4).

Integration of auxiliaries

A key advantage of DPA technology is its capacity to integrate auxiliary components, such as buttons, hooks, anterior bite plates, posterior bite blocks, transpalatal bars, and tongue spurs, directly into the aligner shell during printing. This capability can eliminate separate bonding procedures, streamline chairside workflow, and enhance esthetics by minimizing visible attachments on the tooth surface.

In patients for whom bonding is contraindicated, digitally integrated buttons can be designed into the aligner shell. Figure 5 illustrates this approach in a patient with Class II subdivision left malocclusion and amelogenesis imperfecta. Patterson et al.52 demonstrated that patients with Class II malocclusion achieved only 6.8% of the predicted anteroposterior correction during CAT, often requiring substantial refinements. Finite element analyses by Liu et al.53 showed that Class II elastics can reinforce maxillary anchorage during maxillary molar distalization, reduce forward movement of the anterior teeth, and maintain molar position after distalization. However, this patient’s condition contraindicated bonding auxiliaries to the enamel. Because elastics were still required on the left side to maintain anchorage control during sequential distalization of the maxillary arch for correction of the dental Class II relationship, digitally integrated buttons were designed and incorporated directly into the aligner shell before printing. To further improve retention and optimize the force system, the trim height of the maxillary left canine and mandibular left molar was increased to 2 mm, and the buccal thickness surrounding the button area was reinforced. These targeted modifications created a stiffer, load-bearing region within the aligner, which may help prevent deformation or dislodgment during elastic wear and allow the system to deliver the intended anteroposterior correction more efficiently.

Despite the intrusive effects of CAT, clinical expression of programmed bite closure remains only moderately predictable. Blundell et al.54 evaluated the accuracy of anterior open bite treatment with aligners. On average, only 66% of the programmed bite closure was achieved. These findings indicate that although open-bite closure is more efficient than bite opening with CAT, overall accuracy remains limited. Independent of planned mechanics, correction of the underlying functional etiology, particularly tongue posture, plays an important role. If the habit persists, the open bite may relapse despite appropriate mechanics. DPA technology enables habit-control auxiliaries to be digitally fabricated directly into the aligner shell. Figure 6 exemplifies this approach in a patient with a moderate-to-severe open bite, spacing, and clinical signs of a tongue-thrusting habit. Digitally integrated spurs were used to retrain tongue posture while simultaneously delivering programmed vertical moments (Figure 6B). Because spurs were printed as part of the shell rather than bonded afterward, they maintained precise dimensions, intended angulation, and consistent positioning throughout wear. After 2 months of DPA use, the patient exhibited vertical improvement, including measurable reduction of the open bite and progressive anterior space closure (Figure 7).

Achieving predictable posterior expansion, particularly in the molar regions, remains a biomechanical challenge with conventional horseshoe-shaped TFAs. The open-ended posterior geometry of TFAs can reduce anchorage, allowing the aligner to deform under expansion loads and resulting in rotation, tipping, or insufficient buccal force. A recent study demonstrated that aligners printed without a transpalatal bar generated less controlled buccal forces during expansion, supporting the anchorage limitations of free-end aligner designs.55 This finding aligns with observations from a recent finite element study by Li et al.,56 which showed that alternating expansion between premolars and molars improved overall efficiency only when adequate anchorage was available. This finding suggests that predictable expansion is limited not by software staging alone but also by how well the posterior segments resist deformation.56 Together, these findings support the importance of anchorage reinforcement during controlled transverse movements, particularly in the posterior region. The transpalatal bar may stabilize the anchorage unit, maintain structural stiffness across the arch, and reduce deformation under load. Building on this biomechanical foundation, the following clinical case illustrates the use of digitally designed and direct-printed transpalatal bars attached to both the maxillary first premolars and maxillary first molars during maxillary arch expansion with DPAs (Figure 8). This aligner shell design incorporates a distributed and reinforced anchorage system that may minimize unwanted tipping and improve the reliability of transverse forces. The clinical outcome shown in Figures 8 and 9 illustrates the potential effectiveness of this auxiliary design, with expansion achieved from maxillary first premolars through maxillary first molars.55

Addition of pressure points

DPAs allow precise placement of pressure areas and relief channels on the aligner’s intaglio surface to generate enhanced rotational moments.10,57 This may allow accelerated treatment protocols in which DPAs manage activations of 5° per tray, exceeding the rotational limits of approximately 1.5° per tray with TFAs. In the following case, a maxillary lateral incisor presented with a combined displacement pattern of a distal-out rotation and insufficient lingual root torque (Figure 10), which are among the least predictable movements in CAT. To correct this, the digital setup prescribed 9.1° of distal-in rotation and 8.1° of lingual root torque, a two-plane correction that would traditionally require multiple attachments, overcorrections, and a high likelihood of refinements if attempted with TFAs. To enhance the biomechanical efficiency of the movement, a 1-mm internal pressure area was incorporated into the intaglio surface of the aligner (Figure 10A). This pressure area was designed in a sash orientation and strategically positioned to create a coupled force system that could influence the rotational pathway and torque moment simultaneously, thereby improving rotation and lingual root-torque expression. In addition, following biomechanical concepts later described by Jahng et al.,57 a lingual relief zone was incorporated to prevent binding of the aligner material and provide the space necessary for programmed torque expression without interference. The combination of the targeted pressure zone and relief area created a controlled envelope of motion that may have enhanced force direction and moment delivery. With this digitally engineered approach, the DPAs expressed both rotational and torque components efficiently (Figure 10F and 10G), addressing movements that have historically shown less than 50% predictability with TFAs. Treatment was completed without attachments or additional overcorrection, illustrating how DPAs may expand the biomechanical possibilities for complex anterior corrections. However, general guidelines regarding the need for overcorrection in DPAs are still evolving, and clinical protocols may vary according to material selection, activation magnitude, and individual patient response. Therefore, although this case illustrates the biomechanical potential of digitally customized pressure and relief design, broader clinical validation is required before definitive recommendations can be established.

Anterior extrusion is biomechanically challenging and typically requires auxiliary mechanisms to ensure adequate force transmission with TFAs.10 DPAs can incorporate cervical pressure areas strategically positioned on the labial and palatal surfaces of the cervical third of the crown to simulate a mechanical gripping effect, generate extrusive forces within physiologically acceptable limits, and offer a potential alternative with minimal attachments for vertical movement. McKay et al.42 reported the effectiveness of this design with DPAs, whereas identical TFA designs failed to produce the desired vector. In the following case, a patient undergoing CAT required maxillary left lateral incisor extrusion, one of the least predictable movements with TFAs and one that often requires visible bonded attachments to achieve an adequate force vector. To avoid the esthetic compromise associated with attachments, the DPA was digitally engineered with cervical pressure columns placed on both the buccal and lingual aspects of the cervical third of the crown (Figure 11A–E). These columns were designed to apply an upward squeezing effect that would direct force along the long axis of the tooth and facilitate extrusion. To complement this design, a small relief window was created on the buccal–incisal portion of the aligner to provide adequate space for crown movement as the pressure columns engaged. This combination of targeted compression and strategic relief was intended to support controlled vertical displacement while minimizing counterproductive binding against the aligner shell. As shown in Figure 11F, the tooth demonstrated some vertical improvement; however, the expressed movement was largely relative extrusion due to lingual displacement of the crown rather than true vertical translation. This finding highlights the complexity of anterior extrusion and underscores the need to better define pressure-area configurations, column geometry, and relief design. Although DPAs offer promising strategies for generating extrusive forces without attachments, further research is required to refine these biomechanical principles and identify configurations that enable more predictable vertical correction. This case illustrates the clinical limitations associated with anterior extrusion using aligners and provides a framework for future investigation, including the use of computational approaches such as finite element analysis to better understand and optimize force systems for vertical tooth movement.

Pressure points may also enhance distalization biomechanics. Previous experimental work has shown that adding a pressure point during maxillary second molar distalization with 0.25-mm activation nearly tripled the magnitude of the distal force generated while reducing undesirable rotational and intrusive components.58 This may represent an advantage over conventional TFAs, which can struggle to deliver adequate distalizing forces because of material thinning and insufficient posterior rigidity. The following clinical case illustrates the practical application of this strategy (Figures 12 and 13). The patient presented with a unilateral Class II molar relationship requiring maxillary left first molar distalization as part of a broader Class II correction plan using clear aligners. Given the reported limitations of TFAs, pressure points were incorporated into the DPAs to enhance anchorage and optimize the direction and magnitude of distalizing forces. A targeted pressure area was placed cervically on the mesiobuccal and distolingual areas of the maxillary left first molar to generate a more effective distal vector while limiting the mesial-out rotation and extrusive side effects commonly associated with TFA-based distalization. Figure 13 illustrates the clinical application of this auxiliary design. After two aligners, an improvement in the molar relationship was observed, accompanied by development of a distinct distalization space mesial to the maxillary left first molar, consistent with active distal movement of the molar. Adjacent teeth exhibited minimal unintended rotation or vertical displacement, consistent with the force–moment control previously documented for DPA-based pressure-point mechanics. This case illustrates how DPAs may facilitate posterior movement in biomechanically demanding scenarios, such as single-tooth molar distalization during Class II correction.

Additionally, DPAs may serve as alternatives to functional appliances such as Twin Blocks by integrating mandibular advancement features directly into the aligner shell. The customization capacity of DPAs may improve patient comfort and enhance compliance because the functional correction is integrated into a single esthetic appliance rather than requiring a bulky removable device. Figure 14 illustrates an example in which a digitally designed advancement mechanism was integrated into the maxillary and mandibular DPAs to posture the mandible forward while maintaining full-arch coverage and retention, with eruption spaces incorporated into the design. This example highlights how the design freedom of direct printing may expand the scope of CAT by allowing clinicians to replicate, and in some cases potentially improve on, the biomechanics of traditional functional appliances within a fully customized, patient-specific aligner system.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

The future of DPA technology will likely be shaped by advances in materials science, artificial intelligence (AI), and manufacturing processes. AI-driven platforms may enhance treatment planning by optimizing force systems, movement sequencing, and predicted treatment duration based on large clinical datasets. Emerging developments in multi-material and 4D printing may further enable aligners to deliver time-dependent or stimulus-responsive force systems. Continued innovation in these areas, along with further research on DPA cytocompatibility and environmental impact, may improve biomechanical precision, reduce refinements, and enhance the adaptability and sustainability of DPA therapy.

CONCLUSIONS

The advent of DPAs represents an important advancement in CAT, offering enhanced geometric precision, efficiency, and customizable design through SMPs and precise manufacturing.

The clinical efficacy of DPAs depends on the integration of advanced materials, rigorous digital workflow, and optimized biomechanics, which may enable precise control of force magnitude and direction through light, continuous forces within physiological limits.

Although short-term studies support the biomechanical and clinical advantages of DPAs, further research is needed to validate their long-term effectiveness, stability under intraoral aging, and design protocols, particularly for complex malocclusions (Table 2). As the technology matures through AI-assisted design and multi-material fabrication, DPAs may become a customizable and sustainable component of contemporary orthodontic practice.
