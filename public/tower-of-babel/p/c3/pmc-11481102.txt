Dissecting shared genetic architecture between depression and body mass index
Authors: Hengyu Zhang, Rui Zheng, Binhe Yu, Yuefeng Yu, Xiaomin Luo, Shujuan Yin, Yingjun Zheng, Jie Shi, Sizhi Ai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing body of evidence supports the comorbidity between depression (DEP) and obesity, yet the genetic mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. Our study explored the shared genetic architecture and causal associations of DEP with BMI.MethodsWe investigated the multigene overlap and genetic correlation between DEP (N > 1.3 million) and BMI (N = 806,834) based on genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and using the bivariate causal mixture model and linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC). The causal association was explored by bi-directional Mendelian randomization (MR). Common risk loci were identified through cross-trait meta-analyses. Stratified LDSC and multi-marker gene annotation analyses were applied to investigate single-nucleotide polymorphisms enrichment across tissue types, cell types, and functional categories. Finally, we explored shared functional genes by Summary Data-Based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) and further detected differential expression genes (DEG) in brain tissues of individuals with depression and obesity.ResultsWe found a positive genetic correlation between DEP and BMI (rg = 0.19, P = 4.07 × 10−26), which was more evident in local genomic regions. Cross-trait meta-analyses identified 16 shared genetic loci, 5 of which were newly identified, and they had influence on both diseases in the same direction. MR analysis showed a bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI, with comparable effect sizes estimated in both directions. Combined with gene expression information, we found that genetic correlations between DEP and BMI were enriched in 6 brain regions, predominantly in the nucleus accumbens and anterior cingulate cortex. Moreover, 6 specific cell types and 23 functional genes were found to have an impact on both DEP and BMI across the brain regions. Of which, NEGR1 was identified as the most significant functional gene and associated with DEP and BMI at the genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10−8). Compared with healthy controls, the expression levels of NEGR1 gene were significant lower in brain tissues of individuals with depression and obesity.ConclusionsOur study reveals shared genetic basis underpinnings between DEP and BMI, including genetic correlations and common genes. These insights offer novel opportunities and avenues for future research into their comorbidities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03681-9.Keywords: Depression, Body mass index, Obesity, Share genetic architecture

Background

Depression (DEP) is a prevalent and severe neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by a highly complex genetic and phenotypic profile, with a lifetime prevalence estimated at approximately 15% to 20% [1, 2]. Obesity is another prevalent health concern in modern society and has suggested to be an independent risk factor for various neuropsychiatric diseases, including DEP [3]. Growing evidence suggests that obesity increases the risk of DEP and vice versa [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that individuals who are overweight or obese and experience depression are at a high risk of various adverse health conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and general dysfunction [6–8]. Compared to individuals who have only DEP or overweight/obesity, those with both DEP and overweight/obesity experience greater psychological and socioeconomic burdens [9, 10]. Several possible biological mechanisms may account for the comorbidity of overweight/obesity and DEP, ranging from genes, endocrine, immuno-inflammatory, metabolic mechanisms to brain-related factors [11]. However, research on the mechanistic and therapeutic aspects of DEP and obesity complications remains limited and insufficient [12].

Both DEP and obesity have a strong genetic basis. Family and twin studies have demonstrated that both DEP symptoms and high body mass index (BMI) are strongly heritable, with heritability estimates of approximately 35% and approximately 40% for DEP and BMI, respectively [13]. Positive genetic correlations between DEP and BMI have also been reported [14]. However, the limited sample size in previous studies may have underestimated the shared genetic basis of DEP and BMI. Recently, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have provided well-powered summary-level data [2], allowing for a more comprehensive examination of the shared genetic risk factors between DEP and BMI. This offers a unique perspective on the comorbidities of depression and obesity, an area that remains understudied. Although a pioneering GWAS study has employed the conjoint false discovery rate approach and identified 32 genetic loci associated with both BMI and DEP [15], these findings were not comprehensive and lack of gene enrichment status across different tissues. Our research further integrated single-cell RNA (sc-RNA) and expression quantitative trait locus data to examine gene enrichment across tissues, cells, and functional components of DEP and BMI. Furthermore, we validated our observations through DEG analyses in brain tissues.

In this study, we first investigated the overall and local genetic correlation between DEP and BMI based on large-scale GWAS summary data. We then conducted a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the causal association between DEP and BMI. Subsequently, we conducted a cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis to explore the shared loci between DEP and BMI. Finally, we employed the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and scRNA sequencing databases to comprehensively investigate tissue and cell type-specific genetic associations between DEP and BMI (Fig. 1). We aimed to provide novel insights into the genetic basis of the comorbidities between DEP and BMI more thoroughly.

Methods

Study samples

DEP

The GWAS data for DEP was derived from a meta-analysis of six datasets, comprising over 1.3 million Europeans (including 371,184 participants with DEP), performed by Thomas D et al. in 2023 [2]. This is the most recent and largest GWAS for DEP to date. The summary-level data from 23andMe was excluded due to transfer agreement restrictions, and UK Biobank data was excluded to prevent sample overlap.

BMI

The GWAS data for BMI was derived from a meta-analysis by the GIANT consortium, consisting of 806,834 participants [16]. Data used in our study are from publicly accessible summary statistics. All original GWAS studies had received ethical approval from their respective committees, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Details for sample collection, quality control, and imputation have been described comprehensively in previous publications.

Statistical analyses

Polygenic genetic overlap and genetic correlation

We used MiXeR v.1.3 [17] to assess polygenic overlap between DEP and BMI, estimating the total number of shared and trait-specific causal variants (i.e., variants with non-zero additive genetic effects on the traits). MiXeR addresses the challenge of pinpointing the precise location of causal variants by focusing on estimating their overall count. MiXeR uses a binary Gaussian mixture model to quantify the additive effects of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as (i) SNPs not affecting any phenotype, (ii) unique SNPs affecting one phenotype, (iii) unique SNPs affecting another phenotype, or (iv) SNPs affecting both phenotypes. The Dice coefficient scores (polygenic overlap measure in the 0 ~ 100% scale) were also computed.

We also utilized the bivariate linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) [18, 19] method to estimate the heritability and genetic correlation between DEP and BMI. This method uses the expected association between linkage disequilibrium (LD) and GWAS association statistics to assess the genetic overlap between traits. We excluded SNPs not aligning with the HapMap 3 SNP reference panel from the DEP and BMI GWAS datasets. We then used pre-computed LD scores from the 1000 Genomes Project [20] to calculate the genetic correlation between DEP and BMI. And we further performed a bivariate LDSC to estimate genetic correlations without constraining the intercept. Despite no sample overlap in the pooled GWAS data for DEP and BMI, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using LDSC with constrained intercepts to enhance statistical power.

Identification of local genetic correlations

To quantify the genetic correlation between DEP and BMI in locally independent regions of the genome, we used the method of Heritability Estimation from Summary Statistics (ρ-HESS) [21, 22]. The ρ-HESS is based on the LD model of the genome reference developed for European populations, identifying 1703 potential regions that approximate LD-independent loci [23]. These regions have an average size of approximately 1.5 MB. We calculated SNP heritability and genetic correlation between these regions for the DEP and BMI GWAS datasets, and then applied the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing.

Mendelian randomization analysis

To explore the hypothesized causal association between DEP and BMI, we used the R packages “TwoSampleMR” and “GSMR.” The Mendelian randomization study followed the STROBE-MR guidelines (Additional file 1) [24]. Our MR analysis incorporated five main MR methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted median (WM), MR-Egger, Mendelian randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO), and Generalized Summary Data-Based Mendelian Randomization (GSMR) (Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods) [25–29]. We selected SNPs with genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) as instrumental variables (IVs) for these five methods. The F-statistics for each IV exceeded 10 [30].

Cross-trait meta-analysis

To identify common SNPs and gene regions for DEP and BMI, we conducted a cross-trait meta-analysis using multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) and cross-phenotype association (CPASSOC) (Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods) [31, 32]. We then conducted a two-by-two cross-trait meta-analysis with CPASSOC as a sensitivity analysis. CPASSOC integrates association evidence from GWAS of multiple traits and allows for heterogeneous effects across traits. LD pruning was conducted using PLINK software with an r 2 threshold of 0.01 (r 2 > 0.01) on SNP sets that were significant in both MTAG and CPASSOC (P < 5 × 10−8) within a 1000-kb window on the chromosome. Novel loci were defined as independent SNPs without LD that were significant but not previously reported in GWAS. To assess colocalization at the locus where the novel locus shared between DEP and BMI was located, we used the R package “Coloc” [33] and applied the Bayesian colocalization method. We computed the posterior probability (PP) for each pair of traits to evaluate whether they were clustered in distinct causal variants (PP.H3) or associated and shared a common causal variant (PP.H4). The threshold for causal signals was set at PP.H3 or PP.H4 > 0.80 [34].

Partitioned genetic correlation analysis

To investigate genomic functional elements associated with common genetic traits between DEP and BMI, we used stratified LDSC (S-LDSC) [35]. This method partitions the heritability of the GWAS for both traits, considering the LD between SNPs. S-LDSC uses information from all SNPs in the GWAS to classify them into distinct functional categories and employs explicit modeling of LD to estimate the proportion of SNP heritability for each functional category. In our study, we included a total of 24 generic broad functional annotations, totaling 29 previously published functional elements [35, 36]. Category-specific enrichment was defined as the proportion of SNP heritability to the proportion of SNPs in the same functional category. Results for both traits were considered co-enriched functional group elements only if they remained significant after adjusting for multiple testing by using the FDR correction (P < 0.05).

LDSC-SEG analysis

Considering the central role of specific brain regions in regulating weight and energy balance, which overlap with those involved in emotion regulation [11], we used LDSC-SEG to investigate the brain tissues most associated with SNP heritability for DEP and BMI. GTEx V8 provides 13 brain tissue types, including SNPs associated with quantitative gene expression traits in these tissues [37]. Using S-LDSC, we calculated the degree of heritability enrichment of DEP and BMI in SNPs associated with these 13 brain tissue types. Significance of the heritability enrichment estimates for each SNP was assessed by the P-value of the regression coefficient Z-score. Tissues that maintained significance (P < 0.05) after correction for FDR were considered co-enriched for both traits.

Cell type enrichment analysis

Utilizing a scRNA sequencing [38], dataset of brain tissue co-enriched for DEP and BMI, along with the GWAS for both traits, we performed Multi-marker Analyses of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) [39]. By using cell type specificity analysis with scRNA-seq, we calculated cell-type specificity matrices to assess the similarity between traits at the cell-type level for GWAS of DEP and BMI. The SNP2GENE process on Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) [40] was utilized with GTEx tissues to assess the significance of the heritability enrichment estimate for each gene. The average gene expression from the adjusted scRNA-seq dataset was integrated with the MAGMA results for each trait, and significantly correlated cell types were identified. FDR correction for multiple testing was applied (P < 0.05).

Summary Data-Based Mendelian Randomization

We used Summary Data-Based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) [41] to identify candidate genes with potential statistical associations in cross-trait meta-analyses of DEP and BMI. SMR integrates GWAS data with expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data to detect associations between phenotypes and gene expression. We performed SMR analyses using eQTL data from the GTEx-Brain-V2 [42] meta-analysis and GWAS data for DEP and BMI. Genome-wide significant SNPs were used as IVs for the HEIDI test to distinguish causality or pleiotropy from the linkage. Functional genes significantly shared between DEP and BMI were defined as those passing the FDR and HEIDI-outer tests in both SMR analyses (P SMR < 0.05, P HEIDI > 0.05, N > 10 SNPs). In addition, we complemented this with KEGG/GO analyses to explore the potential molecular functions and signaling pathways involved in the shared functional genes.

Validation of expression of the gene

We assessed the expression levels of genes associated with obesity and depression in brain tissues using the datasets GSE95243 [43] and GSE53987 [44], respectively. Notably, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) were excluded due to their unknown functions. The GSE95243 dataset comprises samples of hypothalamic neurons derived from pluripotent stem cells, including those from individuals who are healthy (N = 7) and those with obesity (N = 5). The GSE53987 dataset includes prefrontal cortex, striatum, and hippocampal tissue samples from healthy individuals (N ranges from 17 to 19) and individuals with major depression (N = 17). We used the Mann–Whitney U test to assess the differences in gene expression between the experimental and control groups. Statistical analyses were performed using R software Version 4.2.3. with the package “GEO2R.”

Results

Quantifying polygenic overlap and genetic correlation

Univariate MiXeR analysis identified a total of 9.4 k (standard deviation [SD] = 0.7 k) and 10.3 k (SD = 0.6 k) genetic variants affecting DEP and BMI, respectively. Of these, 7.4 k (SD = 0.5 k) causal variants associated with DEP were shared with BMI (Fig. 2a). The overall polygenic overlap was 75.0% as quantified by the Dice coefficient. Bivariate LDSC analyses revealed a significantly positive genetic correlation between DEP and BMI (rg = 0.19, se = 0.02, P = 4.07 × 10−26). The genetic correlation estimated under the constrained intercept condition yielded similar results (Additional file 3: Table S1).

Local genetic correlations

The local genetic correlation between DEP and BMI was estimated using the ρ-HESS method, identifying a total of 171 loci with significant genetic correlation (P < 0.05, uncorrected, Additional file 3: Table S2 and S3). After applying the Bonferroni corrections, strong local correlations were observed in 7 distinct regions (Fig. 2b). The largest genetic covariate of the locus is at CHR16 (local genetic covariance = 5.27E − 04, P = 1.18E − 06), while CHR3 shows the most significant P-value of the local genetic covariates (local genetic covariance = 3.16E − 04, P = 2.08E − 07, Additional file 3: Table S4).

Mendelian randomization

GSMR results indicated a bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI (Fig. 2c). A high BMI potentially increases the risk of DEP (IVW: β = 0.17, SE = 0.02, P = 3.29 × 10−15), and conversely, DEP has an effect on increasing the risk of a high BMI (IVW: β = 0.21, SE = 0.05, P = 4.10 × 10−6). To ensure the robustness of the causal inference, we employed multiple MR methods, utilizing the genetic loci significantly associated with each phenotype (P < 5 × 10−8) as IVs [2, 16] (Additional file 3: Table S5 and S6). All IVs had F-statistics > 10. The results consistently indicated a bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI, with comparable effect sizes estimated in both directions (Fig. 3 and Additional file 3: Table S7). Leave-one-out analyses showed that the effect was not driven by any single SNP (Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Identification of genomic risk SNP from cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis

Given the significant genetic correlation between DEP and BMI, we conducted a cross-trait meta-analysis of GWAS using two complementary approaches: MTAG and CPASSOC. We identified 16 genome-wide significant SNPs after LD pruning (P < 5 × 10−8, Additional file 3: Table S8). After excluding SNPs significant in the DEP or BMI GWAS, as well as any previously reported significant SNPs, we identified five new pleiotropic SNPs associated with DEP and BMI (i.e., rs4575255, rs2734838, rs1931388, rs6905113, and rs1499984). Further colocalization analyses were performed to determine if causal variants were associated with the DEP and BMI at the locus level driving the new pleiotropic SNPs (Additional file 3: Table S9). All of the identified pleiotropic SNPs between DEP and BMI had causal variants associated with both traits (PP.H4 or PP.H3 > 0.8).

Partitioned genetic correlation

The SNPs associated with DEP were enriched in 7 of the 29 functional categories, while those associated with BMI were enriched in 17, with all led by conserved regions in primates (DEP: enrichment = 10.5, P = 1.48 × 10−6; BMI: enrichment = 11.7, P = 5.08 × 10−12; Additional file 3: Table S10). Six functional categories were significantly associated with both DEP and BMI (Fig. 4a and Additional file 3: Table S11). To assess the genetic overlap at the functional category level, we calculated the genetic correlation between DEP and BMI for the SNP heritability involved in these six functional group regions (Fig. 4b and Additional file 3: Table S11). DEP had significant genetic correlations with BMI in 5 of the 6 functional categories, with rg ranging from 0.17 (Intron) to 0.19 (Conserved primate).

Tissue-level SNP heritability enrichment

We used publicly available GWAS data and 13 different brain regions from GTEx to assess tissue-level enrichment of DEP and BMI heritability by using LDSC-SEG. A total of 12 brain tissues were found to be significantly enriched in the context of DEP (Fig. 5a and Additional file 3: Table S12). After adjusting the baseline model, we observed significant co-enrichment of DEP and BMI in six tissues (P < 0.05, FDR correction), predominantly in the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) (Fig. 5b and Additional file 3: Table S12).

Cell-level SNP heritability enrichment

Using FUMA in conjunction with single-cell RNA datasets, we explored the SNP heritability enrichment of DEP and BMI in specific cell types. The SNP heritability for both DEP and BMI were found to be enriched in GABAergic neuronal cells in the prefrontal cortex (Additional file 2: Fig. S2). In the hypothalamus, we found that glutamatergic neuronal cells played a role in both DEP and higher BMI (Additional file 2: Fig. S3). In the striatum, DEP and BMI were enriched in neuronal cells, though specific neuronal cell type enrichment was not identified Additional file 2: Fig. S4).

Identification of shared functional genes for DEP and BMI

We utilized SMR to infer potential functional genes shared between DEP and BMI by jointly analyzing the GWAS data of two traits and eQTL data from a meta-analysis of brain tissue from GTEx. We identified 23 shared risk genes (Additional file 3: Table S13), all of which passed the HEIDI outlier test (PHEIDI > 0.05, PSMR < 0.05). The gene NEGR1 was the most significant (PSMR = 2.9 × 10−7, PHEIDI = 0.48; PSMR = 1.8 × 10−12, PHEIDI = 0.42). Among these identified genes, NEGR1 and lncRNA AC134407.2 were previously identified as genetically shared variants in cross-trait meta-analysis between DEP and BMI. Their effects were consistent in the direction (PSMR = 1.0 × 10−6, PHEIDI = 0.24; PSMR = 1.9 × 10−6, PHEIDI = 0.23). We further performed KEGG/GO analysis using 19 shared genes after removing 4 inapplicable LncRNAs. Partially shared genes were enriched only in the molecular function category (ribonucleoside triphosphate phosphatase activity, GTPase activity, acyltransferase activity, etc.) and cellular composition (cell body, dendrite) category of GO-analysis (Supplementary Table S14).

Differential expression of NEGR1 in depression and obesity participants

We further evaluated the mRNA expressions of NEGR1 in tissues from individuals with depression or obesity, compared to healthy controls. The expression of NEGR1 was lower in hypothalamic tissue from individuals with obesity compared to healthy controls (P = 0.037, unpaired t-test, two-sided, Supplementary Table S15). Similarly, individuals with depression had significantly lower NEGR1 expression in prefrontal cortex tissue compared to healthy controls (P = 0.005, unpaired t-test, two-sided, Additional file 3: Table S15). However, no differences in NEGR1 expression were found in hippocampus and striatum between the DEP group and the healthy control group.

Discussion

In the present study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the genetic correlation between DEP and BMI using large-scale phenotypic GWAS and tissue/cell type-specific expression data. Our findings indicated a significant positive genetic correlation between DEP and BMI, particularly pronounced in specific local genomic regions. Cross-trait meta-analyses identified 16 shared risk loci, including 5 novel loci that exert influence on both traits. MR analysis further suggested a bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI. When combined with gene expression data, SNP heritability for both DEP and BMI was found to be enriched in 6 brain regions, predominantly in the NAc and ACC. We also found a co-enrichment of SNP heritability in 6 specific cellular and 6 functional catalogs. Through SMR analysis, we identified 23 functional genes in brain tissue that have an impact on both DEP and BMI, most of which had the same direction of influence on both. Notably, the NEGR1 gene was identified as a significant shared gene for both DEP and BMI. The expression levels of the NEGR1 gene were lower in tissues from individuals with depression and obesity compared to healthy controls. These findings offer novel insights into the common genetic pathological basis underlying the comorbidities of depression and obesity, potentially advancing their prediction, diagnosis, and treatment.

Prior studies have consistently demonstrated a substantial comorbidity between DEP and obesity [4, 5], suggesting a potential vicious circle in their association [11]. Based on multigene overlap analyses, we found that approximately 79% of the causal variants affecting the risk of DEP may also influence changes in individual BMI, consistent with previous findings [45]. Local genetic correlation analyses identified 171 loci with genetic correlations between DEP and BMI, some of which were in the opposite direction to the overall genetic correlation assessment, suggesting a greater overlap of risk genes between DEP and BMI than anticipated. These results support the hypothesis that genetic factors may play an important role in the comorbidity of depression and obesity.

Previous evidence concerning the causal association between BMI and DEP has been inconsistent [46]. In the present study, the MR analyses suggest a bidirectional causal association between BMI and DEP. We utilized various MR methods to mitigates the potential confounding effects of pleiotropy from GWAS sources on the estimation. Notably, our analyses revealed that four of the five MR methods exhibited statistically significant findings for the bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI. This consistency across multiple methodologies underscores the robustness and reliability of our study’s conclusions.

This study represents the first genome-wide cross-trait analysis to systematically investigate the shared genetic basis of DEP and BMI. A total of 16 shared-risk SNPs were identified in both traits by MTAG and CPASSOC, of which 5 were novel SNPs (i.e., rs4575255, rs2734838, rs1931388, rs6905113, rs1499984). Three of the SNPs can be mapped to specific genes. rs4575255 is located in LINC02758, a lncRNA with unclear function. rs2734838 is located in DRD2, a gene encoding the dopamine D2 receptor associated with ingestive behaviors, cognition, and susceptibility to psychiatric disorders [47, 48]. rs6905113 was located in ASCC3, a protein with deconjugating enzyme activity associated with DNA damage repair and immune regulation [49]. Through a colocalization analyses, we further determined that these shared risk SNPs have causal effects on both traits at the locus level (PP.H4 or PP.H3 > 0.8), suggesting that these pleiotropic SNPs may serve as novel targets for treating DEP and obesity. Further studies on the function of these genes are needed.

Enrichment analyses stratified for functional components showed that three annotated regions (Conserved, Human Promoter, Intron) were associated with DEP and BMI. Consistent with our findings, many proteins regulated by genes in conserved regions, such as REDD1, DOC, and the proteome of synapses, are associated with depression, obesity, and metabolic risk [50, 51]. In addition, human promoters belonging to non-coding regions and play a synergistic role in the development of DEP and obesity [52, 53]. Tissue enrichment analyses revealed that DEP and BMI were enriched in 6 out of 13 brain tissues, predominantly in the NAc and ACC. Research has shown that changes in circuit activity of the NAc can alter food-seeking processes and modulate depression-like symptoms [54, 55]. Recent research has suggested that NAc could play an important role in the development of depression-like symptoms induced by a high-fat diet [56]. In addition, ACC plays a crucial role in reward evaluation and the manifestation of emotion-related behavioral deficits [57, 58]. A recent study indicates that ACC is associated with major depression and insulin resistance and may mediate symptoms of increased appetite [59]. Taken together, these findings suggest a possible comorbidity mechanism between DEP and BMI in the brain.

Furthermore, the tissue-level enrichment of genetic heritability for DEP and BMI is likely to be cell-type-specific. Neurons play a crucial role in the complex physiological processes underlying the pathogenesis of depression and obesity, with their effects typically being bidirectional [60]. Our study shows that DEP and BMI are co-enriched in specific brain cells, such as GABAergic in the prefrontal cortex. Individuals with DEP often exhibit a reduced number of GABAergic interneurons in the prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate gyrus [61]. The anti-obesity effects of leptin are mainly mediated by GABAergic neurons [62]. The potential mechanisms through which Oligodendrocyte precursor cells in the prefrontal cortex influence the development of DEP and high BMI need further investigation. Previous studies have suggested that Vglut2 neuronal cells in the hypothalamus are involved in DEP and obesity pathophysiological processes [63, 64]. Our findings provide further evidence supporting the role of Vglut2 neuronal cells in the comorbid state of depression and obesity.

Among the 23 potential functional genes, NEGR1, B3GAT1, and FURIN have been reported in studies related to metabolic diseases and mental health conditions[65–67]. KCNK3, POM121C, PPIL2, and PRDM4 have only been reported to be associated with metabolism and insulin resistance [68–70]. A study has shown that RAB27B may be one of the risk genes for DEP [71]. Fifteen genes are newly identified to be associated with depression or BMI. Among these genes, ZDHHC5 and ZFYVE21 may be involved in activating inflammation [72, 73]. Using SMR and HEIDI, we identified NEGR1 as a potential link between DEP and BMI, and our transcriptomic analyses using trait-related samples further verified this finding. NEGR1 is involved in promoting neurite outgrowth, dendritic tree formation, and adult neurogenesis [74, 75]. Rats with NEGR1 deficiency exhibited a time-dependent increase in behavioral sensitivity to amphetamine, and this response was associated with changes in amphetamine-induced dopamine release in the ventral and dorsal striatum, suggesting altered responsiveness of the midbrain limbic pathway in the absence of NEGR1 expression [76]. It is well known that midbrain limbic system pathways are closely related to reward processes. Dysregulation of reward processing is present in both depressed and obese individuals [77, 78]. NEGR1 gene expression may mediate the development of DEP and obesity through abnormal reward processing. However, further studies are needed to investigate the specific mechanisms by which NEGR1 influences the pathogenesis of DEP and obesity.

Several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, the GWAS data were mainly derived from European population. Previous studies suggested that the impact of risk factors associated with DEP exhibits ethnic disparities [79, 80]. Consequently, generalization of our findings to other ethnic groups should be cautious. Second, our cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis may be subject to potential biases arising from the violations of MTAG assumptions. Nonetheless, these potential biases are likely minimal given our implementation of CPASSOC as a sensitivity analysis, which yielded highly concordant results. Third, sex-stratified analyses were not performed due to sex-specific GWAS summary statistics were not available. Fourth, the selected single-cell RNA dataset had limited tissue sources for DEG analyses, which constrained our ability to perform a more comprehensive analysis.

Conclusions

Our study found a significant genetic correlation and bidirectional causal association between DEP and BMI. We further identified share risk loci and genetic architectures between the two traits, with the putative functional gene NEGR1 potentially involved in the comorbidity between DEP and obesity. Our study provides novel insights into the shared genetic pathogenesis of DEP and BMI, contributing to the development of therapeutic targets that could be beneficial for treating the comorbidities of depression and obesity.
