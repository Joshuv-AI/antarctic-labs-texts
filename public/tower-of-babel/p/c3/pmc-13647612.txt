The role of medical equipment reliability assessment in improving healthcare service quality: a scoping review
Authors: Jidong Zhang, Zhenglin Lou, Xinyi He, Zhongxin Jin, Dan Cao, Yiyi Xu, Weixin Zhang, Junyu Zhou, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the rapid advancement of medical technology, medical equipment plays a vital role in diagnosis, treatment, and patient monitoring. Equipment reliability is not merely an engineering concern but a fundamental determinant of healthcare quality, patient safety, and clinical decision-making. All English-language studies examining the relationship between medical equipment reliability and healthcare service quality were considered. Two reviewers independently performed literature screening, data extraction, and methodological quality assessment (using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool) to characterize the strength of evidence supporting parameter–decision–outcome linkages—an approach consistent with scoping review methodology when the research question involves evidence mapping with quality considerations. Background

The rapid advancement of medical technology has established medical equipment as a cornerstone of modern healthcare, playing an integral role in disease diagnosis, treatment, and patient monitoring. In this context, equipment reliability is no longer merely an engineering concern but a critical factor directly influencing healthcare quality, patient safety, and the accuracy of clinical decisions [1, 2]. Sudden equipment failure or performance degradation can lead to misdiagnosis, treatment interruptions, and even serious adverse events [3]. Consequently, establishing a systematic and scientific reliability assessment framework for proactive monitoring and maintenance has become an urgent priority for healthcare institutions aiming to improve risk management and clinical quality [4].

The core of reliability assessment lies in continuously monitoring and quantitatively analyzing a series of key parameters. Existing research, both domestic and international, has explored various parameters that constitute equipment reliability. These can generally be categorized as follows: (1) Time-based metrics, such as Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) and Mean Time To Repair (MTTR), which reflect equipment stability and maintainability from an availability perspective [5]; (2) Probability and statistics-based indicators, such as failure rate, reliability function, and expected service life, which provide a data-driven foundation for predictive maintenance [6]; and (3) Performance state parameters, including output accuracy, repeatability, and performance degradation trends, which directly affect the quality of clinical outputs [7]. Additionally, technology lifecycle status and whole-lifecycle cost are increasingly considered essential dimensions for evaluating overall reliability and economic efficiency [8]. Although these parameters are theoretically well-established, their practical application remains inconsistent across studies and regions—particularly regarding definitions, measurement standards, weighting methods, and integrated implementation.

A critical unanswered question is whether the key parameters identified in the international literature are compatible with China’s current medical equipment maintenance standards and practices. Medical device regulation in China follows stringent requirements issued by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), including the “Regulations for the Supervision and Administration of Medical Devices” and mandatory preventive maintenance (PM) protocols in healthcare institutions. The Chinese model emphasizes periodic verification, scheduled PM, and close linkage with the adverse event monitoring and reporting system [9]. While this compliance-driven approach shares common goals with international practices, it also presents conceptual tensions with the emerging global trend toward predictive maintenance, which relies on real-time data and risk analysis [8]. Medical equipment reliability is not merely an engineering concern—it is a fundamental determinant of health system performance. Equipment failures directly impact continuity of care, responsiveness, patient safety, and patient-centeredness. Conversely, effective reliability assessment and maintenance management can produce measurable health services improvements: reduced waiting times, fewer repeated procedures, shorter hospital stays, and enhanced patient and staff satisfaction. Despite this intrinsic linkage, no review has explicitly mapped the causal pathways from reliability parameters → maintenance decisions → health services outcomes. Existing reviews focus on technical performance metrics or operational metrics, but stop short of articulating patient-relevant and system-relevant outcomes. This gap limits the translation of reliability research into health policy, hospital management, and clinical practice. This review addresses this gap by: (1) systematically identifying reliability parameters from the literature; (2) empirically these parameters to management decisions; and (3) explicitly tracing the causal linkages from each parameter–decision pairing to measurable health services outcomes, using evidence extracted from included studies and mapped to established WHO health system performance domains.

China is selected as the comparative focus for three interconnected reasons. First, China operates one of the world’s largest medical device markets, with an aging equipment inventory exceeding 1.2 million units in tertiary hospitals alone—creating urgent reliability challenges that are generalizable to other rapidly developing healthcare systems. Second, China’s regulatory framework (NMPA) remains predominantly compliance-driven and periodic verification-based, whereas international best practice has shifted toward predictive, data-driven, and value-based maintenance. This divergence offers a high-contrast case for examining the practical implications of adopting international parameter frameworks. Third, our institutional context (a large Chinese tertiary hospital) enables grounded interpretation of implementation barriers—such as the current limitation of real-time performance monitoring to 12–18% of high-value equipment—that may inform policy in similar settings across Asia, Latin America, and Africa. Thus, while the review’s parameter framework is globally derived, the comparative analysis uses China as an illustrative case of a healthcare system at a critical inflection point between compliance and predictive paradigms.

Methods

Data sources, search strategy, and selection criteria

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [10] and followed its 20-item reporting checklist. Consistent with scoping review methodology, the objective was to map the existing literature, identify key concepts, and examine the extent, range, and nature of research activity, rather than to synthesize effect sizes through meta-analysis. The study protocol was registered on the INPLASY platform (registration number: INPLASY2025120062). A comprehensive literature search was performed across three electronic databases—PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science—from their inception until October 31, 2025. The search strategy combined both controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH in PubMed) and free-text terms. Core search concepts included: (“medical equipment” OR “medical devices” OR “biomedical equipment”) AND (“performance” OR “reliability” OR “maintenance”) AND (“assessment” OR “predict” OR “inspect” OR “priorit*”). The full search strings for all three databases are provided in Supplementary File 1. To minimize publication bias, the reference lists of relevant review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. Grey literature (e.g., institutional reports, conference proceedings, white papers) and guideline databases were not searched, as the review aimed to map peer-reviewed parameter definitions and empirically validated decision linkages rather than capture unpublished policy or implementation documents.

Two reviewers independently performed the literature screening following a standardized procedure. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study design: observational studies (e.g., cross-sectional, cohort, case-control) and literature outlining standards; (2) population: any type of medical device used in clinical settings; (3) topic: explicit exploration, definition, or application of one or more key parameters for medical equipment reliability assessment; and (4) language: published in English. Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) full text unavailable despite contacting authors or inter-library loan; (2) publication type: conference abstracts, letters, editorials, commentaries, or single case reports; (3) topic: focused solely on device engineering principles, circuit design, or material science without any explicit discussion, definition, or application of reliability or maintenance assessment methods; (4) outcome: did not report any parameter related to equipment reliability, performance, or maintenance decision-making; (5) population: non-medical devices (e.g., industrial equipment, laboratory-only instruments not used in patient care); (6) language: non-English publications; (7) duplicate publications of the same study.

Data collection and quality assessment

Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers using a pre-designed, standardized form. The extracted information included: the first author’s surname, publication year, country/region, type of maintenance activity (e.g., PM, corrective maintenance [CM], replacement planning [RP]), outcome metrics, and main findings. Additionally, any content comparing or discussing findings in relation to Chinese standards, regulations, or conventional practices was specifically extracted. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by a third reviewer. Although scoping reviews do not routinely assess study quality for the purpose of excluding studies, methodological guidance permits quality appraisal when the research question seeks to characterize the strength of evidence underlying key concepts. Given that our review aims to empirically link reliability parameters to management decisions and patient safety outcomes, we assessed the methodological quality of included studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018 [11]. The tool evaluates five domains: (1) clarity of study objectives; (2) adequacy of input parameter specification; (3) analytical rigor; (4) validity of outputs; and (5) relevance of outcomes to the research question. Based on this appraisal, studies were classified as having high, moderate, or low quality. The quality assessment was also performed independently by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision. Consistent with scoping review methodology, no study was excluded based on quality scores; instead, quality ratings were used to inform the interpretation of evidence strength for specific parameter–decision–outcome linkages.

Statistical analysis

Given the anticipated heterogeneity among the included studies—particularly in parameter definitions, measurement methods, and study designs—a narrative synthesis approach was primarily employed to summarize and analyze the findings. The extracted key parameters were systematically categorized according to a pre-defined classification framework. Tables were used to present the basic characteristics of the included studies, along with the key parameters and their definitions. A qualitative analysis and thematic summary were conducted to address the research questions, focusing on international consensus regarding parameters, variations in measurement, and their applicability to Chinese practices. Special attention was given to literature that directly referenced the Chinese context, enabling a comparative analysis between internationally reported parameters and the requirements stipulated by Chinese regulations.

Results

Literature search

The initial database search yielded 1,846 records. An additional 6 records were identified through manual screening of reference lists, resulting in a total of 1,852 records. After removing 634 duplicates, 1,218 unique records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. This process led to the exclusion of 1,153 records that did not meet the broad inclusion criteria. The full texts of the remaining 65 articles were thoroughly assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of 42 publications owing to not related to assessment methods for medical devices. Ultimately, 23 studies met all pre-defined inclusion criteria and were included in the scoping review [12–34]. The complete study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Overview of included studies The 23 included studies spanned the period 2011–2025 and were conducted across 12 countries representing four continents: North America (Canada, n = 3), Europe (Italy, n = 3; Bosnia and Herzegovina, n = 3; Germany, n = 1; France/Tunisia, n = 1), Middle East (Lebanon, n = 4; Jordan, n = 1; Iran, n = 1; United Arab Emirates, n = 2), Asia (Malaysia, n = 1; Sri Lanka, n = 1; Indonesia, n = 1; China, n = 1), Latin America (Brazil, n = 1; Mexico, n = 1), and Africa (Egypt, n = 3). A marked increase in publication frequency was observed from 2019 onwards, with 15 of 23 studies (65.2%) published between 2019 and 2025, reflecting growing research interest in machine learning (ML), predictive maintenance, and IoT-based approaches.

Maintenance activities addressed

Regarding the types of maintenance activities investigated, the vast majority of studies involved PM (20/23, 87.0%), and more than half also examined CM (14/23, 60.9%). Ten studies (43.5%) focused on RP. Notably, Zamzam 2021 [30] was the only study to comprehensively address all three maintenance activities (PM, CM, and RP) within a unified framework, validated on a nation-scale dataset of 13,352 devices. Three studies concentrated exclusively on RP [14, 20, 25], while two focused solely on PM prioritization [27, 32].

Device types and sample sizes

The included studies exhibited substantial diversity in device types and sample sizes. Eleven studies addressed broad hospital inventories ranging from 20 to 13,352 devices [12, 13, 17, 18, 20, 21, 24, 27, 29, 30, 31], while others focused on specific device categories including defibrillators (n = 4) [19, 23, 24, 29], infant incubators (n = 4) [17, 26, 30, 32], imaging equipment (MRI/CT) (n = 2) [14, 32], and laboratory analyzers (n = 2) [28, 30]. Sample sizes ranged from 4 devices [29] to 13,352 devices (nation-scale) [30], with cumulative device coverage exceeding 17,000 medical devices across the 23 studies.

Methodological approaches

A wide range of methodological approaches were employed, including multi-criteria decision making (MCDM)/AHP (8 studies) [12, 17, 18, 20, 21, 24, 25, 29], ML (6 studies) [23, 26, 28, 30, 32, 34], mathematical modelling/weighted linear (5 studies) [13, 15, 22, 27, 31], fuzzy logic (4 studies) [15, 17, 19, 29], Quality Function Deployment (2 studies) [16, 19], and implementation research (1 study) [31].

Outcome metrics

The studies used a variety of outcome metrics to quantify reliability and prioritization, including criticality/priority scores (12 studies) [12, 13, 16, 17, 19, 21, 24, 27, 28, 29, 31, 32], prediction accuracy (6 studies) [23, 26, 28, 30, 32, 34], risk classification scores (4 studies) [14, 15, 22, 33], replacement scores/index (4 studies) [18, 20, 25, 30], availability/functionality rates (2 studies) [13, 31], and cost savings/NPV/payback (2 studies) [26, 28].

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of all 23 included studies was assessed using the MMAT version 2018 [11], with detailed results provided in Supplementary File 2. Of the 23 studies, 21 (91.3%) were rated as high quality, 2 (8.7%) as moderate quality, and none as low quality. Exclusion of these two moderate-quality studies did not materially alter the parameter framework, thematic findings, or evidence-based decision linkages. Both studies are retained for completeness but are noted as having methodological limitations that warrant cautious interpretation.

Identification of parameter categories

Thematic analysis process The eight parameter categories were identified through a rigorous, iterative thematic analysis conducted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. The analysis proceeded as follows: (1) familiarization with all 23 full-text articles to identify explicitly reported or operationally defined input parameters; (2) extraction of 47 unique parameter terms (e.g., MTBF, failure rate, device age, clinical function, maintenance complexity, utilization rate, lifecycle cost); (3) grouping of these 47 parameters into 12 preliminary categories based on conceptual similarity; (4) merging of overlapping categories into eight final categories after comparison with established classification frameworks [5–8]; (5) consensus-based assignment of operational definitions designed to be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive; and (6) tabulation of the final framework with frequency counts and representative studies. A third reviewer independently mapped all 47 original parameters to the eight categories, achieving 91.5% agreement (disagreements, n = 4, resolved by discussion), confirming classification robustness.

The eight-category parameter framework

Table 2 presents the final eight-category framework derived from the thematic analysis. All eight categories were empirically grounded in the included studies, with frequency of reporting ranging from 74% (Cost) to 100% (Equipment features, Performance). Equipment features and Performance were reported in all 23 studies (100%), confirming their status as core, universal parameters in medical equipment reliability assessment. Function, Maintenance requirement, and Risk and safety were reported in 22 studies (96%), reflecting their central role in risk classification and prioritization. Availability and readiness was reported in 19 studies (83%), Utilization in 18 studies (78%), and Cost in 17 studies (74%). The lower frequency of cost parameters reflects deliberate exclusion in some PM prioritization models [16, 19] where patient safety was prioritized over financial considerations.

Link between parameters categories and management decisions

The linkage between specific parameter categories and core management decisions is not theoretically assumed but empirically demonstrated across multiple studies. Below we synthesize the evidence for each of the four management decisions, with explicit tracing to supporting studies and quantitative effect sizes where reported.

Risk classification: Three parameter categories consistently formed the foundation of risk classification models: function, risk and safety, and availability and readiness. Patient safety implication: High-risk classification triggered more frequent inspections, immediate corrective action for critical failures, and prioritized replacement—directly reducing adverse event rates [21, 28]. Chaminda et al. demonstrated that a low-cost, multicomponent intervention (SOPs + training + focal points) improved availability of critical devices by 5–29% and functionality by 12–39% in Sri Lankan primary care hospitals [31].

Maintenance prioritization: Maintenance prioritization drew upon all eight parameter categories, but the most frequently weighted parameters were: function (criticality), utilization (intensity), performance (failure rate), maintenance requirement (complexity), and risk (safety impact). Patient safety implication: Proper prioritization ensured that devices with greatest clinical impact received maintenance resources first, minimizing disruption to critical care pathways [17, 31]. Chaminda et al. documented significant improvements in staff knowledge (+ 13–28%), skills (+ 20–26%), and satisfaction (+ 26–43%) following implementation of prioritized maintenance protocols [31].

Maintenance strategy selection: Strategy selection-particularly the decision between preventive, predictive, condition-based, and CM-was primarily informed by performance, maintenance requirement, risk, and cost parameters. Patient safety implication: Predictive and condition-based maintenance identified potential failures before clinical impact, preventing treatment interruptions and equipment-related adverse events [33]. Alkhatib et al. emphasized that human-centered design and stakeholder involvement are essential for translating algorithmic predictions into clinically effective maintenance actions [34].

Replacement planning: Replacement decisions were predominantly driven by performance (remaining useful life, service life, failure rate), equipment features (technology obsolescence, age, asset condition), and cost (lifecycle cost, maintenance cost ratio). Patient safety implication: Timely replacement of obsolete or deteriorating equipment prevented failures associated with aging components and outdated technology [25, 34]. Zamzam et al. explicitly linked replacement priority to patient safety: high-priority devices were predominantly life-support/therapeutic (55%) in critical care locations [30].

Collectively, this evidence suggests that the eight parameter categories are associated with specific management decisions in reproducible ways, as documented across 12 countries, 4 continents, > 17,000 devices, and 14 years of research. However, given the heterogeneity in study designs, parameter definitions, and healthcare contexts, these associations should be interpreted as empirically derived patterns rather than universally generalizable causal relationships. No single parameter category operates in isolation; optimal decision-making requires integrated multi-criteria assessment weighing clinical function, technical performance, safety impact, utilization intensity, maintenance requirements, availability, and economic cost.

Comparative analysis: international vs. Chinese practices

International consensus on parameter categories

The eight-category framework demonstrates strong international consensus across the 23 studies. Performance and Equipment features were universally reported (100%). Function, Maintenance requirement, and Risk and safety were reported in 96% of studies, spanning all geographic regions and methodological approaches. Availability, Utilization, and Cost showed more variable reporting (74–83%), reflecting context-specific prioritization rather than fundamental disagreement.

Variations in parameter measurement and weighting: Despite consensus on parameter categories, substantial variation in measurement methods and weighting was observed. First, MTBF/MTTR was defined inconsistently across studies: seven studies [12, 15, 17, 23, 26, 30, 32] defined MTBF as calendar time between failures, while four studies [13, 22, 28, 31] defined it as operational hours. This discrepancy has material implications: devices used intermittently (e.g., emergency backup equipment) will have substantially different MTBF estimates under these two definitions. Second, function weighting ranged from 25% [29] to 62% [14] of total criticality score, a fourfold variation that likely reflects both genuine contextual differences (tertiary vs. primary care) and methodological arbitrariness. Third, cost integration showed contradictory conclusions regarding its relative importance: Tawfik et al. found cost to be the second-most influential parameter (28% weight) [15], while Azadi Parand et al. reported cost as the least influential (8% weight) [29]. This contradiction may reflect differing healthcare financing contexts (Egypt vs. Iran) or device mixes. Fourth, risk quantification was dominated by process-oriented approaches (severity × probability, fuzzy inference) in the international literature [15, 17, 33], while outcome-oriented metrics (adverse event rates) were rarely reported.

Applicability to Chinese practices

Parameters related to time (age, MTBF, MTTR), probability and statistics (failure rate, useful life), and performance status (downtime, number of failures) are highly compatible with existing Chinese practices, such as “PM scheduling” and “equipment availability assessment” as stipulated in NMPA regulations [9]. These parameters can be directly integrated as valuable supplements to the current management system.

However, three major challenges were identified. First, China’s current regulatory framework emphasizes outcome-oriented metrics such as “adverse event incidence rates.” In contrast, international studies focus on process-oriented evaluations such as “risk severity × probability of occurrence” [6, 15, 17, 33] and dynamic, context-sensitive risk factors [15, 17]. Direct adoption of international risk weightings is not possible; adapted risk weightings that account for specific Chinese healthcare contexts—such as differing device utilization intensities between tertiary and primary care hospitals—are urgently needed. Second, equipment management in Chinese medical institutions typically focuses on procurement and maintenance costs, while dimensions like “downtime loss” and “clinical opportunity cost,” prevalent in international research [25, 28], are not yet fully incorporated. Shamayleh et al. demonstrated that integrating downtime cost and clinical opportunity cost into economic analysis yielded positive NPV ($85,177), 1-year payback, and 25% cost savings [28]. Jarikji et al. showed that environmental factors (economic instability, government policy) contributed 21% of replacement decision weight in Lebanon—a finding highly relevant to China’s rapidly evolving healthcare policy context [25]. Third, technologies widely used internationally, such as real-time performance monitoring, IoT sensors, and big data analytics [7, 23, 26, 28, 32, 34], rely on equipment connectivity and data-sharing platforms. Lin et al. demonstrated that power quality monitoring + deep learning achieved R² = 0.95/0.96 for MRI/CT equipment in a Chinese hospital, but noted that such real-time monitoring is currently limited to 12–18% of high-value imaging and life-support equipment in Chinese tertiary hospitals [32]. The current level of device informatization in primary healthcare institutions remains insufficient, restricting the practical application of these parameters.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes 23 studies spanning 14 years (2011–2025), 12 countries, four continents, and over 17,000 medical devices to address three fundamental questions: (1) What parameters constitute a comprehensive medical equipment reliability assessment framework? (2) How do these parameters inform specific management decisions? and (3) How do international frameworks compare with current Chinese practice? Our principal contribution is the first evidence-based, systematically derived eight-category parameter framework with empirically validated linkages to four core management decisions and explicit patient safety implications.

Our synthesis yielded four principal findings: (1) Eight core parameter categories—Equipment features, Function, Maintenance requirement, Performance, Risk and safety, Availability and readiness, Utilization, and Cost—were identified through rigorous thematic analysis of 47 parameters across 23 studies. These categories demonstrate strong empirical grounding and high inter-rater reliability; (2) Reproducible, quantifiable linkages exist between these parameter categories and four core management decisions: risk classification (12 studies), maintenance prioritization (18 studies), strategy selection (14 studies), and RP (11 studies). These linkages are documented across diverse geographic regions, device types, and methodological approaches; (3) Patient safety impact is explicitly documented for each parameter–decision linkage, with effect sizes ranging from 12 to 39% improvement in equipment functionality [31] to 34–41% reduction in unnecessary maintenance [23, 26] to 25% cost savings with 1-year payback [28]; and (4) Significant divergence exists between international frameworks and current Chinese practice, particularly in risk quantification methodology, lifecycle cost accounting, and predictive maintenance infrastructure. Only one study [32] has directly examined these issues in the Chinese context.

Our eight-category framework both extends and refines prior classification systems proposed in the literature. Our framework confirms and extends the foundational work by integrating parameters that were previously siloed across separate studies into a single, coherent taxonomy. Moreover, our framework contradicts the implicit assumption in some earlier work [5, 6] that time-based metrics (MTBF, MTTR, age) are sufficient for comprehensive reliability assessment. Nineteen of 23 studies (82.6%) in our review incorporated non-time parameters—particularly function, risk, utilization, and maintenance requirement—supporting a multidimensional conceptualization of device reliability that extends beyond traditional engineering definitions. Finally, our framework reveals a conceptual gap in the Chinese NMPA guidelines [9], which do not formally recognize utilization, availability, cost-effectiveness, or predictive analytics as explicit reliability parameters. This gap is not merely academic—it has direct operational and patient safety implications, as demonstrated by the 12–39% functionality improvements achieved when these parameters are actively managed [31].

While our synthesis identified strong consensus on the relevance of the eight parameter categories and their general association with management decisions, we observed notable contradictions and unexplained heterogeneity in how specific parameters are operationalized, weighted, and prioritized: (1) Parameter Definitions and Measurement: MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) was defined inconsistently across studies. Seven studies [12, 15, 17, 23, 26, 30, 32] defined MTBF as calendar time between failures, while four studies [13, 22, 28, 31] defined it as operational hours; (2) Weighting of Function vs. Maintenance Requirement: Function was the dominant criterion in Taghipour 2011 [12] (weight 0.45), Hutagalung 2019 [24] (0.34), and Ben Houria 2016 [21] (0.34). However, Maintenance requirement received the highest single weight (20.4%) in Saleh 2015 [16]—exceeding Function (11.7%) and Physical risk (12.8%); (3) Role of Cost in Prioritization: Four studies [15, 17, 29, 30] included maintenance cost in their prioritization models but reached contradictory conclusions regarding its relative importance; and (4) Optimal Maintenance Strategy Rankings: Ben Houria 2016 [21] and Jamshidi 2015 [17] reached opposite conclusions regarding optimal maintenance strategy rankings.

Our finding that 87.0% of studies involve PM is consistent with prior reviews, but we document a critical temporal shift: among studies published 2019–2025, 73% explicitly addressed predictive or condition-based maintenance, compared to only 25% of studies published 2011–2018 [35]. This challenges the assumption—still pervasive in some national policies including China’s NMPA framework [9]—that scheduled PM remains the international gold standard. For devices amenable to real-time monitoring, risk-based, condition-directed approaches have demonstrated advantages over fixed-interval PM in the included studies. However, the evidence base remains primarily from high-resource tertiary care settings, and generalizability to lower-resource environments requires further validation. However, uncritical adoption of predictive maintenance in the Chinese context is inadvisable: successful implementations require ubiquitous device connectivity, standardized data formats, integrated electronic records, and trained staff—prerequisites that remain unevenly distributed. Even in Chinese tertiary hospitals, real-time performance monitoring is currently limited to 12–18% of high-value equipment [32].

The practical feasibility of implementing predictive maintenance in low-resource healthcare settings warrants explicit consideration, as 15 of 23 included studies originated from high-resource or upper-middle-income contexts. Three categories of barriers are evident from the literature: (1) financial—predictive systems require upfront investment in sensors, data storage, and analytics platforms, with payback periods typically exceeding 12–24 months [28]; (2) infrastructural—reliable device connectivity, standardized data formats, and stable electrical power are prerequisites that cannot be assumed in many low-resource settings; and (3) human capital—effective implementation requires biomedical engineers trained in data analytics and machine learning, skills that are scarce outside tertiary referral centers. However, incremental strategies exist. First, low-resource facilities can prioritize predictive approaches for a small subset of high-risk, high-value devices (e.g., ventilators, defibrillators, incubators) while retaining PM for lower-risk equipment—a hybrid model documented in Zamzam et al. [30]. Second, cloud-based predictive services (software-as-a-service) reduce upfront infrastructure costs, though internet reliability remains a constraint. Third, the Sri Lankan experience reported by Chaminda et al. [31] demonstrates that even basic multicomponent interventions (SOPs, training, focal points) achieve 12–39% functionality improvements without sophisticated predictive analytics. Thus, while full predictive maintenance may be aspirational for many low-resource settings, the underlying principle—moving from fixed-interval to risk- and condition-based prioritization—is universally applicable and can be implemented incrementally.

Only 17 studies (74%) included cost as a parameter category, and only 2 studies (8.7%) performed full economic evaluation (NPV, payback, cost-benefit analysis) [25, 28]. This represents a major evidence gap and contradicts the emphasis on cost-effectiveness in healthcare technology assessment guidelines [13, 36]. We recommend that future studies explicitly justify their approach to cost integration rather than defaulting to exclusion or minimal weighting. Our finding that only one study [34] addressed human factors and organizational readiness resonates with, and is substantially reinforced by, the systematic review of Mahfoud et al. [37]. Their comprehensive synthesis of dependability-based maintenance optimization in healthcare revealed that ‘medical devices are becoming more numerous, various and complex. However, the associated practices related to asset management and maintenance in healthcare are still lacking.’ They identified persistent gaps in: (i) integration of environmental disturbances and stochastic degradation into maintenance models; (ii) interoperability of supportability systems; and (iii) translation of industrial maintenance optimization frameworks into clinically validated decision tools. Our review extends their findings by empirically mapping specific parameter–decision–outcome linkages, thereby moving from problem articulation to solution specification.

Our comparative analysis reveals that China is at a critical inflection point. The current compliance-driven, periodic verification model [9] has achieved important gains in baseline safety and performance assurance. However, it is increasingly misaligned with international best practice and inadequate to address the challenges of rapidly aging equipment inventories, growing complexity, escalating maintenance costs, and uneven resource distribution.

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the 23 included studies exhibited substantial diversity in study designs, parameter definitions, measurement methods, device types, and healthcare settings. Although we employed narrative synthesis with explicit ESRG methodology, this heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of our findings and precludes meta-analysis. Secondly, twelve of 23 studies (52.2%) used AHP or other MCDM methods requiring expert-derived weights. These weights are context-dependent and not directly transferable across institutions, countries, or time periods. Only three studies [16, 19, 28] conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of their weighting schemes. Thirdly, only 10 studies (43.5%) validated their models on real-world hospital data with sample sizes > 100 devices [13, 20, 21, 23, 26, 28, 30, 31, 32, 34]. The remaining studies used numerical examples [14, 29], small convenience samples [12, 24, 27], or did not validate [15, 17, 18, 19, 22, 25, 33]. Fourthly, 15 of 23 studies (65.2%) were conducted in Middle Eastern and European countries. Only one study [32] was conducted in China, and none in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia (except Sri Lanka), or Latin America (except Brazil/Mexico). Findings may not generalize to understudied regions with different healthcare financing models, regulatory frameworks, and resource constraints. Fifth, despite manual screening of reference lists, we cannot exclude the possibility of publication bias favoring studies with positive results. Studies reporting negative or null findings may be underrepresented. Sixth, grey literature and guideline databases were excluded from the search strategy. While this decision focused the review on peer-reviewed parameter definitions and empirical linkages, it may have excluded relevant implementation reports, national maintenance guidelines, or non-published quality improvement data from healthcare systems—particularly from low- and middle-income countries.

This scoping review provides the first evidence-based synthesis and conceptual framework for medical equipment reliability assessment. The evidence establishes that medical device reliability assessment is fundamentally a multi-dimensional decision science requiring integration of technical, clinical, economic, environmental, and human factors. The evidence-based framework presented here provides both a conceptual foundation and an operational roadmap for Chinese healthcare institutions and regulatory bodies to transition from compliance-driven planned maintenance toward proactive, predictive, value-driven lifecycle management.

The available evidence, while heterogeneous, consistently indicates that integrated, context-sensitive reliability assessment is associated with improvements in patient safety, clinical effectiveness, operational efficiency, and cost containment. Direct causal claims require further validation through prospective intervention studies. The gaps between international practice and current Chinese policy are substantial but bridgeable. What is required is concerted, evidence-informed action by hospital administrators, clinical engineers, regulators, and researchers.

Such transformation is not optional—it is essential to simultaneously achieve the dual imperatives of contemporary healthcare delivery: maximizing patient safety and clinical quality while optimizing constrained economic resources. The framework, evidence, and recommendations provided herein offer a rigorous, actionable foundation for this critical endeavor.
