Clinicopathological characteristics of intra-abdominal follicular dendritic cell sarcomas: A multicenter real-world study
Authors: Jia-Jun Su, Jie Wu, You-Li Wu, Yang-Fan Lv, Qiang Ma, Jing-Xian Wu, Qing-Liang Wang, Qing-Ming Jiang, Song Duan, Xiao-Qing Chen, Mian-Fu Cao, Xin-Dong Liu, Xiu-Wu Bian, Xiao-Chu Yan, Guang-Jie Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFollicular dendritic cell sarcoma (FDCS) is a rare malignancy with variable biological behavior. This multicenter retrospective study collected 45 cases of FDCS and 17 cases of EBV-positive inflammatory FDCS (EBV+IFDCS) and comprehensively analyzed their clinicopathological features and prognosis. The results showed intra-abdominal FDCS had a significantly higher recurrence/metastasis rate (81.3%) than extra-abdominal FDCS (26.3%, p = 0.002) and EBV+IFDCS (13.3%, p < 0.001), progression-free survival of which also showed significant differences from the extra-abdominal FDCS (p = 0.041) and EBV+IFDCS (p = 0.001), suggesting the intra-abdominal location could be an adverse prognostic factor of FDCS. Notably, this study demonstrated that 50% of intra-abdominal FDCS were associated with hyaline-vascular Castleman disease (HVCD), which was higher than that of extra-abdominal FDCS (p = 0.011) and EBV+IFDCS (p = 0.001). A morphological progression spectrum was observed from HVCD to dysplasia of FDC, and finally sarcomatous transformation, implying that HVCD might represent a precursor lesion of intra-abdominal FDCS.Subject areas: imaging anatomy, medical biochemistry, oncology

Introduction

Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (FDCS) is a rare malignant neoplasm defined by the morphological and immunophenotypic features of follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) and most commonly occurs in cervical and abdominal lymph nodes.1,2,3,4 Among extranodal sites, the abdomen (especially the gastrointestinal tract and retroperitoneum) and the upper aerodigestive tract are the most frequently involved.5,6 The typical histopathological features of FDCS are characterized by spindle or ovoid cells arranged in storiform, fascicular, or whorled patterns, with scattered mature small lymphocytes interspersed between them. Tumor cells express at least two FDC-specific immunohistochemical markers, such as CD21, CD23, CD35, CXCL13, podoplanin (D2-40), SSTR2, and fascin, but usually show negative staining for S-100 protein and lymphocyte-specific markers.7,8

FDCS is a low-to intermediate-grade malignancy, with a local recurrence rate of 28%–40% and a distant metastasis rate of >25%.6,9 Early studies identified several potential poor prognostic factors for FDCS, including intra-abdominal involvement, large tumor size (≥6 cm), presence of coagulative necrosis, high mitotic count (≥5 per 10 high-power fields [HPF]), and significant cellular atypia.10,11 However, despite the growing number of reported FDCS cases and deeper insights into its biological behavior, controversies, and discrepancies remain regarding the prognostic value of these factors. The 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues only provides a simple stratification for predicting recurrence risk based on tumor size and histology, without mentioning the impact of tumor site. At present, further observation and investigation are needed to clarify the poor prognostic factors for FDCS.

Sporadic reports in the literature have indicated that a small subset of FDCS cases is accompanied by hyaline-vascular Castleman disease (HVCD) in the surrounding tissues.12,13 In some of these cases, hyperplasia and dysplasia of FDCs can be observed in some follicular germinal centers of HVCD.14 Additionally, Chan et al. reported a case where a patient with HVCD developed FDCS at the same site several years later.15 Our previous study also revealed that 18.6% (8/43 cases) of mediastinal FDCS were associated with HVCD,16 suggesting a potential link between FDCS and HVCD. However, whether there are differences in the incidence of HVCD co-occurrence among FDCS cases at different anatomical sites remains unclear.

In this study, we retrospectively collected 45 cases of FDCS (including 18 intra-abdominal and 27 extra-abdominal) and 17 cases of intra-abdominal EBV-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (EBV+IFDCS) diagnosed at 7 medical centers in Chongqing, China, and comparatively analyzed their clinicopathological features and prognosis, focusing on the risk of recurrence/metastasis of intra-abdominal FDCS and its association with HVCD.

Results

Clinical features

Clinical data of the 18 cases of intra-abdominal FDCS are summarized in Table 1. Patients ranged in age from 20 to 70 years (mean, 50.3 years; median, 51.5 years), with 11 males and 7 females. Most patients presented with abdominal discomfort. The most common tumor site was the retroperitoneum (44.4%), followed by the mesentery and hepatogastric space. The follow-up duration ranged from 2 to 138 months (mean, 49.9 months; median, 47.0 months). Two patients were lost to follow-up. Of the 16 patients, 9 cases (56.3%) recurred, 10 cases (62.5%) metastasized, and the recurrence/metastasis rate was 81.3% (13/16), with the shortest time to recurrence/metastasis being 2 months. Additionally, 8 cases (50.0%) died, with the shortest overall survival time (from enrollment to death) being 10 months.

Imaging findings

FDCS typically presented as a solid soft-tissue mass with heterogeneous enhancement (Figure 1A). A subset of FDCS associated with HVCD cases showed rich vascularity within the mass on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), correlating with histologic evidence of prominent microvessel proliferation (Figure 1B).

Imaging findings of FDCS and EBV+IFDCS

(A) Most FDCS present as solid soft-tissue masses, with heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans.

(B) FDCS associated with HVCD shows rich vascularity within the mass.

(C) Hepatic lesions of EBV+IFDCS often exhibit heterogeneous enhancement with a “fast wash-in/fast wash-out” pattern on contrast-enhanced CT.

(D) Splenic lesions of EBV+IFDCS mostly appear as low-density masses on CT.

EBV+IFDCS in the liver and spleen typically manifested as hypodense masses on CT (Figures 1C and 1D). A few cases showed rich vascularity. Hepatic lesions often exhibited heterogeneous enhancement with a “fast wash-in/fast wash-out” pattern on contrast-enhanced CT, mimicking hepatocellular carcinoma and posing a diagnostic challenge. Splenic lesions lacked distinctive imaging characteristics.

Macroscopic appearance

FDCS neoplasms were mostly single, round, ovoid, or lobulated masses, with some having a complete fibrous pseudocapsule. The cut surface was solid and grayish-white (Figure 2A), with occasional hemorrhage and necrosis. The maximum diameter of intra-abdominal FDCS tumors ranged from 3.5 to 20 cm (mean, 8.9 cm), while that of extra-abdominal FDCS tumors ranged from 1.8 to 13 cm (mean, 4.9 cm). EBV+IFDCS neoplasms, which are located in hepatic and splenic tissues, were also mostly single lesions with clear boundaries from surrounding tissues in most cases (Figure 2B). The cut surface was soft to moderately firm, solid, and grayish-white, with occasional necrosis and scar-like changes (Figure 2C). The maximum tumor diameter ranged from 1.0 to 11.0 cm (mean, 6.3 cm).

Macroscopic appearance of FDCS and EBV+IFDCS

(A) FDCS is mostly a single, round, ovoid, or lobulated mass. The cut surface is solid and grayish-white.

(B) EBV+IFDCS located in the liver is also mostly a single lesion with a clear boundary from surrounding tissue.

(C) The cut surface of EBV+IFDCS in the spleen is soft to moderately firm, solid, and grayish-white, with occasional necrosis and scar-like change.

Histological features

Typical morphological features were observed to varying degrees in FDCS. The tumor cells were ovoid or short spindle-shaped, arranged in fascicular, whorled, or storiform patterns. The cytoplasm was lightly stained, and the nuclei were vesicular with distinct nuclear membranes and fine chromatin; 1 to 2 small nucleoli were visible, and mitotic figures were noted. Variable numbers of lymphocytes and plasma cells were scattered among the tumor cells (Figures 3A and 3B). Additionally, lymphocytic cuffing around blood vessels was noted (Figure 3C), and scattered multinucleated giant cells were commonly present (Figure 3D).

Most EBV+IFDCS tumors had relatively well-circumscribed boundaries (Figure 4A). Tumor cells, mostly scattered in a mixed inflammatory background, are spindle or ovoid, with indistinct cell borders, mild nuclear atypia, and rare mitotic figures (Figures 4B and 4C). The background contained numerous small lymphocytes, variable numbers of plasma cells and histiocytes, and occasional eosinophils, neutrophils, or granulomas.

A key finding of this study was the presence of HVCD lesions adjacent to the tumor in 9/18 (50%) cases of intra-abdominal FDCS. These HVCD lesions were characterized by atrophic follicular germinal centers, relatively increased FDCs, and concentric proliferation of small lymphocytes in the mantle zone (resembling “onion skin”). The interfollicular region showed increased high endothelial venules, and some proliferating small blood vessels extended at right angles into the follicular germinal centers, forming a so-called “lollipop” structure. Notably, in case 10, a morphological spectrum was observed in the HVCD background, ranging from atrophic germinal centers with FDC hyperplasia to follicle-like structures with FDC dysplasia, and finally to overt FDCS. Both the latter two components showed strongly positive for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) by immunohistochemistry (Figure 5). In contrast, only 3/27 (11.1%) of cases of extra-abdominal FDCS were associated with HVCD, and no HVCD was detected in EBV+IFDCS. These differences were statistically significant between intra-abdominal FDCS and the other two groups (p = 0.011 and p = 0.001, respectively) (Table 4).

Immunophenotype

Tumor cells of FDCS expressed at least two FDC markers (Figures 3E and 3F). EBV+IFDCS showed similar expression patterns but with generally lower positive rates. The positive rates of these antibodies, for FDCS and EBV+IFDCS, are summarized in Table S2, respectively.

To further characterize the IHC staining pattern of EGFR in FDCS and in nonneoplastic FDCs, tonsil tissues, reactive lymphoid hyperplasia specimens and HVCD lesions were selected as control tissues. EGFR immunoreactivity was semi-quantitatively assessed. The results demonstrated negative to weakly positive EGFR staining in tonsils and reactive lymph nodes, while the FDC hyperplasia of HVCD exhibited weakly-to-moderately positive staining. In contrast, FDC dysplasia within follicular-like structures and FDCS were strongly positive for EGFR (Figure S1).

Results of EBV detection

EBER ISH was negative in all 45 cases of FDCS but positive in all 17 cases of EBV+IFDCS (Figure 4D). For 6 cases of EBV+IFDCS, dual staining with EBER ISH and CD21/CD35 immunohistochemistry revealed blue nuclear staining (indicating EBER-positive) and red cytoplasmic staining (indicating CD21 or CD35-positive) in the same spindle or ovoid tumor cells, confirming co-expression of these markers (Figure 4E). Further EBER+CD3 staining revealed EBER positivity in spindle tumor cells, while CD3 positivity was observed in surrounding T cells (Figure 4F).

Prognostic and survival analysis

Two different grouping clustering strategies were employed to compare the differences between the two tumor types (FDCS and EBV+IFDCS) and across different anatomical locations (intra-abdominal and extra-abdominal), highlighting the reclassification of EBV+IFDCS from a subtype of FDCS (WHO 4th edition) to an independent entity (WHO 5th edition).

First, following the WHO 4th edition, EBV+IFDCS was classified as a subtype of FDCS and merged into the intra-abdominal FDCS (total) group. We compared the prognosis of 31 cases of intra-abdominal FDCS (including 15 cases of EBV+IFDCS) with 19 cases of extra-abdominal FDCS. The results showed that the recurrence/metastasis rate and mortality rate of the intra-abdominal FDCS (total) group were 48.4% and 29.0%, respectively, which did not differ significantly from the extra-abdominal FDCS group (both 26.3%) (χ2 = 2.391 and 0.043, p = 0.148 and 1.000). No significant differences were observed in progression-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS) between the two groups (χ2 = 0.316 and 0.281, p = 0.574 and 0.596, respectively). Detailed data are provided in Table S3 and Figures S2 and S3.

Second, in accordance with the WHO 5th edition, we compared firstly 35 cases of FDCS with 15 cases of EBV+IFDCS. Significant differences were identified in recurrence/metastasis rates between the two groups (51.4% vs. 13.3%; χ2 = 6.349, p = 0.025). The mortality rate of FDCS (37.1%) was higher than that of EBV+IFDCS (6.7%), though this difference didn’t reach statistical significance (χ2 = 3.444, p = 0.063). A significant difference was observed in PFS between the two groups (χ2 = 7.149, p = 0.008), but no significant difference was found in OS (χ2 = 2.858, p = 0.091). These findings (detailed in Table S4; Figures S4 and S5) confirm that the biological behavior of EBV+IFDCS is distinctly different from that of FDCS, emphasizing the necessity of clearly distinguishing between the two entities in prognostic analyses.

Subsequently, the 35 cases of FDCS were further stratified into intra-abdominal (16 cases) and extra-abdominal (19 cases) subgroups, and each subgroup was compared with the EBV+IFDCS group. The results demonstrated that the intra-abdominal FDCS subgroup had a significantly higher recurrence/metastasis rate than both the extra-abdominal FDCS subgroup and the EBV+IFDCS group (p = 0.002 and <0.001, respectively). Additionally, the mortality rate of intra-abdominal FDCS was significantly higher than that of intra-abdominal EBV+IFDCS (p = 0.015), but no significant difference was noted when compared with extra-abdominal FDCS (Table 5).

Discussion

This study focused on the clinicopathological characteristics of intra-abdominal FDCS and its association with HVCD, using extra-abdominal FDCS and intra-abdominal EBV+IFDCS as control groups. We compared recurrence/metastasis and mortality rates across the three groups and followed disease progression. Based on multicenter real-world data, we clearly demonstrated that intra-abdominal FDCS has a significantly higher recurrence/metastasis rate than extra-abdominal FDCS and EBV+IFDCS. The median OS of intra-abdominal FDCS (70 months) was notably shorter than that of extra-abdominal FDCS (150 months); median OS was not calculated for EBV+IFDCS due to its low mortality rate during the follow-up period. These findings confirm that intra-abdominal FDCS has a poor prognosis, in contrast to the favorable prognosis of EBV+IFDCS, which is also located in the intra-abdominal region. Secondly, to our knowledge, this is the first study to report that 50% of cases of intra-abdominal FDCS are associated with HVCD lesions, which is significantly higher than that observed in extra-abdominal FDCS (11.1%) and EBV+IFDCS (0%). In some cases, a morphological progression spectrum was observed: from HVCD to FDC hyperplasia, FDC dysplasia, and finally sarcomatous transformation to FDCS. This further confirms a close association between intra-abdominal FDCS and HVCD, suggesting that HVCD may be a precursor lesion of intra-abdominal FDCS.

Due to the rarity of FDCS, most previous literature consists of case reports, case series, or literature-based reviews, which may result in ongoing controversies and inconsistencies regarding the poor prognostic factors of FDCS. Early studies generally identified intra-abdominal tumor location as a poor prognostic factor for FDCS, along with other factors such as maximum tumor diameter ≥6 cm, presence of coagulative necrosis, high mitotic count (≥5 per 10 HPF), and significant cellular atypia.10,11 However, Shia et al.17 failed to confirm the association of these factors with adverse prognosis in a statistical analysis of 42 cases of FDCS with complete follow-up data. In a review of dendritic cell sarcomas, Saygin et al.5 performed univariate analyses to assess the relationship between adverse outcomes and factors such as intra-abdominal involvement, age, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, tumor size, necrosis, and mitotic count in 343 previously reported cases of FDCS, which indicated that younger age at diagnosis (≤40 years), absence of lymphoplasmacytic reaction, large tumor size (>6 cm), and high mitotic count (≥5 mitoses/10 HPF) were predictive of poor prognosis. Furthermore, multivariate regression analysis of 50 cases of FDCS with complete clinicopathological data also failed to identify necrosis or intra-abdominal involvement as predictors of adverse outcomes. In contrast, a recent review of 809 cases of FDCS re-emphasized that intra-abdominal involvement may be associated with poor prognosis in younger patients.6

A deeper discussion of the aforementioned discrepancies reveals that, beyond inherent differences among reported cases, inconsistencies may stem from the failure to distinguish intra-abdominal EBV+IFDCS (especially located in the liver and spleen) from classic FDCS in previous literature reviews. EBV+IFDCS and classic FDCS share the same ICD-O code (9758/3), and prior to the 5th edition of the WHO classification, EBV+IFDCS was universally recognized as a subtype/variant of FDCS. Consequently, it was challenging to fully identify and exclude EBV+IFDCS when retrieving cases using the ICD-O code (9758/3) or the generic term “FDCS”. Conventionally, EBV+IFDCS, formerly known as inflammatory pseudotumor-like follicular dendritic cell tumor/sarcoma, predominantly arises in the liver and spleen, exhibits indolent biological behavior with rare recurrence or metastasis, and has unique clinicopathological features. It was reclassified as an independent entity in the 5th edition of the WHO classification. Consistent with this reclassification, our study showed significant differences in recurrence/metastasis rates and PFS between EBV+IFDCS and FDCS, providing further evidence for the independent classification of EBV+IFDCS. Thus, merging EBV+IFDCS (a favorable-prognosis entity) into the intra-abdominal FDCS group not only distorts statistical results but also is inappropriate from a tumor classification perspective.

To verify this conjecture, we first merged 16 cases of intra-abdominal FDCS and 15 cases of hepatic/splenic EBV+IFDCS into a single “intra-abdominal FDCS (total) group” and compared it with 19 cases of extra-abdominal FDCS. Consistent with our conjecture, no significant differences were found in recurrence/metastasis rates, mortality rates, PFS, or OS between the merged intra-abdominal FDCS (total) group and the extra-abdominal group. However, when intra-abdominal FDCS, extra-abdominal FDCS and EBV+IFDCS groups were compared, intra-abdominal FDCS showed the worst prognosis, with significant differences in recurrence/metastasis rates and PFS than the other two groups. In contrast, EBV+IFDCS exhibited the most favorable outcomes, underscoring the critical importance of clearly distinguishing between FDCS and EBV+IFDCS in clinical diagnosis and prognostic evaluation.

Another key finding of this study is that 50% of cases of intra-abdominal FDCS were associated with HVCD, which is substantially higher than the 10%–20% association rate reported in the 5th edition of the WHO classification for FDCS overall, indicating an exceptionally close relationship between intra-abdominal FDCS and HVCD. Castleman disease (CD), also known as giant lymph node hyperplasia or angiofollicular lymph node hyperplasia, is a group of benign lymphoproliferative disorders of unknown etiology. It is divided into two subtypes: HVCD (accounting for 80%–90% of CD cases) and mixed/plasmacytic CD. CD typically presents as a painless, slowly progressive enlargement of single or multiple groups of lymph nodes; patients generally have a good general condition and slow disease progression.18 However, a few literature reports have documented malignant transformation of CD, including progression to diffuse large B cell lymphoma and FDCS.15,19 Chan et al.15 reported a case of nasopharyngeal HVCD with FDC hyperplasia and subsequent transformation to FDCS. Li et al.20 described a case of cervical HVCD that recurred as FDCS 8 years after surgery, illustrating the malignant transformation process from HVCD to FDCS. Using cDNA microarray analysis, Sun et al.21 detected epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expression in FDCS; subsequent immunohistochemical studies confirmed EGFR expression in FDCs of CD, suggesting a degree of homogeneity between the two entities.

To further explore the link between CD and FDCS, we retrieved 75 CD cases from our pathological archive (60 HVCD cases and 15 mixed/plasmacytic CD cases; detailed data not shown) and carefully evaluated FDC proliferation status. Hyperplasia and dysplasia of FDCs were observed in the atrophic germinal centers of some CD cases; in 3/60 HVCD cases, focal malignant transformation of FDCs to FDCS was identified. These findings confirm the existence of a continuous progression spectrum in HVCD lesions, ranging from FDC hyperplasia to FDC dysplasia and finally to FDCS. This also serves as a diagnostic reminder: when diagnosing HVCD-especially for intra-abdominal nodules (particularly confluent nodules)-pathologists should carefully examine specimens and perform extensive sampling to detect potential areas of transformation to FDCS, thereby avoiding missed diagnosis of malignant components. For example, in Case 10 of our intra-abdominal FDCS group, initial routine sampling only revealed reactive lymphoid follicles and HVCD-like follicles with minimal adjacent FDC hyperplasia. However, additional extensive sampling uncovered focal areas of FDC hyperplasia, FDC dysplasia, and sarcomatous transformation, leading to the final diagnosis of FDCS (malignant transformation of HVCD). The transformation from HVCD, FDC dysplasia to FDCS may represent a biological evolution from a reactive/clonal proliferation to a distinct mesenchymal neoplasm. Beyond its pathological implications, this distinction is crucial as it indicates a shift in molecular drivers (such as EGFR signaling pathways) and necessitates a change in therapeutic strategies.22 Furthermore, understanding this transformation may pave the way for the development of targeted therapies and allow clinicians to move beyond empirical chemotherapy regimens and consider molecularly guided treatment strategies.

The treatment of intra-abdominal FDCS typically follows the paradigm for soft tissue tumors: complete surgical resection of the mass is the first-line approach (with no routine lymph node dissection), and adjuvant therapy (radiotherapy/chemotherapy) or multimodal therapy is administered in some cases. There is no clear evidence confirming whether the latter two modalities can improve overall survival. Specifically, intra-abdominal FDCS is strongly associated with HVCD, which often presents as lymphadenopathy involving enlargement of single or multiple lymph node groups (including nodes with macroscopically inapparent enlargement). Thus, it can be hypothesized that the high recurrence rate of intra-abdominal FDCS may be partially attributed to incomplete resection of suspicious HVCD lesions (e.g., macroscopically unremarkable lymph nodes) surrounding the primary tumor. Accordingly, it is worthwhile to consider whether, in cases of FDCS complicated by HVCD, more proactive adjuvant therapy should be appropriately administered, such as chemoradiotherapy and anti-interleukin-6 (anti-IL-6) therapy, to mitigate the progressive effects of concurrent HVCD in the surrounding tissues and thus improve the clinical outcomes of patients.

Limitations of the study

This study, however, has inherent limitations that must be acknowledged. First, compared with previous large-sample literature reviews, the sample size of this study is relatively small. Second, due to the retrospective design, there is information bias in the enrolled cases, which may affect the results. Taking selection bias as an example, the present study only enrolled cases from several hospitals in Chongqing, all of which are relatively well-established medical institutions. The exclusion of data from district- and county-level hospitals and from other geographical regions could introduce potential biases and thus affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, lost-to-follow-up represents a critical limitation of this study. This issue not only compromised the reliability of our findings but also reduced the effective sample size, thereby influencing the selection of appropriate statistical methods. Future studies with larger multicenter cohorts are needed to explore the rationality and accuracy of this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participants

This study included 62 samples diagnosed at 7 medical centers in Chongqing, China, between 2007 and 2024. There were 31 males and 31 females, with a balanced gender ratio, and the age range was 20-77 years old. According to the pathological diagnosis, they were divided into FDCS (45 cases) and EBV+IFDCS (17 cases). FDCS was further divided into intra-abdominal (18 cases) and extra-abdominal (27 cases) groups according to the location of the disease. All cases were re-evaluated and diagnosed by at least two subspecialized pathologists. Preoperative imaging findings were reviewed by in-hospital radiology specialists. Clinical information, treatment modalities, and prognostic data were obtained by reviewing electronic medical records, tracking regular follow-up results, and conducting telephone follow-ups. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University (approval number: KY2024207). Patient data were de-identified to protect privacy.

Method details

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The immunohistochemical stain of tissue sections was performed on the automatic immunohistochemical staining device (BenchMarkXT, Roche). The antibodies used are listed in Table S1. Positive staining was defined as the presence of uniform brown granular coloring in the nucleus, cytoplasm, or cell membrane of tumor cells, depending on the intracellular localization of different target antibodies. Known positive sections were used as positive controls, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer solution was used as a negative control. For the negative control, primary antibodies were omitted and replaced with an equivalent volume of PBS. No specific staining was observed in the PBS control group, confirming that the immunoreactivity detected in the experimental groups was specific to the target antigen.

For the IHC staining patterns of EGFR, a 4-tier semi-quantitative scoring system was employed to evaluate the immunoreactivity of tumor cells in a standardized and reproducible manner. The results demonstrated negative (-; no visible specific staining signal in tumor cells), weakly positive (+; faint and focal staining signals observed in partial tumor cells), moderately positive (++; clear and distinguishable staining signals present in a considerable proportion of tumor cells) and strongly positive (+++; intense and diffuse staining signals displayed in most tumor cells).

EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization (ISH)

ISH for EBER was conducted on 62 cases with a commercial detection kit (Roche Diagnostics). Fluorescein-labeled probes were hybridized to specific target RNA sequences in the tissue. After sequential incubation with primary antibodies, secondary antibodies, and enzyme conjugates, the fluorescein-labeled probes were visualized with 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate (BCIP) and nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) chromogens. The blue signal on the nuclei of the tumor cells was defined as a positive reaction. The tissue of nasopharyngeal carcinoma was used for positive control, and the reactive hyperplasia of the lymph node was used for negative control.

Dual staining with EBER ISH and immunohistochemistry(IHC)

In diagnostically challenging EBV+IFDCS cases, dual staining combining EBER ISH with CD21/CD35 or CD3 immunohistochemistry was applied to confirm whether EBER and CD21/CD35 were co-expressed in the same cells. The dual staining protocol for EBER ISH and IHC was performed sequentially on formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the Roche Ventana BenchMark automated platform. The procedure began with deparaffinization and pretreatment of FFPE tissues, followed by proteinase K digestion. Subsequently, EBER ISH was conducted according to the specific parameters outlined in the context, yielding nuclear blue staining signals. Subsequently, antigen retrieval was carried out using CC1 Cell Conditioning Buffer. The IHC staining was then initiated by sequential incubation with primary antibody, Red Multimer, Red Enhancer, and development with Fast red chromogen, yielding cytoplasmic red staining signals. Finally, hematoxylin counterstaining was applied to the nuclei, and slides were mounted with coverslips. Positive and negative controls were included throughout the entire workflow to ensure the specificity of the results. Double positivity was defined as blue nuclear staining (EBER-positive) and red cytoplasmic staining (CD21- or CD35-positive) in tumor cells.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in SPSS 26.0. The log-rank test and Kaplan-Meier analysis were used to compare progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) between groups. Categorical variables (recurrence/metastasis, mortality, HVCD association) were assessed by contingency table analysis. A two-tailed P-value <0.05 indicated significance, with Bonferroni correction ( P<0.016) applied for multiple comparisons. When performing contingency table analysis, if any cell has an expected count < 5, the continuity correction is applied; if the total sample size is < 40, Fisher’s exact test is recommended.
