Programmatic implications of the efficiency of uptake of Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae by Simulium sp. vectors
Authors: Daniel A Boakye, Mike Y Osei-Atweneboana, Simon K Atta, Irene A Larbi, Charles D Mackenzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current global aim for onchocerciasis is to eliminate transmission of infection by administering ivermectin once a year or biannually. However, when ivermectin treatment is given annually for 9–10 y, some individuals still carry low microfilariae (mf) levels, which may allow continued transmission.MethodsAt four different times (0, 8, 90, 365 days) after treatment with ivermectin, 1608 newly emerged Simulium sanctipauli (Pra form) flies were fed on 35 volunteers, with initial dermal microfilarial loads ranging from 0 to >30 Onchocerca volvulus mf/mg skin. Ten days after feeding, the number and load of mf in these flies were assessed by dissection.ResultsNotably, even at the lowest skin-snip mf densities—including those showing zero infection—flies can still ingest O. volvulus mf and become infected; 29% of the 1608 fed flies picked up mf. The lowest skin mf levels were recorded on Day 90 after treatment, at which time vector mf uptake increased by 21% with every unit increase in skin mf density (P < .000).ConclusionsVery low dermal mf levels can still support parasite transmission. Consequently, widespread low O. volvulus infection in human populations with highly productive vector-breeding sites could significantly affect efforts to eliminate transmission.Keywords: dermal microfilariae, elimination, fly feeding, onchocerciasis, skin snipping

Introduction

Before 2012, efforts to control onchocerciasis were considered successful, as the infection in many countries no longer posed a public health problem. This status was achieved in the 11 countries covered by the former Onchocerciasis Control Programme (OCP) in West Africa, almost entirely through vector control. It had always been expected that, at the end of the OCP, the blackfly vectors of the Simulium damnosum s.l. species complex would naturally repopulate the breeding sites previously under insecticidal treatment. However, it was also envisaged that the Onchocerca volvulus microfilarial densities in the skins of human hosts would have diminished to levels where transmission would have been interrupted (i.e. beyond the point of any possible recrudescence of the disease).1,2

The successes of the OCP vector control were enhanced by the discovery that the microfilaricidal agent, ivermectin, could be used for mass treatment,3–5 both in areas under vector control and outside the OCP. A single dose of ivermectin does not interrupt transmission. Nevertheless, when used annually for >10 y, especially when administered twice a year, microfilariae (mf) densities can fall to levels at which transmission is interrupted.6 Ivermectin was therefore used in these former OCP areas and elsewhere, not only for morbidity control and the prevention of infection recrudescence, but also, more recently, for complete interruption of transmission. What is still not fully known, aside from the fact that transmission can indeed be interrupted with ivermectin mass drug administration,7–9 is precisely at what minimum level of host microfilaridermia can transmission be sustained. Not knowing this information can erroneously lead to the premature cessation of control activities, with the resultant recrudescence of the disease.

In the OCP area, low-level microfilaridermia persisted even in areas where ivermectin had been distributed for >6 y.10,11 Similar information from Cameroon has also shown that, after >20 y of annual mass treatment with ivermectin, low-level transmission can still occur in areas of high vector abundance.12,13 These observations indicate a threat to the onchocerciasis-controlled regions in West Africa, especially if members of the S. damnosum s.l. complex can pick up parasites at very low microfilaremia levels, which could lead to continuing transmission.

Studies on vector competence with the S. damnosum complex originally relied on xenodiagnosis using wild flies,14 which, although yielding valuable information, consistently raised ethical concerns about the possibility of infecting or reinfecting study participants if the wild-caught flies were already carrying infections. Furthermore, the wild flies used were usually of different physiological ages. Using newly emerged females eliminates this ethical dilemma but has been hindered by the difficulty of raising flies in captivity. We report here a study to determine the competence of adult female S. damnosum s.l. to become infected at low levels of microfilaridermia by feeding newly emerged flies on patients showing 0 to >30 mf/mg of skin as determined by standard skin snips.

Materials and methods

Study background

This study took place in Ghana during 2001–2. Ethical approval was provided by the Noguchi Institute/University of Ghana Ethics Committee.

Blackfly populations

Simulium damnosum s.l. were collected from breeding sites at Obosomase (Lat.: 5.875, Long.: -0.156) and Krabo (Lat.: 5.883, Long.:- 0.433) on the River Pra in Ghana. Supports (vegetation) with larvae and pupae attached were collected into plastic bags, placed in iceboxes containing ice packs (8–15°C) and transported to the laboratory. In the laboratory, the supports were placed in trays containing tap water to allow the larvae to detach and attach to the tray walls. Thirty to 40 larvae were collected into freshly made Carnoy’s solution for cytotaxonomic identification.14,15 The pupae remained on the supports and were placed in cardboard boxes designed to serve as emergence cages (Fig. 1). Adult males emerging from the cages were placed in a holding chamber with the females, then isolated and maintained on a 5% sucrose solution for at least 6 hours before the blood-feeding experiments.

Fly maintenance. The pupae remained on the supports and were placed into cardboard boxes designed to serve as emergence cages.

Infected individuals

At the baseline point, a single standard skin snip was taken from each iliac crest and calf with a Walser-type corneoscleral punch, and then again at 8, 90 and 365 days. All the study participants were treated with the standard ivermectin dosage (150 μg/kg) at baseline. Many had previously received ivermectin as part of the mass drug administration activities in their home locations.

Fly feeding experiments

Thirty-five individuals with variable microfilarial skin counts were recruited to participate in the study after their informed consent was obtained.15 Patients with <10 mf/mg skin were mostly from the OCP area, whereas all those harbouring >10 mf/mg skin came from outside the OCP. Each patient had four feeding trials within 1 y, and each trial was treated as the results for a single patient. Before each feeding trial, the patients were skin-snipped to determine their level of microfilaridermia.11

Mosquito-netting sleeves were placed on both legs of each patient and tied loosely at both ends using drawstrings (Fig. 2) to avoid discomfort, while still preventing flies from escaping. Fifteen female flies from the holding tubes were blown gently into each sleeve at the top with a mouth aspirator. The flies were left in the sleeve for 30 min to feed on blood. At the end of the feeding period, the flies were removed to new holding tubes and labelled appropriately. Fully blood-fed flies were distinguished from unfed flies by their distended abdomen, and these were placed each into a new individual holding tube with a similar label. The fly feeding events were staggered at 10-min intervals for groups of two patients at a time.

Fly feeding. Mosquito-netting sleeves were placed on both legs of each patient and tied loosely at both ends with drawstrings.

Fly maintenance and dissection

One-half of the fed flies from each patient were killed and dissected immediately to determine their uptake of mf, while the remaining one-half were maintained at between 15 and 20°C on a 10% sucrose solution for 10 days. Each day, any fly found dead among those being maintained was dissected, and the development stage of any O. volvulus present was recorded. Surviving flies were killed and dissected on the 10th day.

Cytotaxonomy

A representative sample of larvae (∼40) was processed through standard cytotaxonomic procedures15,16 to determine their cytotaxonomic profile and, thus, their species identification.

Results

The cytotaxonomic identification of the larval samples showed the flies to be Simulium sanctipauli Pra form. Pupae survival was achieved with >10 000 adult female flies. Initially, the fly feeding activities were poor, with <5% of flies found to have fed: this was likely due to starving the flies for >6 hours after emergence before introducing them to humans. In subsequent experiments, the flies were kept on sucrose solution immediately after emergence and then starved for 5–6 hours before feeding on infected humans. This resulted in much higher feeding rates in each batch (30–90%). A total of 1608 of 4050 flies used in 135 studies with the infected individuals were observed to have fed, with 11% (177/1608) of the fed flies picking up mf.

The proportion of flies picking up mf was compared with the mf density level in the patients' skin and is given as mean mf/mg skin (Table 1). These results indicated that the higher the level of mf load in the skin, the greater the proportion of fed flies that pick up infection. Notably, when the mean density was as low as 0.01 mf/mg skin, ∼1% of flies still ingested parasites, and one fly picked up parasites even when the skin-snip value was negative. Although more tests were performed at the 0.01–10 mg/kg skin level, there was no significant difference in the mean number of flies feeding per patient test across the different categories (t-test, P = .17).

Data summary

Day 0: at baseline (pretreatment), vector mf uptake increased by 1% with every unit increase in skin mf density (P < .049). This is when the highest skin microfilarial densities were recorded.

Day 90: at Day 90 post-treatment, vector mf uptake increased by 21% with every unit increase in skin mf density (P < .000). The lowest skin mf levels were recorded at Day 90 (Figs 5 and 6). Hence, the highest vector mf uptake occurred when the skin mf levels were lowest.

Discussion

The difficulty in maintaining self-perpetuating laboratory colonies of the S. damnosum complex has hampered many laboratory studies on their transmission efficiency. Nevertheless, studies by Raybould and Boakye17 showed that some limited success can indeed be obtained feeding these blackflies in the laboratory. However, using this approach, most studies require large numbers of flies from the field; this has not always been possible due to high adult mortality upon emergence. Pupal and adult survival was high in the present study because the pupae were maintained at a cool temperature (19 degrees C), and the holding chamber on the emergence boxes provided enough room and aeration for the adults at emergence, with less overcrowding and hence better adult survival. Another difference was that the adult flies were maintained in the holding tubes, as previously described.18

One of the main programmatic questions in onchocerciasis, one that is particularly relevant to the OCP areas and anywhere in the global endemic areas that are reaching low levels of infection following control and elimination interventions, is determining whether the cessation of control activities will allow recrudescence, or conversely, what low microfilaridermia levels will still allow transmission by members of the S. damnosum complex. Studies examining the relationship between vector efficiency and microfilaria load in S. damnosum–O. volvulus showed that successful parasite uptake by vectors increases with increasing microfilaria load in the skin.13 The present study observed that when the microfilarial density in the human host is within 0.01–10 mf/mg skin, the proportion of flies picking up infections averages ∼9%. Although at the lower end of this range, ∼1% of feeding flies were still able to become infected, and importantly, this implies the possibility of there being a considerable number of infected flies present, given that in some areas daily biting rates can be as high as 1000 bites/person/day.19 The vector competence found in this study agrees with comparable field experiments with Simulium sirbanum in the OCP area (Y. Bissan, personal communication).

These studies indicate that members of the S. damnosum complex can pick up infection at low levels; the importance of this behaviour for transmission may depend on the number of individuals in the community harbouring infections, even at low levels. These data will help to improve the various onchocerciasis models and predict the levels of community microfilarial load at which any treatment (ivermectin or vector control) could be safely stopped without a recrudescence of onchocerciasis in controlled areas.

Currently, ivermectin remains the primary drug available for safe mass treatment of onchocerciasis. However, new agents of the same pharmacological class with longer-lasting action (i.e. moxidectin) are being developed for challenging situations. The present study showed that during the first months after treatment (8 to 90 days post-treatment), mf loads in the skin are very low, consistent with ivermectin’s effectiveness as a microfilaricide. However, at 365 days post-treatment, although skin mf loads may be low, this might not significantly differ from the preceding pretreatment level. These present findings support the concept that ivermectin treatment in affected communities may have to continue for as long as necessary, possibly with additional intensive monitoring of transmission, to prevent any recrudescence of the disease. This study involved a single species of the S. damnosum complex, and the existence of this phenomenon in other vector species requires confirmation.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes that vector flies are efficient at picking up dermal microfilaria and can do so even when the standard skin snip is zero. Skin snips are, therefore, not useful for stopping treatment decisions. This issue is emphasized when considering a large population, where the probability of remaining infections increases, thereby jeopardizing the decision to stop treatment in the treated population.
