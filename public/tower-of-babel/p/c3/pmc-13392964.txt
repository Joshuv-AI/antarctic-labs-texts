Supportive care needs of women with breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa region: A scoping review
Authors: Alhanouf Alkhyat, Nutmeg Hallett, Walaa H Maghrabi, Annie Topping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with breast cancer can experience a range of informational, psychological, or physical supportive care needs at different stages of their cancer journey. There is limited insight into the breast cancer experience and supportive care needs from the perspective of women living in the Middle East and North Africa region.PurposeTo explore and examine the supportive care literature from the Middle East and North Africa region.MethodsThe scoping review was conducted using the five stages outlined by the Arksey and O’Malley framework and modified by Levac to enhance the reliability of the findings. A best-fit framework synthesis was used to report the findings and was mapped against the 11 supportive care needs domains. Databases, including Embase, Medline, CINAHL, Web of Science, and PsycINFO, were searched originally in March 2020 and updated in July 2024, and no publication date restrictions were applied.ResultsSixty-one studies from 13 countries in the region were included. Findings were mapped across 10 domains—physical, psychological, health system and informational, patient-clinician communication, practical, spiritual, social, family-related, intimacy-related, and cognitive needs—using a modified supportive care needs framework. We highlighted context-specific patterns of supportive care needs in the region, particularly regarding stigma, spirituality, and intimacy-related concerns.ConclusionBreast cancer researchers in this region have focused largely on women’s experiences rather than explicit supportive care needs, with some attention to psychological outcomes, such as anxiety and depression. Existing quantitative tools may not fully capture culturally relevant needs, particularly spiritual and social dimensions. These gaps may limit effective intervention development, highlighting the need for context-specific assessment tools to inform targeted supportive care strategies.Keywords: Supportive care needs, Breast cancer, Cancer, Best fit, Scoping review, Middle East and North Africa region

Introduction

Women diagnosed with breast cancer can encounter numerous challenges, both during treatment and afterwards. The management of breast cancer often involves complex and specialised multimodality treatments (Shao et al., 2019), which are frequently associated with a wide range of physical, psychological, and cognitive side effects that may persist for several years after diagnosis into survivorship (Heins et al., 2022; Tommasi et al., 2022).

Women, irrespective of where they are in their personal breast cancer experience, might have several supportive care needs at different times that require healthcare providers' attention and care (Fan et al., 2023). ‘Supportive care’ is an umbrella term that encompasses both supportive care and palliative care (Hui et al., 2021) and covers a wide range of domains or needs, such as physical, social, psychological, and spiritual. A thorough understanding of supportive care needs is essential for informing nursing practice and research. Identifying these needs enables the development of tailored, patient-centred interventions that address physical, psychological, social, and spiritual concerns (Fitch, 2008; Fan et al., 2023; Khajoei et al., 2023).

Several researchers conducting systematic or scoping reviews (Fan et al., 2023; Khajoei et al., 2023) have examined the supportive care needs of the breast cancer population. However, the studies included in these reviews were largely conducted with Western or East Asian populations. Little is known about the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa countries.

The Middle East and North Africa region is a term commonly used by organisations and political entities, such as the World Health Organisation and the International Monetary Fund (World Atlas, 2024). The constituent countries vary, but most commonly include the following: Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, the occupied Palestinian Territory, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Syrian Arab Republic, Tunisia, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen (World Atlas, 2024). The population of the Middle East and North Africa region is largely homogeneous in terms of religion and language, with Islam representing the dominant religion and Arabic serving as the primary or official language across many countries in the region (Pew Research Center, 2025). Over half of the countries are fuel-producing (Tagliapietra, 2019), making them economically wealthy, whereas others are seen as developing countries with varying levels of economic stability (World Atlas, 2024).

Within this regional context, breast cancer represents a significant public health concern, as it is the most common cancer among women in the Middle East and North Africa region (Fayed et al., 2017; Salem and Daher-Nashif, 2020). Over two million women worldwide were newly diagnosed with breast cancer in 2022, and it is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths (Ferlay et al., 2024). Globally, the age-standardised incidence rate for breast cancer was 46.8 in 2022 (Ferlay et al., 2024). The age-standardised incidence rate of breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa region was 37.5 per 100,000 (Safiri et al., 2022), which is markedly lower than that of Europe and the United States of America (USA) (80.1 and 84.8, respectively). However, the Middle East has a marginally worse mortality rate (15.2) than that of Europe (12.6) and the United States (14.1) (Bray et al., 2018; Naja et al., 2019). Despite the relatively low incidence of breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa region, the disease remains a significant public health concern due to its higher mortality rates. These patterns highlight the importance of understanding the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer in the region to inform the delivery of culturally relevant, patient-centred care and support services. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to identify and map the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa region.

Methods

This review was framed by the supportive care framework described by Paterson et al. (2015). This framework categorises supportive care needs using 11 domains. It was originally designed to assess the supportive care needs of men experiencing prostate cancer. More recently, it has been applied in two systematic reviews of women affected by cancer (Paterson et al., 2021; Khajoei et al., 2023). The review was designed using five stages of the framework outlined by Arksey and O'Malley (2005) and modified by Levac et al. (2010).

Stage 1: Identifying the research question

The following question was developed to guide this review: ‘What are the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa countries during diagnosis, treatment, or follow-up (including advanced disease, end of life and survivorship)?

The following objectives were formulated for the scoping review:

1.To determine what are the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa region.

To determine what are the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa region.

2.To identify whether the 11 domains described by Paterson et al. (2015) provide coverage of the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa region.

To identify whether the 11 domains described by Paterson et al. (2015) provide coverage of the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa region.

3.To identify research gaps, if any, in the existing literature.

Stage 2: Identifying relevant studies

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on the review question. The search included studies that specifically investigated supportive care needs, unmet supportive care needs, or any specific need that might map to the 11 domains framework. Only papers that described the breast cancer experience in the Middle East and North Africa region were included. All studies included women with breast cancer at any point in the cancer pathway and with any stage of cancer. Only empirical design studies that were published in English and Arabic were included. No restrictions were applied regarding the date of publication.

An initial search of the CINAHL and MEDLINE databases was undertaken to identify key terms (Aromataris and Munn, 2017). Search terms were developed using the Population, Concept and Context framework recommended by the JBI (Peters et al., 2015) and can be found in Supplementary Material File 1, and an example of an electronic database search is provided in Supplementary Material File 2.

The second step involved using the identified key terms to search the following databases: Embase, MEDLINE, Web of Science core collection, CINAHL, and PsycINFO (Smith et al., 2011; Bramer et al., 2017). These databases were selected as they provide coverage of literature from such disciplines as nursing, allied health professions, biomedical research, and psychology. After running the searches, all references were exported to EndNote X9 to remove duplicates. Also, a Google Scholar search was performed of the first 200 relevant references (Bramer et al., 2017). All searches began on 25th February 2020 and concluded on 8th March 2020 and were updated on 4th July 2024. Studies that met the original eligibility criteria but did not contribute any new insights were excluded from the update. This approach was taken because the purpose of the review was not to assess the strength of evidence but rather to identify supportive care needs among women living in the Middle East and North Africa region and to explore the applicability of the supportive care needs framework within this population. The update identified two studies that met the inclusion criteria and yielded new findings (Maleki et al., 2021; Al-Azri et al., 2022). The third step involved performing a hand search of the reference lists of the included studies to capture any missing relevant studies, but the search yielded no results.

Stage 3: Study selection

To ensure a replicable and transparent process, the selection of studies was performed by three researchers (AT, NH, and AA) (Levac et al., 2010). Two researchers (AA and AT) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts yielded from the literature search. Studies that were not explicitly relevant to the inclusion criteria were excluded. This process was performed using Rayyan© software (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Then, two reviewers (AA and AT) independently reviewed the full articles for inclusion according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria mentioned above. The third reviewer (NH) was consulted for the final decision if there was any disagreement between the two reviewers at both stages. Fig. 1 shows the study selection process.

Stage 5: Collating, summarising, and reporting the results

First, the characteristics of the included studies were described, including the overall number of studies, study design, year of publication, study populations, location of studies, and mapping and calculating the frequency of the 11 domains appearing across the included studies. Next, the ‘best fit’ framework synthesis method was used to report the results (Carroll et al., 2013). Best fit framework syntheses are used to enhance or build upon an existing model which may differ but remains relevant to the population. This approach was deemed appropriate, as Paterson et al.’s (2015) model warranted further evaluation and adaptation to map the needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa region. Data extracted from the included studies were coded against the prior framework. A synthesis matrix was created to facilitate the integration of qualitative and quantitative data for effective coding. Any extracted evidence that could not be coded against the framework was assigned a new subtheme. New subthemes were incorporated into the a priori framework to ultimately develop a revised framework.

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 61 studies met the inclusion criteria (Table 1). Of the included studies, quantitative methods were used in 67% (n = 41) and qualitative methods in 33% (n = 20). Of the quantitative studies, the majority were cross-sectional studies (n = 26), with fewer being comparative descriptive (n = 5), prospective (n = 5), descriptive correlational (n = 3), and secondary (n = 2). Of the qualitative studies, 10 studies failed to report their methodological approach, and the others were described as phenomenological (n = 5), qualitative exploratory (n = 3), grounded theory (n = 1), and inductive qualitative (n = 1). Most of the studies were undertaken in Iran (n = 25) and Israel (n = 8). The search failed to yield any studies from Algeria, Libya, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, or Yemen.

All studies exclusively recruited women with breast cancer except for one qualitative study (Zamanzadeh et al., 2019) that included participants with different cancer diagnoses, although its data on breast cancer were reported separately and therefore extractable. Fewer than half (n = 25, 42%) of the studies reported participants’ cancer stages or reported this information only in the demographic table. This meant that information on how needs might differ at different stages of cancer was limited. Only four studies linked cancer stage in the analysis (Montazeri et al., 2000; Vahdaninia et al., 2009; Tirgari et al., 2012; Berhili et al., 2017). The included studies recruited women at different points, from diagnosis through initial treatment, palliative care, and beyond, including survivorship. Ten studies (Hasson-Ohayon et al., 2009; Nizamli et al., 2011; Sbitti et al., 2011; Harirchi et al., 2012; Kuzari et al., 2013; Obeidat and Khrais, 2014; Kimiafar et al., 2016; Shakeri et al., 2016; Terzioglu and Hammoudeh, 2017; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018) failed to report the time from participants’ diagnosis to recruitment and data collection. Twelve studies (Gilbar and Gilbar, 2009; El-Hadidy et al., 2012; Joulaee et al., 2012; Kimiafar et al., 2016; Mandelzweig et al., 2016; Shakeri et al., 2016; Terzioglu and Hammoudeh, 2017; Abou Kassm et al., 2018; Almegewly et al., 2018; Ahmadi Gharaei et al., 2019; Nimekari and Naz, 2019; Zamanzadeh et al., 2019) failed to report information about the types of treatment received, but the majority of participants in the other studies appeared to undergo multimodal treatments.

Frequencies of supportive care needs

Researchers focused on the following domains: psychosocial/emotional (35 studies; 57%), interpersonal/intimacy (22 studies; 36%), spiritual (20 studies; 33%), social (19 studies; 31%), physical (15 studies; 25%), health system/information (14 studies; 23%), family-related (13 studies; 21%), patient–clinician communication (12 studies; 20%), practical (11 studies; 18%), and cognitive (1 study; 2%). Frequencies of supportive care needs by domain across included studies.

Most prominent supportive care needs

Women reported supportive care needs across a range of domains. Only five of the included studies examined supportive care needs using a specific survey instrument; namely, the Supportive Care Needs-Short Form 34, and then ranked needs (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2014; Elsayed et al., 2015; Mirzaei et al., 2019; Nimekari and Naz, 2019; Al-Azri et al., 2022). From these studies, the three most frequently reported needs were physical and daily living, health system/information needs, and psychological/emotional needs, whereas the lowest reported needs were patient care and support, followed by sexuality.

Evidence of supportive care needs by domain

Physical needs

Fatigue was one of the most commonly reported physical symptoms that limited daily activity or reduced women’s ability to function due to the side effects of treatment (Prigozin et al., 2010; Nizamli et al., 2011; Joulaee et al., 2012; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017). Pain was another physical symptom that women with breast cancer reported, although cancer stage varied from stage I to stage IV, and some pain was associated with receiving chemotherapy (Doumit et al., 2010a; Nizamli et al., 2011; Jassim and Whitford, 2014).

Other less frequently reported side effects included nausea and vomiting, which were reported as a side effect of chemotherapy (Nizamli et al., 2011). Sleep quality and sleep disorders were investigated in two cross-sectional studies that reported that women had poor sleep quality (Khoramirad et al., 2014; Fekih-Romdhane et al., 2020).

Psychological needs

The most commonly reported symptoms were anxiety, depression, and psychological distress. Other less reported issues were feeling guilty, mood disturbance, hopelessness, denial, and fear of recurrence.

The prevalence of anxiety in women with breast cancer ranged from 25 to 53% (Montazeri et al., 2000; Vahdaninia et al., 2009; El-Hadidy et al., 2012; Abu-Helalah et al., 2014; Al-Zaben et al., 2015; Leila et al., 2016; Akel et al., 2017). Most studies used the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale to measure this state, except for one study (El-Hadidy et al., 2012) that used the Hamilton Anxiety Scale.

Depression prevalence ranged between 22% and 68% in the included studies; this was mostly measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale or the Beck Depression Inventory (Montazeri et al., 2000; Vahdaninia et al., 2009; El-Hadidy et al., 2012; Abu-Helalah et al., 2014; Leila et al., 2016; Shakeri et al., 2016; Akel et al., 2017; Abou Kassm et al., 2018; Fekih-Romdhane et al., 2020). Only one study, from Morocco, showed a low sub-score of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale for anxiety (5.6%) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale for depression (6.7%) (Berhili et al., 2017). Participants in two qualitative studies described experiencing depression (Nizamli et al., 2011; Al-Azri et al., 2014).

Psychological distress was reported in six of the included studies (Kulik and Kronfeld, 2005; Prigozin et al., 2010; Cohen et al., 2011; Elsayed et al., 2015; Berhili et al., 2017; Almegewly et al., 2018). Only two studies (Elsayed et al., 2015; Berhili et al., 2017) examined specifically the prevalence of psychological distress, reporting rates of 46.6% and 44%, respectively, using the Distress Thermometer Scale. Moreover, there was a positive correlation between psychological distress and all the domains of supportive care needs measured by Supportive Care Needs Short-Form-34 (p < 0.001) (Elsayed et al., 2015).

Other less frequently reported psychological needs included the following. At initial diagnosis, some women reported refusing to accept that they had breast cancer. This denial was manifested in seeking a second opinion to confirm the diagnosis (Al-Azri et al., 2014). Feelings of guilt were reported in Hammoudeh et al. (2017), stemming from an inability to fulfil what women perceived as their role as a woman and described themselves as a ‘selfless caretaker’. Another study described women feeling guilty that they may have passed on a genetic predisposition to the disease to other family members, such as daughters or granddaughters (Doumit et al., 2010a). Tirgari et al. (2012) reported sustained mood disturbance in women 1 to 5 years after a mastectomy.

Two studies measured hopelessness: one qualitative (Taleghani et al., 2006) and one cross-sectional (Fekih-Romdhane et al., 2020). The latter reported that 24% of Tunisian women with breast cancer had a high level of hopelessness using the Beck Hopelessness Scale (Fekih-Romdhane et al., 2020). Fear of recurrence emerged in accounts reported in three qualitative studies (Doumit et al., 2010a, 2010b; Jassim and Whitford, 2014).

Family-related needs

The subthemes identified were fear for the family, positive family support, dysfunctional relationships, and concealing negative emotions. Women expressed fear for their families, particularly about leaving children after their death (Doumit et al., 2010a; Nizamli et al., 2011; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Almegewly et al., 2018). Family members, such as parents, siblings, children, and spouses, provided positive support and helped women to cope effectively with breast cancer (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Doumit et al., 2010b; Joulaee et al., 2012; Kuzari et al., 2013; Goldblatt et al., 2013; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Jassim and Whitford, 2014). Only one study reported that families were unsupportive, and this was rare (Hammoudeh et al., 2017).

For married women, husbands were reported as being a major source of support (Taleghani et al., 2006; Kuzari et al., 2013; Saati, 2013; Hammoudeh et al., 2017). In contrast to the previous positive experience, several women in one study considered their husbands a source of stress (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010). Also, some women experienced dysfunctional relationships after having a breast cancer diagnosis, such as breaking off their engagement, getting divorced, or their spouse marrying another wife in countries that permit this (Joulaee et al., 2012; Assaf et al., 2017). Some Bahraini women in a study (Jassim and Whitford, 2014) reported living in constant fear of divorce or of their husbands marrying a second wife. Women reported concealing their anxieties, despair, and fears from their relatives, especially from their children, as they wanted to demonstrate competence (Goldblatt et al., 2013; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018).

Social needs

Sources of social support were reported in several studies (Kulik and Kronfeld, 2005; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Doumit et al., 2010b; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Assaf et al., 2017; Berhili et al., 2017; Mojgan et al., 2020). Women reported receiving positive social support from friends (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010), neighbours (Al-Azri et al., 2014), and colleagues (Doumit et al., 2010b). Only one study reported a lack of social support, including family and friends stopping their visits; however, the reason for this withdrawal was not explicitly stated (Assaf et al., 2017). Despite describing having positive social support, some women still experienced social stigma, reporting negative reactions to the word ‘cancer’, being perceived as someone who would die, or others pitying them or thinking they would spread the disease (Taleghani et al., 2006; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Almegewly et al., 2018). In response, some women with breast cancer avoided the word ‘cancer’ and substituted it with other words, such as ‘bad disease’ or ‘that disease’, as cancer was linked to death (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Goldblatt et al., 2013; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Assaf et al., 2017; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Terzioglu and Hammoudeh, 2017). In contrast, two qualitative studies (Doumit et al., 2010b; Jassim and Whitford, 2014) reported that some women believed that cancer was no different from any other chronic disease, such as diabetes.

The social stigma of breast cancer was associated with hiding the disease from relatives, friends, and others (Doumit et al., 2010b; Nizamli et al., 2011; Goldblatt et al., 2013; Almegewly et al., 2018). The reasons for concealing a breast cancer diagnosis were protective, such as avoiding people expressing pity (Doumit et al., 2010b; Almegewly et al., 2018), avoiding the effects of the devil’s eye—an envious or admiring gaze believed in Saudi culture to cause physical or psychological harm (Almegewly et al., 2018), or avoiding negative comments (Nizamli et al., 2011; Almegewly et al., 2018). As a result of the social stigma linked with cancer, many women reported feeling socially isolated (Nizamli et al., 2011; Joulaee et al., 2012; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Assaf et al., 2017) and lonely (Al-Azri et al., 2014). Several participants who were young, in their 30 s, reported reduced marriage opportunities following a breast cancer diagnosis (Nizamli et al., 2011; Almegewly et al., 2018).

The majority of the women believed that sharing their experience of breast cancer with other women with the same diagnosis helped them to feel strong and to accept the disease (Taleghani et al., 2006; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Doumit et al., 2010a, 2010b; Kuzari et al., 2013; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Assaf et al., 2017). Some women also recommended joining a breast cancer support group and considered it a positive experience (Doumit et al., 2010a, 2010b; Kuzari et al., 2013). Women not only generally felt the need for a support group but also found helping others to be very empowering and satisfying (Doumit et al., 2010a; Goldblatt et al., 2013; Kuzari et al., 2013). However, some women reported that sharing their breast cancer experience with others who had the disease contributed to distress, as many survivors tended to share their negative experiences with them (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Jassim and Whitford, 2014).

Interpersonal/intimacy needs

Women reported hair loss associated with chemotherapy as having the most devastating impact (Doumit et al., 2010a, 2010b; Nizamli et al., 2011; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017). Breast changes were distressing to some, and some reported these changes affected their relationships with their spouses (Nizamli et al., 2011; Joulaee et al., 2012; Assaf et al., 2017; Hammoudeh et al., 2017). In contrast, other women expressed that losing their breasts was not like losing another essential part of the body, such as a leg or a hand, and that they could be replaced (Taleghani et al., 2006).

Reduced body image was demonstrated in two studies that reported lower mean scores in all body image subscales (Sbitti et al., 2011; Sorouri and Yaghubi, 2019), but in contrast, Cohen et al. (2011) found no statistically significant difference between Arab women with breast cancer compared to healthy Arab women in respect of body image. Some women in four qualitative studies reported experiencing low sexual desire or no sexual relationship with their husbands following the diagnosis of breast cancer (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Nizamli et al., 2011; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018), whereas some women reported feeling obligated to fulfil their husbands’ sexual needs by pretending to enjoy sex despite having low or no desire (Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018). Although sexual needs were explored in the above qualitative studies, intimacy needs were ranked the lowest in quantitative studies that ranked supportive care needs (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2014; Elsayed et al., 2015; Mirzaei et al., 2019; Nimekari and Naz, 2019). Despite this low ranking, four studies focusing specifically on sexual function reported a deterioration in women’s sexual function (Sbitti et al., 2011; Harirchi et al., 2012; Leila et al., 2016; Sorouri and Yaghubi, 2019). Three studies used the Female Sexual Function Index (Sbitti et al., 2011; Harirchi et al., 2012; Sorouri and Yaghubi, 2019) but failed to report time since diagnosis, stage of cancer, or point in the treatment pathway, so it is difficult to untangle whether low scores were related to a specific or combined treatment effect. Across the studies, only one Saudi Arabian study (Almegewly et al., 2018) explored fertility and its impact on marital life, reporting that one woman concealed her infertility to avoid losing her husband, while another expressed doubts about finding a husband due to her infertility.

Practical needs

Women reported limitations in activities of daily living (Prigozin et al., 2010; Joulaee et al., 2012; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Assaf et al., 2017), and chemotherapy was identified as a time when activity was most affected (Jassim and Whitford, 2014). Although women reported receiving some assistance from female relatives (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018), some sought to maintain housekeeping work to demonstrate their self-reliance (Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018) and avoid dependence, as self-reliance, coupled with a wish to reclaim care provider roles, was associated with being normal or returning to normality (Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017).

Women who were previously in paid employment expressed some concerns about returning to work (Zamanzadeh et al., 2019), particularly workplace reception and being treated as normal (Doumit et al., 2010b). Only one study reported discrimination in the workplace, where a woman was asked to resign from her job (Assaf et al., 2017).

Two studies reported the need for financial support (Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Mirzaei et al., 2019). Family income was a predictor of having more supportive care needs (p = 0.006), meaning that those with low incomes were more likely to experience a greater number of needs (Mirzaei et al., 2019).

Spiritual/existential needs

In qualitative studies, the experience of breast cancer was closely linked to religious beliefs, with participants emphasizing the importance of faith in coping. Two studies, Goldblatt et al. (2013) and Assaf et al. (2017), included both Muslim and Christian women, while the remaining studies were conducted in predominantly Muslim populations (Taleghani et al., 2006; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Doumit et al., 2010b; Harandy et al., 2010; Joulaee et al., 2012; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Almegewly et al., 2018).

The Muslim belief that the disease was part of God’s will was reported as positive by some women and helped with acceptance of the disease and the demands of treatment (Taleghani et al., 2006; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Doumit et al., 2010b; Harandy et al., 2010; Joulaee et al., 2012; Saati, 2013; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Terzioglu and Hammoudeh, 2017; Almegewly et al., 2018).

Many Muslim women perceived breast cancer as divine will and felt they had a responsibility to undergo and play an active role in their medical treatment (Harandy et al., 2010; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Hammoudeh et al., 2017; Almegewly et al., 2018). Others interpreted breast cancer as a test and felt they should demonstrate their faith through self-control and tolerance without complaint (Taleghani et al., 2006; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Harandy et al., 2010; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Almegewly et al., 2018).

The importance of religion in coping with breast cancer was also apparent in three quantitative studies (Elsheshtawy et al., 2014; Khodaveirdyzadeh et al., 2016; Abou Kassm et al., 2018) in the form of increased religiosity after diagnosis (Abou Kassm et al., 2018), as the most common coping strategy among different patterns of coping (Elsheshtawy et al., 2014), as well as respondents’ reports of a high level of spiritual coping (Khodaveirdyzadeh et al., 2016). This was expressed through religious observances, such as praying, reciting the holy Quran, and drinking holy water, all of which provided solace (Zamzam) and reduced physical symptoms (Taleghani et al., 2006; Goldblatt et al., 2013; Al-Azri et al., 2014; Jassim and Whitford, 2014; Almegewly et al., 2018).

The perception that the diagnosis of cancer was a death sentence was reported in a number of qualitative studies and was particularly associated with the initial diagnosis (Taleghani et al., 2006; Alqaissi and Dickerson, 2010; Nizamli et al., 2011; Joulaee et al., 2012; Almegewly et al., 2018).

Health system/informational needs

A number of studies (Doumit et al., 2010a; Kuzari et al., 2013; Obeidat and Khrais, 2014; Abi Nader et al., 2016; Kimiafar et al., 2016) identified information needs. In two studies, women wanted to know specifically the stage of breast cancer (Doumit et al., 2010a; Obeidat and Khrais, 2014). Four quantitative studies examined informational needs in women with breast cancer, and their findings were varied (Mohamed and El-Sebaee, 2013; Kimiafar et al., 2016; Abi Nader et al., 2016; Sayed et al., 2017). For example, Kimiafar et al. (2016) found that disease management and nutritional options during treatment were the highest ranked areas, whereas in another study (Abi Nader et al., 2016), the highest reported issues were the cancer risk in sisters/daughters (77%), recurrence and metastasis (75%), follow-up to chemotherapy (74%), and managing lymphoedema (74%). Neither study reported the time since diagnosis or cancer stage; therefore, informational needs along the disease trajectory could not be determined.

Some women attributed the cause of cancer to psychological stress (Harandy et al., 2010; Jassim and Whitford, 2014), being envied by others (Jassim and Whitford, 2014), pollution of the physical environment with pesticides (Terzioglu and Hammoudeh, 2017), a sensitive personality, or a history of stressful life events, such as the death of a close relative (Harandy et al., 2010). In one Israeli study, women mainly attributed the cause of cancer to external fate, internal psychological causes (insufficient maintenance of mental health), or external family history (Kulik and Kronfeld, 2005). The informational needs of women might be influenced by their beliefs about cancer. The misattributions of cancer risk factors and causes, as well as preventive behaviours, result in a possible need for clearer, evidence-based information.

Most sources of information were television (Obeidat et al., 2013; Sayed et al., 2017), peer groups (Zeighami Mohammadi et al., 2018), and physicians (Abi Nader et al., 2016; Kimiafar et al., 2016), while the internet and self-help/advocacy groups were less prevalent.

Patient–clinician communication needs

Most women described their experience with healthcare providers as positive and supportive (Kuzari et al., 2013; Saati, 2013; Al-Azri et al., 2014). Only two studies reported decision making (Gilbar and Gilbar, 2009; Jassim and Whitford, 2014). In one study, women reported deferring treatment decisions to their husbands rather than making decisions independently or participating in ‘shared’ decision making, but they felt obligated to agree (Jassim and Whitford, 2014). Likewise, Gilbar and Gilbar (2009) found 92.98% of women felt they should make the decisions, but 84.2% believed that their husbands should participate, and 89.29% reported that their decisions should be made in agreement with their husbands’ views.

Cognitive needs

Only one study investigated cognitive performance during and following chemotherapy (Hormozi et al., 2019). Cognitive performance was normal in women before and one month after chemotherapy but declined after 3 and 6 months. The chemotherapy regimen was not reported.

Discussion

We aimed to synthesize the available evidence on the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa region, assess these needs across different phases of the disease trajectory, and map the findings to the 11 domains of the supportive care framework described by Paterson et al. (2015). This aim was only partially achieved due to the limited availability of studies reporting phase- or stage-specific data, and the framework required adaptation to better reflect the supportive care needs identified in this population (see Fig. 2).

Framework of supportive care needs of women with breast cancer in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) Region.

Based on the ‘best fit’ method (Carroll et al., 2013), the supportive care needs framework developed from this scoping review incorporated all the themes identified by Paterson et al. (2015), with the exception that practical and daily living needs were found to overlap and so were combined into the single domain ‘practical needs’. This covered various aspects of daily living that required assistance. As a result of this consolidation, daily living needs were categorised under practical needs, similar to Fitch (2008) framework. The definition of practical needs provided by Fitch is more relevant to the Middle East and North Africa Region, addressing issues such as childcare, employment concerns and assistance with daily living needs experienced by women. Originally, ‘activities of daily living’ was a term that was used to describe the capabilities required for independent living, such as mobility, eating, and bathing (Edemekong et al., 2019). First introduced by Katz (1983), activities of daily living are classified into two categories: activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living (Spector et al., 1987; Edemekong et al., 2019). Activities of daily living are activities that are necessary for self-care, such as bathing or dressing, whereas instrumental activities of daily living refer to functional activities that require adaptation to the environment, such as shopping or housekeeping (Spector et al., 1987). Both fall under the category of practical needs, as women might require direct assistance with activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living due to functional limitations.

Contrary to previous reviews, which reported that the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer were concentrated primarily on psychological and informational needs (Fiszer et al., 2014; Levesque et al., 2015), we identified physical needs as a prominent area requiring intervention. One possible explanation for the prominence of physical needs is that several included studies involved women receiving active treatment and women with predominantly Stage II–III disease, both of which may contribute to greater symptom burden and physical supportive care needs (Elsayed et al., 2015; Nimekari and Naz, 2019).

Although there was much alignment in the proposed 10 domains of the framework, the supportive care needs of women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa Region showed nuanced differences compared to studies from Western countries. For example, a cancer diagnosis remains stigmatising or women with breast cancer living in the Middle East and North Africa countries perceive that they are socially stigmatised. Stigma manifests in such behaviours as concealing the diagnosis and hiding emotions, withdrawing socially, and experiencing discrimination or unfriendly treatment. As a result of these challenges, women may experience feelings of disease concealment, suppression of emotions, and social withdrawal, and this may negatively affect their overall well-being and social interactions (Zamanian et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023). In this review, women concealed their breast cancer diagnosis, their feelings of anxiety and despair, and their fears from family and social networks for various reasons but primarily to avoid pity or fear of burdening their loved ones with the diagnosis. This finding was consistent with another breast cancer review that examined the Arabic context (Fearon et al., 2020). In contrast, in recent decades, the stigma of breast cancer has decreased in many countries; for example, in the USA, where media campaigns have reframed the disease as a treatment journey to be undertaken with pride (Else-Quest and Jackson, 2014). The stigma of breast cancer undoubtedly affects women’s quality of life, health-seeking behaviours, marital relationships, and rehabilitation processes (Wu et al., 2023). Therefore, regarding cultural variation, healthcare providers may need to be particularly aware of breast cancer stigma (Wang, 2017). In addition to culturally sensitive care, targeted strategies, such as psychosocial screening, safe opportunities for disclosure, and community awareness initiatives, could be considered to reduce stigma and improve engagement with care.

Religion was central to women’s experience with breast cancer in Middle East and North Africa countries and facilitated coping. This was consistent with studies suggesting spirituality and attention to religion are essential aspect of cancer care (Peteet and Balboni, 2013; Palmer Kelly et al., 2020). For Arab women, a commitment to faith brings obligations to convey gratitude to Allah (God) under all circumstances, which is possibly a unique finding for this population regardless of their religious commitment (Fearon et al., 2020). The uncertainty associated with cancer often leads women with breast cancer to contemplate spiritual or religious matters (Aamir et al., 2022). We focussed on faith as a supportive care need that helped patients to cope with treatment and accept illness, a phenomenon observed in other cancer populations from the region (Aamir et al., 2022). Although women perceived a cancer diagnosis as the divine will of God, the ‘testing’ required active engagement with the subsequent medical treatment. Hamdy (2009) argued that a Muslim’s submission to God’s will (al-twakkkul) is not a passive form of fatalism but rather a disposition that requires persistent and active work on the self. This contrasts with cancer fatalism, which was found in a study with Chinese, African American, Indian, Latina, and Malaysian women with low levels of formal education and annual income (Powe and Finnie, 2003).

We suggest that spirituality could be more explicitly integrated into supportive cancer care. In particular, the concept of tawakkul (trust in God) can be understood as an active, meaning-making coping mechanism rather than passive fatalism and may be constructively integrated into care provision. Recognising tawakkul may allow healthcare providers to align clinical care with patients’ belief systems, potentially enhancing engagement with treatment. This may include incorporating spiritual assessment into routine care, facilitating access to faith-informed counselling, and acknowledging the role of religious beliefs in shaping coping strategies and treatment decisions. Such approaches may support culturally responsive care and improve overall patient wellbeing.

In common with other studies (Lam et al., 2011; Levesque et al., 2015), we found that women with breast cancer living in Middle East and North Africa countries reported a low ranking of sexual needs, yet the included studies identified reduced sexual functioning due to cancer (Sbitti et al., 2011; Harirchi et al., 2012; Leila et al., 2016; Sorouri and Yaghubi, 2019). Moreover, the included qualitative evidence (Nizamli et al., 2011; Joulaee et al., 2012; Assaf et al., 2017; Hammoudeh et al., 2017) reported body image concerns and unmet sexual needs, which suggests that this may be an area of concern that warrants further attention. A reason for the low ranking of sexual needs might be the taboo on the discussion of sexual needs; therefore, clinicians are unlikely to raise issues during consultations, and such needs go unreported (Alananzeh et al., 2019). It is recommended that sexual issues could be signalled as acceptable for discussion by cancer patients to overcome any cultural barriers and to assuage any concerns that may be experienced (Miller et al., 2015; Alananzeh et al., 2016; Nair et al., 2018).

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the included studies were limited to those published in English or Arabic, although no Arabic-language studies were identified. Secondly, supportive care needs may change across the breast cancer trajectory; however, the included studies provided limited and inconsistent reporting across different stages (e.g., diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up), and few studies adopted longitudinal designs. This restricted our ability to assess variability and examine stage-specific differences over time. Thirdly, several studies used instruments that assessed single or specific domains (e.g., information, anxiety, and depression) and did not necessarily directly examine comprehensive supportive care needs. As highlighted by Fiszer et al. (2014), accurately identifying patients’ needs requires a holistic and comprehensive assessment approach. Lastly, grey literature was not included due to its limited availability in the region.

Implications for further research

There is limited research that comprehensively assesses supportive care needs, with most studies using the Supportive Care Needs-Short Form 34 instrument that measures cancer patients’ perceived needs across five domains (psychological, health system and information, physical and daily living, patient care and support, and sexuality needs). Consequently, several domains identified in Fitch’s seminal work (2008) and, more recently, in Paterson et al.’s (2015) framework are omitted, including those highlighted in the present review. There is a clear need for the development of a more comprehensive, culturally-attuned instrument capable of capturing the full spectrum of supportive care needs among women living in Middle East and North Africa countries and of demonstrating reliability when used longitudinally.

Conclusion

We found that breast cancer research in the Middle East and North Africa region has largely focused on women’s experiences and specific psychological outcomes, such as anxiety and depression, rather than explicitly examining supportive care needs. Notably, women in this region frequently reported reliance on faith and spirituality as central coping mechanisms, alongside informational and psychological needs. While existing studies provided important insights into these domains, evidence on other areas—such as stigma and intimacy-related needs—remains limited. Further research is required to develop culturally sensitive frameworks and assessment tools to better identify and address supportive care needs in this population.
