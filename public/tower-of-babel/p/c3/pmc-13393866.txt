Could miRNAs be used as markers for distinguishing undescended testes from retractile testes? A prospective controlled pilot/exploratory study
Authors: Mevlüt Keleş, Erdal Benli, Sercan Ergün, Abdullah Çırakoğlu, İbrahim Yazıcı, Nurullah Kadim, Tevfik Noyan, Dilbeste Demir, Mesrur Selçuk Sılay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUndescended testes (UDTs) are common in male infants. Untreated UDT poses risks such as infertility (IF), testicular cancer (TC), and testicular torsion (TT). Retractile testes (RTs) sporadically ascend from the scrotum. UDT requires early surgical correction, whereas RT requires only periodic follow-up. Differentiating these conditions is challenging, making clinical biomarkers potentially useful. The aim of our study was to examine the use of miRNAs as biomarkers in the differential diagnosis of UDT and RT which are difficult to differentiate.MethodsThis prospective pilot/exploratory study included 10 boys with UDT (operated), 9 with RT (followed), and 9 controls. Only palpable (unilateral or bilateral) UDT cases were included, excluding nonpalpable types. To ensure accurate RT diagnosis, initial physician examinations were performed in three positions (supine, semi-supine, standing), followed by a 1-month parental examination (twice daily). Only RT patients whose testes spent > 50% of their time in the scrotum were included. The exclusion criteria also included prior inguinal/scrotal surgery, defective datasets, or unsuitable serum samples. Parent consent and serum samples were collected to evaluate miR-210, miR-34c, and miR-449a expression via real-time PCR.ResultsStatistical analysis revealed no significant difference in miR-34c (p = 0.157) or miR-210 (p = 0.950) expression. However, the miR-449a level differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.033). Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc correction revealed significantly greater miR-449a in the RT group than in the UDT group (p < 0.05). No difference in miR-449a expression was found between the control and UDT groups (p > 0.05). Serum miR-449a demonstrated significant diagnostic potential in differentiating UDT from retractile testis (AUC: 0.822, p = 0.017) with 90.0% sensitivity and 77.8% specificity. These findings suggest that miR-449a could serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker to distinguish true undescended testis from its clinical mimics.ConclusionsSerum miRNA levels represent a potential tool for differentiating UDT from RT. Our pilot/exploratory study partially corroborates this, but comprehensive prospective randomized trials with larger cohorts are essential to definitively clarify miRNA alterations for precise distinction.Trial registrationThis prospective case-control study was registered to clinicaltrials.gov database, ‘retrospectively’. The trial registration number is: NCT07315737, the unique protocol ID is: 2022/25.Keywords: Cryptorchidism, Undescended testis, Retractile testis, miRNA, Biomarker

Introduction

Undescended testes (UDTs) are the most common congenital male malformation, affecting 1.0–4.6% of term infants and up to 45% of preterm infants [1]. UDT can be congenital or acquired; spontaneous descent in congenital cases is rare after six months [2]. Untreated UDT beyond one year of age leads to interstitial fibrosis, degenerated spermatogenesis, and increased infertility risk [3–5]. Furthermore, untreated UDTs carry risks of testicular cancer (TC) and testicular torsion (TT). Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent these complications.

Differentiating true undescended testes (UDTs) from retractile testes (RTs) is clinically challenging. True UDT involves a consistently empty scrotum, with testes never descending. Conversely, RTs can spontaneously descend or be manually reduced, although they may reascend. Treatment for RT depends on the time spent outside the scrotum. Misdiagnosing UDT as RT risks delayed treatment and inevitable complications. Thus, reliable indicators for distinguishing UDTs and RT are necessary.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are ~ 22-nucleotide noncoding RNAs that posttranscriptionally regulate gene expression [6]. MiRNAs are known to regulate spermatogenesis (SP), early embryonic development, sperm function, and fertilization in various species [7]. Given the expected SP disruption in true UDTs, the UDT-miRNA relationship has been explored. Studies have shown altered miRNA levels in UDT: miR-210 is upregulated [8], whereas miR-449a and miR-34c are downregulated [9–11]. Critically, data on miRNA changes in retractile testis (RT) cases are currently lacking. Theoretically, the SP is not expected to be disrupted at RT. Thus, miRNA alterations, reflecting SP status, could differentiate RT from UDT, guiding surgical versus follow-up decisions. This study compared miRNA changes across UDT, RT, and normal control groups prospectively.

Materials and methods

Sample size

This pilot/exploratory study is designed as a prospective controlled trial to compare miRNA changes across UDT, RT, and normal control groups. The sample size was determined via G*Power 3.1.9.6. A one-way ANOVA power analysis for miR-34c, which compared three groups, utilized an effect size (f = 1.2955357) from a previous study [10]. To achieve 99% power and a 1% error rate, a total sample size of 24 was needed. To increase confidence, the study ultimately enrolled 30 patients, who were divided into three groups of 10. While the study design and data collection were conducted prospectively, formal registry entry was completed after the study had commenced.

Patient population

Initially, 10 boys with congenital undescended testes (UDTs), 10 with retractile testes (RTs), and 10 healthy volunteers (controls) from our urology clinic (dates between March 2022 and March 2023) were enrolled. However, one RT patient and one control patient were excluded because of parental refusal for blood sampling. Only palpable (unilateral or bilateral) UDT cases were included, excluding nonpalpable types. To ensure accurate RT diagnosis, initial physician examinations were performed in three positions (supine, semi-supine, standing), followed by a 1-month parental examination (twice daily). In a 1-month period, totally 60 scrotal examination record (twice daily) analyzed. If the scotum is empty, and if the testis/testes palpable, recorded by date and time. Only RT patients whose testes spent > 50% of their time in the scrotum during follow-up period were included. The exclusion criteria also included prior inguinal/scrotal surgery, defective datasets, or unsuitable serum samples.

Collection of patient data and control samples

Detailed patient histories, physical examinations, and routine biochemical tests were performed. UDT blood samples were collected presurgery to prevent misinterpretation. Blood routine liver/kidney function tests were performed to ensure that the serum miRNA profiles were not confounded by systemic metabolic or organ dysfunction. Blood from all the groups was collected in 5 mL biochemistry tubes and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting particle-free serum was transferred to 1.5 mL tubes and stored at -80 °C until analysis

Validation of the expression levels of miR-210, miR-449a and miR-34c via real-time PCR

Serum samples from the urology clinic were subjected to RNA isolation via the Quick-cfRNA™ Serum & Plasma Kit (Zymo Research). The extracted miRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA via the miRNA All-In-One cDNA Synthesis Kit (ABMGood). cDNA quality and quantity were assessed spectrophotometrically with a BioSpec-nano instrument (Shimadzu) before real-time PCR. miRNA expression analysis was performed via a Rotor Gene Q (Qiagen) instrument. The levels of miR-210, miR-449a, and miR-34c were quantified with ready-to-use primers and BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (ABMGood).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and variance equality was assessed via Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics are presented as the means ± SDs for parametric data and medians (IQRs) and means (ranks) for nonparametric data. For group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used for parametric data, and the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used for nonparametric data, followed by the Dunn‒Bonferroni correction for post hoc multiple comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to analyse correlations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

miRNA expression was quantified via qRT‒PCR via Ct (crossing point) values and normalized to RNU6B_13. Relative miRNA expression levels were compared among all groups via the 2-ΔCt method and calculated as 2Ct (target gene) − Ct (reference gene).

An exploratory Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic potential of serum miR-449a in distinguishing patients with UDT from healthy controls, as well as from patients with RT. The area under the curve (AUC) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to assess discriminative power for each comparison. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index (maximizing both sensitivity and specificity). The statistical significance of differences between groups was evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U) test. ROC analyses and graph generations were executed using the ‘pROC’ package in R software. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All the statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS v28 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

To determine the clinical relevance of serum miR-449a, an exploratory ROC analysis was conducted to assess its capacity to differentiate UDT from healthy controls and RT. In the differentiation of UDT from the healthy control group, miR-449a yielded an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.689 (95% CI: 0.422–0.956, p = 0.182), demonstrating a sensitivity of 70.0% and a specificity of 77.8% at the optimal cut-off value of 0.00051.

Notably, in the differentiation of UDT from RT, the diagnostic performance of miR-449a was remarkably higher and statistically significant. The AUC reached 0.822 (95% CI: 0.609–1.000, p = 0.017). At the optimal cut-off value of 0.00214, serum miR-449a exhibited a robust sensitivity of 90.0% and a specificity of 77.8%. These findings suggest that serum miR-449a holds significant potential as a discriminative biomarker, particularly in distinguishing UDT from RT. (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Cryptorchidism carries a risk of serious complications, including infertility (IF) and testicular cancer (TC), necessitating accurate differential diagnosis, particularly from the retractile testis (RT). Conventional methods often fall short in this distinction, leading to potential complications.

The identification of reliable non-invasive biomarkers for UDT remains a critical challenge in pediatric urology. Our findings suggest that serum miR-449a may serve as a promising diagnostic adjunct, particularly in the challenging clinical differentiation between true UDT and RT.

While the capacity of miR-449a to distinguish UDT from healthy controls reached an AUC of 0.689, it did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.182), possibly suggesting that baseline expression levels in healthy individuals share a degree of overlap with the pathological state. However, a markedly different trend was observed when comparing UDT to the RT group. In this context, miR-449a demonstrated robust diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.822 (p = 0.017), coupled with a high sensitivity of 90.0%.

This distinction is of paramount clinical importance. Retractile testes generally do not require surgical intervention and are considered a variant of normal anatomy with a functional cremasteric reflex. In contrast, UDT requires timely orchidopexy to mitigate risks of infertility and germ cell neoplasia. The high sensitivity (90.0%) at the optimal cut-off (0.00214) suggests that miR-449a could potentially reduce unnecessary surgical explorations or, conversely, prevent the misclassification of a truly UDT as merely retractile.

The miR-449 family is known to be involved in ciliogenesis and germ cell differentiation; thus, its differential expression in UDT may reflect the early molecular disturbances or heat-induced stress experienced by the malpositioned gonad. Our results align with emerging evidence that microRNAs can mirror the histological integrity of the testes, providing a “liquid biopsy” that exceeds the accuracy of standard physical maneuvers in borderline cases.

Numerous studies have examined the UDT-miRNA relationship, primarily focusing on miRNA expression changes that disrupt spermatogenesis (SP) in UDT. Early research (2014) in unilateral undescended testicular rats revealed that miR-135a degradation, which is hypothesized to negatively affect Fox01 activity, disrupts spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) stabilization, and leads to infertility (IF) [12]. MiR-210 levels in UDT have also been investigated [8]; its upregulation is linked to hypospermatogenesis and germ cell arrest [13], suggesting a correlation with defective SP in UDT-associated IF [8]. MiRNAs are known to regulate SP and fertility via Sertoli cells [14]. Furthermore, heat-sensitive miRNAs are speculated to contribute to defective SP- and UDT-IF-related gene mutations [15].

Research has also explored persistent miRNA expression changes postorchiopexy. For example, in three young males with postorchiopexy nonobstructive azoospermia, downregulated miR-297 and upregulated miR-152 were observed relative to those in controls, indicating a non-normalized miRNA profile [9]. Therapeutic miRNA manipulation has been proposed; increased miR-34c in UDTs might inhibit Nanos2 and germ cell maturation, suggesting that miR-34c inhibition is a potential target for UDT-associated infertility (IF) [10]. Furthermore, miRNA expression in the contralateral descending testes of unilateral UDT cases has been investigated in horses, corroborating that miRNA changes can predict UDT-associated IF [11]. The role of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway in spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) stabilization and spermatogenesis (SP) suggests that AKT-3-related miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-7-5p and hsa-miR-519d-3p) are potential biomarkers for UDT and UDT-related azoospermia [16]. A systematic review of 185 human and animal tissue samples confirmed the dysregulation of miR-210 (upregulated), miR-449a (downregulated), and miR-34c (downregulated) in UDT [17].

Furthermore, investigations into preventing cryptorchidism-associated male infertility via miRNA pathways have been conducted. One study revealed that miR-22-5p was upregulated and that enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) was downregulated in undescended testicular tissues. These findings suggest that miR-22-5p regulates spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) self-renewal by targeting EZH2, positioning miR-22-5p as a potential biomarker for the treatment of cryptorchidism-induced infertility [18].

Studies on the role of miRNAs in spermatogonia maturation, which is linked to UDT infertility, suggest their potential as prognostic biomarkers for UDT-testicular malignancy. Therefore, miRNAs influencing gonocyte-to-spermatogonia differentiation and potentially contributing to germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS), a precursor to testicular germ cell tumors, were investigated.

Meta-analyses suggest that the hsa-miR-371-373 and hsa-miR-302/367 clusters play active roles in the transformation of gonocytes to germ cell intraepithelial neoplasia (GCNIS) and adult testicular germ cell tumor (TGCT) development. Thus, investigating these malignant transformation-associated miRNAs may yield crucial insights into UDT in children [19]. Furthermore, previous studies on UDT-associated miR-210 expression confirmed a relationship between miRNAs and disordered sex development (DSD). Specifically, miR-210 levels are positively associated with cryptorchidism and hypospadias in 46, XY DSD [20]. An animal study in dogs demonstrated that aging causally impacts miRNA expression, with significant changes also observed in UDTs and testicular cancers (TCs) [21]. Recent research (2023) on miRNA expression in dogs with unilateral cryptorchidism and seminoma revealed higher miR-302c-3p in controls than in undescended, contralateral scrotal, and seminoma-affected testes. This finding indicated similar epigenetic dysregulation in both the undescended/scrotal testes of unilateral cryptorchid dogs and neoplastic testes, suggesting close follow-up of the contralateral scrotal testis [22].

Existing data suggest that miRNAs are potential markers for predicting UDT-related serious complications, such as IF and TC. Our pilot/exploratory study, however, prioritizes accurate UDT diagnosis to prevent these complications, as misdiagnosing UDT as RT can lead to delayed surgery, increasing the risk of IF and TC.

While most of the literature examines miRNA expression in tissue samples, our pilot/exploratory study distinctively analysed serum miRNAs to differentiate UDT from RT using easily accessible samples. Notably, this is the first study to report serum miR-449a levels in children with UDT. While tissue-based studies exist, the absence of circulating miRNA data in current literature highlights the novelty of our liquid biopsy approach.

The stability of miRNAs in extracellular fluid and their resilience to adverse conditions (freezing, thawing, storage, pH, and temperature) make them ideal diagnostic and prognostic markers detectable in both tissues and body fluids [23, 24]. The use of serum samples instead of tissue was necessitated by stringent ethical constraints, as performing invasive testicular biopsies on healthy pediatric controls is clinically and ethically unjustifiable. Consequently, a non-invasive liquid biopsy approach was prioritized to ensure patient safety and study feasibility.

However, the use of serum samples presents limitations. Specifically, in unilateral UDT cases where the contralateral testis is normal, blood analysis might yield contradictory or misleading results, potentially affecting our findings.

A critical consideration in our study is the prepubertal status of the participants. While many studies on microRNAs (miRNAs) in the male reproductive system focus on their role in active spermatogenesis, our findings in a prepubertal cohort suggest a different biological context. In this developmental window, the testis is characterized by the proliferation of immature Sertoli cells and the maintenance of the gonocyte/spermatogonial stem cell pool within the testicular niche. Specifically, miR-449a is known to regulate cell cycle exit and the initiation of differentiation. The observed downregulation of serum miR-449a in the UDT group, compared to the RT and control groups, may reflect an early molecular signalling impairment within the testicular niche maintenance rather than a failure of active sperm production. This suggests that miR-449a could serve as a non-invasive marker for early germ cell environment stress or dysregulation in the cryptorchid gonad, potentially predating the histological changes typically seen in later childhood.

Data collection was completed in 2022, guiding our selection of miR-210, miR-449a, and miR-34c on the basis of the most comprehensive meta-analysis available before then [17]. Unlike the literature, our pilot/exploratory study revealed no significant difference in the levels of these miRNAs between the UDT and control groups. We attribute this discrepancy, potentially stemming from our small sample size, which is due to our institution’s limited patient volume and lack of a dedicated pediatric urology clinic, necessitating patient extraction from the adult population. Furthermore, sociocultural factors often lead to UDT cases presenting after the first year of life, introducing an age-related limitation.

Our significant finding of lower miR-449a levels in UDT patients than in RT patients is noteworthy. This finding supports the hypothesis that serum miRNA measurements can distinguish UDTs requiring surgery from RT requiring only follow-up, thereby significantly contributing to the literature.

Our results cannot be generalized due to our small sample size, which is an important limitation. Also, our initial power calculation was based on miR-34c (derived from tissue-based studies), but our results revealed no significant difference on miR-34c. Additionally, while clinical examination remains the gold standard, the lack of intraoperative or histological confirmation for all subjects is a limitation of the current pilot/exploratory study.

Despite limitations, our study’s prospective, controlled design, exploration of an under-addressed subject, and solution-oriented results render it significant. As our pilot/exploratory study compares miRNA alterations between congenital UDT and RT cases, we also become aware of the potential of miR-449a to serve as a predictive tool for “ascending testes”. Future longitudinal studies comparing miR-449a alterations between RT and ascending testis cases can be a significant clinical challenge.

Conclusion

Given the documented role of miRNAs in infertility (IF) and testicular cancer (TC), which are major complications of cryptorchidism, we explored their utility in differentiating UDT from RT. Our analyses state that while downregulated miR-449a shows potential, larger studies are required to confirm its role as a definitive diagnostic tool. Owing to our small sample size, this study should be considered as a pilot/exploratory study. Nevertheless, larger prospective randomized controlled trials are essential to establish the clinical utility of serum miRNA levels for UDT and RT differentiation.
