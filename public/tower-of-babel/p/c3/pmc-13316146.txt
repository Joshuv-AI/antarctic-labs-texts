Mapping histologic and functional maturation of human endocrine pancreas across early postnatal periods
Authors: Diane C Saunders, Fan Feng, Alexander L Hopkirk, Kristie I Aamodt, Nathaniel Hart, Fong Cheng Pan, Conrad V Reihsmann, Nike Izmaylov, Shaojun Mei, Brandon A Sherrod, Corey Davis, Nongyao Nonpanya, Yasminye D Pettway, Keval P Patel, Kai S Lee, Jeff Duryea, Rachana Haliyur, Radhika Aramandla, Heather Durai, Greg Poffenberger, Alexandra Martin, Amanda L Posgai, Irina Kusmartseva, Maria L Beery, Mingder Yang, Ke Xu, Hakmook Kang, Dale L Greiner, Leonard D Shultz, Jean-Philippe Cartailler, Rita Bottino, Mark A Atkinson, Christopher V E Wright, Alvin C Powers, Marcela Brissova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman endocrine cell differentiation and islet morphogenesis play critical roles in determining islet cell mass and function, but the events and timeline of these processes are incompletely defined. To better understand early human islet cell development and maturation, we collected 123 pediatric pancreata and mapped morphological and spatiotemporal changes from birth through the first ten years of life. Using quantitative analyses and a combination of complementary tissue imaging approaches, including confocal microscopy and whole-slide multiplex imaging, we developed an integrated model for endocrine cell formation and islet architecture, including endocrine cell type heterogeneity and abundance, endocrine cell proliferation, and islet vascularization and innervation. We also assessed insulin and glucagon secretory profiles in isolated islet preparations from pediatric donors aged 2 months to 10 years and found a temporal difference in the maturation of insulin secretion compared to glucagon secretion. This comprehensive summary of postnatal and pediatric pancreatic islet development provides a framework for future studies and for integrating emerging genetic and genomic data related to islet biology and diabetes risk.Subject terms: Organogenesis, Endocrine system and metabolic diseases

Introduction

The reduction of functional β-cell mass and the failure of the pancreatic islet to adequately regulate blood glucose homeostasis are foundational to all forms of diabetes. Human β-cell mass varies among individuals by up to 40-fold during the prenatal period1–5 and three to fivefold in adults6. The composition of adult human islets also shows considerable heterogeneity among individuals7. Although incompletely defined, insulin secretion by human β cells varies between islets isolated from pediatric and adult pancreas8. Collectively, such variations likely affect an individual’s risk and timeline for developing diabetes. Yet significant gaps remain in our understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of β-cell mass and islet cell composition and function during prenatal and postnatal pancreas development. Furthermore, the β cell-directed autoimmunity of type 1 diabetes often appears in the first five years of life9,10, coincident with an active period of pancreas growth and islet development involving significant tissue reorganization and maturation1,11,12.

Defining human prenatal to early postnatal endocrine-cell development has been challenging due to limited access to human tissue at these stages, along with difficulties in visualizing islet developmental processes and cellular relationships at molecular resolution in the context of a rapidly growing and reorganizing organ. Histological analyses of human prenatal tissues have shown that the pancreatic epithelium undergoes active growth and tubular morphogenesis around 6–7 weeks of gestation (G6-7w)13,14. At this point, cells express several key transcription factors such as pancreas and duodenum homeobox 1 (PDX1), sex-determining region Y (SRY) box 9 (SOX9), Nirenberg and Kim homeobox 6.1 (NKX6.1), GATA transcription factor 4, and forkhead box A23,13,15–17, which closely resembles the signature of multipotent progenitor cells (MPCs) in murine pancreas. Using whole-mount labeling and 3D rendering approaches on intact pancreatic tissue samples from the first trimester, clusters of insulin (INS)-positive cells were found to be preferentially located in the center of the pancreas, aligned along the longitudinal axis, and essentially absent from the periphery18,19. Ensuing developmental steps that define tip-trunk compartmentalization of epithelial tubes, endocrine-cell birth, and islet formation—including the degree to which these processes are synchronized across different pancreatic regions with organ expansion during late prenatal development and after birth—are largely uncharacterized in the human pancreas.

Most postnatal human islet development studies have focused on β-cell proliferation and maturation5,8,20. Young β cells are quite proliferative during the gestational and perinatal periods, but their proliferative capacity declines sharply in the years after birth, along with their responsivity to cellular mitogens1,3,5,21,22. Because postnatal β-cell expansion is thought to be primarily achieved through replication, this brief window of proliferative capacity is of great interest to those developing regenerative therapies to replace or restore β-cell mass in diabetes. Furthermore, an initial lower β-cell mass in this early postnatal period may render an individual more susceptible to diabetes. Also unknown is when glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in humans develops and matures, as GSIS is not present prenatally19,20 and pediatric islets have reduced basal insulin secretion compared to adult islets8. Likewise, it is unknown when human islets acquire the essential counter-regulatory mechanisms controlling glucagon secretion. The importance of macrophages in prenatal human pancreas differentiation and endocrine cell development has been described in ref. 23, but it’s unclear what role they, or other immune cells, play in the postnatal pancreas24. Islet vascularization and innervation have also been described during prenatal human development25–29 but have not been extensively characterized postnatally. Based on studies of mouse pancreas development30–32, these processes likely influence endocrine-cell maturation in a reciprocal manner.

Extensive postnatal remodeling in cellular architecture and distribution of islets throughout the pancreas has been described previously5,33,34. More recently, cellular polarity, 3D compaction, and the arrangement of mouse islets have been shown to be important in the functional maturation of the islet unit35. Additional studies have demonstrated that β cells undergo epigenetic remodeling as they age, substantially affecting their function36,37. These studies suggest there are dynamic islet structural and functional changes during postnatal development, but the timing and sequence of these changes remain poorly defined.

Here, we assembled the largest cohort of human pediatric pancreas samples analyzed to date—123 organs spanning birth through the first decade of life—and systematically applied a combination of complementary tissue imaging approaches, including confocal microscopy and whole-slide multiplex imaging, to subsets of this cohort (see Supplementary Data 1) to document key morphogenic events of postnatal pancreas and islet development. We generated an immunohistochemistry-based spatiotemporal map of islet formation, quantification of endocrine-cell birth, proliferation, and composition, and the acquisition of islet vasculature and innervation, identifying critical differences between human and rodent islet development. Finally, we characterized pediatric islet function in a perifusion system and showed that islets in the first two years of life exhibit distinct differences in insulin and glucagon secretion dynamics compared to older pediatric and adult islets. Together, the data reported herein provide an unprecedented overview of human islet development that can inform future disease-specific studies of endocrine-cell differentiation and function, ultimately leading to the development of new diabetes treatments and paving the way to disease prevention.

Results

Collection of large pediatric pancreas cohort through nationwide partnerships with organ procurement organizations

Fortunately, the mortality rates of infants below one year of age and children aged 1–4 years are low in the United States (543.6 and 25.0 deaths per 100,000 population, respectively)38, and clinical transplantations are necessarily prioritized from organ donations. Therefore, a very limited number of pediatric pancreata become available for research each year (3–20 for age <1 year; 11-27 for age 1–5 years; 21–33 for age 6–10 years; Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network national data 2014–2023, https://optn.transplant.hrsa.gov/). Building on the infrastructure developed by the Network for Pancreatic Organ Donors with Diabetes (nPOD; RRID:SCR_014641, http://www.jdrfnpod.org) and their outreach to a nationwide network of organ procurement organizations (OPOs) and nonprofit organizations, our team coordinated the collection of over 100 postnatal pediatric organs for research since 2012.

To ensure accurate tracking of morphological landmarks between tissue samples, coronal sections were taken from the head, body, and tail regions (Fig. 1a). Acknowledging that developmental stages are likely a continuum, donors were stratified to define critical biological processes over time. Approximately corresponding to key postnatal developmental milestones, we stratified our cohort into three categories: neonatal, infancy, and childhood. Although “neonate” is a term usually reserved for babies four weeks of age or younger39, because of the high proportion of anencephalic cases in our cohort (n = 22, aged 0–5 days), we expanded our definition of neonatal to include donors 2 months of age or younger (totaling n = 36; see Supplementary Data 1). The infancy stage (n = 36), defined in this study as ≥3 months to <24 months, corresponds to a period of heightened growth and transitioning nutritional demands40,41. The childhood stage (n = 51) encompassed ages ≥2 years to ≤10 years. Donor sex was approximately even between female (n = 63) and male (n = 60) across age groups (Fig. 1a, b), with most donors being Caucasian (Fig. 1c). Cause of death, outside of the neonatal group, was predominantly anoxia or head trauma (Fig. 1d), with a median of three days on ventilator (Fig. S1A). Where possible, the gestational age of neonatal donors at the time of birth was recorded (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 1). For comparison, fourteen adult donors were also studied (Fig. 1a). Of note, only a subset of donors in the cohort was used for each assay, with donor- and modality-level granularity recorded in Supplementary Data 1.

As shown in Fig. 1f–h, pancreas weight at birth varied almost fourfold (0.98 to 3.71 g; n = 19) but showed a rapid, nonlinear increase with increasing postnatal age (F = 10.98, p = 2.74e-6). Gross anatomy remained consistent across stages (Fig. S1B–D). Because overall body weight varied substantially among individuals of the same postnatal age, pancreas weight was alternatively expressed as a “weight index” (in grams, over total body weight, in kilograms; Fig. 1i). Interestingly, although the weight index showed a nonlinear trend as a function of age (nonlinearity test, F = 11.81, p = 1.21e-6) and means differed among age groups—including between ages 2–5 and 6–10 within the childhood group (Fig. S1E)—standard deviations were not significantly different (Brown–Forsythe test, F = 0.06396, p = 0.9381). This similar trajectory of weight index values across neonatal, infancy, and childhood groups suggests that the variability in pancreas size previously noted in adolescents and adults42,43 might already be established by birth. Importantly, as the cause of death in many neonatal cases was anencephaly (Supplementary Data 1), our studies demonstrated no difference in the pancreas weight when compared with non-anencephalic cases of similar age (Fig. S1F), though the weight index does reflect the decrease in body weight from absent brain tissue in these patients (Fig. S1G, H). Postnatal body weight was highly correlated with pancreas weight (Pearson’s product-moment correlation, R = 0.899, p < 0.001; Fig. 1j).

Human multipotent progenitors and proto-endocrine cells persisted after birth

During the neonatal period, the pancreatic epithelium maintained a tubular structure. The marker GP2, which identifies MPCs44 in addition to acinar cells, was prominently expressed at the edges of pancreatic lobes at birth and in the presumptive acinar tissue, but it was absent in hormone-producing cells (Figs. 2a and S2A, B). This finding is consistent with previous reports showing GP2 production in pancreatic MPCs and acinar progenitor cells and downregulation in differentiated endocrine and ductal cells44,45. We next investigated the compartmentalization of the pancreatic epithelial tubes and visualized the distribution of two key transcription factors, PTF1A and NKX6.1. These factors are known to function as opposing determinants of acinar versus endocrine lineages, respectively, that emerge from MPCs during mouse pancreas organogenesis46. As expected, PTF1A was localized to what has been referred to as the “tip” region of the branched epithelial network46. In contrast, however, to earlier findings in the developing mouse pancreas46,47, we discovered that NKX6.1 production was not restricted to “trunk” regions but also nuclear-located in many cells in the structural “tip” regions, being co-produced with PTF1A in GP2+ cells (Fig. 2b–c’). Among n = 6 donors (1 day to 20 months), an average of 10.7 ± 2.5% and 5.3 ± 2.0% of GP2+ cells were positive only for PTF1A (likely proto-acinar cells) or NKX6.1 (likely proto-endocrine cells), respectively, while 2.3 ± 1.1% were PTF1A + NKX6.1+ (Fig. S2C, D). These observations raise the possibility that a substantial pool of MPCs may continue to be located within tip regions of relatively late-neonatal-stage tissue, and/or that the PTF1A–NKX6.1 cross-repression may be slower in human MPCs compared to mouse, such that tip-located cells remain NKX6.1+ for longer46,48. After identifying PTF1A + NKX6.1+ cells in the postnatal pancreas (Fig. 2b–c’), we also observed proto-endocrine PAX6+ post-mitotic cells that had not yet differentiated toward INS-, GCG-, or SST-producing state specificity, even up to 20 months of age (Figs. 2d–e’ and S2E, E’). These findings are consistent with but cannot prove—in the absence of lineage tracing—the idea that outer-periphery regions of pancreatic lobes retain residual MPCs and their derived late-born endocrine cells. Together, these data highlight the need for further analysis of a potential crucial distinction in human pancreatic endocrine-cell development compared to mouse models47,49,50, namely that MPCs may persist postnatally with significant endocrine-cell neogenesis after birth.

To further characterize proto-endocrine cells in the human postnatal pancreas, we turned to multiplex antibody-based imaging (CO-Detection by indEXing, CODEX), which enabled visualization of multiple transcription factors (TFs) in the same cell as part of a 31-plex marker panel applied to tissue sections from 17 donors (n = 6 neonatal, n = 5 infancy, n = 6 childhood) (Supplementary Data 2A). Of 31 total markers, 26 passed visual inspection quality control (QC) across at least 80% of images, and an additional 4 markers passed QC in at least one image per donor for ≥70% of the donor cohort (Supplementary Data 2B). Pass-QC TFs included ARX, ISL1, NKX2.2, PAX6, and PDX1. NEUROD1 detection was variable across tissue samples and was not used for further quantitative analyses; in Pancreatlas™, spatial localization of NEUROD1 is provided for 9 of 17 donors. Cells annotated using a custom-developed fingerprint pipeline (Fig. S3A) were utilized for both population-based and spatial analyses (Fig. S3B–D).

Hormone-negative cells producing at least two TFs were observed still embedded in epithelial tubes, while hormone-positive cells were primarily present in non-tubule-located clusters (Fig. 2f–f”). PAX6+ hormone-negative cells were significantly more abundant at the neonatal stage, with an average of 24.75 ± 7.22% of these proto-endocrine cells in ductal regions (Fig. 2g–i). This trend was consistent for PAX6+ hormone-positive cells, though to a lesser extent (Fig. 2i). Importantly, this elevated ductal localization of PAX6+ cells in the neonatal pancreas was not attributable to a larger relative ductal area, as the overall proportion of pancreatic cells residing in ductal regions did not significantly differ across developmental stages (Fig. S4A). At the neonatal stage, the vast majority (73.2%) of PAX6+ hormone-negative cells were also producing transcription factors ISL1 and NKX2-2, with most of those cells (84.8%) positive for either PDX1 or ARX, consistent with the expected developing lineage-specific TF profiles for β and α cells, respectively (Fig. 2j). This trend remained consistent at the infancy stage despite fewer proto-endocrine cells overall, while in childhood, the predominant populations were PAX6+ alone, PAX6 + ISL1+, and PAX6 + ISL1 + ARX+ (Fig. S4B, C). Together, these findings highlight likely ongoing postnatal endocrine-cell differentiation within a potential “ductal/tubule” niche.

Neonatal endocrine cells were located more often in small (<50 μm) clusters compared to infancy and childhood stages

Small endocrine-cell clusters were densely packed across the entire pancreas at birth, both in the periphery and central regions of lobes (Figs. 3a, a’ and S5). In infancy, larger clusters of endocrine cells were observed with distinct morphology reminiscent of rodent islets, with a β cell core and (α and δ cell) mantle (Fig. 3b–b’)51. By childhood, islets were generally indistinguishable from adult islets in structure and composition and more sparsely distributed throughout exocrine tissue (Fig. 3c–c’). To determine postnatal islet size and the distribution of α, β, and δ cells in islets versus small clusters, we used immunohistochemical staining from a cohort of n = 9 neonatal, n = 11 infancy, and n = 22 childhood donors in addition to the 17 donor cohort used for CODEX imaging (n = 6 neonatal, n = 5 infancy, n = 6 childhood). Islets, defined as contiguous regions of endocrine cells ≥50 μm in diameter, showed relatively similar size distribution across age groups (Fig. 3d), while a significantly greater percentage of neonatal endocrine cells were in endocrine clusters, defined here as <50 μm in diameter, including single endocrine cells: 36.13 ± 3.20% compared to 25.67 ± 1.60% and 24.15 ± 1.13% in infancy and childhood, respectively (Figs. 3e–g and S6A). Interestingly, this appeared to be driven by β cells, whose cluster-to-islet ratio was significantly higher in neonatal pancreas compared to infancy and childhood (Figs. 3f–h and S6A). This data was consistent across both imaging modalities used for analysis.

Islets in childhood had a greater proportion of β cells and fewer proliferating cells compared to neonatal and infancy stages

Total endocrine-cell fraction was highest at birth (accounting for 11.63 ± 0.64% of total cells, n = 24) but substantially lower thereafter (Figs. 4a and S6B). Proportions of α, β, δ, ε, and γ cells were remarkably consistent among donors of the same age group, with the most striking change occurring between the neonatal and infancy stages (Fig. 4b). In neonatal pancreata, β cells were the most abundant endocrine subtype (42.38 ± 1.75%), followed by δ cells (34.04 ± 1.50%), then α cells (23.58 ± 1.31%). In the infancy stage (n = 21), the proportion of β cells was greater (55.30 ± 1.81%), while the α-cell proportion remained unchanged (21.98 ± 1.38%) and the δ-cell proportion was lower (22.72 ± 0.87%). These β, α, and δ proportions were maintained in childhood at 57.89 ± 1.00, 23.25 ± 0.81, and 19.05 ± 0.62%, respectively. The numbers of ghrelin-producing ε cells and pancreatic polypeptide-producing γ cells remained consistently low (< 6% each, n = 4 per stage) throughout neonatal, infancy, and childhood stages.

To account for the variable growth rates among individuals of similar ages, pancreatic composition was further analyzed as a function of total pancreas weight. Densities of α, β, and δ cells showed significant nonlinearity relative to increasing pancreas weight (Fig. S6C; F = 17.65 α, 11.12 β, 22.90 δ; all p < 0.001), consistent with the trend by donor age (Fig. S6D) and the trajectory of exocrine tissue expansion. Strikingly, β- and δ-cell composition showed nonlinear changes relative to increasing pancreas weight (F = 6.67 β, 8.06 δ; both p < 0.001), while the proportion of α cells remained consistent (approximately 22%; F = 1.03, p = 0.3861; Fig. S6E) with no linear relationship between α cell numbers and pancreas weight (p = 0.876). Again, this trend was quite similar when considered by donor age (Fig. S6F). We tested for nonlinearity by assessing the significance of nonlinear restricted cubic spline terms in each linear regression model using an F-test. These data indicate that postnatal α-cell proportion is surprisingly stable, whereas β and δ cells exhibit differential population growth trajectories from birth to childhood. Consistent with pancreas growth, α-, β-, and δ-cell mass were significantly higher in the childhood stage compared to neonatal (Fig. S6G, H).

Postnatal α-, β-, and δ-cell proliferation, as deduced by Ki67+ status—a proxy for proliferation as it marks non-quiescent cells5—was quite low (less than 2.5%), even during the neonatal stage when endocrine cell density was at its peak (Figs. 4c and S6B). Notably, α-cell proliferation (2.25 ± 0.41%) was significantly greater than δ-cell proliferation (1.09 ± 0.12%; adjusted p = 0.0053) and β-cell proliferation (1.31 ± 0.24%; adjusted p = 0.0055) at this stage. All three subpopulations exhibited comparable proliferation frequency during infancy (1.22 ± 0.32 β, 1.75 ± 0.48 α, 0.87 ± 0.26% δ) and childhood (0.76 ± 0.19 β, 1.20 ± 0.35 α, 0.58 ± 0.14% δ). Comparing between neonatal and childhood stages, only δ cell proliferation was significantly decreased (adjusted p = 0.0203). While previous studies have linked ischemia and on-ventilator time to greater endocrine proliferation52, we did not have enough donors with ischemic time data to evaluate its effect, so it is unknown whether donors with longer ischemic times have decreased Ki67 as a result53. We did not observe a ventilator time correlation among donors for whom the data were available (Fig. S7A, B).

Since the cause of death for many neonatal donors was anencephaly (n = 22, aged 0–5 days), we investigated whether this condition influenced neonatal pancreas or islet composition. When compared to non-anencephalic neonatal donors (n = 13, aged 1 day to <3 months), we observed no differences in endocrine-cell density, endocrine-cell composition, or endocrine-cell proliferation (Fig. S7C–E). These data suggest that in utero pancreatic development occurs normally despite neural tube abnormalities in these cases54,55. Overall, our data point to highly variable postnatal endocrine proliferation among individuals.

ε and γ cells were stable across developmental stages, while proto-endocrine cells were more often in clusters than islets at the neonatal stage

Although fewer donors were analyzed by CODEX imaging, endocrine-cell abundance and composition, as well as distribution between islets and clusters, were largely like those observed in the larger cohort by traditional IHC (Figs. 4d, e and S8A). Additional data points included ε and γ cells, neither of which differed among age groups in terms of their presence in islets versus clusters (Fig. 4e). For PAX6+ hormone-negative proto-endocrine cells described earlier, only 35.63 ± 4.09% were in islets in the neonatal stage, when they were most abundant. In infancy and childhood, these cells were more frequently located in islets at 60.02 ± 3.05 and 59.91 ± 4.26%, respectively (Fig. 4e, f).

We calculated percentages of endocrine cell types positive for each TF of interest as well as combinations of multiple TFs (Figs. 4g and S8–S13). As expected, ARX was detected more frequently in α, ε, and γ cells than in β or δ cells, while PDX1 was detected in more β cells, though also in a considerable number of δ, ε, and γ cells compared to α (Fig. S8B and F). ISL1 and PAX6 were consistently detected in most cells of all endocrine subtypes (Fig. S8C and E). NKX2-2 was the only TF with evidence of age-related trends; the percentage of positive cells appeared to decline from neonatal to childhood stages across all types (Fig. S8D).

Progressively more β cells were located near the islet periphery in childhood compared to infancy and neonatal stages

In addition to proportional changes in islet-cell composition during pancreas growth, spatial alterations were also apparent. To evaluate endocrine-cell composition from the islet periphery to deeper in the islet core, we applied an algorithm that accounted for variability in islet size and shape. Normalized distances (ND) were calculated for each cell within an islet to that islet’s boundary, such that outer (peripheral) cells had values closer to 0 and inner (core) cells had values closer to 1 (Fig. 5a). Just after birth, β cells were found both near the islet boundary (ND 0–0.3) and deeper in the islet core, with more β cells in the core (ND 0.8–1) (Fig. 5b). Infancy and childhood islets had significantly higher percentages of β cells localized near the islet boundary (ND 0–0.3) (Fig. 5b, c), presumably due to the higher overall β-cell proportions compared to neonatal islets (Fig. 4b). There were significantly fewer α cells relative to total α, β, and δ cells, specifically at ND 0.2–0.3, and significantly fewer δ cells at ND 0–0.3 (Fig. 5b). To mitigate the influence of shifting cell proportions, we also considered the distribution of each endocrine subtype independently. Significantly more of the α-cell and β-cell subtypes and significantly less of the δ-cell subtype were found in the islet boundary region (ND 0–0.3) in childhood compared to neonatal islets (Fig. S9A–C). Together, these results corroborate qualitative observations of more mixed-cell-positioning in islets after the first two years of life (Fig. 3a’).

Maturation of glucagon secretion occurred later than insulin secretion

Islet isolation from pediatric pancreas donors expanded on previously published work56,57 and incorporated extensive prior experience in isolating islets from organ donors with type 1 diabetes58, type 2 diabetes59, and cystic fibrosis-related diabetes60. This allowed us to optimize islet isolation and tissue processing of young human pancreata with the aim of consistently obtaining fractions of free, non-mantled islets for islet functional analysis. We performed dynamic perifusion experiments on islets isolated from donors in infancy (n = 8; ages 2–19 months for functional studies) and childhood (n = 20; ages 2–10 years) stages and compared the responses to islets from adult donors (n = 14; ages 24–59 years) isolated at the same islet isolation facility (Figs. 5d–i and S10 and Supplementary Data 3A).

To comprehensively analyze insulin and glucagon secretory response in the presence of different stimuli, we employed an alternate linear mixed-effects model61 that accounts for underlying temporal correlations and demonstrated global statistically significant differences in secretory trajectories across the three age groups of infancy, childhood, and adulthood (see “Statistical Methods” for details). Subsequent binary comparisons were performed between trajectories for each pair of age groups when secretion was normalized to islet volume or insulin content (Supplementary Data 3B). Due to the limited number of donors per group, we also analyzed differences at each time point (plotted in Figs. 5d, g and S10B and F).

Basal insulin secretion was significantly lower in childhood compared to adult islets when normalized to islet equivalents (IEQ) (Fig. 5d, e), but did not reach statistical significance when secretion was normalized to insulin content (Fig. S10B, C). Insulin secretory response normalized to IEQ was also lower in childhood compared to adult islets at specific timepoints in response to stimuli, specifically KCl depolarization (Fig. 5d). However, when analyzed by area under the curve (AUC)—representing total insulin secreted in response to a given stimulus—there were no significant differences between age groups under glucose-stimulated, cAMP-potentiated, or KCl depolarization-mediated conditions (Fig. 5f). Comparison of insulin trajectories by a linear mixed-effect model showed a significant difference in insulin secretion normalized to content in childhood compared to infancy islets, but no significant difference in global or other grouped comparisons normalized to IEQ or insulin content (see Supplementary Data 3B).

Notably, glucagon secretory trajectories analyzed by a linear mixed-effects model revealed statistically significant differences globally and in comparing infancy, childhood, and adult stages (see Supplementary Data 3B), with more glucagon secreted by childhood compared to infancy islets at every time point and by adult compared to childhood islets at most time points when normalized to IEQ (Figs. 5g and S10F). Basal glucagon secretion normalized to IEQ was significantly greater in childhood and adult islets compared to infancy (Fig. 5g, h), and low glucose/epinephrine-stimulated glucagon measured by AUC was significantly greater in childhood and adult islets compared to infancy (Fig. 5i). However, these differences were not discernible when normalizing glucagon secretion to glucagon content (Fig. S10E–H and Supplementary Data 3B). No significant differences were observed in cAMP-potentiated glucagon secretion or high glucose-mediated glucagon suppression by AUC (Figs. 5i and S10H), though most individual time points showed more glucagon secretion by childhood and adult islets compared to infancy when normalized to IEQ (Fig. 5g).

Because of the substantial changes in morphological features across pediatric stages, we also considered whether any functional parameters in our study were associated with age as a continuous variable. There was no statistically significant correlation between islet hormone content and age; however, basal insulin and glucagon secretion normalized to IEQ were negatively and positively associated with age, respectively (Spearman correlation; r = −0.455, approximate p = 0.0148, insulin; r = 0.617, approximate p = 0.0006, glucagon) (Fig. S10K). Overall, these findings demonstrate differences in secretory profiles, particularly glucagon secretion, during postnatal development compared to the adult stage without age-related differences in hormone content, suggesting that functional maturation of α and β cells is not synchronous with each other or with hormone production.

Formation of intra-islet capillary network preceded islet innervation

Vascular endothelial cells have previously been observed adjacent to endocrine cells during prenatal development27,28,33,62,63. While neuronal fibers associate with larger vascular structures, they are reportedly absent in prenatal endocrine cell clusters5,26,29,64. This prenatal vascular pattern was seen during the early postnatal period, and the overall cross-sectional area occupied by CD31+ vascular structures was similar across neonatal, infancy, and childhood stages (Figs. 6a–d and S11A). At birth and in the first two years of life, the pattern of nerve fibers in islets was variable among donors (10–40% islets with interior synapsin+ fibers) compared to childhood, where most islets (> 60%) had interior nerve fibers defined by synapsin I/II production (Figs. 6e–h and S11B). Thus, human islet innervation occurs later than islet vascularization, similar to conclusions made from studies in mice32, although adult human islets are comparatively less vascularized and innervated, while human acinar tissue has higher nerve fiber density65.

With the ability to track more cell types and phenotypes using CODEX, we next compared our traditional IHC findings with those obtained by multiplex imaging. During the neonatal period, intrapancreatic ganglia producing MCAM and laminin (as detected by LAMA2) were visible between epithelial clusters (Fig. 6i) and adjacent to vascular structures (Fig. 6j), while MCAM+ synaptophysin (SYP)+ fibers were also visible directly adjacent to laminin-producing epithelium (Fig. 6j)29,66–69. Endothelial cells were observed both between and around the epithelium and within developing islets and endocrine clusters, including in immediate proximity to MCAM+ pericytes (Fig. 6k). Although neuronal fibers were not captured in our cell annotations due to a lack of nuclear stain in the imaging plane, we analyzed endothelial (CD31+) and smooth muscle (ACTA2+) cells, as well as pericytes (MCAM + ACTA2−), across neonatal, infancy, and childhood stages (Fig. 6l). Endothelial cell abundance was significantly correlated with endocrine cell and pericyte abundance (Fig. S11C, D), but in this smaller cohort of n = 17 total donors, endothelial cell and pericyte abundance were significantly lower in childhood compared to neonatal pancreas (Fig. 6m). This result contrasts our findings from traditional IHC and may be due to individual donor variability but could also be a result of detection methods (counts of cells identified by nuclei in CODEX versus total endothelial area detected in traditional IHC), which is supported by consistent CD31+ area quantified from CODEX images across all age groups when considering islets, clusters, or both combined (Fig. S11E).

Since the glycoprotein CD34, commonly used as a stem-cell biomarker, is produced by some vascular endothelial cells, interstitial cells, and the ECM, this marker was also considered. Total cluster and/or islet CD34+ area did not differ among age groups (Fig. S11E), nor did the percentage of CD31+ area that was also CD34+ (Fig. S11F). Finally, to increase power, we pooled CODEX data across all age groups and found that, compared to clusters, islets had both greater CD31+ area and CD34+ area (Fig. S11G–I), perhaps suggesting that vasculature or basement membrane structures shift in phenotype once in the presence of sufficient endocrine cells.

Macrophages and T lymphocytes were present throughout postnatal pancreas development

Our CODEX panel enabled annotation and visualization of macrophages, monocytes/dendritic cells (DCs), and T cells, with a fourth group defined as “other immune” (CD45+ but not resolved in one of the other subtypes) (Fig. 7a–c). Though the percentage of total immune cells among all cells varied considerably among donors (mean 10.44, standard deviation 5.726), relative proportions of immune cell subtypes were consistent with macrophages accounting for approximately two-thirds of immune cells (Fig. 7b). There was no statistically significant difference in the proportions of any immune cell subtype between age groups, with monocytes/DCs, other immune cells, and T cells representing means of 17, 10, and 6% of total immune cells, respectively (Fig. 7b). There was also no statistically significant difference in CD4 + T cells between age groups (Fig. 7d, e). Regarding macrophage phenotypes, the most abundantly expressed marker was CD163, with robust populations of CD163 + HLA-DR+ macrophages across all age groups (Fig. 7f, g).

To determine whether immune cells were evenly distributed throughout the pancreas, we calculated the proximity of various endocrine cell subtypes to either macrophages specifically (IBA1+) or to immune cells generally (CD45+). Because endocrine cell arrangement in neonatal islets was distinct from that of infancy and childhood, we kept neonatal cells separate and combined infancy and childhood for more power. The proportion of neonatal proto-endocrine cells close to (within 15 µm of) immune cells was consistent whether the proto-endocrine cells were in clusters or in islets (Fig. 7h–j). However, proto-endocrine cells in infancy/childhood were close to immune cells more frequently when they resided in clusters compared to islets (Fig. 7h–j). This contrasted with differentiated (hormone+) endocrine cells proximal to macrophages, which showed no age-related differences in two independent cohorts (Fig. S12A). When all ages were combined, there was a higher percentage of hormone+ cells proximal to macrophages in clusters compared to islets (Fig. S12B), raising the possibility that immune cells may interact with developing and mature endocrine cells in smaller clusters.

Finally, we considered islets as individual units and calculated the percentage of cells within each islet positive for major cell-specific markers. We applied principal component analysis (PCA) to assess the variance in islet features. Islets from each age group clustered together, with islets from individual donors intermingled; interestingly, the childhood islets cluster appeared to bridge the neonatal and infancy clusters (Figs. 7k and S13A). Separation by PC1 was predominantly driven by percent CPEP, GCG, and SST+ cells along with other endocrine markers, while PC2 was heavily weighted by percent CD45 and CD31+ cells and reflected their high correlation (r = 0.697, p < 0.001) with one another (Figs. 7l, m, S13B–F, and S14). Together, this data emphasized that immune cell presence in islets during postnatal development is a major contributor to islet heterogeneity despite relatively consistent proportions among age groups.

Discussion

Human pancreatic endocrine cell differentiation and islet maturation form the basis for islet-cell mass and function, which are crucial for normal glucose homeostasis. These dynamic processes begin in utero and continue into early adulthood, but remain incompletely defined, despite likely contributing to the lifetime risk for all forms of diabetes. Our current understanding is primarily extrapolated from rodent data, which does not sufficiently mimic human development, or from small-sample-size studies related to challenges in acquiring human tissues in this age range. To address these knowledge gaps, this work mapped morphological and spatiotemporal changes in the human pancreas from an unprecedented cohort of 123 organ donors without diabetes, spanning from birth through the first ten years of life. Using quantitative analysis of human islet cells, we identified key steps, insights, and timelines for islet cell development and outlined a comprehensive summary bridging prior work on the prenatal period with our postnatal observations (Fig. 8). We also assessed dynamic islet function using isolated islet preparations from pediatric organs and identified distinct timelines for the maturation of insulin and glucagon-secretory profiles.

Proposed model describing prenatal and postnatal islet formation.

Based on data from earlier studies1,3,10,13,16,18, at the onset of 2nd trimester during the prenatal period, most endocrine cells originated from intra-tip and tip-trunk interface. Small clusters of endocrine cells, including PP-rich regions, were found in the core of pancreatic lobes, and nerves were more abundant in the exocrine tissue. Based on the data from this study, the neonatal stage was marked by high endocrine cell proliferation and rapid expansion of exocrine tissue, with elevated proliferation sustained during infancy. At this time islets exhibited a core-mantle structure reminiscent of mouse islets and clusters were most abundant. During infancy to childhood stages, endocrine cells underwent extensive rearrangement to resemble adult human islets, and endocrine cell proliferation rapidly declined. By this time, islet innervation was achieved. Islet vasculature and immune cell presence remained consistent throughout postnatal development.

Variations in human pancreas size, islet structure, and islet cell composition appeared early in life

In our study of human pancreata from over 100 donors under the age of ten, identified through a national network and processed promptly after death using standardized methods, we discovered that variation in pancreas size among nondiabetic individuals begins as early as the neonatal period and continues during the first months of life. Notably, pancreas weight can differ nearly fourfold at birth. As expected, pancreas weight increased with age and correlated with body weight. These findings strongly suggest that pancreas weight, a potential biomarker for increased risk of type 1 diabetes, is primarily determined prenatally by the intrauterine environment and genetics. A role for genetics in determining pancreas size was supported by a recent genome-wide association study that identified loci correlating with pancreas size70.

We also found variable endocrine cell abundance among donors at all stages of postnatal pancreas development, suggesting that endocrine cell lineage allocation varies among individuals and is defined early in prenatal development. A similar variation in islet cell composition has been noted in adult human islets7,71. Despite this variability, the relative proportions of α, β, and δ cells seemed to reach equilibrium quickly after birth, with β cells predominating over all other endocrine cell populations by three months of age. These results underscore previous studies showing that relative δ cell abundance decreases concomitantly with increased β cell abundance5,72,73. Furthermore, the arrangement of endocrine cells within an islet was also dynamic, with islets in the first two years of life having a core of β cells and a mantle of α and δ cells reminiscent of rodent islets, which has also been observed in prenatal human islets, prior to transitioning to the intermingled cell arrangement seen in adult human islets19.

We also stratified the endocrine cell distribution by endocrine cell clusters (diameter <50 μm) and islets (diameter ≥ 50 μm) and demonstrated that the majority of endocrine cells (60–80%, depending on postnatal stage) are localized to islets. Clusters were more predominant in the neonatal pancreas with larger islet sizes in infancy and childhood. In addition, we found that in the neonatal pancreas, clusters were significantly enriched for β cells compared to infancy and childhood. These observations provide critical developmental context for recent studies that have highlighted the importance of better characterizing the full spectrum of islet sizes, including the role of small endocrine objects or clusters that have not previously been considered74–76. Notably, in studies of pancreas tissue from individuals with type 1 diabetes, small endocrine clusters and islets were preferentially decreased, with larger islet sizes preserved, suggesting that these developmental changes may be critical to endocrine-cell susceptibility in type 1 diabetes74,76,77. Though immune cells are less frequent than in type 1 diabetes24, we found that macrophages and T lymphocytes are present during all developmental stages. Additional studies are needed to identify the mechanisms underlying these developmental changes in islet cell neighborhoods and their intersection with type 1 diabetes pathogenesis.

Human islet cell proliferation varied with age, islet cell type, and among individuals

Postnatal endocrine cell development is characterized by rapidly declining proliferation and an increase in β cell proportion1,3,5,21,22. We extend these prior observations, some of which were fairly limited in sample size; mean β-cell proliferation rates in our large cohort remained well below the frequently cited 2% (1.3 and 1.2% in neonatal and infancy stages, respectively)5. There was, however, greater α-cell proliferation during the neonatal stage with considerable variation among individual donors. These findings suggest that variation in adult β-cell mass has its roots in this early phase of life and that mechanisms other than cellular replication contribute to β-cell mass postnatally. Postnatal β-cell frequencies were low, suggesting that limited proliferation observed in adult β cells is acquired during the first decade of life. The drivers of this process are largely unknown but may involve ligands such as endocannabinoids, noncanonical Notch signaling, and semaphorin–neuropilin pathways78–80.

Postnatal human endocrine-cell neogenesis

Identification of presumed MPCs—cells co-producing PTF1A and NKX6.1 that are localized to epithelial “tip regions”—in human postnatal pancreas suggests that endocrine cells are generated by both neogenesis and proliferation, but likely at different frequencies across the α, β, and δ cell lineages. MPCs have been of great interest since the initial observation that early progenitor-cell proliferation preceded β-cell differentiation in grafted human embryonic pancreas11, followed by the discovery that single islets contained β cells derived from multiple progenitors81. These discoveries informed the development of stem-cell differentiation protocols that have enabled the analysis of chromatin, RNA, and proteomic signatures as well as the modulation of external factors that may affect endocrine cell allocation82–85. Since then, MPC-associated markers have been identified44,86,87 and studied to reconstruct transcriptome-based cell differentiation trajectories62,85,88–90. While spatial resolution and donor number were limited, single-cell RNA-sequencing combined with spatial transcriptomics in the prenatal human pancreas identified several novel genes in these early endocrine-cell clusters and surrounding microenvironment that may play a role in directing endocrine cell differentiation90. We also identified PAX6+ hormone-negative proto-endocrine cells, which were significantly more abundant at the neonatal stage, and characterized differences in lineage-specific transcription factor expression in these cells. Our results suggest that these endocrine-cell differentiation processes are highly relevant even well after birth and thus may be amenable to ameliorative modulation or intervention. Immune cells were also found at all postnatal stages, with predominance of macrophages, which is notable given that human prenatal pancreas-derived CD163+ macrophages have been shown to support endocrine cell differentiation in an in vitro model23. Further studies of MPC-associated markers and genes, such as NEUROG3, along with the identification of additional regulators of prenatal and postnatal endocrine cell differentiation, are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying this process.

Postnatal islet vascularization and innervation

Similar to observations in mouse studies32, the vascularization and innervation of islets in humans are not synchronized during development. While human islets are vascularized at birth, innervation occurs later than vascularization. Interestingly, adult human islets are generally less vascularized and less innervated compared to mouse islets65. In contrast, human acinar tissue exhibits a higher density of nerve fibers, indicating species-specific differences in the neurovascular patterning of the exocrine pancreas compartment and islets29,66–69. Additionally, because human islets have fewer autonomous nerve terminals, paracrine signaling may play a more significant role in cell-cell communication in human islets compared to mouse islets.

Despite islets being vascularized at birth, we observed decreases in overall pancreatic endothelial cell and pericyte abundance postnatally, from neonatal to childhood, in addition to increased vasculature and potentially basement membrane structures associated with islets as opposed to small endocrine clusters. This suggests that vascular and ECM phenotypes are dynamic during this postnatal developmental period, which may be key to establishing islet and acinar-specific endothelial cell phenotypes observed in the adult pancreas91. Additional studies are needed to define the role these vascular changes may have in concurrent endocrine cell and islet morphological changes.

Morphological and functional islet cell maturation were asynchronous

To complement immunohistochemistry-based imaging of developmental processes across pancreatic tissue, we examined the function of isolated islets during infancy and childhood. Insulin secretory responses were more similar between infancy and childhood compared to adult islets, though with subtle shifts in magnitude and baseline secretory response. This insulin secretory data is consistent with previous studies: a study of postnatal pediatric islets (< 10 years of age) normalized to IEQ appeared to have a lower basal secretory response, and a study of infants/toddlers (11–36 months) showed lower basal secretion rates compared to adults. However, these donor islets were not stratified into infancy and childhood categories due to a small sample sizes8,57. The appearance of comparable high glucose-stimulated glucagon inhibition during infancy and childhood, in contrast to epinephrine-stimulated and KCl-mediated glucagon secretory responses, which are significantly reduced during infancy, suggests that counterregulatory responses may be acquired in α cells before the complete maturation of glucagon secretory machinery. These data, along with our observation of later innervation of islets, suggest that it takes several years after birth for α cells to achieve functional maturation for regulated glucagon secretion. In contrast, β cells are responsive to diverse stimuli much earlier. The lack of significant differences in insulin and glucagon content between age groups indicates that functional maturation is likely mediated by factors different from those regulating hormone production. Future studies are warranted to identify the mechanisms contributing to functional immaturity and its progression in young human islets.

Proposed model of dynamic changes throughout prenatal and postnatal islet development

Data in this study support a model of tightly regulated islet formation and endocrine cell maturation spanning the postnatal period of human pancreatic development (Fig. 8). Connecting this data to prior studies in the prenatal pancreas suggests that by early in the second trimester (G13-18w), endocrine cells are forming within the epithelium at the intra-tip and tip-trunk interface. These small clusters of endocrine cells are scattered throughout the developing pancreas, closely associated with blood vessels but away from nerve fibers, which are more abundant in the exocrine tissue3,13–16,18. Due to relatively high endocrine cell proliferation in the neonatal stage, endocrine cells are abundant in the first months after birth, and some α, β, and δ cell proliferation is sustained. However, the rapidly expanding exocrine tissue results in a precipitous decline in overall endocrine cell density despite ongoing endocrine cell expansion, as small clusters of endocrine cells become less prominent and islet size increases. Furthermore, the neonatal and early infancy stage islets exhibit a core-mantle architecture reminiscent of rodent islets. Islets become innervated with nerve fibers months to two years postnatally, considerably later than they become vascularized. By childhood, endocrine cells have undergone extensive rearrangement (possibly because of other islet cell types such as fibroblasts, pericytes, and macrophages, and due to ingrowth of nerve fibers), and islet architecture is very similar to that of adult human islets with intermingled α, β, and δ cells51,92,93. At this point, endocrine cell proliferation is quite low, with β cells comprising the greatest endocrine cell proportion. In summary, the human pancreas is a dynamic organ in the first decade of life, indicating that pancreatic disease or dysfunction may have its origins in this time period.

Limitations

This report presented cross-sectional data; therefore, the conclusions should not be interpreted as a definitive longitudinal trajectory, either as strictly generalizable or as particularly applicable to any given individual. The use of in vitro cell-differentiation systems or other perturbation methods will be required to experimentally test the ideas and speculations based on our observations. Other studies of human β-cell development have similarly used human samples but mostly focused on the prenatal period3,13,16,21,87,94–97. An advantage of the current report is our harmonized experimental approach for quantifying events in postnatal pediatric samples across a large cohort, which supports the generalizability of the proposed model and timeline (Fig. 8). However, one constraint of working with tissue from organ donations is the unfortunately limited clinical information available. This constraint means that pre-collection events or exposures (e.g., cold ischemia time, nutritional status, in utero environment) may influence these observations, especially in very young donors.

Summary, future opportunities, and implications for human diabetes

This work connected postnatal endocrine cell development with key morphogenic events previously described during prenatal development, including endocrine cell birth, islet assembly, and the formation of islet neurovascular connections. Utilizing over 30 different markers, we examined islet architecture, endocrine cell type heterogeneity/abundance, endocrine cell proliferation, transcription factor expression, and islet innervation by immunohistochemistry and multiplexed protein imaging. These robust spatiotemporal datasets will serve as a useful reference for β-cell regeneration efforts and embryonic stem cell studies by establishing the timeline for human islet architectural and functional maturation. Furthermore, quantifying islet developmental changes after birth provides critical information to further study the causes and consequences of autoimmunity associated with type 1 diabetes, which often begins in infancy9,10. Indeed, it is tantalizing to speculate that the dynamic changes in human pancreatic islets in early life might initiate or accelerate this autoimmunity, representing a “susceptibility window” of some sort. In addition, defining the prenatal and postnatal factors that determine β-cell mass in adolescence and adulthood may identify targets for interventions to amplify or enhance β-cell mass across all forms of diabetes.

These findings also provide a framework for human pancreatic endocrine cell and islet development, enabling integration with emerging genetic and genomic data related to islet biology and diabetes risk. Studies mapping the 3D relationships among endocrine cells and epithelium, along with vascular, lymphatic, and neuronal structures, will be crucial to building a comprehensive molecular and morphologic catalog of human pancreas development. Together, these results provide a foundation for forthcoming studies using emerging technologies such as multiplex imaging, spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, lipidomics, and genomics to characterize and understand the phenotypic signatures, signaling mechanisms, and functional maturation of islet endocrine cells in the first decade of life.

Methods

Human postnatal pancreas procurement and processing

Pancreata from nondiabetic postnatal donors (Fig. S1A–C) were obtained through partnerships with the nPOD, the International Institute for Advancement of Medicine, National Disease Research Interchange, Integrated Islet Distribution Program (IIDP). The Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board declared that study on de-identified human pancreatic specimens does not qualify as human subject research. Donor demographic information is summarized in Supplementary Data 1. Specimens conserved for future experiments were transferred to nPOD and any sharing requests should be directed there.

Pancreata were received within 18–24 h from cold clamp and maintained in cold preservation solution on ice until processing. Upon receipt, pancreas was cleaned from connective tissue and fat, measured, and weighed, and then cross-sectional slices of pancreas with 2–3 mm thickness were obtained from the head, body and distal tail (Fig. 1a). For donors ≥1 year of age, pancreatic slices were further divided into four quadrants. Moving from head to tail, sequential tissue slices were processed by alternating protocols: light fixation and cryopreservation, formalin fixation and paraffin embedding, fixation for electron microscopy, and snap freezing with liquid nitrogen; methods are detailed at 10.17504/protocols.io.eq2lyp5nelx9/v2. Briefly, samples for cryopreservation were fixed in 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 2–3 h on ice with mild agitation, washed in four changes of 0.1 M PBS over 2 h, equilibrated in 30% sucrose/0.01 M PBS overnight and embedded in Tissue-Plus optimal cutting temperature compound (Fisher Scientific). Samples for paraffin embedding were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Electron Microscopy Sciences) overnight at 4 °C under mild agitation, washed in four changes of 0.1 M PBS over 3 h, and stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C to process for paraffin embedding. Samples for electron microscopy were manually minced in fixative (2% paraformaldehyde, 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer) warmed to room temperature, then incubated in fixative for 24–72 h at 4 °C under mild agitation. In certain cases, a modified procedure was used that enabled islet isolation and tissue collection from the same organ22,58,98,99.

Islet functional analysis

We utilized an islet phenotyping pipeline that has been applied across different disease states and to more than 500 nondiabetic adult islet preparations7. Islets from infancy (< 2 years for functional studies, n = 8; single 2 month old neonatal donor included with ≥3 months to <24 months infant donors), childhood (2–10 years, n = 20), and adult (24–59 years, n = 14) nondiabetic donors were studied in a dynamic cell perifusion system at a perifusate flow rate of 1 mL/min58. The effluent was collected at 3-min intervals using an automatic fraction collector, then islets were retrieved and lysed with acid-ethanol solution to extract. Insulin and/or glucagon concentrations in each perifusion fraction, as well as total hormone content, were measured by radioimmunoassay (human insulin, Millipore RI-13K; glucagon, Millipore GL-32K), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (human insulin, Mercodia 10-1132-01; glucagon, Mercodia 10-1281-01 and R&D DGCG0), or homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence assay (glucagon, Cisbio 62CGLPEH). Specific assays used are detailed in Supplementary Data 3A. Exclusion criteria were as follows: content-normalized traces were excluded if glucagon fell below the limit of detection in islet extracts or in the case of historic samples, if there was inadequate archived material to perform a more sensitive assay, as well as if part of the islet pellet was lost during retrieval. One content-normalized glucagon trace from the childhood group was excluded by an outlier test. Adult IEQ- or content-normalized traces were excluded if they fell outside two standard deviations of average traces obtained by over 500 adult nondiabetic islet preparations by the Human Islet Phenotyping Program of the IIDP7. AUC above baseline hormone release was calculated with the trapezoidal method in GraphPad Prism 8–10 as previously described in ref. 100, using the following time point boundaries: insulin 16.7 mM glucose, 9–60 min (1st phase 9–24 min); insulin 16.7 mM glucose + IBMX, 63–90 min; insulin 1.7 mM glucose + epinephrine, 90–114 min; insulin KCl, 120–150 min; glucagon 16.7 mM glucose, 12–51 min; glucagon 16.7 mM glucose + IBMX, 69–90 min; glucagon 1.7 mM glucose + epinephrine, 93–117 min; glucagon KCl, 120–144 min. In addition, hormone secretory trajectories were analyzed across different age groups—infancy, childhood, and adulthood—using linear mixed-effect models61 that take into consideration the underlying temporal correlation. In each model, restricted cubic splines were used to capture the nonlinear relationships between hormone secretory trajectories and time. The statistical significance (p < 0.05) of the interaction between the splines and group indicators, assessed through a likelihood ratio test, confirmed differences in hormone secretory trajectories among the three groups. A similar model was employed to compare hormone secretory trajectories between infancy and either childhood or adulthood, and between childhood and adulthood. The statistical significance of the interaction between the splines and a binary group indicator confirmed these differences.

Immunohistochemical staining and imaging

Traditional immunohistochemistry

Staining was performed on 5–8 μm pancreas cryosections from multiple blocks from head, body and tail regions as described previously101. Primary and secondary antibodies and their working dilutions are listed in Supplementary Data 4A, B. Digital images were acquired with a Zeiss LSM510 META laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss), ScanScope FL (Leica/Aperio), and DMI6000B fluorescence microscope equipped with a DFC360FX digital camera (Leica). Unedited digital images were deposited in the publicly available Pancreatlas™ platform102 with only reversible modification of brightness and/or contrast to improve signal visualization where needed. These full-resolution pancreas imaging data are available for interactive exploration, including the isolation of individual channels, at the following link: https://pancreatlas.org/datasets/531/explore. Unless otherwise specified, multiple pancreas regions were analyzed per donor and combined in the analysis.

Multiplex antibody-based imaging

CO-Detection by indEXing (CODEX) imaging of pancreas was performed on 10 µm cryosections from multiple blocks from head, body, and tail from 17 donors (see Supplementary Data 2B for detailed information about blocks used for each donor) per the manufacturer’s protocol using oligo-conjugated antibodies and reporters (see Supplementary Data 2A for detailed information about antibodies and reporters used) as previously described99. A total of 33 tissue sections across the 17 donors were captured (mean 200 mm2 tissue imaged per donor). Image alignment, stitching, background subtraction, and deconvolution were performed using the CODEX Processor v1.7.0.6 (Akoya Biosciences; see https://help.codex.bio/codex/processor/technical-notes for details). The resulting images were deposited to the publicly available Pancreatlas™ platform as described above.

Image analysis by fingerprinting pipeline

Image review and islet identification

Unless otherwise indicated, individual channel images (QPTIFF files for CODEX and AFI files for IHC) were imported into QuPath software v0.5.1 for all subsequent analyses. Channels with excessive background noise, poor staining quality, or low dynamic range were visually inspected and excluded prior to analysis. For CODEX imaging, the channels in each image that passed quality control are listed in Supplementary Data 2B. All channels analyzed in IHC images passed quality control. Tissue regions were detected using a DAPI threshold of 10.0 after applying a Gaussian prefilter (σ = 3.0). Out-of-focus or low-quality tissue regions were manually annotated and excluded in the downstream analysis. For CODEX images, islets and endocrine clusters were annotated using random-forest pixel classifiers trained on CHGA, PAX6, GCG, CPEP, and SST channels. For IHC images, the classifiers were trained with only hormones. The classifier was trained on a composite image assembled from randomly selected 500 × 500 μm cropped regions (one region per image). The minimum object area of the classifier output was set to 25 μm2, corresponding roughly to one cell. The diameter of each islet or cluster was defined as the average of its maximum and minimum diameters. Regions with a diameter ≥50 μm were classified as islets, whereas those <50 μm were classified as clusters. Cell segmentation for IHC and CODEX images was performed using the StarDist plugin of QuPath, with a fine-tuned model for pancreatic cells (probability threshold 0.3; cytoplasm expansion 1.5). Using the same training image as islet/cluster classifiers, random-forest object classifiers were trained for each marker based on ~100 manually annotated cells (approximately 50 positive and 50 negative). The individual classifiers were merged and applied to all 33 CODEX images and 109 IHC images analyzed, assigning a set of positive marker labels to each cell.

Cell segmentation and cell type annotation

We implemented a custom Python script to automatically annotate cell types from CODEX images (see Fig. S3), and this approach was also applied to IHC images. We first constructed a reference table describing marker-cell type associations. Each cell type was defined by two categories of markers: (1) Indicator markers, which are specific to a single cell type or group of related cell types and are not expressed in others (e.g., IBA1 for macrophages, PAX6 for endocrine cells). (2) Positive markers, which are generally expressed in a cell type but may also be present in others (e.g., CD11c in both dendritic and macrophage cells, SYP in neuronal cells). Cell types were organized hierarchically, such that each subtype inherited the indicator and positive markers of its parent type (e.g., “macrophage” inherits markers from “immune cell”). From this information, we generated a cell type-by-marker matrix, in which each row corresponds to a cell type and each column to a marker. The entries take values of 1, 0, or -1, representing the following: 1 is an indicator or positive marker for that cell type; −1 is an indicator marker for another cell type (mutually exclusive); 0 indicates no relevant information is available for that marker-cell type pair. This matrix served as the reference signature for downstream annotation. For each image, we extracted a submatrix from the reference table containing only the markers present in that dataset (denoted as Matrix 1). This accommodates differences in available markers among the CODEX images and IHC images.

For each image, we constructed a cell-by-marker matrix (Matrix 2) from QuPath object classification results. Each entry (1 or 0) indicates whether a marker is positive in a given cell. To correct for signal saturation and spillover inherent to fluorescence imaging, we adjusted these binary values using spatial information. Specifically, if a cell was positive for a given marker but had neighboring cells (centroid distance <15 µm) with higher intensity for that same marker, its score was reduced from 1.0 to 0.8, indicating reduced confidence in positivity. This adjustment effectively reduced “double-positive” assignments between mutually exclusive markers while keeping the identity of “single-positive” cells. Next, we performed a similarity search between individual cells and the reference signature. For each cell, we computed the cosine similarity between its marker profile (row of Matrix 2) and each cell type signature (row of Matrix 1). The cell was assigned the label of the cell type with the highest similarity. If two cell types shared the same highest similarity, the cell was labeled as “to be determined” (TBD), and both candidate types were stored. If all similarity scores were non-positive, the cell was labeled as “Not annotated”. Cells labeled TBD were further resolved using a likelihood-based refinement. For each cell type, we calculated the mean and standard deviation of marker intensities from determined cells of this type and modeled each cell type as a multivariate Gaussian distribution (dimensionality equal to the number of available markers). Each TBD cell was then evaluated against these distributions, and the likelihood of belonging to each candidate cell type was computed. The cell was finally assigned to the type with the highest likelihood score.

Advantages of this approach include the following: (1) Incorporation of expert knowledge. The pipeline integrates biologically curated marker relationships while remaining fully systematic, automated, and non-biased. Because the 33 CODEX images and IHC images contain different marker panels, a fixed decision tree is infeasible. Our matrix-based method dynamically adapts to the available markers for each image. For instance, if KRT19 (ductal marker) is missing, the “ductal cell” row in Matrix 1 contains only 0 or −1 values. Therefore, no cells would be assigned a positive similarity, and no “ductal cell” would be annotated. (2) Correction for imaging artifacts. The use of neighborhood-based intensity adjustment mitigates signal saturation and overspill between adjacent cells, reducing false double-positives (e.g., CPEP⁺/GCG⁺ cells). (3) Probabilistic resolution of ambiguous cells. The likelihood-based refinement step leverages intensity distributions from confidently annotated cells to resolve ambiguous cases, improving annotation robustness.

Downstream analyses

All downstream analysis of images annotated using this fingerprinting pipeline were performed with Python script that we made publicly available (https://github.com/Powers-Brissova-Research-Group/pediatric-pancreas-image-analysis). Information about donor, islet, cluster, and cell numbers for these analyses is included in corresponding figure legends.

Islet architecture analysis

The normalization algorithm for islet architecture analysis (see schematic in Fig. 5A) was conducted as follows: for all cells located within islets (diameter ≥ 50 µm), normalized distances (0–1) were calculated to indicate relative position from boundary to core. Specifically, each cell’s distance to the islet boundary was divided by the maximum distance observed in the same islet (i.e., the distance between the “core cell” to islet boundary in the schematic figure). Thus, values closer to 0 represent “near boundary”, while values closer to 1 represent “near core”. Normalized distances were stratified into 10 bins from 0 to 1 with 0.1 intervals (0–0.1, 0.1–0.2, etc.).

Non-fingerprint image analyses

Endocrine cell abundance and Ki67 positivity

Scanned IHC images were inspected for quality and manually annotated to exclude out-of-focus regions. Hormone and Ki67 positivity were quantified using a tuned cytonuclear v1.2 algorithm (Aperio). Endocrine cell mass was quantified by using pancreas weight and the ratio of hormone-positive cells as identified by cytonuclear logarithm within the entire pancreatic section from multiple blocks representing the head, body, and tail regions.

Postnatal endothelial cell abundance and islet innervation

Scanned IHC images were inspected for quality and manually annotated to exclude out-of-focus regions. Positivity for CD31 (endothelial cell marker) was quantified via tissue classifier using HALO imaging analysis software (Indica Labs). For innervation, islets (estimated diameter ≥50 μm) were annotated based on DAPI and hormone channels. The TUBB3 (neuronal marker) channel was then turned on and each islet was evaluated for whether there was TUBB3 signal ≥ 2 cell layers from the periphery of the islet (considered “interior nerve fibers”).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R software (version 4.5.0). Data are generally presented as mean ± standard error of the mean unless specified otherwise. Due to limited number of donors in each group, for comparisons involving two groups, the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to determine statistical significance. A p-value of less than 0.05, or an FDR-adjusted p-value for multiple comparisons, was deemed statistically significant. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to evaluate differences among groups. For groups exceeding 25 donors (e.g., Fig. S1E; see Supplementary Data 1 for information on donors included in each analysis), one-way ANOVA and t-tests followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were applied.

To assess the nonlinear relationship between pancreas weight or weight index and age, restricted cubic splines of the age variable were used as primary explanatory variables in linear regression models. The statistical significance of the spline terms (p-value < 0.05) was evaluated by comparing models with and without these terms using an F-test.

The differences in perfusion profiles—specifically, insulin and glucagon profiles normalized by internal/external glucose (IEG) or content—across three groups were assessed using a linear mixed-effects model incorporating donor-specific random intercepts. In these models, perfusion serves as the outcome variable, while interaction terms between natural splines of time and group indicators (infancy, childhood, adulthood) are the main explanatory variables. The statistical significance of these interaction terms, determined through an F-test, reveals differences in perfusion trajectories among the groups. For pairwise comparisons, such as infancy versus childhood, the interaction terms included only the two relevant groups, and their statistical significance was also validated via an F-test. The locations of knots for the natural splines were selected based on stimulus onset times. Specifically, knots were positioned at 18, 57, 69, 102, and 129 min for the insulin profile, and at 69, 78, 90, 102, 120, and 129 min for the glucagon profile, corresponding to the onset of stimulus effects (see Supplementary Data 3B). Given the limited number of donors per group, findings were also analyzed by testing differences in insulin and glucagon profiles among groups at each time point using Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, controlling for FDR at 0.05, and plotted in Figs. 5d, g and S10B and F.

Additional detailed statistical information for the experiments is provided in the figure legends and results text as appropriate. Replicate information for representative images in Figs. 2a–g, 4d, 6i–k, and 7c, as well as for all quantitative datasets, is available in Supplementary Data 1.
