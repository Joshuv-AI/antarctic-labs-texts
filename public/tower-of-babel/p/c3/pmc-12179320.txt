Opioid consumption in the first 30 days after surgery was independently associated with new persistent opioid use
Authors: Brittany A Ervin-Sikhondze, Vidhya Gunaseelan, Kao-Ping Chua, Mark C Bicket, Jennifer F Waljee, Michael J Englesbe, Chad M Brummett
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrevious studies suggest that new persistent opioid use (NPOU) after surgery was associated with larger perioperative opioid prescriptions, but the association between NPOU and postoperative opioid consumption is unknown.MethodsThis retrospective study included opioid naïve individuals aged 18–64 who underwent surgical procedures across 70 Michigan hospitals between July 1, 2018 and November 15, 2021 and were prescribed opioids at discharge. We used clinical and patient-reported opioid consumption data from the Michigan Surgical Quality Collaborative, a statewide surgical registry, linked with the state Prescription Drug Monitoring Program. Multivariable logistic regression modeling was used to assess the association between patient-reported opioid consumption during the 30 days after discharge and NPOU, defined as having an opioid fill during both 31–120 days and 121–210 days after discharge.ResultsAmong 36,271 patients included, 482 (1.3%) developed NPOU. These patients consumed more opioid pills in the first 30 days postoperatively than those without NPOU (mean (SD): 7.3 (8.4) 5 mg oxycodone equivalent pills vs 4.1 (5.5), SMD=−0.41). In adjusted analyses, each additional opioid pill consumed in the 30-day postoperative period was associated with a 0.05 percentage-point increase in the predicted probability of NPOU (95% CI 0.04 to 0.07 percentage points). Thus, holding all other variables constant, a 10-pill increase in consumption would be associated with a 0.5 percentage-point increase in the probability of NPOU, or a 38.4% increase relative to the baseline rate of 1.3%.ConclusionDemonstrating that opioid consumption in the first 30 days after surgery was independently associated with NPOU underscores the importance of perioperative opioid prescribing on long-term outcomes.Keywords: Opioids; Acute Pain; Postoperative Complications; Pain, Postoperative

Introduction

New persistent opioid use (NPOU), defined as the utilization of opioids longer than the expected time frame for acute pain in an opioid naïve individual,1 is a common postoperative complication, with ~1%–14% of opioid naïve surgical patients progressing to NPOU after commonly performed cardiac, spine, orthopedic, gynecologic, oncologic, colorectal, and/or gender-affirming surgeries.2,8 NPOU has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality9,12 and surgical complications.13,16 Given the prevalence of NPOU and its associated harms, characterization of the underlying risk factors leading to the development of NPOU may be further useful and may ultimately prompt a paradigm shift toward reconsidering initial opioid exposure risks (ie, further “upstream”) as a next step in addressing potential public health strategies.

Prior studies have suggested that the size of perioperative opioid prescriptions, the duration of these prescriptions, mental health and substance use disorders, payer type, and procedure type are key risk factors in the development of NPOU.17,21 Although Barth et al suggest NPOU is unrelated to the index surgery or opioid prescribing,22 several studies have demonstrated that prescription size is independently associated with NPOU, and rates of NPOU are much higher after surgical episodes with opioid prescribing when compared with non-surgical control cohorts.323,25 Despite the many previous studies of NPOU, we are unaware of any data evaluating the association between opioid consumption and NPOU. Closing this gap could provide insights into the mechanism of the association between the size of perioperative opioid prescriptions and NPOU while moving more promptly toward upstream efforts to decrease opioid exposure, where achievable.

The objective of this study was to assess the association between patient-reported opioid consumption in the first 30 days after surgery and the development of NPOU in an opioid-naïve surgical cohort. To achieve this objective, we analyzed data from a statewide surgical registry linked to a Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP) database in Michigan. We hypothesized that increased opioid consumption would be associated with a higher probability of developing NPOU after surgery.

Methods

The study protocol describing the aims, methods and analytical approach was preregistered at protocols.io (https://www.protocols.io/blind/1904184C6EE311EF911D0A58A9 FEAC02). The study is presented in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Data sources

This retrospective study included data from the Michigan Surgical Quality Collaborative (MSQC), a patient safety organization of patients undergoing general, vascular, and gynecologic surgery procedures from 70 participating hospitals in Michigan. The MSQC registry includes data on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and 30-day surgical outcomes. In addition, patient-reported opioid consumption data are collected using surveys via email, mail, and phone in the 31–90 days after surgery, as previously described.25

Data from MSQC were linked with prescription fulfillment data from the Michigan Automated Prescription System (MAPS) and the Michigan PDMP. The Michigan PDMP includes data on all controlled substances prescriptions filled in Michigan. The MSQC and PDMP datasets were linked using a process approved by the state licencing board using an honest broker to obfuscate patient-level identifiers.26 This linkage enabled us to obtain data on opioid prescriptions that were filled throughout Michigan across all payer types, including cash transactions.

Patient cohort

This study included opioid-naive (no filled prescription for opioids or medications for opioid use disorder in the 180 days to 1 day prior to admission for surgery) patients ages 18–64, who were admitted and discharged from surgery between July 1, 2018 and November 15, 2021. Patients were excluded if they were discharged between April 1, 2020 and August 31, 2020, to mitigate potential bias from the decrease in postoperative opioid dispensing during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic.27 Patients were also excluded if they had a length of stay longer than 14 days or had an invalid length of stay, were not discharged home, died within 30 days of surgery, had a reoperation within 30 days of surgery, were not residents of Michigan, were linked to more than one patient in MAPS, were not opioid-naive, had an invalid opioid prescription in MAPS in the 180 days prior to admission through to 365 days after discharge, did not receive an opioid prescription at discharge, or did not have complete and valid data for patient-reported consumption. Invalid prescriptions were defined as having days supplied <1, days supplied >90, or quantity=0.

Study variables

The exposure was the patient-reported number of pills consumed in the 30 days after surgical discharge. The patient-reported number of pills consumed was converted to total morphine milligram equivalents (MME) by multiplying strength per dose by quantity (as reported by the patient) and MME conversion factors provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s 2022 Clincal Practice Guidelines.28 The total MME was then standardized to 5 mg oxycodone pills (7.5 MME=1 pill of 5 mg oxycodone). People reporting no opioid use in the perioperative period were included. The outcome was NPOU, defined a priori as the occurrence of at least one opioid prescription fulfillment in the 31–120 days after discharge and at least one opioid prescription fulfillment in the 121–210 days after discharge from the PDMP data. Prior work from our group defined NPOU as having an opioid fill within 3 days of discharge, an opioid fill between 4 and 90 days after discharge, and an opioid fill between 91 and 180 days after discharge. The rationale for shifting the time points used for the primary outcome was to ensure that opioid prescriptions dispensed within 30 days were not also included in the definition of NPOU, as opioid consumption in the 30 days after discharge was the primary covariate of interest. We did, however, include the rate of refills in the 4–30 days after discharge from surgery in the descriptive data.

Additional covariates for adjustment included patient demographics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity categorized as white non-Hispanic, black non-Hispanic, Hispanic, other and unknown), insurance type (private, Medicare, Medicaid, and uninsured or other), tobacco use in the year prior to surgery, body mass index (underweight, healthy, overweight, obese and unknown), American Society of Anesthesiologists classification (1, 2, 3, and 4–5), surgical priority (elective vs emergent/urgent), inpatient versus outpatient status, and year of discharge from surgery.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic, clinical and surgical characteristics described above for patients who developed NPOU vs patients who did not develop NPOU. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to describe differences between the two groups because this quantity is not influenced by sample size, unlike p values. Characteristics were considered to be balanced if the absolute SMD was less than or equal to 0.2. Descriptive statistics were also calculated for discharge opioid prescriptions, refills, opioid fills in the 30 days after discharge and opioid consumption. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to evaluate the association between opioid consumption and NPOU, controlling for the covariates described above. The predicted probability of NPOU at different opioid consumption values was estimated. The average marginal effect of the exposure, or the change in predicted probability of NPOU for every additional opioid pill consumed, was also estimated.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the association of opioid consumption and NPOU by using three other definitions used in prior research: (1) as previously described, an additional opioid prescription fulfillment after an initial postoperative fulfillment between discharge and discharge +3 days or at least one fulfillment in the 4–90 days after discharge and at least one opioid prescription fulfillment in the 91–180 days after discharge1 17 26; (2) 10 opioid prescription fulfillments or >120 days supplied in the 365 days after discharge29 and (3) fulfillment of an opioid prescription in each of the four quarters (excluding any fill in the discharge to discharge +3 days) following discharge from surgery. In an additional sensitivity analysis, 30-day surgical outcomes (postoperative complications, emergency department (ED) visit, readmission) were included as covariates in the regression model. MSQC defined postoperative complications, readmissions and emergency department visits were previously described.30 In another sensitivity analysis, patients were excluded if they had postoperative complications, emergency department visits, or readmissions. In a final sensitivity analysis, conducted after study registration, patients who had not consumed any opioid pills (0 pills consumed) were excluded from the cohort.

All analyses used two-sided hypothesis tests with α=0.05. Analyses were conducted by using V.18.0/SE (StataCorp). Code and analyses conducted by study analyst (VG) were reviewed by an additional analyst within the study team (see the “Acknowledgements” section).

Compared with patients included in the sample, those excluded due to lack of patient-reported opioid consumption data were slightly older (mean (SD) age: 53.1 (16.4) vs 49.3 (16.3) and underwent surgery in different years. Otherwise, characteristics were similar (online supplemental file 1).

A total of 482 patients (1.3%) developed NPOU after surgery. Compared with those who did not develop NPOU, these NPOU patients had (1) lower rates of private insurance; (2) were more likely to have an emergency department visit within 30 days of surgery or a readmission within 30 days of surgery; (3) were more likely to be American Society for Anesthesiologists class three or higher; (4) were more likely to undergo inpatient surgery; and (5) were more likely to use tobacco (table 1). Discharge year was also found to be marginally different between the two groups. While there was no difference in the amount of opioid prescribed between these two groups, those with NPOU consumed more opioids (mean (SD): 7.3 (8.4) pills) than those without NPOU (mean (SD): 4.1 (5.5) pills; SMD −0.41). In the entire cohort, 1874 (5.2%) patients had refills in the first 30 days of discharge from surgery. The refill rate among patients with no NPOU was 4.9% (1757 patients) and among patients with NPOU was 24.3% (117 patients). Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of a statewide surgical registry linked to the Michigan PDMP database, opioid consumption in the first 30 days after surgery was independently associated with NPOU among opioid-naive adults undergoing general surgical procedures. Specifically, each additional opioid pill consumed was associated with a 0.05 percentage point increase in the predicted probability of developing NPOU, holding all other variables at their observed values. Thus, a 10-pill increase in consumption would be associated with a 0.5 percentage-point increase in the probability of NPOU, or a 38.4% increase relative to the baseline rate of 1.3%. This association persisted in multiple sensitivity analyses, including when using alternative definitions of NPOU and excluding those who reported no opioid use in the 30-day postoperative period.

This is the first large study to our knowledge to identify an association between postdischarge 30-day opioid consumption and NPOU. Given that prior studies have found an association between the size of perioperative opioid prescriptions and opioid consumption, it may seem logical to reason that interventions that seek to match perioperative opioid prescriptions to the amount patients need may be an effective approach toward reducing opioid consumption and hence NPOU. While we do not discount this possibility, we also acknowledge that it is not supported by the perhaps surprising finding that the size of postdischarge opioid prescriptions was virtually identical among patients with and without NPOU in this study. However, refill rates and total perioperative prescriptions in the first 30 days were higher in the NPOU group. While refills of opioids may be needed to address the acute and subacute pain after surgery, it is important to discuss the pain and opioid plan with the patient to aid in weaning and ensure appropriate use. In the event that opioid use persists, it is important for surgeons to work with primary care and pain physicians to discuss the appropriateness of the long-term opioids. Moreover, we believe our findings suggest that there may be interventions unrelated to discharge opioid prescribing that may be effective in reducing consumption, and it will be important to consider future interventions based on these factors.

Previous studies have suggested that NPOU may not be due to surgical prescribing or pain. Barth et al22 conducted a secondary analysis (n=221 patients) from a prospective clinical trial using a definition of a prescription of >60 day supply withing 3–12 months after surgery. They found most of the prescription fulfillment between 3 and 12 months after surgery were mainly due to new painful conditions or new surgeries, and only 2.3% of patients met criteria for persistent opioid use. Based on the PDMP data available to our group, we were not able to determine the indication for the subsequent opioid prescriptions; however, we did find a consistent association with the amount of opioid consumed in the first 30 days after surgery using multiple definitions for NPOU. Hence, we believe that regardless of the indications for the later prescriptions beyond the first 30 days, these acute care prescriptions and refills are clearly associated in a clinically meaningful way (table 2 and figure 2). The present study also addresses a critique that many of the studies of persistent opioid use leverage prescription fulfillment data but cannot confirm opioid consumption.31 We believe that demonstrating the independent association of self-reported opioid consumption in the first 30 days following surgery in a large, multi-institutional database addresses this concern. Furthermore, these data underscore the importance of addressing opioid weaning in patients who may have a more painful postoperative course.

Among the multiple potential mechanisms driving this association, the development of opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) and opioid tolerance may be considered. OIH represents a paradoxical phenomenon of opioids increasing sensitivity and worsening pain.32 While well described in preclinical studies, clinical examples of OIH are best described in people with opioid use disorder maintained on methadone and in certain cases of experimental pain testing.32 Most of the perioperative research on OIH has focused on intraoperative use of opioids (fentanyl, remifentanil and sufentanil). A meta-analysis of 1304 patients from 23 randomized controlled trial demonstrated opioid-induced sensitivity in the 2 hours to follow surgery; however, there were no differences at the 12-hour and 24-hour assessments, nor were there differences in the postoperative opioid consumption in the first day after surgery.33 Consistent with a review article discussing lack of rationale for opioid-free anesthesia,34 a recent large, single-center study found that intraoperative opioids were associated with lower rates of chronic pain at 3 months and long-term opioid prescribing at 6 months after surgery.35 Whereas the present analysis focused opioid consumption after discharge from surgery, future investigations need to examine the role of intraoperative and acute postoperative opioid administration and consider how intra-anesthetic exposures to less hyperalgesic opioids (eg, methadone,36 buprenorphine) may prevent exposure to usual “upstream” opioids (eg, remifentanil, fentanyl) as one way to potentially reduce the risk of NPOU.37 38 While additional trials of postdischarge prescribing are needed, it is possible that buprenorphine, tapentadol and dextromethorphan may be alternatives to our traditional mu-opioid agonists.39 40

Whereas some NPOU may be attributed to chronic post-surgical pain, opioid tolerance can develop with only a few days of use and may lead to inappropriate, long-term opioid use. In the case of opioid tolerance, acute discontinuation can precipitate withdrawal that can increase pain and discomfort, which is resolved with subsequent opioid consumption. Hence, patients can inappropriately attribute efficacy to the opioids due to the resolution of the withdrawal symptoms. While some studies have demonstrated the value of postdischarge weaning instructions,41 efforts are needed to better characterize the experiences of patients and evaluate weaning protocols after discharge to avoid withdrawal.42

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a novel process for merging statewide prescription fulfillment data from the PDMP database with a large, multi-institutional data abstracted data by trained professionals and patient-reported outcomes from a diverse group of hospitals across the state of Michigan. Additionally, the association between opioid consumption and NPOU was maintained in all sensitivity analyses.

There were limitations. First, we cannot exclude the possibility that the primary association of interest was driven by an unmeasured confounder that affects both opioid consumption and NPOU. Second, due to state initiatives that have reduced opioid prescribing and consumption in the state of Michigan at a faster rate than most other states,17 results obtained from this study may not generalize to other states with higher rates of opioid prescribing and consumption. Third, findings may not generalize to patients undergoing the general, gynecologic and vascular surgeries in the MSQC registry, such as orthopedic procedures. Fourth, the dataset did not allow for an assessment of the reason for the postoperative opioid prescribing. Lastly, the dataset does not include intraoperative opioid use for which studies assessing long-term outcomes are still limited.34 35 43

Conclusion

Our study shows that patient-reported opioid consumption in the 30 days after surgery was independently associated with NPOU. Future research could investigate interventions to avoid unnecessary prescriptions and refills while also supporting weaning to reduce NPOU.
