Physical activity and healthy nutrition campaign for primary school children in developing countries: implementation of the build our kids’ success (BOKS) program in Indonesia
Authors: Agus Mahendra, Hermawati Triwibowo, Placide Simbizi, Mickhael Rajagukguk, Anira, Saskia Rosita Indasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] This study aimed to evaluate the implementation and outcomes of the Build Our Kids’ Success (BOKS) program in Indonesia, conducted in urban Jakarta and rural East Nusa Tenggara (ENT). The program, funded by Sun Life, was carried out to enhance children’s physical activity levels and nutritional knowledge as part of an intervention to address inactivity among children.[Methods] A total of 382 primary school students, comprising 132 from urban and 250 from rural settings, participated in a 12-week BOKS program, consisting of nine core physical activities such as running, burpees, and jumping jacks, led by trained physical education teachers. Quantitative analysis was conducted to assess the proportion of children meeting the World Health Organization’s (WHO) physical activity recommendations, average daily minutes of physical activity, perceived environmental support, and nutrition knowledge. Data were collected pre- and post-intervention using adapted standardized instruments. Paired t-tests were conducted to determine significant changes.[Results] The results showed that the percentage of children meeting WHO physical activity recommendations significantly increased from 64.1% to 83.8%, accompanied by a rise in average daily physical activity from 107.09 minutes to 136.23 minutes. Improvements were observed in both settings, with urban schools showing greater gains in WHO adherence, while rural maintained higher baseline activity levels. Changes in perceived environmental support were not statistically significant, whereas nutrition knowledge reached statistical significance.[Conclusion] The BOKS program effectively improved physical activity levels among Indonesian primary school children in both urban and rural settings. These results supported the integration of structured physical activity and nutrition education into school curricula as a strategy to combat children’s inactivity and promote healthy lifestyles in low-resource settings.

INTRODUCTION

Physical inactivity is a significant global health issue, particularly among adolescents. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that children and adolescents aged 5-17 engage in at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) daily [1]. This recommendation specifically concerns MVPA duration and is distinct from measures of total daily physical activity commonly used in population surveys. However, a comprehensive analysis of 298 school-based surveys across 146 countries showed that approximately 81% of adolescents failed to meet the recommended threshold, with the highest inactivity rates recorded in the high-income Asia-Pacific region [2]. The global trend shows a critical gap in meeting basic physical activity needs, with long-term implications for public health systems.

The increasing trend is further intensified by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has led to a significant decrease in physical activity levels, particularly among boys and adolescents [3]. Numerous factors, such as gender, age, ethnicity, self-concept, social support, and environmental conditions, influence children’s participation in physical activity [4]. Although the burden of inactivity is highest in high-income countries, its impact is most profound in middle-income countries in terms of absolute numbers [5]. To effectively address this issue, comprehensive interventions are needed, ranging from improved surveillance of physical activity from early childhood to the promotion of active lifestyles through education and high access to safe spaces for movement [6,7]. The economic and health costs of physical inactivity show the urgency of scalable, evidence-based solutions [8], particularly in developing countries such as Indonesia.

As a lower-middle-income country in Southeast Asia, there is a significant increase in the prevalence of physical inactivity among children and adolescents in Indonesia. According to the Report Card from Active Healthy Kids Indonesia (AHKI) [9], Indonesia received an “F” grade in several critical indicators, including overall physical activity, organized sport, active play, and physical fitness. This evaluation suggests that approximately 84% of Indonesian children are not meeting the recommended physical activity levels [1]. With six out of ten indicators rated “F,” the data show an urgent need for stronger initiatives to promote physical activity across various settings in the country.

The broader health implications of physical inactivity in Indonesia are similar to the trend observed in several low- and middle-income countries. The consequences of physical inactivity among adolescents are severe and far-reaching, serving as the fourth leading risk factor for global mortality [11], which increases the tendency for non-communicable diseases, poor physical and cognitive function, weight gain, and mental ill-health [10]. Given the scale of the issue, promoting physical activity through evidence-based public health strategies holds the potential to deliver significant health and economic benefits for Indonesia [12].

Physical activity patterns differ between settlements, with rural children generally showing higher levels compared to their urban counterparts. Rural environments often provide more opportunities for natural physical activity and open spaces, leading to better cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, coordination, speed, and agility among rural children [13]. This is due to greater opportunities for outdoor activities and active transportation in rural environments [14]. However, some studies have found conflicting results, with urban children being more active in certain contexts. These differences are influenced by various factors such as safety, built environments, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle variations across the rural-urban continuum [15], indicating that both groups are at risk of health issues related to insufficient physical activity. Urban children face a higher risk of being overweight or obese [16], while rural children show an increasing prevalence of overweight [17]. The lack of physical activity in both groups is associated with a high risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic disorders [18,19]. Therefore, effective strategies are needed to increase physical activity and reduce sedentary behavior among children in both rural and urban settings [20].

To address the challenges, Wahana Visi Indonesia or World Vision Indonesia (WVI), in collaboration with World Vision Canada and Sun Life, implemented the Build Our Kids’ Success (BOKS) program in Indonesia. This initiative is consistent with the WHO’s Global Action Plan on Physical Activity (GAPPA), which advocates for the development of “active people” and “active environments” to combat global inactivity [21]. Adapted from its original Canadian framework, the BOKS program was contextualized to suit Indonesian primary school environments, to increase children’s physical activity, enhance nutritional awareness, and foster a culture of health within the community. The pilot implementation occurred in two contrasting provinces, urban Jakarta and rural East Nusa Tenggara (ENT), showing the program’s adaptability across different geographic and sociocultural contexts. The 12-week intervention integrated functional fitness activities, educational games, and nutrition sessions, all facilitated by trained physical education and home teachers. Moreover, the program emphasized sustainability through teacher capacity-building and community engagement strategies to ensure long-term impact and relevance.

Based on the description above, this study aims to evaluate the implementation and outcomes of the BOKS program in Indonesia. Specifically, it focuses on three key indicators, namely (1) the proportion of children meeting WHO physical activity recommendations, (2) the level of support from schools, communities, and families for physical activity, and (3) the improvement in children’s knowledge of healthy nutrition and lifestyle concepts. By examining the indicators, this study contributes evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of school-based physical activity interventions in low-resource settings, providing actionable insights for policymakers, educators, and health advocates striving to combat inactivity and poor nutrition among school-aged children in Indonesia.

METHODS

This study used a mixed-method design that combined quantitative and qualitative components to comprehensively evaluate the implementation and outcomes of the BOKS program in Indonesia. The quantitative component was used to measure the changes in physical activity levels, environmental support, and knowledge of healthy nutrition among students before and after program implementation. Simultaneously, the qualitative component explored the contextual, perceptual, and behavioral dimensions related to program implementation through interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs). This integrated method enabled a robust understanding of both measurable outcomes and lived experiences in the field.

Setting and Participants

This study was conducted in two geographically and socioeconomically distinct provinces in Indonesia, namely Jakarta, which represented an urban setting with relatively better access to educational and health resources, and ENT, a rural and underserved area with limited infrastructure and a higher prevalence of child malnutrition and inactivity. These provinces were strategically selected in collaboration with WVI, a humanitarian organization, to provide insight into the program’s applicability across varied contexts.

A total of 50 intervention schools were selected, consisting of 40 in ENT and 10 in Jakarta. A proportional sample of 30% (n = 15 schools) was selected from the intervention group using purposive sampling, with selection based on several criteria. These included prior partnership with WVI, willingness to integrate the BOKS program into the school schedule, and strong support from school administrators. Within each selected school, students from grades 3 to 5 were randomly selected to participate, focusing specifically on those enrolled in the BOKS program. A total of 382 students aged 9 to 12 years participated in this study, comprising 132 students from Jakarta and 250 from ENT.

In addition to student participants, the study included 16 physical education teachers, 8 school principals, and 12 parents or guardians. These adult participants were purposively selected based on their inclusion in the program’s planning or delivery, and the capacity to observe its impact at home or in the school environment. The inclusion of stakeholders ensured a multi-perspective evaluation of the program.

BOKS Program Intervention

BOKS program is an evidence-based initiative originally developed in Canada and adapted for the Indonesian context through a collaboration between WVI, World Vision Canada, and Sun Life. The intervention was implemented over 12 weeks.

The core of the program was the delivery of structured daily physical activity sessions integrated into the school schedule. These sessions included functional movement routines, aerobic exercises, and simple games that emphasized fun, safety, and inclusiveness. The BOKS curriculum consisted of nine core activities, such as running, burpees, and jumping, performed at moderate to vigorous intensity (60-85% HRmax). Each session lasted approximately 20-45 minutes and was delivered five times per week over a twelve-week period, using a combination of aerobic and calisthenic modalities. A detailed summary of the exercise components, including intensity, duration, frequency, and modality, is presented in Table 1. Activities were led by trained physical education teachers each morning before formal lessons began, and were complemented by weekly group-based activities that supported physical literacy, teamwork, and positive engagement with movement.

The lesson component of the BOKS program focused on strengthening students’ health and nutrition literacy. Lessons were integrated into classroom routines to build understanding of balanced meals, food groups, hydration, and the importance of regular physical activity. Based on structuring, these lessons were organized into four structured chapters, namely (1) an introduction to balanced meals, (2) identifying food groups and their benefits, (3) understanding water consumption and hydration, and (4) the role of physical activity in maintaining health. Each chapter included step-by-step activities, discussions, and reflection tasks designed to progressively develop students’ knowledge and engagement. These learning materials were adapted to be age-appropriate, culturally relevant, and consistent with the existing school curriculum. To support implementation, teachers received initial training and ongoing supervision, along with printed modules provided by WVI throughout the 12-week program.

These parental inclusion were educational components such as workshops and informational sessions to increase parents’ knowledge and skills related to nutrition, physical activity, and healthy behaviors. The program objectives were facilitated by indirect engagement through regular communication and home activity monitoring, recognizing parents as key environmental factors influencing children’s activity levels and outcomes.

Quantitative Measures

The instruments used in this study were adapted from those developed and applied by the BOKS Canada [22], which included Child-completed NIH-PROMIS surveys, the Physical Literacy Assessment for Youth - Self Assessment (PLAYself), the Physical Activity Questionnaire for Children (PAQ-C), and the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES). These tools are commonly used to assess various aspects of children’s development, including mental health, cognitive function, family and peer relationships, positive emotions, sleep quality, stress levels, and physical activity. For this study, only selected indicators were used, namely (1) the PAQ-C score to assess children’s physical activity levels, (2) average daily minutes of physical activity per child, (3) the percentage of children receiving support from schools, communities (including parents and peers) to be physically active, and (4) the percentage of children showing improved knowledge of nutrition and healthy lifestyle concepts. Although PAQ-C does not provide direct estimates of MVPA minutes, it produces a composite score (1-5) showing general physical activity levels across multiple contexts and intensities. Therefore, the total minutes of physical activity reported in this study represent overall daily activity, not MVPA duration.

Data were collected at two time points, namely before the intervention (baseline) and immediately after the 12-week program (post-test). Instruments used were structured questionnaires administered to students, covering three core areas, including physical activity levels, perceived environmental support, and knowledge of healthy nutrition. The questionnaires were pre-tested for clarity and contextual relevance and administered by trained enumerators in Bahasa Indonesia.

Qualitative Measures

To gain deeper insight into the experiences, perceptions, and contextual factors influencing program implementation, qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews and FGD. A total of eight individual interviews were conducted with school principals and physical education teachers. This sample size was considered appropriate for qualitative inquiry, which prioritized depth of information over numerical representativeness, and was enough after reaching thematic sufficiency. Participants were purposively selected based on their direct inclusion in BOKS implementation, and representation was balanced across Jakarta and ENT to capture urban-rural contextual differences relevant to the quantitative results. The interviews focused on themes directly related to the study’s indicators, including children’s engagement and motivation to participate in daily physical activity, perceived changes in activity levels during and outside school hours, support from schools and communities, as well as the integration of nutrition education within classroom routines.

A total of four FGDs were held with parents in both Jakarta and ENT, each consisting of three participants. These discussions explored parental observations of their children’s behavioral changes, including increased activity at home, application of nutrition messaegs, and overall enthusiasm toward movement-based learning. Furthermore, parents provided contextual explanations for differences in support and activity patterns, complementing the quantitative results. All interviews and FGDs were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia by trained facilitators, audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were translated into English for analysis and checked for fidelity by bilingual researchers.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0, while descriptive statistics were computed to summarize demographic variables and baseline characteristics. Paired-sample t-tests and McNemar’s test were used to compare pre- and post-intervention measures, as appropriate and statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Qualitative data were analyzed thematically using a combination of deductive and inductive coding. A codebook was developed based on the evaluation framework, which included domains such as program acceptability, barriers and enablers, perceived student outcomes, and community engagement. Subsequently, emerging themes were reviewed and refined through peer debriefing to ensure reliability. Triangulation between the perspectives of students, teachers, and parents enhanced the credibility of the results and supported the interpretation of quantitative results.

RESULTS

This study included students aged 8 to 12 years and older from urban and rural settings in Indonesia (Table 2). Pre-post assessment data from the BOKS Program showed significant improvements in two key indicators, namely the percentage of children meeting WHO Physical Activity recommendations and the average minutes of physical activity per day per child. Meanwhile, the percentage of children who were encouraged and given support from schools as well as communities, including those with high knowledge of nutritional and healthy living concepts, did not show statistically significant group-level changes (Table 3).

Table 3 shows the characteristics of all 382 participants. Based on the results, a significant majority were from ENT (65.4%), while 34.6% were from Jakarta. Gender distribution was nearly balanced between boys and girls.

Table 3 shows a summary of the pre-post indicators. The proportion of children meeting WHO physical activity recommendations increased significantly, rising from 64.1% to 83.8% (p = .000). This percentage was derived from PAQ-C cut-off scores ( ≥ 2.9), estimating the likelihood that children meet the recommended 60 minutes of MVPA per day. Therefore, these values should be interpreted as probabilistic estimates of MVPA compliance rather than direct MVPA minutes.

Consistent with PAQ-C scoring procedures, the indicator “average minutes of physical activity per day” showed total daily activity across all intensities, including light, moderate, and vigorous. These minutes also increased significantly, from 107.09 to 136.23 minutes (p = .000). Improvements were specifically pronounced in Jakarta, where average daily activity rose from 81.05 to 161.45 minutes, while increases in ENT were more moderate. Boys consistently reported higher activity levels than girls at both time points, although both genders showed significant increases. The changes in Figure 1 show clear improvements in total physical activity time and estimated adherence to WHO recommendations following program implementation.

Although physical activity indicators increased significantly, support for children to be physically active, including schools, families, peers, and communities, did not show a significant change (overall p = .221). However, qualitative patterns suggested that support remained high, with a slight decrease observed among Jakarta participants and girls. This slight decrease showed the potential effects where baseline support was already elevated, limiting the amount of measurable change detectable through the instrument.

Knowledge of nutrition and healthy living showed significant overall improvement, rising from 77.2% to 81.9% (p = .000), with consistent gains across both Jakarta and ENT, as well as boys and girls. Girls showed particularly meaningful gains in healthy living knowledge, complementing results indicating that boys were generally more physically active. The age distribution of participants ranged from 8 to 12 years (Figure 1), with the largest proportions consisting of 9-year-olds (30.4%) and 10-year-olds (31.7%). This variation played a significant role, as cognitive, motor, and behavioral differences could occur within narrow age gaps. Therefore, including the proportions strengthened the interpretation of subgroup analyses by clarifying the developmental context of the sample.

Although age-stratified statistical tests indicated no significant pre-post differences for any indicator (all p > .05), visual inspection of age trends offered a valuable perspective. As presented in Figure 1, changes across all four indicators by age group showed that patterns of improvement were consistent, with some age-specific nuances.

Figure 2 shows age-related differences across the four key indicators, although none of these age-stratified changes were statistically significant for WHO physical activity recommendations, daily physical activity minutes, perceived support, or nutrition knowledge (p = 0.214, p = 0.178, p = 0.221, p = 0.276, respectively). Panel A showed that the percentage of children meeting WHO recommendations increased across all ages, with the most significant visual improvements among 10- and 11-year-olds. Panel B showed consistent increases in daily physical activity minutes for all age groups, with the largest apparent gains occurring among 9-11-year-olds. Panel C indicated that perceived support for physical activity remained generally stable across ages, despite minor decreases in certain groups. Meanwhile, Panel D showed improvements in nutrition knowledge across all ages, with visually stronger gains among 9-year-olds and 12-year-olds. Based on the results, these patterns suggested that although statistical tests did not detect significant age-specific differences, the visual trends indicated broadly consistent improvements in physical activity and nutrition outcomes across age groups within the context of uniformly high baseline support for active behaviors.

DISCUSSION

Physical Activity Outcomes: Progress Toward WHO Recommendations

The primary objective of the BOKS program was to increase the number of children who met the WHO recommendation of engaging in at least 60 minutes of MVPA daily. The substantial increase in the proportion of children meeting the WHO’s recommendation of at least 60 minutes of MVPA daily, from 64.1% at baseline to 83.8% post-intervention. This shows the effectiveness of the BOKS program in promoting physical activity among Indonesian schoolchildren. The improvement is consistent with previous studies, showing the success of school-based physical activity interventions in increasing children’s participation and reducing sedentary behaviors [23-25]. The BOKS program, through its structured design, contributes to compliance with global recommendations, causing a significant increase in average physical activity time from 107.09 to 136.23 minutes per day.

Although both provinces received the same intervention, the improvement was larger in Jakarta than in ENT. The difference in results can be explained by contextual and environmental factors shown in previous studies, where rural children often begin with higher baseline physical activity due to greater opportunities for outdoor, unstructured play, and household chores [26], as well as lower sedentary time [27]. These conditions create a ceiling effect, leaving limited room for additional measurable increases after a structured intervention such as BOKS. In comparison, urban children typically have lower baseline activity levels and fewer natural opportunities for spontaneous movement, making structured programs more impactful in increasing overall activity [28]. Similarly, results have shown that activity patterns vary by region and season, with urban children reporting higher total physical activity when there is greater access to formal facilities and organized programs [29]. These mixed results suggest that the larger improvements observed in Jakarta show both lower starting activity levels and the program’s ability to fill existing gaps in structured physical activity opportunities, while rural children’s high baseline activity attenuated measurable gains. Furthermore, qualitative data from teachers in Jakarta suggested that the program’s variety and play-based learning components fostered greater student engagement, supporting results that school-based physical activity interventions can enhance children’s motivation, enjoyment, and psychological well-being [24,30].

“...children can learn while playing, and it is not boring; it can be varied,” / “…anak bisa belajar sambil bermain dan tidak membosankan bisa bervariasi,” (Teacher, Jakarta).

The BOKS implementation promoted a holistic method by embedding physical activity across the school day, within the formal PE classes as well as through classroom-based icebreakers and extracurricular events. These methods can positively impact physical fitness and cognitive functioning in children [31,32].

“Children also experience changes in terms of knowledge, behavior, and physical development. Because the activities are balanced between knowledge and physical activity. Through monitoring, we found that children’s knowledge (related to physical activities) increased, and feel proud because they are members of BOKS.” / “…Anak juga mengalami perubahan dari segi pengetahuan, tingkah laku, fisik, karena kegiatannya seimbang dengan aktivitas fisik, sehingga hasil pantauan kami selama ada kegiatan sangat menambah wawasan anak, dan kami merasa bangga karena anak kami tergabung di BOKS,” (Parent, ENT).

Moreover, the program promoted integration between formal instruction and informal learning moments. A teacher in Jakarta described how the BOKS curriculum complemented academic demands while supporting greater physical engagement:

“...usually there used to be more emphasis on calistung (reading, writing, counting) because of the demands of the parents. I collaborate with what BOKS has taught and what I learned from social media, so it provokes children to be more active (moving outside of Sport subject hours), and it makes children happy.” / “…biasanya dulu lebih ditekankan pada calistung (baca, tulis, hitung) karena tuntutan para orang tua. Saya mengkolaborasikan dengan apa yang sudah diajarkan BOKS dan yang saya pelajari dari media sosial, jadi memancing anak untuk lebih aktif (bergerak di luar jam mata pelajaran PJOK), jadi membuat anak senang,” (Teacher, Jakarta).

This method shows the theoretical principles of the health-based physical education model (HBPE), which emphasizes supporting knowledge and habits through structured, integrated, and reflective activities. HBPE promotes the integration of physical activity into academic classroom routines, allowing children to be more physically active during school hours [33]. The BOKS curriculum, through its contextualized game-based learning and life skill focus, such as respect, goal-setting, self-management, provided children with relevant tools for maintaining active as well as healthy behaviors in and out of school.

Parental and Community Support: Sustaining Active Lifestyles

The second outcome indicator assessed the support children received from their schools, families, and communities in engaging in physical activity. Quantitatively, this measure showed a slight decline, which was not statistically significant. Furthermore, it indicated continued support from families and teachers, which showed the contextual importance of social reinforcement in children’s behavior. Multiple studies have found positive associations between parental support and children’s MVPA [34-36]. Specific forms of parental support, such as providing transportation, encouragement, financial, and logistical support, are particularly effective in increasing children’s physical activity [35,37,38].

Parental narratives showed deliberate efforts to limit screen time and encourage active play:

“Because he often plays on his cell phone. I like to forbid him from playing on the phone for a long time so that he has the motivation to play outside the house...” / “Karena dia sering main hp (telepon seluler). Saya suka ngelarang main hp lama-lama, supaya dia punya motivasi main di luar rumah...” (Parent, Jakarta).

“...allowing them to do activities, as long as they are happy,” / “… mengizinkan mereka untuk melakukan aktivitas, asalkan mereka senang,” (Parent, ENT).

Although the BOKS program was primarily implemented in school settings, it also emphasized family inclusion through parent meetings and activity-sharing platforms. This mixed-approach aims to create a more enabling environment that supports behavior change both at school and at home. The integration is critical, as effective promotion of physical activity in children requires support not only within the school environment but also from families and communities. These perspectives are consistent with McKenzie’s assertion that school-based interventions must be complemented by supportive family and neighborhood contexts to sustain behavioral adoption.

Previous studies have shown that successful interventions often include multicomponent strategies such as integrating physical activity into the curriculum, providing playground equipment, offering cycle training, and creating allotments [39]. School-based programs have shown efficacy in improving physical activity levels and enhancing psychosocial health outcomes [40]. However, community- and home-based components remain underutilized despite their proven effectiveness [41]. Environmental features such as well-connected neighborhoods, higher population densities, and diverse accessible destinations are positively associated with children’s physical activity [42]. Long-term, multicomponent interventions that actively engage teachers and families tend to yield the most sustainable outcomes [43]. In this regard, the BOKS’s efforts to include parents represent an important step toward fostering a more holistic and sustainable physical activity culture among children.

Nutritional Knowledge: Incremental Gains and Pedagogical Opportunities

The final indicator analyzed the extent to which the BOKS program enhanced students’ understanding of nutrition and healthy lifestyles. Quantitative data showed an increase in knowledge of balanced nutrition, although this difference was not statistically significant. BOKS programs integrate nutrition education directly into physical education lessons and classroom activities, providing a comprehensive method that supports both physical activity and nutrition concepts simultaneously [44].

Qualitative results provided compelling evidence of behavioral changes. Teachers described how schools promoted healthy eating through practical interventions such as meal regulations, cooking activities, and garden-based learning. A teacher in Jakarta shared:

“In my school, we require parents to bring a balanced nutrition meal every Thursday, and then rules are made to reduce eating instant noodles too, in some cases, also in the canteen has been encouraged not to sell noodles (...) so far parents can cooperate.” / “Kalau di sekolah saya, kami mewajibkan orang tua membawakan bekal gizi seimbang setiap hari kamis. Lalu dibuat aturan untuk mengurangi makan mie instan juga, dalam beberapa kasus juga di kantin sudah digalakkan untuk tidak menjual mie … sejauh ini orang tua bisa diajak kerja sama,” (Teacher, Jakarta).

Another teacher from ENT explained:

“For the implementation of balanced nutrition, because in our school we have gymnastics activities, then after that we eat local food that is processed into healthy and nutritious food. Additionally, there is a practical test (for children) to make other foods such as compote, onde-onde (traditional cake from Indonesia) for healthy children’s snacks, and it is from local ingredients that are processed together,” / “Untuk implementasi gizi seimbang, karena di sekolah kami ada kegiatan senam, maka setelah itu kami memakan makanan lokal yang diolah menjadi makanan yang sehat dan bergizi. Selain itu, ada ujian praktik (kepada anak-anak) juga untuk membuat makanan lain seperti kolak, onde-onde untuk cemilan anak yang sehat, dan itu dari bahan lokal.

These activities are consistent with experiential learning models, emphasizing that knowledge is most effectively internalized through active, hands-on participation. Integrating physical activity with nutrition education, particularly when consistently embedded in school routines and supported by parental inclusion, promotes the holistic development of healthy behaviors in children. A multifaceted method that combines these two components has proven effective. For example, children who receive both physical activity and nutrition education show significantly greater improvements in knowledge scores compared to those administered only one or neither [45]. Engaging parents through take-home materials or facilitated communication on nutrition-related topics also supports lessons learned at school and extends their influence into the home environment [46,47].

Study Limitations

Although this study showed promising outcomes, several methodological limitations should be considered. The questionnaire, adapted from the original BOKS Canada version, consisted of 63 items, which proved cognitively demanding for primary school-aged children in Indonesia. Students from rural areas with lower literacy levels experienced difficulties in understanding and recalling time-specific data related to their physical activity routines. This shows the need to refine assessment instruments to be shorter, culturally contextualized, and age-appropriate. Furthermore, face-to-face data collection, while beneficial for ensuring comprehension and engagement, required considerable time and logistical coordination, particularly in schools with limited administrative capacity. Future studies should benefit from incorporating technology-based tools or observational assessments to enhance accuracy and ease of data collection, particularly in resource-limited settings.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study shows the effectiveness of the BOKS program in improving physical activity levels among school-aged children in both urban and rural settings in Indonesia. The program leads to a significant increase in the percentage of children meeting WHO physical activity recommendations and in the average duration of daily physical activity, particularly in urban Jakarta. These results confirm the potential of structured, school-based interventions to address the widespread issue of physical inactivity among Indonesian children. Furthermore, the results indicate that improvements in community and school support, as well as in children’s knowledge of nutrition and healthy living, are more moderate. This suggests that although the BOKS program effectively increases physical activity, broader behavior change and environmental support require longer-term or more intensive engagement strategies. The persistent disparity in physical activity levels between boys and girls, as well as rural and urban settings, underscores the need for more inclusive and context-specific methods. Ensuring equal opportunities for physical activity, regardless of gender or location, is essential for maximizing the program’s impact and fostering sustainable health behaviors. In line with the analysis, the BOKS program shows promise as a scalable intervention for promoting active lifestyles among Indonesian children. Policymakers and educators are encouraged to integrate similar school-based initiatives within national health and education strategies, with a focus on enhancing community involvement, gender equity, and sustained behavioral change. Moreover, future studies should explore the long-term effects of such programs and investigate ways to strengthen their influence on nutrition knowledge and community support systems.
