Dental, oral, and craniofacial (DOC) health has benefited from fundamental scientific discoveries over the past 50 years, particularly in the areas of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Advances in biomaterials, stem cell therapies, and molecular biology have generated a pipeline of promising technologies with the potential to restore function, esthetics, and long-term health in patients with congenital, traumatic, or disease-related tissue loss.1-3 These technologies, first discussed in the scientific literature in the 1980s, remain of considerable clinical interest today.4,5 Approximately 10% of the total annual budget of the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR), which amounts to approximately $40 million annually, has been invested in regenerative medicine,6 totaling nearly $1 billion since the inception of this field. Yet fewer than 25% of these scientific projects have generated new intellectual property in the form of patents, and only a handful have resulted in Investigational New Drug, Investigational Device Exemption, or 510(k) Premarket Notification filings with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).7

Because translation of technology is frequently not the goal from the outset of a project, adoption of these innovations from the laboratory to the clinic remains limited, particularly in light of regenerative technologies reaching the level of recommendation to be integrated into evidence-based clinical practice guidelines.8-10 Even with technologies more focused on translation and achieving regulatory approval, a central bottleneck lies in the gap between regulatory clearance or approval and the generation of rigorous, practice-changing clinical evidence.11,12 Although the FDA defines the criteria for market entry, these requirements typically fall short of ensuring comparative effectiveness or long-term outcomes in clinically relevant contexts.13-15 As a result, products may enter the market without sufficiently convincing clinicians, payers, or patients of their value, leading to limited broad adoption and underuse.

Beyond evidence gaps, cultural and behavioral factors within dental practice considerably influence whether new technologies are adopted at scale. Clinicians often rely on established workflows and procedural habits, creating inertia against adopting unfamiliar techniques, even when supported by means of emerging data.16 This resistance may be reinforced by means of perceived increases in chair time, training burden, or uncertainty regarding long-term outcomes. Patients also shape adoption patterns; skepticism about novel biomaterials or regenerative therapies, concerns about cost, and variable risk tolerance can weaken demand and dampen clinician enthusiasm.17 In addition, misalignment of incentives among providers, payers, and innovators, particularly when reimbursement structures fail to reward preventive or regenerative solutions, creates further friction for new technologies entering practice.18 Collectively, these cultural and economic dynamics underscore the need for translational strategies that engage clinicians and patients early, address workflow integration, and generate evidence that resonates with real-world decision making.

Meaningful clinical evidence generated in clinical practice settings is not simply a regulatory checkbox; it is the bedrock of adoption, reimbursement, and integration into clinical standards of care.19 This is especially true in cases in which emerging technologies compete with well-established standards of care or lower-cost alternatives. Without high-quality data from robust clinical studies specifically designed to establish their clinical usefulness, clinicians may view novel therapies as experimental, difficult to use, or not cost-effective, despite their FDA clearance or approval.18 In addition, in dentistry postmarket studies and surveillance are often limited.19

METHODS

We performed this narrative review using an iterative, integrative approach to synthesize the evidence and insights relevant to translational bottlenecks in DOC regenerative medicine. We examined peer-reviewed literature, regulatory documents, and clinical practice guidelines published from 1990 through 2025, with targeted searches in PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using terms related to dental regenerative medicine, clinical translation, FDA clearance, clinical adoption, practice-based research networks, and translational research infrastructure. Additional literature was identified through reference tracing of key articles and consensus documents.

To complement the literature review, we conducted a structured analysis of internal data from the NIDCR-funded Translational Resource Center (TRC) portfolio, including aggregated project-level metrics on funding, regulatory interactions, intellectual property generation, and follow-on commercialization activities. These data were reviewed descriptively to identify patterns in translational performance, common barriers encountered by investigators, and evidence gaps associated with limited clinical adoption.

For the evaluation of failed or limited-adoption technologies, products were selected on the basis of meeting the following inclusion criteria: FDA-cleared or approved for a DOC indication, availability of at least 1 pivotal clinical study, and documented evidence of limited market uptake or discontinuation. Information was extracted from peer-reviewed articles, regulatory filings, manufacturer communications, and market analyses.

Insights from multidisciplinary stakeholders, including clinicians, industry advisors, regulatory experts, and technology developers engaged with the TRC, were incorporated through ongoing programmatic review activities, expert working group discussions, and standardized translational readiness assessments. These perspectives informed the development of the right-to-left translational framework presented in our review and provided pragmatic context for recommendations.

Because we synthesized published data and TRC program metrics without involving human participants or identifiable data, institutional review board approval was not required.

ESTABLISHING THE TRANSLATIONAL RESOURCE CENTER

The TRC, part of the National Institutes of Health (NIH)/NIDCR Dental, Oral, and Craniofacial Tissue Regeneration Consortium, coordinates stakeholder pathways to drive innovations into clinical practice. The TRC is an interdisciplinary partnership among academic instutitions (including University of Michigan, University of Pittsburgh, and Harvard University), along with clinical, regulatory, commercial translation, and marketing experts from industry and clinical practice settings. The McGuire Institute, the only practice-based research network to have successfully supported several high-level FDA studies in oral regeneration, is also a strategic partner of the TRC. This collaborative effort has developed resources and strategies to guide the advancement of emerging regenerative technologies and therapeutics through preclinical studies into human clinical trials, commercialization, and, ultimately, broad clinical adoption (Figure 2). Applications are accepted from investigators and teams from US academic or nonprofit institutions as well as from start-up companies. These team projects have sufficiently matured through discovery research, with demonstrable in vivo proof-of-principle data. Generally, these technologies are expected to be within 2 years of submission to the FDA regulatory process for the initiation of a clinical trial or have a minimum Technology Readiness Level of 4.20 The projects are clearly directed toward achieving milestones that substantiate readiness for enabling both FDA submission and clinical adoption through commercialization pathways.

Structure overview of the Translational Resource Center integrated project management.7 Projects are evaluated for scientific, clinical, and commercial merit by interdisciplinary experts and advanced through phased derisking of technical, regulatory, and commercial factors, with early integration of licensing and partnership considerations. Projects may exit when ready for follow-on funding, licensing, or company formation, with the overarching goal of improving clinical trial success and adoption through early, team-based support.

To date, 109 Interdisciplinary Translational Projects have been reviewed, with 22 being awarded funding (20.2%) through 7 years. The TRC investment of $16.4 million has resulted in close to 60 follow-on grants and award recognitions, with follow-on funding totaling $52.4 million. This funding includes 14 Small Business Innovation Research and Small Business Technology Transfer awards, both nondilutive sources of funding from the US federal government, including the NIH, as well as private and external funding sources. This net investment in shepherding promising tissue engineering and regenerative medicine technologies to the clinical arena represents over 3 times return on investment on the initial funding provided by the TRC.21 Of the portfolio as of October 2025, more than 89% of projects have at least 1 issued or pending patent. In addition, 25 FDA interactions have occurred, including presubmission meetings, Initial Targeted Engagement for Regulatory Advice on Center for Biological Evaluation and Research and Center for Drug Evaluation and Research Products meetings, 510(k) premarket notifications, and Investigational Device Exemption submissions.

Project team members report that the TRC’s influence has led them to adopt strategic thinking and evolve into becoming outcome-focused, ensuring that every action supports clinical feasibility, usability, and market adoption. This often results in new product concepts and successful transitions from market need identification to advanced-stage feasibility testing. Specifically, team members attributed their efficiency to a purposeful and impactful experimental design and end point definition and to keeping product development closely aligned with clinical and market needs. Valuable connections with clinicians and end users result in a product development process, and targeted indications that are better aligned with clinical practice and patient needs. The TRC serves as an example of infrastructure that shepherds innovation from the academic environment to clinical adoption by means of thoughtfully surrounding innovative technologies with stage-specific resources and enabling milestone-driven results, consistently reinforcing the importance of early clinical input in experimental design and milestone mapping.

BEGIN WITH THE END IN MIND: INTEGRATING RIGHT-TO-LEFT TRANSLATIONAL STRATEGY TO DRIVE CLINICAL ADOPTION

Challenges of the traditional research pathway (left-to-right approach)

Traditional translational pathways in academic research often emphasize achieving FDA clearance as the central milestone, yet this regulatory success represents only 1 bar on the path to translational impact. Many academic projects focused on clinical product development are grounded in a linearly progressing translational pathway that drives progress from its inception (Figure 3). In this left-to-right approach, preclinical studies are often guided by means of FDA requirements, the clinical end points of studies designed with the end goal of clearance. However, for meaningful clinical impact, FDA success alone is usually insufficient; adoption depends on effective commercialization strategies that align with the needs of payers, providers, and patients. Critical considerations such as patient perception, provider education, workflow integration, pricing, and reimbursement often go unaddressed; yet these cultural and economic factors ultimately determine whether a technology becomes integrated into the standard of clinical care (Figure 1). These challenges are amplified by means of entrenched clinician practice patterns, patient-level skepticism toward unfamiliar regenerative therapies, and reimbursement structures that do not consistently align with innovation.16,17,22

Traditional vs right-to-left translational medicine paradigms. The traditional approach begins with initial discovery, identification of primary indication, and development of a proof of principle technology. After regulatory approval, the technology is introduced and adapted to clinicians and patients through education and marketing. The right-to-left development strategy begins with needs assessment, clinical indication discovery, and stakeholder surveys, using an iterative approach that leads to meaningful clinical end points by means of involving end users in the technology development and iteration. Although we emphasize that FDA approval alone is insufficient to guarantee a meaningful clinical impact, it is equally important to recognize that few technologies progress far enough to even reach the regulatory threshold. Clearance or approval should therefore be viewed as a necessary but not sufficient milestone on the translational pathway leading to clinical adoption. True success requires parallel attention to commercialization and adoption, both of which depend on third-party validation and support from payers, providers, and patients. Without alignment of these stakeholders, even well-designed technologies with robust regulatory packages may fail to achieve broad integration into clinical practice. Thus, translational success must be framed not only as achieving regulatory approval but also as overcoming the cultural, economic, and market barriers to adoption that determine whether a therapy reaches patients at scale (Figure 1).

Benefits of the right-to-left translational pathway

In contrast, the right-to-left model of translation begins with patient evidence from the clinical arena and works backward in a continuous, cyclical, and iterative process in which emerging technologies and therapeutics are designed with a clear objective of clinical adoption in mind.28,30,32 The right-to-left approach considers the key tenets required for clinical adoption, prioritizing efficacy thresholds for clinical settings that are necessary to convince clinicians, ensure patient perception, and establish economic benefit. This includes considerations of cost of goods and manufacturability early in the development process. In addition, information from clinical studies, including patient-reported outcomes, clinician-reported outcomes, and observer-reported outcomes, provides additional key data beyond primary end points (Figure 3).

Our analysis of promising technologies with limited clinical adoption revealed key evidence that may drive adoption, including patient selection and stratification, meaningful clinical end points and measurements, and prospective data on improved and durable patient outcomes as well as clinical usefulness for technologies actively used in the practice of dentistry (Table). Optimal cost of goods, packaging, and handling considerations also influence adoption in the dental market. Altogether, generation of this additional evidence is expected to both enhance the adoption of emerging technologies and refine the development hypothesis of new technologies by means of more specifically targeting key drivers of clinician adoption.

NEW PARADIGMS IN STUDY DESIGN

In the translation of emerging technologies from conception to the clinic, the design of supporting studies, ranging from preclinical proof-of-principle studies to large-scale clinical trials, must evolve beyond fulfilling minimum regulatory requirements.19 Although traditional preclinical and clinical research has centered on establishing safety and basic efficacy, these data often fall short of persuading practicing clinicians, patients, or payers of a technology’s value, limiting adoption as a part of standards of care.34,35 To truly accelerate adoption, studies must be designed to produce clinically meaningful evidence that is intuitive, visual, and directly applicable to dental practice.19 For this to occur, customer input from key opinion leaders (KOLs), clinicians, and patients is crucial.37,39 At the same time, reliance on input of KOLs carries the risk of bias or groupthink, underscoring the importance of incorporating diverse perspectives and balancing expert opinion with objective evidence generation.

The first opportunity to rethink study design lies at the preclinical stage (Figure 4). Although these studies represent the earliest stages of development, they generate data to support clinicians’ belief in the technology. Drawing clear, compelling, and clinically focused insights from rodent models or in vitro data may serve to enhance mechanistic understanding and satisfy this early believability, provided they are designed thoughtfully to avoid requiring complex extrapolation. Although often not required by regulatory agencies, large-animal models, such as canine or porcine models, offer anatomic and biological relevance that allows for direct, intuitive interpretation of outcomes, including defect fill, tissue integration, and clinical healing.41,44,45 However, directives from the FDA and NIH have emphasized a shift away from animal models, encouraging the use of human-relevant, nonanimal systems such as organoids, microphysiological systems, and computational modeling.46,47 Although this movement is primarily rooted in ethical and translational concerns, it also presents an opportunity to align preclinical research more closely with human biology. For developers of regenerative technologies, the challenge is to design these alternative systems not only to meet regulatory expectations but to build clinical confidence by means of mimicking patient scenarios. When thoughtfully constructed, such platforms can generate human-specific data that clinicians find compelling, potentially even more so than traditional large-animal studies, because they offer mechanistic insight, predictability, and relevance without the noise of cross-species translation.

New paradigms in study design. A cyclical model for translational research emphasizing the central role of clinical need. Preclinical studies address US Food and Drug Administration requirements and establish proof of concept, and input from customers and key opinion leaders inform the design of clinical studies. Milestones and benchmarks highlight clinically meaningful outcomes beyond statistical significance and long-term clinical evidence ultimately drives clinician adoption of new technologies.

Likewise, statistical significance and clinical significance must be considered from the outset in study design and data interpretation.48,49 For example, a regenerative product may show a statistically significant 0.2-mm increase in bone fill, but this may have negligible clinical usefulness. Conversely, a product that achieves similar bone gain with one-half the surgical time, reduced postoperative morbidity, or improved patient satisfaction may be far more impactful in clinical decision making, even if P values hover near conventional thresholds.50 Finally, clinician-focused outcomes, such as avoiding the need for autogenous donor sites or reducing complications, may carry greater weight than numerical superiority alone and be more predictive of technology adoption. In clinical trials, phase 1 trials are generally designed to assess safety in healthy volunteers. However, it is both practical and reasonable to assess end points related to clinical efficacy at this early stage, for example, target engagement, changes in biomarkers or imaging metrics, or patient-reported outcomes.51 Study end points should be defined in collaboration with end users, focusing on results that affect decision making, such as reducing the number of surgical procedures, enabling more predictable therapy, or improving esthetic outcomes.52 As such, for DOC technologies, most studies are phase 1 or 2 or pilot clinical studies that combine safety with early-stage efficacy.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine-learning tools have the potential to markedly reshape the translational pathway for DOC technologies by means of strengthening both early discovery and late-stage clinical evidence generation. AI-enabled analyses of large data sets, including electronic dental records, imaging repositories, and practice-based research network data, can help identify clinically meaningful indications, refine patient selection, and highlight end points with greater predictive value for clinical adoption.53 AI-supported adaptive trial designs and predictive modeling may also reduce sample sizes, shorten trial duration, and increase generalizability through synthetic control arms or real-world comparator groups.54,55 In addition, AI-assisted interpretation of radiographic, photographic, or 3-dimensional imaging, along with natural language processing of clinician- and patient-reported outcomes, may increase trial sensitivity and efficiency.56,57

AI-driven postmarket surveillance systems have further potential to strengthen long-term adoption by means of tracking clinician-level performance variability, identifying early safety or efficacy signals, and guiding iterative product refinement.58 As regulatory agencies develop frameworks for integrating AI-derived evidence, its thoughtful incorporation into the right-to-left translational paradigm may accelerate evidence generation, reduce development cost, and enhance alignment between technological innovation and clinical practice needs.

To this end, incorporating the perspective of practicing clinicians, particularly KOLs, into trial design from the outset ensures that the data needed for clinical adoption have a greater likelihood of success.16,19,59,60 Right-to-left thinking identifies the core clinical needs through direct extrapolation from the practice environments and conversations with future users. Clinical protocols can then be tailored to reflect realistic workflows, patient populations, and outcome expectations, which may differ from the investigators’ initial ideas at the stage of fundamental scientific discovery. These clinicians are also best positioned to identify end points that influence behavior in the marketplace, such as chair time, patient acceptance, postoperative discomfort, ease of handling, pricing, or compatibility with commonly used instrumentation and techniques.16

The FDA and other global regulatory bodies are increasingly recognizing the value of clinical evidence (ie, real-world evidence) in supporting both initial approvals and postmarket claims.61 In dental research, practice-based research networks offer an ideal platform for generating real-world evidence that captures variability in patient demographic characteristics, operator technique, and clinical conditions that may influence outcomes representative of clinical practice outside of major academic centers.62,63 These studies can provide data on how regenerative technologies perform outside the rigid constraints of traditional trial designs, insights that are essential for understanding scalability, durability, and implementation barriers.64,65 Key to this shift is the incorporation of clinical outcome assessments,66 which include patient-reported and clinician-reported outcome measures.67 These tools have reshaped how new technologies are evaluated across medicine and dentistry.17,68,69 For instance, patient-reported outcome measures may include patient satisfaction with esthetics, function, or postoperative discomfort; clinician-reported outcome measures may capture clinician impressions of handling or healing quality. Such measures are critical for capturing dimensions of success that traditional histologic or radiographic data may miss. The FDA’s increased support for patient-focused drug development has encouraged more widespread use of clinical outcome assessments in regulatory submissions and postmarket evidence generation.70

CONCLUSIONS

Recognition of the clinical impact of innovative technologies in modern dentistry necessitates a reevaluation of how we design and interpret studies. From the earliest studies in preclinical models to pivotal clinical trials and clinical assessments, the goal must be translational relevance, intuitive outcomes, and clinically relevant change. Such an approach will accelerate the clinical adoption of new technologies to enhance patient care.
