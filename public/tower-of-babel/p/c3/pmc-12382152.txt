Effects of changes in rumen microbial adaptability on rumen fermentation and nutrient digestion in Horqin beef cattle during different seasons of grazing and supplementary feeding
Authors: Wen Peng, Yajing Wang, Manlin Wei, Shiba Liu, Kai Liu, Ming Xiao, Runze Zhang, Yuxiang Wang, Yongjie Zheng, Lue Fang, Yuxi Liu, Chunpeng Zhu, Weijing Meng, Chenyang Dong, Wanxin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of changes in rumen microbial adaptability on rumen fermentation and nutrient digestion in Horqin beef cattle during different seasons of grazing and supplementary feeding. Four healthy female Horqin beef cattle were selected as experimental animals for grazing and supplementary feeding. The feed, feces, and rumen fluid were collected in spring (March), summer (June), autumn (September), and winter (December) to determine nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters and microflora. The microflora was sequenced with 16S rRNA.ResultsThe results revealed that the rumen volatile fatty acid, ammonia nitrogen and microbial protein concentrations in summer were significantly higher than those in the other seasons (P < 0.05). The digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, crude protein and ether extracts decreased significantly in autumn and winter (P < 0.05), especially in winter. Compared with autumn, the rumen bacterial diversity of beef cattle increased significantly in spring (P < 0.05). The results of the rumen microflora revealed that Prevotella was the most dominant bacteria in the rumen of beef cattle in summer, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was the most dominant bacteria in the other seasons. The low quality of forage in spring, autumn and winter resulted in an increase in the relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, NK4A214_group, and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, etc., which are associated with fibre decomposition. In addition, nucleotide metabolism and glycan biosynthesis and metabolism increased significantly in bacteria in the summer (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe results revealed the dynamic adaptability of the rumen microbiota to seasonal and dietary changes and provide references for the grazing and supplementary feeding of Horqin beef cattle in different seasons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04199-7.Keywords: Beef cattle, Seasons, Rumen fermentation, Nutrient digestibility, Microbial adaptability

Introduction

The Horqin beef cattle is a fine local breeding variety in eastern Inner Mongolia. Over the years, its farming scale has continued to expand due to the abundant pasture resources in Inner Mongolia. However, the overgrazing and degradation of grasslands have led to challenges in the sustainable development of animal husbandry [1, 2]. To address this issue, a series of grassland restoration and protection policies, such as grazing suspension, rotational grazing, and grazing prohibition, have been implemented in recent years, and the quality of the grasslands has been restored to a certain extent [3, 4]. However, how to achieve scientific and reasonable grazing and improve feed utilization efficiency is still an issue that needs to be studied and resolved. In particular, the transition from traditional year-round grazing to seasonal grazing, from grazing to semigrazing and semiconfining, as well as the increase in the amount of feedstuffs such as whole-plant corn silage, concentrated feed, and hay in the diet, has occurred. All of these changes have raised new research topics related to grazing patterns. In fact, factors such as climate change, grazing methods, supplementary feeding, and feed formula have certain effects on the rumen status and nutrient digestion and utilization and further affect the production performance of animals [5–7]. Therefore, investigating Horqin beef cattle during different seasons is conducive to improving management and increasing feeding efficiency.

Seasons strongly influence the quality of forage, animal intake, and digestibility, which further affects the health and production performance of livestock [7, 8]. The nutritional components in forage and concentrated feed, especially polysaccharides such as cellulose and starch, can be digested and utilized by various microorganisms in the rumen (bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and archaea) and are converted into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), microbial protein (MCP), and methane gas, etc. [9, 10]. This process is very important because the energy produced by feed fermentation in the rumen accounts for approximately 70% of the energy needs of ruminants [11]. Moreover, the crude protein content of forage also affects the utilization of nitrogen and the synthesis of rumen MCP. Typically, high fibre in forage promotes an increase in fibre-decomposing bacteria in the rumen, whereas high crude protein content increases the abundance and diversity of bacteria in the rumen [12, 13]. Therefore, seasonal changes in forage quality clearly affect the nutritional status of grazing beef cattle. Studies have shown that low-quality forage, owing to its poor palatability, low digestibility and low content of crude protein and nonfibre carbohydrates, reduces the metabolic energy of feed [14].

In recent years, many studies have shown that ruminants can adjust themselves to improve their adaptability to seasonal environments through complex interactions between rumen microorganisms and feed [15–18]. In particular, changes in the abundance of rumen bacterial genera are caused by season [16, 19], which further causes the rumen fermentation parameters to change with season [20]. This allows ruminants to obtain nutrients and energy better in different seasons. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the adaptive changes in rumen microorganisms and fermentation parameters in response to seasonal grazing and supplementary feeding and to provide a reference for improving animal production management.

Materials and methods

Animals and the diet

This study was conducted in Zarut Banner, Tongliao city, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (119° 13'48"~ 121° 56'05"E, 43° 50'13"~ 45° 35'32"N). The average annual temperature is 6.6°C, the average annual sunshine duration is 2882.7 h, and the annual sunshine percentage is 65%. The frost-free period lasts for 139 days per year, with an average annual rainfall of 382.5 mm, average annual evaporation of 1957 mm, and average annual relative humidity of 49% [21]. The grassland type is typical temperate grassland, and the main forage species are Artemisia frigida Willd., Stipa capillata L., and Cleistogenes squarrosa (Trin.) Keng, etc.

Four female Horqin beef cattle with similar body weights (504.3±7.4 kg) at the age of approximately 8 years were selected as experimental animals. The cattle were artificially inseminated from April-May and were fed and managed in the same manner throughout the year. The cattle were grazed in the same pasture in summer and autumn, kept in the same loose barn and fed the same rations in spring and winter. The rumen fluid, feces, and feed samples were collected after a feeding adaptation period of at least 20 days in March (spring), June (summer), September (autumn), and December (winter), respectively. Sample collection

All the natural grass in the 1 m2 quadrat was collected from 3 representative locations of the grazing pasture as 3 replicates. Samples of forage and supplementary feed were collected and mixed according to the dietary formulation. Fecal samples (approximately 200 g) were collected from the rectum at 08:00, 14:00, and 20:00 daily for 4 consecutive days, and the feces were mixed with 20 ml of 10% sulfuric acid solution immediately to bind ammonia before they were stored at −20℃. Rumen fluid was collected through the esophagus via a gastric tube two hours after morning grazing during summer and autumn and two hours after morning feeding during winter and spring. To avoid saliva contamination, the first 200 mL of rumen fluid was discarded, and another 100 mL of rumen fluid was collected and filtered with four layers of gauze. The pH value was immediately determined with a portable pH meter (SX-620, Shanghai, China). Then, part of the rumen fluid was transferred into a 5 mL cryotube and stored in liquid nitrogen for the determination of microflora. The remainder was centrifuged (8000 ×g, 15 min, 4℃) and stored at −20℃ for the determination of rumen fermentation parameters.

Determination of rumen fermentation parameters

The ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) concentration was determined via phenol‒hypochlorous acid colorimetry [22], and the MCP concentration was determined according to the methods of Makkar et al [23]. The concentration of VFAs was determined via gas chromatography (Model GC-6800, Beijing Beifen Tianpu Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: Φ6 mm×2 m quartz glass-filled column (stationary phase 15% FFAP, 80 ~ 100 mesh Chromosorb), column temperature of 150 °C, inlet temperature of 220 °C, sample size of 1 μL, FID detector temperature of 280 °C, and carrier gas of high-purity N2 with a flow rate of 30 mL/min and a pressure of 200 kPa. The gas was H2 with a flow rate of 30 mL/min, and the auxiliary gas was air with a flow rate of 300 mL/min.

Determination of nutrient digestibility

The feed and feces were dried at 65 °C for approximately 48 hours to a constant weight and ground to pass through a 1 mm sieve. The feces were mixed individually. The contents of dry matter (DM), organic matter (OM), ether extract (EE) and crude protein (CP) in the diet and feces were subsequently determined according to the AOAC method [24]. The contents of neutral detergent fibre (NDF) and acid detergent fibre (ADF) were determined according to Van Soest's method via a semiautomatic fibre analyser (ANKOM A200I) [25]. 16S rRNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The total DNA of the microbial communities was extracted from the rumen fluid samples according to the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.), and the quality of the extracted DNA was tested via 1% AGAR gel electrophoresis. The DNA concentration and purity were determined via a NanoDrop2000. The upstream primer 338 F (5'-ActCCtacGGgaggCAGCAGCAGCAGCAGCAGCAGCAG-3') and downstream primer 806R (5'-GACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3') carrying the barcode sequence were used for PCR amplification of the V3-V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene. The libraries were constructed via the TruSeqIM DNA sample preparation kit and sequenced via the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. The raw sequencing files and associated metadata have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (PRJNA1150069).

Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed via an in-house perl script and then quality filtered via fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.19.6) and merged via FLASH (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, version 1.2.11). The optimized sequences were subsequently clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) via UPARSE (http://drive5.com/uparse/, version 7.1) with a 97% sequence similarity level. The most abundant sequence for each OTU was selected as a representative sequence. The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analysed via the RDP Classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, version 2.11) against the 16S rRNA gene database (Silva version 138) with a confidence threshold of 0.7. The metagenomic function was predicted by Tax4fun (http://tax4fun.gobics.de/, version 0.3.1) on the basis of representative OTU sequences.

The Majorbio cloud platform (www.majorbio.com) was used for biometric analysis. On the basis of the OTU information, rarefaction curves and alpha diversity, including observed OTUs, the Chao1 index and Shannon index, were calculated with Mothur (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators, version 1.30.1). The similarity among the microbial communities in different samples was determined via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) on the basis of BrayCurtis dissimilarity via the Vegan (version 2.5-3) package. The PERMANOVA test was used to assess the percentage of variation explained by the treatment along with its statistical significance via the Vegan (version 2.5-3) package. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe) was performed to identify the significantly abundant taxa (phylum to genera) of bacteria among the different groups (LDA score >4, P < 0.05). Correlation networks were constructed to explore the internal community relationships across the samples. A correlation between two nodes was statistically robust if the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was greater than 0.6 or less than −0.6 and if the P value was less than 0.01.

Statistical analysis

A single-factor experimental design was adopted. The experimental data were summarized via Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corp., USA) and statistically analysed via SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were performed, and P < 0.05 indicated a significant difference. R software (version 4.2.3) was used for microbiological analysis [27].

Rumen microflora

Bacterial diversity

Through sequencing analysis of bacterial diversity, 497,760 effective sequences were obtained, with an average of 31,110 effective sequences per sample. According to 97% similarity, operational taxonomic unit (OUT) cluster analysis was performed on the nonrepeating sequences (excluding single sequences) in each season, and a total of 11,798 OTUs were obtained from 25 phyla, 52 classes, 123 orders, 212 families, 464 genera, and 1,001 species. As shown in Fig. 2A, the Sobs index represented the actual number of observed species, which could reflect most of the microbial diversity information in a sample. The end of the Sobs dilution curve tended to flatten, indicating that the sequencing data volume was sufficient to meet the analysis requirements. The total number of OTUs shared by each group was 355 (Fig. 2B). The number of unique OTUs in each season decreased in the order of spring >summer >winter >autumn. The PCoA (Fig. 2C) revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 33.87% and 18.55% of the total variance, respectively, and were far from aggregating across all seasons. The alpha diversity of the rumen fluid is shown in Fig. 2D, E and F, and the number of OTUs and the Shannon index and Chao1 index in spring were significantly higher than those in autumn (P < 0.05). This suggested that the flora was more abundant in spring.

Discussion

Effects of grazing and supplementary feeding during different seasons on rumen fermentation parameters in beef cattle

The pH value and NH3-N, MCP, and VFAs concentrations in the rumen reflect the degree of rumen fermentation and the stability of the rumen environment [28, 29]. The concentration of NH3-N from the degradation of protein, amino acid, and nonprotein nitrogen depends on the feed CP, the degradation efficiency of the rumen, and the microbial activity [19, 30]. CP in feed can be widely degraded by rumen microorganisms into peptides, amino acids, and NH3. In summer, grass grows vigorously, and the content of nutrients, including CP, is relatively high (Table 1). Therefore, the NH3-N concentration in the rumen is higher in summer than in the other seasons. Additionally, the increase in the ruminal NH3-N concentration can promote the utilization of NH3-N by ruminal microorganisms to synthesize MCP, thereby increasing the abundance of VFAs-generating enzymes and promoting the production of VFAs [31], which is consistent with the results of the MCP and VFAs in this experiment. In many studies, the pH value of the ruminal fluid is less than 7.0 [19, 32], which is related to the production of ruminal VFAs [33], of which acetate is used mainly for the de novo synthesis of fat to store energy for the host [34], and propionate is the main precursor for gluconeogenesis in ruminants. To some extent, the A/P ratio reflects the efficiency of utilizing feed energy by animals [35]. The lower concentration of propionate in autumn and winter than in summer and spring and the lower concentrations of acetate and propionate in winter, combined with the highest A/P ratio in winter, generally imply a decrease in the utilization efficiency of feed energy during winter, which is consistent with the nutrient digestibility results. However, the TVFA concentration in beef cattle in winter is higher than that in autumn, which is related to the supplementation of concentrated feed in winter. Previous studies have shown that supplementing concentrated feed in cold winters can increase the concentration of VFAs and improve ruminal fermentation [36], which was confirmed in this study. In addition, the concentrations of isobutyrate and isovalerate significantly increase, both of which are affected mainly by the degradation of protein and the deamination process of amino acids in the feed [37]. The results of this study revealed that the changes in isobutyrate and isovalerate were consistent with the changes in NH3-N.

Effects of grazing and supplementary feeding during different seasons on nutrient digestibility in beef cattle

Nutrient digestibility is a complex parameter influenced by multiple factors and can be affected by the quality of forage, climate change, and the composition of ruminal microorganisms [38, 39]. Among these factors, the quality of forage is highly important. With respect to the diets of beef cattle in this study, the DM content of the forage gradually increased, but the CP content gradually decreased from summer to winter. In particular, in winter, degradable protein in hay is very scarce. Although concentrated feed was supplemented, the digestibility of DM, OM, CP, and EE remained very low. These results are consistent with those of Johnson et al. [40]. However, studies have reported that physiological adaptation occurs when animals are exposed to cold conditions for a long period of time, such as changes in the levels of related hormones (such as thyroid hormone, insulin, and adrenal hormone) [41, 42], and the stimulation of appetite, increased heat production and basic metabolic intensity for maintaining body temperature results in a considerable waste of energy [43]. This undoubtedly reduces the efficiency of feed digestion and the growth performance of animals [44]. The results of this study confirm this conclusion. In contrast, the rumen microflora was more abundant in spring than in winter because of the addition of hay and whole-plant corn silage, which have greater palatability and nutritional value (Fig. 2) and ultimately lead to higher digestibility of the feed [45]. However, the changes in season and feed type did not cause significant differences in NDF or ADF digestibility in summer, autumn, or winter. This may be related to the adaptability of the microbial community. Studies have shown that some microorganisms in the rumen may have similar metabolic functions, and when the type and quality of feed change, the ruminal microbial community can quickly adjust to maintain the digestive efficiency of nutrients such as fibre [17, 46].

Effects of grazing and supplementary feeding during different seasons on the rumen microflora in beef cattle

Rumen bacteria can degrade most organic and fibrous substances in feed and convert them into VFAs, MCP, vitamins, and other products to meet their own needs for growth and proliferation [19]. In this study, PCoA indicated that the distribution of microbial taxa varied significantly among different seasons. The differences in microbial community composition are attributed primarily to the composition and properties of forage, and adaptive changes in microbial composition are expected to improve the degradation, metabolism, and utilization of nutrients in forage. In spring, the number of OTUs, Shannon index, and Chao1 index were higher than those in autumn. This is mainly due to the addition of whole-plant corn silage, which provides more starch and physically effective fibres and has good palatability [47]. The increase in microbial diversity in the beef cattle rumen can be beneficial for obtaining energy from high-fibre forage [48].

Consistent with most research findings, the dominant phyla in the rumen in this study were also Firmicutes and Bacteroidota, regardless of the environment and diet [49, 50]. Firmicutes can produce a variety of lipases, proteases, cellulases, etc., thereby hydrolysing complex macromolecules such as lipids, cellulose, and other polysaccharides [51]. Bacteroidetes are generally considered to play a role in the degradation of proteins and carbohydrates [52]. The results of the random forest analysis (Fig. 4) revealed that Firmicutes had a greater effect on VFAs, which was related to their ability to metabolize carbohydrates in the feed. In comparison, Bacteroidota had a higher effect on feed CP digestibility and the MCP concentration than did Firmicutes, indicating that Bacteroidota plays a more important role in rumen amino acid metabolism and the nitrogen cycle. These results are consistent with the results of Pang et al [53]. In addition, the Actinobacteriota had the greatest effect on the pH value and digestibility of nutrients, except for CP digestibility, indicating that it has a metabolic effect on various nutrients. In fact, the Actinobacteriota functions in the degradation/decomposition of various organic substances (such as cellulose, polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and organic acids) [54].

In terms of seasonal effects, the lower relative abundance of Bacteroidota in autumn and winter in this study was due mainly to the lower quantity and digestibility of proteins and carbohydrates (except fibres) in the diet, which caused adaptive changes in the dominant phyla. The cold season reportedly increased the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidota in the rumen [55], indicating increased cellulose decomposition and increased energy utilization efficiency [56]. In terms of another important phylum, Actinobacteriota was found to be more abundant in the rumen of yaks fed a low-energy diet than in that of yaks fed a high-energy diet [53], which indicated that the Actinobacteriota is associated with improved energy utilization under poor environmental and dietary conditions. In this study, the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota was higher in autumn and winter when the dietary nutrient level was lower than in spring and summer, which confirms the previous conclusion. On the other hand, Actinobacteriota has strong antibacterial properties and is widely used as the main producer of currently used antibiotics and antimicrobial agents [57], which indicates its potential to improve host resistance to harmful bacteria and prevent the colonization of pathogens [58].

At the genus level, in many studies, Prevotella has been reported as the main dominant genus in the rumen of cattle [59, 60]. Prevotella plays a crucial role in the degradation of protein and starch to produce organic acids (succinate, acetate, etc.) and the synthesis of MCP [61, 62]. In terms of the random forest analysis (Fig. 4), Christensenellaceae_R-7_group had a significant effect on the pH value as well as the digestibility of DM, OM, EE, and NDF. pH is a key factor affecting fermentation processes and microbial activity. Christensenellaceae_R-7_group may play an important role in maintaining the stability of the pH value in the rumen. In addition, it participates in the digestion of organic matter and the metabolism of lipids and fibres to increase energy production [50]. The Eubacterium_hallii_group may have specific enzyme systems and metabolic pathways, enabling them to effectively decompose proteins and amino acids, thereby affecting the digestion of proteins [63]. However, Butyrivibrio can produce not only butyrate but also formic acid, lactic acid, and acetate and participate in cross-fermentation by converting intermediate products produced by other bacteria into VFAs [64]. Therefore, there was a strong correlation between TVFA and Butyrivibrio.

In terms of seasonal effects, the relative abundance of Prevotella in Bacteroidetes was much higher in the summer than in the other seasons in this study, which was related to the rich CP content in the diet. Prevotella is believed to increase with increasing CP concentration in the diet and inhibits fibre fermentation by competing for carbohydrates in the substrate [31, 65]. In spring, autumn, and winter, the relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group in Bacteroidetes exceeded that of Prevotella, which may promote Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and the NK4A214_group of Firmicutes and inhibit Prevotella [51, 66]. The results of the microbial correlation analysis in this study also support this conclusion (Fig. 6 and Fig. 7). The relative abundance of Butyrivibrio gradually decreased from summer to winter and spring, which may be related to the decreased carbohydrate and degradable fibre contents in the forage grass. The rumen bacterial species of Butyrivibrio are important for the degradation of polysaccharides, particularly hemicelluloses (arabinoxylans) and pectin [67]. In addition, the relative abundance of Butyrivibrio in summer was significantly higher than that in the other seasons, indicating a positive impact on animal health and energy utilization. Butyrate not only promotes the growth and proliferation of rumen epithelial cells but also plays a crucial role in the cellular energy supply, immune regulation, and elimination of inflammation [68, 69].

In terms of the functional prediction of the ruminal bacteria, the level of carbohydrate metabolism is significantly lower in autumn than in summer and winter, which is consistent with the lower levels of VFAs in autumn [70]. Polysaccharides are the main fermentable substrates and energy sources in the rumen; they are essential for maintaining cell structure and function and play a key role in cell signalling, immunity, and inflammatory responses [71]. These findings indicate that the rumen conditions in summer are conducive to maintaining energy utilization efficiency and animal health. In comparison, the level of amino acid metabolism in summer was significantly lower than that in spring (P < 0.05), which is inconsistent with previous reports [15]. This is related to the increased synthesis of MCP as well as the reduced deamination of ammonia nitrogen. This result is consistent with the increasing levels of nucleotide metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism in summer due to the promotion of the de novo synthesis of nucleotides by microorganisms [72]. In contrast, the metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides in the other seasons was significantly higher than that in the summer. Terpenoids, alkaloids, and polyketides have various biological activities and are the main factors involved in enhancing antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity, and anti-inflammatory and enzyme inhibitory capabilities [73], indicating that the resistance of rumen microorganisms is enhanced by the poorer quality of forage.

Conclusions

During different seasons of grazing and supplementary feeding, changes in rumen microbial adaptability significantly affect fermentation parameters and nutrient digestibility in the rumen of beef cattle. The high content of CP in summer grass promotes increases in NH3-N and MCP concentrations and the production of large amounts of VFAs, competitively inhibiting the growth and reproduction of bacteria related to fibre decomposition (Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and NK4A214_group). The lower quality of forage in spring, autumn, and winter leads to an increase in the abundance of bacteria related to fibre decomposition. The addition of whole-plant corn silage in spring not only improved the digestibility of nutrients but also increased the diversity of rumen microorganisms. In summary, rumen microorganisms adapt to seasonal grazing and supplementary feeding, which results in stable interactions between diet, microbial ecology, and rumen function and thereby contributes to maintaining the metabolic balance and health of ruminants.
