Resting cortisol concentrations in dogs presenting to a university teaching hospital with collapse
Authors: Ana Fernandez Gallego, Craig Robert Breheny, Adam G Gow, Alisdair M Boag
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between collapse and a diagnosis of hypoadrenocorticism is not well understood in dogs.HypothesisTo assess the prevalence of episodes of collapse in dogs screened for hypoadrenocorticism, and to assess the prevalence of confirmed hypoadrenocorticism in dogs presenting with reported collapse.AnimalsSeventy‐three client‐owned dogs with resting cortisol concentrations were measured and presented to a University teaching hospital for collapse.MethodsRetrospective review of medical records of dogs at a single center.ResultsThe prevalence of episodes of collapse in dogs that had a resting cortisol measurement was 73/856 (8.5%; 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 6.7%‐10.6%). Resting cortisol concentration was <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L) in 19 dogs. Cortisol concentration after ACTH stimulation was <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L) in 1 of the 73 dogs in this cohort, consistent with a diagnosis of hypoadrenocorticism, giving a prevalence estimate of hypoadrenocorticism of 1.3% (95% CI, 0.15%‐6.2%). In 8 dogs with an initial resting cortisol concentration <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L), hypoadrenocorticism was excluded based on a repeat resting cortisol concentration >2 μg/dL (>55 nmol/L). The most common diagnosis was vasovagal syncope (10/73), followed by sick sinus syndrome and third‐degree atrioventricular block (2/73). The final diagnosis was unknown in 24/73 dogs.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceHypoadrenocorticism was the final diagnosis in 1 of 73 dogs presented to a teaching hospital either in a collapsed state or with a previous history of episodes of collapse. No dog presenting as cardiovascularly stable for intermittent collapse was found to have hypoadrenocorticism.Keywords: ACTH stimulation test, Addison's disease, collapsing episodes, hypoadrenocorticism

INTRODUCTION

Collapse is a broad clinical term defined as a state of unintentional prostration as a consequence of disease mechanisms. 1 Syncope is more specifically defined as a loss of consciousness because of transient decreased cerebral perfusion, oxygen, or essential nutrients, together with a lack of postural tone and spontaneous recovery. 1 The clinical manifestations of collapse are diverse and a good description of the event is vital to guide diagnostic investigations, this typically relies on the owners' description of the event. 1 Differential diagnoses of collapse include disorders of consciousness (including seizures), skeletal/joint disease, motor unit conditions such as lower motor neuron, upper motor neuron, neuromuscular junction disease and myopathies, disorders of oxygenation, illnesses affecting cardiac output or vascular perfusion, and metabolic disorders. 1 Some dogs present acutely and require emergency stabilization before further investigations can be performed, whereas others might present with intermittent episodes and have a normal clinical examination between events. 1 A thorough history describing the event, together with physical examination findings and clinical investigations are needed to reach a final diagnosis. Studies report that a final diagnosis is reached 50% to 67% of dogs with episodes of collapse. 2 , 3 The lack of a final diagnosis can be a challenge for veterinary surgeons and owners, as it prevents specific treatment and accurate prognostication.

Hypoadrenocorticism is characterized by a lack of glucocorticoids, with or without concurrent mineralocorticoid deficiency, and vague, nonspecific clinical signs that can mimic gastrointestinal, renal, or neurological disease. Hypoadrenocorticism can have an acute presentation or chronic waxing and waning clinical signs. 4 , 5 Anorexia, weakness, lethargy, vomiting, and diarrhea are the most common clinical signs frequently seen with this disease, but some dogs present with either acute or intermittent episodes of collapse. 4 In these cases, episodes of collapse might be related to hypovolemic shock, decreased cardiac output, hypoglycemia, or because of electrolyte imbalances that can lead to cardiac arrhythmias. 1 , 4 , 6

An ACTH stimulation test is needed to diagnose hypoadrenocorticism. 4 Resting cortisol concentration >2 μg/dL (>55 nmol/L) is frequently used to exclude hypoadrenocorticism because of its high sensitivity of around 99.4% to 100%. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 As a consequence, resting cortisol with or without subsequent ACTH stimulation testing to rule out hypoadrenocorticism is frequently included in the clinical investigations for dogs presenting for investigations of episodes of collapse. Studies estimate that up to 10% of dogs with hypoadrenocorticism present with episodes of collapse. 4 No previous studies have reported the prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism in dogs with episodes of collapse.

The aim of this study was initially to assess the prevalence of episodes of collapse in dogs screened for hypoadrenocorticism, and to assess the prevalence of confirmed hypoadrenocorticism in dogs presenting with reported collapse to a veterinary teaching hospital. We included both dogs that had a history of collapse and dogs that were collapsed on presentation. We hypothesized that the prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism in dogs that presented with collapsing events will be low and similar to the overall prevalence of this disease in the general population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Medical records of dogs with a resting cortisol concentration measurement as part of the clinical investigations and a history of collapse that were presented to The Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies (The University of Edinburgh) between May 2015 and November 2018 were retrospectively reviewed. We included dogs where either the sole presenting complaint or one of the primary presenting complaints was episodes of collapse as well as dogs that presented in a collapsed state.

Dogs that had resting cortisol concentration testing were identified based on retrospective review of medical records using the electronic client data management system Tristan (Tristan Veterinary Software Ltd, version 1.8.3.914, Orion Engineering Services, Orion House, Inverness, United Kingdom).

All clinical records of dogs with resting cortisol concentration testing were reviewed. Only dogs with collapse as a presenting complaint (as recorded in the clinical record) were included in this study. Only dogs that were investigated for a clinical suspicion of hypoadrenocorticism were included. Dogs were excluded if resting cortisol concentration testing was part of an ACTH stimulation test or low‐dose dexamethasone suppression test because of suspicion of hyperadrenocorticism, or to monitor trilostane treatment. Dogs were also excluded if they had a history of recent glucocorticoid administration (1 week before presentation for short‐acting glucocorticoids and 4 weeks for long‐acting glucocorticoids), including topical, otic, or optic preparations. 7 , 11 Dogs were also excluded if there was any history of mitotane, ketoconazole, or any other preparations that affect the ACTH stimulation test.

Signalment, admitting service, presenting complaint(s) reported by owner, resting cortisol concentration, and final diagnosis (as documented by a veterinary surgeon in the medical record) were reviewed in all dogs. If available, hematology, biochemistry values, and ACTH stimulation test results were extracted.

Resting cortisol concentration measurements for all dogs were determined using the Immulite 1000 Analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, United Kingdom). Post ACTH stimulation cortisol (5 μg/kg intravenously tetracosactide; Cosacthen 0.25 mg/mL, Dechra Veterinary Products) was measured using the same machine in 10/11 dogs. The remaining case was reported by NationWide Laboratories, United Kingdom, using a radioimmunoassay (RIA).

A lack of stress leukogram was defined as neutrophils and monocytes within normal limits and no lymphopenia. Eosinophilia was defined as the eosinophil count above the laboratory reference range (higher than 1.0 × 109/L). Lymphocytosis was defined as a total lymphocyte count above the laboratory reference range (higher than 4.8 × 109/L). Nonregenerative anemia was defined as a hematocrit concentration below the laboratory reference range (0.39‐0.55 L/L) with a reticulocyte count <60 000/μL in dogs. 12 Azotemia was defined as a creatinine value above the laboratory reference range (higher than 1.49 mg/dL [132 μmol/L]). Hypoglycemia was defined as a blood glucose lower than 72 mg/dL (4 mmol/L).

For statistical evaluation, all descriptive data were organized using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, USA) and data analyses and visualizations were performed in R (R version 4.0.3) using RStudio (version 1.3.1093). Data were assessed for normality by visual assessment of histograms and q‐q plots. Normal data are presented as mean ± SD, non‐normally distributed data are presented as median, range, and interquartile range (IQR).

RESULTS

Dog data

A total of 1182 resting cortisol measurements were performed in 856 dogs, and episodes of collapse was the primary presenting complaint in 73 dogs (73/856, 8.5%). Eight dogs presented in a collapsed state, and 1 dog had a collapsing episode during initial consultation. One dog that was stable on presentation died at home during investigations. The remaining 63 dogs were presented with a history of episodes of collapse.

Thirty‐six dogs of these 73 dogs were male (20 neutered, 16 entire) and 37 were female (20 neutered, 17 entire). The most common breed was Boxer (14), followed by Labrador (6), cross breed (6), West Highland white terrier and Border collie (4). Median age was 4.3 years old (range: 2 months to 13.4 years; IQR: 1.4‐7.1) and median weight was 21.1 kg (range: 1.8‐79.7 kg; IQR: 9.7‐31.0 kg).

Forty‐four dogs of the 73 dogs with both collapse and a measured resting cortisol concentration were seen by the Cardiology Service, and the remaining were admitted through: Emergency and Critical Care Service (12), Internal Medicine Service (7), General Practice (2) and Neurology Service (1); with 7 dogs being seen jointly by 2 or 3 services.

In 50 dogs, the only reported major clinical sign was collapse (34 seen by Cardiology, 7 by ECC, 3 by Internal Medicine and 1 each by Neurology and General Practice, with 4 cases seen by 2 or more Services). In the remaining 23 dogs, 2 (12 dogs), 3 (7 dogs), or 4 or more (4 dogs) clinical signs were reported. Other commonly reported clinical signs were lethargy (7), vomiting and weight loss (4 each), and diarrhea (3).

Seventy dogs had hematology results, 69 had serum biochemistry performed as well. Other investigations performed included blood pressure measurement (67), electrocardiography (55), echocardiography (52), Holter monitoring (42), and cardiac troponin I concentration (30). Specialist neurological examination was performed in 22 dogs. Other diagnostic tests performed included abdominal ultrasound (28), thoracic radiographs (30), urine analysis (25), and serum vitamin B12 testing (5).

Resting cortisol/adrenal testing descriptive

Resting cortisol was >2 μg/dL (>55 nmol/L) in 54/73 (74%) and was <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L) in 19/73 (26%). No dog had a cortisol of 2 μg/dL (55 nmol/L; Figure 1).

Resting serum cortisol concentration for all cases. Red dashed line represents 2 μg/dL (55 nmol/L) cut off.

Of the dogs with a resting cortisol <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L), further adrenal testing was performed in 13/19; 2 dogs had repeat resting cortisol performed, which excluded hypoadrenocorticism. Eleven had post ACTH‐stimulated cortisol measured, which excluded hypoadrenocorticism in 10 and was <2 μg/dL in 1 dog, which was consistent with a diagnosis of hypoadrenocorticism (Figure 2).

Cortisol measurements in cases with ACTH stimulation. Dashed line represents 2 μg/dL (55 nmol/L) cut off.

In total, repeat resting cortisol measurements were performed in 10 dogs with an initial resting cortisol of <2 μg/dL. Of these, 4 dogs the repeat resting cortisol concentrations remained <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L). The ACTH stimulation testing was not consistent with hypoadrenocorticism in any of these dogs. In 6 dogs the second measurement was >2 μg/dL (>55 nmol/L).

No follow up adrenal testing dogs <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L)

There was no further adrenal testing performed in 6 dogs with resting cortisol <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L). The final diagnoses for this group of dogs were one each of parvovirus infection, aortic stenosis, inherited ventricular arrhythmias and intracranial meningioma. The diagnosis was undetermined in the remaining 2 dogs, but 1 of these dogs had brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) and hypoplastic trachea, which were suspected to be the causes for the collapsing episodes.

Dog with hypoadrenocorticism

Hypoadrenocorticism was the final diagnosis in 1/73 dogs, giving a prevalence estimate in this population of 1.3% (95% CI, 0.15%‐6.2%). This was a 5‐year‐old female entire English Springer Spaniel that presented with an acute history of collapse, acute vomiting, altered mentation and lethargy, and clinically dehydrated presenting in hypovolemic shock. This dog did not have hyponatremia and hyperkalemia, and was diagnosed with eunatremic eukalemic hypoadrenocorticism (ALIVE guidelines 13 ) with isolated hypocortisolism suspected, although serum aldosterone concentration was not measured in this dog.

Clinicopathological abnormalities in all dogs

Clinicopathological parameters associated with hypoadrenocorticism, were also assessed, specifically a lack of stress leukogram, eosinophilia, lymphocytosis, nonregenerative anemia, azotemia, hyperkalemia with hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and hypercalcemia. 6 A lack of stress leukogram was present in 37/70 dogs (53%) and did not include the dog with hypoadrenocorticism, which had a mild neutrophilia (neutrophil count 16.082 × 109/L) with increased numbers of nonsegmented neutrophils (2.057 × 109/L). In this group of dogs there was also 1 dog that had a resting cortisol <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L) without an ACTH stimulation test done and no final diagnosis reached. Eosinophilia was present in 4/70 (6%) but not in the dog with hypoadrenocorticism; other diagnoses in this group of dogs included: chronic kidney disease, sick sinus syndrome, atrioventricular block Mobitz type 1, and pericardial effusion. Lymphocytosis was present in 1 dog diagnosed with acute liver failure. Nonregenerative anemia was seen in 10/70 dogs (14%). A Na : K ratio of <27 was not present in any dog. Azotemia was present in 7/69 dogs (10%), including the dog diagnosed with hypoadrenocorticism. Hypoglycemia was reported in 2/69 cases (3%), neither of which was the dog with hypoadrenocorticism. Final diagnoses for these dogs were insulinoma and acute liver injury. Hypercalcemia was not reported in any dog.

Final diagnoses

Final diagnosis was reached in 49/73 dogs (67%). Vasovagal syncope was the final diagnosis in 10/73 dogs (14%), and was the most common final diagnosis, followed by sick sinus syndrome and third‐degree atrioventricular block, seen in 2/73 dogs each. Hypoadrenocorticism was the final diagnosis in 1/73 dogs. Eight of these 49 dogs (16%) presented collapsed, including the dog diagnosed with hypoadrenocorticism and 1 dog had a collapsing episode during the initial consultation. The remaining 40 dogs (82%) were considered cardiovascular stable on presentation.

The final diagnosis was unknown in 24/73 dogs (33%). Seventeen of these dogs presented with only episodes of collapse, and other signs present in the remaining 7 were: lethargy (2), exercise intolerance (1), coughing (1), cyanosis (1), regurgitation (1), and ataxia (1). All 24 dogs presented with a previous history of episodes of collapse but were considered cardiovascularly stable on presentation. However, 1 dog died from unknown cause at home before diagnosis was reached. Seven of these 24 dogs (29%) had a concurrent condition: urinary tract infection (1), mild pulmonary hypertension with mild pulmonic insufficiency, tricuspid regurgitation and mitral regurgitation (1), myxomatous valve disease (1), suspected early sick sinus syndrome (1), tricuspid and mitral valve disease, dysrhythmia and iatrogenic hyperthyroidism (1), mild brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (1), and myxomatous valve disease with borderline left sided congestive heart failure with mild pulmonary hypertension and suspected tracheal collapse (1). One dog with a pacemaker collapsed while exercising but not secondary to pacemaker malfunction, 1 dog was diagnosed with a ventricular arrhythmia of unknown cause and 1 dog was suspected to have seizure episodes of unknown cause.

DISCUSSION

The prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism in this cohort of dogs that had resting cortisol performed and presented for investigation of episodes of collapse was 1.3% (95% CI, 0.15%‐6.2%). This study evaluates the prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism in a cohort of dogs with collapse as a presenting complaint. This prevalence (with wide confidence intervals) is in keeping with prevalence estimates of hypoadrenocorticism in general population of dogs, estimated to be between 0.06% and 1.1% 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and with prevalence estimates in dogs presenting with chronic signs of gastrointestinal disease of between 0.3% and 4%. 10 , 18 Episodes of collapse occur in between 10% and 30% of dogs with hypoadrenocorticism. 4 , 19 However, there are no reports of the prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism in dogs presenting with collapse.

Collapse is broad clinical term used that requires a thorough history to guide its diagnostic approach, as many differential diagnoses can result in this presentation, including neurological conditions, heart disease, endocrine, and metabolic diseases. 1 In dogs with hypoadrenocorticism, collapse events are secondary to a decrease in cardiac output because of hypovolemic shock, especially if presenting in an adrenal crisis (Wray, 2005). 4 Moreover, cortisol enhances catecholamine vasoconstrictive effects, along with decreasing production of nitric oxide. This mechanism also plays a role in peripheral vasodilation and systemic hypotension seen in dogs with lack of endogenous cortisol. 20

Dogs with eunatremic eukalemic hypoadrenocorticism are less likely to present with acute and severe clinical signs such as hypovolemic shock and collapse compared with dogs with hyponatremic hyperkalemic hypoadrenocorticism (ALIVE guidelines 5 , 13 ), as it is assumed these dogs have adequate aldosterone levels to maintain intravascular volume and electrolytes within normal limits. 21 The dog with hypoadrenocorticism in this study presented with signs consistent with hypovolemic shock. However, electrolyte imbalances were not present and they were diagnosed with eunatremic, eukalemic hypoadrenocorticism. Aldosterone was not measured in this dog; undetectable post‐ACTH aldosterone concentrations are reported in dogs with normal serum potassium and sodium concentrations, 22 which therefore could have been the case in this dog.

Acute signs of gastrointestinal disease are a common clinical presentation of dogs with hypoadrenocorticism. 4 , 6 , 13 , 19 The dog with hypoadrenocorticism, as well as acute episodes of collapse, also presented with an acute history of vomiting and diarrhea which might have further contributed to the dog's hypovolemic shock.

Certain hematological and biochemical abnormalities can raise the clinical suspicion of hypoadrenocorticism, such as lack of stress leukogram, lymphocytosis, eosinophilia, azotemia, hypercalcemia and hypoglycemia. 5 Electrolyte imbalances (hyponatremia and hyperkalemia) can also be seen. 5 Consequently, investigations to rule out hypoadrenocorticism might be performed when any of these hematological and biochemical changes are found. In this cohort of dogs, the case of hypoadrenocorticism presented with azotemia that resolved after appropriate treatment.

Twenty‐four cases (24/73, 33%) did not have a final diagnosis, collapse was the only presenting sign in 17 of these dogs. This is in keeping with studies showing a diagnosis was reached in 67% of dogs in a referral population presenting with collapse. 2 The percentage of cases with an unknown cause of collapse in this group of dogs appears to be similar than previously reported in collapsed dogs, despite these investigations having differing inclusion criteria. 2 , 3

Repeat resting cortisol measurement was performed in 10 dogs, and in 6/10 of these cases repeat testing excluded hypoadrenocorticism. These results are similar to a previous publication assessing resting cortisol in dogs with chronic signs of gastrointestinal disease. 10 In that study population, repeat resting cortisol concentration excluded hypoadrenocorticism in 19/28 (67%) dogs with an initial resting cortisol <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L). In circumstances with limited resources, such as no access to synthetic ACTH or because of financial constraints, repeat resting cortisol could be used for ruling out hypoadrenocorticism, the current study expands previous findings to a new population of dogs presenting with episodes of collapse.

There are several limitations in this study, the majority is inherent to its retrospective nature. Two dogs in which a final diagnosis was not achieved, with resting cortisol <2 μg/dL (<55 nmol/L), did not have an ACTH stimulation test. Therefore, hypoadrenocorticism could not be completely ruled out. Moreover, there were 4 dogs that did not have follow up ACTH stimulation test performed because they were diagnosed with another condition that explained their episodes of collapse. Episodes of collapse might happen as a result of multiple diseases, including neurological, cardiogenic and metabolic conditions, including hypoadrenocorticism. The final diagnosis of collapse remained unknown in a third of our study population. Therefore, the overall prevalence of hypoadrenocorticism could have been underestimated.

In conclusion, hypoadrenocorticism was an uncommon diagnosis in a cohort of dogs presented for episodes of collapse. No dog presenting as cardiovascularly stable for intermittent collapse had hypoadrenocorticism. On the basis of this information, the need for resting cortisol testing should be assessed individually for each dog, considering signalment and clinical presentation of the dog. Ultimately, hypoadrenocorticism should be ruled out in dogs with collapse, when no other conditions have been identified and the diagnosis of the dog remains unknown.
