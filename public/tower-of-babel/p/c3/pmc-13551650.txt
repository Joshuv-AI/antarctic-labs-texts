Prevalence and Genomic Characterization of mcr‐Positive Enterobacteriaceae in Retail Meat in Thailand Following the Colistin Ban
Authors: Ahmed Abououf, Sharda Prasad Awasthi, Asmaa M. Elbastawesy, Noritoshi Hatanaka, Atsushi Hinenoya, Srinuan Somroop, Yukihiro Kaneko, Shinji Yamasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence and characteristics of mcr‐positive Enterobacteriaceae in retail meat in Thailand following the national ban on prophylactic colistin use in food producing animals. A total of 152 meat samples (103 chicken and 49 pork) were collected from supermarkets and open markets between July and September 2023. Samples were screened for mcr‐1 to mcr‐5 using multiplex PCR. None of the samples from supermarkets tested positive, whereas mcr genes were detected in 15.4% (6/39) and 13.3% (4/30) of chicken and pork samples, respectively, from open markets, with mcr‐1 and/or mcr‐3 identified. A total of 21 isolates were recovered from PCR‐positive samples (11 from chicken and 10 from pork). Escherichia coli was the predominant species (n = 19), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 2). All mcr‐positive isolates exhibited multidrug resistance. Whole‐genome sequencing was performed for 19 non‐clonal isolates. One K. pneumoniae strain from a pork sample co‐harbored mcr‐1 and mcr‐8, representing the first report of this combination in the animal sector in Thailand. In addition, virulence‐associated genes, including adhesion factors, toxins, and iron acquisition systems, were identified in selected isolates. Core genome SNP‐based phylogenetic analysis revealed substantial genomic diversity among the isolates, suggesting relatedness to strains reported prior to the colistin ban. These findings indicate that retail meat from open markets may serve as an important route for the transmission of mcr‐positive bacteria in Thailand and highlight the urgent need to incorporate systematic retail meat surveillance into national antimicrobial resistance monitoring programs.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been increasingly recognized as a global health threat. It is estimated to have directly caused approximately 1.27 million deaths and contributed to nearly 5 million deaths worldwide [1]. Among resistant pathogens, Gram‐negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae are of particular concern due to their association with high morbidity and mortality [2]. The rapid global spread of extended‐spectrum β‐lactamase (ESBL)‐producing and carbapenem‐resistant bacteria has severely limited treatment options, leading to the renewed clinical reliance on polymyxins. Colistin (polymyxin E), once largely abandoned due to toxicity, has re‐emerged as a last‐resort antibiotic for multidrug‐resistant Gram‐negative infections [3]. Initially, colistin resistance was attributed to chromosomal mutations in regulatory genes such as pmrA, pmrB, phoP, phoQ, and mgrB, which modify lipid A in the lipopolysaccharide layer, reducing its negative charge and thereby impairing colistin binding. This mechanism typically results in a gradual and limited spread of resistance. However, the discovery of plasmid‐mediated mobile colistin resistance (mcr) genes fundamentally altered this paradigm [4]. First identified in China in 2015 in bacteria from food‐producing animals, mcr genes have since diversified into multiple variants (mcr‐2 to mcr‐10) and disseminated globally [5, 6]. Their plasmid‐borne nature enables rapid horizontal transfer across bacterial populations, accelerating the spread of colistin resistance and undermining its role as a last‐resort therapy. Of particular concern is the increasing co‐occurrence of mcr genes in ESBL‐producing bacteria isolated from food‐producing animals and retail food products. These strains frequently exhibit resistance to both third‐generation cephalosporins and colistin, further narrowing already limited therapeutic options [7, 8, 9]. These findings highlight the urgent need for surveillance of ESBL–mcr‐positive bacteria in the food chain.

Thailand is a major global producer and exporter of poultry and pork [10, 11]. The detection of mcr genes in food‐producing animals, particularly pigs, raises concerns regarding contamination of meat and subsequent transmission to humans through the food supply [12, 13, 14]. In response, the Thai government implemented a nationwide ban on colistin use in the animal sector in 2017 [15]. Despite this policy intervention, its impact on the prevalence of mcr genes in retail meat remains unclear. Current surveillance efforts in Thailand have primarily focused on phenotypic resistance in selected bacteria, such as E. coli and Salmonella, across human, animal, and environmental sectors under the One Health framework [16]. However, these studies have largely relied on samples collected at slaughterhouses, with limited investigation of retail meat. Given that retail meat represents a direct interface between animal reservoirs and human exposure, it is likely to play a critical role in the transmission of mcr genes [17].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of the colistin ban on the prevalence of mcr genes in retail meat in Thailand and to characterize mcr‐harboring bacteria.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection, Processing, Bacterial Isolation, and Biochemical Identification

A total of 152 meat samples (103 chicken and 49 pork) were collected from three open markets and five supermarkets in Khlong Paton, Pathum Thani, Thailand, between July and September 2023 (Table 1). Briefly, 25 g of each sample was homogenized with 225 mL of buffered peptone water (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) and processed using a stomacher for 45 s. The homogenates were incubated at 37°C for 16–18 h for enrichment. Subsequently, 500 µL of each sample was centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet (~50 µL) was resuspended in 450 µL of 1× TE buffer (pH 8.0). The suspension was heated at 95°C for 10 min, followed by snap cooling, and centrifugation at 15,000 × g at 4˚C, for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was used as the DNA template. Crude DNA was screened for mcr‐1 to mcr‐5 using multiplex PCR as described by Rebelo et al. [18], with minor modifications. The PCR mixture (20 µL total volume) contained 2 µL of 10× ExTaq buffer (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), 1.6 µL of dNTPs (2.5 mM each), 2.5 µL of primer mix (10 µM each; targeting mcr‐1 to mcr‐5; Table S1), 0.1 µL of ExTaq polymerase (5 U/µL; Takara), 2 µL of DNA template, and 9.3 µL of sterile distilled water. To minimize false‐negative results from crude samples, PCR conditions were modified as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 90 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 3 min. PCR products (3 µL) were separated on 2.0% agarose gels (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) at 100 V for 35 min. Positive controls used were E. coli strains IPN.15‐116 (carrying mcr‐1), EC‐458 (mcr‐3), and EC‐9 (mcr‐5), originally reported by Nahar [19] and Fukuda et al. [20]. For mcr‐2 (accession no. LT598652) and mcr‐4 (accession no. MF543359), synthetic gene fragments kindly provided by Dr. Masato Suzuki at National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Japan were used as controls. Samples positive for mcr genes were streaked onto MacConkey agar (Eiken Chemical Co. Ltd., Tochigi, Japan) supplemented with colistin (1 µg/mL; Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands), with or without cefotaxime (1 µg/mL). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 18 h. Three to five randomly selected colonies (lactose‐fermenting and/or non‐fermenting) were picked for further analysis. The isolates were screened by uniplex PCR to confirm the presence of mcr genes. Genomic DNA was prepared by alkaline lysis as described previously [21]. Briefly, a single colony was inoculated into 3 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 18 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of the culture was centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 85 µL of 50 mM NaOH, heated at 95°C for 10 min, and neutralized with 15 µL of 1 M Tris‐HCl buffer (pH 8.0). After centrifugation under the same conditions, the supernatant was used as the DNA template. Uniplex PCR was performed in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM; specific for each mcr gene; Table S1), 2 µL of 10× rTaq buffer (Takara), 1.6 µL of dNTPs (2.5 mM), 0.1 µL of rTaq polymerase (5 U/µL; Takara), 1 µL of DNA template, and 13.3 µL of sterile distilled water. Amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 3 min. All confirmed mcr‐positive isolates were identified by biochemical tests according to Ewing [22].

Analysis of Clonal Relationships

The E. coli isolates (n = 19) were analyzed by pulsed‐field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) to assess clonal diversity. PFGE was not performed for K. pneumoniae (n = 2) due to the limited number of isolates. PFGE was carried out according to the PulseNet USA protocol (https://www.cdc.gov/gram-negative-bacteria/media/pdfs/Modified-PulsedNet-Procedure-GNB-P.pdf). Briefly, genomic DNA was digested with 30 U of XbaI (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) at 37°C for 2 h, followed by electrophoresis on 1.0% pulsed‐field certified agarose (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) in 0.5× TBE buffer (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, 1.0 mM EDTA [pH 8.0]) at 6 V/cm, with pulse times ranging from 6.76 to 35.38 s for 20 h using a CHEF Mapper system (Bio‐Rad). Salmonella enterica serovar Braenderup H9812 digested with XbaI was used as the molecular size marker. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide (1 µg/ml; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and destained twice with distilled water. PFGE profiles were analyzed using BioNumerics software version 8 (Applied Maths, Kortrijk, Belgium).

Determination of Colistin Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs)

Colistin minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined by the broth microdilution method according to CLSI guidelines (2018) [23]. A stock solution of colistin sulfate (Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands) was prepared in distilled water and sterilized using a 0.22 µm membrane filter (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Two‐fold serial dilutions of colistin were prepared in cation‐adjusted Mueller‐Hinton broth (CAMHB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to achieve final concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 32 µg/mL in sterile 96‐well microplates (Violamo, Tokyo, Japan). For inoculum preparation, a single colony of each mcr‐positive isolate was cultured in 3 mL of TSB at 37°C for 18 h under static conditions. Subsequently, 3 µl of the culture was transferred into 3 ml of fresh TSB and incubated at 37°C for 3 h with shaking (180 rpm) to obtain cells in the exponential phase. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and further diluted 1:100 in CAMHB to achieve a final inoculum of approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL. An aliquot of 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well containing 100 µl of the corresponding colistin dilution, resulting in a final volume of 200 µL per well. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 16 h. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing and Detection of β‐lactamase Genes

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of mcr‐positive isolates was performed by the disk diffusion method according to CLSI guidelines (2018) [23]. A total of 14 antimicrobial agents representing eight classes were tested. Briefly, isolates were streaked onto l‐agar (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and incubated at 37°C for 18 h. Three to five colonies were suspended in 1.0 ml of sterile 0.85% NaCl and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard. The suspension was uniformly spread onto Mueller‐Hinton agar (MHA; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) using sterile cotton swabs (Kawamoto Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Antimicrobial disks (Nihon Becton Dickinson, Tokyo, Japan) were applied, and inhibition zones were measured after incubation at 37°C for 16–18 h. The antimicrobials tested were ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), cefoxitin (FOX, 30 µg), chloramphenicol (CHL, 30 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), fosfomycin (FOS, 50 µg), gentamicin (GEN, 10 µg), imipenem (IPM, 10 µg), kanamycin (KAN, 30 µg), nalidixic acid (NAL, 30 µg), streptomycin (STR, 10 µg), sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim (SXT, 23.75/1.25 µg), and tetracycline (TET, 30 µg). Results were interpreted according to CLSI guidelines (2018) [23].

Extended‐spectrum β‐lactamase (ESBL) production was assessed by the double‐disk synergy test in accordance with CLSI guidelines (2018) [23]. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain. Isolates were classified as multidrug‐resistant (MDR) if they exhibited resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes.

Detection of β‐lactamase genes (bla CTX‐M‐1, bla CTX‐M‐2, bla CTX‐M‐8/25, bla CTX‐M‐9, bla TEM, and bla SHV) was performed by multiplex PCR in phenotypically confirmed ESBL‐producing isolates (n = 8), as described by Le et al. [24]. PCR amplification was conducted using the Qiagen Multiplex PCR Plus Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A mixture of positive control strains was kindly provided by Dr. Ryuji Kawahara (Osaka Institute of Public Health, Osaka, Japan).

S1‐nuclease PFGE and Southern Hybridization

To determine plasmid profiles of mcr‐positive isolates, S1 nuclease–treated PFGE (S1‐PFGE) was performed, followed by Southern hybridization to identify the location of mcr gene, as previously described [25] with slight modifications. Briefly, isolates were cultured in 3 mL of Luria‐Bertani (LB) broth (Nacalai Tesque) at 37°C for 16 h under static conditions, followed by subculture into fresh LB and incubation at 37°C for 3 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (6,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C) and resuspended in S1 cell suspension buffer (10 mM Tris‐HCl [pH 7.2], 20 mM NaCl, 100 mM EDTA) to an OD600 of approximately 1.8.

An aliquot (300 µl) was mixed with an equal volume of 1.0% SeaKem Gold agarose (Lonza, Rockland, ME, USA) and cast into plug molds (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). The plugs were incubated in cell lysis buffer (10 mM Tris‐HCl [pH 7.2], 50 mM NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 0.2% SDS, 0.5% N‐lauroylsarcosine) at 70°C for 90 min with gentle agitation. Following lysis, plugs were washed with washing buffer (20 mM Tris‐HCl [pH 8.0], 50 mM EDTA), treated with proteinase K solution (0.5 mg/ml proteinase K [P8044‐5G, Sigma Aldrich], 100 mM EDTA, 0.2% SDS, 1% N‐lauroylsarcosine) at 42°C for 18 h, and washed twice with diluted washing buffer. Plug slices (~4 mm) were equilibrated in 1× S1 nuclease buffer (30 mM sodium acetate [pH 4.6], 1.0 mM zinc acetate, 50% glycerol) and digested with 4U S1 nuclease (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C for 40 min. The digested plugs were subjected to PFGE on 1.0% pulsed‐field certified agarose (Bio‐Rad) in 0.5× TBE buffer using a CHEF Mapper system (Bio‐Rad) under the following conditions: 6 V/cm, pulse times of 2.2–54.8 s, for 20 h. Following electrophoresis, gels were stained with ethidium bromide, destained with DW and photographed. DNA fragments were transferred to a nylon membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 10× SSC buffer (1.5 M NaCl, 0.15 M sodium citrate [pH 7.0]). Southern hybridization was performed using 32P‐labeled probes specific for mcr‐1, mcr‐3, bla CTX‐M‐1, and bla SHV. Probes were prepared from DNA of E. coli strains IPN.15‐116 [7], TC46CSTCTX (this study), CD15‐1 and CD21‐1 [26] by random priming using the MultiPrime DNA Labeling System (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK) with [α‐32P]‐dCTP (111 TBq/mmol). Hybridization signals were detected using an FLA‐7000 biomolecular imager (GE Healthcare).

Conjugation Assay

To assess the transferability of mcr‐carrying plasmids, conjugation assays were performed by solid mating as described by Lampkowska et al. [27], with minor modifications. mcr‐positive isolates (n = 21) and sodium azide–resistant E. coli C600 were used as donor and recipient strains, respectively. Donor and recipient strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) supplemented with colistin (1 µg/ml) and sodium azide (300 µg/ml), respectively, and incubated at 37°C for 16 h with shaking (180 rpm). Subsequently, 10 µl of each culture was inoculated into 3 ml of fresh TSB and incubated at 37°C for 3 h. One milliliter each of donor and recipient cultures was centrifuged (6,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), washed twice with 0.85% NaCl, and resuspended in 100 µl of 0.85% NaCl. Equal volumes of donor and recipient suspensions were mixed, and 50 µl of the mixture was spotted onto Mueller‐Hinton (MH) agar plates (Becton, Dickinson and Company) and incubated at 37°C for 16 h. After incubation, cells were recovered from the mating spots, resuspended in 5 ml of 0.85% NaCl, and subjected to ten‐fold serial dilution.

Aliquots were plated onto MH agar containing both colistin (1 µg/ml) and sodium azide (300 µg/ml) to enumerate transconjugants, and onto MH agar containing sodium azide alone to determine transconjugant plus recipient counts. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Conjugation frequency was calculated as the number of transconjugants divided by the total number of transconjugant and recipient cells. Transfer of mcr genes was confirmed by PCR and by determination of colistin MICs of the transconjugants. Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of transconjugants were also determined to assess co‐transfer of additional resistance determinants. Representative transconjugants exhibiting diverse AMR profiles and carrying plasmids of different sizes were selected for further confirmation by Southern hybridization.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis

To further characterize mcr‐positive strains, whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 19 representative isolates after excluding clonal duplicates. Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions for Gram‐negative bacteria. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the QIAseq FX DNA Library Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were normalized and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 1000 platform using the NextSeq 1000/2000 XLEAP‐SBS P1 kit (600 cycles), generating paired‐end reads. Raw reads were quality‐filtered and trimmed using fastp (v0.23.4), and de novo assembly was performed using SPAdes (v3.13.1). Genome annotation was conducted using DFAST (DDBJ Fast Annotation and Submission Tool). The genetic environments surrounding mcr genes were visualized using Easyfig (v2.2.5). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using MLST Finder (v2.0.9). AMR genes, virulence‐associated genes, and plasmid replicons were identified using ResFinder (v4.7.2), VirulenceFinder (v2.0.5), and PlasmidFinder (v2.0.1), respectively, via the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) platform (http://www.genomicepidemiology.org). Core genome single‐nucleotide polymorphism (cgSNP) analysis was performed using Snippy (v4.6.0), with E. coli K‐12 MG1655 (accession no. NC_000913.3) as the reference genome [28]. Reads were mapped to the reference genome, and variants were called using default parameters. Publicly available genomes from meat (n = 10) and clinical samples (n = 8) collected prior to the colistin ban were included for comparative analysis [29, 40] Recombination regions were identified and removed using Gubbins (v3.4.3) [31]. A maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from 8,191 recombination‐filtered core genome SNPs under the GTR + G substitution model with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates using IQ‐TREE [32]. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL [33]. The sequencing data were deposited to the DNA databank of Japan (DDBJ) under BioProject accession number PRJDB42446 with BioSample accessions SAMD01927054‐ SAMD01927072.

Results

Occurrence of mcr Genes in Retail Meat in Thailand and Bacterial Isolation

Out of 152 meat samples (103 chicken and 49 pork), 10 samples (6.7%; 6 chicken and 4 pork) tested were positive for mcr‐1 and/or mcr‐3 by multiplex PCR (Table 1). All positive samples were obtained from open market, while none of the supermarket samples were positive (Table 1). The mcr‐1 gene alone was detected in 10.2% (4/39) of chicken samples and 3.3% (1/30) of pork samples. Co‐occurrence of mcr‐1 and mcr‐3 was observed in 5.1% (2/39) of chicken samples and 10.0% (3/30) of pork samples. Isolation of mcr‐positive bacteria was performed using two selective approaches. In the first approach, MacConkey agar supplemented with colistin was used. Using this method, mcr‐1‐positive isolates (n = 6) were recovered from all six PCR‐positive chicken samples. Although the mcr‐3 gene was detected in two chicken samples, no mcr‐3‐positive isolates were obtained from these samples. In pork samples, mcr‐1‐positive isolates (n = 7) were recovered from all four PCR‐positive samples, and mcr‐3‐positive isolates (n = 3) were obtained from three samples (one carrying mcr‐3 alone and two co‐harboring mcr‐1 and mcr‐3) (Table 2). In the second approach, MacConkey agar supplemented with both colistin and cefotaxime was used to select isolates resistant to both antimicrobials. No growth was observed in two samples (one chicken and one pork). However, this method enabled the recovery of two mcr‐1/mcr‐3‐positive isolates from chicken samples that were PCR‐positive but yielded no isolates using the first approach. From pork samples, three isolates were obtained: one mcr‐1‐positive isolate and two isolates co‐harboring mcr‐1 and mcr‐3, each from different samples (Table 2). In total, 21 isolates were recovered, including 11 from chicken and 10 from pork samples (Table 2). Among these, 14 isolates carried mcr‐1 (9 chicken and 5 pork), one isolate carried mcr‐3 alone (pork), and six isolates co‐harbored mcr‐1 and mcr‐3 (2 chicken and 4 pork). Biochemical identification revealed that 19 of the 21 isolates were E. coli, while two isolates (one from chicken and one from pork) were identified as K. pneumoniae.

Clonal Diversity of mcr‐Positive Isolates

Nineteen mcr‐positive E. coli isolates were analyzed by PFGE, yielding 17 distinct pulsotypes (Figure 1). Two pairs of isolates exhibited identical pulsotypes: TC12.6 and TC16.2 (pulsotype 5), and TC16CSTCTX and TC18CSTCTX (pulsotype 6). Notably, isolates within each pair were obtained from the samples collected on the same day (Figure 1).

Colistin Resistance, Antimicrobial Resistance Profiles, and bla Genes of mcr‐Positive Strains

Colistin resistance was confirmed for all mcr‐positive strains (n = 21; 19 E. coli and 2 K. pneumoniae) by MIC determination. All isolates were resistant to colistin, with MICs ranging from 8 to 16 µg/mL (Table 3). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that all isolates exhibited multidrug‐resistant (MDR) phenotypes (Table 3). Among chicken isolates, resistance was most frequently observed to AMP (100%), TET (100%), CHL (81.9%), NAL (81.9%), and SXT (63.6%). Among pork isolates, resistance was observed to AMP (100%), CHL (100%), TET (90%), STR (90%), GEN (80%), SXT (70%), and KAN (60%). Notably, fosfomycin resistance was detected in five isolates (three [27%] from chicken and two [20%] from pork) (Table 3). An ESBL phenotype was observed in 36.4% (4/11) of chicken isolates and 40% (4/10) of pork isolates (Table 3). Among ESBL producers, bla CTX‐M‐1 and bla TEM were the most prevalent, detected in seven (3 chicken, 4 pork) and six isolates (4 chicken, 2 pork), respectively. Additionally, bla SHV and bla CTX‐M‐9 were each detected in one isolate from chicken and pork, respectively (Table 3).

Localization and Transferability of mcr Genes

Southern hybridization following S1‐PFGE was performed to determine the location of mcr genes. Among chicken isolates (n = 10), nine carried mcr‐1 on plasmids of varying sizes (~32–70 kb), whereas one isolate harbored chromosomal mcr‐1 (Figure S1). Among pork isolates (n = 9), seven carried plasmid‐borne mcr‐1 (~ 32–245 kb), while two isolates carried chromosomal mcr‐1 (Figure S2). All mcr‐3‐positive isolates harbored the gene on plasmids (~70–120 kb). Notably, strain TP15.2, which carried mcr‐3 alone, harbored two copies of mcr‐3, one on a large plasmid (~200 kb) and another on a small plasmid (< 20 kb) (Figure S3).

The transferability of mcr‐carrying plasmids was assessed by conjugation assays. Of the 21 isolates, 15 (eight from chicken and seven from pork) successfully transferred mcr‐carrying plasmids, with conjugation frequencies ranging from ≥ 5.7 × 10−3 to ≥ 2.1 × 10−1 (Table 3). In contrast, six isolates showed no detectable transfer (< 1 × 10−7). Transfer of mcr genes to recipient strain (transconjugant) was confirmed by Southern hybridization (Figure 2). Interestingly, isolates co‐harboring mcr‐1 and mcr‐3 transferred only mcr‐3. Furthermore, two pork isolates carrying mcr‐1, mcr‐3, and ESBL genes co‐transferred mcr‐3 and bla CTX‐M‐1, and the resulting transconjugants exhibited an ESBL phenotype. Southern hybridization confirmed that mcr‐3 and bla CTX‐M‐1 were located on the same plasmid (Figure 3).

Genetic Characteristics of mcr‐Positive Strains

Based on PFGE results, and after excluding clonal duplicates, 19 isolates (17 E. coli and 2 K. pneumoniae) were subjected to whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) to characterize mcr genes and associated genetic features. Sequence analysis revealed that all mcr‐1 sequences were identical to mcr‐1.1 in the NCBI database. Isolates carrying mcr‐1 on ~64 kb plasmids (as determined by Southern hybridization) possessed gene cassettes similar to those first reported in China (accession no. KP347127). Notably, the upstream tnpA element was absent in these isolates, although confirmation requires long‐read sequencing (Figure 4A). In contrast, isolates harboring mcr‐1 on ~32 kb plasmids exhibited genetic structures consistent with IncX4‐type plasmids (accession no. KY075653) (Figure 4A). All mcr‐3 sequences corresponded to mcr‐3.5 (accession no. MF489760) (Figure 4B). Interestingly, one K. pneumoniae isolate (TP20.1) carried mcr‐8 in addition to mcr‐1. The genetic structure of the mcr‐8 in this isolate was similar to that in swine fecal isolate (MG736312) (Figure 4C). To our knowledge, this finding shows the first report of this gene combination in a pork isolate. MLST revealed high clonal diversity among the E. coli isolates. Among the 17 isolates, 16 distinct sequence types (STs) were identified, including ST10, ST34, ST48, ST355, ST446, ST12817 (each represented by one isolate), and ST101 (n = 2), all of which have been previously associated with mcr genes. Additional STs (ST906, ST1648, ST2197, ST3941, ST5995, ST6931, ST9378, and ST11350) were also identified, each represented by a single isolate, while one isolate belonged to a novel or unassigned ST (Table S1). The two K. pneumoniae isolates belonged to ST35 and ST1530 (Table S1).

Analysis of AMR genes confirmed a high burden of multidrug resistance. β‐lactamase genes were widely distributed. Among E. coli isolates recovered on colistin‐supplemented plates, bla TEM and bla CTX‐M‐55 were detected in 90.9% (10/11) and 18.2% (2/11) of isolates, respectively. In contrast, among isolates obtained from plates supplemented with both colistin and cefotaxime, these genes were detected in 50.0% (3/6) and 66.7% (4/6) of isolates, respectively (Table S1). Additional β‐lactamase genes, including bla CTX‐M‐14, bla CMY‐2, and bla LAP‐2, were each identified in one isolate from the colistin/cefotaxime plates. The two K. pneumoniae isolates carried bla SHV, with one also harboring bla TEM (Table S1). Regardless of the isolation method, genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines (tet), aminoglycosides (aadA, aph, aac(3)), sulfonamides (sul), and phenicols (floR) were detected in 100% (19/19), 100% (19/19), 94.7% (18/19), and 52.6% (10/19) of isolates, respectively (Table S1). Quinolone resistance genes, primarily qnrS variants, were identified in 78.9% (15/19) of isolates. The two K. pneumoniae isolates also carried oqxA/B and fosA, conferring resistance to fluoroquinolones and fosfomycin, respectively. Virulence gene analysis revealed a heterogeneous distribution, with adhesion‐associated genes being the most prevalent. All mcr‐positive E. coli isolates and one K. pneumoniae isolate carried fimH (type 1 fimbriae), and 16 E. coli isolates harbored csgA (curlin major subunit). Toxin‐associated genes were also detected, including hemolysin genes (hlyE and hlyF) in 14 E. coli isolates and astA (EAEC heat‐stable enterotoxin) in 6 isolates. Core genome SNP (cgSNP) analysis using E. coli K‐12 MG1655 as the reference genome demonstrated substantial genomic diversity (Figure 5). Notably, pork isolates from this study clustered closely with pig isolates reported prior to the colistin ban, suggesting persistence of mcr‐positive lineages in the food chain despite the ban. For example, isolates TP42.4 and TP46.3 clustered with pre‐ban pig strains (JAVLUP01.1 and JAVHZH01.1), with SNP differences of 286 and 677, respectively. Similarly, ST10 isolates from different sources clustered together (Figure 5), indicating that this lineage may serve as a stable host for mcr genes across environments. Furthermore, pre‐ban human isolates (JACGZR01.1 and JACGZQ02.1) clustered with a post‐ban chicken isolate (TC12.6), with SNP differences of 511 and 512, respectively. This observation supports the concept that strains from different sequence types may still belong to broader evolutionary lineages [34], suggesting possible cross‐sector transmission and/or persistence of mcr‐positive strains in the food chain.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical knowledge gap regarding the role of retail meat in the transmission of mcr genes following colistin restriction policies in Thailand. Our results revealed a clear contrast in the prevalence of mcr genes in meat samples from different retail market types. All meat samples obtained from supermarkets were negative for mcr genes, whereas substantial contamination was observed in meat samples from open markets. Specifically, mcr‐1 was detected in 15.4% of chicken and 13.3% of pork samples, while mcr‐3 was identified in 5.1% and 10.0%, respectively (Table 1). The absence of mcr genes in meat samples from supermarkets likely reflects stricter hygiene and handling practices and antimicrobial stewardship practices in large‐scale commercial production systems [35]. In contrast, the higher prevalence observed in meats from open markets suggests that these environments may act as reservoirs for the zoonotic transmission of colistin resistance. Open markets are typically supplied by smallholder farms, where antimicrobial use is more difficult to monitor and regulate [36]. In addition, the sale of unpackaged meat at ambient temperatures increases the risk of environmental contamination, including transmission via vectors such as flies, which have been shown to carry mcr‐positive bacteria [37]. The prevalence observed in this study is consistent with recent post‐ban surveillance. For example Noenchat et al. [38], reported mcr‐1 in 14.3% of Salmonella isolates from slaughterhouse chickens. Notably, the prevalence in retail chicken meat in the present study exceeds that reported in farm‐level fecal samples (8.5%) one year after the ban [14], suggesting that contamination may be amplified during processing, handling, or distribution.

The detection of mcr genes in pork samples is particularly concerning, as the observed prevalence exceeds pre‐ban levels reported in Thailand [12]. Khanawapee et al. [12] detected mcr genes in 7.8% of slaughtered pigs, and Pungpian et al. [39] reported much lower rates (0.69%). Although differences in sampling strategies (e.g., surface swabbing vs. homogenization), isolation protocols, and geographic regions may partly explain these discrepancies, the persistence of mcr genes in retail pork suggests that the expected decline following colistin restriction has not yet been fully realized at the consumer level. Before the implementation of colistin restrictions in Japan, a study by Fukuda et. al (2018) reported higher colistin resistance among E. coli isolates from diseased pigs (60.8%) than those from healthy pigs (9.5%), highlighting the substantial selective pressure associated with colistin use in swine production systems in many countries. The co‐occurrence of mcr‐1 and mcr‐3 (28.6% of isolates) represents a significant public health concern. Although this combination may not necessarily increase colistin MICs [40], it expands the genetic reservoir of resistance and enhances the potential for horizontal gene transfer. Similar co‐occurrence has been reported in clinical multidrug‐resistant E. coli isolates in Thailand [40], suggesting that contaminated meat may contribute to the transmission of such strains to humans. Consistent with previous reports, E. coli was the predominant host of mcr genes [13, 30]. All isolates exhibited multidrug resistance and phenotypic resistance to colistin (MICs 8–16 µg/mL), indicating limited therapeutic options in clinical settings. High resistance rates to commonly used antimicrobials such as ampicillin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol reflect antimicrobial usage patterns in livestock production in Thailand [41]. Importantly, resistance to fosfomycin, an antimicrobial classified as critically important for human medicine, was detected in multiple isolates, raising additional concern regarding treatment options [42]. The co‐occurrence of mcr genes with ESBL determinants further exacerbates this issue. In this study, ESBL‐producing mcr‐positive strains were detected in both chicken and pork meats. Notably, two pork isolates carried mcr‐3 and bla CTX‐M‐1 on the same plasmid, suggesting that co‐selection driven by β‐lactam use may contribute to the persistence of colistin resistance even in the absence of direct colistin pressure. This finding is consistent with previous reports from Thailand and other regions [7, 30, 43, 44].

Furthermore, this study reports for the first time the isolation of ESBL‐producing, mcr‐1‐positive K. pneumoniae from chicken meat in Thailand, highlighting poultry as a potential reservoir of multidrug‐resistant pathogens. The recent detection of ESBL‐producing, colistin‐resistant K. pneumoniae in environmental samples surrounding livestock farms [45] further supports the possibility of transmission across animal, environmental, and food sectors. The genomic context of mcr genes provides insight into their dissemination dynamics. While chromosomal integration was observed in a minority of isolates, plasmid‐mediated mcr genes predominated, with a wide range of plasmid sizes (~32–240 kb). Conjugation assays confirmed high transfer frequencies in multiple isolates, indicating a strong potential for horizontal dissemination. A particularly notable finding was the identification of a K. pneumoniae isolate carrying both mcr‐1 and mcr‐8, representing the first report of this combination in retail pork. Interestingly, mcr‐8 was detected only after WGS of this isolate. We retrospectively attempted to detect mcr‐6 to mcr‐9 in all the crude DNA templates using the multiplex PCR assay described by Borowiak et al. [46]. However, probably due to using crude DNA templates, many non‐specific amplifications were observed, and the reliable results cannot be interpreted (data not shown). Since the PCR assay developed by Borowiak et al. was validated using bacterial isolates rather than crude DNA templates, it may not be suitable for crude samples. Therefore, the presence of other mcr variants in the meat samples cannot be excluded. This strain also exhibited multidrug resistance and belonged to ST35, a lineage associated with virulence. Although rare, this gene combination has been reported in clinical isolates [47], and its presence in the food chain suggests ongoing genetic exchange.

MLST and cgSNP analyses revealed high genetic diversity and provided evidence of persistence of mcr‐positive lineages across time. Several sequence types previously associated with mcr carriage (e.g., ST10, ST34, ST48, ST101) were identified, indicating their continued role as reservoirs. Phylogenetic clustering with pre‐ban isolates from animals and humans suggests that certain lineages have persisted despite regulatory interventions. Moreover, the identification of mcr genes in diverse and previously unreported sequence types indicates ongoing dissemination across bacterial populations. The detection of virulence‐associated genes in mcr‐positive isolates further increases their clinical relevance. Adhesion factors such as fimH and csgA were highly prevalent and may contribute to colonization and persistence. Toxin‐associated genes, including astA, were also identified, suggesting the potential for diarrheagenic infections linked to contaminated meat [48]. Overall, these findings demonstrate that mcr‐positive, multidrug‐resistant bacteria persist in the Thai food chain despite colistin restriction policies. The continued presence of these strains, particularly in open‐market meat, highlights the importance of strengthening surveillance at the retail level and implementing integrated One Health strategies to mitigate the spread of antimicrobial resistance.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the prevalence and molecular characteristics of mcr genes and mcr‐harboring bacteria in retail meat in Thailand following the national ban on prophylactic colistin use in food producing animals. The detection of mcr‐1 and mcr‐3 in chicken and pork from open markets highlights retail meat as an important reservoir for the transmission of colistin resistance. Notably, the identification of an mcr‐1‐positive, ESBL‐producing, multidrug‐resistant K. pneumoniae from chicken meat represents the first report of such a strain in Thailand and raises concerns regarding the emergence of high‐risk clones under ongoing antimicrobial pressure. The persistence of mcr‐positive strains despite the colistin ban suggests that continued or uncontrolled use of other antimicrobials, particularly in small‐scale farming systems, may sustain their circulation. These findings underscore the urgent need for systematic surveillance of mcr genes in retail meat and for strengthened enforcement of antimicrobial stewardship across the food production chain. Integration of retail meat monitoring into Thailand's One Health AMR framework will be essential to limit the dissemination of colistin resistance and to protect both public health and food safety.
