Insight into the Rate-Determining Step in Photocatalytic Z-Scheme Overall Water Splitting by Employing A Series of Perovskite RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) as Model Photocatalysts
Authors: Hai Zou, Yu Qi, Shiwen Du, Yunfeng Bao, Xueshang Xin, Wenjun Fan, Yejun Xiao, Shengye Jin, Zhaochi Feng, Fuxiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Photocatalysis is an intricate process that involves a multitude of physical and chemical factors operating across diverse temporal and spatial scales. Identifying the dominant factors that influence photocatalyst performance is one of the central challenges in the field. Here, we synthesized a series of perovskite RTaON2 semiconductors with different A-site rare earth atoms (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) as model photocatalysts to discuss the influence of the A-site modulation on their local structures as well as both physical and chemical properties and to get insight into the rate-determining step in photocatalytic Z-scheme overall water splitting (OWS). It is interesting to find that, with a decreasing ionic radius of the A-site cations, the RTaON2 compounds exhibit continuous blue shift of light absorption and a concomitant reduction in the lifetime of photogenerated carriers, revealing a significant influence of A-site atoms on the light absorption and charge separation processes. On the other hand, the A-site atomic substitution was revealed to significantly modulate the valence band positions as well as surface oxidation kinetics. By employing the Pt-modified RTaON2 as H2-evolving photocatalysts, the activity of photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS for hydrogen production on them is found to be determined by its surface oxidation process instead of light absorption or charge separation. Our results give the first experimental demonstration of the rate-determining step during the photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS processes, as should be instructive for the design and development of other efficient solar-to-chemical energy conversion systems.

Introduction

Hydrogen energy, generated through photocatalytic water splitting under solar irradiation, stands out as a highly anticipated clean energy alternative.1−3 Photocatalytic overall water splitting (OWS) is a multifaceted process that can be broadly divided into three sequential stages: light absorption, charge separation and transfer, and surface catalytic reaction.4 It is important to recognize that photocatalysis encompasses a complex interplay of physicochemical phenomena across various temporal and spatial scales, with numerous interdependent factors at play.5−7 For instance, the light absorption efficiency of a semiconductor is intricately linked to the position of its conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB), which in turn dictates the thermodynamic driving force for the ensuing surface catalytic reactions.8,9 Furthermore, the concentration of charge carriers during the separation and transfer process is influenced by both the generation of carriers upon light absorption and their subsequent consumption at recombination centers.10,11 As for cocatalyst, in addition to reducing the reaction overpotential and promoting the reaction, it will also facilitate charge separation and transfer by Schottky junction and built-in electric field.12−14 Despite a substantial literature has addressed the impact of individual factors on photocatalytic water splitting, to date, a definite understanding of the rate-determining step and interrelationships among these factors remains elusive in consideration of the structural and process complexity during photocatalysis.

The semiconductor photocatalysts with a perovskite ABX3 structure have attracted extensive attention due to their good physical and chemical properties.15−17 It is general to know that the choice of the B-site cation is pivotal for the material to possess a suitable CB for reduction reactions, while the anions at the X site play a crucial role in determining the VB positions. Comparatively, the modulation of A-site cations has been given less attention due to their main functionality of maintaining perovskite framework.18 Recently, increasing work demonstrates a remarkable effect of A-site modulation on their physical and chemical properties, in which some Ta-based alkali metal oxides (LiTaO3 (4.7 eV), NaTaO3 (4.0 eV), and KTaO3 (3.6 eV)) and Ta-based alkali earth metal oxynitrides (CaTaON2 (2.4 eV), SrTaON2 (2.1 eV), and BaTaON2 (1.9 eV)) have been investigated.19,20 Given the similar atomic radii of rare earth elements, the strategic substitution of A-site cations with rare earth atoms offers a unique opportunity to synthesize a series of homologous compounds. In this case, they share a common compositional framework and structural scaffold, providing an ideal platform to gain insight into the intrinsic factor that governs the photocatalytic activity.

Here, a series of perovskite RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) with different A-site atoms have been synthesized to examine the systematic variation in the photophysical and photochemical properties of these materials. Utilizing these materials as model photocatalysts, we have attempted to disentangle the complex interplay of factors influencing photocatalytic activity and to identify the rate-determining step in photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS. A suite of characterization techniques was employed to analyze the band structure and carrier dynamics, shedding light on the interrelated phenomena of light absorption efficiency and charge separation efficiency. Our results reveal that the RTaON2 materials with a more positive VB demonstrate enhanced photocatalytic activity in the proton reduction reaction and within a Z-scheme OWS system, even though these materials absorb fewer photons and exhibit a shorter carrier lifetime. Our findings suggest that the efficiency of the surface oxidation reaction within the photocatalytic system is a critical determinant of overall photocatalytic activity, which is considered as the rate-determining step of the Z-scheme OWS.

Results and Discussion

A series of perovskite RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) were obtained by nitriding the corresponding RTaO4 precursor at 1273 K for 10 h. The resulting powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for the prepared samples are presented in Figure 1a. Notably, the XRD peak positions across the RTaON2 series exhibit subtle yet systematic shifts to higher angles from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2, which is attributed to the progressive decrease in lattice parameters by the reduction in ionic radius of the rare earth elements from Pr3+ (1.126 Å), Nd3+ (1.109 Å), and Sm3+ (1.079 Å) to Gd3+ (1.053 Å).21 Concurrently, the diminishing A-site ionic radius influences the perovskite’s tolerance factor, t, which is calculated as . Here, RA, RB, and RO denote the ionic radii of the A-site, B-site, and oxygen atoms, respectively. As the rare earth elements progress from Pr to Gd, the tolerance factor increasingly deviates from the ideal value of 1, indicative of a departure from the optimal perovskite structure. This shift is marked by the appearance of double diffraction peaks at the (020) and (132) positions in the XRD pattern, underscoring the structural perturbations induced by the variation in ionic radii. To precisely ascertain the cell parameters, XRD refinement was performed, as detailed in Figure S1, and reasonable convergence factors were obtained. The tabulated results in Table S1 indicate that all RTaON2 compounds crystallize in the Pnma (62) space group with cell parameters that correlate with the ionic radii of the respective rare earth elements. The crystallographic model of GdTaON2, depicted in Figure 1b, is accompanied by specific atomic coordinates provided in Table S2. In this structure, Gd atoms are positioned at the 4c Wyckoff sites, while the O or N atoms at the 4c and 8d sites form edge-shared octahedra with Ta atoms at the 4b sites, creating vertex-connected chains. These observations consolidate the structural uniformity across the RTaON2 series. Meanwhile, the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of RTaON2 in Figure 1c–f reveal that all RTaON2 samples exhibit small particle aggregation with a porous structure. The formation of the porous structure in oxynitrides is believed to result from the cell volume shrinkage due to the replacement of oxide ions (O2–) with nitride ions (N3−) during the nitridation process.22 Additionally, the variation in surface porosity is conjectured to correlate with the rate of the nitriding phase transition, where a quicker transition potentially leads to a greater number of surface voids. To quantitatively assess the surface area, the N2 adsorption–desorption experiment was performed (Figure S2), based on which the derived Brunauer–Emmett–Teller areas for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2 were calculated to be 6, 7, 5, and 4 m2 g–1, respectively. Besides, the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses, as shown in Figure 1h–k for GdTaON2 and in Figure S3 for the other compounds, confirm the uniform distribution of the constituent elements, attesting to the structural homogeneity of the synthesized materials. Collectively, these findings establish that the RTaON2 series shares similar structural, morphological, and surface area characteristics.

Furthermore, an in-depth analysis of the band structure characteristics is presented in Figure 2. Ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS) measurements indicate a progressive blue shift in the absorption edge of the RTaON2 series with an increasing atomic number of the incorporated rare earth elements (Figure 2a). Concurrently, the absorption coefficient preceding the absorption band edge can be deduced from the y-axis value of the Kubelka–Munk Function. The variation in the rare earth atoms induces only a modest alteration in the absorption coefficient. This implies that the influence of the absorption coefficient on the quantity of absorbed photons before the band edge is relatively insignificant within the visible light spectrum. This trend suggests a corresponding decrease in the number of photons from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2 that can be absorbed and subsequently utilized for photocatalysis. Additionally, calculations of the density of states (DOS) were performed to elucidate the electronic composition of the band structure. As exhibited in Figure 2b, the VB of GdTaON2 is mainly constituted of the N 2p orbital, and the CB is mainly composed of the Ta 5d orbital. Further insight into the band structure is provided by the analysis presented in Figure 2c, which confirms that GdTaON2, akin to PrTaON2, NdTaON2, and SmTaON2 (as shown in Figure S4), exhibits the properties of a direct band gap semiconductor. Based on it, the band gaps for these materials determined using Tauc plots are 2.09, 2.16, 2.25, and 2.35 eV for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2, respectively (Figure 2d). Additionally, the flatband potentials of the RTaON2 series were determined using Mott–Schottky plots in 0.5 M Na2SO4, which included 25 mM of both K4[Fe(CN)6] and K3[Fe(CN)6] for a better ensure a well-defined depletion layer. The experiments were conducted under nitrogen-purged conditions to eliminate air. Also, the CB positions for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2 are calculated to be −0.46, −0.43, −0.28, and −0.17 V (vs RHE), respectively (Figure S6 and Table S3). Meanwhile, the VB positions were ascertained through ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy measurements (UPS) (Figure S7), which were in good agreement with the results calculated from the Mott–Schottky and Tauc plots with an error of less than 0.1 eV (Table S3 and Table S4). Integrating the above analysis, we present a schematic representation of the RTaON2 band structure in Figure 2f. It is evident that the band structures for all RTaON2 compositions span the necessary potential range to drive both proton reduction and water oxidation reactions, signifying the thermodynamic viability of these processes. Furthermore, upon progression from PrTaON2 to NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2, there is a gradual positive shift observed at both the CB and VB positions. This trend suggests a diminishing driving force for the reduction reaction, while the driving force for the oxidation reaction is oppositely increased.

Figure 3 presents the characterization of charge separation and transfer processes, which are significantly influenced by the recombination of carriers and are pivotal in determining their lifetime.24 Here, the lifetime of carriers was evaluated by both time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and the transient absorption spectrum (TAS). TRPL is a fluorescence technique that probes the time-dependent dynamics of the excited-state radiative transition spectrum under irradiation with pulsed monochromatic light, thereby providing insights into the overall lifetime of photogenerated electrons and holes.25 According to the fitting results of TRPL in Figure 3a and Table S5, the average lifetimes (τAV) for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2 are 11.6, 10.2, 6.78, and 4.26 ns, respectively. Additionally, TAS is considered a “pump-probe” technique, which can detect the concentration of a specific species by detecting changes in light absorbance.26,27 Generally, the intensive absorption peaks at the range of 20,000–14,000 cm–1 result from the absorption of photogenerated holes, peaks at the range of 14,000–4000 cm–1 attribute to the deeply trapped electron and/or holes, and peaks below 4000 cm–1 should be assigned to free electron.28−30 As shown in Figure S8, the pronounced absorption peak at about 20,000 cm–1 for RTaON2 is assigned to the photogenerated holes. The TAS decay fitting in Figure 3b and the summarized data in Table S6 yield average lifetimes of 0.92 ps for PrTaON2, 0.11 ps for NdTaON2, 0.070 ps for SmTaON2, and 0.068 ps for GdTaON2. An extended carrier lifetime is indicative of a lower recombination rate, which is beneficial for improving the efficiency of charge separation and transfer. Consequently, the efficiency of charge separation and transfer is inferred to decrease progressively from PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2. The observed modifications in the band structure and carrier separation efficiency of RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) are predominantly ascribed to structural alterations induced by the incorporation of distinct R atoms. Theoretical calculations (Figure 2b and Figure S4) suggest that the CB and VB are predominantly composed of Ta 5d and N 2p orbitals, respectively; therefore, variations in the Ta–N bond length and bond angles are considered to be critical factors. To elucidate these structural nuances, X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) for Ta L-edge was measured. As displayed in Figure S9, the white line intensity peak positions indicated the valence states of the Ta element were decreased from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2. The calculated oxidation states of Ta element in RTaON2 compounds, relative to Ta2O5 and Ta metal, are +4.7, +4.5, +4.3, and +4.2 for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2, respectively. The Fourier transform-extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra of Ta L-edges are presented in Figure 3c, where the main peaks located between 1.5 and 2 Å correspond to the first coordination shell of the Ta–O/N bond. Upon magnifying the spectral region, it is observed that the Ta–O/N bond length is the shortest in PrTaON2 and remains consistent across NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2. This observation indicates that the variation in the band gap is not attributable to changes in the Ta–O/N bond length, a finding that diverges from some previous studies.31 Furthermore, it is noted that the bond angle between the Ta–O and N octahedra progressively decreases with the contraction of the rare earth atomic radius, as detailed in Table S7. This reduction in bond angle diminishes the symmetry of the material, leading to a reduced overlap between Ta and O/N orbitals.32 Therefore, the widening band gap and decreasing carrier lifetime were speculated to arise from the decreasing angle between the Ta–O/N octahedra, which impacts the electronic structure and, by extension, the photocatalytic properties of the RTaON2 materials.

In addition, the photocatalytic activities of the RTaON2 series (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) for water splitting were assessed to determine the principal factors influencing their performance. The proton reduction half-reaction was facilitated with platinum (Pt) as a cocatalyst and methanol as a sacrificial hole scavenger. As illustrated in Figure 4a and Figure S10, the H2-evolving rate increases sequentially from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2, a trend that does not align with the order of the carrier lifetime or photon absorption efficiency. It is generally known that the photocatalytic activity is integrally determined by the efficiency of light absorption, charge separation, and surface catalysis, and the light absorption and carrier lifetime did not significantly impact the photocatalytic activity in this work. Accordingly, it is reasonable to deduce that the surface catalysis should be the rate-determining step of the photocatalysis in this work. Additionally, the driving force for the photocatalytic reaction, inferred from the positions of the CB and VB, illustrated in Figure 2f, was considered. The continuous decrease in the CB position from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2 is not anticipated to benefit activity enhancement. Conversely, the positive shift in the VB position, which provides a larger driving force for the surface reaction, is believed to have a positive impact on the observed increase in activity, as illustrated in Scheme S1.

To validate this hypothesis, the oxidation reaction was further probed using photoelectrochemical techniques. Figure 4b presents the photocurrent responses in a current–potential curve for the RTaON2 series in a Na2SO4 solution with CH3OH added to quench photogenerated holes. The observed photocurrent sequential increase from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2 correlates well with the trend in the H2-evolution activity. Meanwhile, the open-circuit potential under interrupted illumination was measured to appraise the photoinduced driving voltage. As shown in Figure S11, a pronounced potential drop is observed upon transitioning from dark to light conditions, indicative of the n-type semiconductor characteristic of the RTaON2 series and consistent with the positive slope observed in the Mott–Schottky analysis (Figure 2e). The observed photovoltages for PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2 are 77, 117, 128, and 187 mV, respectively. The photovoltage is attributed to the accumulation of photogenerated holes at the electrode surface, which shifts the quasi-Fermi level of the holes closer to the VB position, and higher photovoltage results from a more positive VB position.33 Therefore, the potential change observed during light-dark cycling is a direct reflection of the photodriven oxidation potential, with a higher photovoltage indicating a greater driving force for the oxidation reaction.34 Moreover, electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were conducted to evaluate the resistance of charge transfer (Rct), which is determined by oxidation reaction kinetics here. As shown in Figure S12 and Table S8, the smaller semicircle corresponding to smaller Rct values implies a progressively facilitated water oxidation reaction. This trend suggests that the oxidation kinetics in the presence of CH3OH as a hole scavenger increase in the order of PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2. Furthermore, the photocatalytic proton reduction activity was explored through the assembly of a photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS system. This system integrates Pt/RTaON2 as the H2-evolving photocatalyst, PtOx/WO3 as the O2-evolving photocatalyst, and IO3–/I– as the electron shuttle ions (Scheme S2). As presented in Figure 4c and Figure S13, the ratio of evolved H2 and O2 closely approximates the stoichiometric ratio of 2:1, indicating the successful establishment of the Z-scheme OWS system. The OWS activity exhibits a trend of an increase from PrTaON2 to GdTaON2, mirroring the behavior observed in the photocatalytic proton reduction half-reaction within a CH3OH solution, as depicted in Figure 4a. Additionally, the chopped photocurrent responses observed in Figure 4d and the corresponding Rct derived from EIS in Figure S14 are in harmony with the trends of H2 and O2 evolution within the Z-scheme system. Based on the above-mentioned discussion, the activity for proton reduction on RTaON2 correlates with the observed trend in electrochemical anodic oxidation reactions. It is thus logical to deduce that the surface oxidation reaction is a determinant factor in the photocatalytic proton reduction performance. This inference implies that the oxidation reaction significantly influences the activity of the photocatalytic OWS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a series of RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) with analogous structural and morphological features were synthesized and employed as model photocatalysts to discuss the role of various factors on photocatalyst and to identify the rate-determining step in photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS. Through a comprehensive characterization of photophysical properties, we observed a systematic blue shift in the absorption edge of the RTaON2 series, accompanied by a corresponding positive shift in the positions of both the CB and VB across the sequence PrTaON2, NdTaON2, SmTaON2, and GdTaON2. Concurrently, there was a gradual decrease in the carrier lifetime, following the same trend. Notably, our findings elucidate that samples exhibiting a narrower absorption edge and shorter carrier lifetime display enhanced photocatalytic activity for proton reduction, as mainly result from enhanced surface oxidation driving force as well as oxidation kinetics. This experimental evidence underscores the direct influence of oxidation kinetics on the photocatalytic activity of reduction reactions. The present study offers a valuable framework for understanding the rate-determining step in photocatalytic OWS, providing instructive insights for the strategic design of high-efficiency photocatalytic systems.

Experimental Section

Preparation of RTaON2

RTaON2 was obtained by nitriding the corresponding RTaO4 precursors. To produce RTaO4, stoichiometric amounts of Ta2O5 and rare earth oxide were combined with an equal mass of KCl in an agate mortar. The mixture was thoroughly ground with a suitable amount of ethanol to ensure a uniform blend. After grinding to the point of dryness, the mixture was transferred into an alumina crucible and heated at a rate of 10 K per minute to a temperature of 1423 K, where it was maintained for 5 h. Upon cooling, the resultant salt was removed by washing with deionized water, followed by drying in an oven at 333 K. The final step involved nitriding the dried powder at 1273 K under a flow of ammonia gas at a rate of 250 mL per minute for 10 h, yielding the desired RTaON2 product.

Computational Method

All the calculations in the present work were performed using the density functional theory method implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP).35,36 The projector augmented wave (PAW)37 and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)38 with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)39 functional were employed for the total energies calculations. The energy cutoff for the calculations was adopted as 500 eV. With a mesh of 5 × 5 × 3 of the k-point grid, the structure and electronic properties of bulk RTaON2 (R = Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) were optimized accordingly. During the structure optimization, all atomic positions and lattice parameters were fully relaxed with no symmetry restriction. The force criteria and energy criteria were set as 0.01 eV·Å–1 and 1.0 × 10–7 eV, respectively. During the calculations, the PBE + U approach was employed to describe the localized f-electrons and d-electrons of rare element elements and Ta element, with the U values chosen as 4.5, 6.0, 6.0, 6.0, and 1.5 eV for Pr 4f, Nd 4f, Sm 4f, Gd 4f, and Ta 5d states according to the previous reports,40−44 respectively.

Photocatalytic Tests

Photocatalytic water splitting was carried out in a Pyrex top irradiation-type reaction vessel with a closed system connected to the gas chromatogram. For hydrogen evolution, 50 mg of sample was dispersed in 100 mL of solution by ultrasound, and 20 vol % methanol was used as the hole sacrificial reagent. For the Z-scheme OWS, 50 mg of 1.5 wt % Pt/RTaON2 (R= Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) and 75 mg of 0.5 wt % PtOx/WO3 were dispersed in 1 mM KI solution. The initial input concentration of IO3– was null, and the IO3– ions were generated through the oxidation of I– in the course of the photocatalytic Z-scheme OWS process. The air was completely removed by pumping for 30 min before each photocatalytic reaction. A 300 W xenon lamp with a filtration mirror (Hoya, L-42; λ ≥ 420 nm) was employed as the light source. The current of the xenon lamp was set at 15 A. The reaction solution was maintained at 288 K by circulating water during the photocatalysis process.

Electrochemical Tests

The electrode was prepared by electrophoretic deposition. The sample (50 mg) was dispersed in 50 mL of acetone solution containing 20 mg of iodine by ultrasound for 30 min. Two pieces of parallel FTO electrodes (1 × 2 cm2) were immersed in this solution. The sample powder was deposited on the cathode at a voltage of 30 V for 5 min by a potentiostat (ITECH IT6834). After dropping 10 μL of TaCl5 methanol solution (10 mM) 5 times, the electrode was heated at 723 K for 30 min under NH3 flow as a necking treatment. The Mott–Schottky plots were recorded on a Princeton electrochemical workstation. A Pt electrode and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The electrolyte was 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution containing 25 mM potassium ferricyanide (K4[Fe(CN)6]) and 25 mM potassium ferrocyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]), under nitrogen-purged conditions to exclude air. The amplitude of the alternating current potential was set as 50 mV. The photoelectrochemistry measurements were performed on a powerful electrochemical workstation (Modulab Xm, AMETEK). A Pt electrode and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The linear sweep voltammetry curves were performed in 0.1 M KI or 0.5 M Na2SO4 solution containing 10 vol % methanol with chopped light. The tested frequency range for EIS was 0.1 Hz–10 kHz with back-irradiation using a 300 W xenon lamp (λ ≥ 420 nm). A fitting analysis of the equivalent circuit was performed with Z-view software.
