Chromosome-scale genome assembly and annotation of Jonah’s icefish Neopagetopsis ionah
Authors: Shunan Cao, Wenhao Li, Gianfranco Santovito, Minhao Gao, Gaojing Fan, Shouwen Jiang, Ruiqin Hu, Wanying Zhai, Zhiwei Gao, Bo Xu, Qianghua Xu, Yong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Jonah’s icefish Neopagetopsis ionah is endemic to the frigid, oxygen-rich waters of the Southern Ocean. This species has evolved remarkable genetic and physiological adaptations, notably the loss of functional hemoglobin, making it an exemplary vertebrate model for studying evolutionary loss is gain and adaptation to extreme environments. In this study, we present a chromosome-level genome assembly of N. ionah (1.27 Gb) integrating Illumina, PacBio HiFi, and Hi-C sequencing data. The assembly comprises 24 chromosomes, with an anchoring efficiency of 96.8%. Genome annotation revealed that 42.14% of the genome consists of repetitive elements and identified 31,777 protein-coding genes. The high genomic collinearity with related species corroborates the assembly’s accuracy at the macroscale. This high-quality reference genome provides a crucial resource for elucidating the genetic basis of cold adaptation, trait loss, and the unique cardiovascular innovations in Antarctic icefish.

Background & Summary

Antarctica is encircled by continental shelf waters that are frigid and glacial, exhibiting relatively stable temperatures from −1.86 °C to +1 °C, alongside consistently high oxygen levels1. As the Southern Ocean cooled to subzero temperatures, Antarctic icefish evolved genetic adaptations to thrive in this hyperoxygenated environment. Notably, Antarctic icefish represent a remarkable vertebrate group capable of surviving without hemoglobin in their red blood cells, resulting in optically clear blood2–4. Consequently, Antarctic icefish serve as a model for studying anemias4–6. These “anemic” fish exemplify gene loss and specialization, providing an ideal model to investigate scenarios of loss is gain or loss without penalty.

The loss of the vertebrate oxygen transporter is a characteristic feature of icefish1. In these species, oxygen is transported exclusively in physical solution, resulting in an oxygen-carrying capacity per unit of blood volume that is less than 10% of that observed in closely related red-blooded Antarctic notothenioid fish3,7. This gene loss represents a major adaptive strategy6,8, prompting the evolution of an alternative cardio-circulatory design. Firstly, various modifications have occurred in the cardiovascular system to enhance oxygen delivery9,10. Icefish possess a hypertrophic heart, with a weight-specific cardiac output that is four to five times greater than that of red-blooded species, making them a model for cardiomegaly6. Secondly, mitochondrial density in the heart and skeletal muscle of icefish is exceptionally high, thereby enhancing oxygen storage and diffusion within cells11. Thirdly, icefish exhibit increased skin vascularization, facilitating molecular oxygen absorption through the skin, along with increased blood vessel lumens, reduced blood viscosity12, and hyperbranching of the vasculature10. These unique phenotypic traits are adaptations to the loss of the oxygen transporter gene.

Recent years have seen the publication of new Antarctic icefish genomes, offering a unique genomic perspective6,13. The comparative and population genomics of an Antarctic icefish, Chionodraco hamatus, elucidate mechanisms underlying glacier-driven adaptive radiation14. Additionally, gene families associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis have expanded in the genome of blackfin icefish, Chaenocephalus aceratus, indicating molecular adaptations to extreme environments15,16. To investigate secondary temperate adaptation, the genomes of the pike icefish, Champsocephalus esox, which has colonized temperate South American waters, and its Antarctic sister species, C. gunnari, were sequenced and assembled17. However, the limited number of icefish species included in each study, along with the scarcity of sibling species, limits the ability to investigate the adaptive mechanisms associated with icefish in depth. Therefore, it is essential to integrate the available high-quality icefish genomes with the recently published genomes of red-blooded Antarctic fish to identify icefish-specific mutations.

Jonah’s icefish, scientifically known as Neopagetopsis ionah, is a member of the family Channichthyidae and is the sole representative of the genus Neopagetopsis18. Notably, unlike the other 15 species of icefish, which retain only a pseudogene comprising the 3′ fragment of the α-globin gene, Jonah’s icefish possesses a complete but nonfunctional adult αβ-globin complex, which may have originated via interspecific introgression1,19. Consequently, the globin pseudogene complex of N. ionah is considered a “genomic fossil“1. Jonah’s icefish is distributed broadly, potentially in a circumpolar pattern across Antarctic and Sub-Antarctic regions. Benthopelagic adults have been documented in the Weddell Sea, Kapp Norvegica, Halley Bay, Vahsel Bay, and the Ross Sea, while pelagic juveniles have been sampled from the Weddell Sea, South Shetland Islands, and McMurdo Sound20. Similar to other Antarctic icefish species, Jonah’s icefish demonstrates low fecundity and produces large eggs, which are associated with parental care and nesting behaviors21. In situ observations have revealed exceptionally high fish biomass. A substantial spawning aggregation of the notothenioid icefish has been identified, with nesting grounds covering at least 240 km² of the Weddell Sea seabed. The colony, comprising approximately 60 million active nests, indicates that these nesting sites significantly influence the entire Weddell Sea ecosystem20,21. In this study, we present a chromosome-scale reference genome for N. ionah, developed using an integrative methodology that incorporates Illumina short-read sequencing, PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing, and Hi-C chromatin interaction mapping. This genomic resource is of significant importance to the Antarctic ecosystem and will facilitate further research on the genetic and evolutionary innovations of Antarctic icefish.

Methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

The adult specimen of Jonah’s icefish was collected from Prydz Bay (66°30′04.64″S, 72°59′35.86″E) on January 25, 2024, during the 40th Chinese Antarctic Research Expedition. Following collection, the specimen was transported to the Polar Sample Repository at the Polar Research Institute of China and stored at −80 °C. All handling of fish was carried out in accordance with the guidelines on the care and use of animals for scientific purposes set up by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Ocean University (SHOU), Shanghai, China (Approved No. SHOU-DW-2023-041). All experimental procedures and reporting in this study were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Muscle tissue was excised and preserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA and DNA extraction. RNA was isolated from muscle tissue using the TRIzol reagent. The concentration and purity of the isolated nucleic acids were determined utilizing a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), while their integrity was assessed using a LabChip GX system (PerkinElmer). Subsequently, the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (13533ES96) was employed for the construction of the transcriptome library. DNA was extracted using a CTAB-based method. Concentration of DNA was measured using a Qubit fluorometer (YEASEN) with the 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit for Qubit. Integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. In Hi-C, formaldehyde crosslinking preserves DNA interactions, followed by restriction enzyme digestion (DpnII) and biotin labeling. The DNA was then circularized, crosslinks were reversed, and biotin-tagged fragments were enriched using streptavidin beads. DNA libraries were constructed utilizing the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (ND617-02) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Furthermore, the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0 was utilized for the preparation of DNA and RNA libraries for PacBio sequencing.

The Illumina sequencing platform (NovaSeq X Plus) produced 60.95 Gb of paired-end (150 bp) reads, providing a total sequencing depth of approximately 47.80X. In contrast, HiFi sequencing (Revio, PacBio) generated 33.63 Gb of data with an average read length of 14.88 kb, yielding approximately 26.5X sequencing depth. Additionally, Hi-C sequencing yielded 461.36 million reads, culminating in 138.04 Gb of high-quality data generated on the Illumina platform (Table 1). The mitochondrial COI, 12S, and 16S rRNA gene sequences were retrieved from the genome assembly by MitoZ22 (v3.6) and aligned with reference sequences from GenBank (Sequence ID: NC_057671.1; Description: N. ionah mitochondrion, complete genome), confirming that the specimen belonged to N. ionah.

De novo assembly of the N. ionah genome

A chromosome-scale genome assembly of N. ionah was developed using an integrated methodology that combined Illumina short-read sequencing, PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing, and Hi-C scaffolding. The genome size, repetitive sequence ratio, and heterozygosity were evaluated by constructing a k-mer (k = 19) frequency distribution, utilizing data from a 350 bp insert library. K-mer counting was performed with Jellyfish23 (v2.1.4), using the parameter -h 1000000000, and the distribution was modeled with GenomeScope24 (v2.0), with the parameters -k 19 -p 2. Estimates derived from Illumina sequencing data indicate a diploid genome for this species, with a haploid genome size of 1.28 Gb, a heterozygosity rate of 0.78%, and a repeat content of 61.76%. Using Hifiasm software25 (v0.19), the genome assembly of N. ionah was generated, yielding contigs totaling 1.27 Gb and a contig N50 of 7.14 Mb. A total of 461,364,206 Hi-C read pairs were sequenced, resulting in 812,607,619 mapped reads, which constituted 88.08% of the total reads. After excluding multi-mapped and improperly paired reads, 367,458,298 uniquely mapped read pairs were retained, representing 79.65% of the initial raw pairs. Subsequent to rigorous filtering to eliminate non-specific Hi-C artifacts, such as self-ligation, dangling ends, and PCR duplicates, 258,809,874 valid Hi-C read pairs were obtained, corresponding to 70.43% of the uniquely mapped pairs. The chromosome-level genome assembly was accomplished using LACHESIS software26 (v2.0.1) by partitioning, ordering, and orienting genomic sequences into groups, followed by manual curation and validation, with the following parameters: CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 43; CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2; ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_TRUNK = 158; ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_SHREDS = 230. Following Hi-C assembly and manual adjustment of the heatmap, a total of 1,234.19 Mb of genomic sequence was successfully anchored to 24 chromosomes, representing 96.8% of the genome. Of these anchored sequences, 1,184.32 Mb were assigned both order and orientation, accounting for 95.96% of the total length of the chromosomal sequences. Subsequent Hi-C-based error correction, chromosome-scale scaffolding, and redundancy removal led to the final version of the genome assembly for this project (Fig. 1A, Table 2). Strong diagonal interactions and distinct pseudochromosome boundaries support precise chromosome-scale contig ordering and orientation (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). The assembly statistics indicate a scaffold N50 of 51.82 Mb.

Repetitive element annotation in the N. ionah genome

Repetitive sequences were identified de novo using RepeatModeler227 (v2.0.1), which utilizes two de novo prediction tools, RECON28 (v1.0.8) and RepeatScout29 (v1.0.6). The predicted repeats were subsequently classified using RepeatClassifier with reference to the known Dfam database (v3.5). To identify long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, a specialized prediction was performed using LTR_retriever30 (v2.9.0), which synthesizes results from LTRharvest31 (v1.5.10) and LTR_FINDER32 (v1.07). The de novo predictions were integrated with known database entries, and redundancies were removed to create a species-specific library of repetitive sequences. Finally, RepeatMasker33 (v4.1.2) was employed to annotate transposable element (TE) sequences across the genome using the custom library. This analysis identified approximately 537,339,856 base pairs of TE sequences, accounting for 42.14% of the assembled genome (Table 3).

Gene prediction and annotation

Gene prediction was performed using a three-pronged strategy comprising ab initio, homology-based, and transcriptome-based approaches. The ab initio prediction was carried out utilizing Augustus36 (v3.1.0) and SNAP37 (2006-07-28). For homology-based prediction, GeMoMa38 (v1.7) was employed, using protein sequences from phylogenetically related species. In the transcriptome-based prediction, two complementary methodologies were adopted. First, RNA-seq reads (Table 1) were aligned to the genome using HISAT239 (v2.1.0), assembled into transcripts with StringTie40 (v2.1.4), and subsequently used for gene prediction with GeneMarkS-T41 (v5.1). Second, de novo transcriptome assembly was performed using Trinity42 (v2.11), and the resulting transcripts were used for gene prediction with PASA43 (v2.4.1). For the long-read Iso-seq data (Table 1), reads were aligned to the genome using gmap44 (2020-06-30), processed for splice-site identification, and then used for gene prediction with PASA43 (v2.4.1). The predictions from all three approaches were integrated using EVidenceModeler (EVM)45 (v1.1.1), and the final gene models were refined and updated using PASA43 (v2.4.1). The pipeline identified 31,777 protein-coding genes (Table 5). The Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO)46 (v6.0.0) analysis of the gene sets (CDS or peptide sequences) against the Actinopterygii database (OrthoDB v12, Creation date: 2025-07-01) recovered 92.3% of conserved genes as complete (Table 6). Functional annotation of these predicted genes was conducted by querying multiple public databases, including NR, eggNOG47, GO, KEGG48, TrEMBL49, KOG, SWISS-PROT49, and Pfam50. Overall, 95.94% of the predicted genes were successfully annotated in at least one of these databases (Table 7). To elucidate the genomic characteristics of N. ionah, the Circlize software was used to generate circular diagrams, primarily to visualize TE density, SSR density, gene density, and GC content (Fig. 1B). Then, we performed a five-way comparison of gene families using OrthoFinder51 (v3.1.2) to determine shared or species-specific families in the genomes of N. ionah, C. gunnari (GCA_036324595.1), C. esox (GCA_036324585.1), C. aceratus (GCA_023974075.1), and Pseudochaenichthys georgianus (GCA_902827115.2) (Fig. 1C).

The annotation of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) was conducted utilizing tools tailored to specific ncRNA categories. Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were identified using tRNAscan-SE52 (v1.3.1), while ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were predicted with barrnap (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap). Other ncRNA types, including microRNAs (miRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), were predicted using Infernal53 (v1.1) against the Rfam54 (v14.5) database.

Pseudogenes, which are genomic sequences homologous to functional genes but contain premature stop codons or frameshift mutations, were identified through an initial homology search against the protein database using GenBlastA55 (v1.0.4) on the repeat-masked genome, followed by detailed analysis for disabling mutations using GeneWise56 (v2.4.1). This analysis predicted a total of 320 pseudogenes.

All requested details about genome assembly, gene prediction and annotation—including exact commands, parameters, software versions, and database versions—are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Genome synteny between N. ionah and P. georgianus

The synteny analysis method used Diamond57 (v0.9.29.130) to align gene sequences between two species and identify similar gene pairs. Then, based on the GFF3 files, it is determined whether the similar gene pairs are adjacent on the chromosomes. This process is primarily conducted using MCScanX58, ultimately identifying all genes within the synteny blocks. Figure 1D illustrates the dot plots and Fig. 1E illustrates the linear synteny diagrams for N. ionah and P. georgianus, generated using JCVI59 (v1.4.16).

Reconstructing the phylogenetic tree and locating the hemoglobin cluster

To reconstruct the phylogenetic tree, four icefish species—C. gunnari, C. esox, C. aceratus, and P. georgianus—along with six red-blooded Notothenioid fish species—Cottoperca gobio (GCA_900634415.1), Eleginops maclovinus (GCA_036324505.1), Dissostichus mawsoni (GCA_011823955.1), Trematomus bernacchii (GCA_902827165.1), Notothenia coriiceps (GCA_053567765.1), and Gymnodraco acuticeps (GCA_902827175.1)—were selected to estimate the evolutionary history of N. ionah. Phylogenetic inference of the species tree (Fig. 2A) was performed using RAxML software60 (v8.2.12) based on the concatenated sequences of conserved non-coding elements (CNEs) from 11 species, which was identified by BLASTN with the CNEs of N. coriiceps61 as queries. The results indicate that Jonah’s icefish N. ionah and the four icefish species form a single clade (Fig. 2A).

The ranking of the hemoglobin cluster (LA and MN) and neighboring genes was obtained for six species—E. maclovinus, D. mawsoni, T. bernacchii, G. acuticeps, C. aceratus, and P. georgianus—based on the finding of Desvignes et al.19, as well as for five additional species—C. gobio, N. coriiceps, C. gunnari, C. esox, and N. ionah—identified in the current study (Fig. 2B and C). The methodology for hemoglobin gene retrieval and nomenclature follows the system proposed by Desvignes et al.19. Briefly, the position and strand orientation of individual exons for each alpha and beta gene were manually extracted from the genomes of each species using BLASTN with T. bernacchii as reference for exon boundaries. In the LA cluster, with the exception of N. ionah, the four icefish species exhibited a loss of the entire cluster, excluding the third exon of the hbala2 gene, as previously documented13,19. Conversely, in N. ionah, our assembly predicted the presence of the complete hemoglobin subunit alpha and beta (hbbla1.1 and hbala2) (Fig. 2B). In the MN cluster, all five icefish species demonstrated a complete loss of the entire cluster (Fig. 2C).

Hemoglobin gene expression analysis

The NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) was searched for transcriptomic sequencing libraries from N. ionah to study hemoglobin gene expression. However, only a spleen library (ERR2587161) was found from N. ionah. In addition, a muscle library was constructed in the study. Then, the low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using fastp62 (v1.3.3). The clean reads were aligned to the N. ionah genome utilizing STAR63 (v2.7.6). The mapped reads from both spleen and muscle tissues were visualized with the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)64. The expression of hemoglobin subunits alpha and beta was found to be detectable in the spleen of N. ionah, but only marginally detectable in muscle tissue. Additionally, the distribution of RNA-seq reads was uneven, with reads present in the second and third exons but absent from the first exon of the two hemoglobin genes (Fig. 2D). Two potential explanations may account for this phenomenon: (1) the RNA-seq library may be enriched for 3′ UTRs; or (2) the transcription start sites of the hemoglobin genes in N. ionah may have been altered, hindering the production of complete, functional coding sequences. To discern between these possibilities, further validation is necessary, utilizing additional tissues and high-depth or full-length transcriptome data.

Technical Validation

The genome assembly’s completeness and accuracy were rigorously evaluated using a comprehensive, multifaceted methodology. Completeness was assessed at the gene-space level using two benchmarked universal single-copy ortholog sets. The Core Eukaryotic Genes Mapping Approach (CEGMA) analysis revealed a completeness of 92.14%. In contrast, the BUSCO46 (v6.0.0) analysis, utilizing the Actinopterygii database (OrthoDB v12, Creation date: 2025-07-01), indicated a comparable completeness score of 6,901 (95.8%), consisting of 6,587 (91.4%) single-copy BUSCOs, 314 (4.4%) duplicated BUSCOs, 50 (0.7%) fragmented BUSCOs, and 256 (3.6%) missing BUSCOs (Table 6). To assess the base-level accuracy and continuity of the assembly, the original sequencing reads were aligned back to the assembled contigs. The short-read Illumina data exhibited a mapping rate of 99.01%, achieving 99.76% genome coverage. In addition, the long-read PacBio/HiFi data demonstrated a mapping rate of 99.85%, covering 99.99% of the genome. The consensus quality value (QV) and k-mer completeness of the N. ionah assembly evaluated by Merqury73 software were 46.97 and 90.73%, respectively. Furthermore, the contamination screening conducted with NCBI’s FCS-GX pipeline74 (v0.5.5) did not identify any sequences flagged as contaminants within the N. ionah assembly. The genome assembly contains 793 gaps, with a total N-content of 79,300 bp. The high mapping rates and nearly complete coverage from both sequencing platforms, along with data from chromosome quality assessment software, robustly support the assembly’s contiguity and base-level accuracy.

Data Availability Statement

The raw sequencing dataset has been deposited at NCBI (PRJNA139993570); the assembled genome of N. ionah has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession JBWLES00000000071. The version described in this paper is version JBWLES010000000; and the assembled genome and gene annotation have been deposited in the figshare database (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.3103320172).

No custom code was used in this study. All data analyses were conducted using published bioinformatics software with default settings, unless otherwise specified.
