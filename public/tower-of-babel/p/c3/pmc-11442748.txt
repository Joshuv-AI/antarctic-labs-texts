Glycemic metrics in Japanese isCGM users – Analysis by diabetes type and therapy
Authors: Wataru Ogawa, Tatsuhiko Urakami, Takashi Kadowaki, Kalvin Kao, Laura Brandner, Kenichiro Shimizu, Timothy C Dunn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionThe FreeStyle Libre (FSL) intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring (isCGM) system continually measures interstitial glucose levels and provides the data to users in numerical and graphical formats that guide users in their daily diabetes self‐management. Although numerous studies have demonstrated the glycemic benefits of FSL in pediatric and adult populations, few studies have characterized FSL use specifically by Japanese adults with type 1 or 2 diabetes. We utilized established CGM metrics to assess glycemic control in a large cohort of Japanese adults with type 1 and 2 diabetes.Materials and MethodsA total of 3,463 anonymized FSL users provided categorization into one of four therapy groups of interest: type 1 diabetes (n = 1,768), type 2 diabetes‐multiple daily injections (MDI) (n = 612), type 2 diabetes‐basal (BOI) (n = 343), and type 2 diabetes‐non‐insulin (NIT) (n = 740). Established CGM metrics were used to assess glycemic control.ResultsAll study groups showed relatively good glycemic control. Type 1 diabetes users showed the highest glucose variability (SD, 61 mg/dL; and %CV, 40%), above the established target level (%CV ≤ 36%). type 2 diabetes‐MDI and type 2 diabetes‐BOI users had similar levels of glucose variability (both within target). Type 2 diabetes‐NIT users had the highest mean % time in range (TIR) (84.3%) and largest percentage of users that met the target of %TIR > 70% (87.4%). In contrast, type 1 diabetes users had the lowest mean %TIR (62.6%) and the lowest percentage meeting the established %TIR target (30.5%).ConclusionsBy utilizing CGM devices in daily diabetes care, both healthcare professionals and patients can monitor glycemic excursions and gain insights into their historical glucose control patterns.Keywords: Glycemic control, Intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring, Real world evidence

INTRODUCTION

Advances in medical device technologies have led to the development of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems, which continually measure and report interstitial glucose levels and provide users with tabular and graphical summaries of their data to guide users in their daily diabetes self‐management. One such device is the FreeStyle Libre (FSL) intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring system (isCGM) (Abbott Diabetes Care, Alameda, USA).

The FSL system utilizes a filament sensor inserted below the skin that monitors glucose in the interstitial fluid for up to 14 days. A smartphone with an app installed or a dedicated reader is used to scan the sensor at any time to collect the current glucose, glucose trend, and up to 8 h of glucose readings (recorded at 15 min intervals). Data can be uploaded to a dedicated software program for analysis of glucose metrics, which are presented in standardized reports.

During the past 5 years, randomized clinical trials have demonstrated the clinical value and utility of FSL among adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) in controlled research settings 1 , 2 , 3 . Moreover, a growing number of large registry studies and database analyses in the USA and Europe have shown that the use of FSL is associated with significant improvements in overall glycemic control 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , diabetes‐related hospitalizations 10 , 11 , reductions in hypoglycemia 4 , 5 , 6 , decreased absenteeism 4 , 5 , and improvements in treatment satisfaction 4 , 12 . However, few studies have characterized FSL use by Japanese adults with type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes, in whom diabetes tends to develop at a younger age in Japanese patients. In contrast with diabetes in Caucasian patients, diabetes in Japanese patients is characterized as persistent β‐cell dysfunction concomitant with insulin resistance due to increased visceral adiposity, regardless of a lower BMI 13 .

We utilized the CGM metrics that were recently established by an International Consensus Panel 14 to assess glycemic control in a large cohort of Japanese adults with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes and summarized their personal characteristics and engagement‐related behaviors as well.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collected by FSL readers were included in this analysis (this analysis does not include FreeStyle Libre 2 or other later product versions). The sensor data gathered by these readers was anonymized and uploaded to a database by the upload software, which includes an agreement that such data will be collected by use of the software and be used for research purposes. In addition to glucose data, the upload software also presents an optional survey allowing users to volunteer details about their diabetes type, therapy choices, frequency of insulin use, age group, daily frequency of fingerstick blood glucose monitoring (BGM) prior to FSL adoption, years since diagnosis of diabetes, FSL reimbursement status, comorbidities, and sex. The survey questions and their response options are presented in Table S1.

Based on the survey responses obtained, respondents were categorized into four diabetes therapy groups: type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes treated with multiple daily insulin injections (T2D‐MDI), type 2 diabetes treated with basal‐only insulin therapy (T2D‐BOI), and type 2 diabetes treated with noninsulin therapy (T2D‐NIT) (Table S2). Only data from Japanese adults were selected for analysis.

Glucose values and demographic information from the selected FSL users, which were uploaded between January 2017 and June 2021, were analyzed and stratified by diabetes therapy. January 2017 was the start date for continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) reimbursement in Japan.

The analysis required that each sensor was operational for 5 days or longer in order to ensure reliable glucose control measures. Data from all sensors belonging to the same reader were aggregated to calculate each user's glucose metrics.

The glucose measures assessed aligned with the standardized metrics outlined in the international consensus report 14 . The metrics of interest included scanning frequency, average glucose, glucose management indicator (GMI), glucose standard deviation (SD), glucose coefficient of variation (%CV), percentage of time in range (%TIR, 70–180 mg/dL), percentage time above range (%TAR, >250 mg/dL, >180 mg/dL), and percentage of time below range (%TBR, <70 mg/dL, <54 mg/dL) 14 . Scanning frequency for each reader was assessed by dividing the reader's total number of scans by the duration of use over all its sensors. Users’ responses to the optional survey were also summarized and stratified by insulin therapy group.

Statistical analysis

The database was analyzed by structured query language routines, the Python programming language (www.python.org), and the R programming language (https://www.r‐project.org/). In anticipation of a significant result from a one‐way anova, we planned a post‐hoc test comprising pairwise statistical hypothesis tests. For comparing the means of therapy groups, independent samples t‐tests were used. For comparing %TBR between therapy groups, the bootstrapped 99.9% confidence intervals of the median were used. For comparing the proportions of each group achieving international consensus targets and having particular survey responses, two‐proportions z‐tests were used.

RESULTS

User characteristics

A total of 3,463 eligible FSL users were identified and categorized into one of the four therapy groups of interest: type 1 diabetes (n = 1,768), T2D‐MDI (n = 612), T2D‐BOI (n = 343), and T2D‐NIT (n = 740). As shown in Table 1, the majority of users in all groups were male, and most were between the ages of 25–64 years. The T2D‐NIT group had the highest proportion of users who were diagnosed with diabetes within 5 years, followed by type 1 diabetes (P < 0.001), T2D‐BOI and T2D‐MDI users (there was no significant difference between the T2D‐BOI and T2D‐MDI users; P < 0.001 for all other pairwise differences). Although the majority of T2D‐NIT users reported performing blood glucose monitoring (BGM) <1 time per day prior to FSL use, this group scanned their sensor more frequently than the other study groups (P < 0.001) and had the highest percentage of users who reported receiving no reimbursement for CGM (P < 0.001).

Type 1 diabetes users showed the highest glucose variability (SD and %CV) (P < 0.001 in both cases), which were well above the established target level (%CV ≤ 36%) 14 (Table 2). Whereas T2D‐MDI and T2D‐BOI users had similar levels of glucose variability (both within target), T2D‐NIT users had notably lower levels than those of T2D‐MDI and T2D‐BOI users (P < 0.001 for both SD and %CV).

The T2D‐NIT users had the highest mean %TIR (P < 0.001) and the largest percentage meeting established targets (P < 0.001). In contrast, type 1 diabetes users had the lowest mean %TIR (P < 0.001), and the lowest percentage meeting established targets (P < 0.001). The frequency distribution of %TIR illustrates the tendency of type 1 diabetes users to have a lower %TIR relative to the other therapy groups, as well as the greater relative %TIR of the T2D‐NIT group (Figure 1a, Figure S1). The median %TB54 and %TB70 of the T1D group was noticeably greater than that of the other therapy groups (P < 0.001 for the difference in medians), and the frequency distribution of %TB70 demonstrates this tendency of type 1 diabetes users to have higher rates of hypoglycemia (Figure 1b; Figure S2). The frequency distribution of %TA180 shows the lower incidence of hyperglycemia of T2D‐NIT users relative to the other therapy groups (Figure 1c; Figure S3) – indeed their mean %TA180 was substantially lower than that of the other therapy groups (P < 0.001). This smaller rate of hyperglycemia of T2D‐NIT users is also reflected in the frequency distribution of GMI (Figure 1d; Figure S4a,b), which visualizes the generally lower GMI of T2D‐NIT users, relative to other therapy groups (P < 0.001 for the difference in mean GMI).

DISCUSSION

This was the first study to assess the glycemic metrics associated with FSL use in a large cohort of Japanese adults that included a relatively equal number of FSL users with type 1 diabetes (n = 1,768) and type 2 diabetes (1,695). Overall, this cross‐sectional study describes the similarities and differences in glycemic metrics for different diabetes types and therapy types in this cohort. There are still very few reports looking at glycemic metrics by patient population. It will be one reference for future comparison with data from other regions; direct comparison with other regions will need to account for differences in access, therapy, and standards of care.

Although there are similar results in GMI and mean glucose between type 1 diabetes, T2D‐MDI, and T2D‐BOI, the rate of achieving target %TIRs (especially TBR) are quite different between those groups. Indeed, we did not detect a difference in the mean average glucose between the insulin‐using groups, while a significant difference was detected in both mean %TIR and the rate of achieving >70% TIR between the type 1 diabetes and both the T2D‐MDI and T2D‐BOI groups. These differences demonstrate the limitations of assessing glycemic control using only average glucose, HbA1c, or GMI and highlights the importance of seeing in‐depth glucose trends such as time‐in‐hypoglycemia, TIR, and the Ambulatory Glucose Profile using CGM devices including FSL.

Findings from our study revealed that there are differences in the distributions of CGM metrics among therapy groups, especially that fewer type 1 diabetes patients achieve targets for TIR and TBR compared with the T2 population. Therefore, it appears necessary to carefully control blood glucose by checking the TIR and TBR values, especially in type 1 diabetes. Type 1 diabetes demonstrates greater TBR than does type 2 diabetes, and the percentage of people with type 1 diabetes who have reached the international consensus target is low. Type 1 diabetes requires more careful management of hypoglycemia.

On the other hand, in type 2 diabetes using insulin, more patients have achieved the TIR > 70% target, but 40–50% of subjects still have not reached this target. Also, 20–30% of patients with type 2 diabetes did not reach the TB54 target of less than 1%. Even though the mean time in severe hypoglycemia is low for the type 2 diabetes insulin‐using population in this study, there is still a risk of severe hypoglycemia in this group, which should be monitored with CGM. For people with type 2 diabetes using insulin, there was room for improvement in glycemic control not only in the T2D‐MDI group but also in the T2D‐BOI group.

A key strength of our study was the use of an observational, cross‐sectional design, utilizing reliable CGM data that provided detailed information about each participant's glycemic status according to standardized metrics. Additionally, using the median vs. mean to report %TBR accounts for the inherent skew in the distribution of this metric. Limitations include the patient‐reported data for diabetes type and therapy and the fact that the effect of FreeStyle Libre use is unknown because the pre‐FSL baseline data were not available. This dataset includes only patients who are using FreeStyle Libre and who responded to an optional patient survey (responses were over‐indexed on males), and therefore may be different from all diabetes patients in Japan. For example, the mean GMI for type 1 diabetes patients in this analysis is 6.95%, and other literature has shown the A1c for the type 1 diabetes group in Japan to be 7.7–7.8% 15 , 16 . In addition, data from the female population also needs verification. The survey response group included 740 users who reported not using insulin, which was not a CGM‐reimbursed population in Japan during this study period. This group is likely self‐funding their use of CGM as demonstrated by their response – 89% reported no CGM reimbursement. Between 2017 and March 2020, isCGM was reimbursed in Japan only for adjunctive use to SMBG. From April 2021 and through to the end of this study analysis period, isCGM was reimbursed in Japan mainly for people with diabetes who take multiple daily injections of insulin. By utilizing CGM devices in daily diabetes care, both healthcare professionals and patients could use the data as an opportunity to deal with potential problems in glucose management to achieve better glucose control.

This first study of a large cohort of Japanese adults with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes using FreeStyle Libre showed different glucose profiles by diabetes type and therapy. These findings provide support for the use of CGM in patients with both type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes to reveal what needs to be improved in each patient. CGM is needed to reveal the glucose profile for patients because average glucose and GMI are not sufficient to tell their whole story. Healthcare professionals need to monitor type 1 diabetes patients’ time below range to prevent hypoglycemia.
