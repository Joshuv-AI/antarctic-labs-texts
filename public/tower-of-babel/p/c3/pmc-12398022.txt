Development and psychometric evaluation of the hospitalized patients’ expectations for treatment scale-patient version-satisfaction scale
Authors: Chunfeng Xiao, Yufei Wang, Aoxue Wu, Tao Li, Yanping Duan, Yinan Jiang, Lili Shi, Xia Hong, Wenqi Geng, Jiarui Li, Jianhua Du, Jiaojiao Hu, Jinya Cao, Jing Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to develop and initially assess the reliability and psychometric properties of the Hospitalized Patients’ Expectations for Treatment Scale - Patient Version - Satisfaction Scale (HOPE-PS), a structured assessment tool that measures patient satisfaction based on expectations.MethodsMedical experts and patients were consulted, and their opinions were gathered and consolidated using the Delphi method. A total of 1,000 inpatients from various wards of a general hospital in China were recruited for the study. Exploratory graph analysis (EGA) was implemented to identify the dimensionality of measurement scale. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of three competing models (i.e., unidimensional model, two-factor model, and bifactor model) was conducted to determine the final structure of the scale.ResultsEGA revealed a two-factor structure (satisfactions with treatment experience-related expectations and treatment outcome-related expectations), while the bifactor model showed the best fit indices among three competing models in CFA. The full scale showed good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.88.ConclusionThe HOPE-PS scale has demonstrated promising internal consistency and reliability in this study, a more comprehensive validation process is warranted.Keywords: General hospital, Patient safety, Bifactor analysis, Exploratory graph analysis, Psychometrics

Background

Patient satisfaction is a widely used measure to evaluate the quality of healthcare services and encompasses the overall patient experience [1]. It significantly impacts clinical outcomes, patient retention, and the doctor-patient relationship, with satisfied patients are more likely to adhere to medical advice and engage in cooperative behavior [1, 2]. The scope of patient satisfaction extends across various dimensions of healthcare, including communication, technical skills, and the nature of specific treatments, underscoring its multifaceted nature and pivotal role in healthcare quality improvement. Studies such as those by Otani et al. and Manary et al. underscore the importance of communication as a fundamental determinant of patient satisfaction, suggesting that effective doctor-patient communication can significantly enhance satisfaction levels [3, 4]. Similarly, the work of Prakash [5] demonstrates how patients’ perceptions of healthcare providers’ technical skills directly influence their overall satisfaction with care, indicating the necessity for healthcare professionals to exhibit high competence in their clinical abilities.

A study by Bleich et al. examined data from 21 European Union countries to explore the factors influencing satisfaction with the healthcare system [6]. The results showed that satisfaction with the healthcare system is primarily influenced by external factors such as patient expectations, health status, and the type of care required, rather than solely relying on the care experience itself. Importantly, patient satisfaction is closely linked to patient expectations, as satisfaction levels can vary even when healthcare services remain unchanged. Patient expectations for treatment typically arise before medical interventions, while patient satisfaction is experienced after receiving medical care. Both factors significantly impact treatment outcomes and are crucial in guiding personalized healthcare. Given the existing body of research, it is clear that assessing patient satisfaction based on treatment expectations is of great importance [7]. This consideration stems from the strong correlation between expectations and satisfaction, with the goal of enhancing the ability of satisfaction assessments to inform clinical decision-making and facilitate appropriate adjustments to medical care based on patient expectations.

Furthermore, integrating patient expectations and satisfaction into the patient safety system is crucial for ensuring comprehensive and effective healthcare management [8, 9]. A comprehensive psychological assessment and intervention system within the patient safety framework should encompass various dimensions, including direct safety factors but also mental and behavioral aspects, and indirect safety factors such as treatment expectations and physician burnout. Numerous studies have highlighted the association between patient expectations, satisfaction, and patient safety [10], emphasizing the need to incorporate patient expectations and satisfaction into the maintenance of a robust patient safety system. Assessing satisfaction in relation to patient expectations holds significant clinical value, providing valuable insights for guiding healthcare practices.

However, despite the recognition of the importance of understanding patient expectations and satisfaction, and the availability of some assessment tools for measuring patient satisfaction, there is currently a lack of a specifically designed and standardized assessment tool for evaluating patient satisfaction based on treatment expectations in clinical settings [11]. Therefore, our study aimed to validate a structured assessment tool that evaluates patient satisfaction based on expectations. The objective was to provide a reliable and practical evaluation tool for future clinical practice, addressing the need for enhanced personalized healthcare and the promotion of a robust patient safety system. In our preliminary work, we successfully validated the Hospitalized Patients’ Expectations for Treatment Scale - Patient version (HOPE-P), demonstrating its validity and reliability in assessing treatment expectations among hospitalized patients [12]. In this study, we utilized a similar scale structure to HOPE-P to develop an assessment tool for patient satisfaction based on expectations, aiming to establish a comprehensive evaluation system for patient expectations and satisfaction in future clinical practice.

Methods

Item generation

In this study, the development of the Hospitalized Patients’ Expectations for Treatment Scale - Patient version - Satisfaction Questionnaire (HOPE-PS), a structured tool to measure patient satisfaction with hospital care based on their expectations building upon the prior scale HOPE-P, was a collaborative effort involving 35 medical experts across various disciplines: nursing, psychiatry, internal medicine, surgery, and medical management [8]. Recognizing the importance of patient involvement in creating a patient-centered questionnaire, we further enriched our item generation process by interviewing ten inpatients from diverse medical specialties at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, including general surgery, orthopedics, vascular surgery, plastic surgery, gastroenterology, infectious medicine, and neurology. To address the concern regarding the balance between clinician and patient input, we meticulously integrated insights from these patient interviews into our discussions. These interactions provided invaluable patient-centered perspectives, ensuring that the scale resonated with patient experiences and expectations.

Employing the Delphi method, we conducted three rounds of discussions with a key question (“What do patients expect from their hospitalization and interactions with doctors, and what concerns do they prioritize?”) to refine our scale items. This iterative process allowed for a dynamic integration of expert opinions with patient feedback, ensuring a comprehensive representation of both medical and patient perspectives. Patients were included in the Delphi process through the incorporation of their feedback from the interviews into the discussions, enabling their direct influence on item selection and scale structure.

Through three rounds of in-depth discussions, a consensus emerged around three pivotal domains that encapsulate patient satisfactions: the quality of doctor-patient communication, anticipated improvements in clinical condition during the hospital stay, and perceptions surrounding the management of their condition as a long-term health issue. The final version of the HOPE-PS is a self-administered scale that consists of two domains and nine items, reflecting a consensus on key areas of patient satisfaction. The first two domains assess satisfaction with expectations related to communication between doctors and patients (Items 1–4 and 9) and treatment outcomes (Items 5–8). Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with expectations from doctors and the hospital. A 5-point Likert scale was used to rate each item on the scale.

Participants

During the period from February 2023 to May 2024, a total of 1,000 patients receiving treatment across ten different departments at Peking Union Medical College Hospital in China were invited to participate in this study. Eligible participants were required to be aged 10 years or older, hospitalized for more than 24 h with an expected discharge within a week, and possess the ability to comprehend and cooperate with the study procedures. Exclusion criteria included acute suicidal tendencies, limited writing skills, language barriers, organic brain disorders, cognitive impairment, dementia, and psychosis. Considering the inclusion of children in our study sample, special attention was given to ensuring their ability to participate meaningfully and ethically. Responses to the scale from participants under the age of 18 were obtained directly from the children themselves, under the premise that they could comprehend the questions and possessed the requisite literacy skills for participation. To facilitate their participation without necessitating mobile phone usage for scanning QR codes, alternative methods of accessing and completing the questionnaire were provided, such as the use of their parents’ mobile phones and assistance from a study coordinator when needed. This approach ensured that all participants, regardless of age or access to technology, could contribute their perspectives effectively. All participants provided written informed consent to participate. For those under the age of 18, additional written informed consent was secured from their parents or legal guardians, ensuring a comprehensive understanding and agreement with the study’s procedures.

This study obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital in advance, all data would be reported anonymously in an aggregated form.

Administration

The investigators involved in this study were psychiatrists or graduate students specializing in psychiatry. They were responsible for conducting personal visits to each participant and providing detailed information about the investigation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. Participants were then given a QR code to scan, allowing them to access and complete the questionnaires using their mobile phones. Throughout the scale completion process, the investigators were available to assist and support participants in case of any difficulties or uncertainties.

Sample-size estimation

Our study proposes to utilize exploratory graph analysis (EGA) method for factor identification, followed by evaluating the goodness of fit of three competing models, including a bifactor model, in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Regarding the EGA method, simulation study has indicated its superior accuracy in factor identification with a sample size of 500 [13]. As for the bifactor model, simulation study has also suggested that a sample size of 500 is often adequate to achieve acceptable convergence rates and parameter estimates [14].

Considering the distinct sample requirements for factor identification based on EGA and CFA, this study opts for a total sample size of 1,000. The entire sample is then randomly split into two halves, with each half allocated for EGA and CFA analyses, respectively, ensuring that both analyses are conducted with an adequate sample size of 500.

Structural Validity: The sample was randomly split half to perform EGA and CFA with R version 4.3.2, package EGAnet and Mplus 7.4. EGA is a new method for determining the number of dimensions developed in recent years, and has been demonstrated to exhibit superior performance when applied to ordinal data for dimension identification [16]. EGA can use the Walktrap community detection algorithm within a triangulated maximally filtered graph (TMFG) framework to identify the optimum number of dimensions that may be present in the network [13, 17, 18]. The EGA approach has been described as superior to traditional dimension reduction techniques (such as exploratory factor analysis; EFA) because it does not require the researcher to make decisions about the rotation of axes [13]. Subsequently, a series of CFAs were conducted to confirm factor structure of the scale. CFAs were performed using the diagonal weighted least squares estimation method for it was especially designed for ordinal data [19]. Three competing measurement models (i.e., one-factor, two-factor and bifactor) were tested. We tested a bifactor model to assess the possibility of a single general factor that reflects the common variance of all items beyond the variance accounted for by the two subscales [20]. To specify the bifactor model of the scale, all 9 items were assigned to load on a general factor, while each item was also assigned to a specific factor according to the result of EGA. The covariation between all factors (general factor and specific factors identified in EGA) was set to zero (i.e., the three factors were orthogonal). In other words, each item was allowed to load on both a general factor and its respective group factor, which allows researchers to identify how much reliable variance is captured by the general factor versus the group factors. The criteria for acceptable model fit included standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.05, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.10, and values of the following indexes: Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) and comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90 [21, 22]. Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values were used to compare the fit of each model. A BIC value difference exceeding 10 provides strong evidence of model fit difference [23]; the model with the lower BIC value is considered to have superior model fit. When the items of an instrument conform to a bifactor structure, it becomes necessary to provide model-based reliability evidence that the total and subscale scores of the instrument truly represent the target constructs of interest [24]. A series of model-based reliability coefficients were next calculated with Bifactor Indices Calculater provided by Dueber [25]. Coefficient Omega (ω) stands for the proportion of total score variance that can be attributed to all common factors (i.e., true score variance, which excludes error variance). It can also be adapted to measure the proportion of subscale score variance that can be attributed to all common factors. Coefficient Omega Hierarchical (ωH) measures the proportion of total score variance that can be attributed to a single general factor after accounting for group (i.e., subscale) factors [26]. Coefficient Omega Subscale (ωS) is a version of ωH that measures the proportion of subscale score variance that is uniquely attributable to that group (i.e., subscale) factor after controlling for the general factor. An ωH over 0.75 would indicate that the total score predominantly reflects a single general factor despite the presence of multidimensionality across items, which in turn would permit researchers to interpret the total score as a sufficiently reliable and appropriate measure of the general construct. An ωS less than 0.50 would indicate that the majority of the subscale’s variance is due to the general factor and that negligible unique variance is attributable to that group factor. In other words, the reliability of the subscale score is overwhelmingly inflated by the general factor and does not actually reliably measure the narrower subdomain construct that the subscale was purported to measure. In short, to interpret such a subscale as capturing something unique could be misleading. On the contrary, when ωH is less than 0.75 while ωS exceeds 0.50, it indicates that despite the presence of a general factor, the general factor alone is insufficient to adequately explain the construct of the entire scale in a unidimensional manner. Therefore, there remains significant value in interpreting the scoring of the subscales [27, 28].

Structural validity analysis

We implemented an EGA approach on half of the randomly selected sample (n = 500) to facilitate the interpretation of potential communities within the scale. Utilizing the Walktrap community detection algorithm within an TMFG framework, we identified the optimum number of dimensions that may be present in the network [13]. The emphasis of interpretation in our network estimated with EGA-Walktrap is on the identification of communities. As shown in Fig. 1, the optimal solution delineated the presence of two dimensions.

Community belonging of items identified by exploratory graph analysis

Upon assessing the meaning and community belonging of every item, items 1–4 and item 9 were classified under a dimension called “satisfaction with treatment experience-related expectations”, representing patients’ satisfaction levels regarding their treatment experience. In contrast, items 5–8 were assigned to another dimension, “satisfaction with treatment outcome-related expectations,” reflecting patients’ satisfaction levels concerning their treatment results. Subsequently, CFA using weighted least squares estimation was performed on the other randomly selected sample (n = 500) to test the model fit indices for three competing models. The results of model fit indices were shown in Table 2. The bifactor model demonstrated the best fit, and was chosen as the final model (Fig. 2) [26]. Table 3 summarizes the ω, ωH, ωS and ECV coefficients for the bifactor solution. Results indicated an ωH value less than 0.75 for the general factor and ωS values over 0.50 for both subscales. In summary, model-based reliability estimates provided evidence for the use of subscale scores to represent the narrower purported subdomains of treatment expectations. Reliability analysis

Considering the reverse scoring of item 9, recoding was performed before conducting the reliability analysis in this study. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the full scale of HOPE-PS was calculated as 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI] = [0.87, 0.89]). The Cronbach’s α coefficients for subscale A and subscale B of HOPE-PS were calculated as 0.81 (95% CI = [0.79, 0.82]) and 0.86 (95% CI = [0.85, 0.87]), respectively. Discussion

This study aimed to develop and initially evaluate the reliability and psychometric properties of the HOPE-PS scale, a tool designed to measure patient satisfaction with hospital medical care based on their expectations. Participants were inpatients from various wards in a general hospital in China. The findings aligned with the bifactor model, encompassing one general factor and two specific factors standing for satisfactions with treatment experience-related expectations and treatment outcome-related expectations respectively. The scale proved effective in identifying and addressing instances of low satisfaction with hospital medical care, thereby facilitating the provision of personalized medical services and the timely resolution of potential conflicts. Although our findings are an initial step in the validation process, the HOPE-PS scale presents an innovative approach to assessing patient satisfaction with medical care, particularly within the Chinese cultural context.

Patients with different diseases may display varying levels of satisfaction with treatment expectations. The diverse treatments and management strategies received by patients, influenced by their specific diseases, lead to a wide range of treatment experiences and outcomes, ultimately impacting their satisfaction. These findings align with observations in general clinical practice. The choice of treatment modality plays a crucial role in shaping patients’ treatment experiences and their satisfaction with the treatment process. Gospos et al. conducted a study on patients receiving oral anticoagulant therapy, investigating treatment expectations, convenience, and satisfaction [30]. They discovered that while gender and disease influence patients’ treatment expectations, they do not significantly affect their overall treatment satisfaction. The discrepancy in findings could be due to the fact that the assessment of patient satisfaction in the aforementioned study did not specifically consider expectations, whereas our study evaluated patient satisfaction by incorporating treatment expectations. Similarly, previous research in related fields has often utilized more comprehensive assessments with a global perspective, aiming to provide a broader understanding of overall experiences across various aspects. However, such assessments may face challenges in offering targeted practical assistance for improving clinical practice. In this regard, the development and validation of the HOPE-PS, focusing on satisfaction with treatment experience-related expectations and satisfaction with treatment outcome-related expectations, provides a patient satisfaction assessment tool that emphasizes actionable insights for clinical practice and physician behavior in future healthcare settings.

Our study findings indicate that the factor structure of HOPE-PS aligns with the bifactor model. Moreover, in addition to the explanations provided by the general factor, the scores of the two subscales also possess independent indicative significance. The dimension of satisfaction with treatment experience-related expectations encompasses two key aspects: patient satisfaction with doctor-patient communication and their cognitive evaluation of long-term disease management. Effective communication, enhanced by positive attitudes such as understanding, listening, respect, and empathy, can significantly improve patient satisfaction [31, 32]. Communication is a dynamic process involving the exchange of information or ideas between a sender (communicator) and a receiver (recipient) [33]. In the clinical context, doctor-patient communication represents a unique form of communication, with the doctor as the communicator and the patient as the recipient. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that doctor-patient communication carries significant implications, especially when it pertains to matters of life and health. Therefore, caution and professionalism are essential in such situations. Establishing a strong interpersonal relationship serves as the cornerstone of effective doctor-patient communication, wherein the doctor acts as an attentive listener, facilitates information exchange, and formulates patient-centered management plans [34, 35]. Previous research has demonstrated that the quality of doctor-patient communication directly or indirectly influences patients’ health outcomes [36, 37]. Effective doctor-patient communication plays a pivotal role in fostering patients’ trust in their physicians and overall satisfaction, while also indirectly impacting health outcomes such as symptom improvement and adherence to medical treatments [33]. Thus, ensuring effective and efficient communication is integral to strategies aimed at delivering high-quality healthcare to patients [38, 39]. The three items within the HOPE-PS scale specifically assess patients’ satisfaction with doctor-patient communication. These items include listening to patients’ treatment opinions, informing patients about their disease condition and involving them in medical decisions, and displaying a caring attitude. These items closely align with the focal points of prior research on doctor-patient communication, reflecting the principles of patient autonomy, shared decision-making, and humanitarianism, which are crucial considerations in personalized medicine. It is important to note that patients’ cognitive evaluation of long-term disease management significantly influences the course of their illness, as long-term management often relies on the patient’s voluntary actions. Understanding and being aware of patients’ satisfaction with their expectations regarding long-term disease management are therefore critical. This knowledge can guide physicians in making timely adjustments and providing higher-quality and forward-thinking medical services in clinical practice. While subscale A focuses primarily on doctor-patient communication, it is essential to acknowledge the significant role other healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, play in the overall patient care continuum. Their contributions, from direct care to emotional support, are pivotal to patient satisfaction. This broader perspective of healthcare delivery, involving various healthcare providers, underlines the complexity of measuring and interpreting patient satisfaction solely based on doctor behavior. Therefore, future iterations of the HOPE-PS could benefit from including items that more explicitly reflect the multidisciplinary nature of patient care.

Research in psychology and psychiatry has provided compelling evidence regarding the influence of patients’ treatment outcome-related expectations on disease treatment outcomes. Outcome expectation (OE) refers to a patient’s personal belief about the potential benefits of a specific treatment they have undergone or plan to undergo, in relation to their prognosis [40]. Evaluating patient satisfaction based on OE is crucial for guiding clinical practice and predicting treatment outcomes [40, 41]. Items 5 to 8 in the HOPE-PS scale specifically target patients’ satisfaction concerning their expectations regarding treatment outcomes, encompassing aspects such as disease improvement, recovery, and functional restoration. It’s important to acknowledge that an interaction exists between patients’ OE and doctor-patient communication, and their correlation should not be overlooked.

However, this study has several limitations. The inclusion of inpatients was limited to ten departments, primarily from internal medicine departments. While a broader range might have provided additional insights, practical constraints such as resource limitations and access permissions necessitated a focus on these ten departments. To enhance the generalizability of the findings, future research should expand the sample size and include a broader range of departments. Moreover, this study did not assess the test-retest reliability of the scale. Additionally, while the current set of items in the HOPE-PS scale aims to capture key dimensions that require attention, further exploration is warranted to identify other potential dimensions that require adequate attention, thereby augmenting the scale’s comprehensiveness.

Conclusion

The HOPE-PS scale has demonstrated promising internal consistency and reliability in this preliminary study, aligning with the bifactor model, in which two specific factors encompasses treatment experience-related expectations and treatment outcome-related expectations.
