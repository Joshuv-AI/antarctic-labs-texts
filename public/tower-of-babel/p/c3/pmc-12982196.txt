Daily executive function in children and adolescents with non-syndromic craniosynostosis: association with timing of surgical intervention
Authors: Julieta Moreno-Villagómez, Guillermina Yáñez-Téllez, Belén Prieto-Corona, Miguel Castillo-Mimila, Elizabeth Hernández-Echeagaray, Antonio García-Méndez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to assess executive functioning in everyday activities of participants with non-syndromic craniosynostosis, to compare their performance with a control group, to assess the relationship between the timing of surgery and executive functioning, and to determine the proportion of participants scoring in the clinical range for executive functions.MethodsTwenty-nine participants with craniosynostosis and 30 controls were assessed with the parent version of the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function 2 (BRIEF-2).ResultsBoth groups scored similarly. However, the older the participant with craniosynostosis at the time of the surgery, the greater their difficulties in Task Initiation, Working Memory, Planning, and Monitoring. Only a small percentage (0–17%) of participants with craniosynostosis scored in the clinically significant range; this proportion was similar to that of the control group.ConclusionThis study did not find substantial difficulties in executive functions in daily activities in participants with non-syndromic craniosynostosis, but it did find an association between late surgery and lower scores in some cold executive functions. The study suggests that early surgical intervention could have a potential neurodevelopmental benefit for children and adolescents with craniosynostosis.

Introduction

Craniosynostosis is a condition in infants where the cranial sutures fuse prematurely, leading to an abnormal skull shape. In non-syndromic craniosynostosis, typically only a single suture is affected. This form of the condition is usually isolated and does not occur alongside other physical abnormalities or dysmorphisms [1]. However, it can be associated, even after surgical correction, with various structural and functional abnormalities, such as raised intracranial pressure [2], benign extra-axial fluid, ventriculomegaly [3], decreased volume of corpus callosum and cerebellar vermis [4], and abnormal brain connectivity.

Although many children with craniosynostosis achieve an intelligence quotient within the average range for the general population [5–8], research indicates that some of them may still face subtle but significant cognitive challenges, such as deficits in attention [6, 9], in language [10], in visuospatial and constructional abilities [6], and in visuomotor functioning [11], as well as learning difficulties [6, 8, 12] and low academic achievement [13]. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive neuropsychological assessment and tailored support for children with craniosynostosis to address these cognitive vulnerabilities and optimize their academic and developmental outcomes.

The mechanisms contributing to the cognitive characteristics associated with craniosynostosis are not yet fully understood. However, a number of possible factors have been proposed, including the location of the suture [5, 14–17], socioeconomic factors [18, 19], and age at surgical intervention [16, 20, 21].

Executive functions comprise four distinct domains: attentional control, information processing, cognitive flexibility, and goal setting. These domains include processes such as divided attention, working memory, planning, inhibition, self-monitoring, and processing speed [22]. The assessment of executive functions remains complex and has been controversial. As Lezak [23] has highlighted, deficits in executive functions might not be recognized by examiners in highly structured clinical environments, given their dependence on context. This observation underscores the need for ecologically valid assessment methods that capture functioning in real-world settings [24]. An additional challenge is the frequently weak-to-moderate correlation observed between performance-based measures and self- or informant-reported assessments of executive functioning [25–27].

Studies have demonstrated that executive functioning in childhood predicts important developmental outcomes, including academic achievement in adolescence [28] and intelligence and reading accuracy in middle childhood [29], and that deficits in childhood executive functions can be associated with the development of psychiatric disorders in later life [30].

Research on executive functioning in children with craniosynostosis has yielded inconsistent findings. While some studies have reported small proportions of affected children exhibiting a risk of executive dysfunction [14, 31], others have documented lower scores in specific executive domains than in a control group [9]. Some authors have found no significant differences between children with craniosynostosis and controls [32]. Given these mixed results, the primary objective of this study was to compare parent-reported daily executive functioning between children and adolescents with and without craniosynostosis. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the relationship between surgery timing and executive functioning in the craniosynostosis group. We also analyzed the proportion of participants in both groups scoring within the clinical range for executive dysfunction. We hypothesized that there would be small but discernible differences in daily executive functioning between the groups, with the craniosynostosis group demonstrating poorer performance, and that a correlation would exist between the age at surgery and executive function performance in children and adolescents with craniosynostosis, with earlier surgery associated with better outcomes.

Methods

This study employed an observational and cross-sectional cohort design, with data collected between 2021 and 2023. The median interval between age at surgery and the assessment was 8 years. This study was conducted at a single center.

Participants

Participants were recruited exclusively from the Pediatric Neurosurgery Department of the General Hospital Gaudencio González Garza in Mexico City. Parents of children and adolescents aged 6–17 years with confirmed diagnoses of non-syndromic craniosynostosis were contacted if they had previously expressed interest in participating in research projects. The inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of non-syndromic craniosynostosis (single-suture or multi-sutural) and participants had to have undergone cranial vault remodeling surgery. The diagnosis of craniosynostosis was initially made by a pediatric neurosurgeon and subsequently confirmed with a computed tomography scan. Participants were excluded if they had any other medical or neurological condition and were eliminated from the study if their parents did not complete the study questionnaire. Participants without craniosynostosis were recruited on social media and by snowball sampling using a convenience sampling approach. Eligibility was based on having an age range comparable to that of participants with craniosynostosis.

Parents were informed about the study by telephone. If they agreed to participate, parents and participants provided written informed consent or assent. A semi-structured interview was conducted by video call to collect demographic and clinical information. Additionally, four subscales of the Wechsler Intelligence Scales were administered to participants during this video call. The questionnaire was sent to parents for completion via chat or email. The study was approved by the Facultad de Estudios Superiores Iztacala ethics committee (Approval No. CE/FESI/042021/1391) and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Instruments

Wechsler Intelligence Scales

To assess global cognition, we administered four subscales (Vocabulary, Similarities, Matrix Reasoning, and Symbol Search) from the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children IV (WISC-IV) [33] or the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale IV (WAIS-IV) [34], depending on the participant’s age. The subscales were administered via video call using the methodology proposed by Serrano-Juárez et al. [35]. The correlation between these subscales and the full scale of the intelligence quotient ranges from 0.66 to 0.79. Mexican population norms were used for scoring.

Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function 2 (BRIEF-2) [36]

Executive function behaviors were assessed using the parent report form of the BRIEF-2. This questionnaire measures executive function behaviors in children and adolescents from 5 to 18 years of age across home and school environments with nine clinical scales (Inhibit, Self-Monitor, Shift, Emotional Control, Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organize, Task-Monitor, and Organization of Materials). These scales are combined into three indexes (Behavior Regulation Index, Emotion Regulation Index, and Cognitive Regulation Index) and a Global Executive Composite score. The BRIEF-2 additionally includes three validity scales. T-scores are standardized with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, where higher scores indicate greater difficulties in executive functioning. The BRIEF-2 normative data for Spanish-speaking populations include age and sex corrections.

Data analysis

We used non-parametric tests to compare demographics and questionnaire scores between the craniosynostosis and control groups. Since there was a significant difference in monthly income between groups, we employed a Quade ANCOVA to compare the BRIEF-2 scores with monthly income as a covariate. A Spearman rank-order correlation was run to assess the relationship between age at surgery and the domains assessed by the BRIEF-2. The interpretation of the significant correlations was: weak, 0.1–0.3; moderate, 0.4–0.6; and strong, 0.7–0.9 [37]. The frequency of participants who scored above the clinical range (T > 65) was compared with a Fisher exact test. Questionnaires were excluded from the analysis if one of the three validity scales was in the range of caution.

Results

A total of 72 participants were initially recruited for this study (37 with craniosynostosis and 35 without). Some of these were excluded from the final analysis: four with craniosynostosis and five without whose parents did not return the questionnaire. One participant from the craniosynostosis group was excluded due to a history of epilepsy. Three participants from the craniosynostosis group were excluded because one of their validity indexes fell within the caution range. This screening process resulted in a final sample of 29 participants in the craniosynostosis group and 30 in the control group.

Table 1 details the sample characteristics. The mean age at surgery was 10 months and 17 participants (58.8%) underwent surgery before one year of age. Both groups exhibited similar global cognition, which was measured with four subscales of the Wechsler Intelligence Scales. However, a significant difference in monthly family income was observed between groups. Comparisons of the BRIEF-2 scores (Table 2) revealed no statistical differences across any domains (p > 0.05), and the medians of both groups were not elevated over the test norms (T > 65). Discussion

This study aimed to compare executive function behaviors in the daily activities of children and adolescents with and without non-syndromic craniosynostosis, to investigate the relationship between the timing of surgery and executive functioning, and to assess the proportion of participants that scored above the clinical range on the BRIEF-2 in both groups.

Our findings indicate that children and adolescents with and without craniosynostosis have similar executive function profiles, while the timing of the surgery showed some significant correlations with specific executive domains. However, the overall proportion of participants scoring above the clinical range was similar in both groups, and neither group’s median scores indicated clinically significant difficulties, based on the instrument’s norms.

Our findings align with those reported by Chapman et al. [31], who also used the BRIEF-2 to asses executive functions and reported that overall means were not clinically elevated. They found that 8–22% of children with sagittal synostosis scored above the clinical range, which is comparable to our finding of 0–17%. Similarly, Osborn et al. [32] reported that 19% of participants with sagittal craniosynostosis scored above the clinical range on the Global Executive Composite score. In contrast, Almeida, Alper, Long, et al. [14] observed a higher proportion (31%) of participants with metopic synostosis showing difficulties on that score. However, we did not make a comparison between different types of craniosynostosis.

Our findings support the results of Collett et al. [9], who also found that both craniosynostosis and control groups scored within normal ranges on executive function measures. In their study, however, craniosynostosis participants consistently scored lower in performance-based and informant-reported measures, but our study did not find this pattern. Osborn et al. [32] reported no significant group differences, a result consistent with our own, although they observed small to moderate effect sizes in behavior and emotion regulation indexes for school-aged children with sagittal synostosis, after adjusting for socioeconomic status.

Our study corroborates previous studies of the relationship between the timing of surgery and executive functions. Collett et al. [9] reported a negative association between working memory tasks and later surgery, although they found only minor differences between cases and controls on neuropsychological tests. Similarly, Almeida, Alper, Long et al. [14] found a higher proportion of executive function difficulties, specifically on the BRIEF-2 Cognitive Regulation Index, among children with metopic synostosis who underwent surgery after 6 months of age. Patel et al. [38] reported higher full-scale intelligence quotient scores and better academic achievement in patients who underwent surgery before 6 months of age compared with those operated later, however, no differences were observed in the BRIEF scores. Our study builds upon these insights by assessing everyday functioning through parent-reported measures that capture real-life impact.

We also found a positive correlation between later surgery and greater difficulties in executive functioning, particularly within the scales making up the Cognitive Regulation Index. This suggests that delayed surgery may exacerbate negative neurological effects of craniosynostosis, such as impaired cerebral blood flow, increased intracranial pressure, and dysmorphic brain organization [39, 40], potentially leading to greater brain damage, although this association with cognitive function has not been proven. It is important to note that the domains showing a correlation with age at surgery are classified as cool executive functions [41]. These functions have been associated with the integrity of the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortices, particularly the right inferior frontal gyrus, which interacts with the cingulate cortex and subcortical regions [42]. Although previous studies have reported normalization of brain morphology after surgery in adolescents with sagittal craniosynostosis, persistent dysmorphology has been reported in frontoparietal regions [43], along with abnormal connectivity in the medial prefrontal region and the anterior cingulate [44].

Although our findings do not prove that delayed surgery causes executive difficulties, they align with evidence suggesting potential cognitive and behavioral benefits of earlier intervention [9, 14, 38]. Children whose surgery is delayed warrant systematic follow-up to detect and address any cognitive or behavioral challenges that could affect their academic or social development. If replicated in future studies, these findings suggest that craniosynostosis repair within the first year of life, when is feasible, may help reduce the risk of subsequent executive function impairments. Also, while the median BRIEF-2 scores for both groups were within the non-clinical range, individual case analysis revealed that some participants scored above the clinical threshold on specific scales. In such cases, targeted follow-up is essential to understand possible effects on daily life and provide parents with effective behavioral regulation strategies.

A significant limitation of the present study is its small sample size; our findings should not be generalized to the entire craniosynostosis population. The small sample size also made it impossible to perform a comparison of performance across different synostosis types, which is crucial given the reported variations in cognitive outcomes [7, 17, 45]. Moreover, children who underwent later surgery may have differed in clinical (e.g., milder initial presentation) or socioeconomic variables that could delay surgery, that were not assessed. Future studies should adjust for such confounders. The study would also have benefited from the use of performance-based measures of executive function alongside parent-reported assessments of everyday functioning. As Chapman et al. [31] have demonstrated, these two assessment types can yield different insights: their study revealed parental concerns about emotional regulation, task monitoring, and task initiation, while participants exhibited difficulties on processing speed and working memory tasks.

In conclusion, children and adolescents with non-syndromic craniosynostosis showed similar results as their peers on a questionnaire that assessed their everyday executive functioning, but there was a significant correlation between age at surgery and their results on variables related to cognitive executive functioning, indicating that participants whose surgery was performed later had more difficulties.
