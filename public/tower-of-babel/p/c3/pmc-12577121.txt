16p13.11 microduplication in 14 fetuses: prenatal diagnosis and postnatal follow-up
Authors: Yuchun Pan, Yu Hu, Chonglan Gao, Han Kang, Yifei Chen, Lingxi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground16p13.11 Microduplication is a rare genetic disorder with variable expression and incomplete penetrance, primarily reported in adults and children, with limited information available on fetal cases. This study aims to analyze the characteristics of prenatal diagnosis indications and postnatal follow-up in fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication, and to explore the genotype-phenotype correlation for improving genetic counseling and patients’ care.MethodsA total of 4552 pregnant women who underwent amniocentesis for SNP-array were retrospectively analyzed at the prenatal diagnosis department of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital from March 2022 to February 2024.ResultsSNP-array identified a microduplication at the 16p13.11 region in 14 fetuses, with sizes ranging from 0.8 to 1.65 Mb. Among the indications for prenatal diagnosis, Case 1, 13, 14 (3/14) presented a high-risk screening result for Down syndrome, Case 5, 8, 10,11 (4/14) demonstrated advanced maternal age, Case 7 (1/14) demonstrated abnormal reproductive history, Case 4 (1/14) demonstrated the pregnant woman was a known carrier of the 16p13.11 microduplication, and Case 2, 3, 6, 9, 12 (5/14) demonstrated ultrasound structural abnormalities. Only 5/14 underwent family verification, and all were inherited from one parent. 2/14 chose to terminate pregnancy, while the remaining cases resulted in term delivery. During follow-up, all but three cases showed no significant abnormalities: one had severe sensorineural hearing loss, one exhibited mild developmental delay, and one was diagnosed with a ventricular septal defect.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication typically exhibit a nonspecific prenatal phenotype but may be highly correlated with ultrasound abnormalities. Most affected individuals were in good health during follow-up. Furthermore, a systematic review of medical history, genetic diagnosis, and follow-up data could be useful for genetic counseling and patients’ growth management.Keywoeds16p13.11; microduplication; SNP-array; prenatal diagnosis; follow-up.

Introduction

Chromosome 16 is particularly enriched in low-copy repeats (LCRs), especially within 16p. These repetitive segments are thought to increase the risk of chromosomal structural abnormalities by non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR). Consequently, this region is highly susceptible to unbalanced chromosomal rearrangements, most commonly 16p13.11 microdeletions and microduplications [1]. Single-nucleotide polymorphism microarray (SNP-array) enables efficient genome-wide detection of these copy number variations (CNVs) [2]. While 16p13.11 microdeletions are established as pathogenic due to a well-defined genotype-phenotype correlation [3], the clinical significance of microduplications remains controversial.

The 16p13.11 microduplication syndrome is characterized by variable clinical presentations, including intellectual disability, developmental delay, behavioral abnormalities, epilepsy, schizophrenia, variable dysmorphic features, and anomalies in other organs [4–6]. While these manifestations have been more extensively documented in children and adults, prenatal phenotypic associations remain poorly characterized. In this study, we used SNP-array to identify recurrent 16p13.11 microduplication in fetuses. We further analyzed prenatal diagnosis indications, genetic results, pregnancy outcomes, and follow-up data to improve the understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations, clinical diagnosis, and growth monitoring in fetuses with this genomic imbalance.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective analysis included 4552 pregnant women who underwent invasive prenatal diagnosis with SNP-array for various indications at the prenatal diagnosis department of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital from March 2022 to February 2024. The average age of pregnant women was 31.9 years (range: 24–40 years), and the average gestational age was 21 weeks (range: 18–29 weeks). All participants signed informed consent after detailed genetic counseling. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital (No.2024(114)).

SNP-array

Genomic DNA was extracted from amniotic fluid of pregnant women and peripheral blood of the couple using a genome-wide DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, and was subsequently quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Cytoscan 750k chip (Affymetrix, USA) was used to detect genome-wide copy number variations (CNVs). Qualified genomic DNA samples were subjected to digestion, ligation, PCR amplification, labeling, hybridization, washing, and scanning according to standard operating procedures. Data were analyzed using the Chromosome analysis suite (ChAS) version 4.5 with alignment to the GRCh38 human reference. Further assessment of pathogenicity for the detected CNVs was conducted through relevant databases. The following databases were mainly referred to: DGV benign variation database (http://dgv.tcag.ca/dgv/app/home), DECIPHER pathological variation database (http://www.deciphergenomics.org/browser), the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen database) (http://www.clinicalgenome.org/), the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM database) (http://omim.org/), the International Standards for Cytogenomic Arrays (ISCA database) (http://www.iscaconsortium.org/), and the PubMed database, etc. Variant classification is based on the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG,2019), which recommended five categories (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, and benign) for CNVs [7].

Follow-up of pregnancy outcome

All pregnant women received regular prenatal check-ups, including blood tests and ultrasound examinations throughout their pregnancies. Pregnancy outcomes and neonatal health status were followed up via telephone. Under the national free basic child healthcare program, live-born infants underwent regular health assessments by specialists at 1, 3, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months of age. We then contacted their parents to record the children’s height and weight, neurodevelopment, and motor development.

Results

SNP-array results of the fetus

SNP-array analysis identified 16p13.11 microduplication in 14 fetuses (Table 1), with a prevalence of 3‰. The duplicated segments ranged in size from 0.8 to 1.65 Mb and encompassed between 7 and 13 OMIM genes (Fig. 1). According to the ClinGen database, all cases overlapped the 16p13.11 recurrent region (BP2-BP3, includes MYH11), with a triplosensitivity score of 2 (last evaluated on August 16, 2024), which indicates insufficient evidence of pathogenicity. This duplication retained its uncertain significance based on the ACMG variant classification guidelines.

Prenatal diagnosis indications

Of the 14 fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication, Case 1, 13, 14 (3/14) demonstrated a high-risk screening result for Down syndrome, Case 5, 8, 10, 11 (4/14) demonstrated advanced maternal age, Case 7 (1/14) demonstrated maternal reproductive history abnormally (a history of induced abortion for a fetus with CHD), Case 4 (1/14) demonstrated the pregnant woman was a known carrier of the CNV and Case 2, 3, 6, 9, 12 (5/14) demonstrated ultrasound structural abnormalities (Table 2). Case 2 suggested possible anal atresia, as the “target sign” in the perianal region is not completely visualized. For Case 3, it was recommended to rule out spina bifida occulta. Case 6 showed nuchal translucency (NT) thickening (2.5 mm) and an abdominal calcified lesion, with several hyperechoic foci noted, and the largest measured 1.5 cm in length and 0.3 cm in width. Case 9 exhibited a persistent right umbilical vein, which anastomosed with the right portal vein. Case 12 demonstrated moderate tricuspid regurgitation, confirmed by color flow Doppler and pulse wave Doppler, which revealed holosystolic tricuspid valve regurgitation.

Results of SNP-array family verification

Of the nine fetuses, parental segregation testing was declined by the families. Among the five that underwent pedigree analysis, three variants were of paternal origin (3/5) and two were maternal (2/5); all parents were asymptomatic carriers (Table 1).

Pregnancy outcome and follow-up results

Unfortunately, the fetuses of Case 2, 3 were terminated after detailed genetic counseling. The remaining 12 fetuses continued a pregnancy to full-term deliveries. The ages of these 12 children ranged from 1 year and 1 month to 3 years. The child in Case 6 was diagnosed with extremely severe hearing impairment caused by a homozygous mutation of SLC26A4 IVS7-2 A > G. There were two children exhibiting clinical phenotypes: one with mild developmental delay and weaker language skills (Case 7) and the other with a ventricular septal defect (VSD) (Case 12). No significant abnormalities were identified in the remaining nine cases (Table 2), as reported by parents during telephone follow-up.

Discussion

Genomic copy number variation (CNV) represents one of the most common causes of human genetic disorders. The underlying mechanism is attributed to the clustering of breakpoints within LCRs region, which was mediated by non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR). Many genomic disorders arise from this pathogenic mechanism, including microdeletion and microduplication syndromes at 16p13.11 [8]. In this study, we described 14 fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication, with variable prenatal indications and rearrangement sizes. All seven OMIM genes (MYH11, NDE1, ABCC1, ABCC6, CEP20, MARF1, MPV17L) were located within the duplicated region. Among these, the NDE1 gene encodes a protein essential for mitosis and brain development, making it a candidate gene for neurocognitive phenotypes. Dosage alteration of this gene may directly contribute to clinical manifestations such as brain dysfunction [9]. Although the pathogenic effects of NDE1 deletions have been confirmed in animal models [10], the role of its duplication remains unclear.

16p13.11 microduplications are associated with variable phenotypes and incomplete penetrance, with an estimated penetrance rate of approximately 7%11. Clinical features in carriers are often non-specific and may include neurocognitive abnormalities (developmental delay, intellectual disability, language delay, learning difficulties), behavioral abnormalities (autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder), cardiovascular diseases (CHD, aortic dissection), schizophrenia, epilepsy, skeletal abnormalities, visual impairments, etc [12–14].

However, prenatal ultrasound findings in fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication remain limited. REDAELLI S et al. reported 6 cases of 16p13.11 microduplication, including a 22w + 2 d fetus with non-immune hydrops and malformation in utero [15]. DĄBKOWSKA S et al. reported a 16p13.11 microduplication in a fetus with brain herniation [16]. ZHAO Y et al. found that the prenatal phenotypes of the fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication were highly correlated with ultrasound abnormalities but lacked specificity [17]. CAI M et al. investigated prenatal ultrasound findings in 15 fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication and reported an association between this chromosomal abnormality and fetal echogenic bowel [18]. Further intrauterine manifestations have also been documented in additional studies, including polyhydramnios, talipes, agenesis of the corpus callosum, cerebral cysts, cystic hygroma, and intrauterine growth restriction [19].

The clinical significance of the 16p13.11 microduplication remains controversial. While some researchers have suggested that it may represent a rare benign variant, as it has been identified in unaffected control populations [20], numerous studies have also reported associations with various clinical features, supporting a potential pathogenic role [21–23]. The DECIPHER database encompasses multiple cases classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, along with cases of similar variants with uncertain clinical significance. According to the ClinGen database, the duplicated regions overlapped with the 16p13.11 recurrent region (BP2-BP3) (encompassing MYH11), which demonstrated a triplosensitivity score of 2 (last evaluated on August 16, 2024), indicating little evidence for pathogenicity. Through systematic evaluation following ACMG guidelines, we would rather consider this CNV as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) with potential pathogenic implications. Furthermore, recombination within alternate LCRs in the 16p13.11 region can result in recurrent duplications of different sizes, however, genotype-phenotype correlations indicate that the size of the duplicated segment does not influence the severity of the phenotype, which is consistent with observations in our cohort, and genes such as NDE1, miR-484, and MYH11 seem to play critical roles in the associated phenotypic manifestations [22]. Additionally, this recurrent CNV was found to be inherited commonly [24]. In our study, most parents refused family verification and chose to continue the pregnancy because of low penetrance, and even if parents carried the same CNV as the fetus, the postnatal phenotype of the fetus cannot be predicted through extrapolation from parents. Only 5/14 underwent family verification, with 3/5 identified as paternal inheritance, and 2/5 as maternal inheritance. All parents were asymptomatic carriers. However, even when parents are phenotypically normal, familial analysis must be performed to distinguish between de novo mutations and familial transmission, which is critical for prenatal genetic counseling.

Among the prenatal indications of 14 fetuses, five (5/14) presented structural abnormalities on ultrasound. These findings included an anal atresia, spina bifida occulta, NT thickening, an abdominal calcified lesion, a persistent right umbilical vein and moderate tricuspid regurgitation. The sonographic phenotypes showed considerable heterogeneity, and no consistent characteristic features were identified. In our cohort, Case 4 involved a mother (G3P2, gravida 3 para 2) who was a known carrier of 16p13.11 microduplication, and she exhibited no abnormalities. Her first child (currently 5 years old) carried a 16p13.11 microduplication yet exhibited normal neurodevelopment, while the second child was diagnosed with epilepsy owing to a de novo pathogenic mutation unrelated to the CNV. So prenatal diagnosis was undertaken in the third pregnancy to assess genetic risk. Case 7 involved a maternal history of induced abortion for a fetus with CHD. The mother (G3P1, gravida 3 para 1) reported that her first child, now 14 years old, did not undergo genetic screening and showed normal physical and neurodevelopmental development. The second pregnancy was terminated due to a VSD, but no genetic testing was performed. The absence of genetic testing results for both parents and the second fetus complicated genetic counseling. Therefore, for families with a history of fetal abnormalities, it is recommended that thorough etiological investigations be carried out before evaluating recurrence risk in future pregnancies. Other common indications for prenatal diagnosis in the cohort included high-risk screening results for Down syndrome and advanced maternal age.

During follow-up, Case 2, 3 (2/14) with poor-prognosis fetal anomalies chose to terminate the pregnancy after multidisciplinary clinical counseling. Among 12 continuing pregnancies, we find that the child in Case 6, who was last followed up at the age of 2 years and 7 months, was diagnosed with severe sensorineural hearing loss. Molecular analysis identified a homozygous mutation of c.919-2 A > G in SLC26A4, which is consistent with a diagnosis of autosomal recessive non-syndromic hearing loss. However, the finding is not associated with the 16p13.11 microduplication syndrome. Case 7, 12 exhibited the relevant clinical phenotypes, with a penetrance rate of 16.7%, which is higher than previously reported [11]. This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as limited sample size, undetected mutations, and subtle clinical manifestations that went unrecognized. The child in Case 7, who was last followed up at the age of 2 years and 8 months, was diagnosed with mild developmental delay characterized by short stature, delayed speech, and sensitivity to cold. He was delivered via cesarean section at term, and achieved head control at 3 months, independent sitting at 6 months, and ambulation at 15 months, though crawling was not observed. The physical examination at the age of 2 showed that the height was 87 cm, the weight was 9.5kg, and the pronunciation was mostly reduplicative babbling, such as “mama” and” papa”, with a few coherent sentences. The child in Case 12, who was last followed up at the age of 2 years and 2 months, was confirmed with VSD, which was consistent with prenatal ultrasound examination. Other developmental parameters remained within the normal range. The remaining nine patients maintained age-appropriate developmental trajectories throughout surveillance.

This study still has some limitations. First, the sample size is small, with only 14 fetuses with 16p13.11 microduplication collected, and only 5 underwent family verification. Second, the correlation between ultrasound structural abnormalities and genomic CNVs is relatively complex, and point mutation was not conducted to further elucidate potential etiologic contributors. Third, the follow-up time is short, with the age ranges from 1 year and 1 month to 3 years. It is not possible to rule out delayed-onset symptoms or milder clinical presentations that may have been misclassified as completely normal. Finally, the causes of intellectual abnormalities are diverse, and the absence of whole exome sequencing to rule out other potential factors is a limitation in Case 7. So, future studies need larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and comprehensive clinical results to better understand the correlation of genotype-phenotype.

In conclusion, SNP-array appears to be an accurate method for detecting 16p13.11 microduplication, which shows an association with ultrasound structural abnormalities, though it may lack specificity. Follow-up observations suggest that many children with this CNV exhibit favorable outcomes after birth; however, continued monitoring of growth and development is still recommended.
