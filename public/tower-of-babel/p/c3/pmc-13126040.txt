HBV biomarkers outperform host genetic variants in distinguishing occult HBV infection from chronic carriers
Authors: Lin Zhou, Ying Yan, Huimin Ji, Huizhen Sun, Zhuoqun Lu, Le Chang, Lunan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study aimed to determine if host genetic variants could improve the differentiation of occult hepatitis B virus infection (OBI) from overt chronic infection in Chinese blood donors, beyond standard biomarkers. Through a multicenter screening of over 1.14 million donations and a two-year follow-up, we established a cohort of 147 confirmed carriers (74 OBI and 73 overt) who underwent whole-exome sequencing. A model based on 5 routinely available biomarkers—older age, higher HBV DNA Ct value (indicating lower viral load), elevated anti-HBs, decreased anti-HBc, and anti-HBe negativity—achieved high accuracy (AUCs: 0.898–0.910). In contrast, while 9 missense variants showed initial associations, none were validated, and their inclusion reduced model generalizability. We conclude that serological and virological biomarkers are highly effective for OBI risk stratification, whereas common host genetic variants offer minimal incremental value, highlighting the practical superiority of biomarker profiling and setting a benchmark for future research.

Introduction

Occult hepatitis B virus infection (OBI) represents a special clinical condition distinct from chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, characterized by detectable replication-competent HBV DNA in liver tissue despite serum HBsAg negativity.1 OBI prevalence in blood donors correlated with HBV endemicity levels, with rates increasing from 0.06% in low-endemicity countries to 0.98% in high-endemicity countries,2 but this likely underestimated the true rate—for example, HBV DNA was found in 16% of Italians with normal liver histology during abdominal surgery (2002–2006).3 At present, OBI poses dual clinical challenges: potential transmission through blood transfusions and organ transplants4,5,6 and increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma development.7,8 The precise mechanisms governing OBI establishment remain elusive, though emerging evidence suggests host factors may play pivotal roles.9

Current diagnostic paradigms for OBI face critical limitations. Although detection of replication-competent HBV DNA in liver tissue remains the diagnostic gold standard,1 it requires invasive liver biopsy, which is often unacceptable for asymptomatic or non-manifest OBI cases. Although serological markers (anti-HBs and anti-HBc) and viral load measurements are commonly used biomarkers,10,11,12 their predictive value for OBI risk stratification requires further validation. Most importantly, a systematic evaluation of host genetic determinants in well-characterized OBI cohorts is still entirely lacking.1 This knowledge gap impedes the development of robust prediction models.

To overcome these limitations, a pivotal question must be addressed regarding what host-specific factors determine the distinct clinical outcome of OBI, characterized by persistent infection without detectable HBsAg, after chronic HBV infection is established. While comparing OBI with cases of past HBV infection is a clinically nuanced challenge, our study specifically focuses on contrasting OBI against overt HBsAg-positive chronic infection. This design was chosen to directly investigate the host-virus interactions underlying the suppression of HBsAg production among individuals with confirmed chronic viral persistence. Using overt infection as the comparator provides a more homogeneous and interpretable model for identifying host factors associated specifically with the occult phenotype, minimizing the confounding heterogeneity inherent in past infection groups.

To address this gap, we conducted an exploratory genetic investigation to establish baseline data and evaluate the relative contribution of host genetics against established biomarkers. Through systematic screening of over 1.14 million blood donations and rigorous longitudinal follow-up, we established a well-characterized cohort of 147 HBV-infected individuals (74 OBI cases and 73 overt carriers) with whole-exome sequencing data. Using this cohort, we performed a systematic, head-to-head comparison to determine whether host genetic variants provide complementary predictive power for discriminating OBI from overt infection, or if established biomarkers remain the dominant predictors.

Results

Development and validation of the HBV indicator-based OBI occurrence risk model

The study included 147 participants, who were divided into training and validation sets based on the chronological order of their blood donation (Figure 1; Tables S1 and S2). Based on the training samples, we identified 5 HBV indicators showing significant differences between occult and overt HBV infection groups: older age (p = 3.521 × 10−4), higher HBV DNA Ct values (p = 4.058 × 10−5), elevated anti-HBs titers (p = 1.128 × 10−10), decreased anti-HBc S/CO ratios (p = 3.412 × 10−6), and a higher proportion of anti-HBe negativity (p = 2.931 × 10−3) (Figure 2; Table 1). After log-transforming anti-HBs titers, these features were incorporated into an indicator-based logistic regression model for assessing OBI occurrence risk. The probability of OBI was calculated as P(OBI)=1/(1+e−z), where z=−5.358+(0.122×Age)+(−1.409×AntiHBeStatus)+(0.034×HBVDNACt)+(1.968×log10(AntiHBs)+(−0.175×AntiHBcS/CO)). For the binary variable anti-HBe_Status, positive was coded as 1 and negative as 0. The model demonstrated excellent performance in both training (leave-one-out area under the curve [LOO AUC]: 0.898, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.838–0.958) and validation set (AUC: 0.910, 95% CI: 0.811–1). Limited contribution of candidate missense genetic variants to the OBI risk prediction

We identified 9 missense genetic variants (rs4849167, rs144850646, rs11578336, rs706107, rs2244008, rs11544238, rs6692611, rs3741628, and rs914358) showing significant differences between occult and overt HBV infection groups in the training set (p < 0.002; Tables S3 and S4) as described in the STAR Methods. Using genotype-encoded features of these variants, we constructed a genetic risk model for OBI based on the random forest algorithm. Subsequently, the OBI genetic risk scores were combined with the 5 HBV indicators (age, HBV DNA Ct value, anti-HBs titer, anti-HBc level, and anti-HBe status) to develop an OBI indicator + genetic risk model (Table S2). However, in contrast to the 5 indicators, which demonstrated strong generalizability by performing well in both the training and validation samples, the inclusion of genetic factors did not improve the generalizability of the indicator-based model (Figure 3). The HBV indicator-based model maintained excellent performance in both the training set (AUC: 0.904, 95% CI: 0.843–0.965) and the validation set (AUC: 0.905, 95% CI: 0.802–1.000). Consistent with the results prior to exclusion, incorporating the 9 genetic missense variants improved performance in the training set (AUC: 0.936, 95% CI: 0.889–0.984) but led to reduced performance in the validation set (AUC: 0.887, 95% CI: 0.775–1.000). These results suggest that the genetic markers identified from the training set may represent false-positive findings. This is likely attributable to the limited sample size, and also reflects the possibility that genetic variants—at least missense variants in the whole exome—may lack strong discriminatory features comparable to those of the HBV indicators, which effectively distinguish between occult and overt HBV infection groups.

Divergent generalizability of HBV indicators and genetic markers

We further examined the distribution patterns of the 5 indicators (age, HBV DNA Ct value, anti-HBs titers, anti-HBc, and anti-HBe) and 9 genetic variants across case/control groups in both training and validation set. The 4 indicators consistently exhibited significant differences between occult and overt HBV infection in both training and validation sets (Figure 4). Stratified analysis by vaccination status

To assess the potential influence of HBV vaccination on anti-HBs levels and overall model accuracy, we stratified samples into vaccinated (N = 36) and unvaccinated (N = 95) groups and compared the distribution patterns of the 5 OBI-related indicators between occult and overt HBV infections. Consistent and statistically significant differential trends were observed for four of the five indicators in both groups (all p < 0.05; Figure 6). The exception was anti-HBe status, which did not reach significance in the smaller vaccinated group (p = 0.146), but still exhibited a congruent trend of higher anti-HBe negativity in the OBI group. These results suggest that HBV vaccination status does not substantially alter the phenotypic differences between occult and overt HBV infections. Consequently, the indicator-based model demonstrated robust performance across vaccination statuses, showing high predictive performance in both groups. Discussion

This study presents a foundational, benchmark-setting investigation of OBI in Chinese blood donors. Its primary contribution lies not in immediate clinical translation, but definitively delineates the respective role of established biomarkers and host genetics. To investigate the host-virus interaction that determines the infection outcome after HBV exposure, we deliberately selected overt HBV infection cases rather than uninfected healthy subjects as controls. This design allows a direct comparison between two established chronic HBV infection states, avoiding the uncertainty of previous exposure status in healthy populations and aligning with our research objective of identifying factors that may predispose toward occult rather than overt infection following HBV exposure. Our research identified 5 robust indicators that effectively differentiate OBI from overt HBV infection: older age (p = 3.521 × 10−4), higher HBV DNA Ct values (p = 4.058 × 10−5, reflecting lower viral load), elevated anti-HBs titers (p = 1.128 × 10−10), decreased anti-HBc levels (p = 3.412 × 10−6), and higher proportion of anti-HBe negativity (p = 2.931 × 10−3). The model based on these indicators demonstrated reasonable performance, achieving an AUC of 0.898 (95% CI: 0.838–0.958) in the training cohort, and maintained similar accuracy in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.910, 95% CI: 0.811–1). Furthermore, the higher model performance in the vaccinated group (sensitivity/specificity: 94.44%/94.44%) compared to the unvaccinated group (80.77%/81.40%) raises an intriguing possibility, even when considering the potential influence of sample size. Specially, HBV vaccination might not only prevent new infections but also modulate the host-virus interaction in established infections, potentially amplifying phenotypic differences between occult and overt states. This hypothesis, which suggests that vaccination could make OBI “more occult” and overt infection “more overt”, warrants further investigation in larger prospective studies.

Our genetic analysis yielded a critical benchmark finding, which we explicitly frame as a key negative result. Contrary to initial assumptions, the common missense variants we tested provided no significant predictive value beyond established biomarkers. While 9 missense variants initially showed nominally significant associations in the discovery phase (p < 0.002), none were replicated in the validation set (p > 0.05). More importantly, the inclusion of genetic variants actually reduced model generalizability (validation AUC dropped from 0.910 to 0.890). These results demonstrate that the incremental value of these common genetic variants is negligible for differentiating OBI from overt infection when compared to routine serological and virological markers. To elucidate the biological context of these findings, we performed a systematic functional annotation of the 9 candidate variants. Strikingly, DAVID13 pathway enrichment analysis revealed no significant enrichment for any pathway related to viral infection or immune response, indicating that these genes do not constitute a biologically coherent set. Further individual analysis showed that the encoded proteins—such as PSD4 (involved in ARF6 GTPase signaling14), LAMA2 (an extracellular matrix component15), and ARHGAP9 (a Rho GTPases regulator in hematopoietic cells16)—are implicated in diverse fundamental processes, including cytoskeletal organization and intracellular signaling. Critically, however, none of these genes have a well-established, direct role in HBV replication, persistence, or the specific antiviral immune responses pertinent to OBI. This profound lack of biological plausibility, strongly corroborated by the statistical failure to validate, supports the conclusion that the initial associations likely represent false positives attributable to the exploratory study design rather than genuine genetic risk factors for OBI. This finding sets a crucial performance benchmark, guiding future research away from similar variant types and toward more promising candidates.

Our failure to detect clear genetic signals may be attributed to several factors: first, the population heterogeneity across 21 geographically diverse blood centers—while enhancing generalizability—may have diluted region-specific genetic effects observed in prior studies of homogeneous cohorts.17,18 Second, statistical power was constrained by the limited sample size. Third, by focusing exclusively on missense variants called by GATK, we may have overlooked other functional genetic elements. This includes non-coding regulatory variations, structural variants, or variations in complex genomic regions that standard GATK pipelines might fail to detect, such as those previously implicated in OBI (such as the IL-10 promoter19). Future studies combining whole-genome sequencing with deep immunogenetic profiling in larger OBI cohorts may better resolve these apparent discrepancies and uncover more robust genetic determinants.

The distinct profile of OBI cases characterized by older age, lower viral load, elevated anti-HBs titers, decreased anti-HBc levels, and a higher proportion of anti-HBe negativity suggests unique immunological control mechanisms differentiating it from overt HBV infection. The association with older age may reflect longer duration of infection or age-related immune changes though this observation requires further investigation. The strong association with elevated anti-HBs titers particularly supports the hypothesis that humoral immunity plays a crucial role in suppressing HBsAg expression while maintaining low-level viral replication. This aligns with emerging evidence about the importance of host immune factors in OBI pathogenesis.20,21,22 Notably, the significantly lower anti-HBc levels observed in OBI cases further reinforce serological differences between these infection states. Anti-HBc is traditionally regarded as an evidential serological marker of HBV infections.23 Our use of quantitative S/CO ratios reveals a gradient in immune response between groups. Although only 4 of the 147 samples fell below the positive cutoff (S/CO < 1), OBI cases demonstrated significantly lower median anti-HBc levels than overt infection controls (training: 7.96 [6.47, 8.53] vs. 9.2 [8.24, 9.86]; validation: 7.74 [5.93, 8.54] vs. 9.34 [8.65, 9.8]). This pattern combined with elevated anti-HBs and a higher rate of anti-HBe negativity suggests a distinct serological profile in OBI. This interpretation aligns with existing literature indicating that anti-HBc titers correlate with intrahepatic cccDNA persistence11 and HBV DNA detectability in blood.24 The comparison of biomarkers between OBI and chronic HBV carriers offers insights into host-virus interactions that may contribute to occult infection states.

An important consideration raised by our findings and consistent with clinical guidelines (e.g., EASL25) is whether OBI represents a distinct trajectory following HBV exposure or rather a subsequent phase in the natural history of chronic HBV infection. The observed profile of OBI cases—particularly older age, lower viral load, higher anti-HBs, and reduced anti-HBc levels—could be interpreted as evidence of OBI being a later stage of immune control following a period of overt HBsAg-positive infection. This perspective aligns with the concept of OBI as a potential outcome of long-term host-virus interaction. However, our longitudinal data over a two-year follow-up period present a nuanced picture. While we did observe rare instances of transition between OBI and overt infection states, the overwhelming majority of participants maintained stable serological profiles throughout the observation window. This stability could reflect the relatively short duration of follow up, which may be insufficient to capture long-term transitions that potentially occur over decades. Alternatively, it may suggest that a significant proportion of OBI cases arise without progressing through a prolonged overt infection phase, possibly due to particular host immune responses that effectively suppress HBsAg production from the outset. The quantitative anti-HBc analysis provides further insight into this dynamic. The significantly lower anti-HBc levels in OBI cases compared to overt infection controls may reflect reduced antigenic stimulation over time, consistent with better immune containment of viral replication. This pattern reinforces the serological distinction between these infection states and supports the interpretation of differing immune landscapes. Thus, while our data are consistent with the interpretation that OBI can represent a late phase of chronic infection in some individuals, they also leave open the possibility of alternative pathways where OBI arises directly following HBV exposure. Future studies with longer-term follow up and detailed historical serological data are needed to fully resolve these potential trajectories.

In conclusion, this exploratory study delivers a clear message for OBI risk stratification: routinely available HBV biomarkers are highly effective, while the incremental value of common host genetic variants, as tested here, is minimal. Our findings not only provide insights but, more importantly, help define the boundaries of current knowledge. By establishing that a simple model based on five biomarkers can achieve high accuracy (AUC≈0.9), we provide a robust baseline against which future studies (whether investigating non-coding genetic variants, genetic variants in complex regions, viral factors, or novel omics approaches) must be measured. Future research should therefore prioritize these alternative avenues beyond common missense variants to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms of OBI.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was constrained by difficulties in donor follow up during the COVID-19 pandemic, which resulted in a limited number of confirmed OBI and overt HBV cases available for analysis. Additionally, a proportion of extracted nucleic acid samples did not meet the quality requirements for whole-exome sequencing, further reducing the genetic analysis sample size. Second, although viral factors such as HBV genotype and S-region mutations are known to influence OBI, these were not investigated due to the extremely low viral load in OBI cases, which poses significant challenges for genotyping and sequencing. Third, as our findings are based on a multi-center Chinese blood donor population, the applicability to other populations may be limited.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Species

Human, Chinese blood donors.

Genotype

Not applicable (this study aimed to explore host genetic variants but did not pre-screen participants based on specific genotypes). All participants underwent whole-exome sequencing to identify genetic variants.

Age

The median age of the total study population was 42.0 years (Interquartile Range, IQR: 33.0, 49.0). The Occult HBV Infection (OBI) group was significantly older (Training set median: 48.0 years [IQR: 38.0, 51.0]) than the overt infection group (Training set median: 38.0 years [IQR: 29.0, 46.0]).

Sex

The total number of participants was 147, comprising 110 males (74.8%) and 37 females (25.2%). The distribution of sexes was generally balanced between case (OBI) and control (overt infection) groups in both the training and validation sets (Training set: OBI Male/Female = 42/11, Overt Male/Female = 40/13; Validation set: OBI Male/Female = 17/4, Overt Male/Female = 11/9). While sex was included as a candidate predictor in the initial univariate analysis, it was not identified as a statistically significant factor for distinguishing OBI from overt infection (p > 0.05, Fisher’s exact test) and was therefore not selected for inclusion in the final biomarker-based logistic regression model. The overall male-to-female ratio in the cohort reflects the general demographic pattern observed in blood donor populations in China. The balanced distribution of sexes between the compared groups within the training and validation sets helps to minimize potential confounding bias arising from sex disparities in this specific analysis. However, the relatively smaller proportion of female participants limits the statistical power for a robust, sex-stratified analysis of the prediction model’s performance. Therefore, the generalizability of the model’s performance across different sex groups, while not indicating a significant difference based on available data, should be further validated in future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts.

Ancestry, race, and ethnicity

Participants were recruited from 13 provinces and autonomous regions across China, with an uneven geographical distribution (highest proportion from Southwest China [38.8%] and South China [33.3%]). Speific information on ancestry, race, and ethnicity was not reported.

Method details

Study population and cohort establishment

A total of 1,141,331 blood donation samples from 21 blood establishments in China (March 2020 to January 2021) were initially screened for HBV infection. 2,690 donors testing initially “positive” for HBsAg or HBV DNA underwent additional confirmatory testing for HBV serological and nucleic acid markers using the ARCHITECT HBsAg Qualitative II assay (Abbott Ireland Diagnostics Division; LOD: 0.05 IU/mL), ARCHITECT HBsAg Qualitative II Confirmatory assay (Abbott), and Cobas TaqScreen MPX Test, version 2.0 (Roche Molecular Systems; LOD: 2.3 IU/mL). 2,315 confirmed donors were invited for regular semi-annual follow-up. During the follow-up, one HBsAg-negative individual with an HBV DNA level >200 IU/mL was identified and excluded from the OBI cohort. Finally, 147 participants meeting the strict inclusion criteria were enrolled and split into a training set (n = 106; 53 OBI cases, 53 overt infection controls) comprising donors from March-July 2020, and a validation set (n = 41; 21 OBI cases, 20 overt infection controls) comprising donors from August 2020-January 2021. Importantly, all enrolled donors were confirmed to have no history of antiviral therapy for HBV prior to and during the study follow-up period.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics (age, sex) were collected through questionnaires at enrollment. Age and sex were recorded as reported during enrollment. Serological markers assessed during the first follow-up visit were used in this study. ALT levels were measured using automatic biochemical analyzer. HBV DNA viral load was quantified by Roche Molecular Systems, with Cycle threshold (Ct) values reported according to manufacturer recommendations (maximum cycles: 60). HBV serological markers (anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, anti-HBc) were tested using ARCHITECT immunoassays. Recognizing that universal newborn vaccination was officially prioritized in 1992 in China,30 we utilized a combined method for vaccination status classification, with questionnaire results taking precedence. Participants were categorized as: (i) vaccinated, self-reported receiving HBV vaccine before first follow-up, and (ii) unvaccinated, reported no HBV vaccination before first follow-up. Participants born on or after January 1, 1992, but with a negative or uncertain vaccination history in the questionnaire, were excluded from the comparative analysis to ensure group clarity. All information were listed in Table S1.

Laboratory procedures

Whole exome sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA was extracted from white blood cells (WBCs using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) for whole exome sequencing (WES). Agilent 5400 fragment analyzer system was used for DNA sample quality control. The Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit was used for capture and library preparation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Paired-end sequencing with a 150 bp read length was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing information for the 147 participants used in the study are summarized in Table S2.

Germline variant calling

Germline variants of exome targets have been called following the GATK best practices workflow (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/best-practices). Sequence reads were aligned to GRCh37 genomes using ‘BWA (v0.7.17) MEM’. Indel realignment, base quality score recalibrations (BQSR), and removal of PCR duplicates from the resultant BAM files were performed according to the GATK (v4.4). Genotypes were called by GATK HaplotypeCaller for each sample at all variant positions. GenomicsDBImport was used to merge genotype files. Germline SNPs and indels were filtered with variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) by setting truth sensitivity filter level at 100. Joint variant calling was performed on 147 samples. Variants were retained using the following criteria: (i) A minimum of 10 was required for read depth of each sample. (ii) A minimum of 30 was required for average read depth over training samples. (iii) Variants annotated high or moderate impact were retained. snpEFF and snpSift program were used for annotating the variants’ function.

Statistical analysis procedures

Identification of predictive indicators and model construction

Using 106 training samples, we evaluated demographic characteristics (age and sex), serum biomarkers (ALT, anti-HBs titer, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc level), and viral load (HBV DNA Ct value) for their ability to stratify chronic HBV carriers by HBsAg status (+/−; OBI cases vs. overt HBV infection controls) (Figure 2). To incorporate viral load as continuous measures in the predictive model, with a maximum cycle threshold of 60, an HBV DNA Ct value of 60 was assigned to samples with undetectable viral loads (below the lower limit of detection). This approach is particularly relevant for OBI cases, where viral loads are typically extremely low and often fall below the limit of quantitative assays. The anti-HBc level was quantified using the S/CO ratio obtained from the ARCHITECT assay. To identify significant predictors, categorial variables (sex [male = 1, female = 0], anti-HBe, and HBeAgc [positive = 1, negative = 0; cutoff = 1 S/CO]) were analyzed using a two-sided Fisher exact test, while continuous variables (age, HBV DNA Ct value, anti-HBs titer, anti-HBc S/CO ratio and ALT level) were assessed via the Mann-Whitney U test. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied for both tests. Predictors showing significant differences between groups were selected as potential indicators for OBI occurrence. Subsequently, an L2-regularized logistic regression with default settings was used to construct the indicator-based risk stratification model for OBI occurrence in chronic HBV carriers.

Identification of predictive genetic variants and model construction

We performed a genetic association analysis in PLINK 1.9 using Fisher’s exact test on the training samples, comparing 53 OBI cases with 53 overt HBV infection controls. The analysis was based on GATK-called missense variants that met all quality criteria. Based on GATK-called missense variants that qualified the criteria mentioned above. We ranked the missense variants based on their strength of association (from the smallest p value to the largest). It is notable that only nine variants had a p value less than 0.002, given the relatively small sample size (Figure 2). Genotypes were numerically encoded as: 0 for reference homozygous, 1 for heterozygous, and 2 for alternative homozygous. The nine variants with p < 0.002 were subsequently used to construct a random forest prediction model (n_estimators = 10, random_state = 1) for OBI classification.

Evaluation of genetic factors on model performance

To assess the impact of genetic factors on predictive performance, we constructed an ensemble model incorporating both HBV indicators and genetic risk scores (GRS) derived from leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation on training samples. We further compared the performance between the indicator-based logistic regression model and the GRS-enrolled combined model using LOO evaluation on training data and independent validation of AUC differences. The risk stratification threshold was set to 0.55, with subsequent calculation of sensitivity and specificity in both training and validation cohorts.

Experimental design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study. The sample size was determined by the number of available samples meeting the stringent inclusion criteria, not by a priori sample size calculation. Group assignment (OBI case vs. overt infection control) was based on longitudinal serological and virological test results, not randomization. Blinding was not implemented for laboratory analyses or statistical assessments, consistent with the exploratory genetic study design. Data inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed above.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and software

Statistical analyses were performed using Python v3.8 for Fisher’s exact test, Mann-Whitney U test, logistic regression and random forest modeling, and PLINK 1.9 for genetic association analysis. Detailed statistical results are reported in the main text, figure legends, and tables (Table 1; Figures 3, 4, 5, and 6; Tables S1 and S3).

Statistical tests and data presentation

Group comparisons

Categorical variables were analyzed using two-sided Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables, reported as median and IQR (as their distribution was not assumed to be normal), were compared using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. In figure legends, p values are reported directly. Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Sample size (n)

The value n represents the number of participants/samples. n = 106 for the training set (53 OBI, 53 overt). n = 41 for the validation set (21 OBI, 20 overt). For stratified analysis by vaccination status, n = 36 (vaccinated) and n = 95 (unvaccinated). For genetic association analysis, n = 106 (training set).

Data presentation

Continuous variables (e.g., age, ALT, anti-HBs, anti-HBc, HBV DNA Ct) are presented as median and IQR as measures of center and dispersion. Categorical variables (e.g., HBeAg status, anti-HBe status, sex) are presented as counts and proportions. In figures presenting pooled data, the center of boxplots represents the median, the box boundaries represent the IQR, and the whiskers extend to 1.5 times the IQR. Individual data points are overlaid where appropriate.

Model performance evaluation

The logistic regression model was evaluated via Leave-One-Out (LOO) cross-validation on the training set and on the independent validation set. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) with its 95% Confidence Interval (CI) is reported. Sensitivity and specificity at the chosen threshold (0.55) are also provided. The CI indicates the precision of the AUC estimate.

Statistical methods related to experimental design

Randomization and stratification

Randomization was not employed. Cases (OBI) and controls (overt infection) were defined based on clear serological and virological criteria. The split into training and validation sets was based on the chronological order of blood donation.

Sample size estimation

As an exploratory study, the sample size was determined by the availability of samples meeting the strict inclusion/exclusion criteria, not by a prior power analysis.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria of data/subjects

As detailed in the Methods. To assess robustness, model performance was re-evaluated after excluding 12 samples from East China (11 in training, 1 in validation) due to regional imbalance.

Assessment of statistical assumptions

Formal testing for normality or homogeneity of variances was not performed, as non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U) and resampling methods (LOO) were used.
