Multidecadal decoupling between coral calcifying fluid and seawater saturation states
Authors: Jessica C. Hankins, Thomas M. DeCarlo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ocean acidification poses a threat to coral skeleton formation via reductions in the saturation state of aragonite (ΩAr) in seawater. Given that corals precipitate their skeletons from a calcifying fluid supplied by seawater, reductions in seawater ΩAr should, in theory, confound calcification. Here, we reconstruct up to 200 years of coral calcifying fluid ΩAr, using Raman spectroscopy techniques, at approximately monthly resolution in two Porites sp. skeletal cores from the Coral Sea region to investigate (i) the regulation of coral calcifying fluid ΩAr and (ii) the skeletal calcification response to industrial-era ocean acidification. Our results reveal a significant increase in calcifying fluid ΩAr, suggesting that some corals may adjust to the pace of acidification in the wild more effectively than suggested by short-term laboratory studies.

INTRODUCTION

As eco-engineers, corals support complex and biodiverse ecosystems by constructing their calcium carbonate (CaCO3) skeletons, which ultimately form the framework of coral reefs over thousands of years. Ongoing ocean absorption of anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) gradually decreases seawater pH and carbonate ion concentration ([CO32−]) through a process called ocean acidification (1). The reduction of seawater carbonate ion concentrations reduces the saturation state of CaCO3 in seawater as defined in Eq. 1Ω=[Ca2+][CO32−]Ksp*(1)where the saturation state of CaCO3 (Ω) is a function of calcium and carbonate ion concentration and a thermodynamic solubility product ( Ksp* ) for the appropriate polymorph of interest (i.e., calcite, aragonite, and vaterite). Under a high-emission scenario, ocean acidification is expected to induce a 0.3- to 0.4-unit decrease in pH by 2100, resulting in a 50% reduction in seawater carbonate ion concentrations relative to preindustrial concentrations (2). Given that Ω defines the favorability for precipitation of coral aragonitic skeletons, ocean acidification–driven declines in seawater Ω raise concerns about coral skeletal formation and reef structures.

Coral calcification (i.e., precipitation of skeletal aragonite) occurs in a semi-isolated environment located between the living tissue and existing skeleton (3) called the calcifying fluid, which is thought to originate as ambient seawater (4–6). Corals actively regulate their calcifying fluid chemistry to induce high aragonite saturation states through a variety of mechanisms including proton and/or Ca2+ pumping, CO2 regulation, and organic matrix production, yielding an optimal environment for rapid precipitation of aragonite crystals (7, 8). The rate at which corals precipitate their skeletal aragonite has been shown to be dependent on external carbonate ion concentration ([CO32−]) (9). Therefore, ocean acidification has the potential to reduce gross calcification through reductions in seawater ΩAr, affecting calcifying fluid chemistry and slowing rates of aragonite crystal growth.

Despite the impending threat of ocean acidification, mechanisms underlying coral skeletal sensitivity are complex and variable. Corals have been shown in experimental settings to have the reduced ability to regulate their calcifying fluid chemistry during simulated ocean acidification, as this regulation is energetically costly under low seawater ΩAr (10, 11). A meta-analysis of experimental studies demonstrated that calcification responses to declines in ΩAr are highly variable, ranging from a 25% increase to a 66% decrease, averaging a decline of ~15% per unit decrease in ΩAr (12). In the wild, calcification rates of some long-lived Porites spp. colonies on Australia’s Great Barrier Reef (GBR) began to decline in the late 20th century, with decreasing seawater ΩAr thought to be one of the primary drivers (13), although this decline was not observed in a more recent analysis (14). In addition, numerical models have suggested that massive Porites corals are sensitive to changes in seawater chemistry despite some ability to modulate their internal calcifying fluid chemistry (15). However, coral calcification sensitivity to human CO2-induced acidification has typically been inferred on short timescales with few studies lasting longer than 1 year (16, 17). Considering the multidecadal timescale of changes in ocean chemistry expected from CO2 emissions throughout the industrial era, a crucial step toward identifying how corals may respond to anthropogenic climate change in the future is to characterize long-term changes in calcifying fluid chemistry.

Coral skeletal cores serve as ideal archives to assess the long-term response of coral sensitivity to ocean acidification. Massive, reef-building corals such as Porites spp. precipitate their skeletons in successive layers, where the structure and chemistry of each layer preserve a geochemical signature reflecting coral calcifying fluid conditions at the time of skeletal formation (7, 18, 19). Indeed, the relative abundance of trace elements and isotopic signatures encoded within coral skeletons are the foundation of geochemical proxies developed to infer temporal variability in calcifying fluid chemistry (6). Boron systematics (i.e., δ11B and B/Ca) are key geochemical proxies that enable the reconstruction of calcifying fluid chemistry by constraining pH and [CO32−] (20, 21). However, these geochemical proxies applied to coral skeletons suffer from two main issues: Confounding effects of temperature (22) and existing methods are unable to directly quantify changes in ΩAr (hereafter referred to as Ωcf for the calcifying fluid and Ωsw for external seawater).

Raman spectroscopy is an advantageous technique for testing the sensitivity of Ωcf to ocean acidification due to its ability to measure at the micrometer scale and high sensitivity to the saturation state at which aragonite crystals were formed (23). Raman shifts characterize the internal and lattice vibrations within and between carbonate molecules (24), and in particular, the v1 peak (~1085 cm−1) represents the symmetric stretching of the carbonate C─O bond (25). The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the v1 peak represents positional disorder in the carbonate ion within the aragonite crystal lattice (25). FWHM increases as crystals are formed under higher ΩAr because crystals that grow under supersaturated conditions incorporate more impurities in their crystal lattice, leading to greater positional disorder and variability of the C─O bond lengths (24). Here, we leverage Raman spectroscopy techniques to assess the response of coral Ωcf to ocean acidification over decadal-to-centennial timescales through the evaluation of two Porites sp. coral skeletal cores collected from the greater Coral Sea region (Fig. 1) dating from 1818 to 2017 and 1902 to 2017, respectively.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We applied transmission Raman spectroscopy to skeletal cores from long-lived coral colonies to develop centennial reconstructions of Ωcf. Given observations of coral sensitivity to ocean acidification in field (26, 27), laboratory (16, 28, 29), and modeling (30–32) studies, we expected declining trends of Ωcf in these cores, tracking changes in Ωsw driven by human CO2 emissions. However, contrary to our expectations, Porites Ωcf increased for both the Yonge Reef and Coral Sea (Fig. 2A) cores, beginning in 1976 and 1839, respectively. On the basis of a change-point analysis, the Coral Sea core (Fig. 2A) showed a decline in Ωcf from 1818 to 1839 (P < 0.001), followed by a significant (P < 0.001) increase in Ωcf from 1839 to 2017 (95% confidence interval: 0.0028 ± 0.0007 Ωcf year−1), with an average Ωcf of 12.17 ± 0.05 (1 std. err.). The Yonge Reef core (Fig. 1A) showed no significant increase in Ωcf from 1902 to 1975 (P = 0.15), followed by a significant increase (P < 0.001) in Ωcf from 1975 to 2017 (95% confidence interval: 0.0134 ± 0.0043 Ωcf year−1), with an average Ωcf of 12.95 ± 0.04 (1 std. err.). Although these cores portray interannual to multidecadal variability, we observed an overall increase in Ωcf despite clear declines in Ωsw during the industrial era.

Our observed trend of increasing Ωcf is seemingly inconsistent with findings from similar studies that were conducted in the same region but under different methods. A reconstruction of pHcf and [CO32−] cf based on boron isotope systematics (δ11B and B/Ca) in a Porites core collected from Lizard Island, located ~18 km west of Yonge Reef, indicates that coral calcifying fluid chemistry is sensitive to both seawater pH and temperature on seasonal to multidecadal timescales (22). In addition, a multidecadal δ11B reconstruction of seawater pH from a Porites core collected from the central GBR showed declining calcifying fluid pH over the past 60 years (33). Yet, it is important to note that boron systematics (δ11B and B/Ca proxies) only provide estimates of pHcf and [CO32−]cf, and to estimate Ωcf requires making an assumption regarding calcifying fluid calcium ion concentration ([Ca2+]cf) (e.g., that it is the same as [Ca2+]sw). The assumption of constant [Ca2+]cf may be particularly important because corals regulate [Ca2+]cf, as evidenced by (i) isotopic fractionation of Ca in coral skeletons relative to seawater (34, 35), (ii) combined boron/Raman assessments of [Ca2+]cf (36), and (iii) direct microsensor measurements of the calcifying fluid (37, 38). Although strong correlations have been observed between boron-derived and Raman-derived Ωcf on seasonal timescales (22), there is still potential that the reported pHcf and [CO32−]cf declines in (22) were decoupled from Ωcf as a result of [Ca2+]cf.

Although methodological differences between boron and Raman proxies could explain the discrepancies between our results and those in (22), our multidecadal to centennial trends of increasing Ωcf are also inconsistent with the trend of Ωsw. We found that annual Ωsw and Ωcf were significantly negatively correlated in both study locations (Coral Sea: P < 0.001, Fig. 2B; Yonge Reef: P < 0.001, Fig. 2B). Several potential explanations exist to reconcile this initially unexpected result. First, although we observed no significant effect of Ωcf on calcification (Coral Sea: P > 0.05; Yonge Reef: P > 0.05) or linear extension at Yonge Reef (P > 0.05), Ωcf did have a significant effect on linear extension in the Coral Sea (P < 0.05). Nonetheless, all three parameters exhibit a positive trend with time both in the Coral Sea and on Yonge Reef (Fig. 2, A, D, and E). Coral growth parameters (i.e., linear extension and calcification) have shown varying responses to changes in seawater carbonate chemistry depending on coral species (39, 40). In particular, Porites corals have demonstrated the potential to maintain their growth rates despite declines in Ωsw (41), and some authors have considered them to be “winners” in the face of acidified ocean conditions (42). Linear extension is not necessarily limited by Ωsw, as Mollica et al. (31) found that linear extension remained constant when Porites corals were exposed to a wide range of Ωsw. Both our study and D’Olivo et al.’s work (22) observed growth responses coincident with calcifying fluid carbonate chemistry responses: The decline in calcifying fluid pH and [CO32−] in (22) occurred alongside a decline in coral calcification, whereas in our study, the increase in Ωcf occurred in corals that showed increased calcification over time. However, the coral growth response observed in (22) correlated with seawater carbonate chemistry (i.e., declining growth with declining Ωsw), while ours did not. This discrepancy raises questions regarding the extent to which seawater carbonate chemistry drives both coral calcifying fluid carbonate chemistry and growth responses. In other words, coral growth trends may reflect Ωcf, but Ωcf can be decoupled from trends in Ωsw.

Perhaps the only trend we observed that adhered to expectations with ocean acidification was a significant decrease in coral skeletal density over time in the Coral Sea (Fig. 2E; P < 0.05). Previous studies on the GBR found similar declines in skeletal density of Porites corals with increased stress from ocean acidification (31, 32). However, the Yonge Reef core showed no significant temporal trend in density (Fig. 2E; P > 0.05). Skeletal density did not show a significant dependence on Ωcf in either core (P > 0.05), which is unexpected as previous research posits that of all environmental drivers, coral skeletal density is most affected by fluctuations in Ωsw (15). The ways in which corals actively modify the skeletal surface area for aragonite precipitation may reconcile the decoupling of skeletal density and Ωcf. Precipitation of aragonite decreases the ΩAr of the solution, as precipitation of a mineral reduces the amount of ion constitutes remaining for additional precipitation. Consequently, high rates of aragonite precipitation indicate reduced Ωcf if the fluid is isolated or semi-isolated from the external environment (4, 23). Thus, if corals precipitate less skeletal aragonite per amount of calcifying fluid, then Ωcf will be relatively high, if the biological processes that elevate Ωcf remain constant. However, the decreased precipitation in this explanation is inconsistent with the increased overall calcification rate, so there would need to be other changes such as fewer or smaller pockets of calcifying fluid from which precipitation occurred, leading to lower skeletal density. Although this mechanism may dampen the apparent effects of ocean acidification on Ωcf, the secular increases in Ωcf may have a trade-off: a morphological shift toward more porous, and less dense, coral skeletons under ocean acidification (43), increasing the susceptibility of coral skeletons to bioerosion, dissolution, and storm damage. On the basis of our results, we might expect structurally weaker and more vulnerable coral reefs in the 21st century despite maintained regulation of Ωcf.

Another potential explanation for increasing Ωcf is that corals may be able to use supplemental energy reserves to maintain the regulation of Ωcf, and thus calcification, rate despite declines in Ωsw. As corals actively modify the pH of their internal calcifying fluid through the pumping of excess [H+] (protons) out of the fluid (7), the acid-base equilibrium between dissolved inorganic carbon species in the fluid shifts to favor [CO32−]. Greater [CO32−]cf increases Ωcf, countering reductions in ambient Ωsw, but with an energetic cost associated with proton pumping. Proton fluxes may play an important role in coral calcification by facilitating the removal of [H+] from the calcifying fluid to increase Ωcf (7) and by exporting excess [H+] into the diffusive boundary layer above the coral polyp as metabolic waste (10, 40). Given that [H+] is a byproduct of calcification, it must be removed at the same rate that CaCO3 is produced, which is accomplished by constraining internal pH conditions to establish diffusion gradients with external seawater (44). However, as the seawater [H+] concentration increases with ocean acidification, the diffusion of corals’ waste [H+] into bulk seawater is slowed, potentially inhibiting calcification (40). Regardless, overcoming the increasing energetic costs of calcification under ocean acidification may be possible with additional energy allocations from either the coral’s symbiotic algae or heterotrophic feeding (11, 45). These sources of energy can be enhanced under nutrient-replete conditions in surrounding seawater, allowing corals to regulate calcifying fluid chemistry despite acidified ocean conditions. While human impacts (especially agricultural runoff) have likely increased nutrient supply to the GBR in recent decades (46, 47), changes to nutrient sources on reefs in the Coral Sea are poorly resolved; inputs may include atmospheric deposition (48), upwelling, or bird populations (49).

Other processes that could contribute to our observed trend of increasing Ωcf include organic matter (OM) in the skeleton, changes in aragonite crystallography, and temperature. Skeletal OM provides a template for initiating calcification because it reduces the free energy required for CaCO3 nucleation (50, 51). One potential mechanism for corals to counteract ocean acidification is to produce more OM to induce aragonite nucleation. Recent laboratory experiments with synthetic aragonite showed that aspartic acid, the most abundant amino acid in coral skeletal OM (52), induces additional disorder into the aragonite crystal lattice, increasing Raman FWHM (53). However, the effect of aspartic acid on FWHM begins only near the highest concentrations found in corals (53), and the magnitude of FWHM change up to the highest potential calcifying fluid aspartic acid concentration is ~0.1 cm−1 or ~3 units of Ωcf. Thus, increasing amounts of skeletal OM over time could contribute—in part—to our apparent trends of increasing Ωcf but with an upper limit of only ~25% of that observed. Similarly, differences in aragonite crystals between granular nucleation structures, commonly called “centers of calcification” (54), and surrounding fibers could influence our observed Ωcf trends. Centers of calcification form under relatively high Ωcf and OM concentrations, with both factors contributing to overcoming the crystal nucleation barrier (55). However, given that the Ωcf difference between centers and fibers is only ~4 units (55), even a hypothetical, unrealistic shift from the skeleton composed of entirely fibers to entirely centers of calcification could only explain part of our observed Ωcf trends.

Lastly, increases in temperature in principle would increase Ωcf because of the effect of temperature on the apparent solubility product ksp. However, the ~1°C warming in the Coral Sea region since 1900 (Fig. 2C) would only increase Ωsw by ~0.03 units, negligible in relation to our observed Ωsw trends. Although temperature variability does not explain variation in Ωcf for either core (P > 0.05), the temporal trend in Ωcf mirrors the observed increase in sea surface temperature over time (Fig. 2C; P < 0.001). A more likely way temperature could influence Ωcf is indirectly by increasing coral metabolic rates, that is until a species-specific thermal optimum is reached (56, 57). Early 20th century increases in Porites calcification in the GBR have been shown to be coincident with increases in temperature (58), which may be attributed to an increase in the reaction rate of CaCO3 precipitation when sea surface temperatures were still below the thermal tolerance level but are less likely to have occurred in the 21st century with GBR seawater temperatures repeatedly reaching heat-stress conditions (59).

Overall, we demonstrate resistance in Porites Ωcf in the face of increasing pressure from ocean acidification. Increases in Ωcf on multidecadal-to-centennial timescales imply that long-term acclimation in corals has the potential to mitigate some adverse responses to environmental stressors. Our findings highlight the importance of directly quantifying Ωcf, as our results unexpectedly deviate from trends observed in prior work using coral skeletal isotope proxies. Increases in Ωcf, growth, and calcification do not necessarily represent all corals, or even Porites corals located in other regions, under acidified ocean conditions; however, they support the premise that mechanisms actively modifying calcifying fluid chemistry are complex and variable and our understanding of them is incomplete. The synergistic effects of geochemical and physiological mechanisms on calcifying fluid chemistry regulation are critical in assessing coral skeleton resilience to climate change amid historically unprecedented declines in ocean pH.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Coral sample collection

Porites coral skeletal cores were extracted off the northeast coast of Australia from two reef systems: Yonge Reef (14.57°S, 145.61°E) located in the northern GBR and Willis Middle Cay (16.21°S, 149.99°E) located in the northwestern Coral Sea (Fig. 1). Cores were collected using pneumatic drills with 5-cm-diameter bits in September (Yonge Reef) and December (Willis Middle Cay) 2017 at water depths of 9.5 and 11 m, respectively. Cores were rinsed with fresh water, sonicated, dried, computed tomography (CT) scanned [see (60), methods 2.C], and cut into slabs before analysis.

Raman spectroscopy

Raman measurements were collected on the Renishaw inVia Confocal Raman Microscope located at Hawai‘i Pacific University. Instrument settings for all measurements consisted of 785-nm diode laser in transmission configuration, 100% laser power, 1200 cm−1 diffraction grating, and a 5× microscope objective (NA 0.12). The transmission laser source uses forward-scattering detection rather than conventional backscattering detection, where the excitation laser is focused at the base of the sample and transmitted vertically through the substance, allowing for bulk sample analysis.

Abiogenic aragonite

Abiogenic aragonites are laboratory-generated aragonite powders precipitated from seawater with known ΩAr that exhibit broadly similar geochemical composition to coral aragonites (61, 62). Although previous studies have used abiogenic aragonite to create a calibration between the v1 peak FWHM and ΩAr (23, 61), our differences in the Raman instrument and method required us to refit the equation relating the v1 peak FWHM to ΩAr from (23). Therefore, a subset of seven abiogenic aragonite powders (61) was placed on silica microscope slides and analyzed on a 500 μm–by–500 μm grid with 10-s acquisitions at 50-μm step intervals, resulting in 121 spectra collected per sample.

Coral core analyses

Raman spectra for Porites coral cores were collected at 1-mm increments along three parallel transect lines 5 mm apart, accounting for spatial variability in Ωcf, and averaged at each respective increment in our final calculation. Each spectral measurement overlaps the previous measurement by 39.1% given that the transmission laser has a spot diameter of ~2 mm. Start and finish collection points were marked with a graphite pencil dot to ensure the precise alignment of sampling tracks.

We used the Porites international coral standard JCp-1 (63) as a consistency standard to correct coral core FWHM for instrumental drift. Nine JCp-1 spectra were collected at 50-μm intervals on a 100 μm–by–100 μm grid at the beginning and end of each collection day using the same instrument settings as core analyses.

Coral growth and calcification

Individual coral CT scans were evaluated using Horos software with settings consisting of mean intensity projection, linear table opacity, and a slab thickness of 3 mm to visualize annual growth and density bands. We correlated Raman measurements downcore to time by measuring the distance between high- and low-density bands approximating annual growth.

The coral calcification rate is calculated as the product of linear extension (i.e., vertical growth) and skeletal density (64). The annual extension rate is quantified by measurements acquired along axial corallite growth between annual density bands. The absolute skeletal density is estimated through the addition of individual density band anomalies to whole-core density, processed through CoralCT (65). Density band anomalies are calculated by obtaining relative density measurements at individual respective growth bands along the length of each CT scan conducted through CoralCT.

Modeling seawater parameters

To explore potential drivers of variability in Ωcf, we relate calculated Ωcf to sea surface temperature and the seawater carbonate system. Monthly temperature data were obtained from the Hadley Centre Global Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature dataset dating to 1871. We acquired single salinity values for the Coral Sea and Yonge Reef from the NOAA World Ocean Database (WOD18) using the longitude, latitude, and depth of core collection sites. Seawater total alkalinity was estimated from (66) using an average sea surface temperature value calculated for each location and the corresponding salinity value from WOD18.

Atmospheric CO2 was procured from the Scripps Institution of Oceanography Ice-Core Merged Products on the basis of ice core data before and including 1959 and observations from Mauna Loa Observatory from 1960 to 2017. The ocean partial pressure of CO2 (Pco2) was then calculated through a MATLAB translation of the x2pCO2 and vapress functions in seacarb (67) converting the mole fraction of CO2 (parts per million) to Pco2 using a single salinity value, sea surface temperature, atmospheric pressure of 1 atm, and atmospheric CO2.

We calculated the seawater carbonate system parameters for each location through the usage of the program CO2SYS (68), generally following the approach in (22) as there is a lack of long-term carbon chemistry data from individual sites in the GBR. pH was determined on the total hydrogen scale, where we use dissociation constants (69) refit by Dickson and Millero (70) for stoichiometric carbonic acid constants K1 and K2, Dickson (71) for the bisulfate dissociation constant KSO4, Perez and Fraga (72) for the hydrogen fluoride dissociation constant KHF, and Lee et al. (73) for the boric acid dissociation constant KB and an atmospheric pressure value of 1 atm. Our inputs consisted of total alkalinity [estimated from (66)], ocean Pco2 (calculated), temperature (time-varying), and salinity (constant).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were carried out in MATLAB R2022b. Linear regression models were created using the function fitlm to assess the response of Ωcf to predictor variables consisting of seawater ΩAr and sea surface temperature and the response of calcification, linear extension, and skeletal density to changes in Ωcf as a predictor variable. Residuals were evaluated for normality, heteroskedasticity, and autocorrelation through the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Engle test, and Ljung-Box Q-test. Given that residuals were shown to be highly autocorrelated, each model included an additional autocorrelated Ωcf parameter as a predictor variable to account for a lack of independence between individual data points (i.e., a lagged version of the Ωcf time series was used as a predictor of the Ωcf time series because this accounts for the variance explained by the previous data point, which is the basis of autocorrelation). We also recognize overlapping data points (i.e., Raman laser spots partially overlap from one sample to the next) as further reasoning to include an autocorrelation parameter in our linear regression model. In addition, we used a linear fit for Ωcf and Ωsw with one change point allowed, enabling the model to account for nonlinear responses in data.
