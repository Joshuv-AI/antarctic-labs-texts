Nucleation dynamics in amyloid-beta dimerization revealed by single-molecule fingerprinting
Authors: AKM Kafi, Mathias Bogetoft Danielsen, Shixi Song, Deepak Karna, Sajan Shakya, Jiahao Ji, Yitong Hao, Pelayo Alvarez Penanes, Frank Kjeldsen, Hanbin Mao, Chenguang Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe aggregation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptides (Aβ1-40 or Aβ1-42) is closely related to the pathology of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Soluble oligomers that appear during Aβ aggregation are primary neurotoxic species; however, their misfolding kinetics have yet to be determined. Here, we report a bottom-up construction of parallel and antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimers, the first oligomers formed during Aβ aggregation. We apply single-molecule mechanical unfolding in optical tweezers to investigate the dynamic structural evolution of these dimers at the single-amino-acid resolution. We observe three intermediates during the association and dissociation of individual Aβ1-40 dimers, with the diphenylalanine Aβ19-20 dimer having the highest formation probability. Our single-molecule fingerprinting method reveals that a known Aβ aggregation inhibitor, rosmarinic acid, can reduce Aβ1-40 dimerization by binding to the Aβ19-20 site. We anticipate that the molecular tool innovated in our study is extensible to investigating other amyloid aggregations responsible for a myriad of neurodegenerative diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) affects about 60 million patients world-wide.1 According to the “amyloid hypothesis,”2 it is amyloid-beta (Aβ) aggregation that causes the neurodegenerative effects in Alzheimer’s patients. In the diseased state, Aβ monomers misfold into sequential aggregation states, starting from soluble oligomers to protofibrils, fibrils, and plaques.3 Plaques were found to be deposited in the brains of Alzheimer’s patients. This abnormal accumulation of pathological proteins leads to the hypothesis that misfolded Aβ fibrils were the cause of AD.4 More recently, soluble Aβ oligomers, formed in the early Aβ aggregation process, are reported to be more toxic to neuronal cells.5,6 This discovery implicates a new type of protein target to develop lead compounds for the treatment of AD.

Two strategies exist to reduce the level of neurotoxic Aβ oligomers by disrupting the dynamic equilibrium among different aggregation states. One is to stabilize the Aβ monomeric state, preventing further Aβ oligomerization. The other is to disassemble the neurotoxic Aβ oligomers to the non-toxic Aβ monomers or to induce the oligomers to form less toxic Aβ protofibrils and fibrils. In both approaches, it is of critical importance to understand the dynamic Aβ oligomerization process. However, Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42, the two main species of Aβ proteins,7,8 are intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs).4 It is extremely difficult for conventional structural characterization tools such as NMR and X-ray to profile the full spectrum of Aβ1-40 or Aβ1-42 monomer conformations, let alone their oligomer states during rapid equilibrium of Aβ aggregation.

Both Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 are present in various amyloid aggregates, and they have similar aggregation mechanisms. Aβ1-40 was chosen for this study due to its higher solubility and lower aggregation propensity compared with Aβ1-42,9 which mitigates the synthesis challenge and facilitates the single-molecule analysis. It has been shown that free Aβ1-40 peptides first aggregate into a neurotoxic mixture of dimers, trimers, and tetramers, which then leads to the formation of higher-order Aβ1-40 oligomers.10 As the smallest Aβ1-40 oligomer, we hypothesize that the Aβ1-40 dimer can serve as a model target to illuminate the Aβ protein nucleation process. However, due to the rapid equilibrium among different Aβ oligomers, it is not possible to isolate Aβ dimers to reveal their properties. The only available high-resolution structures for the full-length Aβ dimers were obtained through in silico molecular dynamics (MD) simulations,11,12 which artificially focus on dimers made of two single-molecule Aβ peptide strands.

Single-molecule force spectroscopy (SMFS) performed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) has been reported to investigate early Aβ oligomerization and specific Aβ oligomers.13–19 However, due to the limited resolution of AFM force spectroscopy,20 the Aβ intermolecular interactions could be deconvoluted only at the level of protein domains such as hydrophobic/C-terminal regions in full-length Aβ peptides.13–19 Recently, our lab has pioneered a high-throughput optical-tweezers method to investigate intermolecular interactions between two molecules at the resolution that could resolve individual amino acid residues.21 Using a duplex DNA with its two strands serving as templates, we have successfully isolated and investigated a dimer made of two diphenylalanine strands (Aβ19-20 or FF).22 It has been found that FF can assemble into fibrils,23,24 leading to the hypothesis that FF may serve as a nucleation site to form Aβ peptide dimers, oligomers, and further aggregates. Due to the lack of structural characterization on IDP targets, this hypothesis is yet to be tested during the aggregation of full-length Aβ peptides (Aβ1-40 or Aβ1-42). Current data are contradictory, in that F19 increases, whereas F20 reduces, the aggregation kinetics.25 Moreover, in brain plaques of AD, both parallel and antiparallel Aβ1-40 or Aβ1-42 dimers are observed.26 However, it is not clear which orientation represents a thermodynamically more stable conformation.

Using the high-resolution optical-tweezers instrument, we provide high signal-to-noise data to show that two Aβ1-40 peptides register very well in a parallel fashion to form Aβ1-40 dimers, whereas the antiparallel dimer is less stable. We also confirm that FF serves as the nucleus to initiate the Aβ1-40 dimerization, which is followed by the dimer formation of I31-I31 close to the C terminus and subsequent dimerization of Y10-Y10 close to the N terminus. Such a fingerprinting strategy at single-amino-acid resolution allows us to reveal that rosmarinic acid (RA), a known Aβ fibril inhibitor,27 disrupts the Aβ1-40 dimer formation by binding to the FF site Aβ19-20. We anticipate that this force-based single-molecule fingerprinting approach will offer an unprecedented opportunity to isolate and investigate a particular oligomer of intrinsically disordered proteins/peptides that equilibrate in a mixture of oligomers during their aggregation processes. These processes are highly relevant in many neurodegenerative diseases; yet they have thus far evaded structural characterization tools due to their disordered structures and dynamic equilibrium nature.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Investigation of Aβ1-40 dimerization by mechanical unfolding at the single-molecule level

To achieve DNA-guided bottom-up self-assembly of specific Aβ oligomers, we chose the strain-promoted alkyne-azide cycloaddition (SPAAC) reaction28,29 to prepare desired Aβ1-40-DNA conjugates (Figures S1 and S2). To optimize the synthesis, the azide functional group was chosen to modify the Aβ1-40 peptide, while the cyclooctyne group was used to label the DNA sequences. Two types of modified Aβ1-40 peptides were synthesized via automated solid-phase peptide synthesis: one peptide sequence carried a 6-azidohexanoyl modification on the N terminus, while the other had an L-azidolysine residue on the C terminus (see “general procedure for Aβ1–40 peptide synthesis, purification, and analysis”). Such a design would enable us to construct both parallel and antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimers. The compositions and purities (>98%) of these peptides were confirmed by high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HR-MS) and reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC), respectively (see “general procedure for Aβ1–40 peptide synthesis, purification, and analysis”; Figures S5 and S6). Two NH2-modified DNA sequences (ON1 and ON2, see “general procedure for ON synthesis, purification, and analysis”; Table S1; Figure S9) were synthesized through automated solid-phase DNA synthesis, and the bicyclo[6.1.0]nonyne (BCN) functional group was introduced via NHS chemistry in the post-oligonucleotide (ON) synthesis (ON1-BCN and ON2-BCN; Table S2; see “BCN functionalization of amino-modified ONs”; Figure S1). The chemical compositions and purities (>98%) were characterized by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectroscopy (MALDI-MS) and ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography (IE-HPLC), respectively (see “BCN functionalization of amino-modified ONs”; Figures S12 and S13).

Next, the SPAAC reaction was carried out to covalently link the azido-modified Aβ1-40 peptides and the BCN-functionalized DNAs in a microwave-assisted condition (see “Conjugating ON-BCNs to azido-modified Aβ1–40 peptides”; Figure 1A), followed by RP-HPLC purification. The chemical compositions were confirmed by HR-MS, while the purities were checked by the denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) (A𝛃1-40-N-ON1,A𝛃1-40-N-ON2,A𝛃1-40-C-ON1, and A𝛃1-40-C-ON2, see “Conjugating ON-BCNs to azido-modified Aβ1–40 peptides”; Table S3; Figures S18–S20).

Each Aβ1-40 peptide-DNA conjugate contains a DNA domain capable of forming a 9-bp duplex DNA serving as a template to bring together the two peptide-ON conjugates (POCs) (see “synthesis of DNA constructs for single-molecule investigations” and “single-molecule force ramping experiments”; Figure S25”). Mediated by the hybridization of the two complementary DNA template strands, this design facilitates the exclusive formation of an Aβ1-40 dimer. The DNA section at the end of each POC was ligated to a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) handle (Figures 1A and 1B). In single-molecule mechanical unfolding experiments, the two dsDNA handles separately linked to two POCs were respectively attached to two optically trapped beads through digoxigenin/antibody and biotin/streptavidin affinity linkages (Figure 1B). The poly(dT)146 loop acts as a bridge between the two dsDNA handles. This bridge is crucial in maintaining the proximity of the two POCs once they are dissociated, facilitating rapid reassociation of the POC dimer complexes and increasing the characterization throughput of the dimerization process.

To evaluate the nucleation and stability of the Aβ1-40 dimer, we performed single-molecule mechanical folding and unfolding experiments in a microfluidic chamber, which houses the entire DNA-peptide assembly (DPA) as discussed above. To start the experiment, two beads were separately trapped by two laser foci. These two beads were then brought closer to form a tether that contained the DPA in between. The tether was stretched by moving one bead away from the other using a steerable mirror that guided one of the trapping lasers.30 As a result, the tension experienced by the DPA construct increased, which led to the dissociation of the DPA from the dimerized state to two separate single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and peptide conjugates.

Observation of intermediates during dissociation of Aβ1-40 dimers

During the force-ramping procedure, the force-loading rate was set to 5.5 pN/s to apply mechanical force to the DPA at 23°C in a 10 mM Tris buffer with 150 Mm K+ ions at pH 7.4. The force-extension (F-X) curves obtained during this protocol revealed the mechanical properties of associated Aβ1-40 dimers (Figures 1C and 1D). The observation of rupture events suggests that the applied force should be sufficient to induce dissociation in the DPA. In contrast, the presence of multiple rupture events in F-X curves suggests the formation of different intermediates (Figure 1C).

With this approach, we first performed mechanical unfolding of the Aβ1-40 dimer in which each Aβ1-40 was attached to the DNA via its N-terminal end (i.e., parallel arrangement, see Figure 2A Figure S25). Regions with abrupt changes in the extension of F-X curves indicated rupture events within the Aβ1-40 dimer. We found 40% F-X curves that show these rupture features. In comparison, in the absence of DNA templates, the formation percentage drastically dropped to <10%. Since rupture events reflect the association of the Aβ1-40 dimers, such a difference indicates the importance of the DNA templating effect on the Aβ1-40 dimer formation in the DPA. The templating effect of the 9-bp DNA duplex is likely caused by the synergistic stabilization between the DNA duplex and the Aβ1-40 dimer that are covalently linked. Similar cooperative stabilizing effects have also been reported between DNA scaffolds and peptide dimers/trimers in our preceding works.22,28,31–33 To measure the size of these rupture events, we transformed the F-X curves into change-in-contour-length (ΔL) vs. force graphs (ΔL vs. F),34 as shown in Figure 1D, right inset, and Figure 2B (for details please see “single-molecule force ramping experiments” and “Conversion of F-X curves into ΔL vs. F graphs”). Such a transformation removed the background of increasing force or extension in the F-X curves, making it straightforward to identify and measure the rupture events that correspond to different intermediate states. After completely unfolding each Aβ1-40 dimer, we chose a 60-s incubation period to allow reassociation of the Aβ1-40 dimers before the next round of force ramping procedure.

The ΔL vs. F plots in Figure 2B clearly show rupture events, with ΔL ranging from 60 nm (the totally folded population) to 0 nm (the totally unfolded species). The ΔL determined from the ΔL-F plot analysis matches remarkably well with those measured from individual F-X curves (Figure 2A), validating the accuracy of the ΔL-F method. After analyzing n=381 rupture events, we found that the dissociation of Aβ1-40 dimers was composed of a series of smaller steps (Figure 2C). The ΔL histogram revealed distinct populations corresponding to different intermediates in the dissociation process, suggesting that parallel Aβ1-40 dimers dissociate through multiple, sequential steps rather than a single cooperative event.

Fingerprinting the intermediates in Aβ1-40 dimers

To reveal possible structures of these intermediates, we measured ΔL values in each intermediate (Figure 2C), which showed values of 11, 20, 33, 42, and 51 nm. Calculations (see “calculations of expected ΔL” and “calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers”; Figures S26–S28) showed that the ΔL=60nm population is consistent with that expected for complete dissociation of the 9-bp DNA and the Aβ1-40 dimer. The 9-bp DNA served as a template to facilitate the Aβ1-40 dimer formation. Without this template, the Aβ1-40 dimerization has a much-reduced formation percentage (without and with the 9-bp DNA template: 10% vs. 40%). (The corresponding DPA without the 9-bp DNA template was assembled with A𝛃1-40-N-ON5 and A𝛃1-40-N-ON6; see Figure S22. These two conjugates were synthesized from ON5 and ON6 via ON5-BCN and ON6-BCN as the intermediates; see Figures S11, S16, and S17.) Once the 9-bp DNA was dissociated, it released an 18-nt DNA plus the synthetic linkers between the DNAs and the Aβ1-40 peptides, which would deduct a total of ~10 nm from the contour length (or ΔL). This was indeed observed in the ΔL population of ~51 nm. To pinpoint the location of the intermediates during the rest of the dissociation of the Aβ1-40 dimers, we converted ΔL populations in Figure 2C into the number of interacting amino acids in the Aβ1-40 peptides (see “calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers”).

To this end, we first considered ΔL=11nm as the population in which the two Aβ1-40 strands just touch each other via their terminal amino acids (this ΔL exists because the poly(dT)146 bridge is much longer than the combined contour length of the two extended DNA-linker-Aβ1-40 strands). Therefore, the total observed contour length change for the dissociation of the Aβ1-40 dimer is 40 nm, a value equivalent to the difference between the ΔL=51nm (“DNA + linker”) and the ΔL=11nm (“d”) populations in the histogram (Figure 2C). Next, we assumed that all 40 amino acids (aa) in each Aβ1-40 participate in the formation of a parallel dimer, leading to the release of 80 aa, which corresponds to 40 nm in ΔL. This produces an observed contour length of 0.5 nm/aa (i.e., 40 nm/80 aa), a value consistent with the literature.35 This value was then used to calculate the number of amino acids involved in the intermediates with ΔL of 42, 33, and 20 nm, respectively (Figures 2B and 2C, see “calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers” for details), which revealed that 62 aa are involved in the ΔL=42nm intermediate, 44 aa in the ΔL=33nm population, and 18 aa in the ΔL=20nm species. By considering full registration between two parallelly aligned Aβ1-40 strands, a scenario consistent with literature findings,36–38 the 42, 33, and 20 nm ΔL populations therefore correspond to the tyrosine-tyrosine (Y10-Y10), phenylalanine-phenylalanine (F19-F19), and isoleu-cine-isoleucine (I31-I31) interactions between two Aβ1-40 strands, respectively. To confirm the biological relevance, we repeated the mechanical unfolding experiments in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, and a mixture of 10 mM Na2HPO4 and 10 mM KH2PO4 [pH 7.4]). Similar results were obtained in terms of intermediate populations (data not shown).

To confirm this assignment, we conjugated each Aβ1-40 strand with a 13-mer ssDNA strand (ON3-BCN and ON4-BCN; Table S2; Figures S14 and S15; synthesized from ON3 and ON4; Table S1; Figures S1 and S10) instead of the 9-mer strands from the same N-terminal ends (A𝛃1-40-N-ON3 and A𝛃1-40-N-ON4; Figure 1A; Figure S21). The extra 4-bp DNA in each POC complex is expected to increase the contour length of the dsDNA by 3.6 nm (4 nt × 2 × 0.45 nm/nt39–42), while that of the Aβ1-40 peptide should remain the same. Using the procedures described in Figure 2, we obtained the ΔL vs. F diagram for this 13-mer-containing construct (Figure 3A) and compared it with the 9-mer construct obtained from Figure 2. Indeed, we observed that Aβ1-40 dimers with the 13-bp DNA template showed ΔL of 48 nm, about 3 nm smaller than that with the 9-bp DNA template (Figure 3B). When we interrogated the ΔL histogram in Figure 3B, we found that the changes in contour length of the full-length Aβ1-40 peptide and its intermediates remained unchanged. These results confirmed accurate intermediate assignments described above.

Another verification of the assignment came from RA, a ligand known to inhibit aggregation of Aβ peptides,27 including FF peptides Aβ19-20.23 Therefore, we expected that formation of the F19-F19 intermediate (ΔL=33nm) should decrease in the presence of RA, as illustrated in Figure 3C. After repeating the mechanical unfolding experiments on the parallel Aβ1-40 dimer linked to the 9-bp dsDNA via the N termini (see Figure S29), we indeed observed a significant suppression of the F19-F19 intermediate population (Figure 3D, bottom orange histogram) in the presence of 100μM RA. This finding further supports the correct assignment of the intermediates, especially the ΔL=33nm intermediate (i.e., F19-F19).

Since each intermediate is stabilized by specific amino acids between two Aβ1-40 strands, we postulated that corresponding intermediates stabilized by the same amino acids should exist if each Aβ1-40 strand switches its terminal to link to the DNA template. To test this hypothesis, we conjugated each 9-mer ssDNA to an Aβ1-40 strand from its C terminus instead of its N terminus (Figure S25). A𝛃1-40-C-ON1 and A𝛃1-40-C-ON2 were synthesized and used to assemble the C-terminal parallel Aβ1-40 dimer. After analyzing the rupture events of these dimers in the F-X curves and ΔL-F plots (Figure S30), we indeed observed three intermediates with their ΔL values matching those determined by the interacting amino acids found in the intermediates of the N-terminal parallel Aβ1-40 dimers (Figure 3D, top histogram; 41, 29, and 20 nm for the I31-I31, F20-F20, and Y10-Y10 intermediates, respectively; see detailed calculations in calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers). When we added 100μM RA ligand to the C-terminal parallel Aβ1-40 dimers, we again observed a drastic reduction of the F20-F20 intermediate (Figure 3D, top orange histograms), confirming our intermediate assignment.

Our previous experiment showed that two Aβ19-20 (FF) strands can form a parallel dimer with a formation percentage of 58%,22 which again supported the existence of the FF intermediate observed here. To further confirm the existence of the Y10-Y10 and I31-I31 intermediates, we replaced each Aβ1-40 peptide with either a G9Y10 (Aβ9-10) or an I31I32 (Aβ31-32) in the DPA (“single-molecule mechanical unfolding of dimers of dipeptides corresponding to specific Aβ1–40 intermediates”). Following the same method developed in our labs to compose the Aβ1-40-DNA conjugates,22 the two azido-modified dipeptides, II-N-azide and GY-N-azide, were chemically synthesized (see “general procedure for dipeptide synthesis, purification, and analysis”; Figures S7 and S8). Next, each dipeptide was conjugated to the two alkyne-modified DNA strands via the copper-catalyzed alkyne-azide cycloaddition (CuAAC) reaction to afford two pairs of dipeptide-DNA conjugates (II-N-ON1, II-N-ON2, and GY-NON1, GY-N-ON2; see “alkyne functionalization in post-ON synthesis” and “conjugating ON-alkynes to azido-modified II or azido-modified GY dipeptides”; Figures S3, S4, S23, and S24). Similar mechanical unfolding experiments showed that the parallel G9Y10 dimer exhibited a formation percentage of 46%, while the parallel I31-I32 dimer demonstrated 23%, both significantly higher than that of the control (18%), in which no peptide was present in the DPA (Figure S31). These findings strongly support the involvement of the Y10-Y10 and I31-I31 segments in the dimerization process, confirming their roles in the formation of the intermediates.

To determine whether FF association is also important in the antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimers, we prepared two antiparallel-oriented Aβ1-40 strands by conjugating them with the 9-mer ssDNA via the N and C termini, respectively (Figure S25). After analyzing ΔL histograms (Figure 3E) obtained from the rupture events recorded in F-X curves (Figure S32), we observed three unfolding intermediates (ΔL=20,32, and 40 nm). Calculation based on the contour length of 0.5 nm per amino acid (see “calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers” for the detailed calculation) revealed that the ΔL=32nm species is likely the FF intermediate in this antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimer. As expected, the addition of RA reduced the occurrence of this FF intermediate (Figure 3E, orange histogram). All these data indicate the critical importance of the FF intermediates in the parallel and antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimers. It is notable that parallel Aβ1-40 dimers are easier to form than antiparallel dimers (Figure 3F, percentage features of 40%:30%:28% for N-terminal parallel:C-terminal parallel:antiparallel Aβ1-40 dimers, respectively), which is consistent with the finding that parallelly arranged strands are more abundant in Aβ aggregates.36–38 The discrepancy between N-terminal parallel and C-terminal parallel may result from the inverted peptide sequence polarity. The N terminus and the C terminus of the Aβ1-40 peptide differ in chemical composition, which could form different non-covalent contacts (H-bonding/electrostatic/hydrophobic interactions, etc.) with the interdomain linkers and/or the DNA template. A similar difference was noticed in our prior study when the DNA-conjugation site was changed from the N terminus to the C terminus of a peptide coiled-coil model.33

Aβ19-20 dimer formation serves as a nucleus for Aβ1-40 dimerization

The observed importance of FF intermediates in structural formation is consistent with their potential nucleation role in the Aβ1-40 dimerization. To confirm this role, we first mechanically unfolded all parallel Aβ1-40 dimers (N terminus linked to DNA). We then relaxed the construct to 0 pN and varied the incubation time at 0, 15, 30, 45, or 60 s before performing the next round of force ramping experiments (Figure 4A). After converting F-X curves to the ΔL vs. F plots (Figure 4B), we identified and calculated the abundance of three intermediates (Y10-Y10, F19-F19, and I31-I31) formed during a specific incubation time (Figure 4C). No intermediate was observed at 0- or 15-s incubations, indicating their formation was longer than 15 s. After 30-s incubation, we started to observe the formation of intermediates, with F19-F19 demonstrating the highest probability, followed by the I31-I31 (medium) and the Y10-Y10 (smallest) intermediates. When incubation time became longer, the percentage formation of F19-F19 decreased most, followed by a slight decrease in the I31-I31 intermediate. In contrast, the Y10-Y10 immediate showed an overall increasing trend (Figure 4C). Although we cannot rule out the possibility that all three intermediates contribute to the nucleation step of Aβ1-40 dimerization in different probabilities, our results indicate that, most likely, F19-F19 serves as the nucleus formed within 30 s during the Aβ1-40 dimerization, followed by the I31-I31 formation close to the C terminus and finally by the Y10-Y10 association close to the N terminus.

If such a sequential dimerization process exists (F19-F19 → I31-I31 → Y10-Y10), then it is expected that the same dimerization order should prevail when the parallel dimerization occurs in the Aβ1-40 strands with their C termini connected to the 9-mer DNA (Figure 4D). When we investigated such Aβ1-40 dimers, we indeed observed the expected sequence: the F20-F20 association was followed by the I31-I31 and finally by the Y10-Y10 association in order (Figure 4E). To the best of our knowledge, such a detailed folding order has never been observed in the Aβ1-40 dimerization process by existing methods. The fact that hierarchical folding starts with the nucleation of the FF dimers confirms that FF can be used as a mini-amyloid model to identify small-molecule leads to interfere with physiologically important aggregation of Aβ peptides.22,23

While the single-molecule laser-tweezers setup described here does not provide full degrees of freedom43 for the Aβ1-40 peptide and may not fully replicate the complex extracellular environment where the native Aβ1-40 dimers are formed, it provides valuable insights into the fundamental mechanism by which amyloid peptides form amyloid aggregates from a much simplified perspective of well-registered Aβ1-40 dimers. This simplified system offers a controlled way to isolate Aβ dimers from which the stability of various intermediates and their transition dynamics can be revealed for the first time. Although it does not capture all the complexities of peptide behaviors in a more physiologically relevant condition, it provides a unique and simplified perspective to elucidate the very first step, the nucleation, during the amyloid aggregation process.

By employing high-resolution single-molecule mechanical unfolding using optical tweezers, we investigated the dimerization of Aβ1-40 peptides, the first step during the Aβ aggregation leading to AD. Although the degree of freedom of the Aβ1-40 peptide may compromise due to its conjugation with DNA, the templating DNA employed in the DPA construct ensures exclusive formation of Aβ1-40 dimers in a well-registered manner, surpassing conventional ensemble methods in specificity and sensitivity to precisely characterize the structure and property of a particular oligomer state during amyloid aggregations. Through our fingerprinting method, we identified three intermediates during Aβ1-40 dimerization, among which Aβ19-20 (FF) is the most abundant species, serving as a nucleus for the Aβ1-40 dimerization. The innovative single-molecule fingerprinting provides insights underscoring the importance of specific amino acid interactions in the neurotoxic amyloid aggregation processes. The formation order of different intermediates in the Aβ1-40 dimer provides guidelines for designing small molecules that can interfere with Aβ aggregation by binding to desired intermediates. This opens a door to mitigating the neurotoxic effects of Aβ oligomers by disrupting their formation. Our platform and findings described here not only shed light on elucidating the Aβ aggregation related to AD but also hold promise to be extended to investigate inherently disordered amyloid proteins responsible for a myriad of neurodegenerative disorders.

METHODS

General experimental methods

All basic chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Acetonitrile, DCI activator (0.25 M, dissolved in acetonitrile), and N,N-dimethyl-formamide (DMF) were dried over activated molecular sieves (3 Å, 8–12 mesh), and their water content was measured using a Karl Fischer titrator (less than 15 ppm). The 5′-amino-modifier C6, 3′-PT-amino-modifier C6 CPG, Click-easy BCN N-hydroxysuccinimide ester I, and other reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, GE Healthcare, Glen Research, Biosearch Technologies, or Link Technologies. For all experiments, the concentrations of ONs, ON-BCNs, and POCs were determined using the theoretical values of ε260nm: 3.725 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON1/ON1-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON1/A𝛃1-40-C-ON1/II-N-ON1/GY-N-ON1, 3.476 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON2/ON2-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON2/A𝛃1-40-C-ON2/II-N-ON2/GY-N-ON2, 3.859 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON3/ON3-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON3, 4.086 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON4/ON4-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON4, 2.698 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON5/ON5-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON5, and 2.43 × 105 M−1 cm−1 for ON6/ON6-BCN/A𝛃1-40-N-ON6. For the preparation of DNA constructs in SMFS, DNA ONs were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA). Enzymes were purchased from New England Biolabs (NEB, Ipswich, MA). General procedure for Aβ1-40 peptide synthesis, purification, and analysis

The two azido-modified Aβ1-40 peptides (A𝛃1-40-N-azide and A𝛃1-40-C-azide, Figure 1) were assembled using fully automated, microwave-assisted Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis procedures on a CEM Liberty Blue peptide synthesizer. The synthesis was carried out on a TG R Wang (90μm) 0.19 mmol/g resin (lris Biotech) at a 0.1 mmol scale. Monomer couplings were achieved through alternating cycles of amino acid couplings and Fmoc deprotections, using N, N′-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC; 1 M in DMF) and ethyl (2E)-2-cyano-2-hydroxyiminoacetate (Oxyma, 1 M in DMF) as the activator and activator base solutions and 20% piperidine in DMF for Fmoc deprotection. Double couplings were applied for each amino acid incorporation beyond the 20 aa length of the Aβ1-40 peptide synthesis, as the standard single coupling procedure caused the major product to be deletion sequences. After synthesis, the peptides were washed with DMF (four times) and DCM (five times) and then cleaved from the resin using a solution of 95% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), 2.5% Milli-Q water, and 2.5% triisopropylsilane (TIPS) for 1.5 h. The cleavage solution was purged with argon before being added to the resin. The TFA was reduced under nitrogen flow before precipitating the A𝛃1-40-N-azide and A𝛃1-40-C-azide modified peptides in cold diethyl ether (−20°C). The precipitated peptides were dissolved in a deaerated solution of 15% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 20% acetonitrile, and 65% Milli-Q water and then subjected to sonication for 5 min prior to the RP-HPLC purification. The sonication serves two purposes: reducing the possible oligomerization before sample injection and removing any oxygen that could oxidize the methionine in the Aβ1-40 sequence. When the crude Aβ1-40 peptide was prepared at a 2 mg/mL concentration, it balanced between a decent purification scale and the high purity of the desirable azido-modified Aβ1-40 peptide product. The oligomerization was also suppressed at a relatively low level. RP-HPLC purification was carried out on a Waters LC Prep 150 system connected through a Waters 2545 Quaternary Gradient Module, a Waters 2489 UV-visible detector, and a Waters 2707 Autosampler that had been equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 (21.2×150mm5μm, Agilent) semi-prep column heated to 60°C. Elution was performed starting with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (80/20, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 60% B buffer over 40 min at a flow rate of 5.0 mL/min (A, 99.9% Milli-Q water and 0.1% TFA; B, 89.95% acetonitrile, 9.95% Milli-Q water, and 0.1% TFA). RP-HPLC analysis (Figures S5 and S6) was carried out on a Waters LC Prep 150 System connected through a Waters 2545 Quaternary Gradient Module, a Waters 2489 UV-visible detector, and a Waters 2707 Autosampler that had been equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column 4.6×150mm,5μm, Agilent) heated to 60°C. Elution was performed starting with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (80/20, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 60% B buffer over 40 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min (A, 10 mM ammonium acetate in Milli-Q water [pH 9.0]; B, 10 mM ammonium acetate in 80% MeCN and 20% Milli-Q water). High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) was performed to confirm their chemical compositions (Figures S5 and S6). An HPLC separation was coupled to the high-resolution ESI-MS. HPLC separation was performed using an Ultimate 3000 LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germering, Germany). A self-packed pre-column (XBridge Premier Peptide BEH C18, 2.5μm, 130 Å, 150-μm inner diameter, 3-cm length; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) and a self-packed analytical column (XBridge Premier Peptide BEH C18, 2.5μm, 130 Å, 75-μm inner diameter, 5-cm length; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) were used. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water, and mobile phase B consisted of 95% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water. Samples were dissolved in mobile phase A prior to injection into the LC system. The chromatographic gradient after injection into the column was as follows: from 2% B to 40% B in 25 min, from 40% B to 70% B in 5 min, from 70% B to 95% B in 0.5 min, and 3 min of wash at 95% B before returning to 2% B in 0.5 min for column equilibration for 6 min. A blank (mobile phase A) was injected after each sample to ensure that the column was clean of the previous sample before injecting a new one. High-resolution ESI-MS of the samples was performed using an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Full MS scans were acquired with 240,000 resolution. Ion transfer tube temperature was set to 300°C. The spray voltage was tuned daily to ensure maximum spray stability, and the value was 2.3 kV(±0.1 kV) in all cases.

General procedure for dipeptide synthesis, purification, and analysis

The two azido-modified II and GY dipeptides (II-N-azide and GY-N-azide) were synthesized following a protocol similar to that of azido-modified Aβ1-40 peptides. The synthesis was carried out on a Tenta Gel XV RAM 0.27 mmol/g resin (RAPP Polymere GmbH) at a 0.1 mmol scale. The monomer couplings were carried out with a standard single coupling procedure by using the same solvents for coupling and Fmoc deprotections. Cleavage of the two dipeptides proceeded as described above (cleavage of azido-modified Aβ1-40 peptides). After synthesis, the two dipeptides were washed with DMF (four times) and DCM (five times) and then cleaved from the resin using a solution of 95% TFA, 2.5% Milli-Q water, and 2.5% TIPS for 1.5 h. The cleavage solution was purged with argon before being added to the resin. The TFA was reduced under nitrogen flow before precipitating the II-N-azide and GY-N-azide modified peptides in cold diethyl ether (−20°C). The crude II-N-azide dipeptide was dissolved in a deaerated solution of 20% acetonitrile and 80% A buffer. The crude GY-N-azide dipeptide was dissolved in a deaerated solution of A buffer. Then they were subjected to sonication for 5 min and heated to 60°C for 15 min prior to the RP-HPLC purification. The aim of sonication and heating was to dissolve the dipeptide crudes completely and to diminish the possible oligomerization before sample injection. RP-HPLC purification was carried on a Waters LC Prep 150 system connected through a Waters 2545 Quaternary Gradient Module, a Waters 2489 UV-visible detector, and a Waters 2707 Autosampler that had been equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 (21.2×150mm,5μm, Agilent) semi-prep column heated to 60°C. Elution was performed starting with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (80/20, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 100% B buffer over 32 min at a flow rate of 5.0 mL/min for II-N-azide dipeptide purification. For GY-N-azide dipeptide purification, the elution process was performed beginning with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (95/5, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 80% B buffer over a period of 30 min at a flow rate of 5.0 mL/min (A, 99.9% Milli-Q water and 0.1% TFA; B, 89.95% acetonitrile, 9.95% Milli-Q water, and 0.1% TFA). RP-HPLC analysis (Figures S7 and S8) was carried out on a Waters LC Prep 150 System connected through a Waters 2545 Quaternary Gradient Module, a Waters 2489 UV-visible detector, and a Waters 2707 Autosampler that had been equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6×150mm,5μm, Agilent) heated to 60°C. Elution was performed starting with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (60/40, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 100% B buffer over 24 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min for II-N-azide dipeptide analysis (A, 89.95% Milli-Q water, 9.95% acetonitrile, and 0.1% TFA; B, 89.95% acetonitrile, 9.95% Milli-Q water, and 0.1% TFA). For GY-N-azide dipeptide analysis, the elution process was performed beginning with an isocratic hold of A/B buffer (95/5, v/v) for 3 min followed by a linear gradient to 80% B buffer over 30 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min (A, 99.9% Milli-Q water and 0.1% TFA; B, 89.95% acetonitrile, 9.95% Milli-Q water, and 0.1% TFA). High-resolution ESI-MS in the positive mode was carried out to confirm their chemical compositions (see “general procedure for Aβ1–40 peptide synthesis, purification, and analysis” for detailed protocols; Figures S7 and S8). An HPLC separation was coupled to the high-resolution ESI-MS.

General procedure for ON synthesis, purification, and analysis

ON synthesis was performed on an ÄKTA Oligopilot Plus 10 system (CPG support, 3′-dG-pre-loaded) or a PerSeptive Biosystems Expedite 8909 automated DNA/RNA synthesizer (3′-PT-amino-modifier C6 CPG) at a 1.0-μmol scale using the phosphoramidite method, following the manufacturer’s standard protocols. The coupling time for standard DNA monomers was 720 s, with stepwise coupling efficiencies exceeding 98.0%, determined by measuring the absorbance of the trityl cation at 500 nm. For ON1, ON3, and ON5 (Table S1), the primary amine modification was introduced at the 3′ end via a hexamethylene linker by starting the synthesis with a special solid support (3′-PT-amino-modifier C6 CPG, Glen Research). For ON2, ON4, and ON6 (Table S1), the 5′-amino-modifier C6 (Glen Research) was used to introduce the primary amine function at the 5′ end via a hexamethylene linker. Standard deprotection and purification were conducted for all synthesized ONs. Cleavage from the solid support and removal of nucleobase protecting groups was accomplished using 28% aqueous ammonia at 55°C for 16 h. The resulting ONs were purified as MMTr-ONs or DMTr-ONs by RP-HPLC using a Waters System 600 equipped with a Waters XBridge BEH C18 column (5μm/135Å,100×19mm) heated to 50°C. Elution was performed starting with a 5-min isocratic hold of A buffer followed by a linear gradient to 70% B buffer over 16.5 min at a flow rate of 5.0 mL/min. The A buffer consisted of 0.05 M triethylammonium acetate in Milli-Q water (pH 7.4), and B buffer consisted of 25% A buffer and 75% acetonitrile. After all solvents were removed under nitrogen flow, ONs were detritylated using 100μL of an 80% aqueous acetic acid solution for 20 min. They were then desalted with an aqueous solution of 3 M sodium acetate (15μL) and 5 M sodium perchlorate (15μL) followed by cold ethanol (1 mL; 99% w/w; −20°C). The resulting suspension was stored at −20°C for 1 h. After centrifugation (16,100×g,5min,4∘C), the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with cold ethanol (2 × 1 mL; 99% w/w; −20°C), dried for 30 min under nitrogen flow, and dissolved in 1.0 mL of Milli-Q water. Further purification was performed for ONs with purity below 90% to remove shoulder peaks. These ONs were purified by IE-HPLC using the DIONEX Ultimate 3000 system equipped with a DNAPac PA100 semi-preparative column (13μm,250×9mm) heated to 60°C. Elution was performed with a 10% buffer B isocratic hold, starting with a 2-min hold of 2% buffer A in Milli-Q water followed by a linear gradient to 25% buffer A over 20 min at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. Buffer A was 1.0 M sodium perchlorate, and buffer B was 0.25 M Tris-Cl (pH 8.0). After IE-HPLC purification, the appropriate fractions were combined, and all solvents were removed under nitrogen flow. The residues were dissolved in Milli-Q water (150μL) and precipitated in cold ethanol (1.5 mL; 99% w/w; −20°C). The resulting suspension was stored at −20°C for 1 h. After centrifugation 16,100×g,5min,4∘C, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with cold ethanol (2 ×1 mL; 99% w/w; −20°C), dried for 30 min under nitrogen flow, and dissolved in 1.0 mL of Milli-Q water. Mass spectra of the ONs were recorded on a Bruker Daltonics Microflex LT MALDI-TOF MS instrument in negative mode (representative MS are shown in Figures S9–S11). Analytical IE-HPLC traces were recorded on a Merck-Hitachi Lachrom system equipped with a DNAPac PA100 analytical column (13μm,250×4mm) heated to 60°C. Elution was performed with a 10% buffer B isocratic hold, starting with a 2-min hold using 2% buffer A in Milli-Q water followed by a linear gradient to 30% buffer A over 23 min at a flow rate of 1.1 mL/min (buffer A, 1.0 M sodium perchlorate; buffer B, 0.25 M Tris-Cl [pH 8.0]) (representative IE-HPLC traces are shown in Figures S9–S11). The concentrations of purified ONs were determined by UV absorption measurements at 260 nm.

Synthesis of DNA constructs for single-molecule investigations

An ON fragment containing poly(deoxythymidine)146 (poly(dT)146) was first annealed with two POCs at its 5′ and 3′ flanking regions, respectively. The resulting construct was phosphorylated to add a phosphate group to its 5′ end. This construct was then ligated with two non-palindromic dsDNA handles 1,558 and 2,391 bp in length. The 1,558-bp DNA handle was prepared through PCR using a pBR322 plasmid (NEB) as a template and two specific primers, 5′-biotin-TEG-GCA TTA GGA AGC CCA GTA GG and 5′-AAA CCA TAG AGG CTA CAC TAG AAG GAC AGT ATT TG. The purified PCR product was digested using Bsal-HFv2 enzyme (NEB) to generate a cohesive end compatible with the annealed fragment discussed above. The other dsDNA handle, 2,391 bp, was synthesized from PCR using a λ-DNA as a template and two specific primers, 5′-AAA AAA AAG AGC TCC TGA CGC TGG CAT TCG CAT CAA AG and 5’-AAA AAA AAG GTC TCG CCT GGT TGC GAG GCT TTG TGC TTC TC, followed by digestion using Sacl restriction enzyme (NEB), which was subsequently labeled with digoxigenin-dUTP at the 3′ Sacl over-hang by terminal transferase (NEB) for 8 h at 37°C. The dsDNA handle was then digested again using Bsal-HFv2 to create a cohesive end compatible with the poly(dT)146-containing construct. Finally, the poly(dT)146-containing construct and the two DNA handles with non-palindromic overhangs were ligated together using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) at 16°C for 16 h. The resulting ligated DNA construct was stored at −20°C.

Single-molecule force ramping experiments

Single-molecule experiments were performed according to prior procedures using a home-built dual-beam laser-tweezers instrument30 at 23°C in 10 mM Tris buffer containing 150 mM KCl (pH 7.4). Briefly, the DNA construct was first incubated with digoxigenin-coated polystyrene beads (2.32μm in diameter, Spherotech, Lake Forest, IL), one of which was then captured using one of the two laser traps. One of the streptavidin-coated beads (1.76μm in diameter, Spherotech, Lake Forest, IL) was also captured in a separate laser trap. The DNA construct was tethered between the two beads using biotin/streptavidin and digoxigenin-antibody/digoxigenin affinity linkages. One of the 1,064 nm laser foci was fixed, while the other was moveable by a steerable mirror. When the two trapped beads were moved apart by steering the moveable laser focus, the DNA tether was stretched, generating tension on the DPA. The F-X curves were recorded using the LabView program (National Instruments, Austin, TX) at 1 kHz with a loading rate of 5.5 pN/s (in the 10–30 pN range). The ~70-pN plateau observed on the F-X curve confirmed that the tethered molecule was single, as it was due to the force-induced melting of individual duplex DNA handles.45 Three X-t and F-t traces, corresponding to the F-X curves of the molecules shown in Figures 2A and 2B, were included in Figure S33. To filter the collected data in F-X curves, the Savitzky-Golay function was used at a time constant of 10 ms in the MATLAB program (The MathWorks, Natick, MA). The mechanical stability and the percentage formation of various Aβ1-40 dimers were determined from the rupture events in the F-X curves, which indicate the dissociation of previously formed Aβ1-40 dimers.

Single-molecule mechanical unfolding of dimers of dipeptides corresponding to specific Aβ1-40 intermediates

Experimental design

Based on the calculations in “calculations of expected ΔL” and “calculation of folding intermediates in Aβ1–40 dimers,” three locations (I31, F19-F20, and Y10) were identified as possible intermediates (Figures 2C and 3D). To confirm these possible intermediates, we selected three dipeptides, Aβ31-32 (or II), Aβ19-20 (or FF), and Aβ9-10 (or GY), and a control sample, which contained only duplex DNA with two respective linkers (see Figure 1), for further investigations.

Sample synthesis

The syntheses of the II and GY dipeptide samples were described in “general procedure for dipeptide synthesis, purification, and analysis,” “alkyne functionalization in post-ON synthesis,” and “conjugating ON-alkynes to azido-modified II or azido-modified GY dipeptides.” Details on the FF sample were described in our previous work.22

Single-molecule force-jump experiments

We followed the general procedure of the optical-tweezers experiment described in “single-molecule force ramping experiments” and Figure S25.

During the force-jump experiments,49 the tethered DNA-peptide sample was initially stretched from 0 to 15 pN at a loading rate of 15 pN/s. After a rupture event as shown in Figure S31 (left), which indicated the dissociation of the DNA-peptide dimer assembly, the force was rapidly reduced from 15 to 0.5 pN within 10 ms, allowing the DNA-peptide dimer to re-form. This was followed by a jump-up procedure to 1 pN force, from which the force was increased to the maximum force of 15 pN at the loading rate of 15 pN/s. Such an F-X procedure was used to probe whether the DNA-peptide dimer had been formed during the prior incubation at 0.5 pN. If a rupture feature was observed in such an F-X curve (Figure S31, left), it indicated the prior formation of a DNA-peptide dimer. The percentage of curves exhibiting this feature represents the percentage formation of the dimer (marked in red numbers on the left side of Figure S31). The rupture force histograms (the middle of Figure S31) provided information on the mechanical stability of the folded DNA-peptide dimer, while the change-in-con-tour-length (ΔL) histograms (right of Figure S31) determined the size of the rupture features.

The expected ΔL for the DNA-dipeptide sample was 36.7 nm, which was calculated by Equation 2 in “calculations of expected ΔL”:ΔL=L-x, where L is the contour length of the poly(dT)90 and x is the end-to-end distance of the structure.
