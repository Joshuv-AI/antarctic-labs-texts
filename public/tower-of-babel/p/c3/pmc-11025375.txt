British Columbia beekeeping revenues and costs: survey data and profit modeling
Authors: Miriam Bixby, Morgan Cunningham, Leonard Foster, Heather Higo, Nuria Morfin, Juliana Rangel
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBritish Columbia beekeepers, like many beekeepers around the world, are currently facing declines in honey bee health and high overwinter colony losses. To better understand the economics and the cycle of yearly colony loss and replacement of this critical agricultural industry, we collected and analyzed survey data on beekeeping costs and returns. Forty British Columbia beekeepers provided details about revenue sources, variable costs, capital costs, and investments. Ten surveyed beekeepers managed between 1 and 9 colonies, 10 managed between 10 and 39 colonies, 9 managed between 40 and 100 colonies, 5 managed between 101 and 299 colonies, 3 managed between 300 and 699 colonies, and 3 managed 700 colonies or more. The data was used to calculate beekeeping profit and to parameterize a model that explores the economic impact of colony loss rates and replacement strategies. Survey results show that when the data is aggregated, revenues exceed costs for beekeeping operations in British Columbia with a per colony profit of $56.92 or $0.87 per pound of honey produced. Surveyed operations with fewer than 100 colonies have negative profits, while operations with 100–299 colonies have positive profits. Surveyed operations in the Cariboo, North Coast, and Okanagan regions have the highest profits while surveyed operations in the Peace region have the lowest profits. Profit modeling shows that replacing losses with packages generates lower profit than replacing losses with split colonies. Our modeling shows that operations that diversify their revenue to include bee sales and commercial pollination accrue higher profits and can withstand higher winter loss rates.Keywords: apiculture, crop pollination, pollination, social insect, agriculture

Introduction

In 2022, there were more than 760,000 honey bee colonies managed by nearly 14,000 beekeepers across Canada (SC 2022). These colonies produced over 74 million pounds of honey valued at CAD $253 million dollars (AAFC 2020, SC 2022). Canadian honey bee colonies also pollinate many lucrative crops, including hybrid canola and blueberries, with their aggregate pollination services valued at CAD $4.5–$6 billion dollars annually (AAFC 2020, SC 2021). As the Canadian province with the most temperate weather, British Columbia is an active beekeeping region, contributing honey, live bee products, and pollination services. There were 4,300 beekeepers in British Columbia in 2022 representing 31% of all Canadian beekeepers (SC 2022). British Columbia beekeepers managed 63,693 colonies in 2022, producing 3.8 million pounds of honey worth over CAD $17 million dollars (SC 2022). British Columbia beekeepers also produced nearly 50,000 queens in 2022 (BC 2022), representing approximately 50% of all queens produced in Canada (Bixby et al. 2020) and produced over 55,000 nucleus colonies (nucs) (BC 2022). British Columbia beekeepers also engage in commercial pollination of several lucrative crops including blueberries, cherries, apples, cucurbits, and hybrid canola seed among others (SC 2021, 2022). Over the past few decades, however, beekeepers in Canada and worldwide have experienced increasing colony losses due to a variety of causes including the parasitic mite Varroa destructor, queen health issues, as well as other biological and environmental factors (CAPA 2010, Genersch et al. 2010, Guzman-Novoa et al. 2010, Le Conte et al. 2010, Spleen et al. 2013, vanEnglesdorp 2013, Liu et al. 2016, Bixby et al. 2019, Brown and Robertson 2019). In 2022, Canadian beekeepers reported an average winter mortality of 45%, with provincial losses ranging from 15.3% to 57.2% (CAPA 2022). British Columbia beekeepers reported losing just under one third of all honey bee colonies over the 2022 winter (CAPA 2022). Several surveys looking at honey bee colony losses in Canada have focused on loss rates and the multifaceted causes of loss (Currie et al. 2010, van der Zee et al. 2012, Emunu 2020, CAPA 2022). The economic implication of these high winter losses to Canadian beekeepers has largely been implied and under investigated. In 2016, a comprehensive survey of Alberta beekeepers lead to detailed reports of costs and returns of beekeeping in that province (Laate 2017). The Alberta report gave beekeepers and apiculture professionals empirical data on the economics of the apiculture sector in that region. Despite the significant role that the apicultural sector of British Columbia plays in its economy and Canadian agriculture as a whole and the continually high colony winter mortality rates faced by British Columbia beekeepers, there has not yet been a detailed examination of the profitability of beekeeping in the province. This paper offers the first comprehensive analysis of beekeeping economics in British Columbia. During the fall of 2022, the British Columbia Honey Producers Association’s B.C. Technology Transfer Program (B.C.-TTP) received funding to conduct a beekeeping sector analysis of the province’s honey bee industry. The objective of this beekeeping industry study is to provide empirical data and modeling to describe the current economic conditions within apiculture in British Columbia and to provide some analysis and insight about the prospects for British Columbia beekeeping in the future.

Materials and Methods

Between November 2022 and January 2023, an online survey asking questions about revenue and costs associated with beekeeping was disseminated across British Columbia through local beekeeping associations, regional bee clubs, and apicultural specialists. The survey asked beekeepers for detailed revenue and cost information directly related to their beekeeping operations. The survey was completed by 40 beekeepers from across British Columbia, representing 6 regions of the province: Cariboo, Kootenays, Peace, Northwest Coast, Okanagan, and Southwest Coast-Islands. Several beekeepers were contacted individually to complete missing data and clarify any responses. Once the data collection was complete, all identifying information from respondents was removed to insure confidentiality. The data is subset by operation size, beekeeping activity, and region. Operation size refers to the number of colonies within the operation and is subset into 6 categories: 1–9 colonies, 10–39 colonies, 40–100 colonies, 101–299 colonies, 300–699 colonies, and 700+ colonies. Beekeeping activity is subset into 3 categories: honey production only, honey production and bee production (queens and/or nucs), and honey production, bee production, and commercial pollination. Region is subset into 6 categories: Cariboo, Kootenay, Peace, North Coast, Okanagan, and South Coast-Islands.

Colony winter loss profit model.

The survey data was used to inform and parameterize a profit model. To develop the model, assumptions of linear regression in the data were satisfied as follows: observations were collected independently; linear relationships, normality, and homoscedasticity were examined by plotting; heteroscedasticity was corrected via weighted regression where observations were weighted by residual error; multicollinearity was determined by variance inflation factor (VIF) and predictors exhibiting high VIF were removed from models; and outliers were examined and removed on a case-by-case basis. In this model, we describe a representative beekeeper in British Columbia who runs their operation given a set of revenue and cost variables informed by the survey data and winter colony loss rates. The revenue and cost data from the survey include the costs of current colony losses and colony replacement. Therefore, an assumption of the model is that a beekeeper’s revenues and costs reflect an average loss rate within the province of British Columbia. As a result, the model’s initial parameters are based on a colony winter mortality rate of 25.7%, which is the average colony loss rate in British Columbia over the period of 2007–2022 (CAPA 2022). The model also explores profitability at colony loss rates above 25.7% to analyze the economic impact of increasing colony health issues and mortality within Canadian beekeeping (CAPA 2010, Genersch et al. 2010, Guzman-Novoa et al. 2010, Le Conte et al. 2010, Spleen et al. 2013, vanEnglesdorp 2013, Liu et al. 2016, Bixby et al. 2019, Brown and Robertson 2019). To calculate beekeeping profit at losses greater than 25.7%, the model adds the cost of replacement for those additional losses, a cost that depends on the colony replacement method chosen by the beekeeper. The representative beekeeper has the option to replace a colony loss by splitting an existing colony into 2 colonies (splits) from their own operation or purchasing a package of bees (1–2 kg of bees including a queen that is shipped in a cardboard or wooden container and installed in a hive). For operations that produce queens, an in-house queen is added to a split colony and for those operations that do not produce queens, a queen is purchased at the market price and added to the split. For the representative beekeeper, any replacement splits that are used to re-build after winter losses and any colonies rented out for commercial pollination are assumed to build up in time to produce an average honey crop that season (the profit results are likely upper bound estimates as relevant literature indicates that in some cases, pollinating colonies produce 87% of the honey that a successfully overwintered colony would produce [Nelson and Jay 1982, Pernal et al. 2008], and both parent and offspring split colonies may produce 75% of the amount of honey that the dedicated honey producing colonies would produce based on evidence that there is a later build-up resulting in a delayed honey crop [Farrar 1937, Szabo and Lefkovitch 1988, Pernal et al. 2008, Gabka 2014]). The cost of making a replacement split is the labor cost only as most beekeepers use existing beekeeping materials and the cost to purchase a package is the current market price.

Results (All Results Are Reported in Canadian Dollars)

Survey Data: Cost, Revenue, Profit

Forty beekeepers responded to the online survey and collectively manage over 7,300 colonies representing nearly 12% of the total number of colonies in British Columbia in 2022. The surveyed beekeepers manage an average of 185 colonies per operation compared to 15 colonies for British Columbia beekeepers on average, indicating that the survey sample was likely weighted towards larger commercial beekeepers compared to hobbyist/backyard beekeepers who tend to manage fewer colonies (BC 2022). The sample of 40 beekeepers produces a total of over 480,000 pounds of honey worth over $2.5 million dollars, representing 13% of total provincial honey yield and 23% of honey value in 2022. The average honey value for the surveyed beekeepers is $5.37/pound compared to $2.92/pound for the province. The surveyed beekeepers produce almost 6,000 queens and over 4,000 nucs, representing 12% and 7% of British Columbia queen and nuc production respectively (BC 2022, SC 2022). All surveyed beekeepers produced honey for sale while just less than one quarter rented colonies for commercial pollination, about one third produced queens for sale, and half produced nucs for sale (Table 1). The number of beekeepers, colonies, average honey price, and yield vary by operation size (Table 2), beekeeping activity (Table 3), and region (Table 4). The average revenue when all survey respondents are included is $108,207 per beekeeper (Table 5).

Total revenue–variable costs model

Bee production (β1 = 1.03, P ≤ 0.001), colony feed (β2 = 4.23, P ≤ 0.001), colony treatment (β3 = 23.93, P ≤ 0.001), fees marketing and travel (β4 = −16.84, P ≤ 0.001), and total labor (β5 = 1.01, P < 0.01) significantly predict total revenue (Table 11). The regression coefficients suggest that when all other variables are held constant: for every $1 spent on producing bees, revenue increases by $1.09; for every $1 spent on colony feed, revenue increases by $4.23; for every $1 spent on colony treatment, revenue increases by $23.63; for every $1 spent on fees, marketing and travel, revenue decreases by $18.36; and for every $1 spent on total labor, revenue increases by $1.01.

Profit and Colony Loss Model Results

For beekeepers with 101 colonies or more, the model results show that when colony winter losses range from 25.7% to 45.6%, revenues exceed total costs in all replacement scenarios except for package replacement with 45.6% loss for operations with 300–699 colonies where per colony profit is −$17 (Tables 13 and 14). For operations with 1–100 colonies, total costs always exceed total revenues at all mortality rates and replacement scenarios. As colony numbers rise from 1–9 colonies to 10–39 colonies and then to 40–100 colonies, profits steadily increase, with per colony profits rising from −$1,109 to −$502 to −$46 with the baseline winter loss rate of 25.7%. This pattern is repeated at all winter loss rates in the analysis. For operations producing honey only and those producing honey and bees, total costs exceed total revenues at all winter loss rates and under all replacement strategies except for operations that produce honey and bees at the baseline winter loss rate of 25.7% (Tables 15 and 16). For operations that engage in honey production alone, losses range from −$480 per colony with a loss rate of 45.6% and package bee replacement, to −$433 per colony at the average loss rate of 25.7%. For operations that diversify their revenue sources to include honey and bee sales and pollination rentals, total revenue exceeds total costs under all mortality rates, and with all replacement scenarios. Profits range from $76 per colony for these highly diversified operations with package replacement to $124 profit per colony at the average loss rate. For beekeeping operations in the Cariboo, North Coast, and Okanagan, revenues always exceed total costs at every winter loss rate and replacement scenario. For the South Coast-Islands region, winter mortality rates of 25.7% generate positive profit for all replacement scenarios (Tables 17 and 18). When winter losses fall to 30%, these operations generate positive profit when losses are replaced with splits and negative profits when losses are replaced with packages. At loss rates of 38% and 45.6%, per colony profit estimates are negative for both split and package replacement in this region. In the Kootenay and Peace regions, the model estimates that total costs exceed total revenues at all winter mortality rates and for split and package replacement.

Discussion

This analysis provides insight into beekeeping economics in British Columbia and has been the foundation for creating a set of recommendations to help achieve sustainability for the beekeeping industry (Supplementary able S1). The study highlights several key issues in British Columbia beekeeping: the uneven distribution of revenue income from beekeeping activities; the economic impact of diversifying and scaling up beekeeping activities within an operation; the impact of domestic bee production on profitability; and the regional economic beekeeping disparities across the province. Since honey is the single largest contributor to revenue for all surveyed beekeepers, representing 60% of all revenue sources, this study recommends prioritizing regulatory efforts to maintain consistent honey prices for beekeepers to ensure that revenues remain high and contribute to higher profits. Bee production costs (nuc and queen production costs) are positively correlated with total revenue: operations in British Columbia that diversify revenue sources beyond honey to include queen and/or nuc sales experience profit increases. In addition, replacing colony losses with splits instead of imported bees generates higher profit, regardless of beekeeping activity. Despite these findings, British Columbia beekeepers imported over 56,000 queens and nearly 4,000 nucs in 2022 (BC 2022), a practice that is not economically optimal. There may be even further positive health and ultimately economic value accrued by beekeepers choosing to replace losses with domestic bees that do not carry the same health risks as imported bees. Queens, packages, and nucs imported into Canada each year threaten to undermine Canadian bee health with the introduction of new pathogens and pests, over-heated and long transportation, bees maladjusted for the Canadian climate, and a myriad of queen issues. (CFIA 2013, Pettis et al. 2016, Bixby et al. 2019, 2021, McAfee et al. 2020).

It is recommended that strategic and targeted provincial and regional resources be allocated to support the existing local bee production industries and educate and encourage new producers to make splits for replacement and begin raising queens and nucs in British Columbia. For operations that diversify their revenue sources further to include pollination rentals, our modeling shows that total revenue exceeds total costs under all mortality rates and with all colony replacement scenarios. However, since pollination rentals account for only 10% of surveyed beekeeper revenue and there is some evidence that commercial pollination of blueberries may result in deteriorated bee health (Traynor et al. 2016, Graham et al. 2022) and increases in treatment costs, diversifying operations into commercial pollination to increase profitability becomes more nuanced and complex. Given the challenges and agroeconomic imperative of commercial pollination in British Columbia, financial and bee health extension support for beekeepers to encourage pollination services of key Canadian crops is recommended to ensure food security and the maintenance of agroecosystems.

The survey results show that labor is the single largest component of variable costs and that on average, smaller operations are less profitable than larger ones. The profit model results also show that smaller operations with fewer colonies (<100) are less profitable than larger operations at all colony mortality rates and replacement scenarios, suggesting that small-scale beekeepers have more difficulty recovering from overwinter colony losses. These results indicate that scaling up operations may yield higher profits which require hiring additional labor. However, sourcing and retaining skilled workers has become very challenging for agricultural sectors due to labor shortages since the COVID-19 pandemic (CAHRC 2015, Bixby et al. 2021). Growing an operation also requires increasing the number of colonies, directly impacting variable costs, which in the short run for most operations that have a small or negative profit margin creates economic uncertainty and risk. To encourage and facilitate beekeepers striving to scale up their operations, labor, and materials subsidies for apiculture would improve industry profitability and sustainability. In British Columbia, the majority of beekeepers are considered small-scale, as only 3% of the beekeepers operate more than 50 colonies, exemplifying the need to find more effective business models for the industry (Paul van Westendorp, personal communication, 22 June 2023). Profits for surveyed beekeepers also vary by region. Differences in profitability between regions could be attributed to sample size, living costs, costs of transportation, the accessibility of markets, and the ability to find qualified labor. In the profit model, for beekeeping operations in certain regions, revenues always exceed total costs at every winter loss rate and replacement scenario, whereas in other regions, the model estimates that even when replacing colonies with less expensive splits and in-house queens at average winter loss rates, profits are consistently negative. These results indicate that to ensure economic stability across the province, certain regions may need additional financial and technical support from regulators and extension programs. Despite the many challenges faced by beekeepers and the complexity of beekeeping economics, with policy and financial support, there are opportunities for beekeepers across the province to scale up and diversify operations to achieve agricultural, economic, and bee health sustainability in the apiculture industry in British Columbia.
