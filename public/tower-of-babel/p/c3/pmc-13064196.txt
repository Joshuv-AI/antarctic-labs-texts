Impact of a clinical pharmacist-led stewardship programme on antibiotic utilisation and clinical outcomes in thoracic surgery: a retrospective interrupted time-series study at a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Guangjie Wu, Fuying Guo, Yan He, Jianhua Lu, Dong Liu, Xiaoxv Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to assess the impact of clinical pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship on antibiotic use and postoperative pulmonary infection in a surgical department.DesignRetrospective and cross-sectional.SettingA tertiary hospital in Wuhan, China.ParticipantsA total of 9157 patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic lung surgery (VATLS) were included (4949 and 4208 pre-implementation and post-implementation).MethodsAn interrupted time-series design with segmented regression analysis was employed to evaluate changes in antibiotic use intensity (defined daily dose/100 bed-days), antibiotic spectrum coverage (scores/100 bed-days) and postoperative pulmonary infection rate before and after implementation of the stewardship programme. Between-group comparisons of antibiotic use rates and treatment duration were performed using χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate.Outcome measuresAntibiotic use intensity (defined daily dose/100 bed-days), antibiotic spectrum coverage (scores/100 bed-days), postoperative pulmonary infection rates, the proportion and the duration of antibiotic use.ResultsFollowing implementation, antibiotic use intensity decreased immediately by 96.65 defined daily doses/100 bed-days (95% CI −122.56 to −70.74; p<0.001). Antibiotic spectrum coverage (ASC) also showed an immediate reduction of 4.77 scores/100 bed-days (95% CI −6.12 to −3.42; p<0.001), although a gradual upward trend in ASC was observed during the post-implementation period. No significant change was seen for postoperative pulmonary infection rates between the two periods. Prescribing patterns shifted towards narrower-spectrum agents: the proportion of first-generation cephalosporins increased from 17.6% to 45.5% (p<0.001), while usage of fourth-generation cephalosporins (12.3% to 1.5%), β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors (63.9% to 12.6%), quinolones (57.4% to 27.4%), carbapenems (0.8% to 0.4%) and glycopeptides (8.2% to 3.1%) declined significantly (all p<0.05). The median duration of antimicrobial therapy was shortened across most antibiotic classes.ConclusionClinical pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship can reduce the antibiotic use intensity among patients undergoing VATLS in thoracic surgery, decrease the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and shorten the duration of antibiotic therapy.

Introduction

Antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) have been widely adopted globally to promote rational antibiotic use and combat rising bacterial resistance. Clinical pharmacists are central to these efforts. Evidence indicates that pharmacist-led ASPs optimise prescribing practices by guiding drug selection, reducing unnecessary use and standardising treatment regimens, ultimately improving patient care and clinical outcomes.1

In China, the National Health Commission has issued continuous directives to promote rational antibiotic use and address rising bacterial resistance. Healthcare facilities nationwide have responded by implementing measures to improve prescribing practices. Long-term cross-sectional studies over 7 years have shown that pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship significantly affects antibiotic consumption and resistance patterns in clinical settings.2 Despite a general decrease in antibiotic usage, a reduction in prophylactic prescriptions, a decline in resistance rates among key bacterial strains and enhancements across various ASP indicators, the study’s scope is somewhat limited by the inclusion of patients from diverse departments and a lack of clarity on baseline details, such as specifics of infectious diseases and surgical interventions. This limitation constrains the depth of analysis and limits practical applicability. Pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship has shown promise in specialised settings, including haematological malignancy units,3 urgent care,46 intensive care units7 and general wards.8 9 These findings suggest that department-specific or disease-specific studies within the ASP framework could yield more actionable insights for clinical pharmacists.

Surgical units are a critical setting for clinical pharmacists involvement in ASPs.1012 Antibiotic prescribing in surgical units differs from that in other medical wards, characterised by the frequent use of prophylactic and empiric antibiotics during the perioperative periods. Surgeons may prioritise operative interventions over pharmacological management, which can lead to higher rates of inappropriate antibiotic use in surgical departments compared with other medical wards.13 Clinical pharmacists can help address these gaps by reviewing prescriptions, providing evidence-based recommendations and monitoring adherence to stewardship guidelines.

In this study, we report on a pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship initiative in the thoracic surgery department of a tertiary teaching hospital in China. Thoracic surgery involves complex procedures with a high risk of postoperative infections, making appropriate antibiotic use particularly important in this setting. This study describes the trends in antibiotic consumption, prescribing patterns and postoperative pulmonary infections, following implementation of the interventions. The findings provide a reference for implementing and evaluating ASPs in other surgical departments, where similar challenges in antibiotic prescribing may exist.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a quasi-experimental pre-implementation and post-implementation study with interrupted time-series (ITS) analysis. The pre-implementation period was from January 2019 to January 2023 (except January to February 2020 due to the pandemic), while the post-implementation period was from February 2023 to March 2024.

All consecutive patients admitted to the thoracic surgery department and who received video-assisted thoracoscopic lung surgery (VATLS) were included. Patients who underwent conversion to open-chest surgery during VATLS, those who were transferred to other departments, or those who experienced in-hospital mortality were excluded from the study cohort. The patient’s personal information was hidden. Data were extracted directly from the hospital information system and electronic medical records of all patients.14 As these systems are mandatory for all prescribing and testing at our hospital, data completeness was ensured. No patients were excluded due to missing records.

Antimicrobial stewardship interventions

Prior to February 2023, the thoracic surgery department lacked a formal, structured antimicrobial stewardship programme. The sole measure was a departmental target for antibiotic use intensity, enforced by financial penalties for non-compliance and no other stewardship initiatives were in place beyond this. Consequently, the following strategy (details are shown in online supplemental table S1) was established and sustained from February 2023 through the end of the post-implementation study period in March 2024.

Leadership commitment and accountability

This strategy was reinforced through two key steps. First, the director of the thoracic surgery department was formally appointed as a core member of the hospital-wide Antimicrobial Stewardship Committee attached to the Pharmaceutical Management and Therapeutics Committee, creating a clear line of responsibility. Second, the hospital provided dedicated support (including pharmaceutical expertise and information technology resources) and set specific stewardship performance goals for the department. This framework ensured that accountability was embedded from the leadership level down to daily clinical practice.

Actions to improve antimicrobial prescribing

Clinical pharmacists conduct monthly reviews and provide feedback on antimicrobial use, lead group discussions on significant irrational prescribing issues, offer consultations for special-grade antimicrobials (including but not limited to carbapenems, glycopeptides, oxazolidinones and tigecycline), implement restrictions on prophylactic antimicrobial use in VATLS, and consult on infectious disease cases in the thoracic surgery department.

Tracking and reporting antimicrobial use and outcomes

Clinical pharmacists deliver monthly reports on antibiotic use intensity and track hospital-acquired infections in VATLS patients.

Education

Training is provided by clinical pharmacists to enhance knowledge of basic antimicrobial principles and promote rational perioperative prophylactic use of antibiotics during VATLS.

Statistical analysis

Data on antibiotic usage were collected for each patient encounter in our study cohort, with monthly intervals serving as the smallest unit of observation. The consumption of antibiotics was normalised using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system, a widely recognised standard. To quantify usage, we employed the defined daily doses (DDD) metric, as endorsed by the WHO Collaborating Centre for Drug Statistics Methodology.2 Antibiotic use intensity was quantified by DDDs/100 bed-days.

For assessing the spectrum of antibiotic coverage, we adopted the antibiotic spectrum coverage (ASC) scoring system, as delineated by Michihiko Goto and colleagues.15 Expanding on their methodology, we incorporated data for additional antibiotics that, while not part of their original study, are routinely administered in our thoracic surgery department. To enable standardised comparisons of monthly trends, ASC was calculated as scores/100 bed-days, consistent with the metric used for antibiotic use intensity.

Postoperative pulmonary infection was defined based on chest imaging reports. Patients with infection noted in the radiology report before discharge were classified as having a postoperative pulmonary infection.

We used segmented regression analysis of an interrupted time series to evaluate the effect of the intervention on monthly inpatient antibiotic use intensity, ASC and infection rate. The observation period ran from January 2019 to March 2024. The series was split into pre-implementation and post-implementation phases based on the intervention start date. The model was specified as:

In this model, Yt is the outcome at month t; β0 is the baseline level at t=0 (January 2019); Tt denotes time in months from the start; β1 represents the pre-intervention trend; interventiont is an indicator variable (0 before, 1 after the intervention), with β2 estimating the immediate level change; and TimeAftert (0 before, incrementing by 1 each month after) captures the change in slope post-intervention (β3).

To account for potential autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in the residuals, we applied Newey-West heteroskedasticity-consistent and autocorrelation-consistent SEs with a lag of 1, consistent with standard practice for monthly data.

We also assessed seasonal variation by including 11 dummy variables for calendar months (January as reference) in an auxiliary model and conducting a joint F-test of their coefficients. If the test indicated significant seasonality (p<0.05), these terms would be retained in the final model; otherwise, they were excluded.

All analyses were conducted using Stata V.17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) and SPSS V.25 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages. Between-group comparisons were made using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for samples of 50 or fewer observations and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for larger samples. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. Continuous variables are reported as mean±SD when normally distributed, and as median with IQR otherwise. Group comparisons for continuous outcomes were performed using the independent-samples t-test when assumptions of normality and equal variance were met; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Interrupted time series analysis of major outcomes

61 months of observational data were analysed using segmented regression to evaluate the impact of the intervention on antibiotic use intensity, antibiotic spectrum coverage and postoperative pulmonary infection rates. All models accounted for heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation using Newey-West SEs. Joint tests for seasonality across 11 monthly indicator variables revealed no significant seasonal effects for any outcome (all p>0.05).

Before the intervention, antibiotic use intensity showed no significant temporal trend (β=0.11, 95% CI −0.53 to 0.75; p=0.737). Implementation of the intervention was associated with an immediate level decrease of 101.25 units (95% CI −123.15 to −79.36; p<0.001). The post-implementation slope indicated a non-significant monthly increase of 1.28 units (95% CI −0.28 to 2.84; p=0.105), suggesting the reduction in antibiotic use intensity was sustained throughout the post-implementation period. A significant decreasing trend in ASC was observed prior to the intervention (β=−0.07, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.03; p=0.001). Following implementation, ASC decreased immediately by 4.77 units (95% CI −6.12 to −3.42; p<0.001). However, the post-intervention slope increased by 0.16 units per month (95% CI 0.06 to 0.26; p=0.002), indicating a partial rebound in spectrum coverage over time. See figure 1. The ASC scoring system was expanded to include 32 antibiotics (incorporating 18 additional agents specific to thoracic surgery) following established methodologies (online supplemental table S2).

For postoperative pulmonary infection rates, the pre-implementation baseline trend showed a non-significant decline (β=−0.0002, 95% CI −0.0007 to 0.0003; p=0.483). No immediate change was detected following the intervention (β=−0.0027, 95% CI −0.0201 to 0.0147; p=0.767), and the post-implementation slope remained unchanged (β=−0.00001, 95% CI −0.0005 to 0.0005; p=0.990), indicating stability in infection rates throughout the study period. See figure 1. To verify these findings, we also performed a simple pre–post comparison using the χ2 test. The results were consistent with the ITS analysis, showing no significant difference in infection rates between the pre-implementation and post-implementation periods (4.6% vs 4.1%, p=0.213) (online supplemental table S3).

Additionally, to address potential confounding by baseline characteristics, we performed a post hoc sensitivity analysis incorporating these variables as covariates in the ITS models. The adjusted results were highly consistent with our primary findings. For antibiotic use intensity, the estimated immediate level change remained robust at −102.05 DDDs/100 bed-days (95% CI −127.07 to −77.02; p<0.001), virtually identical to the unadjusted estimate of −101.25. Similarly, the reduction in the ASC persisted in the adjusted model (level change: −4.63; 95% CI −6.26 to −3.01; p<0.001). Regarding postoperative pulmonary infection, the adjusted model confirmed no significant association between the intervention and the infection rates (coefficient: 0.0142; 95% CI −0.0116 to 0.0399; p=0.27).

Full parameter of the analysis and model diagnostic details are provided in online supplemental tables S4–S10 and online supplemental figure 1S.

Rate and duration of antibiotics usage between the pre-implementation and post-implementation periods

Following the implementation of the intervention, significant changes were observed in both prescribing rates and treatment duration across multiple antibiotic classes. During the post-implementation period, first-generation cephalosporin use rose from 17.6% to 45.5% (p<0.001), with median duration falling from 7 to 4 days (p<0.001). Second-generation use declined from 25.0% to 9.6% (p<0.001); duration shortened from 6 to 5 days (p<0.001). Third-generation use increased from 15.9% to 22.2% (p<0.001), duration from 6 to 4 days (p<0.001). Fourth-generation use dropped from 12.3% to 1.5% (p<0.001), duration from 7 to 4 days (p<0.001). β-lactamase/β-lactamase inhibitors (BL/BLIs) decreased from 63.9% to 12.6% (p<0.001); duration fell from 8 to 5 days (p<0.001). Cephamycins fell from 14.8% to 0.2% (p<0.001); duration unchanged (p=0.980). Carbapenems declined from 0.8% to 0.4% (p=0.010); duration unchanged (p=0.490). Quinolones use dropped from 57.4% to 27.4% (p<0.001); duration from 6 to 5 days (p<0.001). Penicillins increased from 0% to 0.3% (p<0.001). Glycylcyclines and nitroimidazoles remained rare with no significant change. Glycopeptide use fell from 8.2% to 3.1% (p<0.001); duration from 6 to 4 days (p<0.001). Oxazolidinones (p=0.007) and triazoles (p=0.042) showed small but significant declines. Details are shown in table 2.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of a clinical pharmacist-led ASP on antibiotic use and postoperative pulmonary infection rates in patients undergoing VATLS. Using an ITS design, we observed significant reductions in antibiotic use intensity, a shift towards narrower-spectrum agents and shorter treatment durations following the intervention was observed, while no significant changes in postoperative pulmonary infections were seen. These findings add to the evidence supporting the effectiveness of ASPs in surgical settings and clarify the specific role of clinical pharmacists in their implementation.

The primary aim of this study was to assess whether a multifaceted ASP—encompassing leadership engagement, prescribing audit and feedback, tracking of antibiotic use and education—could improve antibiotic prescribing practice in the surgical department. Our results demonstrate clear improvements across multiple indicators. Antibiotic use intensity dropped significantly immediately after the intervention, and this reduction was sustained throughout the post-implementation period. Although ASC showed a partial rebound over time, the initial decline suggests that the programme successfully encouraged the use of narrower-spectrum agents. These changes were accompanied by a marked reduction in the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics such as fourth-generation cephalosporins, BL/BLIs, carbapenems and quinolones, while the use of first-generation cephalosporins increased—consistent with guideline-recommended prophylaxis for thoracic surgery.16 17 Importantly, these prescribing changes occurred without an increase in postoperative pulmonary infection rates, indicating that the ASP did not compromise patient safety. Notably, these findings remained robust in a post hoc sensitivity analysis adjusting for significant baseline covariates (including age, smoking history and comorbidities), where the magnitude and direction of the intervention effects were virtually unchanged. This consistency suggests that the observed improvements are attributable to the stewardship programme itself rather than confounding by patient characteristics.

It is important to distinguish between the effectiveness of the ASP itself and the suitability of clinical pharmacists as the workforce to deliver it. In this study, clinical pharmacists were responsible for implementing core ASP components: conducting monthly prescription reviews, providing feedback, tracking outcomes and delivering education. Their integration into the thoracic surgery team enabled consistent, data-driven oversight of antibiotic use. The success of the programme can be attributed not only to the presence of pharmacists but also to the structured and multifaceted nature of the intervention. This included leadership commitment, clear accountability and real-time data feedback—elements that are critical to ASP success regardless of who delivers them.1 18 However, the findings also highlight the unique contributions of pharmacists, particularly in conducting detailed antimicrobial audits, offering therapeutic consultations and facilitating behaviour change through education. These activities require specialised knowledge in infectious diseases and antimicrobial pharmacology, which positions clinical pharmacists as well-suited leaders for ASPs in specialised surgical units. Nevertheless, the rebound in ASC over time suggests that the impact of pharmacist-led interventions may wane without ongoing reinforcement. This pattern is consistent with our earlier experience during the pre-implementation period. Between August 2021 and May 2022, a marked rise in antibiotic use intensity was observed, which appeared to be driven by irrational prescribing practices such as unnecessary combinations, excessive dosing or prolonged prophylaxis. In response, the clinical pharmacist organised two department-wide training sessions on perioperative antimicrobial use in September 2021 and May 2022, and shared feedback on irrational prescribing cases. Although these sessions led to temporary reductions in antibiotic use, the effects were short and did not translate into sustained improvement. This experience reinforced the limitations of standalone educational interventions and highlighted the need for a more structured and ongoing ASP, the approach formally implemented in February 2023. This highlights that maintaining these improvements requires ongoing engagement, regular education and possibly automated decision support. It confirms that ASPs are not a one-time solution but demand lasting institutional commitment.

Our findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that pharmacist-led ASPs can reduce antibiotic use and improve prescribing patterns in hospital settings,27 8 1922 including thoracic surgery, urology, orthopaedics, haematology and general medical wards. In these diverse clinical settings, structured stewardship interventions have successfully optimised antibiotic consumption and duration without compromising patient safety or increasing infection rates. These observations suggest that ASPs can effectively optimise prescribing practices across diverse surgical and medical populations while maintaining clinical outcomes. Additionally, the use of novel metrics such as antibiotic spectrum coverage, alongside traditional use intensity, provided a more nuanced view of antibiotic use and helped track shifts in spectrum coverage over time.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, which may limit its generalisability. However, the focus on a specific procedure (VATLS) enhances internal validity and offers a model for similar units. Second, we did not assess the clinical appropriateness of individual antibiotic prescriptions, which remains a key goal of ASPs but is difficult to quantify objectively. Third, patients with complicated postoperative courses (eg, conversion to open surgery, transfer or death) were excluded to maintain cohort homogeneity; their exclusion may lead to underestimation of antibiotic use in complex cases. Finally, as with any observational design, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Unmeasured factors such as changes in national antibiotic policies, seasonal infection patterns or patient-level variables (eg, disease severity, nutritional status) could potentially influence outcomes; however, no major policy shifts occurred during the study period, seasonality tests were non-significant and the homogeneous surgical cohort minimises case-mix variations. We therefore believe these factors are unlikely to fully explain the observed improvements.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that a structured, multifaceted antimicrobial stewardship programme—delivered by clinical pharmacists—can significantly reduce antibiotic use intensity, promote the use of narrow-spectrum agents and shorten treatment duration in patients undergoing VATLS. While the programme was effective, the observed rebound in antibiotic spectrum coverage highlights the need for sustained efforts. These findings support both the effectiveness of ASPs in surgical settings and the critical role of clinical pharmacists in leading such programmes.
