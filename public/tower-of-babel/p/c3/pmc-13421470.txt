Construction and validation of a nomogram for overall survival prognosis in patients with advanced (stage III/IV) pancreatic cancer
Authors: Dongqi Yang, Chenjie Wang, Ke Su, Xin Liu, Zunyuan Tan, Jianwen Zhang, Han Li, Zhenjiang Li, Kun He, Yunwei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In order to provide an additional tool for clinical prognosis evaluation, this research attempts to build a nomogram for predicting the survival of patients with advanced (stage Ⅲ/Ⅳ) pancreatic cancer and to preliminary evaluate its predictive impact. Data from 336 patients diagnosed with stage III/IV pancreatic cancer (2018–2025) across two Chinese hospitals were analyzed. Multivariate Cox regression identified independent predictors in the training set, which were used to construct a nomogram estimating 6-, 12-, and 24-month overall survival. The model underwent internal and external validation via ROC curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis. Multivariate Cox regression analysis of the training set revealed that serum albumin (P = 0.001), liver metastasis (P = 0.023), ALT≥40U/L (P = 0.010), and CA199 level (P = 0.037) were independent predictors of overall survival. Based on this, a nomogram model was constructed in the training cohort, with a C-index of 0.741. In the internal validation, the AUC values for predicting 6, 12, and 24-month survival rates were 0.806, 0.753, and 0.628, respectively, and in the external validation, they were 0.922, 0.662, and 0.650, respectively. The calibration curve showed that the predicted probabilities were in good agreement with the actual observed results. The discrimination and calibration of this model in internal verification are acceptable, but its incremental value for clinical decision-making is limited, and large-scale multi-center studies are required to further verify its generalizability.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is a highly malignant solid tumor of the digestive system1. Due to its high invasiveness, difficulty in early diagnosis, and generally poor prognosis, pancreatic cancer has become one of the cancer types with the highest mortality rate in the world2,3. In the United States, the 5-year survival rate for pancreatic cancer is only 10%, and more than half of the patients are diagnosed after the age of 704; in 2022, pancreatic cancer has become the seventh leading cause of death from tumor diseases worldwide5. In recent decades, the prevalence of pancreatic cancer has increased. In 1990, there were 196,000 cases of pancreatic cancer globally, which rose to 441,000 by 20176. In recent years, the number of diagnosed cases of pancreatic cancer has continued to rise, and the incidence among young people has also increased5,6. Due to the lack of specificity of early symptoms (such as abdominal pain, nausea, or weight loss), and the presence of various comorbidities or complications in patients, the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer is somewhat difficult7. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage (stage III/IV) and have a poor clinical prognosis. In recent years, research on diagnostic techniques for pancreatic cancer has made certain progress. For instance, Abakundana Nsenga Ariston Gabriel et al. elucidated the prospective significance of exosomes in the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer8; Mai et al. believe that platelets may provide potential information for the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer9; Johnston et al. reported that a blood test called Galleri can be used for the screening of various cancers and may provide a new strategy for the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer10.

Currently, surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, combination therapy, and other treatments are used to treat pancreatic cancer11. Although the treatment methods are constantly evolving and improving, for patients in the advanced stage, the conventional treatment measures are still relatively limited in improving the survival effect. At the same time, as a disease with complex and multiple risk factors, the risk factors of pancreatic cancer can be divided into modifiable factors and non-modifiable factors5. Natalia Khalaf summarized the main modifiable risk factors of pancreatic cancer, including smoking, obesity, lack of physical activity, and dietary type, etc.12. Luo et al. pointed out that age, gender, genetic susceptibility and genetic factors of family syndromes are inevitable risk factors for pancreatic cancer, such as being over 75 years old, being male, having a family history of pancreatic cancer in the first-degree relatives, and BRCA 2 gene mutation2. These factors may lead to different degrees of differences in the survival outcomes of patients. Therefore, screening, prevention and determining the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients have certain clinical significance.

Nomogram is a visualization tool that can integrate multiple variables into a visual graph. Users only need simple plotting operations to obtain the solution or estimate value of the equation without the need for complex calculations13. This makes it convenient for quick estimation of individual prognosis. In recent years, this nomogram model has been increasingly applied in tumor prognosis assessment14. Peng et al. constructed a specific survival nomogram model based on postoperative information of pancreatic cancer patients, providing a possible method for clinicians to assess patients’ survival periods and determine treatment strategies15; Shi et al. used the nomogram to predict the overall survival and specific survival of young pancreatic cancer patients. This study provides a possibility for estimating the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients16. Currently, some studies on nomogram models for advanced (stage III/IV) pancreatic cancer patients may be conducted based on public databases, lacking common clinical information such as laboratory tests, past medical history, and personal history17; at the same time, some studies may focus on postoperative patients or specific age groups, and the applicability to advanced (stage III/IV) patients needs further observation. Therefore, the goal of this study is to develop a nomogram for predicting the overall survival of patients with advanced (stage III/IV) pancreatic cancer based on clinical data from two Chinese hospitals. The nomogram model is then validated using both internal and external validation sets. This model may provide auxiliary reference for clinicians in prognostic assessment of advanced pancreatic cancer patients and offer a certain degree of reference for formulating treatment strategies.

Methods

Patients selection

Patients with pancreatic cancer from two Chinese hospitals between 2018 and 2025 served as the study’s subjects. Two pieces of data were present. Shandong Cancer Hospital (Center 1) provided the first data set, which was split into a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio. All of the patients in the second data set, which was obtained from Southwest Medical University Affiliated Hospital in Sichuan Province (Center 2), served as the external validation set. The following are the requirements for patient inclusion: (1) Individuals with a pathologic or clinical diagnosis of pancreatic cancer; (2) Lesions measurable using RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours); (3) Stages III and IV. After excluding cases of synchronous double primary cancers, patients at stage I/II, and those with incomplete data, two researchers independently reviewed the medical records and excluded subjects whose key information could not be traced. Ultimately, 336 complete cases were included for analysis. The integrated workflow diagram for case inclusion/exclusion and missing data handling is shown in Fig. 1. The Ethics Review Committees of Southwest Medical University Affiliated Hospital and Shandong Cancer Hospital have both authorized this study. Informed consent forms have been signed by every patient.

Variable selection

Each patient participated in this research was asked for the following information: (1) Demographic variables: patient ID, patient age, gender; (2) Past medical history and personal history: diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption; (3) Indicators of oncological characteristics: maximum tumor diameter(Size), TNM staging status (including clinical, T, N, and M stages) and distant metastasis status (divided into liver metastasis and metastasis to other sites except the liver); (4) Laboratory tests: blood routine indicators (including white blood cell count and classification, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, hemoglobin, platelet count), liver function indicators (including alanine aminotransferase and classification, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, total bilirubin) and tumor marker CA199; (5) Survival outcomes: overall survival status and overall survival time.

The endpoint of all the cohorts in this study was the overall survival period (Overall Survival, OS), which refers to the time from the start of treatment to death due to any cause.

Statistical analysis

The baseline data comparison of all groups in this study was conducted according to the following strategy: For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to confirm that the data distribution was normal; for categorical variables, the χ2 test was used. Variables that met the normal distribution were compared using the T test, and those that did not meet the normal distribution were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. This study created survival curves and estimated survival rates for the training cohort, internal validation cohort, and external validation cohort using the Kaplan-Meier method. The median survival time and 95% confidence intervals of the corresponding groups were computed, and the differences in overall survival periods were compared using the Log-rank test. This study created nomogram models for overall survival at 6, 12, and 24 months and identified independent predictive variables using multivariate Cox regression analysis. Model validation was carried out using the following strategies: (1) Determine the model’s discriminative capacity by calculating the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (Time-dependent AUC); (2) Create calibration curves to assess the model’s calibration performance; (3) To assess the model’s clinical usefulness, use decision curve analysis (DCA). All statistical analyses were completed in R 4.5.1 (R version 4.5.1) and SPSS 26.0. Two-sided test with P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall survival nomogram.

This study systematically evaluated the performance of the nomogram model using multiple methods. First, as seen in Fig. 4, the ROC curve was utilized to evaluate how well the nomogram predicted patients’ OS. The areas under the ROC curves (AUC-ROC) for predicting 6-, 12-, and 24-month OS in the internal validation cohort were 0.806, 0.753, and 0.628, respectively (Fig. 4a), and in the external validation cohort were 0.922, 0.662, and 0.650, respectively (Fig. 4b). The results indicated that the nomogram model had certain discriminatory ability, and its discrimination efficacy fluctuated as the prediction time increased. Decision curve analysis of the internal validation cohort demonstrated that, within a specific threshold probability range, the nomogram model yielded higher net benefits compared to the intervention-for-all and no-intervention strategies (Fig. 5a). However, in the external validation cohort, the net benefit curve of this model almost entirely coincided with that of the intervention-for-all strategy across nearly all threshold probability ranges (Fig. 5b). According to the calibration curve in Fig. 6, the consistency between the predicted survival status of 6-month, 12-month, and 24-month and the observed survival status was strong, indicating that the constructed nomogram model had high stability. The C-index of this nomogram model was 0.741. Overall, this nomogram model has certain robustness and reliability.

Discussion

According to the most recent epidemiological research, pancreatic cancer has a high death rate worldwide and is one of the highly lethal and poorly prognosed malignant tumors18. It not only poses a serious threat to human life and health, but also imposes a heavy burden on the public health sector19. The early stage of pancreatic cancer lacks obvious specific symptoms, which may cause patients to miss the best opportunity for diagnosis and treatment. This is one of the important reasons for the extremely poor prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients20; at the same time, due to the high invasiveness and easy metastasis of pancreatic cancer, most pancreatic cancer patients are already in the advanced stage (stage III/IV) at the time of diagnosis, which poses significant challenges in determining treatment strategies and evaluating prognosis. Although the diagnostic techniques for pancreatic cancer have improved in recent years, there are still certain limitations in evaluating the prognosis of advanced patients. For example, Hameed believes that the use of imaging techniques such as computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), and positron emission tomography (PET) can help with the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer, but these imaging examinations may not provide information on the biological behavior of the tumor, and thus may have limited effect on evaluating the prognosis of advanced patients21. In 2018, the eighth version of the pancreatic cancer TNM staging manual was formally published by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). This manual provides relatively standard TNM staging for clinicians and pancreatic cancer patients, and plays an important role in guiding personalized treatment, identifying clinically beneficial patients, and evaluating patient prognosis22. However, this staging standard is mainly based on anatomical and pathological information, and considers host and tumor markers relatively less. There are still certain limitations in prognosis prediction22–25, making this manual difficult to meet the clinical needs for evaluating the prognosis of advanced pancreatic cancer patients. Therefore, developing an effective model for evaluating the prognosis of advanced pancreatic cancer patients has potential significance for optimizing clinical decisions and rationally allocating medical resource allocation26.

This study is based on the clinical data of pancreatic cancer patients from two hospitals in China, aiming to develop a survival prediction model applicable to patients with advanced pancreatic cancer27. Firstly, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were set, and 336 patients meeting the criteria were selected26. The clinical information of these patients was collected. Then, through Cox regression analysis, four independent prognostic factors - liver metastasis, ALT ≥ 40 U/L, low albumin, and high CA199 - were screened out. These were used to build a nomogram model that predicted overall survival at 6, 12, and 24 months. The C-index of this model was calculated as 0.741, and relevant validations were conducted. The validation results indicated that the constructed nomogram model showed good predictive ability and clinical reference value in advanced (stage III/IV) pancreatic cancer patients. Regarding discriminating abilities, the model’s AUC for predicting overall survival at six months was 0.806 in the internal validation group and 0.922 in the external validation group, suggesting good short-term prognosis discrimination ability; the AUC for 12 months and 24 months predictions in the internal validation were 0.753 and 0.628, and in the external validation, they were 0.662 and 0.650.Furthermore, the number of endpoint events in the training set significantly exceeded the number of variables ultimately included, aligning with the empirical criteria for event counts in the proportional hazards model, thereby minimizing the risk of severe overfitting due to data sparsity. The nomogram demonstrated adequate short-term discriminatory capability; however, its predictive performance for medium- to long-term outcomes was attenuated due to the exclusion of factors such as treatment response, drug resistance, and dynamic nutritional-immunological status. External validation through decision curve analysis revealed substantial overlap in net benefit between the model and the treat-all strategy, indicating limited incremental clinical value in external cohorts. The generalizability of the model requires further validation through larger-scale prospective multicenter studies. The clinical net benefit of this nomogram model within a specific probability threshold range was higher than the all intervention and no intervention strategies in the internal validation cohort, according to the decision curve analysis; however, due to the unmeasured variable factors such as gene mutations, treatment intensity and supportive care levels existing among the various centers, and the fact that this model only relies on four routine indicators, it limits its ability to comprehensively capture the heterogeneity of prognosis in the population. External validation shows that the net benefit of this model is basically the same as the treat all strategy. This indicates that in the external cohort, the added value of this model for clinical decision-making is relatively limited. As shown in Fig. 2, although the log-rank test of the three survival curves yielded a P value less than 0.05, which did not reach the standard of statistical significance, the difference in median overall survival between the groups exceeded seven months. This difference may have potential clinical significance in advanced pancreatic cancer, and its cause may be attributed to undetected molecular characteristics, differences in treatment intensity, and regional variations in clinical practice in different areas. Compared with some previous studies, the nomogram model developed in this research has the following characteristics. Some previous prognostic model studies often included patients at all stages in the analysis, and their results may have limited specificity for patients in the advanced stage; while this research limited the research subjects to patients with clinical stage III/IV pancreatic cancer, making the model closer to the actual situation of this group with poorer prognosis, which is conducive to more accurately exploring the prognosis of this group and related factors. At the same time, some earlier research’data came from public databases, which might not have included personal data or laboratory signs, which may lack laboratory indicators and personal information; this research collected more comprehensive clinical data, covering demographic characteristics information, past history and personal history, oncological characteristics indicators and laboratory test results, which may to some extent make up for the limitations of some retrospective studies based on large public databases in lacking some clinical information, making the model closer to the real clinical situation. Compared with many previous studies that mainly relied on a single public database for development and validation, the data of this research came from two different clinical hospitals in China, the multi-center source can to some extent improve the representativeness of the sample, reduce selection bias, and at the same time adopt the internal and external validation framework, which is conducive to more objectively evaluating the real performance of the model.

Previous studies have shown that liver metastasis of pancreatic cancer is associated to some extent with the prognosis of advanced pancreatic cancer28, and there may be a connection between patient survival and serum ALT, albumin, and CA199 levels29–31. These studies’ findings are generally in line with this study’s, indicating the importance of these variables in determining the prognosis of advanced pancreatic cancer. The combined overall survival risk ratio for patients with advanced pancreatic cancer with liver metastases who had surgical resection was 0.41 (P < 0.01), according to a meta-analysis by Clements et al. This survival advantage is closely related to strict patient screening, and preoperative CA199 level, treatment response, and albumin-related inflammatory nutritional status are key assessment indicators31. The prognosis of patients with advanced liver metastatic pancreatic cancer is correlated with the level change of CA199, a frequently used pancreatic tumor marker. According to the study’s findings, CA199 is a reliable predictor of advanced pancreatic cancer, suggesting that it may help in patient risk stratification and provide a certain degree of reference for pancreatic cancer risk stratification and treatment assessment. In addition, there is a potential association between CA199 level and liver metastasis burden, treatment sensitivity, and inflammatory nutritional status, and the application value of CA199 in individualized prognosis judgment and treatment strategies needs to be further explored. Albumin is a commonly used indicator reflecting the nutritional status and systemic inflammatory response of the body, and it has some bearing on how well individuals with advanced pancreatic cancer survive. The study by Shahin Hajibandeh et al. suggests that the CRP/albumin ratio (CAR) composed of albumin and C-reactive protein (CRP) may be associated with the prognosis of patients with advanced pancreatic cancer with liver metastasis32. To exclude the possibility that elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is a concomitant manifestation of liver metastasis or biliary obstruction, collinearity and correlation analyses were performed in this study. The variance inflation factor (VIF) for both liver metastasis and total bilirubin was 1.027, indicating the absence of collinearity. The Pearson correlation coefficient between ALT and liver metastasis was 0.0029, and that between ALT and total bilirubin was 0.359, suggesting a weak positive correlation between the latter two. The above results indicate that elevated ALT is not a surrogate indicator for liver metastasis, nor is it primarily dependent on the presence of biliary obstruction. Therefore, elevated ALT (≥ 40 U/L) can independently predict survival prognosis and provide additional predictive information beyond anatomical indicators, and this effect may be related to the widespread systemic inflammation in advanced pancreatic cancer. Inflammatory mediators released by the tumor microenvironment (such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α) can affect the stability or permeability of hepatocyte membranes through the systemic circulation, and this process is independent of liver metastasis or biliary obstruction. Furthermore, pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes, metabolites, or circulating tumor DNA can also exert an indirect remote regulation effect on the liver, inducing subclinical hepatocyte stress or metabolic reprogramming. In the model constructed in this study, both ALT and albumin are independent prognostic factors, but they reflect different pathophysiological dimensions: albumin indicates the body’s nutritional status and systemic inflammatory response, reflecting the body’s reserve capacity, while elevated ALT is more indicative of tumor-related metabolic burden or intrahepatic microenvironmental inflammatory response. Therefore, ALT is not a simple surrogate indicator for liver metastasis or cholestasis, and its independent prognostic value deserves further investigation. Furthermore, setting 40 U/L as the cutoff value is mainly based on the upper limit of the clinical reference range, which is consistent with the subgroup classification method adopted in previous studies on advanced pancreatic cancer33–35. To evaluate the rationality of this classification, this study further adopted restricted cubic spline to test the nonlinear relationships between albumin, logarithm-transformed CA199, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and risk respectively. The nonlinear tests for all three indicators did not reach statistical significance (albumin: χ2 = 0.14, P = 0.9314; logarithm-transformed CA199: χ2 = 2.08, P = 0.3528; ALT: χ2 = 3.36, P = 0.1861), and the joint test of the three indicators also showed no statistical significance (χ2 = 0.14, P = 0.5068), indicating that the overall model did not significantly deviate from the linear assumption. Therefore, binary classification with 40 U/L as the cutoff point can not only effectively distinguish different risk characteristics, but also does not impair the linear property of the model. It can be seen that liver metastasis, CA199, and albumin have received attention in pancreatic cancer prognosis assessment research in recent years. This study includes these indicators in the model, which may provide certain reference for clinical judgment of the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients.

As a retrospective analysis, this study has certain limitations. Even though two centers’ worth of data were used to build and verify the model, the total sample size is still rather small, and external validation comes from just one center. Further validation of the model across a wider range of demographics and medical situations is required. At the same time, retrospective studies themselves are difficult to completely avoid selection bias. For example, removing instances with insufficient clinical information may have an impact on the sample’s representativeness. In the future, through conducting prospective studies, formulating standardized data collection procedures, or using methods such as multiple imputation to handle missing data, the reliability of the conclusion can be enhanced. This study did not incorporate specific intervention measures such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy into the multivariate model36,37, mainly because there were significant differences in treatment plans among various medical centers and across different years, making it difficult to achieve standardized quantification. To overcome this limitation, future research should adopt a prospective, multi-center design, systematically and standardly collecting key parameters such as treatment plans, dose intensity, and duration of treatment courses. Additionally, although the model incorporates multiple clinical indicators, it may still miss other important prognostic factors, such as hypertension, high triglyceride levels, hepatitis B, etc., as well as emerging molecular markers like ctDNA. Subsequent multi-center collaboration can establish a more comprehensive clinical database, which will will enhance the model’s clinical usefulness and prediction effectiveness. Finally, the model performs well in short-term (such as 6 months) survival prediction, but its long-term (such as 24 months) predictive ability declines, and there is a deviation in the survival rate estimation for some extreme-risk patients in the calibration analysis. Furthermore, as the prediction time range extends, the model’s discriminatory power gradually weakens, indicating that the nomogram is more suitable for short-term survival stratification but performs poorly in long-term prognosis prediction. Subsequently, the model calibration can be improved by introducing the Bootstrap resampling method, and dynamic monitoring indicators can be integrated to construct the prediction model, thereby enhancing the stability of long-term predictions.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a nomogram model for predicting overall survival in advanced pancreatic cancer based on data from two domestic medical institutions. The model incorporates four independent prognostic factors: liver metastasis, ALT ≥ 40 U/L, albumin levels, and CA199. Internal validation demonstrated moderate discriminative ability and calibration, with acceptable short-term predictive performance. However, decision curve analysis in external validation revealed overlapping clinical net benefit curves between the model and the treat-all strategy, indicating limited incremental decision-making value in the external cohort. Consequently, this model is suitable only for short-term risk stratification in populations similar to those in the source centers. Future research should focus on expanding sample sizes, conducting prospective multicenter studies, and incorporating additional biomarkers to enhance the model’s generalizability.
