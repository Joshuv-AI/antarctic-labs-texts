Large animal models of right heart failure due to pulmonary hypertension: a bridge to translational success
Authors: Sally E. Prins, Madelyn J. Blake, Kristi L. Helke, Thomas Di Salvo, Ryan J. Tedford, Steven Hsu, Kurt W. Prins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Right heart failure drives morbidity and mortality in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). At present, there are no chronic pharmaceutical interventions that directly augment right heart function in humans despite many promising results in rodent studies. The lack of translational success may be due to the relative scarcity of studies examining large animal models of right heart failure. However, in recent years, a rapid expansion of large animal investigations worldwide is generating new important physiological and mechanistic data. Large animal models provide more human-relevant biology, which can be paired with physiological assessments and multi-omic profiling to expand our knowledge of right heart failure. Here, we highlight findings obtained from pigs, sheep, cows and dogs, and define some potential limitations of each large animal model. In addition, we outline important questions that could be addressed in future research. Although imperfect, large animals will likely serve important roles in the development of right heart-directed therapies ranging from mechanical interventions to pharmaceutical approaches as they move up the translational pipeline. Hopefully, the field will continue these critical lines of research to promote the discovery of novel therapeutics for our patients who are in desperate need of new options.

Introduction

Right heart failure is the leading cause of mortality in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [1], yet effective therapies for this complex and deadly clinical condition are lacking. Therapies with efficacy in left heart failure are largely ineffective or have neutral effects in PAH-associated right heart failure [2]. Although researchers have generated an impressive and extensive body of histological, physiological and molecular data from multiple rodent models of right heart failure [1, 3], these discoveries have not yet translated into viable treatments for patients. One barrier may be inherent species differences between rodents and humans [4, 5]. Heart rate, cardiac size and lifespan are some important examples of variables that distinguish rodents from humans, which limits our ability to translate promising findings [6]. Thus, intermediate species that can bridge this translational gap and improve the likelihood that right-heart-directed therapies will succeed in future clinical trials are needed.

Large animal models may circumvent limitations of rodents and serve as a critical bridge from promising preclinical discoveries to novel human therapies. Large animal models are considered important intermediates between rodents and humans because they have more comparable cardiac anatomy and physiology to humans [7, 8]. Perhaps the best-studied large animal model of cardiac dysfunction is the pig [8]. There are a number of porcine studies probing cardiac biology in multiple disease states, including coronary artery disease [9], cardiac arrest and resuscitation [10], chronic left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction [11], valvular disease [11], and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [12]. In addition, pigs are heavily investigated for xenotransplantation [13] and genetically modified pig hearts can be successfully transplanted into humans [14], although the long-term viability of porcine xenotransplants remains poor [14–16]. At present, there are robust datasets evaluating porcine right heart failure, but additional data from ovine, bovine and canine models are advancing our efforts to understand and treat this deadly condition.

In this review, we highlight a rapidly expanding literature of large animal models of right heart failure. We summarise the histological and physiological findings, discuss the emerging molecular insights gained from multi-omics analyses, and spotlight therapeutic evaluations ranging from extracorporeal support to targeted medical intervention. In addition, we acknowledge key differences between each species and humans, which will need to be considered as we continue to evaluate these key models. We hope this comprehensive evaluation will guide investigators in their model selection as our field strives to advance right heart failure therapies from promising preclinical studies to approved therapeutics.

Search strategy

We searched PubMed using the following terms: “right ventricle”, “large animal model” and “pulmonary hypertension”. Then, we evaluated the references from the manuscripts we identified to identify other important references.

Porcine models of right heart failure

To date, porcine models of right heart failure are the most intensively studied of all large animal models, which provides the field with the most extensive body of data. Multiple approaches ranging from proximal pulmonary artery banding (PAB), induction of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) and pulmonary vein banding are used to induce right heart compromise (figure 1). These models offer distinct phenotypes that are being examined by investigators throughout the world.

Pulmonary artery banded animals

To better understand the progression of right heart failure, a group of investigators used conventional cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hyperpolarised cardiac imaging following infusion of (1-13C) pyruvate, echocardiography and blood sampling to evaluate temporal changes in female Danish Landrace PAB pigs [17]. 16 weeks post-banding, pigs developed right ventricular (RV) dilation, exhibited a reduction in cardiac output and had elevated serum N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide levels. However, there was no change in mean±sd RV ejection fraction (RVEF) (control: 51±10%; PAB: 57±10%) or RV–pulmonary artery (PA) coupling (end-systolic elastance (Ees)/arterial elastance (Ea)) (control: 1.9±0.5; PAB: 1.4±0.4), suggesting this was a well-compensated model, but this could be due to less severe stress as well. Interestingly, the hyperpolarised MRI data delineated a reduction in pyruvate-to-bicarbonate conversion and a rise in the lactate/bicarbonate ratio in the left ventricle. These findings demonstrate that right-sided impairment compromises LV metabolism and these metabolic disturbances arise before overt RV dysfunction. This suggests alterations in LV metabolism may be a biomarker of impending right heart failure.

Studies are now examining how therapeutic interventions in PAB pigs (castrated male pigs, Yorkshire breed, aged 3–5 weeks) modulate molecular and physiological phenotypes by integrating multi-omics with haemodynamics and cardiac MRI in pigs with more severe RV compromise. First, the effects of inhibiting ferroptosis, a type of cell death initiated by lipid peroxidation due to mitochondrial dysfunction [18], with the small molecule ferrostatin-1 were examined [19]. In this experiment, animals were randomised to ferrostatin-1 or placebo and treatment began 3 weeks after PAB. At study completion, the ferrostatin-1-treated pigs had a higher RVEF (ferrostatin-1 treated: 41%; vehicle: 30%), less RV dilation and subsequent tricuspid regurgitation and superior right atrial emptying fraction (ferrostatin-1 treated: 47%; vehicle: 33%). In addition, ferrostatin-1 treatment improved mitochondrial cristae integrity and, accordingly, proteomic analysis demonstrated it also restored electron transport chain protein abundances. Metabolomic and lipidomic profiling identified reductions in triacylglycerides, diacylglycerides and acylcarnitines in PAB right ventricles, and these phenotypes were partially ameliorated by ferrostatin-1. Thus, the summation of these data suggested ferroptosis inhibition exerted therapeutic effects by restoring mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism and supporting electron transport chain structure/function.

Second, the effect of glycoprotein-130 (GP130) inhibition was examined in PAB pigs (castrated male pigs, Yorkshire breed, aged 3–5 weeks). Again, this study implemented a translational approach by starting injections of the small-molecule GP130 inhibitor SC144 3 weeks after PAB and treatment continued for 3 weeks [20]. SC144 treatment significantly improved mean±sd RVEF (22±10% in vehicle-treated PAB pigs, 37±6% in the SC144 treated pigs), despite identical afterload. Single nucleus RNAseq (snRNAseq) analysis demonstrated that SC144 blunted the accumulation of pro-inflammatory macrophages, increased cardiomyocyte abundance and rebalanced microvascular gene programmes. Phosphoproteomic and kinase pathway analyses indicated SC144 counteracted pathological induction of the mTORC1 (mechanistic (or mammalian) target of rapamycin complex 1) pathway, which in turn normalised autophagy proteins and improved mitochondrial cristae integrity. Integrated lipidomics, metabolomics and proteomics showed SC144 preserved fatty acid metabolism and restored oxidative-phosphorylation enzyme abundances. Thus, glycoprotein-130 antagonism exerted beneficial effects via pleiotropic mechanisms that included suppression of pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration, reprogrammed cardiomyocyte metabolism and augmented microvascular perfusion. One weakness of this model is the lack of a fibrotic response. Thus, the effects of glycoprotein-130 antagonism on RV fibrosis are not well understood in the pig model, but rodent data suggests this may be an important aspect of its therapeutic effects [21].

This study employed snRNAseq to define the cell-type specific alterations in right heart failure. This analysis highlighted important changes in the RV cellular landscape that promote right heart failure. In particular, PAB pigs had higher relative abundances of macrophages (6–13% of nuclei) and lymphocytes (38%), which supports the hypothesis that inflammation compromises right heart function. In addition, the loss of cardiomyocytes was profound as abundances dropped from 70% of nuclei in the control to 51% of nuclei in PAB animals. Finally, the microvasculature was dysregulated in PAB pigs as both pericytes (4–7%) and endothelial cells (2–4%) increased in relative abundance, and there was evidence of altered pericyte–endothelial gene programme regulation and pericyte–endothelial cell interactions. Thus, these data provided the first unbiased analysis of the cellular landscape of right heart failure, which could open new therapeutic approaches by targeting phenotypes in distinct cell types.

Porcine models of CTEPH

In addition to PAB, a CTEPH pig model of right heart failure, induced by surgically ligating the left PA and performing weekly embolisation of the right lower-lobe PAs, was pioneered by a group of investigators from France [22]. 6 weeks after the initial insult, 2-month-old Large White CTEPH pigs developed significant pulmonary hypertension (PH) (mean±sd PA pressure (PAP): control: 14±2 mmHg, CTEPH: 34±9 mmHg; total pulmonary resistance: control: 364±61 dyn·s·cm−5, CTEPH: 993±572 dyn·s·cm−5). Echocardiography revealed RV remodelling, including RV dilation and hypertrophy, along with compromised RV function with a reduction in tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) (control: 2.0±0.2 cm; CTEPH: 1.4±0.4 cm). Conductance catheter analysis defined impairments in RV–PA coupling (control 1.2±0.2; CTEPH: 0.7±0.2) and RV diastolic dysfunction as quantified by RV Tau (τ) (control: 18±7 ms; CTEPH: 34±4 ms). Finally, RV reserve was depressed in CTEPH piglets as dobutamine infusion at a dose of 5 μg·kg−1·min−1 elicited smaller relative increases in RV stroke volume index (control: 47±5%; CTEPH: 20±5%) and Ees (control: 81±37%; CTEPH: 32±14%). Thus, this study provides an eloquent and detailed physiological characterisation of RV compromise in CTEPH pigs and highlights the model's utility in understanding right heart failure and right heart reserve.

Another group developed a CTEPH model by performing weekly PA embolisation with silk sutures over 3 weeks in uncastrated male (Landrace×Large White) pigs aged 2–3 months [23]. This approach induced robust CTEPH, evidenced by increased mean±sd PAP (sham: 14±2 mmHg versus CTEPH: 38±2 mmHg) and reduced RV function, as measured by pressure–volume loop-derived ejection fraction (sham: 51±3% versus CTEPH: 35±2%) and TAPSE (sham: 24±3 mm versus CTEPH: 15±1 mm). Histological analysis revealed RV cardiomyocyte hypertrophy without a significant increase in fibrosis. At the molecular level, real-time PCR demonstrated shifts in myosin isoform expression along with increased RV tumour necrosis factor expression. Functionally, skinned RV cardiomyocytes exhibited heightened maximal calcium-activated tension but there was no change in myofilament calcium sensitivity. Finally, sarcomere mechanics analysis demonstrated greater passive stiffness.

Although the previous CTEPH studies focused on physiology and targeted molecular changes, multi-omics analyses now enable a deeper understanding of right heart failure. An example includes the use of bulk RNAseq to gain insights into RV dysfunction in the CTEPH piglet model [24]. In this study, the investigators showed Large White male CTEPH piglets had higher mean PAP and total pulmonary resistance, which resulted in RV dilation and dysfunction with significant reductions in echocardiography-determined RV fractional area change and TAPSE. Transcriptomic profiling of RV tissue identified downregulation of calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial transcripts and upregulation of hypertrophy and fibrosis transcripts. These data were corroborated by histological evaluations showing cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and fibrosis. In addition, CTEPH cardiomyocytes had less L-type calcium current, lower expression of calcium voltage-gated channel subunits and smaller, slower calcium transients. Sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium content was also depleted, which was linked to decreased sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2a (SERCA2a) protein levels. Furthermore, ryanodine receptor clusters were smaller and more dispersed, and there was loss of T-tubule regularity and density. These data provided a morphological and molecular explanation for the spatially heterogeneous calcium release. Moreover, RV cardiomyocytes had enhanced store-operated calcium entry mediated by ectopic expression of the long stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1L) isoform. Interestingly, STIM1L was also detectable in RV tissue in human PAH patients. In summary, this work highlights the importance of metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis and impairments in calcium handling in right heart dysfunction.

There is another expert review article on large animal models of CTEPH that highlights both vascular and RV mechanisms by Stam et al. [25].

Porcine models of group 2 PH

In another study, a porcine model of post-capillary PH induced by surgical banding of the common inferior pulmonary vein was evaluated using physiological and targeted molecular approaches [26]. At the completion of the study, the banded animals were categorised into two groups, namely compensated or decompensated remodelling, based on clinical signs of RV failure including respiratory distress and poor weight gain. Both groups demonstrated elevated PAP and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), but decompensated pigs had markedly worse pulmonary vascular disease (mean PAP: 42 (interquartile range (IQR) 39–47) versus 28 (IQR 25–33) mmHg; PVR index: 8.8 (IQR 6.8–12.3) versus 3.0 (IQR 2.1–3.9) WU·m2). Pressure–volume loop analysis revealed that although RV contractility increased in both groups, RV–PA coupling was preserved in compensated pigs but depressed in decompensated pigs (compensated: 0.58 (IQR 0.46–0.69); decompensated: 0.30 (IQR 0.26–0.50)). Diastolic impairments were more pronounced in the decompensated group as the RV end-diastolic pressure–volume slope was steeper (compensated: 0.7 (IQR 0.59–0.81); decompensated: 1.7 (IQR 1.25–1.58)) but RV τ (compensated: 31 (IQR 30–40) ms; decompensated: 29 (IQR 24–37) ms) was not different. Echocardiography reinforced the distinct RV phenotypes; while both PH groups had RV dilation, the decompensated pigs had lower RVEF (compensated: 67 (IQR 63–70)%; decompensated: 54 (IQR 41–61)%) and TAPSE (compensated: 20 (IQR 19–22); decompensated: 15 (IQR 14–18) cm), higher myocardial performance index (compensated: 0.406 (IQR 0.390–0.480); decompensated: 0.590 (IQR 0.460–0.650)), and worse longitudinal strain (compensated: −25 (IQR −25–−22); decompensated: −20 (IQR −21–−18)). Histopathological analysis showed comparable cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and fibrosis. Molecular analyses demonstrated the decompensated animals had SERCA2a downregulation, heightened endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and higher circulating aldosterone levels. Overall, these findings indicated compensated remodelling involved cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, RV fibrosis and mild diastolic changes but preserved RV–PA coupling. On the other hand, decompensated RV failure was characterised by RV–PA uncoupling, impaired calcium handling, ER stress and neurohormonal activation.

In addition to descriptive studies, an intervention approached delineated the effects of sildenafil on RV performance in group 2 PH in 4-week-old, castrated male Large White pigs [27]. To model group 2 PH, investigators surgically banded the confluence of both inferior pulmonary veins. Animals were randomised to sildenafil or placebo beginning 2 weeks post-surgery and then animals were treated for 6 weeks. At study completion, sildenafil-treated pigs exhibited similar mean PAPs compared to placebo (placebo: median 32.0 (IQR 26.3–43.8) mmHg versus sildenafil: median 32.0 (IQR 26.3–43.8) mmHg). However, cardiac MRI demonstrated improved RV function, including higher RVEF (placebo: median 59.5% (IQR 53.5–60.7) versus sildenafil: median 59.5% (IQR 53.5–60.7)) and lower RV end-diastolic volume (placebo: median 46.0 (IQR 39.0–54.0) mL·m−2 versus sildenafil: median 38.2 (IQR 32.8–46.7) mL·m−2). These findings are consistent with a compensated RV phenotype. Functionally, these improvements were accompanied by reduced RV apoptosis. Real-time PCR showed increased expression of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase 1 and 2) and decreased expression of pro-oxidant enzymes (NOX1/2). Taken together, these data suggest that sildenafil exerts RV-enhancing effects in this porcine model, potentially through attenuation of apoptosis mediated by reduced oxidative stress.

Comparison of multiple RV stressors

Next, investigators from Spain performed a comprehensive comparison of Yucatan pigs with three different RV stressors, 1) pulmonary vein banding to model post-capillary PH, 2) aorto–pulmonary shunting to model shunt-associated PH and 3) PAB to model proximal obstruction [28]. Physiological characterisation demonstrated mild PH, RV hypertrophy and RV dilation, but minimal impairments in RV function as cardiac MRI-derived RVEFs were not significantly lower (shunt: 54 (IQR 47–63); PAB: 60.5 (IQR 56.1–67.8); pulmonary vein band: 54.5 (IQR 50.5–59.9)) than controls (62.0 (IQR 59.0–66.5)). Plasma metabolomics revealed pulmonary vein-banded animals had lower arginine and histidine levels, shunt animals had lower arginine, histidine, taurine, purine and higher fatty acids, and PAB animals had higher circulating triglycerides, ceramides and fatty acid esters of hydroxy fatty acids. Serum proteomic analysis indicated activation of the complement system, perturbations in coagulation/haemostasis pathways and alterations in lipid-binding/apolipoprotein abundance in all three RV stressors. An integrated network analysis linking metabolomics and proteomics with imaging and haemodynamic data demonstrated arginine–histidine–purine deficiency, complement activation and free fatty acid accumulation were all associated with RV hypertrophy and dysfunction. Thus, these data nominated metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms as key drivers of RV remodelling and impaired function.

Understanding right atrial dysfunction in PAB swine

While most of the molecular work in large animals is focused on the RV, the compromised right atrium in PAB pigs was recently evaluated using a combination of cardiac MRI, histological analysis and snRNAseq [29]. In this study, PAB pigs exhibited right atrial compromise with right atrial dilation, lower right atrial emptying fraction (control: 60%; PAB: 35%) and right atrial cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Mechanistic insights inferred from snRNAseq data suggest there is an increase in abundance of pro-inflammatory resident macrophages and more cardiomyocytes with compromised ER homeostasis and mitochondrial function. Thus, these data nominate mechanisms that could be targeted to augment right atrial function, which appear to be distinct from those in the right ventricle.

Although porcine models offer many advantages for translational investigations, there are limitations that must be acknowledged. First, some studies using male pigs include castrated animals because animals obtained from agricultural sources are castrated shortly after birth. Second, pigs have a species-specific cardiomyocyte polynucleation phenotype in which stressed cardiomyocytes can have as many as 16 nuclei per cell [30]. Third, the poor antibody cross-reactivity limits analyses using traditional biochemical approaches. Fourth, pigs have a higher basal body temperature than humans [31], which could have implications for protein stability. Finally, the ratio of wall thickness between the left and right ventricles is significantly higher in pigs than in humans [32]. Many human proteins have porcine orthologues with sequence variations resulting in poor antibody cross-reactivity in some cases. Proteins often have similar functions, but may have different sequences, signalling or half-life.

Ovine model of RV failure due to PAB

Investigators at Vanderbilt University have developed and characterised an ovine model of right heart failure through the ligation of the left PA and gradual banding of the main PA (figure 2). In their first report, a single animal was evaluated. Over about 7 weeks, the animal maintained a compensated phenotype despite very high RV systolic pressures (>90 mmHg), but heart rate and respiratory rate increased. However, lab markers of renal function and perfusion remained mostly stable. Then, between weeks 7 and 10, decompensation began to appear as mixed-venous oxygen saturation dropped, creatinine rose significantly and by week 10 the animal developed subcutaneous oedema. Under anaesthesia, the animal suffered sudden cardiorespiratory collapse and died. Post mortem analysis found large pleural effusions, ascites, RV dilation with eccentric hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis and congestive changes in lung and liver, mimicking human right heart failure [33].

Extracorporeal support improves end-organ function and permits ambulation in an ovine model of right heart failure induced by progressive pulmonary artery (PA) banding. ECMO: extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; RV: right ventricle.

Next, the investigators examined how implantation of a compact extracorporeal support system in a right-atrium to left-atrium configuration, supported the failing right ventricle and preserved left heart filling in their model. In six sheep, the circuit (delivering 2.8 L·min−1 flow) ran for 2–7 days and three animals completed the full 7-day support duration. Extracorporeal support restored acid/base status and hemodynamic parameters and lowered right-ventricular systolic pressure. Impressively, two sheep ambulated while receiving extracorporeal support [34].

This ovine model provides a robust physiological characterisation of right heart failure, but some limitations remain. For instance, sheep have a lower heart weight to body weight ratio than humans (humans: 5 g·kg−1; sheep: 3 g·kg−1 [35]). Sheep have a slightly faster heart rate than humans ranging from 60 to 120 bpm [36]. Finally, like pigs, sheep have a higher resting body temperature [31] and antibody cross-reactivity can be a challenge.

Bovine model of PAH

Investigators from Colorado pioneered a bovine model of PH-associated RV compromise by exposing male Holstein calves to hypoxia for 14 days (figure 3). This model resulted in robust PH (RV end-systolic pressure: control: 45.8 mmHg (8.7); hypoxia: 118.3 mmHg (25.2)), but only mild RV impairments with no significant change in mean±sd RVEF (control: 60.5±11.5%; hypoxia: 53.814.7%) or RV Tau (control: 26.5±18.0; hypoxia: 26.5±5.6) as quantified by pressure–volume loop analysis. Transcriptomic analysis of RV specimens revealed activation of the YAP (Yes-associated protein)–TAZ (transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif) mechanotransduction pathway, extracellular matrix remodelling, inflammation and angiogenesis pathways were activated. Proteomic and immunohistochemistry data confirmed these findings, showing myocyte hypertrophy, extracellular matrix reorganisation, immune cell invasion and endothelial proliferation in the RV tissue. Altogether, the data suggested coordinated remodelling among cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and immune cells maintained RV function under severe pressure overload [37].

Hypoxic calves are an emerging translational model of compensated right heart remodelling. ECM: extracellular matrix; RV: right ventricle; YAP: Yes-associated protein.

Investigators evaluated sarcomeric remodelling in a bovine model of RV pressure overload by assessing both total protein abundance and phosphorylation status of multiple proteins [38]. Total levels of α-myosin, desmin and troponin T were unchanged, whereas myosin-binding protein C abundance increased. Interestingly, there was no evidence of myosin heavy chain isoform switching. In contrast, post-translational regulation revealed more pronounced abnormalities. Phosphorylation of troponin I at the protein kinase A site Ser22/23 decreased, along with levels of phosphorylated troponin T and myosin light chain 2, while desmin phosphorylation remained unaffected. RV extracts also showed elevated protein phosphatase 1 expression without changes in protein phosphatase 2, potentially contributing to the observed sarcomeric changes. These molecular changes were associated with modest functional impairments including reduced calcium sensitivity. However, maximal calcium-saturated force normalised to cross-sectional area, total force generation, and cooperativity were preserved. Despite the presence of severe PH (mean±sd PA systolic pressure 137±8 mmHg), the RV phenotype was not directly assessed by imaging. As discussed above, this bovine model has a preserved RV phenotype, which highlights the need for additional large animal studies to define sarcomeric remodelling in the setting of severe RV compromise.

Again, the bovine heart has important anatomical differences when compared to a human heart. The bovine heart has Ossa cordis or a cardiac bone [39] and the resting heart rate in newborn calves ranges between 100 and 140 bpm [31]. In addition, cows also have a higher basal body temperature [31] than humans and available validated antibodies are limited.

Canine model of RV failure induced by CTEPH

Finally, a recent manuscript described a novel beagle model of CTEPH and defined how it induced right heart dysfunction and remodelling (figure 4). In this study, Mulchrone et al. [40] developed a canine model of CTEPH by repeatedly injecting microspheres into the PAs of four male beagles, which gradually elevated mean±sd PAP (baseline: 17±2.0 mmHg, CTEPH: 34±6.0 mmHg) over a time of approximately 4 months [40]. At completion of the study, the animals had RV dilation (baseline right ventricular end-diastolic volume (RVEDV): 2.7±0.1 mL·kg−1, CTEPH RVEDV: 3.0±0.02 mL·kg−1) and compromised RV function demonstrated by a reduction in RVEF, cardiac index and estimated RV-PA coupling. Thus, this model recapitulated key functional hallmarks of right heart compromise, making it a valuable platform for studying disease mechanisms and testing therapeutics.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) beagles exhibit right heart compromise. MRI: magnetic resonance imaging; PA: pulmonary artery; RV: right ventricle.

Again, the canine heart is distinct from the human heart. First, the canine heart has a higher ratio of heart weight to body weight (6.95 g·kg−1) [35]. Second, the inferoseptal recess is lacking in the canine heart, which affects the conduction system [41]. Third, there are less pulmonary veins in the canine heart.

Conclusions

In summary, we highlight the now burgeoning literature of large animal models of right heart compromise that has provided deeper physiological, cellular and molecular insights into the pathobiology of right heart failure. Hopefully, the continued implementation of these more human-relevant models will help us cross the translational valley of death and bring right heart failure directed therapeutics to our patients.

Questions for future research

While there is a rapidly emerging literature describing physiology and the molecular underpinnings of right heart failure in multiple large-animal species, there are many important unknowns that remain. Here we outline specific questions that we hope will be answered in the coming years. Which animal model best recapitulates specific phenotypes such as pathological RV inflammation, fibrosis, metabolic derangements or other molecular mechanisms of human RV dysfunction? As we continue to gain insights from multi-omic profiling of human PAH RV tissue [42, 43], we will need to compare the molecular biology and biochemical findings from each of these models to delineate which species most accurately models different aspects of human right heart failure. This will be important as we continue to examine interventions targeting distinct pathogenic drivers of right heart failure. In young Hanford pigs, the heart is 0.4% of the body weight whereas in older pigs (1.2–4 years) the heart drops to 0.28–0.39% of body weight. In 23-month-old Yucatan breed pigs, the heart is 0.45–0.52% of body weight [50].

Thus, a comparison across breeds may be useful to gain additional insights into the mechanisms that protect the right heart in the setting of pressure overload.

Does chronicity of RV compromise alter the cellular and molecular phenotypes observed? In human RV failure from PH, the disease course is generally over years, but nearly all animal studies occur in weeks to months. Chronicity may be an important variable to evaluate in the future as humans generally have disease for years before an intervention can be performed.

Does the site of the lesion alter the RV phenotype? As discussed above, many investigators use PAB strategies to induce RV compromise. While this approach is certainly effective, it does not model most types of PH. In nearly all types of PH, the lesions are distal and there can be heterogeneous vascular remodelling. Interestingly, the CTEPH literature suggest proximal obstruction incurs a great load on the RV and compromises the RV more than distal CTEPH [46]. Therefore, the PAB model may better reflect proximal CTEPH rather than distal disease observed in other aetiologies of PH.

How does biological sex modulate the molecular biology and physiology in large animal models? Female sex is associated with superior RV adaption in humans [47, 48], but a robust comparison of male and female RV biology in large animals has not been performed. A future study evaluating these concepts could provide novel insights into the biology of sexually divergent RV phenotypes and nominate sex-specific pathways to enhance right heart function.

Several datasets of different omics approaches are now available in multiple species of large animals. However, the use of more sophisticated approaches such as spatial transcriptomics, ATACseq (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing), spatial proteomics and spatial metabolomics could be performed to define specific niches in the RV that drive RV failure.

Do RV-directed therapies augment end-organ function in large animal models? The lethality of right heart failure is driven by end-organ compromise [1, 49], and thus future studies could include a molecular, cellular and physiological evaluation of important organs including the liver, kidney and brain.

Another variable that needs to be considered in these studies is the age of the animal. Many studies using large animals are using paediatric or juvenile animals to facilitate housing space and costs in a research setting. Not only do age-related changes in development need to be considered, but also that in younger animals the heart is a greater percent of body weight thus potentially not working as hard to perfuse a larger body. In young Hanford pigs, the heart is 0.4% of the body weight whereas in older pigs (1.2–4 years) the heart drops to 0.28–0.39% of body weight. In 23-month-old Yucatan breed pigs, the heart is 0.45–0.52% of body weight [50].
