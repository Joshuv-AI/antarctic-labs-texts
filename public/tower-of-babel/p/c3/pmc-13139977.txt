The VAccess program: codesign and development of a web-based tool to address barriers to care for vascular anomalies
Authors: Bryan A. Sisk, Stephanie Milam, Christine Bereitschaft, Lauren Baker, Inez Y. Oh, Sally Cohen-Cutler, Anna M. Kerr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•VAccess is an innovative web-based platform that addresses many of the barriers to care for patients with VAs.•VAccess gives PCCs timely access to VA education and connects them with VA experts across the United States.

Introduction

Vascular anomalies (VAs) result from maldevelopment of blood and lymphatic vessels. Congenital or developmental VAs are present at birth or become apparent during childhood and produce profound lifelong complications, including acute and chronic pain, bleeding, thrombosis, infection, deformity, disability, and death.1, 2, 3 Because of the rarity of the condition and inconsistent terminologies used to describe VAs by physicians less familiar with VAs, patients are often misdiagnosed, and some carry an incorrect diagnosis (often hemangioma) until they reach multidisciplinary expert care.4,5 Likewise, many patients are not diagnosed until later in life, despite most VAs being present at birth or shortly thereafter, and some patients never receive a diagnosis.6 VAs often require combinations of medical and interventional therapies that must be adapted to each patient’s unique needs.1, 2, 3 Patients who are undiagnosed or misdiagnosed may experience delays in care or receive unnecessary, invasive interventions.7,8

Patients with VAs experience many barriers to receiving expert-informed VA care. Because of the rarity of the disease, primary care clinicians (PCCs) are often unfamiliar with VAs, leading to delayed or absent referrals to VA experts.7,8 Furthermore, care coordination for patients with VAs is challenging because these diseases require management by multiple specialties. Even after referral, patients may have to wait >6 months and travel hundreds of miles to access expert care.6,8 Nearly all VA centers are housed in children’s hospitals, and a study from 2023 showed that 13 US states lacked multidisciplinary VA clinics.9 Adults with VAs experience greater barriers because of a shortage of adult VA physicians with sufficient interest, knowledge, and willingness to care for them.10,11 Rurality creates an additional barrier to finding clinicians with experience managing VAs.8 We previously found that improving knowledge among patients and their PCCs about VAs might improve their ability to navigate the health care system and access needed care.6

Barriers to expert-informed care can lead to misdiagnosis, incorrect treatment, and poor outcomes in patients with VAs. Addressing these barriers is essential to improving VA health outcomes. In this study, we describe the development of an innovative web-based collaborative clinician network designed to support PCCs in managing care for patients with VAs, called the Vascular Anomaly Care Access (VAccess) program. We also created resources for patients and caregivers that are housed within this same platform.

Methods

The research team engaged PCCs using user-centered design methods to develop VAccess.12, 13, 14 Through 23 semistructured interviews, 7 participatory codesign sessions, and 10 usability testing sessions, 3 design phases were completed: initial exploration, discovery, and prototyping (Table 1).13 The initial exploration and discovery phases focused on clinicians’ experiences and goals related to caring for patients with VAs or other rare diagnoses as well as tool-related needs and desired features. During the prototyping phase, the VAccess tool was iteratively developed and refined. Study phases

During the codesign process, the web-based platform was developed in collaboration with HICAPPS, a software development company with expertise in creating health-related digital tools. HICAPPS relied on a methodology called “extreme programming,” which includes frequent releases in short development cycles, and is intended to improve productivity and adaptability to customer needs.15 Once a working prototype was available, usability sessions were conducted to elicit feedback and further refine the tool.

Participants and recruitment

For all development phases, PCCs (physicians, nurse practitioners, or physician assistants) in pediatrics, family medicine, and internal medicine were invited via email in collaboration with the Washington University Pediatric and Ambulatory Research Consortium and the Missouri Practice-Based Innovations Network. We also contacted PCCs of patients with VAs who received care at the Washington University VAs Center and shared study details with clinicians in our personal and professional networks. All PCCs were eligible to participate, and no website design knowledge or experience was required. However, we purposively sampled for representation from rural, urban, and suburban practices; academic, private, or group practices, and Veterans Affairs facilities. After providing informed consent, participants completed a brief online demographic survey and then participated in a phone or Zoom interview (phases 1 and 2) or a Zoom-based design or usability session (phase 3). Participants received $50 USD per interview and $100 per design session.

During the design process, an expert steering committee also provided additional feedback and guidance. The committee included 3 primary care physicians, 1 advocacy group director, and 14 VA experts, including 12 physicians (representing hematology and oncology, interventional radiology, genetics, dermatology, surgery, and otolaryngology), 1 nurse practitioner, and 1 nurse coordinator.

Semistructured interviews

Semistructured interviews were conducted between August 2024 and January 2025. All interviews were conducted by B.A.S., a pediatric oncologist and VA specialist with extensive experience conducting qualitative interviews. During the interview, the researcher presented a hypothetical patient with a complex VA, prompting clinicians to discuss how they would approach care for the patient, including the potential barriers and challenges. Next, the interviewer described plans to build web-based resources for clinicians and asked participants to brainstorm useful features, potential barriers, and facilitators of implementation. The interview guide is available in the supplemental Materials.

Participatory design sessions

Participatory design sessions occurred between April 2025 and July 2025. We invited 7 clinicians to participate in each workshop. Following previously published methods,16 the facilitation guide was based on the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology17 and prompted discussion about care coordination needs, possible components to include in the tool, and how potential end users might interact with VAccess. The conversations addressed features needed to support the following anticipated functions: (1) asking for expert input on complicated patients, (2) identifying referral centers, and (3) providing educational materials. However, clinicians were encouraged to identify, prioritize, and refine any desired components.

Usability testing

Two rounds of usability testing were completed between July 2025 and December 2025. Ten clinicians (5 per phase) participated in one-on-one Zoom usability sessions, during which they accessed a VAccess prototype or the final VAccess platform and completed a series of tasks while the researcher asked probing questions. After completing the tasks, participants responded to open-ended questions informed by the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire, assessing their perceptions of the tool’s usefulness, information quality, and interface quality.18

Data collection and analyses

Phase 1 interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed, and uploaded to Dedoose, a collaborative qualitative analysis platform. Three coders (A.M.K., L.B., and S.M.) conducted thematic analysis.19 During the first round of coding, all 3 coders descriptively coded 20% of the transcripts. The team then met to discuss initial categories and develop a preliminary codebook. During the second round, the coders independently coded 3 additional transcripts to refine these categories. Upon finalizing the codebook, 2 rounds of coding were completed during which the researchers independently coded transcripts, reviewed each other’s code application, marked disagreements, and met to resolve discrepancies by discussion. The results from the analysis were used to inform a prototype mock-up for the initial participatory design sessions.

The first 2 design sessions in phase 2 were facilitated using Mural,20 a visual collaboration platform that allows users to interact and brainstorm in real-time. During the session, participants added or rearranged sticky notes to brainstorm or prioritize features (Figure 1). The moderators used probing questions to explore participants’ ideas. Once a functional early-stage version of the tool was available, design sessions involved displaying the VAccess website over Zoom and inviting participants to complete tasks and refine features as they were built. After each session, participant verbal comments, Zoom chat comments, Mural sticky notes, and researcher notes were merged into a design summary and analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis.21,22 The research team met weekly to review the session summary, provide instructions for the tool developers, and determine activities for the next design session. This iterative process was repeated until a near-complete prototype was created and was ready for final usability testing.Figure 1.Screenshot of mural board from design workshops. Previsit and postvisit planning involved self-education about the disease and its impact on primary care needs (eg, vaccinations), communication with other clinicians (eg, curbside conversations with specialists), and requesting patient records. During initial visits with new patients, clinicians’ goals included taking a detailed history, assessing the urgency of symptoms, and documenting exam findings with clinical photos. A common goal was to make referrals to appropriate subspecialists, especially for patients with multisystem involvement. When presented with a clinical photo and history of a hypothetical patient with a complex VA during the interview, clinicians indicated that they would refer to varied specialists, including dermatology, orthopedics, plastic surgery, vascular medicine, vascular surgery, nephrology, rheumatology, endocrinology, hematology, or oncology.

The PCCs’ involvement in the initial evaluation of patients with rare disorders ranged from initiating workup with guidance from a specialist or peer, initiating workup independently (without specialist guidance), or deferring all workup to specialists. Many PCCs felt confident ordering routine blood work and imaging, with ultrasonography often being cited as the first step. However, they explained that they may delay workup in nonurgent cases to avoid unnecessary testing. Once a patient establishes care with a subspecialist, PCCs mostly agreed that they wanted to be involved in ongoing care. Most clinicians expressed the importance of being aware of specialized care to be better prepared to discuss it with patients and families and assist with local care coordination.

The current gaps and challenges related to care for patients with rare diseases are centered around system-related, patient/family-related, and clinician-related issues. At the systems level, clinicians cited barriers such as long wait times and travel distances, limited health care resources, insurance barriers, and electronic health record challenges. Patient- and family-related barriers included financial burdens and a lack of supportive resources, including social support and time off work. Clinician-related barriers included a lack of knowledge and awareness about VAs and the system of care, limited engagement from subspecialists or PCCs’ hesitation to engage in rare disease care, and limited time and capacity. A lack of interpersonal relationships between PCCs and subspecialists was also considered a challenge in rare disease care, where clinicians found it more difficult to engage in informal conversations about patients with complex conditions when they did not have a network of colleagues they interacted with regularly and established familiar or trusting relationships with, especially if they were not near a major academic center.

From these interviews, optimal characteristics of the proposed VAccess tool were identified. The PCCs’ desired content related to (1) referrals, (2) evaluation plans and next steps, (3) education, and (4) resources for patients and families. Desired information about referrals included a directory of specialists and multidisciplinary centers and an explanation of the referral process. They also requested information about the ideal first (or next) steps for initial evaluation and management, including imaging, laboratories, and treatment they can initiate before a patient is seen by the subspecialist and a list of red flags or complications warranting immediate attention. The educational information they requested included common terms and definitions, pathophysiology, disease progression and expectations, screening and monitoring guidelines, acute management, and a photo gallery with different presentations and skin tones. They also requested resources they could share with patients and caregivers, including plain-language education, support, and advocacy materials.

Another preferred feature was the ability to communicate with VA specialists. Clinicians described the advantages and disadvantages of both synchronous and asynchronous communication. Asynchronous communication was considered to be less burdensome; however, efficient response times were important. Desired features in an asynchronous tool included the ability to securely send the details of a case, clinical photos, and specific questions to get input from a VA specialist about the diagnosis, next steps, and appropriate referral. Many clinicians suggested the ability to request a phone call or virtual consult for urgent scenarios or as a follow-up to the initial inquiry. They also indicated that the website should have an efficient search function, be user-friendly, prioritize privacy and security, and include content that is credible, concise, and easy to read.

After semistructured interviews, results and key features to build into the VAccess prototype were identified and used to develop participatory design sessions for further revisions and refinements.

Phase 3: prototyping

Participatory design sessions

Seven codesign sessions were conducted with 32 PCCs. The first 2 design sessions occurred after the first meeting with the steering committee and before the first meeting with the software developers. The remaining design sessions reflected an iterative process of gathering user feedback, sending feedback to the developers, receiving revised features, and gathering user input on the revised tool. Design session results are summarized below. A detailed version is available in the supplemental Materials.

Design session 1

The first design session (n = 7) focused on how clinicians can access and use VAccess, including account creation and clinician verification processes. Nearly all participants agreed that the ability to send clinical questions to VA experts should be restricted to verified clinical users. Account creation was not considered a barrier to use because it is a common practice on other frequently used clinical websites. Clinicians agreed that providing basic information about name, email address, practice location, and National Provider Identifier number would be reasonable.

Design session 2

The second session (n = 6) focused on desirable features, navigation, and layout. The most substantial design impact was that most clinicians agreed that users should be able to submit a clinical question before their clinician account is verified. The clinicians also requested the ability to upload clinical photos and request that a specialist contact them by phone. Regarding the login process, clinicians expressed a desire for features such as single sign-on using existing credentials (ie, Google or Microsoft). Clinicians’ suggestions for the homepage included prominently displaying patient education materials, clinician resources, and contact information for specialists. They also introduced the idea of adding a discussion forum for clinicians.

Design sessions 3 and 4

An early-stage version of VAccess was presented during design sessions 3 (n = 5) and four (n = 6). Clinicians were invited to use the “Ask a Specialist” tool and provide feedback. The clinicians’ feedback in session 3 led to changing the language from “primary care clinician” to “healthcare professional” to be more inclusive. They also suggested simplifying navigation from the home page to the account creation page and “Ask a Specialist” form. They recommended providing more explicit descriptions of the purpose of the tool, who will be receiving and responding to questions, and what to expect for a response time. During session 4, clinicians suggested the ability to edit questions after submitting.

Design sessions 5 and 6

During sessions 5 (n = 6) and 6 (n = 4), clinicians provided input on the feature designed to help clinicians find VA centers (VACs). The most significant design impact was making the navigational icons larger, changing the search options, and minimizing visual clutter on the page. The clinicians recommended adding more details about VACs, such as specialties represented, insurances accepted, and patient age ranges, especially for pediatric centers that will also see adult patients.

Design session 7

During the final design session (n = 3), clinicians received a link to the prototype and were asked to submit an “Ask a Specialist” request and provide feedback. This process allowed our team to identify barriers or pain points during task completion, such as unexpected error messages and features that were available but not readily apparent (eg, how to agree to the terms of service and how to attach a photo).

Usability testing

In the first round of usability testing, 5 clinicians accessed a VAccess prototype that included all available tool features but had placeholders for content such as educational documents. Based on the clinicians’ feedback in round 1, revisions were made in 5 areas, which included (1) naming conventions of menu items and navigational content; (2) “Ask a Specialist” form fields, saved drafts, and confirmation messages; (3) “Find a VA Center” search results and filters; (4) default views when entering and exiting web pages; and (5) user account display names.

In the second round, 5 clinicians used the final VAccess tool. Based on clinicians’ feedback in round 2, minor issues, including typographical errors, character limits, and hyperlink functionality–related issues, were resolved. A list of features for the tool developers to add in a future build was also generated, including adding Spanish-language resources, improving the “Find a VAC” search functionality, streamlining account creation, adding profile editing privileges, incorporating clinical images, and repositioning navigational buttons.

Overall, users were satisfied with the VAccess tool and agreed that it was useful for PCCs. Strengths included credibility, ease of use, and a well-defined purpose. The clinicians valued the ability to message specialists directly. All clinicians indicated that they would be highly likely to use the tool in the future.

The VAccess program

The VAccess program (vaccess.org) is a web-based tool designed to help health care professionals (HCPs) who care for patients with VAs. Key features include educational materials, a list of multidisciplinary VACs, and the ability to ask a network of specialists for guidance on VA care.

The educational materials hub includes distinct VA resources developed for either HCPs or patients and families. The HCP resources include clinical summaries of VA diagnoses that our research team developed, in collaboration with the steering committee, based on feedback we received during our qualitative needs assessment. These summaries provide an overview of the diagnosis (eg, complex lymphatic anomalies), key features and clinical findings, diagnostic approaches (imaging, biopsies, laboratories, etc), complications and acute concerns, treatment options, referrals, and screening and surveillance. These materials were developed by synthesizing information from relevant peer-reviewed literature, with final approval by B.A.S., who is a VA specialist. The HCP resources also include the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies glossary of terms and VAs classification. The patients and caregivers portion of the educational materials hub includes external links to information about VA diagnoses and syndromes available on hospital or professional organization websites. To vet these materials, B.A.S. reviewed available materials from multiple academic institutions and selected the most accurate and comprehensive information.

The “Find a VAC” tool includes an interactive database of 88 VACs across the United States. The research team compiled the list of centers by searching children’s hospital websites and directories from the International Society for the Study of VAs and American Society of Pediatric Hematology/Oncology and called or emailed each center to verify program offerings, age limits, and contact information. To locate VACs in the United States, users can select the list or map view and can narrow their results by city, state, ZIP code, or institution name to find centers nearby. The tool also indicates the distance in miles from the user if they have allowed the website to use their current location. The results can be filtered by the age of patients seen at the VAC, which was an important feature for clinicians who care for adults with VAs, who may need to be seen at a pediatric institution. When users click on the expandable card of any VAC, they receive more information including the street address, phone number, and email address (if available) for the VAC, an external link to the VAC-specific website, an external link to Google Maps with directions, and a label with the ages of patients seen including a note with any caveats regarding adult patients (eg, “Adult patients up to age 24” or “Adult patients seen on case by case basis.”).

The “Ask a Specialist” tool (Figure 2) allows users to submit clinical questions. Users can provide deidentified information, including the patient’s age, chief complaint, clinical case details (eg, relevant history, physical examination findings, and diagnostic studies), and a list of questions for the specialist. They can also upload deidentified images or other documents, such as test results, and indicate if they would like a specialist to contact them by phone. Users must confirm that all patient information has been properly deidentified and that proper consent has been obtained. To ensure privacy, VAccess.org is housed within Washington University’s secure environment. In addition, the research staff review each submission and modify/delete protected health information, if present. Immediately after submission, the clinician receives a disclaimer explaining that the responses are intended to be educational rather than serving as clinical consultations and is informed that the typical response time is 1 to 2 business days. Users can find their submitted requests in the “My Requests” folder of their dashboard, where they can see the status and edit or delete their request until it has been assigned to a specialist. Each request includes a discussion forum where users can see and respond to specialist responses (see the supplemental Materials). Discussion

Using user-centered design methods, we developed the VAccess program, an innovative web-based platform that aims to improve timely referrals and care coordination for patients with VAs. To our knowledge, this is the first tool of its kind in VAs and advances the field by transitioning from descriptive communication research to the development of a tailored intervention that intends to improve communication and care. Previous research has documented the obstacles patients with VAs face in accessing expert care because of the rarity of their condition.8 Like many rare disease contexts, a central challenge in the case of VAs is that PCCs have limited exposure to, knowledge of, and familiarity with VAs and their system of care.23,24 VAccess can help address this challenge by providing education for clinicians, helping them locate multidisciplinary VACs, and allowing them to submit clinical questions directly to VA specialists who can provide further information and guidance. Asynchronous specialist electronic consultation programs, such as VAccess, can help PCCs by improving their referral decision-making, increasing their confidence in care management, and assisting their care coordination efforts.25,26

A particular strength of VAccess is its ability to connect clinicians with a network of VA specialists from multiple subspecialties and geographic locations. Many existing electronic consultation programs are health-system specific, connecting PCCs to subspecialists within their own network.25, 26, 27, 28 This model is not sufficient for improving VA care, given that many health systems do not have VA experts.9 This model also prohibits clinicians who are not affiliated with a health system from accessing much-needed resources. Another challenge with an institution-specific model is that most expert VA care is offered in pediatric institutions,9,10 meaning that there are PCCs caring for adults with VAs who are unable to access care because of age restrictions. Thus, VAccess offers a way to overcome several health-system barriers to VA care and creates a nationwide network of communication for patients with VAs and the clinicians who care for them. However, we must acknowledge the potential for the “Ask a Specialist” tool to increase specialist workload. We are currently expanding the VAN to increase the number of VA specialists available to respond to “Ask a Specialist” queries.

Overall, VAccess is a tool that was codeveloped with clinicians and designed to be a sustainable and adaptive platform that allows for ongoing integration of new features, resources, articles, and tools, enabling it to adapt to user needs and evolve alongside advancements in the field. Our next steps are to share VAccess with clinicians. We plan to disseminate through primary care professional societies, rural health organizations, and children’s hospital primary care networks. We also intend to request that VAccess be included as a recommended resource on clinical sites, such as UpToDate. Finally, we will rely on Google search engine optimization and sponsored advertisements to ensure VAccess is reached by Google searches.

The next phases of website development will include expanding the patient and caregiver hub with resources through participatory design sessions with patients, caregivers, and patient advocacy groups to identify how VAccess may support patients and families. Engaging leaders from patient advocacy groups is particularly necessary to ensure that VAccess serves as a harmonious resource and is not perceived to be redundant or competitive. We anticipate that VAccess can serve as a model for other rare diseases that face similar care and communication challenges.

Limitations

Our methods and final tool are not without limitations. Our tool and resources are currently available only in English, which limits the accessibility of the tool for clinicians who are fluent in other languages or who are seeking resources for patients who do not speak English. Furthermore, more studies are needed to disseminate this resource, characterize its use and uptake, and assess end users’ experiences and preferences.

Conclusions

The VAccess program is an innovative web-based platform designed to help PCCs and other HCPs in caring for patients with VAs. VAccess was designed using rigorous user-centered design methods that engaged clinician stakeholders from initial exploration to final prototyping. By providing education about VAs, helping clinicians make appropriate referrals, and connecting clinicians directly with VA experts for guidance and support, the tool addresses longstanding challenges in providing care for patients with VAs.
