Current and future applications of local field potential-guided programming for Parkinson’s disease with the Percept™ rechargeable neurostimulator
Authors: Weerawat Saengphatrachai, Joohi Jimenez-Shahed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeep brain stimulation (DBS) has been established as an effective neuromodulatory treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD) with motor complications or refractory tremor. Various DBS devices with unique technology platforms are commercially available and deliver continuous, open-loop stimulation. The Percept™ family of neurostimulators use BrainSense™ technology with five key features to sense local field potentials while stimulating, enabling integration of physiologic data into the routine practice of DBS programming. The newly approved Percept™ rechargeable RC implantable pulse generator offers a smaller, thinner design and reduced recharge time with prolonged recharge interval. In this review, we describe the application of local field potential sensing-based programming in PD and highlight the potential future clinical implementation of closed-loop stimulation using the Percept™ RC implantable pulse generator.

Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has been established as an effective neuromodulatory treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD) with motor complications or refractory tremor [1]. The mainstay of treatment is oral levodopa but as the disease progresses despite this treatment, PD patients begin to suffer from increasingly troublesome motor complications of the therapy including fluctuations (i.e., wearing off effects) and levodopa-induced dyskinesia that significantly impact their health-related quality of life [2]. DBS is indicated for managing those motor complications in advanced PD.

DBS devices consist of three components: electrodes, extension wires and implanted pulse generators (IPGs) [3]. Additional equipment needed are a clinician programmer and a patient controller [4]. Clinician programmers are used to program the DBS device through access to a variety of software capabilities, while the patient controller allows the patient and/or care provider to interact with their therapy in a number of ways (e.g., adjusting stimulation, enabling safe imaging modes or turning therapy on or off) [3,4]. When a rechargeable IPG is being used, additional charging hardware is required [4].

There are three DBS device platforms approved for commercial use in the United States. Each has robust clinical trials evidence supporting its use in eligible patients with PD, with fairly consistent findings of reduced OFF time, increased ON time, and reduced dyskinesia [5–7]. Stimulation in general offers more favorable results compared with continuing best medical therapy [5,8–10], and improves quality of life [9–11]. DBS platforms can be differentiated based on their programming features, which include elements such as integrated patient-specific anatomy for image-guided programming, integrated brain sensing for physiology-guided programming, methods of effecting current steering, or remote programming capabilities [3,12–14]. In this review, we will explore the current rechargeable DBS devices available on the market or under development, and explain the clinical correlation of local field potentials (LFPs) in PD. We will then highlight the features of the Percept™ RC IPG including its key BrainSense™ functions, and explore clinical concepts related to these functions that can be leveraged by clinicians to optimize DBS outcomes. Lastly, we will explore adaptive DBS and its potential for enhancing the clinical practice of DBS in PD.

Overview of the field

Eligibility for DBS therapy is usually determined by a series of preoperative assessments [15]. DBS can be implanted bilaterally, and can be adjusted over time as PD symptoms progress or change. The most common targets include the subthalamic nucleus (STN) or globus pallidus interna (GPi), both of which can improve motor complications, allow medication reduction and improve quality of life [16]. Thalamic DBS is less commonly performed due to its limitation in treating only tremor. The choice of DBS target that is used to treat an individual PD patient may depend on various factors and is beyond the scope of this review [15]. The choice of DBS device brand that is implanted may depend on a number of factors including patient preference, clinician preference, or institutional factors such as purchasing contracts or device approvals [17]. Similarly, the use of a rechargeable device may also vary depending on patient characteristics (e.g., age, disease being treated, access to the treatment center, comfort with or preference regarding charging procedures, prior experience with non rechargeable IPGs).

Overview of implantable pulse generators (IPGs)

IPGs associated with DBS devices contain both the power source for the delivery of electrical current into the deep brain structures as well as a power module, a central processing unit and program memory, as well as a microprocessor managing all the device’s functions, including activation/deactivation, internal diagnostics and communication with external devices [18]. DBS pulses are generally delivered as square waves continuously over a 24 h period [19]. The DBS devices and their associated software platforms that are either commercially available or under development have been described elsewhere [3,20]. The Percept™ PC IPG (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN 55432, USA) was the first commercially available bi-directional interface capable of recording brain signals in the form of LFPs and delivering stimulation for DBS in movement disorders and became available in 2020 [21]. The Percept™ RC (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN 55432, USA) received a Conformité Européenne (CE) mark in December 2023, then US FDA approval in January 2024 [22,23].

All commercially available DBS systems in the United States now have both primary cell (PC) IPGs and rechargeable (RC) IPGs, which confer advantages such as reduced size, increased battery longevity, reduced need for battery replacements and prolonged therapy continuation (Supplementary Table S1). Vercise Genus™ and Vercise Gevia™ from Boston Scientific® are designed with an individual power source to each electrode contact, the so called “multiple independent current control”, giving clinicians more options for steering the current [3,7]. DirectSTIM™ by Aleva Neurotherapeutics has recently launched quadripolar leads with segmental contacts in all levels [24,25] in which the current can also be delivered individually to each contact with multiple independent power sources [25]. The programming platforms designed for Abbott®‘s Liberta RC™, PIN Medical®'s G102R and SceneRay®'s Aaxon RND™ are capable of remote programming, and G102R can also be used for variable-frequency stimulation [20,26]. Variable-frequency stimulation delivers both high and low frequency stimulation in a varying pattern that can help improve axial symptoms compared with continuous high frequency stimulation [27,28]. Lastly, the AlphaDBS® from Newronika®, and the Picostim™, the first skull-mounted DBS for treatment of PD from Bioinduction®, have both LFP sensing and closed-loop programming capabilities and are currently undergoing clinical trials [29–32].

Overview of LFPs in PD

LFPs represent the extracellular electrical currents discharged simultaneously from multiple neighboring neuronal synapses, and have been increasingly studied in PD [33]. Motor symptoms of PD in the hypodopaminergic state are associated with excessive synchronization of neuronal oscillations in the basal ganglia [34,35], particularly in the beta frequency range (13–30 Hz), often including periods of long beta bursts [36,37]. Beta bursts are transient occurrences of exaggerated beta band oscillatory activity whose temporal dynamics are altered in the Parkinsonian state and correlate best to rigidity and bradykinesia [36–40]. This beta frequency activity is suppressible after treatment with levodopa or DBS and recurs with discontinuation of those treatments [35,41–43]. The highest beta activity is associated with the dorsolateral sensorimotor area of STN where DBS electrodes are placed, while the highest alpha activity (8–12 Hz) is localized to the ventromedial limbic area of STN that connects with the premotor frontal cortex [44]. Additionally, the localization of both upper and lower beta frequency hot spots are positively correlated to the location of the clinical efficacy hot spot; both are contained predominantly in the superolateral STN [41]. By contrast, fast gamma (110–140 Hz) and slow high frequency oscillation (200–300 Hz) do not overlap with the clinical efficacy hotspot [45,46].

Beta frequency oscillations in PD have been proposed as a potential biomarker that could be used as a control variable in closed loop DBS systems [46]. Other symptoms such as tremor, freezing of gait or dyskinesia, although not consistently correlated to beta frequency oscillations, are associated with different frequency spectra as shown in Table 1. High frequency oscillations (HFO) detected in the STN of PD patients have been shown to be coupled with beta frequency oscillations in the OFF state, the so-called beta-HFO phase-amplitude coupling [47,48]. Phase-amplitude coupling also progressively increases with longer beta burst duration [49]. This activity does not directly correlate to parkinsonian symptoms, however, previous studies have shown a shift from slow HFO (200–300 Hz) to fast HFO (300–400 Hz), and coupling between fast HFO and high beta frequencies, in the PD medication ON-state [50,51]. The ratio of slow and fast HFO and beta-HFO coupling are associated with motor symptom manifestations in PD [50–52]. We will explore the clinical utility of some of these LFP characteristics in later sections.

Introduction to the device: Technology of Percept™ RC

LFP sensing and data streaming in conjunction with stimulation delivery require higher energy expenditure than non-sensing devices, resulting in shorter battery longevity in non rechargeable devices [3]. The Percept™ RC IPG combines both the LFP sensing capability with rechargeability, supporting long-term use and development of closed-loop DBS. With this iteration, all original BrainSense™ features available with the PC IPG remain [21] (Figure 1), but with additional features compared with this and the previous Activa™ RC IPG, including a smaller and thinner design, shorter recharging sessions and longer recharging intervals combined with recharging interval estimation [60,61] (Supplementary Figure S1).

Five Key BrainSense™ functions used to apply information about local field potentials to deep brain stimulation programming in Parkinson’s disease.

Percept™ RC IPG (Model B35300), like its primary cell counterpart (Percept™ PC), both delivers brain stimulation and is capable of LFP signal capture and recording. Percept™ RC is made from titanium, polysulfone with titanium dioxide and silicone and is characterized by both a lighter weight (29 grams) and a thinner shape (57 mm × 47 mm × 6 mm) [62]. The power source in Percept™ RC IPG is a lithium titanate ion battery instead of lithium graphite, preventing expansion/reduction and leading to rapid rechargeability in less than one hour per session. This composition and design ultimately yield a lifespan of up to 15 years with no mandatory shut-off in case of complete battery depletion [62].

To recharge the Percept™ RC IPG, the wireless recharger is placed in an over-the-shoulder drape so that it overlies the IPG. The recharger itself is charged from a dock when not in use. During recharging, patients can track and adjust the recharging speed using the recharger application accessible on the patient’s programmer [63]. The recharging can be performed approximately once every 12 days or for 15 min each day depending on the intensity of stimulation delivery, use of brain sensing, system impedance or other factors such as length of the implanted lead and extension and the IPG depth. A recharge interval estimator is available on the clinician programming tablet. The rechargeability and longevity of the Percept™ RC IPG make it more suitable for supporting long-term LFP sensing, streaming and data processing [62].

The Percept™ RC IPG is compatible with Medtronic DBS 1 × 4 leads (Models 3387, 3389 and 3391), Medtronic 1 × 4 extensions (Models 37085 and 37086), SenSight™ directional leads (Models B33005 and B33015) and SenSight™ extensions (Model B34000) [62,64]. This device is labeled magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) conditional for both 1.5 Tesla and 3 Tesla MRI machines [65]. However, before proceeding to the MRI scan, the stimulation should be placed in a bipolar configuration in order to maintain stimulation while undergoing scanning. Also, the MRI mode must be activated to allow the system to be in the appropriate state in the scanner. If the RC IPG is fully depleted, it cannot enter MRI mode and must be recharged back to at least 10% in order to do so. The IPG should then be charged sufficiently to avoid depletion during the MRI. Strict adherence to MRI guidelines and workflow is also required to ensure the integrity of the system and to safeguard patients [65].

To implement LFP sensing and recording into clinical practice, Percept™ RC provides BrainSense™ (BS) technology with five features including BrainSense™ survey, signal test (ST), streaming, chronic timeline recording and event marking [64] (Figure 1). Details of these features are described elsewhere [21,64]. Various factors may influence the presence of a usable signal including the patient's medication state, patient motion or location of the DBS lead [37,44,66]. Specifically in the case of PD, beta frequency power attenuates in the medication ON state and during movement (e.g., speaking, examination tasks or standing/walking). Furthermore, since beta frequency activity is most often localized within the sensorimotor STN, the proximity of the electrode or sensing contact to this region will affect the ability to detect a robust beta signal. These should be taken into consideration by the treating clinician before utilizing LFP data in programming sessions. The ‘BrainSense™ survey’ (BSS) feature screens all eligible contact pairs and generates a graph of the differential in LFP signal between them [61,64]. This can be performed between levels (in ring mode) and between individual segments when a directional lead is being used (Figure 2). Raw LFP signals are captured over a period of about 90 s with stimulation OFF via sampling at 250 Hz, are passed through two low pass filters at 100 Hz and two high pass filters at 1 Hz and another adjustable one (1 or 10 Hz), and undergo real-time fast Fourier transform to display the LFP spectrum [61]. For longer LFP data capture OFF stimulation (up to 8 h of cumulative streaming per session) from all possible contact pairs and at all frequencies, the ‘indefinite streaming’ mode is available from the BSS menu [14,61]. The data generated is not visible on the programming tablet, and has to be exported in the JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) file format for further analysis offline [14]. Real-time streaming of the LFP band of interest and chronic LFP sensing, both during the stimulation ON condition, are established by navigating to ‘BrainSense™ Setup’ and conducting a ST (Figure 3). There is no time limit for streaming, however, one hour of telemetry can deplete the battery by about 6% without affecting battery longevity [61].

The ‘Timeline’ view represents the chronic LFP recording data in the band of interest. The LFP power from the selected frequency band ± 2.5 Hz in each hemisphere is displayed over time. Stimulation amplitude is also displayed and depicts when patient self-adjustments of stimulation parameters have occurred. Both LFP power and stimulation amplitude are continuously recorded and averaged over 10 min epochs. Additional annotations mark when stimulation has been turned OFF or ON, when group settings have been changed, during recharging sessions, when entering MRI mode and for preprogrammed events. The Percept™ RC IPG saves timeline data up to 35 days, compared with 60 days for the Percept™ PC IPG, before overwriting the data. The Percept™ RC IPG can record up to 100 LFP snapshots from event markers from each hemisphere compared with 200 snapshots with the Percept™ PC IPG. The system overwrites the previous snapshots if further events are marked [61,62].

Lastly, clinicians can designate the upper and lower thresholds of LFP magnitude in the frequency band of interest for classifying the LFP data. Thresholds are defined as the LFP power at which the earliest clinical response to the lowest stimulation amplitude (upper threshold) is observed, and the LFP power at which stimulation has its maximal clinical effect (lower threshold) [61,64]. These thresholds are displayed in BS Timeline and BS Streaming modes to help clinicians categorize the degree of symptom control based on time spent above, below or between thresholds. This functionality will be required for setting up closed-loop DBS parameters when it becomes available, using either a single threshold or dual threshold paradigm [61,67].

Other features provided with the Percept™ RC IPG are a recharge interval calculator that can estimate the duration between a full-battery charge to the end of battery charge while the patient is in an active program [64]. SureTune™ 4 is an imaging software (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN 55432, USA) that can co-register both preoperative brain MRI and postoperative CT images in order to visualize the implanted DBS lead, and estimate the volume of tissue activated within that anatomy, in order to guide the clinician toward appropriate stimulation. Thus, using the Percept™ RC IPG, it is feasible for the clinician to integrate clinical, neurophysiological and imaging data together within the Percept™ RC system for use in stimulation optimization [68].

Clinical efficacy & real-world evidence

No new clinical trials were required to demonstrate stimulation efficacy for the Percept™ platform [21]. After Percept™ PC was launched in 2020, many research efforts have attempted to decode LFP patterns in various disease states for which DBS is used or being investigated (e.g., essential tremor, dystonia, Tourette syndrome, obsessive-compulsive disorder and depression) [69–72], though findings similar to beta band dynamics in PD are not yet identified. For investigations related to PD, several reports have particularly investigated how a better understanding of beta frequency dynamics can inform daily DBS programming practice, as described in the next sections [21,73].

LFP-based DBS programming

To date, case reports, observational and open-label trials in PD using BrainSense™ technology have been reported largely with STN stimulation. However, the LFP characteristics of PD in the GPi are similar [74–76]. Real-time (in-clinic) and timeline (out-of-clinic) data regarding LFPs can provide information about the optimal stimulation contact, response to stimulation, degree of PD symptoms (e.g., bradykinesia/rigidity and dyskinesia), relate patient-specific symptoms to LFP dynamics, and give insight into diurnal patterns and response to medications [14,77,78]. Furthermore, there are demonstrated efficiencies to using physiology-guided DBS programming [46,79–82]. Based on these clinical studies and reports, we suggest four scenarios in which BS technology can be used to assist with the clinical practice of DBS programming.

Contact selection using BrainSense™ survey & Signal test

Conventional DBS programming requires incremental and iterative stimulation adjustment and clinical evaluation in order to identify the contact with the widest therapeutic window, which can be a time-intensive process [83,84]. It has long been recognized in patients with PD and DBS that the macroelectrode contact with the highest beta power is often the contact with the greatest clinical response to stimulation [83–85]. While many of these investigations were performed within the intraoperative setting, the availability of implanted recording devices, namely the Percept™ PC IPG, has enabled replication of this finding. The use of BrainSense data for the selection of a stimulating contact during the initial programming session remains one of the most common uses of this technology. Lewis et al. [86] demonstrated stability of theta, beta and gamma power across three serial BSS. In a study of 62 subjects (124 hemispheres), Fasano et al. [76] used BSS and reported that 82.3% (including both STN and GPi targets) had at least one peak in the beta range, and that 59.7% of leads were programmed to the contact between sensing pairs with the largest peak. Busch et al. [82] found that among 16 patients with bilateral STN DBS, stimulation using directional segments with higher beta power during BSS showed greater improvement in movement velocity as stimulation amplitude was increased (p = 0.023).

To investigate the efficiency of LFP-guided programming, Binder et al. [81] conducted a randomized, blinded crossover trial comparing clinically-guided, image-guided and beta-guided programming in eight subjects with bilateral STN DBS and found that beta-guided programming significantly reduced programming time compared with clinically-guided programming (19 min [SD 4.17] vs 60 min [SD 11.99]). Strelow et al. [80] used an algorithmic approach to determine appropriate contacts for stimulation based on the highest levels of beta activity, starting first with the stimulating level and then the segments of that level if present. Among 14 hemispheres, the stimulation level with either the highest or second highest level of beta activity was correlated with the level that had the best clinical efficacy in all hemispheres. In ten hemispheres, the directional segment with the highest beta activity was the same as the one with the highest clinical efficacy. Together, these data support the use of BSS during the first programming session to identify the contact with highest beta power for initial testing. This approach is time-efficient but additional clinical judgment must be applied to confirm that this approach offers the best treatment results for an individual patient.

Assessment of clinical response to stimulation using BrainSense™ streaming

Beta frequency power is modulated by both medication and electrical stimulation in a dose–response relationship, resulting in improvement of motor function in PD [66,87]. After selection of the desired frequency band for tracking in the ST, activation of BS Streaming can display the tracked frequency in real time, while stimulation is titrated [64]. Although the stimulated contact with highest beta frequency power is associated with the contact with the best clinical outcome [14,79], Busch et al. [82] further demonstrated that movement velocity positively correlated to the degree of beta suppression (p < 0.001) for both ring mode and directional stimulation. Additionally, beta suppression across segmented contacts was associated with motor performance and stronger suppression led to increased movement velocity (p = 0.029) at a stimulation amplitude of 2 mA. In Strelow et al. [80], using BS Streaming during stimulation titration, low beta band suppression was determined to be a better predictor for contact selection (probability of 75%; prediction accuracy, area under curve: 0.74) compared with high beta (probability: 66%; prediction accuracy, area under curve: 0.64). Feldmann et al. [46] also showed that using the contact with the highest beta power, suppression of low beta activity corresponded with motor improvement, though high beta was also modulated. No significant modulation through DBS was observed in theta (p = 0.26) and alpha (p = 0.475) bands [46]. Thus, after using ST to select the contact for long-term stimulation and frequency for chronic sensing, BS Streaming can be employed to simultaneously assess clinical response and the degree of beta suppression with stimulation titration (Figure 3).

Assessment for stimulation optimization using timeline

Initial programming marks the beginning of an optimization period during which medications and stimulation are both adjusted, in order for patients to capture the most benefit from DBS therapy [84]. Once the frequency band of interest is set and stimulation is titrated, the BS Timeline can capture longer-term changes in the LFP power between clinic visits, thereby providing greater insight into motor symptom control. Dose-related fluctuations in the tracked band of interest since the last programming session may be demonstrated, or changes in response to stimulation adjustment made by the patient may be visualized (Figure 4). The clinician can review the BS timeline data on the clinician programmer during the subsequent follow-up visit.

Van Rheede et al. [78] analyzed long term timeline recordings in 11 patients with bilateral STN DBS, demonstrating periods of increased and decreased power in the beta band over a 24 h period, as well as diurnal fluctuations (lower power at night). They also identified a sharp increase in beta power prior to the first scheduled medication dose that patients took after waking. Giannini et al. [88] report on a patient with bilateral STN DBS in whom the high beta frequency (26–34 Hz) activity increased in power from 25–70 min after medication intake before decreasing again, demonstrating a dose response. Vaou et al. [77] used Timeline view to visualize fluctuations in beta power related to timing of OFF periods reported by patients and in gamma power related to timing of dyskinesia. In three cases, this information was used to optimize stimulation with improved clinical results.

In our own experience, Timeline view has demonstrated ability in recognizing fluctuations in response to medication doses, even in the absence of marking medication intake (Figure 4), that may signal the need to adjust medications. It has also been able to demonstrate patient self-adjustment of stimulator settings (Figure 4) and has facilitated discussion with patients about best management strategies. Another BS feature is the ability to set thresholds, which can also provide additional insights into the overall degree of physiological optimization on a daily, weekly or monthly basis (Figure 5) that may also inform therapeutic decision making. When using these features, however, clinicians should be mindful that physiologic changes do not absolutely represent clinically meaningful symptom or state changes, and as such, these observed features should be carefully interpreted in the context of rigorously obtained clinical data.

Symptom characterization using event markers

Few published reports have demonstrated the use and/or utility of event markers. A 30 s averaged fast Fourier transform of the LFP from 0–100 Hz is recorded immediately after the patient triggers an event marker on their controller. In the same patient reported by Giannini et al. [88], rigidity events were found to be associated with the presence of a low beta (17 Hz) peak and absence of a higher beta (30 Hz) peak. Vaou et al. [77] demonstrated the utility of capturing event snapshots related to symptoms of interest and combining it with information from the patient's timeline view in informing programming in three cases.

In our experience, event markers with LFP snapshots can identify physiologic characteristics or markers of symptoms such as dystonia or dyskinesia, or may help provide a better understanding of the timing of these events with relation to overall symptom control (Figure 5). For example, an event that is consistently marked during periods of relatively low beta power may be construed as occurring during an “ON” period, while one that is marked during periods of relatively higher beta power may be attributed to an “OFF” symptom. Because of the time involved in locating the programmer, connecting it to the IPG and then navigating to the application in order to mark an event, the use of these markers may be less conducive to identifying the LFP correlates of transient events (e.g., gait freezing or falls) [14], and may be more suited to monitoring or inferring state changes (e.g., when several minutes of dyskinesia or dystonia occur). When attempting to identify a potential physiologic marker of a reported clinical event, clinicians should be aware that some symptoms may not be represented by the tracked frequency, and that beta frequency can be modulated by other factors than medications such as circadian rhythm or movement [78,89].

Anecdotally, providers have used the event markers to ask patients to note the time that medications were taken even in the absence of LFP snapshots, which may give insight into compliance or the relationship between medication intake and occurrence of other symptoms that are also marked. In this way, some BS functions may still be informative even in the absence of a trackable LFP band of interest.

Introduction to adaptive DBS

Although current open-loop DBS provides substantial improvement in motor symptoms, medication reduction and quality of life for PD, chronic constant stimulation can induce unwanted stimulation-related adverse effects such as speech, gait and balance impairments or neuropsychiatric symptoms [10,84,90]. Clinical evaluation is dependent on patient feedback and long programming sessions can be exhausting for both patients and clinicians [84]. Adaptive DBS (aDBS), also known as closed-loop DBS, may mitigate these issues and has been under investigation in the past several years. This DBS approach uses the LFP power as a control mechanism to automatically adjust DBS settings according to an individual patient’s physiologic state, lessening stimulation-induced adverse effects from chronic stimulation while also prolonging battery life because of lower energy use compared with conventional DBS [91]. However, certain limitations should be considered. Beta band power, the current main input for aDBS in many adaptive clinical studies, is mainly correlated to akinetic-rigid symptoms of PD and can be reproduced even after years of medical and DBS treatment [92]. However, it is also modulated by factors such as dopaminergic medication, some non-dopaminergic medications [93], normal movement [89], circadian rhythms [78], and hormonal level [94]. Other problematic PD motor symptoms such as tremor or freezing of gait are not always correlated to beta power [53]. Additionally, low beta and high beta frequency might be responsive to an intervention in different ways, for instance, low beta activity is consistently suppressed with levodopa [95], but high beta frequency is more consistently suppressed with stimulation [88]. Apart from LFP power, other inputs in aDBS programming have been investigated, such as cortical recording, wearable sensors or mobile applications to overcome the limitations related to LFP driven adjustments [96]. However, there are questions regarding the redundancy, validity or tractability that have yet to be further investigated [97].

Programming current open-loop DBS devices requires adjusting the electrode configuration as well as specific stimulation parameters including amplitude, pulse width and frequency. For aDBS, additional parameters need to be considered, particularly those that define the control policy for automated stimulation adjustment, such as LFP thresholds for stimulation delivery, the range of stimulation amplitude, stimulation ramping time and the recording refractory period before additional adjustments are made [98]. Of particular interest is the “threshold policy” for adaptive algorithms, which can be either a single-threshold or dual-threshold policy. In the single-threshold policy, LFP power crossing above a single threshold would provoke a change in stimulation amplitude to the pre-determined target amplitude, and when it drops below that same threshold, the stimulation amplitude would drop back to a pre-determined lower amplitude. Dual-threshold policy requires setting an upper and lower threshold of LFP power for triggering stimulation changes. The system would maintain the amplitude if the sensed LFP power remains within the window, but would adjust the amplitude up or down if the sensed LFP power goes beyond or below the threshold, respectively [98].

Another control policy is the “proportional policy” in which the stimulation amplitude is automatically adjusted in proportion to the magnitude of the sensed LFP power [98]. The other necessary parameters are the stimulation amplitude range that correlates to clinical efficacy and side effects from stimulation in the ON- and OFF- medication state. The upper limit of amplitude should provide clinical improvement without inducing side effects if medications have worn OFF. The lower limit of amplitude provides minimal current for reserving the battery and can help avoid side effects in the ON-medication state [98]. For both threshold and proportional policies, the clinician can also designate the input smoothing time, amplitude ramping time and refractory period recording time for aDBS [98]. Since the advent of aDBS, investigators have explored different aspects such as the appropriate feedback control, the specific adjustment algorithm and various targets, along with the associated clinical benefits, adverse events and amount of energy savings compared with open-loop DBS in PD patients (Table 2).

Tolerability, efficacy & safety of adaptive DBS

Currently, open-label clinical trials of aDBS for PD have been performed, as shown in Table 2 mostly using single-threshold and dual-threshold algorithms, and demonstrating good clinical performance in PD patients with aDBS over patients without DBS or with open-loop DBS. One study showed improved speech intelligibility in PD patients with aDBS compared with open-loop DBS [101]. Nakajima et al. [108] reported a prospective observational post market study of STN aDBS in 12 PD patients. All patients tolerated either single or dual threshold algorithms without significant adverse events. 66% of those patients showed mild to decided improvement using a dual-threshold algorithm and 80% showed mild to decided improvement when a single-threshold algorithm was used. Nine of 12 patients preferred to continue on aDBS (seven of nine patients preferred the dual-threshold algorithm and two of nine patients preferred the single-threshold algorithm).

In December 2020, Medtronic, Inc. launched a multicenter (USA, European Union and Canada), prospective, single-blinded, randomized, crossover clinical trial investigating the safety and clinical efficacy of aDBS in the ADAPT-PD study, with the goal of future regulatory approval of this programming feature (NCT04547712). The primary outcome measure is the proportion of aDBS subjects with “ON” time without troublesome dyskinesia exceeding the threshold (i.e., aDBS is no worse than one standard deviation less than conventional DBS). Sixty eight PD patients who underwent STN or GPi DBS with Percept™ PC IPG placement meeting inclusion/exclusion criteria were enrolled. The investigational feature for aDBS programming was unlocked and used an 8–30 Hz LFP control signal. 84% (40 patients with STN DBS and 17 patients with GPi DBS) had clinician-identified LFP peaks in both ON- and OFF-medication states and 71.4% of them had automated algorithm-identified LFP peaks. The automated algorithm identified the same beta peak as the clinician in 56.8% of STN and 56.3% of GPi target. For STN, 43.4% had low-beta peaks and 32.1% had high-beta peaks, and for GPi, 44.4% had low-beta peaks and 44.4% had high-beta peaks [109].

Either single-threshold or dual-threshold aDBS was determined feasible in 45 patients and these subjects were randomized into either aDBS or open-loop DBS for 30 days, followed by a cross-over to the other modality, if feasible [110]. Single threshold aDBS improved “ON” time without troublesome dyskinesia compared with open-loop DBS by 0.6 ± 3.6 hours (nonsignificant), while dual threshold aDBS improved it by 1.4 ± 3.0 hours in a statistically significant manner (97.5% confidence interval 0.2–2.5, p < 0.0125). Furthermore, total energy expenditure was reduced in aDBS compared with continuous DBS (median 13% in single threshold and 11% in dual threshold aDBS). 97.8% of patients elected to continue aDBS in subsequent follow-up. Similar adverse events occurred in aDBS compared with open-loop DBS and no serious adverse events were reported. This study demonstrated the short-term safety and efficacy of aDBS as well as its energy-saving capability [110]. Further studies into the long-term safety and efficacy of aDBS are needed, as well as a better understanding of the benefits of single threshold versus dual-threshold DBS.

Regulatory affairs

Percept™ RC IPG received FDA approval on 8 January 2024, and received its CE-mark for use in Europe on 5 December 2023 [22,23]. Prior to this it was available in Japan, the only country with regulatory approval for aDBS and where the first RC IPG was implanted in October, 2023. In the United States, the Percept™ RC IPG is indicated for use in patients with PD, essential tremor, dystonia and epilepsy, but not obsessive-compulsive disorder [111].

Conclusion

DBS is indicated to manage medication-refractory motor complications or tremor in patients with advancing PD. Symptoms such as bradykinesia and rigidity relate to the power of abnormally synchronized beta frequency oscillations within the motor networks of patients with PD, and these are now measurable outside of the operating room and in the real-world setting of DBS care using implanted DBS systems. Among DBS devices available on the market, the Percept™ software and RC IPG are unique in being the first to offer a platform for integrating physiologic and imaging data with clinical information during DBS programming sessions. The five key BrainSense™ technology features enable correlation of clinical information to physiologic information for DBS optimization in clinical practice, with use cases for selecting the optimal contact for stimulation using BS survey, assessment of clinical response to stimulation using BS streaming, assessment for stimulation optimization using BS Timeline, and symptom characterization using event markers. Setting thresholds can provide insight into daily, weekly or monthly fluctuations in symptom control, and sets the stage for future implementation of aDBS, which has been studied using both single-threshold and dual-threshold paradigms. Early evidence suggests tolerability and safety of aDBS along with efficacy favoring the dual-threshold mode for improving motor complications, but longer-term follow-up is needed. The Percept™ RC IPG itself is smaller, with more efficient charging sessions and longer lasting charging intervals than previous platforms. A recharge interval estimator can further ease the charging process for patients. Lastly it is MRI conditionally safe for both 1.5T and 3.0T scanners and when using a patient-enabled MRI safe mode. In the case of future approval of aDBS, a simple software unlock will make this technology available to all PD patients implanted with Percept™ IPGs.

While the Percept™ RC neurostimulator incorporates many improvements, there are some key limitations that should be recognized. Similar to the Percept™ PC, LFP recordings can only be made during active stimulation using pre-specified contact configurations, and the device can only record activity occurring between 0–96.6 Hz. This may limit exploration of potential biomarkers that could facilitate DBS programming in PD and other conditions, or exploration of alternate control features that can be used for aDBS. Another constraint is that low frequency activity (e.g., less than 8 Hz) is unavailable for chronic sensing, which could limit our understanding of how these oscillations relate to PD features (e.g., nonmotor symptoms), or of non-PD disease states where these frequencies are relevant. For clinicians or researchers who wish to interact with the neurophysiologic data generated from Percept™ devices, the process of downloading JSON files is another limitation, as it can only be done in clinic and with a data storage capacity of 35 days in the case of timeline data from the RC IPG. As a consequence, the frequency or timing of patient visits will need to be adjusted in order to capture events of interest or specific timeframes of data. As mentioned, patient event markers require interaction with the patient controller, resulting in delayed marking of brief, episodic events without a loop recording function. While an alternate solution could come from the use of wearable devices or using patient reported data on smartphone applications, there is a parallel need for facilitation of data synchronization and integration, since it is often the case that multiple devices and mixed platforms are required. Solutions to some of these limitations will depend on continued engineering development and innovation, or alternate software solutions. Regardless, the field of LFP-guided programming and future opportunities for understanding how LFPs relate to disease, the effects of neuromodulation, or closing the loop in neuromodulation have been greatly enhanced by the availability of the Percept™ RC IPG, and any increased requirement for on-board computing power or software will be readily addressed.
