Lifestyle factors impact sperm microbiota and are associated with biological and clinical issues in infertile patients undergoing assisted reproductive technologies, a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Valentine Renouf, Clémence Gachet, Manon Prat, Philippe Grivard, Quentin Riche Piotaix, Matthieu Egloff, Christophe Burucoa, Maxime Pichon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSperm microbiota originates from local (urogenital, intestinal, cutaneous) and partner microbiomes, influenced by sexual activity. Its composition and diversity may impact male fertility, but research remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between lifestyle factors, sperm microbiota composition, and fertility outcomes. A monocentric prospective study was conducted on men undergoing assisted reproductive technology at Poitiers University Hospital between May and December 2021. Samples were analysed using high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatic pipelines to assess microbial diversity, taxonomic composition, and relative abundance. Lifestyle factors (BMI, diet, tobacco, alcohol, stress, physical activity) were collected through self-administered standardized questionnaires and medical interviews. Sperm quality was evaluated based on WHO 2021 criteria. Alpha and beta diversity metrics were calculated, and statistical correlations were assessed using ANCOM and LEfSe analyses. Two profiles were identified: one defined by Actinomycetota predominance (> 50%) (n = 34 patients) and the other by Bacillota predominance (n = 10 patients). Sperm microbiota alpha diversity was found to be higher in patients under 31 years of age or over 40 years of age, with a BMI ≥ 30, high fat intake, red meat consumption, and lower tobacco consumption (p < 0.05). Sperm microbiota beta diversity was found to differ according to age, sugar, coffee, and alcohol consumption (p < 0.05). Staphylococcus spp. was significantly enriched in non-pregnant couples, suggesting a possible negative impact on fertility. These findings suggest that the diversity of microbes in sperm can be affected by lifestyle choices and may play a role in fertility issues. This could help in the management of male infertility by changing harmful habits that might affect fertility and sperm microbiota. Introduction

In conjunction with the human body, microorganisms with considerable variability constitute a substantial metabolic unit, named“microbiome”1. This role is becoming increasingly recognised, but further research is now required to understand the associations between imbalances and specific pathologies2.

Sperm microbiota are derived from a combination of local microbiota (urogenital, cutaneous and intestinal) and, to a lesser extent, female genital microbiota, in contact during sexual activity (nearly all of the taxa detected were common to the spermatic and vaginal microbiota of the members of the couple)3,4. However, it is imperative that these data be evaluated with a high degree of caution, as they may be significantly influenced by factors such as demographics of the tested populations, duration since the last sexual encounter, and the methodologies employed during the pre-analytical and analytical processes, which can markedly vary between studies.

Initial research on sperm microbiota was based on culture or PCR, targeting specific microorganisms based on their pathogenicity5. While the first publications on the study of sperm microbiota using high-throughput sequencing date from 2013 to 2014, with the majority published since 2019, only a limited number of human studies have appeared in recent years6.

In various studies, the sperm microbiota has been described as having a lower bacterial concentration but higher diversity than that of the vaginal microbiota, and a high degree of inter-individual variability4,7.

The concept of three microbial profiles in sperm microbiota, according to the predominant genus, has emerged recently8. First, the predominant Lactobacillus spp.; second, the Prevotella spp. and third, the Pseudomonas spp. have been profiled9. In terms of genus, most studies on this microbiota concur on the recurrent presence of the genera Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Pseudomonas, Gardnerella, Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, Finegoldia, Enterococcus and Staphylococcus among the main ones8. Regarding species, Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus crispatus, Gardnerella vaginalis and Corynebacterium seminale have frequently been found to be the most abundant in sperm10.

Research into the relation of lifestyle factors to the imbalance of sperm microbiota is less advanced than that pertaining to the gut microbiome, despite human and canine studies.6,11–13. An association has been found between the composition of the spermatic microbiota and patient age, which may be explained by differing frequency of sexual activity (with a reduction in bifidobacteria being a feature)10. Following a period of description, it is imperative to analyse this microbiota from the perspective of human habits. This analysis will yield specific, personalised recommendations designed to optimise clinical management of infertility, particularly in cases of male infertility without where no evident cause6.

Significant changes in the sperm microbiota have been observed in mice fed a temporary fat-enriched diet (characterised by lessened diversity with an increase in Corynebacterium spp.) through feeding or transferring faecal microbiota to mice14,15. Conversely, a study on a fibre-enriched diet in animals demonstrated enhanced sperm parameters; however, no information was available regarding sperm microbiota16.

In the absence of information regarding the sperm, studies of the stool microbiota have demonstrated increased abundance of the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, associated with the consumption of milk and yoghurt as well as large quantities of coffee (45 to 500 mL per day) or in persons who engage in physical activity with contradictory results depending on effort intensity)17–19. Tobacco has been shown to markedly influence the diversity of the stool microbiota, with increased Prevotella spp., Desulfovibrio spp. and Alphaproteobacteria17. Given the incompatibility of findings from these two very different ecological niches, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive investigation on the impact of such lifestyle factors on sperm microbiota and the mechanisms linking specific microbial taxa to fertility outcomes, as recommendations for improving sperm health are mainly based on gut discoveries.

In the absence of data focusing on sperm microbiota, the present study aimed to analyse potential associations between sperm microbiota and sperm quality (represented by different spermogram parameters) according to patients’ lifestyles. It is hypothesised that these findings could help manage male infertility by modifying habits prejudicial to fertility and sperm microbiota.

Methods

Study participants

Patients were selected through a sperm analysis procedure performed at the Reproductive Biology laboratory of Poitiers University Hospital between 1 May and 31 December 2021. Were analysed: (i) (demographic criteria) patients aged between 18 and 60 years, (ii) (anamnestic criteria) patients without documented history of urological surgery (including testis trauma, testicular torsion, operated hydrocele, operated cryptorchidism and unilateral orchidectomy), without recent intake of antibiotics or probiotics for 3 weeks before sampling; (iii) (clinical criteria) patients experiencing infertility as a couple, undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) treatment, and having abstained from sexual intercourse for three to seven days; (iv) (analytical criteria) patients with sperm volume less than 1.5 mL (allowing routine management of the sample and supplementary analyses), and who provided a second sample (allowing longitudinal analysis of the microbiome on successive samples).

Patients with abnormal sperm parameters underwent further evaluation. Of note, (i) patients with a count < 10 M/mL underwent a testicular ultrasound, hormonal testing, and karyotyping; and (ii) patients with a count < 1 M/mL underwent testing for Y chromosome microdeletion screening20. No genetic abnormalities were identified in the included patients.

As a proof-of-concept study, and based on previous studies, it was determined that approximately 40 patients would be sufficient to provide answers to the hypotheses formulated. However, no power analysis was performed to justify the sample size presented in this study.

Clinical characteristics

The clinical (including lifestyle information) and biological data were meticulously documented prospectively in a Clinical Recording File, using an homemade self-administered frequency questionnaire for nutrition or other consumption, stress or physical activity, or clinical file on ART management (pregnancy beginning and/or conclusion) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The patients’ ages were grouped in tens, and their BMI was stratified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification21. Sample collection

All subjects participating in the study provided a sperm sample following masturbation in the assisted reproduction laboratory (no testicular biopsy or epididymal aspiration). Sperm was collected in a sterile jar and processed after 30 min of liquefaction at room temperature. Volume estimation was then performed by weighting on a digital balance (density of 1 g/mL) before distribution for the following analyses: (i) 50 µL were stretched on slide after staining using the Spermoscan kit (RAL Diagnostics, Martillac, France) (morphological analysis, only on the first sample for each patient); (ii) 50 µL were immediately stained using the VitalScreen kit (FertiPro, Beern em, Belgium) (vitality estimation); (iii) 50 µL were deposited on a chromogenic strip (pH estimation); (iv) 10 µL were observed at fresh state under light microscope (mobility estimation); and (v) 10 to 50 µL were diluted in sterile water before numeration on a counting cell (improved Neubaueur® type) (concentration estimation). The classification of sperm parameters was determined as “normal” or “abnormal” in accordance with the 2021 WHO classification26. All patients were subjected to testing for sexually transmitted infections, with a particular focus on Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma sp. This testing was conducted utilising molecular biology techniques, and the results indicated no infection at the time of sampling. Thereafter, 250 µL of the initial ejaculate were mixed with 500 µL of RNAlater (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) in a LoBind tube (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Study samples were identified using pre-filled labels and stored at 4 °C for a maximum of 48 h before being sent to the Infectious Agents Department of Poitiers University Hospital for storage at -70 °C before analysis. As sperm samples were low biomass samples, a no-template control consisting of a sterile jar filled with 1.5 ml of PCR-free water) was included in the present cohort. This control was followed throughout the process (including nucleic acid extraction, PCR amplification, library preparation/sequencing and bioinformatic analysis) to verify the absence of contamination from the environment or reagents throughout the process.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification of bacterial 16 S rDNA region

Total DNA was extracted from all collected samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA® kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The efficacy of this step was evaluated by quantifying DNA following elution using the Qubit® 4.0 fluorometer and Qubit™ 1X dsDNA High Sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Extracts were subsequently stored at −70 °C.

The bacterial DNA region of interest was amplified using in-house developed sequences (“V1forward” and “V3reverse”) according to the Illumina custom primer design protocol (Illumina, CA, USA)6,27,28. PCR was performed using the KAPA Hifi PCR kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The thermal cycle consisted of 3 min at 95 °C; followed by 35 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 55 °C and 30 s at 72 °C; and then by a final elongation of 5 min at 72 °C. A negative control (TE buffer, Eurobio, France) was treated as one of the samples. The integrity of the amplicons was then assessed by means of 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, which was used to identify a band at 509 bp. After that, purification was carried out using magnetic beads and the NucleoMag® NGS Clean-up protocol (Macherey-Nagel, Hoerdt, France), after which the samples were stored at -70 °C.

Library construction and sequencing

Libraries were prepared from 5 µL of each purified amplified product using the Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina). Amplification PCR (8 cycles) was performed using the KAPA Hifi PCR kit (Roche). After amplification, libraries were purified to obtain 25 µL of final eluate prior to quantification as described above. The negative control validated correct preparation of the libraries (and sequencing). In accordance with the Illumina “16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation” protocol, following dilution to the minimum concentration, the libraries were then pooled in equimolar concentrations for the final library, subsequently undergoing a chemical denaturation process prior to final dilution to a concentration of 15 pM. Denatured PhiX (PhiX Control v3, Illumina) was then added to the final library (resulting in a final representation of 5%). Final libraries were loaded onto MiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina) in a MiSeq v3 300 × 2 pair-end cartridge (Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s protocol usingcustom primer approaches allowing to obtain FASTQ files. Then later were analysed in the bioinformatic step.

Data analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted using a bespoke pipeline that had been configured within the Infectious Agents Department of Poitiers University Hospital6. Statistical analysis was conducted utilising GraphPad Version 9. Microbiome bioinformatics were performed with QIIME 2 2018.829,30. The analysis pipeline was utilised to eliminate low-quality reads and sequence artefacts, including chimeras, singletons and sequence errors. The process involved merging matched reads to generate fragments with an approximate length of 500 base pairs. Subsequently, raw sequence data underwent demultiplexing, trimming to a length of 280 base pairs to avoid skewing with diversity estimate, and quality filtering (with an expected error rate of less than 0.1%) using the q2-demux plugin. This was followed by denoising with DADA2. All amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were aligned with mafft and used to construct phylogeny with fasttree2 against the Silva138 99% ASVs sequences. To guarantee optimal comparability of the samples and equitable representation, the relative abundance of each sample was normalized according to the lowest value recorded. The rarefaction curves confirmed that the chosen depth was sufficient to record all the ASVs present in each sample. The ASV table was rarefied to a depth of 4,871reads.

Following rarefaction, alpha-diversity metrics (Chao1, Shannon, Pielou, Simpson indexes and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity), and beta-diversity metrics (weighted UniFrac, unweighted UniFrac, Bray-Curtis and Jaccard dissimilarity) were estimated. ANCOM and Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LefSe) were used to identify differentially abundant genera among the groups. Metacyc approaches were utilised in the metagenomic analysis of metabolic pathways. Notwithstanding limited statistical power, all biological sperm characteristics were categorised for analysis to mitigate information biases arising from inter-operator variability in the assessment of sperm parameters.

Taxonomic relative abundance graphs were prepared, dividing microbiome populations into their respective taxonomic levels (phylum, genus, species). Data were analysed using a permanova statistical test and a student’s t-test and reported as follows: p ≤ 0.05 for statistical significance and 0.05 < p ≤ 0.10 for statistical trend. The Bonferroni-Hochberg correction was employed to account for increased statistical risk, and to control the false positive rate. The raw data are available under the Sequence Read Archive BioProject PRJNA854168 (SRR19906338 to SRR19906303).

Ethical considerations

In accordance with the stipulations outlined in Article L1211-2 of the Public Health Code, all samples utilised in the present study were obtained with the written explicit consent of the subjects, who were informed of the purposes of the study and were given the option to decline participation. This prospective monocentric study, which was conducted from May to December 2021, was reported to the Clinical Research and Innovation Department of the Poitiers University Hospital. The bio-bank constitution file number DC-2019-3719 was approved by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research (Supplementary Fig. 2). Samples were analysed after anonymisation and identification with laboratory identifiers allocated at inclusion.

Quality control and population analyses

Following implementation of a rigorous quality filtering process on the sequences, a total of 95 samples were validated. The median value for reads per sample was determined to be 49,772 (minimum: 4871; maximum: 592,012). Following the quality filtering process, the absence of sequences in the no-template control sample showed the results to be exempt from contamination, which is frequent in low biomass samples.

At the phylum level, Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota were predominantly represented. Two profiles were identified: one defined by Actinomycetota predominance (> 50%) (n = 34 patients) and by the other Bacillota predominance (n = 10 patients).

The main genera were Lactobacillus (with L. crispatus as the main species), Microbacterium, Peptoniphilus, Prevotella and then Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum, Fenollaria, Finegoldia and Streptococcus. Two thirds of the samples (66/95; 69%) showed a predominant profile of Lactobacillus spp.; 16 (16/95; 17%) showed a predominant profile of Microbacterium spp; the remaining samples were predominantly Streptococcus spp. (4/95; 4. 2%), Peptoniphilus spp. (4/95; 4.2%), Fenollaria spp. (2/95; 2.1%), Prevotella spp. (1; 1.1%), Corynebacterium spp. (1; 1.1%) and Finegoldia spp. (1; 1.1%).

Age had a significant effect on beta diversity (based on Jaccard and unweighted UniFrac indices; p < 0.05), with patients aged 31–40 years having lower beta diversity than samples from patients aged 41 years and more (Jaccard and unweighted UniFrac indices; p < 0.05). Analyses of the composition of microbiome (ANCOM)

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LefSe) approaches demonstrated lower prevalence of the phylum Epsilonbactereota among non-consumers of red meat, in comparison to moderate consumers, with no statistically significant variation observed in the other comparisons.

At the phylum and genus levels, prevalence of Bacillota (including Finegoldia spp. and Enterococcus spp.) and Actinomycetota (including Gordonia spp., Microbacterium spp. and Actinotignum spp.) increased significantly in the BMI ≥ 30 group, while Pseudomonadota likewise increased with BMI. Among the genera showing increased prevalence in obese patients were Prevotella, Sphingomonas, Brevundimonas, Comamonas, and Methylobacterium-methylorubrum.

Regarding pregnancy outcome, increased Pseudomonadota was observed in the ‘pregnancy’ group. At the genus scale, significantly increased Staphylococcus spp. was observed in the ‘non-pregnancy’ group (p < 0.05).

Analysis of the type of stress showed a significant difference in bacterial representation: Achromobacter spp. were overrepresented in the ‘no stress’ group, Actinomyces spp. were reduced in the ‘time stress’ group and Prevotella spp. and Methylobacterium-methylorubrum spp. were increased and reduced respectively in the ‘thermal stress’ group (p < 0.05).

In addition, Actinotignum spp. prevalence significantly increased with age and Microbacterium spp. significantly increased in the 31–40 age group (p < 0.05). About fibre consumption, Mobiluncus spp. and Porphyromonas spp. significantly reduced with high fibre consumption, whereas Ezakiella spp., Aerococcus spp. and Actinotignum spp. significantly increased. (p < 0.05). Finally, regarding male infertility, Dialister propionicifaciens significantly increased in the ‘secondary’ infertility group. Discussion

The present study was conducted against the backdrop of a gradual increase in the rate of infertility of unexplained origin and an increasing number of discoveries implicating the microbiota in human health and pathology. It is one of the first to study the relationship between lifestyle and sperm microbiota, as well as its evolution, with the aim of better understanding male infertility. The present study demonstrates that the composition of sperm microbiota varies according to BMI, age, and smoking, and is associated with pregnancy outcomes (Fig. 2.)

Scheme summary.

When interviewing patients presenting with infertility, it is important to consider their lifestyle, dietary habits, and exposure to toxins. Furthermore, patients often seek expert advice on how to improve their fertility. This study cannot presently be applied in routine clinical practice at this time due to its reliance on a cohort study, which is subject to improvement and does not currently allow for individual conclusions to be drawn31. Further well-conducted studies with robust results would help to guide patients towards lifestyle changes that have been shown to improve natural fertility.

As outlined above, a multitude of lifestyle factors have been identified as potentially associated withalterations in the balance of the intestinal microbiota and sperm parameters. Obesity and smoking have been identified as significantly associated with male infertility, though the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, necessitating further inquiry into the role of lifestyle in infertility and its interdependence with sperm microbiota32.

The predominance of the Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota phyla found in this study is consistent with previous research8,9. The genus Lactobacillus was found to predominate in 69% of samples, as described in precious studies, but the second predominant genus, Microbacterium, had not8,9. However, the predominance of the genus varies from study to study and discrepancies remain (Enterococcus spp., Prevotella spp. or Pseudomonas spp.), and the distinction of profiles requires further observations to ensure a robust definition7,8. It is noteworthy that the preponderance of Lactobacillus crispatus (a predominant constituent of the vaginal microbiota) can be associated with sexual activity4.

As for consequences on pregnancy, this study demonstrated a significant increase in the genus Staphylococcus in men whose couples remained without pregnancy during follow-up in comparison with those in whom pregnancy was achieved. In several studies, Staphylococcus spp. in sperm fluid, particularly S. aureus, was associated with reduced sperm quality (reduced mobility by agglutination) or fertility (local inflammation and production of oxygenated derivatives), except for a single study in which it was enriched in the group with a normal spermogram9,33. The alteration of sperm parameters by Staphylococcus may be mediated by inflammation and an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can result in impaired sperm motility and increased DNA fragmentation33, possibly associated with genetic material less suitable for IVF attempts. The urogenital pathogenicity of coagulase-negative staphylococci remains contentious due to their abundant presence in the skin microbiota and frequent role as skin contaminants of the sperm microbiota. Similarly, the need to treat asymptomatic bacteriospermia remains debated. Conversely, in women, an endometrial microbial profile containing Staphylococcus spp. has been associated with unsuccessful attempts at in vitro fertilisation34,35.

About type of infertility, in contrast to men with secondary infertility, increased alpha diversity was found in men with primary infertility and in those whose couple did not achieve pregnancy during the study, in line with the results obtained for sperm parameters (increased alpha diversity in abnormal counts). This work is consistent with observations of increased alpha diversity in samples with leukospermia associated with impaired count and motility6,36. In contrast to the beneficial diversity of the intestinal microbiota, the present study demonstrates that diversified sperm microbiota is associated with a loss of chance of pregnancy and a reduction in gamete quality. This phenomenon is also associated with obesity and high fat consumption. In conclusion, it can be posited that increased alpha diversity may be deleterious, possibly indicative of dysbiosis. This contrasts with the observations made in the context of stool microbiota pathophysiology, which suggests that these parameters could not be considered as universal, but vary in their interpretation depending on the analysed microbiota. In BMI ≥ 30, increased alpha diversity (as well as high fat consumption), beta diversity and the Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio were consistent with observations in the literature on the stool microbiota (aside from alpha diversity, which was reduced). At the taxonomic level, although no correspondence was observed between the genera found in this work and those in the literature, it appears that BMI and high fat consumption were associated with modification of the microbiota in a non-beneficial way (increasing diversity of the sperm microbiota, reflecting dysbiosis, as stated earlier)37,38.

In consideration of the impact of additional dietary and exogenous factors, no extant studies have hitherto examined sperm microbiota; therefore, the ensuing discussion will focus on the comparison with intestinal microbiota. A decrease in the phylum Epsilonbactereota was observed in individuals who consumed low amounts of red meat and milk39. The increased alpha diversity and significant difference in beta diversity for the consumption of dairy products are congruent with previous study39. The results concerning coffee consumption are consistent with the existing literature, suggesting a possible dose-dependent effect. About tobacco consumption, the different levels of tobacco consumption present different beta diversities, as well as a difference in richness for former smokers. Smoking has been shown to be associated with reduction in the richness of the microbiota, which tends to return to normal when smoking is stopped. These data are consistent with the literature and help to explain weight gain after smoking cessation40.

As for the impact of environmental factors, and since categories of physical activity have been associated with varying levels of alpha diversity, it can be hypothesised that sporting activities may significantly be associated with modification of the composition of sperm microbiota. This finding, based on declarative data, aligns with the existing literature on stool microbiota. However, it should be noted that the study’s findings require validation through additional research. Specifically, further studies are necessary to elucidate the intensity-dependent impact on sperm microbiota. The nature of the environmental stress experienced by the patients under study may be associated with differential impact on sperm microbiota. Hourly stress, characterised by disruption to the circadian rhythm, has been associated with a reduction in alpha diversity and richness of the sperm microbiota, a finding that aligns with existing literature on the subject. Additionally, a lessened proportion of Actinomyces spp. was observed. Given the rhythmic nature of the microbiota, which is linked to sleep, energy expenditure and food intake, composition and metabolism exhibit fluctuations throughout the nychthemeron. Disruption of the circadian rhythm is hypothesised to induce dysbiosis, in association with an elevated risk of developing metabolic or inflammatory diseases41,42. Conversely, heat stress has been associated with increased richness, suggesting microbiota plasticity and adaptability of different bacterial species (possibly due to the need for thermogenesis or adaptation to excess heat). Furthermore, while reduced sperm quality has been associated with various disturbances in circadian rhythm and/or thermal disturbance, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained38. The microbiome could be a contributing factor.

A major strength and possible weakness of this study consists in the cohort studied, that must be considered as exploratory by nature. Even if based on a single-center design, the 45 patients included constituted a cohort similar in size to those in the literature, validating the significant results obtained, which were nonetheless limited by the fact that a second sample could not be obtained for a large number of patients at strictly the same time interval between samples, due in particular to follow-up before the medically assisted procreation attempt (completion of diagnostic tests, successive consultations, analysis of the file at the multidisciplinary reunion, attempts at simple hormonal stimulation). Most patients were aged between 31 and 40 years, which may be explained by prolonged infertility (12 months of attempts before consulting a doctor), decline in the age at which couples try to conceive (the average age in France at childbirth was 31.0 for women and 33.1 for men), and secondary infertility43. Subsequent studies must include fertile men within a fertile couple, thereby ensuring that the observed signals can be regarded as indicative of infertile men implicated in infertile couples. Furthermore, in subsequent studies, it would be intriguing to analyse the genital microbiota of both males and females on a larger cohort to ascertain their role in the success or failure of procreation (such as attributing pregnancy failure to the presence of male sperm microorganisms only). In the present study, because of the absence of strict control or scoring of female factors implicated in couple fertility, observations must be considered as exploratory (especially in subgroups with very low numbers of patients) rather than direct evidence that sperm microbiota influences ART success. Furthermore, in the absence offemale microbiota determination, it could not be ruled out that the high frequency of Lactobacillus (including L. crispatus) may reflect transfer from the partner’s microbiota or sexual contact. While a period of abstinence can help to limit (though not completely prevent) contamination of the sperm microbiome by the female microbiome, this cannot be guaranteed. By contrast, analysis couldpermit longitudinal study of the relevant parameters, with appropriate control of potential confounding factors. In the present study, the analysis of two successive samples, spaced at least one female menstrual cycle (approximately one month) apart, has been shown to limit the impact of short-term environmental variations on the microbiota by grouping the analyses. However, most studies and findings on sperm microbiota in human pathophysiology enable only correlations or associations, rather than mechanistic conclusions, to be determined. To further advance, it would be necessary to set up in vitro, in vivo, and controlled clinical trials to control both the male and the female parties, the embryo, and clinical procedures. Finally, even if patients with a count < 10 M/mL underwent testicular ultrasound, hormonal testing, and karyotyping; and patients with a count < 1 M/mL underwent testing for Y chromosome microdeletion screening, no genetic abnormalities were identified. A critical challenge in interpreting microbiota findings lies in the context of azoospermia and severe spermatogenic disorders, which represent highly heterogeneous clinical and biological entities. The analysis of all azoospermic patients as a single group has limits the ability to discern whether observed differences in microbiota composition are linked to infertility itself or to distinct, hitherto uncharacterised underlying etiologies44. Indeed, male infertility, particularly non-obstructive azoospermia, is characterised by a considerable degree of genetic and biological complexity. Chromosomal abnormalities and Y-chromosome microdeletions account for numerous cases, while a substantial number remain idiopathic, with thousands of genes implicated in spermatogenic failure45. These divergent molecular mechanisms and clinical implications, rendering any unified etiological interpretation inadequate. Consequently, when is no stratification by pathological subtype or comprehensive exclusion of known genetic causes, associations between seminal microbiota composition and infertility should be interpreted with caution. Identifying a between-group difference is not strictly equivalent to demonstrating an association, and determining causation is far more complex than merely associating a group with a given outcome (requiring modelling of microbial features through regression techniques). Furthermore, it is imperative to ascertain whether the observed effect is indicative solely of a presence-based risk or if it exhibits a dose-response relationship. This can be achieved by determining bacterial concentration rather than merely bacterial proportion within an ecological niche, such as the microbiome. The observed microbial differences are more likely to represent secondary correlates rather than primary drivers of infertility46. Recent research into the relationship between microbiota and outcomes in medically assisted procreation has highlighted a new diagnostic and therapeutic direction47. The predominance of Lactobacillus spp. in the vaginal microbiota is associated with better success rates, in contrast to Staphylococcus spp. and E. coli. A similar association has been observed between the composition of sperm microbiota and in vitro fertilization success, with a reduced Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio and Bacteroides spp. leading to improved outcomes48. Antibiotic treatment, specifically doxycycline, has been associated with enhancement of sperm parameters, reduction leukospermia, and increasing of pregnancy rates, suggesting an impact on the microbial environment of spermatozoa36,49. These results are further supported by rare pilot studies in humans describing improved sperm parameters following the administration of different probiotic strains (L. rhamnosus, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, L. fermentum, Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp.)3,50,51. These strains have been shown to improve sperm motility and reduce the rate of DNA fragmentation. In a similar vein, supplementation with symbiotics (including L. paracasei) has been associated with enhanced motility, number, and typical morphology52,53. Another prospect, microbiota transplantation, applied to the sperm microbiota of mice, has been associated with improvement of sperm parameters in mice on an unbalanced diet54. However, to utilise these findings to develop interventions for patients with infertility—whether based on probiotics or on modulating the microbiome through antibiotic therapy—it is necessary to conduct appropriate interventional studies (using therapies, but also habitus modification) to assess clinical feasibility, efficacy. Furthermore, larger sample sizes must be set up to focus on this pathophysiological situation (as in the present study, some group present low number of patients). In addition, it appears pertinent to extend this characterisation by investigating other components of the sperm microbiome (including the virome and the mycobiome), implementing more regular longitudinal follow-up (to document the impact of the interval between ejaculations), examining the sperm microbiome in diverse sexual orientations (MSM vs. heterosexual men) and, finally, assessing the impact of the environmental exposome (by comparing urban vs. rural residences, or low-resource countries compared to other countries).

Conclusions

The field of reproductive medicine has evidenced a growing interest in microbiological management. The findings of this study have the potential to pave the way for the consideration of preventive and/or supplementation measures (focusing on adaptation of red meat, fibre, fat and fibre intake, but also limitation of tobacco, alcohol, and coffee consumption), particularly in the context of treatment for medically assisted procreation. A wide range of therapeutic hypotheses are currently under investigation, and future studies may explore whether modulation of sperm microbiota (especially Bacillota and Pseudomonadota, with a focus on Actinomycetota and Staphylococcus) via probiotics could enhance outcomes in assisted reproduction treatments. Nevertheless, it is imperative to acknowledge that subsequent investigations, incorporating interventions designed to regulate habitus modulation and microbiome modification through pro-/anti-biotics, may be necessary prior to reaching any definitive conclusions.
