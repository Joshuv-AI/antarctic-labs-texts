Twenty‐Four Hour Rhythms in Cardiovascular Physiology
Authors: Morten B. Thomsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany physiological parameters, such as heart rate and blood pressure, display pronounced daily rhythms, with significant differences between day and night levels. The ability to anticipate the 24 h cycle of ambient light confers an adaptive advantage, allowing organisms to prepare for periods of activity and rest. Considering chronophysiology is essential when designing, conducting, and interpreting laboratory experiments because of the often considerable amplitude of the rhythms. This review introduces a straightforward mathematical tool to detect 24 h rhythms in physiological datasets. Next, the review examines 24‐h diurnal rhythms in heart rate, QT interval, and blood pressure, based on telemetry recordings from conscious, freely moving mice. These examples illustrate how long‐term, continuous monitoring of physiological parameters enables precise characterization of daily cycles when present. Furthermore, the large datasets obtained through methods such as telemetry make it possible to determine whether the observed rhythms are dependent on heart rate. Circadian and diurnal rhythms play an essential role in cardiovascular physiology, influencing key parameters such as heart rate and blood pressure. Considering chronophysiology is therefore essential when designing, conducting, and interpreting physiological studies.

Introduction

It takes about 24 h for the Earth to rotate once around its own axis; the exact time varies with the Earth's orbit. This near‐24 h period is further divided into a period of daylight and a period of darkness which has a critical impact on physiology in all living organisms. The benefit of being able to anticipate and adapt to changes in the environment based on the Earth's rotation is an evolutionary advantage. Internal, biological clocks are the basis for this anticipation, and the rhythm of these clocks is about (circa) 24 h (diem), i.e., circadian. Thus, circadian rhythms are oscillations of a given physiological parameter with a period of about 24 h, even in the absence of external stimuli, e.g., light, alarm clocks, or food availability. When we are exposed to one or more external time cues, the 24 h rhythm is diurnal. In biology, diurnal and nocturnal can be used as opposites—when the animal is active during the day or night, respectively, but here, diurnal rhythms mean that a time cue (zeitgeber) is present.

These 24 h rhythms are important to acknowledge in research, in medical practice, and in physiology in particular. Disruption of the diurnal rhythm aggravates maladaptive cardiac remodeling in mice after myocardial infarction [1]. Altered light cycles, either mimicking the seasonal light hours in the lab [2] or mimicking time‐zone travels [3], modulate energy metabolism and cardiac arrhythmia susceptibility, respectively. If a lab trainee performs experiments early in the morning because he or she is an early starter, can the data be compared to those from another lab trainee who is a night owl that runs experiments much later in the day?

The aim of this review is to outline the basic principles of chronophysiology, rather than to offer an exhaustive overview of the field. Here, I define chronophysiology as a branch within chronobiology that deals with how physiological systems, e.g., blood pressure and heart rate, are timed and synchronized. The focus then shifts to cardiovascular physiology, presenting a practical method for assessing whether a given parameter exhibits a 24‐h rhythmic pattern. Additionally, because heart rate plays a significant role in cardiovascular physiology, the review includes a brief discussion on how to account for its effects, with a special focus on QT intervals. Most examples are drawn from studies in mice, as this species is commonly used as a model for cardiovascular physiology and pathology.

Cellular Clock

Every cell type of the body has a molecular clock and depending on the organ, a significant fraction of the expressed genes have a circadian expression profile [4]. The body's master clock is situated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus in the anterior part of the hypothalamus, right above the crossing of the optic nerve. During diurnal conditions, the nucleus receives retinal information and integrates it with other information of, e.g., physical activity and food intake, after which it entrains the many peripheral or cellular clocks throughout the body. In the heart, 8%–13% of all the expressed genes have a 24 h rhythm [5, 6]. During circadian conditions, where no external stimuli are received, the suprachiasmatic nucleus still entrains the peripheral clocks, but with the internal period of the nucleus, which is slightly different than 24 h [7]. Interestingly, the mechanisms and components allowing communication between the central clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the peripheral clocks have yet to be identified, although melatonin and cortisol are important pieces in the puzzle [8, 9]. When the suprachiasmatic nucleus is ablated, peripheral clocks are no longer entrained and quickly become asynchronous; however, this simple, hierarchical view of the system has been challenged. Regulation of biological timing is more complex including neuronal and non‐neuronal mechanisms and crosstalk between peripheral clocks that all contribute to time regulation in the intact organism [10, 11].

Central in the molecular clock is a system of transcription‐translation feedback loops [12]. It starts with the heterodimerization of the two proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 that facilitates transcription of the Per and Cry genes. The resulting proteins translocate to the nucleus, interact with CLOCK and BMAL1, and repress transcription of Clock, Bmal1, and the Per and Cry gene families. The transcriptional inhibition of Clock and Bmal1 is lifted as the Per and Cry gene products are degraded, which initiates a new cycle in the feedback loop. CLOCK and BMAL1 also affect transcriptional activity on a host of output genes that show rhythmic expression [4]. This cell‐autonomous oscillatory mechanism is found in virtually all cells and tissues of the body and is expertly reviewed elsewhere [13, 14, 15].

In cardiovascular physiology, it is generally acknowledged that the 24 h variation in blood pressure is very important. Hypertensive patients who cannot lower their blood pressure at night are called ‘non‐dippers’ and are at increased risk for cardiovascular complications [16, 17]. In healthy adults, blood pressure may drop by as much as 50 mmHg at night as a result of circadian changes in combination with differences in physical activity and environmental changes [18]. Endothelial function and platelet aggregation also follow 24 h cycles, and as a result, myocardial infarction and stroke exhibit circadian variation with the highest incidence in the early morning hours [19, 20]. Sudden death occurs also predominantly in the morning hours [21, 22], as does ventricular tachyarrhythmia in patients with different monitoring devices [23, 24].

How to Determine the Presence of a 24 h Rhythm

Figure 1 shows the principal elements of analyzing a 24 h rhythm in any given physiological parameter, e.g., heart rate or blood pressure. Time, light, and dark phases are indicated on the x‐axis, so it is shown that a time cue is available in the system—here, the light is turned on, and this time point (zeitgeber time, ZT) is t = 0. A minimum of 24 h should be depicted on the graph. The value of the physiological variable in question is depicted on the y‐axis. A visual inspection of the data over a period of 24 h or more is often useful. Any regular, repeating patterns will give important clues to whether any rhythm is present.

Parameters obtained from a four‐parameter fit to experimental data with a 24 h diurnal rhythm. The mesor is the mean value of the parameter; the amplitude is the maximum deviation from the mesor. Thus, the distance from peak to trough is two times the amplitude. The period is the time duration of one full oscillation and is 24 h here. The phase is the time point of the maximal amplitude, which can be in hours or in a fraction of 2π, which is the full period.

Data can be fitted to a 4‐parameter sinusoidal fit, and the mesor (midline estimating statistic of rhythm), amplitude, phase, and period of the fit are obtained (Figure 1). The mesor is the 24 h mean of the parameter around which the parameter oscillates. The amplitude is the peak of the parameter from the mesor. The period is the wavelength or the time it takes to complete a full cycle. Sometimes, the period can be constrained to 24 h. The phase depends on the timing of the peak—see later.

If the amplitude is statistically different from zero, the presence of a 24 h rhythm in the given physiological parameter is accepted. The mathematical fit requires that sufficient data are available: If only two to four measurements over the cycle are available, another strategy for analysis should be used. There should be continuous data collection or at least a data point every 3–4 h for a 24 h period [25]. Note that repeated measurements from the same subject are correlated, and this should be accounted for during the mathematical and statistical phases of the analysis.

A cosine model can be fitted to the data: Fx=Mesor+Amplitude×cos2π×xPeriod+Phase where F(x) represents the function that models the physiological parameter. The mesor, amplitude, and period are explained above. The phase is the relative angular displacement on the full circle between ZT0 and the peak of the oscillation. A diurnal rhythm can be acknowledged if the fit to the data produces an amplitude that is significantly different from zero. If serial data are available, e.g., continuous blood pressure recordings from the same animal for 24 h, it is an advantage to fit data from each animal and evaluate the mesor, amplitude, period, and phase in group comparisons.

Both sinusoidal and cosinor analyses can be used in chronobiology research. While both cosine and sine functions can represent the same periodic phenomena (differing only by a phase shift of a quarter of the period), many use the cosine form because it simplifies the interpretation of the phase: in the cosine fit, the peak is at ZT0 if the phase is 0. In the sine fit, the peak is at ZT6 if the phase is 0. Nevertheless, both sine and cosine functions can be used, but the cosine form is standard in the field and aligns with established reporting conventions [26].

Presence of 24 h Rhythms in Heart Rate

Many hemodynamic and electrophysiological parameters are influenced by heart rate. Heart rate is high during the active phase of the 24 h, aligning with increased levels of physical activity, sympathetic drive, food intake, body temperature, blood pressure, etc. In humans, this is during daylight; in mice, it is during the night. In normal, conscious mice, heart rate is between 500 and 600 beats/min. Figure 2A shows the 24 h rhythm in RR intervals, where each data point on the graph is the hourly mean from seven mice [27]. The cosine fit to the data reveals a diurnal rhythm with an amplitude of 11.0 [8.5 to 13.5] ms and a mesor of 110 [108 to 112] ms (mean and [95% confidence intervals]). The heart rate reaches about 750 beats/min in short bouts associated with high physical activity. It falls just below 300 beats per minute during the first hours of light, when the mice sleep. Ideally, measurements of physiological parameters should be done at comparable heart rates because the given parameter of interest may be heart‐rate dependent. For example, QT intervals shorten when heart rate increases in larger animals, including humans [28, 29]. This appears to be the same in conscious mice [30, 31, 32].

Heart rate is determined by the sinus node where HCN channels act in concert with a calcium‐based leak cycle to cause a spontaneous depolarization at regular time intervals, setting the beating rate of the heart. A 24 h rhythm in HCN expression appears to be a critical component of the diurnal rhythm in heart rate [33]. Notwithstanding, the autonomic nervous system affects heart rate and the 24 h rhythm herein. β1‐adrenergic receptors on the cardiomyocytes of the sinus node convey an increase in heart rate once the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system is active. Conversely, when the parasympathetic branch is active, the cholinergic M2 receptors are activated and heart rate decreases. The sympathetic nervous system is mostly active in the active phases in mice during the dark period. Conversely, the parasympathetic nervous system is more active during rest. Complete pharmacological block of the autonomic nervous system reduces heart rate in mice considerably and removes the 24 h rhythm [34, 35, 36, 37]. Assessment of the heart‐rate variability is a frequently used tool to gauge the activities (either basic tone or responsiveness) of the two branches of the autonomic nervous system. Expert protocols are available for determining heart‐rate variability from mice [38]; however, there appears to be a paucity of detailed studies on the 24 h rhythms in heart‐rate variability in mice reported in the literature [36, 39]. Additionally, focused studies on the 24 h rhythms in heart‐rate variability are necessary to fully understand the diurnal and circadian rhythms in heart‐rate variability and how heart rate itself is impacting this.

Presence of 24 h Rhythms in QT Intervals

Cardiac repolarization measured as QT intervals on the ECG also shows a diurnal rhythm in humans, with shorter values during the day [40, 41]. Abnormalities in the QT interval duration—whether it is prolonged or shortened—are associated with increased arrhythmia risk. Prolonged QT intervals are known from the congenital long‐QT syndromes and the drug‐induced long QT adverse effects. Both forms are accompanied by an increased risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmias that may culminate in sudden cardiac death. The short‐QT syndromes are much rarer, most often due to inheritable ion‐channel mutations, but also linked to ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death. Thus, accurate QT interval measurements are critical for clinical management, and in preclinical research, they can indicate situations of elevated proarrhythmic risk. Perturbations of 24 h rhythms in the QT interval and cardiac electrophysiology in general affect the risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [42, 43].

Figure 2B shows how the QT interval has a 24 h cycle. The mathematical fit to the data identifies a cycle with an amplitude of 2.6 [2.0 to 3.2] ms and a mesor of 48.0 [47.6 to 48.4] ms. The QT interval is longest during the inactive phase when the lights are on and shortest during the active, dark phase. The underlying molecular mechanisms are not clear. Kchnh2‐encoded KV11.1 potassium channels have solid 24 h rhythms in cardiomyocytes [44, 45], but the current has very limited, if any, impact on repolarization in the adult mouse heart [46, 47]. Kcnd2‐encoded KV4.2 channels also have a 24 h rhythm, and this current is expressed in the mouse ventricles. 24 h rhythms are preserved in mice lacking the KV4.2 auxiliary subunit [39], suggesting that this is also not the central ion current responsible for the 24 h rhythm in QT interval. A complex interplay between a number of ionic currents, some of which cycle with a 24 h period, almost certainly dictates the QT rhythm.

Genetically modified mice that lack a functional Bmal1 gene in the heart have dysfunctional cardiac molecular clock. These cardiac‐specific Bmal1 knockout (CBK) mice also have depressed metabolism of glucose and ketone bodies and experience heart failure at around 6 months of age [6, 48, 49]. Twenty‐four hour recording of the ECG in these mice can be used to verify the hypothesis that repolarization has a diurnal and circadian rhythm independent of the 24 h cycle in heart rate. Heart rate is slower and QT intervals are longer in CBK mice compared to control mice with an intact cardiac clock [27]. Correcting the QT interval for heart rate according to Mitchell et al. [30] removed much of the diurnal rhythm in the QT interval [27]. To circumvent this potential problem, we took advantage of the large‐scale data set obtained in recording 24 h ECGs in rodents [39].

Exploiting the Power of Telemetry Recordings

Radiotelemetry recordings of physiological parameters, e.g., ECG, blood pressure, or blood glucose, provide the advantage of obtaining continuous data from conscious and unrestrained animals from their home cage. Recording ECG or blood pressure with a mean heart rate between 500 and 600 beats per minute for 24 h gives about 800 000 heart beats to analyze. There are clear advantages of such large datasets; however, there are also potential pitfalls. Especially, blinding to intervention or group during analysis is important if the effect size is small since much can be lost in the natural variation in heart rate with, e.g., physical activity. Recordings of ECGs can be affected by the physical activity of the mouse and by external electrical noise. Much can be improved by enabling digital filters to the signal, but they may distort the signal and may not suffice. Long recordings of many ECG complexes will greatly enhance the quality of the data analysis—either by facilitating signal‐averaging analysis, which effectively removes random noise and enhances the signal, or by allowing analysis in shorter noise‐free periods.

A strategy to correct the QT interval for heart‐rate fluctuation circumvents the imperfect mathematical heart‐rate correction. It takes advantage of the large amount of data that is available for analysis: By choosing only those ECG complexes with an RR interval just around the mean, all analysis is performed at the same heart rate. In Figure 2C only the RR intervals between 114.5 and 115.5 ms are selected for analysis. On average, 544 ± 154 (range 300–877) ECG complexes per mouse per hour were included in this sub‐analysis. Because of the selection, the mathematical fit shows no 24 h rhythm (amplitude = 0 ms). When QT intervals are measured in ECG complexes only within this narrow RR range, the heart rate is identical, and correction becomes obsolete (Figure 2D). The amplitude of the 24 h rhythm in QT intervals is 2.1 ms [1.4 to 2.8], suggesting that there is a 24 h rhythm in repolarization duration that is independent of heart rate. In this approach, any change in QT interval is independent of heart rate.

When this approach was used in the genetically modified CBK mice, clear 24 h oscillations in the QT interval were present in both CBK mice and in littermate control mice [27]. Additionally, the QT intervals were longer in the CBK mice compared to the control mice at identical heart rates. Thus, the diurnal and circadian rhythms in the QT intervals are independent of the 24 h cycle in heart rate, and the relative bradycardia in the CBK mice is not the underlying cause of the repolarization prolongation in these mice [27].

Controlling heart rate by electrical pacing is another way to check if repolarization prolongation is heart rate dependent [50]. In isolated, perfused hearts, without influence from any neurohumoral factors, there is no difference in the intrinsic rate between hearts from CBK mice and wild‐type control mice, and during ventricular pacing, repolarization duration is longer in CBK mice [27]. This supports the conclusion that repolarization is prolonged in mice without a functional molecular cardiac clock and that this is not a consequence of the relative bradycardia these mice have [27].

Circadian Rhythm in Blood Pressure

Human heart rate and blood pressure change during the day starting with a sharp increase in the morning and a slower fall during the evening [40], partly explained by voluntary physical exercise. Notwithstanding, in study subjects keeping supine, calm and awake there are also clear 24 h cycles in heart rate and blood pressure. Patients with spinal cord injury at the cervical level have no 24 h cycle in blood pressure, but the rhythm appears to be preserved in patients with spinal cord injuries affecting the lower extremities only [51].

Blood pressure can also be analyzed by means of telemetry (Figure 3). It is widely accepted that mice experience an increase in blood pressure during the night [52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57]; however, comprehensive analysis of 24 h blood pressure recordings in mice, both with and without sinusoidal fitting of the data, is rarely found in scientific literature. Using baseline data from a previously published study, the 24 h rhythm in blood pressure was determined from 15 mice (Figure 3). A 24 h rhythm in systolic and diastolic blood pressure is clear from Figure 3. The 24 h mesors were 119 [118 to 120] and 91 [90 to 92] mmHg in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, respectively. The amplitudes were 6.8 [5.5 to 8.2] and 6.2 [5.0 to 7.4] mmHg, respectively (Figure 3).

Conclusions

Circadian rhythms are vital in research and medicine. Disruptions can worsen heart conditions, alter metabolism, and increase susceptibility to cardiac arrhythmias. Experimental timing can significantly affect the results of physiological studies.

Cosinor analysis to fit data and determine key parameters, like mesor, amplitude, phase, and period, is used to determine if a physiological parameter follows a 24 h rhythm. Sufficient and frequent data points are needed for reliable analysis.

Telemetry allows for large‐scale, continuous physiological data collection in conscious animals, enabling more precise analysis of circadian rhythms. Selecting data within narrow heart rate ranges can eliminate the need for mathematical corrections and reveal true circadian effects.

Using large‐scale telemetry data from conscious mice, 24 h rhythms in blood pressure, heart rate, and QT intervals are readily identifiable. Proper experimental design and analysis are essential to account for chronophysiology.
