Supraglottic airway devices in awake tracheal intubation: a viable alternative to fiberoptic and video laryngoscopy
Authors: Mehmet Yilmaz, Ahmet Yuksek, Ayse Zeynep Turan Civraz, Merve Yazici Kara, Sema Candan, Kemal Tolga Saracoglu, Ayten Saracoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAwake tracheal intubation is advised for patients with known difficult airways. While flexible bronchoscopy is the conventional technique, video laryngoscopy offers faster intubation. Recent studies reveal that supraglottic airway devices can also be effectively used for awake intubation; however, few investigations compare their effectiveness with that of flexible bronchoscopy and video laryngoscopy. This study aims to compare the effectiveness of these three. methods: flexible bronchoscopy, video laryngoscopy, and awake tracheal intubation using supraglottic airway devices.MethodsPatients were divided into three groups: fiberoptic bronchoscopy, video laryngoscopy, and supraglottic airway groups. All patients received Sedation with remifentanil and midazolam, Topical anesthesia with inhaled and sprayed lidocaine, Oxygenation with high-flow oxygen therapy, and intubation Performed by the same operator, known as the ‘STOP’ protocol. The primary outcomes encompassed first-attempt success rate, alternative method success rate, and overall success rates. Secondary outcomes included intubation times and procedural complications.ResultsSuccess and complication rates were similar across groups. The supraglottic airway group had a mean vocal cord imaging time of 74.93 ± 55 s, longer than video laryngoscopy but shorter than fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Intubation times were longer for the supraglottic airway group (210.0 ± 120 s) than for video laryngoscopy but shorter than fiberoptic bronchoscopy.ConclusionThe awake supraglottic airway technique offers advantages over traditional awake intubation methods, including the ability to monitor tidal volume and maintain continuous oxygenation throughout the procedure. This technique achieves comparable success rates, intubation times, and complication rates to video laryngoscopy and fiberoptic bronchoscopy, making it a reliable alternative for difficult airways.Trial registrationhttps://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06279416, registered on 19.02.2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03331-4.Keywords: Awake tracheal intubation, Difficult airway, Supraglottic airway devices, Fiberoptic laryngoscopy, Video laryngoscopy

Introduction

Guidelines recommend awake tracheal intubation (ATI) when a difficult airway is anticipated [1, 2]. ATI helps prevent the potential risks and complications associated with difficult airway management by preserving two critical physiological traits: spontaneous breathing and intrinsic airway tone [3–7].

Traditionally, ATI is performed using flexible bronchoscopy (FB). However, in recent years, video laryngoscopy (VL) has emerged as an alternative technique [8, 9]. Both methods have shown high success rates, although VL is linked to shorter intubation times [10, 11]. While direct comparisons between FB and VL are still limited in the literature, studies are on the rise [11]. A systematic review and meta-analysis involving 429 patients revealed that while VL facilitated faster ATI, both techniques had comparable safety profiles and success rates [10]. Additionally, Dutta et al. assessed cervical spine motion during ATI in patients undergoing cervical spine instability surgery, observing greater mobility at the C1/C2 level with VL compared to FB [12].

Supraglottic airway devices (SADs) serve as an essential rescue technique in cases of unexpected difficult airways [13]. SADs can independently provide adequate ventilation in difficult airway scenarios and also facilitate tracheal intubation in conjunction with fiberoptics [2, 14]. Current guidelines recognize SADs as a conduit for fiberoptic-assisted ATI [1, 15]. There are clinical studies on the use of SAD in awake intubation [16, 17]. These studies suggest that using SAD during ATI is a safe and effective alternative to classical ATI: FB and ATI: VL. However, we did not find any studies in the literature comparing the use of SAD during ATI with other ATI techniques.

We apply classical methods for awake intubation (ATI), such as nasal fiberoptic and oral videolaryngoscopic techniques. Also, we utilize a SAD-mediated approach that employs an Aintree catheter and a fiberoptic bronchoscope. This method offers a distinct advantage by maximizing patient safety. During awake SAD placement, we can continuously monitor the spontaneous respiratory rate and end-tidal CO2 levels. By using a swivel connector, we maintain continuous oxygenation throughout the procedure.

This prospective observational study primarily aims to compare the success rates of ATI using FB, VL, and SAD. The secondary objective is to assess and compare the intubation times and complication rates across these three ATI techniques.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, observational study was conducted in the operating theaters of our University Hospital from January 2024 to December 2024, following approval by the local ethics committee (protocol number: 2023-31, date: 28.12.2023) and retrospectively registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06279416 on 19.02.2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were also collected and presented in accordance with CONSORT guidelines. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) risk score of I or II, an anticipated difficult airway, and a need for awake tracheal intubation. Patients were excluded if they were emergency cases, mouth opening smaller than 1.5 cm, uncooperative, failed to provide written informed consent, or had a known allergy to lidocaine.

Patients and techniques

In our clinic, awake intubation is frequently performed in patients with a difficult airway using nasal fiberoptic, oral video laryngoscopy and SAD. Patients without any known limitations for the fiberoptic, videolaryngoscope, or SAD awake intubation methods were included in the study. Randomization was not implemented due to the observational nature of the study. Patients were divided into three groups based on the chosen method of awake intubation. The awake intubation method (FB, VL, or SAD) was determined for each patient by an experienced anesthesiologist based on anatomical factors (inter-incisor distance, neck circumference, presence of supraglottic pathology) and surgical requirements (need for nasal/oral route). The decision not to randomize was intentional to reflect real-world clinical practice where patient-specific individualized approaches are prioritized. With a single, experienced anesthesiologist, procedural uniformity was achieved and operator-related variability was minimized.

Upon arrival in the operating theatre, all patients underwent standard monitoring, including electrocardiogram (ECG), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), non-invasive arterial blood pressure, and temperature measurements. Sedation was achieved with a remifentanil (ULTIVA, GlaxoSmithKline Manufacturing S.p.A, Parma, Italy) infusion (4–10 mcg.kg−1.h−1) and a 0.5 mg bolus of midazolam (DORMICUM, DEVA İlaç, Istanbul, Türkiye) with an additional 0.5 mg administered for persistent anxiety. Midazolam doses were adjusted in low doses and increments to maintain the sedation level at a minimal level that would not impair cooperation. Sedation level was not measured with any numerical value. For airway topicalization, 100 mg of 2% lidocaine (ARITMAL, Osel Drogs, Istanbul, Türkiye) was nebulized over 15 min, supplemented by direct administration of liquid 10% lidocaine (XYLOCAINE®, AstraZeneca AB, Södertälje, Swedish): 40 mg each to the right and left base of the tongue, 40 mg to the midline of the mouth, and 20 mg to each nostril. High-flow oxygen therapy (40–60 L.min−1) was initiated upon admission. Reinforced endotracheal tubes (ETT) were used in all patient groups.

ATI techniques

Group FB

The patient’s dominant nostril identified for breathing was established. A reinforced ETT with an inner-diameter of 6 to 7 mm was preloaded onto the fiberoptic bronchoscope (Broncoflex 3.9/1.4 Agile, The Surgical Company, France), and nasal dilation was performed using a nasopharyngeal airway. The fiberoptic bronchoscope was advanced through the dominant nostril, allowing visualization of the vocal cords, tracheal rings, and carina. The ETT was then carefully guided over the bronchoscope into the trachea. Correct placement was confirmed through capnography and auscultation prior to anesthesia induction.

Group VL

After airway topicalization, a C-MAC® D-blade (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) video laryngoscope was introduced through the mouth. The epiglottis was visualized, and the ETT preloaded onto the stylet was advanced 2–4 cm beyond the vocal cords. Proper placement was confirmed by capnography and auscultation before anesthesia induction.

Group SAD

An Aintree catheter (Cook India Medical Devices, Tamilnadu, India) was preloaded onto the fiberoptic bronchoscope (BRONCOFLEX® AGILE, The Surgical Company, France), both lubricated with gel (Fig. 1) [18]. After topicalization, patients opened their mouths for the placement of an i-gel® SAD (Intersurgical Ltd, Berkshire, UK). The SAD was connected to an anesthetic circuit via a swivel connector, ensuring spontaneous respiration and adequate ventilation. An assistant secured the SAD while the fiberoptic bronchoscope, loaded with the Aintree catheter, was advanced through the apex of the swivel connector. The epiglottis, vocal cords, tracheal rings, and carina were sequentially visualized. The Aintree catheter was positioned above the carina, the bronchoscope was removed, and the SAD was withdrawn over the Aintree catheter. A reinforced ETT with an inner-diameter of 7 mm was then advanced over the Aintree catheter into the trachea, after which the catheter was removed, and the ETT was connected to the anesthesia circuit.

Preloaded Aintree catheter onto a fiberoptic bronchoscope

Correct placement was confirmed by capnography and auscultation before anesthesia induction in all groups. Subsequently, tube position was optimized with a flexible bronchoscope. Ancillary maneuvers used during the technique were recorded.

Anesthesia induction and data collection

Anesthesia was induced in all patients using propofol (PROPOFOL-PF %1, AROMA Drogs, Tekirdağ, Türkiye) at a dosage of 2–2.5 mg.kg−1, fentanyl (FENTAVER, Osel Drogs, Istanbul, Türkiye) at 2 mcg.kg−1, and rocuronium bromide (ESMERON, Merck Sharp Dohme Drogs, Istanbul, Türkiye) at 0.5 mg.kg−1. Demographic data, Mallampati score, inter-incisor distance, thyromental distance, neck circumference, and history of airway obstruction were recorded. The initial ATI technique was attempted first, with alternative ATI techniques employed in failure cases (Fig. 2: Flow Chart). ATI failure was defined as the inability to achieve successful tracheal intubation after two attempts. The practitioner’s decision influenced the choice of alternative technique.

Additionally, the number of attempts, optimization maneuvers, the incidence of SpO₂ <94%, and complications including bleeding, arrhythmia, nausea, and vomiting were documented.

Statistical analysis

In evaluating the results obtained from the study, statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22. A one-way ANOVA test was employed for continuous variables with a normal distribution, while the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied to those that did not meet the normal distribution criteria. The Independent Samples T test was also used for variables that did not fulfill the normal distribution requirements, and the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for those that lacked normal distribution. Additionally, the Chi-Square test was implemented to analyze discrete variables. The results were assessed at the 95% confidence interval, with significance determined at the p < 0.05 level.

Sample size analysis

In a comparable three-group study assessing endotracheal intubation times, a power analysis was conducted using the significant results from the comparison of laryngeal mask intubation techniques. It was concluded that the three groups should include a minimum total of 45 patients to achieve an effect size of 0.56, a margin of error of 0.05, and a power of 0.90 [19].

Tracheal intubation was performed using the FB method in fifteen patients, the VL method in fifteen patients, and a SAD in fifteen patients. T1, T2, and T3 durations were compared between groups. Statistically significant differences in T1, T2, and T3 durations were identified among the groups. The T1 duration was significantly higher in the FB group and lower in the VL group. The T2 duration was significantly higher in the SAD group and lower in the VL group. The number of attempts and the number of optimization maneuvers performed were statistically similar between groups. Ancillary techniques such as chin lift were used in the ATI: SAD technique, and BURP was used in the ATI: VL. Head rotation and assistance were used in advancing the tube during the ATI: FB technique. Complications such as hypoxemia, arrhythmia, nausea, and vomiting were not observed in any patient (Table 4).

Discussion

The time required to visualize the vocal cords and the total tracheal intubation time for the SAD groups were 74.93 ± 55 s and 210.0 ± 120 s, respectively. These values were significantly higher than those in the VL group but significantly lower than those in the FB group. The time from vocal cord visualization to confirmation of tracheal intubation via capnography in the SAD group was 128.4 ± 55.8 s, which was significantly higher than in both the VL and FB groups. However, the SAD technique demonstrated statistical similarity to the FB and VL techniques regarding its use as the primary method, as an alternative technique, and in overall success rates. Additionally, the need for optimization maneuvers and the complication rate in the SAD group were comparable to those in the FB and VL groups.

Several studies have compared FB and VL awake intubation techniques [12, 13, 18, 20]. However, no prior research has directly compared the SAD with FB and VL guided awake tracheal intubation. To our knowledge, this study is the first prospective investigation addressing this gap in the literature.

Lim et al. successfully performed awake fiberoptic intubation using the SAD technique in a series of 10 patients [16]. However, their approach differed from ours as they preloaded the fiberoptic bronchoscope with an endotracheal tube instead of using the Aintree catheter-guided method recommended by the Difficult Airway Society (DAS) guidelines [18]. We believe that advancing the endotracheal tube directly through the SAD without an Aintree catheter can be technically challenging, as it does not permit optimization maneuvers during ETT advancement. In contrast, our approach involves advancing the fiberoptic bronchoscope preloaded with an Aintree catheter through the SAD to the carina, leaving the Aintree catheter in place, and then sequentially removing the fiberoptic bronchoscope and SAD. The ETT is then advanced over the Aintree catheter, enabling the use of optimization maneuvers such as the “hanging maneuver” with a laryngoscope.

Another key difference between our method and that of Lim et al. is the oxygenation strategy. Lim et al. provided oxygen therapy via insufflation at 10–15 L.min-1 through a nasal cannula after advancing the fiberoptic bronchoscope. However, this method does not allow real-time monitoring of spontaneous breathing and does not support positive pressure ventilation in cases of respiratory suppression due to excessive sedation. In contrast, our approach involves connecting the SAD to the anesthesia circuit with a swivel connector, allowing continuous monitoring of spontaneous respiration and providing positive pressure support when needed. This feature enhances patient safety and aligns with the fundamental principle of awake intubation—preserving spontaneous breathing. Hypoxemia due to respiratory depression during the procedure was not observed in any patient in our study, and no patient required respiratory support with positive pressure.

Wong et al. highlighted certain advantages and disadvantages of SAD-mediated fiberoptic intubation in awake patients [21]. We agree with the first two limitations they mentioned: (1) the technique is unsuitable for patients with limited mouth opening, and (2) it is not appropriate for cases requiring nasal intubation due to surgical site considerations. However, they also noted that the maximum ETT diameter that can pass through the SAD is constrained by the size of the SAD ventilation port, which may lead to ventilation failure in patients needing larger ETTs because of high airway resistance [22]. This limitation is mitigated in our SAD technique through the use of the Aintree catheter, which facilitates the placement of an appropriately sized ETT tailored to the patient’s needs.

The 2020 Awake Intubation Guidelines describe the STOP framework, which consists of Sedation (S), Topicalization (T), Oxygenation (O), and Performance (P). Sedation aims to reduce procedural anxiety while maintaining patient cooperation and ensuring spontaneous breathing. The guidelines recommend midazolam and remifentanil for sedation, but Irwin has suggested that these agents may lead to excessive sedation and respiratory depression, advocating for dexmedetomidine as an alternative [1, 23]. The awake SAD technique reduces the risk of hypoxemia during periods of respiratory suppression by allowing continuous positive pressure support.

Studies comparing awake FB and VL techniques have reported similar success and complication rates but have consistently shown that the VL technique is associated with a significantly shorter intubation time than FB [10, 11, 24]. In our study, the first-attempt success rate, alternative technique success rate, and overall success rate were statistically comparable across the FB, VL, and SAD groups, aligning with findings from previous literature.

Consistent with prior studies, the T1, T2, and T3 times in the VL group were significantly shorter than those in the FB group. The T1 and T3 times for the SAD group were higher than those for the VL group but lower than those for the FB group, while the T2 time for SAD was significantly longer than that for both VL and FB. The awake VL technique offered the advantage of rapid glottic visualization and ETT advancement, while the SAD technique, despite additional steps such as SAD placement and Aintree catheter manipulation, still exhibited superior glottic visualization compared to awake FB.

In our study, all three awake intubation techniques resulted in comparable hemodynamic changes, with similar increases in systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and peak heart rate following tracheal intubation. These changes were not statistically significant, suggesting that all three methods maintain hemodynamic stability. We monitored for complications such as hypoxemia, arrhythmia, nausea, and vomiting; however, none of the patients in our study developed any complications.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. The most notable limitation is its prospective observational design, which did not allow for the randomization of patient groups. This could introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. The study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the external validity of the results. Multicenter studies would provide broader applicability and more robust conclusions. Although this is the first prospective study comparing these three awake intubation techniques, the sample size may still be insufficient to detect subtle differences in success rates and complications. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings. Finally, the study focused on immediate procedural success and complication rates. However, long-term outcomes, such as post-intubation airway complications or patient-reported comfort levels, were not assessed.

Conclusion

Compared to other awake intubation techniques, the SAD method provides distinct advantages, such as the ability to monitor tidal volume during spontaneous breathing and to offer positive pressure support in cases of excessive sedation. The awake SAD technique demonstrated a comparable total intubation time to awake VL and FB. Furthermore, the success rates, need for optimization maneuvers, and complication rates were similar among the three techniques. Given these advantages, SAD represents a safe and effective alternative for awake intubation in patients with anticipated difficult airways.
