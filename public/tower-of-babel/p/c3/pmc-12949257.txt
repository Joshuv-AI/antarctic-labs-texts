Affective dimensions of self-efficacy in palliative and end-of-life care: A cross-cultural reflection on the study by Natuhwera et al. (2025)
Authors: Elia Firda Mufidah, Mudhar Mudhar
Published: 2026

Re: Natuhwera, G., Namisango, E., & Ellis, P. (2025). Knowledge, self-efficacy, and correlates in palliative and end-of-life care: quantitative insights from final-year nursing and medical students in a mixed-methods study. Palliative Care & Social Practice, 19, 1–22.

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article by Natuhwera et al. (2025) examining predictors of knowledge and self-efficacy among final-year nursing and medical students in Uganda. The study provides valuable empirical insights into educational gaps within palliative and end-of-life care (PELC) training in low- and middle-income countries. The findings on significant factors, gender, course of study, religion, and clinical experience, are informative, particularly regarding the low self-efficacy observed in communication about death, dying, and bereavement.

However, I would like to extend the discussion on the correlates of self-efficacy in palliative care. While the authors rightly identify exposure and training as significant predictors, I believe that self-efficacy in this sensitive domain cannot be fully understood without considering the affective and emotional dimensions of human experience.

According to social cognitive theory, emotional arousal is one of the four primary sources of self-efficacy, alongside mastery experience, vicarious learning, and verbal persuasion.1,2 The ability to regulate emotions mediates the relationship between emotion beliefs (control/acceptance) and distress, such that individuals who can flexibly manage their emotions tend to be more confident and effective in coping with psychological pressure. 3 In PELC, where healthcare professionals constantly confront suffering, loss, and existential pain, self-efficacy is inseparable from affective maturity and emotional resilience. 4 Even with adequate knowledge and clinical exposure, unresolved emotional discomfort or death anxiety can critically weaken the confidence to deliver compassionate care.

From a broader psychological perspective, self-efficacy has often been categorized as a cognitive construct; however, emerging research affirms that it also fundamentally operates within the affective domain. Self-efficacy grows from emotional self-trust and an individual’s affective stance toward their own capability rather than mere logical appraisal.4–6 Self-efficacy results from self-reflective thought that engages both motivational and affective systems. Emotions, in this light, are not simply by-products of efficacy but integral sources that shape and energize it. 7 Self-efficacy thus represents a form of affective knowledge, an inner conviction that aligns emotion, cognition, and behavior into purposeful action. 4

This interpretation resonates profoundly with Kawruh Jiwa, the Javanese psychological philosophy developed by Ki Ageng Suryomentaram, which situates rasa (feeling) as the core of human awareness. Within this framework, confidence and composure arise from raos sakeca, a feeling of inner steadiness or mantap batin reflecting a state of harmony between rasa, pikir, and laku.8–10 Emotional clarity (eling lan waspada, mindful awareness) and acceptance of impermanence (narima ing pandum) nurture tatag lan tentrem, an affective balance analogous to emotional regulation in modern psychology. Hence, affective equilibrium becomes the root of efficacious action.

In this intercultural synthesis, self-efficacy in palliative care should be viewed not only as a function of training and experience, but as an outcome of affective integration, the alignment between emotional awareness and cognitive mastery. Students who cultivate raos sakeca (inner composure) are likely to demonstrate higher confidence and compassion in death-related communication and caregiving, even in contexts of profound emotional distress.

Future research might therefore explore the integration of affective-domain training, including emotional reflection, peer sharing, and culturally grounded practices, into palliative care education. In contexts like Uganda and other culturally rich societies, such integration could strengthen not only cognitive competence but also the emotional and ethical maturity required for truly person-centered end-of-life care.

In sum, the findings by Natuhwera et al. provide a valuable foundation. Yet, advancing palliative care education requires an expanded model of self-efficacy, one that encompasses not only the cognitive and experiential but also the affective and cultural dimensions of human capability. Drawing from both Bandura’s theory and Suryomentaram’s Kawruh Jiwa, self-efficacy may best be understood as an integration of skill, awareness, and inner composure, qualities indispensable to those who accompany others through suffering, dying, and healing.
