Basketball‐Related Injuries Increased in Pediatric Patients Presenting to United States Emergency Departments Between 2019 and 2023 With Strains and Sprains Being the Most Common Injury Types
Authors: Samisha M Khangaonkar, Marshall S Yuan, Calvin J Wang, Ryan Cheng, Billy I Kim, Joseph Manzi, Jay Moran, Andrew E Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo estimate the number, risk factors, and types of basketball‐related injuries in pediatric patients who presented to United States emergency departments between 2019 and 2023, as estimated by the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS).MethodsPediatric patients (<18 years old) with basketball‐related injuries who presented to NEISS‐affiliated emergency departments between 2019 and 2023 were identified. Narratives were retrospectively reviewed and data were categorized by injury type and location. National weighted estimates provided by the NEISS based on geographical location were then used to stratify injuries by patient demographics, discharge disposition, and hospital size. Univariate analyses were conducted to compare hospital admission rates by age group, sex, and injury‐related variables.ResultsFrom 2020 to 2023, the number of estimated basketball‐related pediatric injuries increased 89% for children (ages 7‐11, 19,869 vs. 37,616 estimated injuries) and 64% for adolescents (ages 12‐17, 95,504 vs. 156,599 estimated injuries). Strains, sprains, and fractures represented over half (53%) of all injuries (92,548 estimated injuries). Multivariable analyses showed that children were at a decreased risk for hospitalization (OR: .80, 95% CI: .63‐.99, P = .044) compared with adolescents, whereas men were at an increased risk of hospitalization (OR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.54‐2.67, P < .001) compared with women. Patients who presented to small hospitals (OR: .28, 95% CI: .17‐.43, P < .001) and medium hospitals (OR: .16, 95% CI: .07‐.32, P < .001) were significantly less likely to be admitted compared with those who presented to large, very large, and children's hospitals.ConclusionsThe number of estimated basketball‐related injuries for children and adolescents substantially increased between 2020 and 2023, where strains and sprains represented the most common injury types. Risk factors for hospital admission included older age at the time of presentation, male sex, fractures, dislocations, and larger hospital size.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective case series.

Organized sports are a vital component of youth life and development, with nearly 60 million pediatric sports participants registered annually across the United States. 1 Among these sports, basketball is one of the most popular for children and adolescents. According to Project Play, an initiative launched by the Sports and Society Program of the Aspen Institute, approximately 15% of United States youth between the ages of 6 and 17 (7.3 million children) reported playing basketball regularly in 2023. 2

As participation in the sport continues to grow, basketball‐related injuries, particularly in the pediatric population (defined as those aged <18 years old), have become an increasingly popular area of research. Zynda et al., for instance, utilized the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) database, from 2012 to 2018, and reported an estimated 294,920 annual pediatric basketball‐related injuries, with strains, sprains, fractures, and concussions accounting for the most significant proportion of injuries. 3 Injury prevalence increased 4‐fold from childhood (7‐11 years) to adolescence (13‐17 years), and female players experienced a disproportionate increase in knee injuries and concussions from childhood to adolescence, suggesting the need for the development of additional education and injury prevention measures.

Although prior research has examined basketball‐related injuries in the pediatric population, most of these studies have been limited to specific injury types (e.g. ankle sprains), single‐institution data, and/or data prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic. 3 , 4 , 5 The COVID‐19 pandemic had a profound impact on sports‐related injuries due to deconditioning, resource constraints, and fewer injury prevention strategies. Understanding how injury patterns have evolved after prolonged periods of inactivity and periods when healthcare resources were more limited is critical for updating prevention and treatment strategies and guiding future clinical care. The purpose of this study was to estimate the number, risk factors, and types of basketball‐related injuries in pediatric patients who presented to United States emergency departments between 2019 and 2023, as estimated by the NEISS. We hypothesized that the incidence of basketball‐related injuries increased in the post‐pandemic years, potentially reflecting a resurgence in participation and potential deficits in physical conditioning due to prolonged activity pauses.

METHODS

The current study used data from the NEISS database, operated by the United States Consumer Product Safety Commission and publicly accessible through the NEISS Estimates Query Builder. 6 The NEISS database includes injury data from the emergency departments of 96 participating hospitals that the Consumer Product Safety Commission selected as a probability sample of all United States hospitals with a 24‐hour emergency department with at least 6 beds. These academic and community hospitals are chosen based on their representation of all other United States hospitals of their size and unique characteristics, which allows the Consumer Product Safety Commission to calculate national injury estimates from the statistical weights assigned to each hospital. At each emergency department visit, the NEISS database collects patient information, including the date of injury, patient characteristics and demographics, discharge disposition, location of injury, activities and products associated with the injury, narrative, and a suspected injury diagnosis. The current study is Institutional Review Board exempt as the NEISS database consists only of deidentified patient data.

Patient Selection and Data Collection

All pediatric (defined as patients aged <18 years old) encounters at the 96 participating NEISS hospitals between January 2019 and December 2023 associated with “sports and recreation equipment” were first identified. Basketball‐related injuries were then identified through a manual review of the narratives for each patient encounter. Cases with incomplete narratives and injuries that occurred at basketball games but were not sustained while playing basketball (e.g. fracture after falling off the bleachers) were excluded from the final analyses. Injuries were then subcategorized by patient characteristics and demographics, discharge disposition, hospital size/type (small, medium, large, very large, children's), injury type (e.g. strain/sprain, fracture, dislocation), site of injury (e.g. head/face, upper extremity, trunk, lower extremity), and orthopaedic (strains/sprains, fractures, dislocations, tears, avulsions/amputations) versus nonorthopaedic (contusions and abrasions, lacerations, concussions, and traumatic brain injuries, and internal injuries). At the authors’ home institutions, nail avulsions are most commonly treated by the orthopaedic hand service and were therefore categorized as orthopaedic injuries. This classification aligns with institutional practices and ensures consistency across injury types, reflecting the specialized care required.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations and categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. The estimated number of injuries per year was calculated using weights assigned by the NEISS database based on the geographical location in which the injury was sustained. Univariate analyses were performed to compare patient demographics and injury characteristics for pediatric patients who were admitted and those who were treated and discharged from the emergency department. Multivariate analyses for factors predictive of hospital admission were performed controlling for patient age, sex, injury type, and site of injury. All statistical analyses were performed using Rv3.6.1 (RFoundation, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

RESULTS

Analysis of NEISS‐reported injuries from January 2019 to December 2023 identified 31,238 basketball‐related injuries in pediatric patients, representing a national weighted estimate of 874,393 injuries. Since the decline during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the estimated basketball‐related injuries presenting to United States emergency departments has grown steadily from 2020 (children: 19,869 estimated injuries; adolescents: 95,504 estimated injuries) to 2023 (children: 37,616 estimated injuries; adolescents 156,599 estimated injuries) (Figure 1). Injuries were proportionally greater in men compared with women (76%, 132,082 estimated injuries per year) and in adolescents between ages 12 and 17 (82%, 143,601 estimated injuries per year) (Table 1). Orthopaedic injuries accounted for a greater proportion of injuries (56%) than nonorthopaedic injuries (44%). Most patients were treated/examined and discharged from the emergency room (97%). Approximately 2500 patients per year (2%) were treated and admitted or treated and transferred. More patients presented to small and medium hospitals (55%, 96,124 estimated injuries per year) compared with large, very large, and children's hospitals (45%, 78,754 estimated injuries per year).

Strains, sprains, and fractures represented over half (53%) of all injuries (92,548 estimated injuries) (Table 2). Less common injuries included contusions and abrasions (9%, 16,488 estimated injuries), lacerations (7%, 11,432 estimated injuries), and concussions and traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) (5%, 8256 estimated injuries). Although strains and sprains represented the majority of injuries in both male and female patients and occurred at comparable rates (men: 35%, women: 38%), fractures accounted for a larger proportion of injuries in men (20%) compared with women (14%) (Tables 3 and 4). Concussions and TBIs represented double the proportion of injuries in women (8%, 16,413 estimated injuries per year) compared with men (4%, 4973 estimated injuries per year).

Further subcategorization of pediatric basketball‐related injuries showed that strains and sprains were most commonly of the lower extremity (70%, 42,888 estimated injuries) (Table 5). In contrast, fractures were most commonly of the upper extremity (65%, 20,731 estimated injuries). Although further stratification of injuries by sex showed that the location of strains and sprains were consistent between men and women, fractures of the lower extremity were more common in men (30%, 7648 estimated injuries per year) compared with women (19%, 1130 estimated injuries per year) (Tables 6 and 7). Dislocations for men were more commonly of the upper extremity (65%, 2893 estimated injuries per year), whereas dislocations for women were more commonly of the lower extremity (52%, 511 estimated injuries per year).

The proportion of children (7‐11 years) and adolescents (12‐17 years) who were admitted versus examined and released did not differ significantly (P = .428) (Table 8). However, hospital admission rates were significantly higher for men and for those sustaining orthopaedic injuries (all P < .001). Hospital admission rates also differed significantly by injury type, injury location, and hospital size/type (all P < .001). When controlling for age, sex, injury type, and hospital size, multivariable analyses showed that children were at a decreased risk for hospitalization (OR: .80, 95% CI: .63‐.99, P = .044) compared with adolescents, whereas men were at an increased risk of hospitalization (OR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.54‐2.67, P < .001) compared with women (Table 9). Patients who presented to small hospitals (OR: .28, 95% CI: .17‐.43, P < .001) and medium hospitals (OR: .16, 95% CI: .07‐.32, P < .001) were significantly less likely to be admitted compared with those who presented to large, very large, and children's hospitals.

DISCUSSION

The most important findings of this study are as follows: the number of estimated basketball‐related injuries increased by 89% in children (19,869 to 37,616 estimated injuries) and 64% in adolescents (95,504 to 156,599 estimated injuries) from 2020 to 2023; the most common injury types included strains and sprains (35%, 60,844 estimated injuries per year), fractures (31%, 31,704 estimated injuries per year), and contusions and abrasions (9%, 16,448 estimated injuries per year); risk factors for hospital admission included older age, male sex, fractures, dislocations, and larger hospital size. As return‐to‐play has increased following the COVID‐19 pandemic, it is critical to identify the trends and types of pediatric basketball‐related injuries presenting to the emergency department and develop up‐to‐date preventative measures.

The current study examined pediatric basketball‐related injuries presenting to United States emergency departments between 2019 and 2023, focusing specifically on the effects of the post‐COVID‐19 return‐to‐play, relative to previous NEISS studies. Although previous NEISS studies noted a fall in basketball‐related injuries during the COVID era relative to the pre‐COVID period, our findings show that this downward trend has likely reversed secondary to increased athletic participation. 3 , 4 , 7 In the post‐COVID period, there was an increase in basketball‐related injuries in both children (89%) and adolescents (64%), respectively. Strains and sprains continue to be the most common type of basketball‐related injury in the pediatric population, as shown by Clifton and others' analysis of data from the High School Reporting Information Online system surveys and the National Collegiate Athletic Association Injury Surveillance program. 5 , 8

Further subanalysis showed sex‐related differences in injury patterns, where men were more likely to have fractures to the lower extremities compared with women and women experienced a larger proportion of concussions and TBIs progressing from childhood to adolescence compared with women. Allen et al. conducted a study using data from the National Athletic Treatment, Injury and Outcomes Network injury‐surveillance program and also found that women were at an increased risk of concussions and TBIs, particularly during competition. 9 These findings show the need for further medical education for athletic programs, coaches, and players and prevention measures for this growing population. Possible prevention strategies include proper protective headwear and the development of new institutional policies and rule changes that discourage overaggressive play (e.g. lower threshold for foul calls). 10

In the current study, increased age at the time of presentation, male sex, fractures, dislocations, and larger hospital size were risk factors for hospitalization. These results are consistent with the findings of Schneuer et al. who examined pediatric sports injuries using the New South Wales (NSW) Admitted Patient Data Collection and found that patients 5 to 12 years old constituted 30% of hospitalizations (N = 6058), while patients 13 to 15 constituted 70% of hospitalizations (N = 13,976). Adolescents may be at an increased risk for hospitalization due to increased intensity of play, especially as players become heavier and play becomes faster. Schneuer et al. also reported that men represented a larger proportion of hospitalization rates than women (81%, n = 16,260 versus 19%, n = 3774). While these differences in hospitalization rates between men and women have been previously attributed to increased intensity during play for men compared with women as well as gender disparities in diagnosis and treatment, further research is needed in order to determine the various factors that place male pediatric patients at an increased risk of hospitalization after sustaining a basketball‐related injury. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Lastly, the current study found that patients who presented to small or medium sized hospitals were less likely to be hospitalized compared with their counterparts who presented to large, very large, and children's hospitals. One potential explanation suggests that smaller hospitals may not be equipped with the proper orthopaedic teams and/or medical resources needed to treat patients adequately, especially due to the uneven distribution of pediatric orthopaedic specialists throughout the country. Pediatric orthopaedic call may also be handled by private clinicians, who may be more often inclined to manage injuries on an outpatient basis and encourage discharge with outpatient follow‐up. Regardless of hospital size and inpatient/outpatient management, ensuring comprehensive treatment is critical, as traumatic injuries such as fractures and dislocations may lead to devastating complications such as avascular necrosis if not treated promptly, particularly for this young population.

The increasing rates of basketball‐related injuries continue to pose an overwhelming burden on the United States healthcare system. A systematic review conducted in 2019 found that direct costs of ankle sprains, one of the most common basketball‐related injuries, ranged from $292 to $2268 per patient depending on severity and treatment strategy. 16 Furthermore, pediatric injuries may have additional implications for both patients and their families, as children may be forced to miss school and parents may need to take additional time off work. Although the direct costs of missing school and work are beyond the scope of this study, these costs emphasize the need for prevention measures to reduce the economic burden of basketball‐related injuries. For example, prior studies have shown that basketball‐specific balance training programs can reduce the incidence of lateral ankle sprains compared with general training programs. 17 , 18 Other injury prevention measures may include proper warm‐up techniques and protective equipment (e.g. headgear, ankle braces).

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The retrospective nature creates a risk of selection bias. The true quantity of annual basketball‐related injuries presenting to United States emergency departments is not captured by the NEISS database, as only NEISS‐affiliated hospitals were included in the analysis. Sampling bias may be introduced due to the usage of this database, potentially underestimating basketball‐related injuries and the distribution of injury types. As a retrospective study, it is also difficult to account for confounders that may be contributing to the data results. The database also lacks certain granular variables of interest such as geographic location, whether an injury occurred indoors versus outdoors, or whether it occurred during organized competition versus recreational play. This limits the scope of subgroup analyses and opportunities for root cause identification. Finally, the usage of the NEISS database required manual coders to interpret, characterize, and code the dataset, which is subject to human error.

CONCLUSIONS

The number of estimated basketball‐related injuries for children and adolescents substantially increased between 2020 and 2023, where strains and sprains represented the most common injury types. Risk factors for hospital admission included older age at the time of presentation, male sex, fractures, dislocations, and larger hospital size.
