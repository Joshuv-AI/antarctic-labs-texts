ATR-FTIR assessment of degree of conversion across curing-state transitions in Bis-EMA- and UDMA-based microfilled dental resins for additive manufacturing
Authors: Frank Alifui-Segbaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Assessing the degree of conversion is important for evaluating processing quality in additively manufactured dental methacrylates. However, the widely used attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) method, based on the 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio, may be unstable in contemporary methacrylate systems because of spectral congestion and the formulation-dependent behaviour of the aromatic reference band. This study evaluated a low-variance ATR-FTIR protocol based on a polymerisation-sensitive, ester-associated band pair at 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ to estimate surface or near-surface conversion in two commercially available microfilled dental resins for additive manufacturing, with matrices dominated by ethoxylated bisphenol A dimethacrylate (Bis-EMA) and urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA). The conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio was analysed in parallel as a comparative method. Across the manufacturer-recommended processing stages, from the green state before postcuring to the final postcured state, the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ model yielded chemically plausible and statistically stable conversion trajectories. In contrast, the conventional aromatic-normalised method showed greater dependence on formulation and workflow, and lower analytical stability. In the UDMA-dominant resin, conversion increased from 68.44% to 91.61% after light postcuring. For the Bis-EMA-dominant resin, the same model revealed a significant processing-stage effect, with a practical plateau near 91% after combined light and thermal treatment. The coefficients of variation for the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol were low (0.54–5.58%) compared with those of the conventional method (29.13–673.09%), which also yielded nonphysical negative estimates in some groups. Under the conditions tested, these findings support the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ ATR-FTIR workflow as a practical approach for surface or near-surface characterisation of the two material-workflow systems studied. However, broader validation against orthogonal analytical methods and performance-related outcomes is needed before wider adoption.

Introduction

With the rapid uptake of dental three-dimensional (3D) printing, photopolymerisation-based additive manufacturing is increasingly used to produce denture teeth, provisional restorations, and definitive resin-based devices. In these systems, multifunctional methacrylate monomers and oligomers are converted into crosslinked polymer networks via photoactivated radical polymerisation [1]. The extent of this conversion, commonly expressed as the degree of conversion (DC), is an important processing parameter that may influence material performance [2]. Accordingly, accurate and reproducible conversion measurements are important for both research comparisons and manufacturing quality control in additive manufacturing.

Polymerisation in vat-photopolymerised systems is typically biphasic [3]. During primary photopolymerisation, rapid network formation is followed by progressive vitrification, which restricts segmental motion and reduces radical mobility. Postcuring with light, and in some workflows, with heat, can further increase conversion by transiently enhancing molecular mobility and sustaining reactive species [4]. The extent of this secondary conversion depends on material chemistry and postprocessing conditions [5–8], making the analytical method used to quantify conversion especially important for comparing modern dental resins.

ATR-FTIR is widely used to assess methacrylate conversion, most commonly via ratios involving the aliphatic C = C stretching band near 1636–1640 cm⁻¹ and a nominally invariant internal reference band [7–10]. However, the traditional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ metric is not universally robust. The 1500–1800 cm⁻¹ spectral region is often congested and baseline-sensitive, and the 1608 cm⁻¹ aromatic reference band may be weak, absent, or formulation-dependent in aliphatic-rich systems. These features can introduce systematic bias and, in some cases, yield mathematically unstable conversion estimates. Recent work has highlighted the analytical utility of ester-associated bands in the 1320–1355 cm⁻¹ region as lower-variance alternatives for polymerisation analysis in methacrylate systems [11]. In this region, the band near 1320 cm⁻¹ is sensitive to polymerisation-related structural changes, whereas the neighbouring band near 1352 cm⁻¹ may provide a comparatively stable internal reference. The ~1320/1352 cm⁻¹ pair is attributed primarily to coupled ester C–O–C stretching and aliphatic C–H bending vibrations in methacrylate polymers, which change reproducibly with polymerisation-associated network formation while being less susceptible to formulation-dependent variability than aromatic reference bands.

The present study, therefore, evaluated an ATR-FTIR protocol using the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ band pair as a potentially lower-variance approach to estimating conversion in two commercial microfilled dental resin systems with matrices dominated by ethoxylated bisphenol A dimethacrylate (Bis-EMA) and urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA). These chemistries are representative of widely used resin systems in both filled and unfilled dental materials for additive manufacturing [12]. The aims were to quantify processing-dependent changes in surface or near-surface conversion estimates across the manufacturer-recommended workflow and to compare the performance of the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ method with the proposed 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol. By addressing this analytical gap, the study sought to inform evidence-based assessments of surface or near-surface conversion and postprocessing evaluations of 3D-printed devices.

Materials and methods

Materials, specimen fabrication, processing, and sampling

Disc-shaped samples (10 mm diameter × 2.0 mm thickness) were printed from two methacrylate resins: Saremco Print CrownTEC (SPC; Saremco Dental AG, Rebstein, Switzerland) and Asiga DentaTooth (ADT; Asiga Pty Ltd, Sydney, Australia). Printing was performed on an Asiga MAX UV DLP 3D printer operating at 385 nm, with a 50 µm layer thickness and the manufacturer-recommended build profiles for each material. Before printing, all resins were homogenised on an LC-3D roller mixer for 60 min to minimise compositional gradients. All solid samples for each resin were fabricated in a single print run to eliminate potential inter-build variability and isolate processing-stage effects.

Postprint cleaning followed the validated, manufacturer-specific workflow routinely used for each material. The SPC samples were washed in 96% ethanol, whereas the ADT samples were washed in >98% isopropyl alcohol. Washing was performed by sonication in a Coltene Biosonic UC300 for 3 min per cycle for SPC and 30 s per cycle for ADT, with two washing steps using a fresh solvent volume of 250 mL per wash. After washing, all samples were air-dried for 2 h inside a ducted PVC laboratory fume cupboard operated under standard laboratory ventilation conditions, with certification compliant with AS/NZS 2243.8 safety standards.

In this study, the term “green state” refers to samples after the manufacturer-specific washing step and 2 h of air-drying, but before any light and/or thermal postcuring. This definition was adopted because the ATR-FTIR measurements were intended to characterise the pre- and post-cure surface conditions within each manufacturer-recommended workflow. These cleaning steps were treated as integral components of each material’s intended processing pathway rather than as independent experimental variables. Accordingly, this study was designed to assess analytically relevant outcomes under realistic material-workflow conditions rather than to isolate the independent effects of solvent type, filler content, light source, or postcuring device.

SPC samples were evaluated at four sequential processing stages: the green state, after standard light postcuring (LC; NextDent LC-3D Print Box, 30 min in a spectral range 300–550 nm), after combined light curing and boiling–water treatment (LCB; 100 °C for 2 min), and after the complete light/thermal/UV workflow (CBL; additional 2 × 20 s UV exposure in an LC-6 oven in a spectral range 350–450 nm). ADT samples were analysed in the green state and after the manufacturer-recommended light postcuring stage only (LC; NK Optik Otoflash G171, 4 × 2000 flashes, spectral range 230–950 nm). Thermal postcuring was not applied to ADT because it is not part of the manufacturer-validated, recommended workflow for this material.

Spectral processing and surface or near-surface conversion estimates

ATR-FTIR spectra were collected from the top surface of the solid samples (n = 5 per processing group). Unpolymerised reference spectra were obtained from liquid resin samples (n = 3 per material). To prevent premature photoinitiation, all liquid samples were shielded from ambient light during acquisition. All spectra were acquired on a Bruker ALPHA II spectrometer over the range 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, with 16 coadded scans. Additionally, all solid samples subjected to elevated temperatures, including those in the combined light-curing and boiling–water treatment (LCB) group, were allowed to fully equilibrate to ambient room temperature before ATR-FTIR analysis to prevent temperature-induced spectral artefacts.

Preliminary inspection of the feature-free high-wavenumber and mid-infrared regions across all liquid and solid spectra indicated near-zero baselines with minimal slopes under the acquisition conditions used. On that basis, no global numerical baseline correction was applied before peak-height extraction. Although raw peak-maximum extraction can be susceptible to minor local baseline slopes, this risk was mitigated in the present study by using predefined search windows. To reduce sensitivity to local spectral drift and formulation-dependent band migration, peak maxima were determined within predefined search windows centred on the nominal target bands.

The primary analytical outcome was DC1320/1352, defined here as the ATR-FTIR-derived surface or near-surface conversion estimate calculated from a polymerisation-sensitive ester-associated peak ratio using the absorbance maximum near 1320 cm⁻¹ and a comparatively stable internal reference band near 1352 cm⁻¹. Because small but consistent peak shifts were observed across materials and processing stages, the absorbance maxima were identified within ±10 cm⁻¹ windows: 1310–1330 cm⁻¹ for the conversion-sensitive band and 1340–1360 cm⁻¹ for the reference band. This windowed approach was adopted to capture the relevant local maxima while minimising noise-driven artefacts and preserving reproducibility across differing resin chemistries. Furthermore, because the chosen bands are in close spectral proximity, any minor linear slope across this narrow window has a mathematically negligible effect on their relative ratio.

For each solid sample, the absorbance ratio R = A1320/A1352 was normalised to the corresponding mean liquid-state ratio R̄liquid, and conversion was calculated as DC1320/1352 (%) = (1 − [Rsolid/R̄liquid]) × 100. A conventional comparative model based on the 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio was processed in parallel to assess method stability and chemical plausibility across the material-workflow systems. In this study, the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ outcome is interpreted as a workflow-specific ATR-FTIR-derived conversion estimate, not as a universal or fully validated surrogate for bulk conversion or clinical performance.

Statistical analysis

All spectral processing, peak identification, area integration, and conversion-estimate calculations were performed using custom Python scripts (version 3.11). The data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs), with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) reported for group means to improve the interpretability of estimate precision. Method stability was summarised using the coefficient of variation (CV, %), calculated as SD/mean × 100. However, CV values were not interpreted when the underlying surface conversion estimate was negative or approached zero, as the CV becomes mathematically unstable and analytically misleading under such conditions.

For SPC, differences among the four processing stages were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test. For ADT, the green and LC groups were compared using an independent-samples t-test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. To strengthen the inferential interpretation, partial η² was reported for the SPC ANOVA, and the mean difference with a 95% CI was reported for the ADT comparison.

Results

Spectral behaviour and rationale for windowed peak extraction

Initial spectral inspection revealed formulation-dependent shifts in the 1320–1360 cm⁻¹ region. The nominal reference band near 1352 cm⁻¹ appeared at approximately 1342 cm⁻¹ in the ADT and at approximately 1358 cm⁻¹ in the SPC, confirming that a fixed single-point measurement would be vulnerable to chemistry-dependent band displacement. The predefined ±10 cm⁻¹ search window, therefore, provided an operationally robust means of capturing the relevant local maxima in both materials and across all processing stages. As a secondary sensitivity analysis, band-area indices were examined over the same spectral windows using a local linear baseline; however, because these analyses produced only slightly higher absolute values without altering the overall interpretation, the peak‒maximum model was retained as the primary analytical outcome.

Surface or near-surface conversion estimates of SPC

Using the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol, SPC showed a high initial green-state conversion and a statistically significant postprocessing effect. The mean DC1320/1352 increased from 82.55 ± 1.05% (95% CI 81.25–83.85) in the green state to 88.26 ± 4.02% (95% CI 83.27–93.25) after light curing, 91.52 ± 2.38% (95% CI 88.56–94.48) after light curing plus boiling–water treatment, and 91.29 ± 1.28% (95% CI 89.70–92.88) after the complete light/thermal/UV workflow. One-way ANOVA confirmed a significant overall effect of processing stage on conversion (F(3,16) = 14.23, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.727). Tukey’s HSD analysis indicated that green-state samples were significantly less converted than those processed later, whereas the LCB and CBL stages formed a practical conversion plateau near 91%. The analytical dispersion associated with the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol remained low in SPC, with CVs ranging from 1.27% to 4.55% across the four processing stages (Table 1, 2; Figs. 1–3). Surface or near-surface conversion estimates of ADT

Compared with SPC, ADT produced lower green-state conversion but a marked postcuring response. The mean DC1320/1352 increased from 68.44 ± 3.82% (95% CI 63.70–73.18) in the green state to 91.61 ± 0.50% (95% CI 90.99–92.23) after light postcuring (Table 1, 2, Figs. 1, 3, 4). This represented a mean increase of 23.17 percentage points (95% CI: 19.20–27.14), and the difference between groups was highly significant (t(8) = −13.46, p < 0.001). Comparison of the 1320/1352 cm−1 and 1637/1608 cm−1 methods

The 1320/1352 cm−1 model produced physically plausible conversion trajectories in both materials: lower values in the green state, substantial increases after postcuring, and a clear plateau in the SPC after combined light and thermal treatment. By contrast, the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ model showed pronounced material dependence and numerical instability. In SPC, the conventional approach produced non-physical negative conversion values after postcuring, with a mean of −96.38 ± 33.22% in the LC group. In the ADT, the green-state conversion values derived from the conventional model were likewise unstable, exhibiting extreme relative dispersion. These findings indicate that the conventional aromatic-normalised model did not yield consistently interpretable analytical results across the tested formulations.

Area-based comparisons showed that the conventional and ester-associated models tracked similar directional changes in ADT, but agreement was poor in absolute terms and substantially weaker in SPC. Specifically, the reported correlation between area-based indices was positive in ADT (r = 0.925) but poor and negative in SPC (r = −0.499), indicating that the conventional model did not behave consistently across the two material-workflow systems. These correlations are from exploratory area-based comparisons and are presented to assess intermodel directional agreement rather than agreement with the primary peak–max outcome metric. Because ADT was evaluated only under its validated manufacturer-recommended workflow, cross-material comparisons should be interpreted as comparisons of analytically relevant processing outcomes rather than matched stage-by-stage thermal responses.

Representative spectral trends

Representative ATR-FTIR spectra confirmed the progressive attenuation of polymerisation-sensitive features from the liquid state to the printed and postcured states. For both materials, the liquid resin showed the strongest absorbance in the relevant conversion-sensitive bands, and these signals decreased after printing and postcuring. In ADT, the absence of a distinct aromatic reference feature at 1608 cm⁻¹ helps explain the poor performance of the conventional method. In SPC, the 1580–1660 cm⁻¹ region was more congested and susceptible to local baseline and overlap effects, which likely destabilised the aromatic-normalised ratio during progressive vitrification. By comparison, the 1320–1355 cm⁻¹ region remained better resolved across the tested conditions.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether an ATR-FTIR protocol based on the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ band pair could provide a more stable and chemically interpretable estimate of surface or near-surface conversion than the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio in two dental 3D-printed resin systems. The principal finding was that, under the conditions tested, the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol produced processing-responsive and statistically stable conversion trajectories in both SPC and ADT. In contrast, the conventional aromatic-normalised metric behaved inconsistently and, in some groups, yielded non-physical results.

The comparison between SPC and ADT should be understood as a comparison of two material-workflow systems rather than an isolated comparison of Bis-EMA- and UDMA-dominant chemistries. Although the materials differ in their dominant matrix chemistry, they also differ in filler characteristics, cleaning solvent, postcuring device, light-exposure profile, and, in the case of SPC, the inclusion of an additional thermal-treatment stage. In contemporary dental vat-photopolymerisation (VPP) systems, both unfilled and microfilled resins are typically formulated around Bis-EMA- and/or UDMA-based dimethacrylate backbones, with specific diluents, photoinitiators, and filler architectures tuned for their intended applications [12, 13]. In this study, SPC represented a highly filled Bis-EMA-dominant permanent restorative resin system (30–50 wt% silanised glass and pyrogenic silica; particle size ~0.7 µm), whereas ADT represented a UDMA-dominant microfilled resin with lower inorganic loading (27.7 wt%, 0.04–0.7 µm particle size) and a different intended processing profile [14, 15]. Within this workflow-based design, the experimental data revealed a markedly higher green-state conversion estimate in SPC ( ~ 83%) than in ADT ( ~ 68%). This difference likely reflects not a single variable in isolation but rather the combined effects of matrix chemistry, filler loading, build and exposure settings, and a manufacturer-specific postprocessing design.

Despite these different green-state baselines, both materials yielded similar final postcured conversion near 91%, indicating that the manufacturer-recommended workflows were sufficient to drive substantial additional network formation under the tested conditions. For ADT, postcuring resulted in a large increase in conversion. In contrast, in SPC, the material begins with a comparatively high green-state estimate and then reaches a practical plateau after the LCB stage. The absence of a further statistically meaningful increase after the final UV step indicates a practical plateau in the measured conversion under the tested workflow.

Several mechanistic explanations may be consistent with these patterns, but they should be interpreted cautiously, as they were not directly measured in the present study. For example, the large increase in ADT after postcuring may reflect substantial residual reactivity in the green-state network. In contrast, the SPC plateau after combined light and thermal treatment may reflect increasing vitrification-related restrictions on segmental mobility. This pattern is also consistent with polymerisation theory, in which increasing vitrification restricts segmental mobility, reduces radical diffusion, and makes further double-bond conversion progressively more difficult [16]. Likewise, differences in filler loading, filler-matrix organisation, and silane-associated methacrylate contributions may have influenced the observed behaviour [17]. However, these possibilities remain interpretive and are cited here as plausible explanations informed by prior literature rather than mechanisms demonstrated directly by the present ATR-FTIR measurements.

The postcuring trends observed here are consistent with recent studies on additively manufactured dental resins, which show that additional light and/or heat treatment can improve conversion and related material properties. However, the magnitude of improvement depends strongly on formulation and process conditions [5–8]. The present findings extend this body of work by indicating that the ATR-FTIR metric used to quantify conversion can, in turn, influence analytical interpretability. In other words, the question is not only whether postcuring increases conversion, which is well supported in the literature, but also whether the chosen ATR-FTIR ratio remains valid across different material-workflow systems. The improved performance of the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ model should therefore be interpreted with caution.

The band near 1320 cm⁻¹ is best described as a polymerisation-sensitive ester-associated feature rather than a directly “consumed ester bond” in a simplistic mechanistic sense. Its analytical value lies in its reproducible local spectral behaviour during methacrylate polymerisation. Moreover, the neighbouring band near 1352 cm⁻¹ served as a relatively stable internal reference for the tested materials. This interpretation is more chemically precise and aligns with recommendations that ATR-FTIR conversion measurements should be based on bands that are both formulation-appropriate and demonstrably stable, rather than adopted by convention without validation [11, 18, 19]. Delgado and Young [11] emphasised that peak determination and band selection are critical for robust FTIR interpretation in dental methacrylate systems. The present results support that position and indicate that a windowed peak-max approach in the 1320–1355 cm⁻¹ region can serve as a lower-variance ATR-FTIR-derived outcome measure in the tested microfilled VPP resins.

In contrast, the performance of the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio was strongly dependent on the formulation and workflow. As a UDMA-dominant matrix, ADT did not exhibit a distinct aromatic reference feature at 1608 cm⁻¹, indicating that the conventional model may have been normalised against the spectral background rather than a structurally invariant band. This likely contributed to the extreme instability of the green-state estimates. In SPC, aromatic content was present, but the spectra revealed substantial congestion in the 1580–1660 cm⁻¹ region as curing progressed. Under those conditions, baseline drift, band overlap, and broadening can destabilise the local environment near 1608 cm⁻¹, thereby reducing its suitability as an invariant denominator. Collares et al. [19] previously highlighted discrepancies in FTIR-based conversion measurements, and Almusa et al. [18] reported that local compositional and spectral conditions can strongly influence the behaviour of ATR-FTIR bands during polymerisation. The nonphysical negative conversion values produced here by the conventional model are therefore best interpreted as evidence of analytical failure in the selected reference-band approach under these conditions rather than as true polymerisation behaviour.

The intermodel behaviour further underscores this point. In ADT, the conventional and ester-associated approaches showed directionally similar changes, although the conventional method produced imprecise, underestimated values. As a result, the correlation remained strongly positive in the ADT group, despite poor absolute interpretability. In SPC, however, the two approaches diverged substantially, yielding a moderate negative correlation and demonstrating clear methodological disagreement. This suggests that once the aromatic reference is destabilised by formulation-specific congestion and curing-induced spectral changes, the conventional metric may no longer meaningfully reflect the progress of polymerisation. This distinction is important because correlation alone does not establish agreement or interchangeability between methods; rather, it indicates whether the methods move in the same or opposite directions under the tested conditions.

The practical implications of this analytical instability are nontrivial. If the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ model underestimates or destabilises conversion estimates, comparisons across studies, materials, or postcuring protocols may be misinterpreted. For example, a recent study using a conventional FTIR-derived approach reported a lower conversion value (74.65 ± 3.89%) for SPC under a similar postcuring protocol [10]. Other studies have likewise reported comparatively low conversion values for printed dental resins using conventional aliphatic/reference band ratios, including aromatic- and carbonyl-normalised approaches [20, 21]. Accordingly, a more stable ATR-FTIR metric may have value for materials research and, potentially, for future workflow monitoring or quality-assurance applications; however, it should be interpreted as a chemical characterisation tool rather than a standalone determinant of biological or clinical performance.

The statistical behaviour of the present dataset supports the practicality of the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ model in the tested systems. In SPC, the ANOVA showed a large stage effect (partial η² = 0.727), confirming that the ester-associated metric was sensitive to meaningful postprocessing changes across the manufacturer-recommended workflow. During ADT, postcuring increased the conversion estimate by 23.17 percentage points, with a narrow confidence interval, confirming that the effect was both statistically significant and precisely estimated. Across all groups, the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ model maintained low coefficients of variation, whereas the conventional model produced extreme or uninterpretable CV behaviour because its mean values became unstable or negative. These findings indicate that the ester-associated approach was chemically plausible and analytically repeatable under routine specimen-to-specimen variation in the two systems studied.

Conclusion

Under the conditions tested, the conventional 1637/1608 cm⁻¹ ratio did not consistently yield interpretable estimates of surface or near-surface conversion in the two material-workflow systems evaluated. By contrast, the 1320/1352 cm⁻¹ protocol produced lower-variance, processing-responsive estimates of conversion in both systems. However, broader validation across additional resin systems and against orthogonal analytical and performance-related measures will be necessary before wider adoption. Because the present study did not assess bulk conversion, monomer elution, cytotoxicity, ageing, wear, mechanical properties, or clinical performance, any relationship between the measured ATR-FTIR-derived estimates and downstream physicochemical, biological, mechanical, or clinical outcomes should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only.
