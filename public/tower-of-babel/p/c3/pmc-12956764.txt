Mechanisms and clinical implications of bacterial persistence in recurrent urinary tract infections
Authors: Changil Choi, Dong Soo Kim, Jin Bong Choi, Taesoo Choi, Jeong Woo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recurrent urinary tract infections (rUTIs) represent a significant clinical challenge, particularly among women and catheterized patients, leading to diminished quality of life and increased healthcare utilization. A central factor in rUTI pathogenesis is bacterial persistence despite seemingly appropriate antibiotic therapy. Unlike classical antibiotic resistance, persistence involves phenotypic adaptations that allow uropathogens to evade both host immune responses and antimicrobial agents. This review examines the multifactorial mechanisms underlying bacterial persistence, including intracellular survival strategies, biofilm formation, antibiotic tolerance, and host immune evasion. An improved understanding of these processes is essential for developing novel, targeted therapeutic strategies that go beyond traditional antimicrobial approaches and address the root causes of recurrence.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the most common bacterial infections in women, with 50% to 60% of adult women experiencing UTIs during their lifetime. Notably UTIs are among the most prevalent bacterial infections, particularly affecting women, the elderly, and individuals with indwelling urinary catheters [123].

While acute episodes are generally responsive to short courses of antibiotic therapy, approximately 20%–30% of patients experience recurrent infections within months of initial treatment [4]. Recurrent urinary tract infection (rUTI) is clinically defined as two or more episodes within six months or three or more within a twelve-month period.

A major factor contributing to rUTI is bacterial persistence, an adaptive survival state in which uropathogens evade both host immune clearance and antimicrobial eradication. Unlike conventional antibiotic resistance, which involves stable genetic mutations, persistence arises from phenotypic plasticity and the formation of protective bacterial reservoirs [56]. These reservoirs, often intracellular or biofilm-associated, enable bacteria to survive in a dormant or metabolically altered state, reactivating intermittently to cause symptomatic relapse.

Understanding the underlying mechanisms of persistence is essential for addressing the limitations of current treatment approaches and developing more effective, long-term solutions for rUTI management. This review integrates current evidence on the microbial strategies and host factors that drive bacterial persistence in rUTI and discusses their clinical implications and future therapeutic directions.

MECHANISMS OF BACTERIAL PERSISTENCE

1. Intracellular bacterial communities

Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), the primary causative agent of rUTIs, can invade superficial umbrella cells of the bladder urothelium. Once internalized, UPEC rapidly proliferates and forms structured intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs), which resemble early-stage biofilms. These IBCs are enclosed within host-derived vacuoles and are shielded from both innate immune responses and antibiotics that target extracellular or rapidly dividing bacteria. After several hours, some bacteria escape into the bladder lumen to initiate new cycles of infection, while others transition into quiescent intracellular forms that may persist for extended periods [7]. Recent study have highlighted persistent bacterial infection, including intracellular survival and biofilm formation, as major contributors to recurrent UTIs [8].

2. Quiescent intracellular reservoirs

Following the acute phase of infection, a subset of E. coli may transition into a non-replicating or slowly replicating state within bladder epithelial cells, forming what are known as quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs). These bacterial populations are metabolically dormant and remain undetectable by standard culture techniques and largely unrecognized by host innate immune sensors. Their phenotypic state renders them highly tolerant to antibiotics that target actively dividing bacteria. QIRs can persist for weeks to months and, upon reactivation, reseed the bladder lumen, leading to symptomatic recurrence of infection [9].

3. Biofilm formation

Biofilm formation is a critical mechanism by which uropathogens persist in the urinary tract, particularly in cases of catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) and chronic or recurrent infections. A biofilm is a structured microbial community encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) that adheres to biological or inert surfaces, such as the uroepithelium or indwelling medical devices. Within this matrix, bacterial cells exhibit altered phenotypes with respect to growth rate, gene expression, and antibiotic susceptibility [10].

UPEC, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis are among the most common biofilm-forming organisms implicated in rUTIs. These pathogens can attach to urinary tract surfaces through adhesins such as fimbriae and pili, initiating biofilm development. Maturation of the biofilm architecture provides spatial and metabolic heterogeneity, allowing subpopulations of bacteria to enter dormant states or form persister cells, which are transiently tolerant to antimicrobial agents [11].

Biofilm-associated infections are notoriously difficult to eradicate due to the protective nature of the EPS, limited antibiotic penetration, reduced metabolic activity of resident bacteria, and the presence of efflux pumps and quorum sensing-regulated defense systems. It has been estimated that bacteria within biofilms can exhibit up to a 1,000-fold increase in antibiotic tolerance compared to planktonic cells [12]. Furthermore, biofilms serve as a reservoir for recurrent infection. Even after symptomatic resolution, residual biofilm fragments can reseed infection sites, particularly when host immunity is compromised or when indwelling devices are left in place.

Addressing biofilm-associated persistence requires therapeutic approaches beyond conventional antibiotics, including anti-biofilm agents, quorum sensing inhibitors, and strategies that disrupt EPS integrity or enhance host immune clearance.

4. Antibiotic tolerance and persister cells

While antibiotic resistance involves heritable genetic changes that allow bacteria to grow in the presence of antimicrobials, antibiotic tolerance refers to the transient ability of a bacterial subpopulation to survive lethal antibiotic exposure without acquiring resistance genes. This phenomenon is especially relevant in the context of rUTIs, where antibiotic courses often suppress symptoms temporarily but fail to eradicate the underlying bacterial population.

A central contributor to antibiotic tolerance is the formation of persister cells, a small fraction of phenotypically distinct, dormant bacterial cells that are highly tolerant to antibiotics. These cells are not mutants. Rather, they exist in a reversible state of metabolic inactivity or growth arrest that renders them impervious to antibiotics targeting actively dividing cells [11]. Upon removal of antibiotic pressure, persister cells can awaken and repopulate the infection site, leading to clinical relapse.

The formation of persister cells is induced by various stress conditions commonly encountered during infection, including nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, low pH, and sublethal antibiotic exposure [13]. Molecular mechanisms underlying persistence include activation of toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems, stringent response pathways, and global regulators such as HipA. Importantly, the presence of persisters has been documented in UPEC during both planktonic and biofilm growth phases [14].

From a therapeutic perspective, persister cells pose a formidable challenge. They are not detectable by conventional antibiotic susceptibility tests, and no antibiotics are specifically designed to eliminate them. Moreover, repeated antibiotic exposure may actually increase the proportion of persisters through selection of high-persistence phenotypes. Novel strategies under investigation include metabolic reactivation, anti-persister compounds, and combination therapies that target both actively growing and dormant subpopulations.

It is important to clearly distinguish bacterial persistence from classical antibiotic resistance. Genetic antibiotic resistance refers to heritable mutations or acquisition of resistance genes that enable bacterial growth in the presence of antibiotics at clinically relevant concentrations. In contrast, phenotypic antibiotic tolerance, exemplified by persister cells, represents a reversible, non-heritable state in which a subpopulation of bacteria survives antibiotic exposure by transiently entering a metabolically inactive or slow-growing state without an increase in minimum inhibitory concentration.

Intracellular dormancy, such as QIRs observed in uropathogenic E. coli, further differs from both concepts in that bacteria evade antibiotic activity and host immunity by residing within host cells in a dormant but viable state. While these mechanisms may coexist during rUTIs, they represent biologically distinct processes with different therapeutic implications.

5. Genomic and phenotypic adaptations

rUTIs impose selective pressures that drive both genomic evolution and phenotypic plasticity in uropathogens, particularly E. coli strains associated with recurrence. During repeated cycles of infection, clearance, and antibiotic exposure, bacterial populations undergo adaptive changes that enhance their ability to colonize, evade host defenses, and persist under antimicrobial stress [15].

Genomically, recurrent strains often harbor mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, transposons, and pathogenicity islands that confer increased virulence or antibiotic resistance. For instance, extended-spectrum β-lactamases and fluoroquinolone resistance genes are frequently detected in rUTI isolates, limiting the efficacy of first-line treatments [16]. Comparative genomic studies have revealed that UPEC strains involved in recurrence may accumulate mutations in regulatory genes that modulate surface structures, such as type 1 pili and P fimbriae, allowing enhanced adhesion and invasion of uroepithelial cells.

At the phenotypic level, these adaptations include alterations in surface antigens, increased production of protective capsules, and enhanced expression of stress response systems, including TA modules. Some UPEC strains have demonstrated the ability to switch between hypermotile and sessile states depending on environmental cues, contributing to both dissemination and biofilm formation [17]. These dynamic shifts allow pathogens to optimize survival in diverse niches within the urinary tract, from intracellular reservoirs to catheter surfaces.

The interplay between genetic adaptability and phenotypic switching underscores the limitations of conventional diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, which often fail to capture the evolving nature of the uropathogen. As a result, recurrent infections may involve clonal variants that are increasingly fit for persistence and relapse. HOST FACTORS CONTRIBUTING TO PERSISTENCE

While bacterial mechanisms play a central role in rUTI pathogenesis, host factors significantly contribute to the persistence and recurrence of infection. Anatomical, immunological, hormonal, and metabolic elements can compromise urinary tract defenses, creating an environment conducive to bacterial survival and reactivation.

1. Impaired innate immunity

The innate immune system serves as the first and most immediate line of defense against uropathogenic invasion. In the urinary tract, epithelial cells are not merely passive barriers but are immunologically active, expressing a range of pattern recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and inflammasomes. These receptors detect conserved microbial motifs—such as lipopolysaccharide from Gram-negative bacteria—and initiate rapid signaling cascades that recruit neutrophils, induce proinflammatory cytokine release, and stimulate the production of antimicrobial peptides like defensins and cathelicidins [18].

However, in certain individuals, this front-line immune response is insufficient or dysregulated, allowing bacteria to evade early clearance and establish persistent reservoirs. Genetic polymorphisms in TLR genes, particularly TLR4 and TLR5, have been associated with increased susceptibility to rUTIs. These polymorphisms may reduce receptor sensitivity or alter downstream signaling, resulting in suboptimal neutrophil recruitment and delayed pathogen clearance [1920].

In addition to genetic variation, age-related immunosenescence and hormonal changes, especially in postmenopausal women, can impair innate immune responsiveness. Declining estrogen levels reduce epithelial barrier integrity and downregulate key components of innate defense, such as β-defensins and secretory IgA, further increasing vulnerability to infection [21]. Moreover, in diabetic patients, hyperglycemia and oxidative stress have been shown to inhibit neutrophil function and phagocytic activity, exacerbating the risk of persistent infection [22].

Some studies have also suggested that chronic or repeated infections may lead to immune tolerance in the bladder, whereby epithelial and immune cells become desensitized to bacterial stimuli, reducing inflammation but allowing low-level bacterial survival. This phenomenon may partially explain the minimal inflammatory response observed in some patients with rUTI despite bacterial colonization [23].

Ultimately, impairments in innate immunity, whether genetically determined, hormonally mediated, or metabolically induced, play a central role in the failure to eliminate uropathogens during the initial stages of infection. This failure facilitates the establishment of intracellular reservoirs, biofilm formation, and the development of antibiotic-tolerant phenotypes, all of which contribute to recurrence.

2. Anatomical and functional abnormalities

Anatomical and functional abnormalities of the urinary tract significantly predispose individuals to persistent and recurrent UTIs. These structural vulnerabilities create microenvironments that support bacterial colonization, hinder complete voiding, and promote the formation of biofilms or intracellular reservoirs.

One of the most well-documented risk factors is vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), a congenital or acquired condition characterized by retrograde urine flow from the bladder into the ureters and kidneys. VUR facilitates the ascent of bacteria into the upper urinary tract and compromises mucosal immunity by exposing sterile regions to contaminated urine. Repeated reflux episodes can cause renal scarring and chronic inflammation, leading to persistent colonization despite antibiotic therapy [24].

Another major contributor is incomplete bladder emptying, which may result from neurogenic bladder, outlet obstruction, or dysfunctional voiding. Residual urine acts as a reservoir for uropathogens, reduces urinary flushing, and enhances the likelihood of biofilm formation on the bladder epithelium [25]. In these patients, UPEC and other organisms can persist in the bladder niche even after antibiotic treatment, especially when the flow of urine fails to mechanically clear the bacteria.

Perhaps the most critical iatrogenic factor is the use of indwelling urinary catheters, which provide a direct surface for biofilm development. CAUTIs account for a large proportion of healthcare-associated infections and are especially difficult to treat once biofilms have formed on catheter surfaces. These biofilms shield bacteria from both immune detection and antimicrobial agents, allowing chronic, subclinical infection to persist. Additionally, the catheter disrupts normal bladder reflexes and mucosal immunity, further exacerbating susceptibility [2627].

Management of rUTI in individuals with these abnormalities often requires more than antibiotic therapy alone. Structural correction (e.g., surgical intervention for VUR), intermittent catheterization, and bladder training may be necessary to break the cycle of persistence and recurrence.

3. Estrogen deficiency

Estrogen plays a critical role in maintaining the structural and immunological integrity of the female lower urinary tract. Its deficiency, particularly in postmenopausal women, has been strongly linked to an increased risk of rUTIs. This heightened susceptibility arises from both anatomical and immunological changes induced by the loss of estrogenic stimulation.

One of the primary consequences of estrogen deficiency is urogenital atrophy, characterized by thinning of the urogenital epithelium, decreased epithelial cell turnover, and diminished tight junction integrity. These alterations compromise the barrier function of the bladder and vaginal mucosa, allowing uropathogens easier access to underlying tissues [21]. Estrogen also regulates the expression of urothelial antimicrobial peptides such as β-defensins and cathelicidins, which are critical for early immune defense. In estrogen-deprived states, the production of these peptides is reduced, weakening the local immune response [28].

Furthermore, estrogen shapes the composition of the vaginal microbiota. In premenopausal women, a Lactobacillus-dominant microbiome helps maintain an acidic vaginal pH that inhibits colonization by uropathogens such as E. coli. After menopause, the decline in estrogen leads to a reduction in Lactobacillus species and an increase in pH, creating an environment conducive to pathogenic colonization [29].

Importantly, clinical studies have demonstrated that topical estrogen therapy, such as intravaginal estriol or estradiol, can restore the urogenital epithelium, lower vaginal pH, and reestablish a protective Lactobacillus-dominant microbiota. Randomized controlled trials have shown that such therapy significantly reduces the incidence of rUTIs in postmenopausal women without systemic adverse effects, making it a safe and effective non-antibiotic strategy for prevention [30].

Given the multifactorial impact of estrogen on mucosal integrity, immunity, and microbiota composition, hormone replacement should be considered a cornerstone of rUTI prevention in postmenopausal populations. Topical formulations are especially effective due to their local action and minimal systemic effects.

4. Metabolic and comorbid conditions

Metabolic disorders and comorbid conditions significantly influence host susceptibility to rUTIs, primarily by impairing immune function, altering the urinary environment, and facilitating bacterial persistence. Among these, diabetes mellitus is the most extensively studied and consistently associated with increased rUTI risk.

In individuals with poorly controlled diabetes, hyperglycemia compromises multiple components of the innate immune system. Neutrophil chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and oxidative burst activity are markedly reduced, thereby weakening the body's ability to eliminate invading uropathogens [21]. Furthermore, the presence of glucosuria provides a readily available nutrient source for bacteria, particularly E. coli, promoting microbial growth and facilitating colonization of the bladder wall [31]. High glucose concentrations in urine also favor biofilm formation, especially on catheter surfaces or damaged urothelium, increasing the likelihood of persistent infection.

Beyond diabetes, a range of immunosuppressive conditions predispose patients to rUTIs by reducing their capacity to mount effective immune responses. These include solid organ transplantation, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, and immunosuppressive therapies involving corticosteroids or biologic agents. In transplant recipients, asymptomatic bacteriuria frequently progresses to symptomatic infection, and uropathogens isolated from these patients are more likely to exhibit multidrug resistance, complicating treatment and increasing recurrence risk [32].

Obesity and metabolic syndrome may also play indirect roles by contributing to chronic inflammation, urinary stasis, and alterations in the urinary microbiome, though these associations are less well-characterized and remain an area of active research [33].

Overall, metabolic and comorbid conditions not only predispose individuals to initial infection but also prolong bacterial persistence, increase the frequency and severity of recurrences, and reduce the efficacy of standard antimicrobial therapy. Management strategies in these populations should therefore address both infectious and systemic components, emphasizing metabolic control, immune monitoring, and individualized antimicrobial regimens.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

rUTIs impose a significant clinical burden due to their high prevalence, negative impact on quality of life, and the limitations of current therapeutic strategies. Although standard antibiotic regimens often provide short-term symptom relief, they frequently fail to eradicate the underlying reservoirs of persistent bacteria, thereby contributing to relapse, antimicrobial resistance, and microbiota disruption.

One of the major clinical challenges in rUTI is the inadequacy of current diagnostic approaches to detect persistent or intracellular bacterial populations. Conventional urine culture techniques are designed to identify planktonic, extracellular bacteria in the bladder lumen but are often unable to detect intracellular uropathogens or bacteria embedded within biofilms. This limitation can lead to false-negative results and inappropriate discontinuation of therapy [34].

Beyond bacterial factors, host-mediated inflammatory responses also play a critical role in sustaining recurrent infection. Recent experimental research has explored inflammatory mechanisms underlying cystitis models, demonstrating potential targets for modulating bladder inflammation relevant to UTI persistence and recurrence [35].

Furthermore, the repeated use of antibiotics in rUTI patients exerts selective pressure on both uropathogens and the commensal microbiota. This can lead to dysbiosis, characterized by a loss of protective bacterial species such as Lactobacillus and an increase in opportunistic pathogens or antibiotic-resistant organisms [36]. Broad-spectrum antibiotics in particular can increase the risk of Clostridioides difficile infection and yeast overgrowth as well as gastrointestinal side effects, underscoring the need for more targeted and judicious antimicrobial use.

To address these limitations, several novel therapeutic strategies are currently under investigation. Among these, intracellularly active antibiotics such as fosfomycin and nitrofurantoin have demonstrated favorable penetration into urothelial cells and retain antimicrobial activity against quiescent or biofilm-associated bacteria [7]. In addition, adjunctive therapies including anti-biofilm agents (e.g., N-acetylcysteine), immunomodulators, and probiotics have been explored for their potential to restore mucosal immune function and reestablish microbial homeostasis within the urinary tract. Topical vaginal estrogen therapy has shown substantial efficacy in postmenopausal women by promoting urogenital epithelial repair and maintaining a Lactobacillus-dominant vaginal microbiota, which provides a natural defense against uropathogen colonization. Furthermore, emerging preventive strategies such as vaccine development and anti-adhesion therapies are also gaining attention. Among these, mannoside receptor blockers, which inhibit the binding of UPEC to urothelial surfaces, are currently undergoing evaluation in advanced stages of clinical research [37].

In addition to pathogen-directed and host-modulating approaches, broader clinical urology research continues to explore complementary frameworks for managing chronic lower urinary tract conditions. Recent evidence suggests that extracorporeal shockwave therapy may represent a complementary approach in male patients with chronic pelvic pain syndrome, a condition that often overlaps with lower urinary tract symptoms and may share inflammatory mechanisms with persistent urinary tract disease [38].

Ultimately, effective rUTI management requires a perpersonalized and mechanism-informed approach. This involves identifying whether persistence is driven by biofilm formation, intracellular survival, host immunodeficiency, or anatomical abnormality, and selecting treatment strategies accordingly. Integrating diagnostic biomarkers and phenotypic classification of rUTI may lead to more precise and durable clinical outcomes.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Despite growing understanding of the biological and clinical underpinnings of rUTIs, significant gaps remain in our ability to predict, diagnose, and effectively manage persistent infections. Future research must prioritize the development of mechanism-specific diagnostics, precision treatment strategies, and preventive interventions tailored to the heterogeneity of rUTI pathogenesis.

Advancements in single-cell transcriptomics and spatial omics technologies offer promising opportunities to map the complex microbial and immune microenvironments of the urinary bladder. These tools may uncover tissue-specific niches where uropathogens persist, identify bacterial states associated with dormancy or immune evasion, and define the host epithelial and immune cell responses that permit or prevent recurrence [17].

Additionally, the identification of host-derived biomarkers predictive of persistence or treatment failure could guide patient stratification and individualized care. Genomic and transcriptomic profiling of both the host and pathogen may reveal susceptibility patterns, immune deficiencies, or pathogen adaptations that inform risk assessment and therapeutic selection [16].

Therapeutically, disrupting bacterial persistence mechanisms represents a central target. Inhibition of quorum sensing, neutralization of TA systems, and disruption of biofilm integrity are all areas of active investigation. Novel agents, including bacteriophage therapy, live biotherapeutics, and synthetic antimicrobial peptides, are being explored as adjuncts or alternatives to antibiotics, especially in patients with multidrug-resistant infections or contraindications to conventional therapy [39].

Equally important is the need for clinical trial designs that incorporate microbiological and host phenotyping. Most rUTI studies to date have focused on antibiotic comparisons without accounting for bacterial persistence or immunological variation. Future trials should adopt more mechanism-based frameworks, possibly integrating biomarkers, imaging, and omics data to better evaluate therapeutic efficacy in biologically defined subgroups.

In summary, advancing the care of patients with rUTIs will require a paradigm shift from empirical, symptom-driven management to precision medicine approaches informed by microbial behavior, host response, and emerging biological technologies.
