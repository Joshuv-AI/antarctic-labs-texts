Influence of ultrasonication power and treatment duration on the functional and structural properties of elephant foot yam starch
Authors: Tamma Medha, Paidi Sai Deepika, T Selva Kumar, Venkata Rao Vantaku, Mohammed Amanullah, Palanisamy Bruntha Devi, Ramachandran Vinayagam, Chagam Koteswara Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElephant foot yam (Amorphophallus paeoniifolius) starch is an underutilized C-type tuber starch, whose native properties may limit its functionality in processed food systems. Ultrasonication is a sustainable, reagent-free technique for modifying starch structure and improving its performance. This study investigated the effects of probe ultrasonication power levels (20% and 30% amplitude) and treatment duration (20 and 30 min) on the structural, physicochemical, thermal, textural, and micrometric properties of elephant foot yam starch (EFYS). Ultrasonication significantly increased the apparent amylose content (24.68–26.68%), indicating partial amylopectin depolymerization. Swelling power (13.61–15.92 g/g) and solubility (0.33–0.49%) increased, whereas water absorption capacity decreased (1.13–0.75 g/g). Enhanced solubilization improved paste clarity, and light transmittance. FE-SEM analysis revealed surface erosion and fissures without thermal gelatinization. XRD confirmed the retention of the C-type crystalline pattern with reduced crystallinity, while FTIR indicated disruption of hydrogen bonding and short-range molecular order without chemical alteration. Thermal analysis showed a reduction in gelatinization onset temperature (62.44–50.40°C), and an increase in enthalpy (15.15–17.66 J/g), suggesting molecular rearrangement. Gel hardness and gumminess increased, while powder flowability slightly declined. Overall, controlled ultrasonication efficiently tailored the EFYS properties, indicating its potential applicability in thickening systems and gel-based food formulations.Keywords: Elephant foot yam starch, Ultrasonication, Physicochemical properties, Morphological properties, Crystalline properties

Introduction

Elephant foot yam (Amorphophallus paeoniifolius) is a tropical perennial tuber crop of the family Araceae, widely cultivated and consumed in South and Southeast Asia due to its substantial carbohydrate reserves, culinary variety, and cultural importance [1]. Despite its high starch yield and unique physicochemical properties, the elephant foot yam starch (EFYS) remains underutilized compared to conventional tuber starches such as potato and cassava [2]. Typically, native EFYS exhibits a C-type crystalline pattern and moderate amylose content, which can limit its functionality in processed food systems. Starch is a fundamental component of food, serves as the primary energy source in human diets [3]. It consists of two forms of α-D-glucose polymers: amylose and amylopectin, which form semi crystalline granule structures. Amylose is located in the amorphous regions, whereas amylopectin is found in crystalline regions, where its outer chains form double helices [4]. Starch granules can generally be classified as A-, B-, or C-type based on their crystalline structures as determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. The A-type polymorph features compactly packed starch double helix structure, with water molecules positioned in between each double helix. In contrast, the B-type polymorph, has a more open structure, with six double helices surrounding water molecules in the central cavity. The C-type polymorph is more complex, as it contains characteristics of both A- and B-type polymorphs [5].

The structural properties of starch are typically modified through various processing techniques, including heat-moisture treatment, microwave treatment, hydrothermal treatment, high pressure treatment, and ultrasonication treatment, all of which can further influence the digestion behaviours of starch [2], [5]. Among these methods, ultrasonication is frequently used to produce modified starch because it is environmentally safe, with higher product yields, reduced processing time, and absence of chemical residues [6]. The cavitation effect and mechanical vibrations induced during ultrasonication can disrupt cell walls, degrade molecules, and produce free radicals. Precisely, ultrasonic treatment is a favoured non-thermal processing technique that produces modified starch without chemical residues, and minimal by-products. Ultrasound can alter the physical characteristics of food ingredients, resulting in new structures or functions [7]. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ultrasonication in modifying starches from various botanical sources, including corn, potatoes, and cassava [5], [7]. These modifications can significantly affect digestibility, crystallinity, morphology, and overall physicochemical properties [8].

Although extensive research has been conducted on the ultrasonication of conventional starches, limited information is available on C-type starches, such as EFYS, primarily due to their complex hybrid crystalline structure. In particular, there is a lack of systematic understanding of how ultrasonication parameters such as power and treatment duration affect the structural transformations, and functional properties of EFYS. Furthermore, the relationships between structural changes (e.g., molecular order, crystallinity, and morphology) and functional properties (e.g., swelling, solubility, and gel texture) have not been clearly established. Since the effects of ultrasonication are highly influenced by the botanical origin and crystalline polymorphism, even subtle variations in processing conditions may induce non-linear and system-specific changes, highlighting the need for controlled investigation.

To address this gap, the present study systematically investigates the effects of ultrasonication on EFYS. Specifically, it examines the influence of ultrasonication power and treatment duration on physicochemical and functional properties, characterizes structural modifications using FTIR, XRD, and SEM analyses, explores relationships between structural changes and functional properties through correlation analysis, and evaluates the potential application of modified starch. The selected ultrasonication conditions were designed to achieve controlled structural modification without complete granule disruption, enabling a clearer understanding of structure–function relationships relevant to gel-based, and targeted food applications.

Material and methods

Raw materials and reagents

Freshly harvested elephant foot yam tubers were procured from the local traditional market in Visakhapatnam, India. All analytical-grade chemicals and solvents used in this study were acquired from Hi-Media Co. in Mumbai, India, and were employed in strict accordance with the supplier's protocol.

Isolation of starch

Starch was extracted following the method of Medha et al. [9]. Tubers were peeled, sliced, and soaked in 0.2% sodium metabisulphite solution (1:4 w/v) for 10 min. The mixture was then homogenized for 10 min and filtered through a 100 µm sieve. The unfiltered materials after rinsing with distilled water were filtered three times until the filtrate was clear. Following a 4 h precipitation period, the supernatant was discarded and the sediment was collected by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 15 min. The starch was dried at 40°C overnight in a hot air oven, grounded using a mortar and pestle, sieved through a 125 µm sieve, and stored in airtight containers at room temperature. The obtained starch was designated as native elephant foot yam starch (NEFYS).

Ultrasonication treatment

EFYS was modified by ultrasonication following the method described by Babu et al. [10] with minor modifications. A 2 mm probe with a fixed power of 500 W and a frequency of 20 kHz was used to sonicate the native starch in a 1:2 starch–water ratio (g/mL) using a Vibra Cell TM Sonics, USA. Ultrasonication was applied at power rates of 20% and 30% for 20 and 30 min, with regulated pulse intervals of 30 sec on and 59 sec off. The selected ultrasonication conditions were optimized to induce controlled structural modifications without causing complete gelatinization or excessive molecular degradation [11]. Throughout the treatment, the sample was placed in an ice bath to maintain the temperature at 37°C. After sonication, the samples were cooled to room temperature, dried at 40°C overnight in a hot air oven, subsequent to pulverization. The ultrasonicated samples were labelled as follows: US-1 for 20 min at 20% power, US-2 for 20 min at 30% power, US-3 for 30 min at 20% power, and US-4 for 30 min at 30% power.

Apparent amylose content

Apparent amylose content was determined using the method described by Bist et al. [11]. Initially, starch (0.1 g) was dispersed in 1 mL of 95% ethanol and subsequently treated with 9 mL of 1 N NaOH. The resulting suspension was heated in a water bath at 90°C for 10 min to ensure total gelatinization. Following cooling, the volume was adjusted to 100 mL with distilled water. A 5 mL aliquot was then transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask, to which 1 mL of acetic acid and 2 mL of iodine-potassium iodide (I-KI) solution were added. The transmittance at 620 nm was measured using a UH5300 HITACHI spectrophotometer after the mixture was thoroughly mixed and allowed to stand for 20 min.

Functional properties

The functional properties of starch were evaluated using the methodology outlined by Medha et al. [12], which includes the evaluation of water absorption capacity (WAC), swelling power (SP), and solubility (S). In a stirring water bath, the starch dispersion (10 mg/mL) was heated to 90°C for 30 min. Following incubation, the samples underwent centrifugation for 15 min at 5000 rpm. X-ray diffraction

A powder X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance, Germany) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) was used to record the X-ray diffraction patterns of the EFYS samples. The data were obtained at a scanning rate of 1.5°/min over a 2θ range of 5°–50°. The relative crystallinity (RC) of the starch samples was determined using DIFFRAC.EVA software.

Thermal analysis

A thermogravimetric analyser (TGA, TA Q500 Instruments) operating within a nitrogen-filled chamber at a flow rate of 50 mL/min was used to assess the thermal stability of the samples. A steady heating rate of 10°C/min was applied to heat about 5 mg of each sample from 30°C to 600°C.

Field emission- scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM)

A field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Tescan Mira-3, Czech Republic) running at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV was used to examine the surface morphology of the starch granules. Prior to imaging, a small quantity of starch powder was placed on aluminium stubs using double-sided tape and, to improve electrical conductivity, was sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold under vacuum conditions.

Attenuated total reflectance-fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy

An ALPHA II spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) was used to record the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of both native and modified EFYS samples. Each spectrum was generated from 64 scans using the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet technique, with data collected in the range of 4000–500 cm−1.

Water activity and color parameters

A 4TE Aqualab water activity meter was used to determine the water activity of both native and modified starch samples at ambient temperature.

A HunterLab Ultra Scan VIS spectrophotometer (HunterLab, Reston, Virginia, USA) was used to assess the color characteristics of the native and modified starch samples, following the calibration of the instrument. The chromatic parameters L* (lightness), a* (green–red), and b* (blue–yellow) were measured in triplicate after samples were placed in plastic sample cups [13]. Texture profile analysis of starch gels

In accordance with the methodology delineated by Makroo et al. [14] the solidified gel was carefully removed from the tube and examined for textural profile analysis (TPA) using TA HD-plus Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro System Ltd. Surrey, UK). A cylindrical probe with a diameter of 25 mm (P/25P) was utilized for the two-bite compression test, conducted up to a distance 12 mm with a trigger force of 0.049 N. The pre-test, post-test, and test speeds were set at 1.0 mm s−1 and 0.5 mm s−1, respectively. The maximum force of the initial peak was used to calculate hardness (g); the ratio of time to peak 2 to time to peak 1 was used to calculate springiness; and the ratio of the second positive area under the compression curve to that of the first positive area was used to calculate cohesiveness [15]. The TPA characteristics, including hardness, cohesiveness, chewiness, gumminess and springiness were recorded.

Light transmittance

To measure the transmittance of native and sonicated starch pastes, 1% (w/v) starch suspensions were prepared in distilled water using screw-cap tubes. To reduce shear effects, the dispersions were heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min with continuous stirring. Following the heating process, the samples were cooled under running water to room temperature. Transmittance at 650 nm was measured using a UH5300 HITACHI spectrophotometer, with distilled water serving as the blank [8].

Micromeritics properties

A 10 mL graduated cylinder was loaded with 3 g of starch, and the cylinder was tapped until no further change in volume was observed to determine bulk and tapped densities. Carr's compressibility index and the Hausner ratio were subsequently determined using the standard equations [16].

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with all analyses that were carried out in triplicate. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's Multiple Range Test at a confidence level of (p ≤ 0.05), employing SPSS software (IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA). Additionally, correlation studies were conducted using the same software.

Among the treated samples, the amylose content of the US-1 was considerably greater than that of the NEFYS [18]. Conversely, US-2 had a slightly lower amylose content of 24.07%, which was considerably lower than US-1 but comparable to NEFYS. The amylose content of the US-3 was 25.90%, which was significantly higher than that of NEFYS and US-2 but statistically similar to US-1. The maximal amylose value was measured in the US-4 sample at 26.68%, compared to other samples, and was significantly different (p < 0.05). Overall, the apparent amylose content followed the sequence: US-4 > US-3 ≈ US-1 > NEFYS ≈ US-2, indicating that ultrasonication induced a measurable change in amylose content [19]. These results clearly demonstrate that ultrasonic processing altered the amylose fraction in EFYS without introducing unpredictable variations across treatments. According to Vela et al. [18], ultrasonic treatments can lead to partial depolymerization of amylopectin chains by cleaving alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 glycosidic bonds, thereby increasing the proportion of linear chains, as measured by apparent amylose. Mieles-Gómez et al. [20] reported similar observations for mango kernel starch, where an increase of 13–18% in amylose was attributed to this cavitation-induced structural modification. The slight decrease observed at higher power levels in this study may reflect further degradation into smaller non-starchy fragments, a phenomenon also observed in arrowroot starch nanoparticles, where treatment intensity influenced the final molecular distribution. These observations indicate that ultrasonication induces controlled depolymerization of amylopectin chains rather than random degradation, highlighting a mechanism-driven restructuring of starch molecular architecture.

Functional properties

The functional properties of native and ultrasonicated EFY starches were measured using WAC, SP, and S, and their results are presented in Table 1. The WAC of NEFYS was 1.13 g/g, which was much greater than the WAC of all the ultrasonicated starch samples (p < 0.05) [21]. A significant decrease in WAC was observed following ultrasonic treatment. Specifically, US-1 and US-3 exhibited WAC values of 0.94 g/g and 0.89 g/g, respectively. Further, reductions were observed in the samples treated at higher power levels, while US-2 and US-4 both showing WAC values of 0.75 g/g. The lowest WAC was observed for US-4, indicating that prolonged ultrasonication at higher power intensity significantly reduced the starch's water-holding capacity.

The SP of the starch samples ranged from 13.61 to 15.92 g/g, with NEFYS displaying the lowest SP value of 13.61 g/g. Ultrasonication enhanced the swelling capacity of starch granules, with US-1 exhibiting the highest SP of 15.92 g/g, followed by US-4 at 15.01 g/g and US-3 at 14.63 g/g. US-2 (13.69 g/g) was similar to the SP of native starch, suggesting that the parameters of intensity and duration of power differentially influenced the swelling behaviour [18].

The solubility of the starch samples increased significantly post ultrasound treatment. Native starch exhibited the lowest solubility value (0.33%), whereas ultrasonic samples demonstrated considerably higher solubility values, ranging from 0.44% to 0.49%. US-1 (0.49%), US-4 (0.47%), US-3 (0.45%), and US-2 (0.44%) were found to be the most soluble, with these values being statistically significant compared to NEFYS. The findings clearly indicate that ultrasonic treatment significantly influenced the functional characteristics of EFYS: WAC decreased, while SP and S increased with increasing treatment intensity and duration. The simultaneous increase in SP and S observed here is consistent with the behaviour of ultrasonically modified durian and buckwheat starches [22]. Rahaman et al. [23] observed that acoustic cavitation creates grooves and notches on the starch surface, increasing the surface area for water penetration and enhancing hydration. While traditional thermal treatments often involve a trade-off between swelling and solubility, ultrasound-induced modification of corn and cassava starches showed that structural loosening allows for greater expansion of the granule matrix while simultaneously facilitating the leaching of amylose [23]. These findings confirm that ultrasonication effectively overcomes the native resistance of the EFYS granules. This behaviour demonstrates that ultrasonication facilitates coordinated structural loosening and enhances water accessibility, rather than causing isolated changes in properties, thereby establishing a definitive structure–function relationship.

Transmittance

The clarity of native and ultrasonically modified EFYS suspensions was determined by light transmittance, and the results are presented in Table 1. The light transmittance of NEFYS was the lowest among the samples, indicating the formation of a relatively opaque starch paste. This reduced clarity can be attributed to the preserved granular structure and fewer molecular ruptures, which hamper light penetration in the starch suspension. The ultrasonicated starch samples showed a significantly higher transmittance than native starch (p < 0.05), indicating that ultrasonic treatment enhanced the paste clarity. Among the treated samples, US-1 demonstrated significant increase in transmittance compared to NEFYS, indicating a partial disruption of the starch granules and better dispersion of the starch molecules in the aqueous medium. This increase indicates that, following moderate ultrasonic treatment, the amylose fractions exhibit higher solubility [24].

The transmittance further increased with US-2, indicating that ultrasonic power influences the starch structure. The increased energy input probably enhanced granule erosion and the rearrangement of molecules, enabling thorough light transmission through the starch paste. The samples subjected to prolonged ultrasonication, US-3 and US-4 had the highest transmittance values among all samples. Specifically, the maximum transmittance was observed in US-4, suggesting substantial disruption of the starch granule structure. The overall outcome of the treatment duration and power intensity was greater starch solubilization and reduced light scattering due to aggregated granules [25]. The gradual increase in transmittance with increasing ultrasonication severity indicates that ultrasonication effectively alters the molecular structure of starch, thereby enhancing the paste's transparency. The transmittance results demonstrate that ultrasonic treatment improves the transparency of EFYS paste, with higher power and longer treatment time resulting in a more transparent system. The observed reduction in light transmittance following ultrasonication aligns with findings reported by Vela et al. [18], who reported that strong sonication can cause granule disintegration and the leaching of linear amylose molecules, thereby increasing the paste's turbidity. Unlike sorghum starch, which occasionally shows improved clarity due to particle size reduction, EFYS appears more sensitive to cavitation-induced fissures that scatter light [19]. This suggests that, for this specific botanical source, molecular restructuring plays a more significant role than particle size reduction in determining optical properties. The progressive increase in transmittance further supports the hypothesis that cavitation-induced molecular dispersion governs optical behaviour, reflecting systematic internal restructuring.

Water activity and color

Table 2 presents the water activity and color properties of both native and ultrasonic EFYS. The water activity of NEFYS was measured at 0.41, indicating a low level of free water and high storage stability. While ultrasonication did not cause any radical changes in water activity, statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were recorded between the samples based on treatment conditions. The water activity values for ultrasonicated starches were 0.42 and 0.45. In the treated samples, US-1 exhibited a water activity of 0.42, similar to that of the native starch. US-2 and US-3 showed a slight increase with the values of 0.43 and 0.44, respectively. The maximum water activity was observed in US-4 at 0.45, indicating that increased ultrasonic intensity and treatment time had a modest positive effect on the moisture availability in the starch matrix. Despite these variations, all samples remained within the low water activity range, thereby demonstrating their applicability for dry storage.

FE-SEM

The morphological characteristics of the native and ultrasonicated EFYS granules are depicted in Fig. 1. The NEFYS granules exhibit irregular morphologies, including spherical, elliptical, and polygonal forms, characterized by sharp edges and mainly smooth surfaces (Fig. 1a). Ultrasonication treatment induced progressive surface modifications in the starch granules. US-1 did not reveal any noticeable change in the overall shape of the granules; however, minor surface roughness and shallow pits could be noticed on several granules (Fig. 1b). These subtle changes indicate the initial effects of aquatic cavitation, where microbubble produces negligible shear forces sufficient to dislodge the granular surface, without causing substantial structural damage. Upon increasing the ultrasonic power to 30% for the same treatment duration (US-2), there was a noticeable increase in surface erosion with the appearance of fissures, surface peeling, and cracks (Fig. 1c). The increased cavitation at the increased power probably facilitated the greater mechanical shear, loosening the intermolecular association on the granule surfaces. These characteristics are consistent with Sharma et al. [27], where high-power ultrasonication resulted in similar granule surface disruption. US-3 induced more pronounced morphological changes, such as granule distortion and aggregation of the fragmented particles (Fig. 1d). Despite lower power settings compared to US-2, the extended exposure time seems to have intensified the cumulative mechanical stress, suggesting that duration is a crucial factor in granular disruption. US-4 granules were heavily fragmented, displaying irregular debris and agglomeration (Fig. 1e). The combined effect of accumulated power and long exposure time caused severe cavitation, penetrated both exterior and interior regions of the granules, leading to significant disruption of granular integrity. Notably, no signs of thermal gelatinization were observed in any of the sonicated samples, indicating that the morphological changes were primarily due to mechanical disruption from cavitation rather than heating. Sujka and Wiącek [28] observed similar surface damage in corn and potato starches, where micro-jets produced by collapsing bubbles physically eroded the granule periphery. These structural defects are directly responsible for the enhanced functional properties reported earlier, as they serve as conduits for water entry and enzymatic attack. These morphological transitions clearly illustrate a power–time dependent cavitation mechanism, progressing from surface erosion to internal fragmentation.

FT-IR spectra analysis

Fig. 2 represents the FTIR spectra of native and ultrasonicated EFYS indicating the impact of ultrasonication power and duration on the molecular structure of starch. The FTIR spectrum of NEFYS displays characteristic absorption bands associated with starch polysaccharides, validating the presence of a backbone with nine alpha-D-glucopyranosyl units. In the native starch, a broad absorption pattern with a peak of around 3273 cm−1 is observed, corresponding to OH-stretching vibrations resulting from extensive intra- and inter-molecular hydrogen bonding between the starch chains. The peak observed at 2931 cm−1, attributed to the C-H stretching vibrations of the methylene groups within the glucose units of the starch polymer. In the lower wavenumber region, the absorption band at 1641 cm−1 corresponds to H-O-H bending vibrations of bound water molecules, indicating the retention of physically bound moisture across all samples despite ultrasonication. The strong absorption bands at 1149 cm−1, 1076 cm -1, and 995 cm−1, represent C-O-C and C-O vibrations of the anhydro glucose ring and glycosidic linkages, forming the fingerprint region of starch [29]. After ultrasonication, all treated samples showed the same characteristic absorption bands as the native starch indicating that ultrasonication did not cause any chemical modification or cleavage of the glycosidic bonds, thereby preserving the primary molecular structure of starch.

FT-IR spectra of native and ultrasonicated EFYS in the range of 4000–500 cm−1. All samples retain characteristic starch absorption bands, confirming the preservation of chemical structure. However, variations in band intensity, particularly in the O–H stretching region (∼3273 cm−1), indicate disruption and rearrangement of hydrogen bonding with increasing ultrasonication intensity. Treatment conditions: US-1 (20 min, 20% amplitude), US-2 (20 min, 30% amplitude), US-3 (30 min, 20% amplitude), and US-4 (30 min, 30% amplitude).

However, significant changes in band intensity were observed increased ultrasonication power and treatment duration. Specifically, the O-H stretching band at 3273 cm−1 exhibited a slower reduction in intensity and slight broadening under higher ultrasonic power and prolonged exposure (US-3 and US-4). The changes suggest that ultrasonication induces partial rupture and reorganisation of hydrogen-bonding interactions within starch granules due to mechanical forces generated by cavitation [17]. Conversely, the band at 995 cm−1, commonly associated with the amorphous regions of starch, remained consistently clear across all samples. This observation indicates that ultrasonication selectively effects the ordered regions and while leaving the amorphous region intact. Notably, all ultrasonicated samples showed no significant peak shift, suggesting that the basic structure of the starch molecules remained unchanged. FTIR analysis demonstrates that ultrasonication can modify EFYS by primarily altering hydrogen bonding and short-range molecular order, while the chemical identity of the starch polymer remains unchanged. The stability of the primary functional groups in the FTIR spectra suggests that ultrasonication is a purely physical modification that does not introduce new chemical entities, consistent with studies on chestnut and potato starches. Wang et al. [30] reported that, while fingerprint regions may exhibit intensity variations due to changes in short-range order, the fundamental chemical backbone of the starch remains intact. This finding substantiates that ultrasonication predominantly modifies intermolecular interactions and short-range order, rather than affecting the primary chemical structure.

XRD

Fig. 3 presents the X-ray diffraction patterns of native and ultrasonicated treated EFYS. The NEFYS showed clear refraction peaks at 15°, 17°, 18.2°, and 23°, characteristic of the C-type starch. Samples US-1 to US-4 exhibited the identical diffraction peak positions to the native starch, indicating that ultrasonication did not induce a crystalline polymorphic transition. This preservation of crystalline-type suggest that the primary effect of ultrasound treatment is to alter the degree of molecular order rather than modify the fundamental crystalline framework of EFYS. This observation aligns with findings in ultrasonically modified starches from potato, cassava, and maize, where crystal polymorph remained unchanged despite variations in processing intensity [31].

All samples exhibit characteristic C-type crystalline peaks, indicating no polymorphic transition after ultrasonication. However, reduced peak intensity and increased broadening in treated samples (US-1 to US-4) suggest a progressive decrease in relative crystallinity due to cavitation-induced structural disruption. Treatment conditions: US-1 (20 min, 20% amplitude), US-2 (20 min, 30% amplitude), US-3 (30 min, 20% amplitude), and US-4 (30 min, 30% amplitude).

Although the peak positions remained constant, the sharpness and intensity of the peaks changed significantly in the ultrasonicated samples, indicating a change in relative crystallinity. There was a slight decrease in the peak intensity of US-1 whereas, NEFYS showed a significant reduction, indicating minimal disruption of crystalline domains even under mild ultrasonication conditions. Conversely, US-2 showed a more significant reduction in peak intensity. US-3 exhibited broader, less intense peaks, indicating a greater loss of long-range molecular order. The most substantial decrease in peak intensity and increase in broadening were observed in US-4, indicating that an increase in ultrasonic energy input markedly disrupted the crystalline lamellae of the starch grains. This pattern is consistent with SEM observations of surface erosion and grain disruption, as well as FTIR results, which indicated a shift in hydrogen-bonding patterns [32]. The preservation of the crystalline structure, despite a reduction in crystallinity, indicates a selective disruption of ordered domains without inducing polymorphic transformation. This represents a fundamental mechanistic aspect of controlled ultrasonication.

Thermal analysis

The thermal properties of both native and ultrasonicated EFYS samples are summarized in Table 3. NEFYS exhibited an onset temperature (To) of 62.44°C, a peak temperature (Tp) of 82.45°C, and a conclusion temperature (Tc) of 95.66°C. These temperatures delineate the range within which the semi-crystalline starch granules transition to an amorphous state. The enthalpy of gelatinization (ΔH) for NEFYS was 15.15 J/g, reflecting the energy required to disrupt the molecular arrangement and double helices within the granules. Ultrasonic treatment significantly influenced the thermal transition temperatures (p < 0.05). Especially, while sonication generally lowered the onset of gelatinization, it resulted in a substantial increase in the conclusion temperature and energy requirement. For US-1, the To decreased to 52.95°C, yet the Tc increased sharply to 120.20°C, with an increased ΔH of 17.46 J/g. A similar pattern was observed in US-2, where the Tc further increased to 126.73°C, and the ΔH remained high at 17.16 J/g [33]. The most pronounced changes were observed with longer exposure times. US-3 recorded the highest values for Tp (88.93°C), Tc (129.57°C), and ΔH (17.66 J/g). Even at the highest power rate in US-4, the enthalpy remained significantly higher than native starch at 17.42 J/g, despite a very low To of 50.40°C. This concurrent decrease in To an increase in ΔH suggests that while cavitation disrupts the surface and amorphous regions (facilitating earlier water entry), the high-frequency vibrations and localized heat may also induce possible re-ordering of the remaining crystalline lamellae. This phenomenon is often attributed to a sonication-induced annealing effect, wherein fragmented chains reorganize into more stable, heat-resistant structures that require higher energy to melt [18]. The concurrent decrease in To and increase in ΔH indicates a dual effect of structural disruption and molecular reorganization, thereby reinforcing the non-linear response of EFYS to ultrasonication. Fig. 5 illustrates the general mechanism of ultrasonication induced changes, which encompasses cavitation, improved water interaction and structural disruption.

Textural properties

To analyze the mechanical properties of gels derived from native and ultrasonically modified EFYS, texture profile analysis (TPA) was employed and the results are presented in Table 4. The native EFYS demonstrated a relatively soft gel, as evidenced by a hardness measurement of 5.56 N. Subjecting the gel to ultrasonic treatment (US-1) resulted in a significant increase in gel hardness (p < 0.05), reaching values of 7.77 N, 7.28 N, as observed in US-1, US-2, US-3, and US-4, respectively. The hardness value of US-3 increased with prolonged exposure at moderate power, suggesting that extended exposure time at moderate power was more effective in intensifying the gel network than merely increasing power intensity. This phenomenon is consistent with the effects of cavitation, which disrupt starch granules, thereby increasing molecular mobility and the intensity of intermolecular hydrogen bonding during gelation and retrogradation. Cohesiveness, indicative of internal structural integrity and resistance to disintegration, decreased from 44.92 in native starch to 36.23 in US-1 and 33.90 in US-2, respectively, indicating a weakening of the internal network to some extent following ultrasonic fragmentation of the polymer chains. The US-3 (38.45) showed a slight recovery, indicating that the longer the treatment time, the better fragmented starch chains were re-associated into more organised junction zones. Cohesiveness in US-2 was lowest, suggesting that an increase in power over a shorter period might, rather than stabilising the network, lead to excessive structural disruption [34].

Ultrasonication significantly enhanced both chewiness and gumminess. Chewiness increased to 94.25 after ultrasonication. Gumminess showed a particularly high increase in US3, with a value of 334.95, compared to 112.89 in the native starch. This substantial increase demonstrates that ultrasonic treatment significantly increased the gel's internal resistance, consistent with its high hardness and intermediate cohesiveness under these conditions. Springiness showed relatively minor changes but remained statistically significant. NEFYS had a springiness of 46.39, with ultrasonicated samples ranging from 36.27 to 47.62. The slight decrease in springiness with more severe treatment indicates that, as rigidity increases, elastic recovery after deformation decreases [35]. The observed decrease in gel hardness and chewiness after sonication is consistent with the “thinning effect” described by Wang et al. [30] for chestnut starch. The mechanical shear forces and free radicals generated during cavitation disrupt long-chain polymers, thereby weakening the three-dimensional gel network. These findings substantiate that ultrasonication enhances gel properties through restructuring of starch networks rather than simple weakening or degradation.

Micromeritics properties

Table 2 summarizes the micromeritic properties of both native and ultrasonicated EFYS, including the Carr index (CI), Hausner ratio (HR), water activity (WA), and color parameters. These variables provide insights into particle packing, flowability, moisture stability, and the surface optical properties. Native starch exhibited a CI of 8.35 and an HR of 1.13, indicating good flowability and low interparticle cohesion. The CI values for US-1, US-2, US-3, and US-4 CI were significantly raised to 10.84, 9.81, 11.35, and 12.03, respectively, following ultrasonic treatment (p < 0.05). Similarly, the HR for US-1 increased to 1.20, and in all the ultrasonicated samples, it exceeded 1.10. These increases indicate reduced flowability and increased compressibility of the powder post-ultrasonic modification. The consistent rise in CI with the intensity of treatment suggests that cavitation-induced surface erosion and micro fracturing resulted in finer particles and irregular surfaces, thereby enhancing interparticle friction and mechanical interlocking during packing [36].

Pearson correlation coefficient analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to elucidate the interrelationships among functional, micromeritic, color, thermal, and textural parameters of both native and ultrasonicated EFYS (Fig. 4). Only statistically significant correlations (p < 0.05) were considered. Apparent amylose content exhibited strong positive correlations with SP (r = 0.57), S (r = 0.49), CI (r = 0.99), and chewiness (r = 0.50), suggesting that increased amylose availability enhances hydration behaviour, reduces powder flowability, and increases gel resistance during mastication. In contrast, amylose content demonstrated strong negative correlations with transmittance (r = –0.86) and thermal parameters such as peak temperature (r = –0.94), indicating that higher amylose fractions diminish optical clarity and thermal stability due to disruption of crystalline domains. SP and S were strongly correlated with each other (r = 0.96) and with HR (r = 0.99), confirming that powders with higher hydration capacity tend to exhibit greater interparticle cohesion and reduced flowability. Transmittance showed strong negative correlations with SP (r = –0.42), S (r = –0.23), and b* value (r = –0.70), indicating increased light scattering in structurally disrupted systems. Thermal parameters (To, Tp, Tc) were highly intercorrelated (r = 0.91–0.95), confirming consistent gelatinization behaviour across treatments. These parameters exhibited strong negative correlations with chewiness (r = –0.76 to –0.91) and gumminess (r = –0.28 to –0.89), indicating that samples with lower gelatinization temperatures tend to form stronger and more resistant gel networks [18]. Textural parameters demonstrated strong internal coherence. Hardness correlated positively with cohesiveness (r = 0.73) and gumminess (r = 0.51), while chewiness correlated strongly with SP (r = 0.87) and S (r = 0.74), confirming the influence of hydration behavior on gel mechanical strength. Springiness showed moderate correlations with SP (r = 0.67) and negative correlations with gumminess (r = –0.78), indicating a trade-off between elastic recovery and structural rigidity. The color parameter b* showed strong correlations with amylose (r = 0.93), S (r = 0.75), and transmittance (r = –0.70). The correlation matrix highlights an interconnected system where ultrasonic treatment simultaneously alters molecular organization, hydration behavior, thermal stability, powder handling, and mechanical performance. These statistically significant relationships confirm that ultrasonication is an effective physical modification method capable of precisely adjusting starch functionality across various performance aspects. The strong correlations between amylose content, SP, and textural hardness found in this study reflect the interconnected system of starch functionality described by Zou et al. [37]. In their study of nine yam varieties, Pearson correlation highlighted that molecular weight and amylose content are the primary drivers of pasting and rheological behavior. The high correlation coefficients found here (r = 0.93 for amylose and color) confirm that the physical changes induced by ultrasound are systematic rather than random. These changes can be attributed to ultrasonic cavitation, in which the collapse of microbubbles generates intense localized shear forces. These forces cause surface damage, create microcracks, and facilitating greater water penetration into the starch granules. Consequently, hydrogen bonding and crystalline regions are partially disrupted, leading to enhanced solubility, swelling, and gel strength. Overall, these results confirm that ultrasonication induces a coordinated reorganization of molecular, crystalline, and granular structures, which collectively govern the functional behaviour of EFYS. This indicates that the observed changes are systematic and mechanistically driven, rather than isolated or purely descriptive effects.

Despite the comprehensive structural and functional analysis, this study has certain limitations. The findings are primarily based on indirect characterization techniques, and no validation was performed in real food systems, which limits the direct applicability of the results. Although clear structure–function relationships were established, a deeper understanding of molecular-level changes requires advanced analytical techniques such as solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and molecular weight distribution analysis (GPC/SEC). Future research should therefore focus on validating the performance of ultrasonically modified EFYS in both model and real food systems, as well as examining its behaviour under processing conditions relevant to industrial applications. Such investigations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of its functional potential and practical feasibility.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that ultrasonication is a sustainable, reagent-free strategy for improving the techno-functional properties of underutilized C-type EFYS, whose native properties restricts its broader application in processed foods. Increasing the ultrasonic power and treatment duration significantly altered the structural and functional characteristics of EFYS without modifying its chemical composition. Apparent amylose content increased from 24.68% to 26.68%, accompanied by enhanced swelling power, solubility, paste clarity, and gel strength, although water absorption capacity, and powder flowability decreased due to granule erosion and surface roughening. Structural analyses confirmed retention of the native C-type crystalline pattern with reduced relative crystallinity and weakening hydrogen bonding. Thermal analysis revealed increased gelatinization enthalpy and earlier gelatinization onset, suggesting molecular reorganization and partial disruption of amorphous regions. Improved compressibility and gel texture were also observed. Importantly, this study demonstrates that even mild ultrasonication conditions can induce non-linear and system-specific structural transformations, enabling controlled tuning of starch functionality. These findings establish a clear structure–function relationship framework for EFYS, and highlight its potential as a sustainable and tunable ingredient for food applications. Overall, this work provides new insights into mechanism-driven modification of underutilized C-type starches, moving beyond conventional parameter optimization approaches.
