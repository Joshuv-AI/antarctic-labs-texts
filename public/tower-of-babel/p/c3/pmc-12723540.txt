Prenatal Exposure to PM2.5 Increased Vulnerability to a Second Hit of a High-Fat Diet in Mouse Offspring
Authors: Limin Wang, Renjie Hu, Zhenghong Yao, Lu Zhang, Li Qin, Ran Li, Qinghua Sun, Cuiqing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence indicates that prenatal exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) may program metabolic syndrome (MS) in adult offspring. However, the metabolic response to the second hit of a high-fat diet (HFD) (prenatal PM2.5 exposure as the first hit) in adult offspring and its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Using a whole-body inhalational exposure system, we achieved the following observations. First, prenatal PM2.5 exposure significantly increased the fat mass, inhibited the insulin signaling pathway in chow diet (CD)-fed offspring, and exacerbated HFD-induced metabolic disorders evidenced by impaired insulin signaling in metabolic organs like liver, epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), and brown adipose tissue (BAT). Second, prenatal PM2.5 exposure enhanced hepatic lipid accumulation and hypertrophy of adipocytes in eWAT and BAT, accompanied by lipid metabolic disorders in the liver and eWAT. Finally, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 exaggerated HFD-induced cellular senescence in both hepatic and eWAT, as well as cell cycle arrest in BAT, which ultimately led to the activation of chronic inflammation in offspring. In conclusion, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 increased the vulnerability to the second hit of HFD feeding in adult offspring, in which chronic inflammation triggered by cellular senescence may be involved. These findings underscore the heightened vulnerability to metabolic diseases in individuals following early life exposure to air pollution, particularly in the setting of compounding with a high-fat dietary pattern.

Introduction

With the rapid development in industry and transportation, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been identified as one of the most serious environmental hazards impacting metabolic syndrome (MS), , which is a complex metabolic disorder, including central obesity, glucose metabolism disorders, dyslipidemia. In 2012, there was an upsurge in the incidence of MS among adults, rising from 25.3% to 34.2%. Based on the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) paradigm, maternal exposure to adverse factors increases the risk of chronic diseases in offspring, providing a plausible explanation for the widespread prevalence of MS. Epidemiological research has demonstrated that maternal exposure to PM2.5 led to obesity and metabolic disorders in progeny. Consistently, prenatal exposure to concentrated ambient PM2.5 has been shown in toxicological investigations to increase adiposity in adult offspring and to impair insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance in male offspring. Prenatal PM2.5 exposure elicited inflammatory responses in the lung and epididymal adipose tissue (eWAT) of rat offspring, exacerbated systemic oxidative stress, and ultimately resulted in metabolic dysfunctions. Collectively, the findings of these investigations robustly affirm the DOHaD paradigm from the perspective of metabolic disorders. It is commonly recognized that intake of a high-fat diet (HFD) is a notable contributing factor and inducer for the onset of MS. Considering the consistent air pollution in the past decades and the widespread preference for high-calorie foods, it is necessary and urgent to elucidate whether prenatal exposure to PM2.5 could enhance the vulnerability of the offspring to the second hit of HFD feeding.

Emerging evidence suggested this vulnerability might stem from prenatal PM2.5 exposure-induced cellular senescence. PM2.5 exposure induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in fetal tissues, thereby activating DNA damage responses, leading to cellular senescence. Senescence is a reversible cell cycle arrest state accompanied by the secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs) factors. Chronic inflammation triggered by SASPs is considered an integrative hallmark of senescence, which impaird organ function, particularly in the liver and adipose tissues, thereby contributing to the MS progression. Prenatal PM2.5 exposure increased the inflammation level in fetuses, leading to lipid and glucose metabolism disorders in adult offspring. HFD-induced obesity was directly correlated with the burden of senescent cells in metabolic organs, and SASPs factors altered the tissue microenvironment to promote chronic liver diseases and dyslipidemia in mature adipocytes. Notably, prenatal exposure to air pollution programed offspring to exhibit increased susceptibility to diet-induced weight gain in adulthood. However, whether prenatal exposure to PM2.5 exacerbates HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction through the amplification of senescence remains to be studied.

In this study, we established a two-hit model combining prenatal PM2.5 exposure with an HFD challenge in adulthood. We hypothesize that prenatal PM2.5 exposure amplifies HFD-induced cellular senescence in key metabolic organs (liver and adipose tissues) of offspring, with senescent cells creating a pro-inflammatory microenvironment through SASPs factors that exacerbate metabolic dysfunction. The results of this investigation offer insights into the consequences and underlying mechanisms by which prenatal exposure to PM2.5 exacerbates the adverse health impacts on offspring growth and development, thereby highlighting the critical role of early life environmental exposure in shaping long-term health outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Animal Use

Six-week-old C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratory (Charles River, Beijing, China) and housed in standard cages in the filtered air with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, temperatures ranging between 18 and 25 °C and relative humidity of 40%–60%. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (ZCMU) (IACUC-202409–23).

Ambient Whole-Body Inhalational PM2.5 Exposure System

The Zhejiang Whole-body Exposure System (ZJ-WES) at ZCMU comprises two temperature-controlled chambers that simulate real-world exposure conditions. The PM2.5 chamber employs a cyclone separator to remove particles >2.5 μm while maintaining natural particle distribution, while the FA chamber uses HEPA filtration to eliminate PM2.5. Our quality control protocol included continuous real-time monitoring using an Aerosol Monitoring Meter (Thermo Scientific, China), which was calibrated to zero before each use according to manufacturer specifications, along with weekly system calibrations. Environmental conditions were maintained at stable levels (temperature: 22 ± 3 °C; relative humidity: 50% ± 10%) through daily monitoring, and wind pressure was controlled at approximately −400 Pa. These stringent measures guaranteed the accuracy and reproducibility of the exposure concentrations throughout the study.

PM2.5 Exposure and HFD Feeding

After 2-week acclimation, the female and male mice (8 week olds) were mated at a ratio of 2:1 overnight at 20:00 every night. The next morning, at 8:00 a.m., the females were examined, and the emergence of a vaginal plug was taken as an indication of the beginning of gestation. Once the vaginal plug was observed (gestational day, GD0), pregnant mice were randomized into the filtered air (FA) group and concentrated PM2.5 (PM) group based on body weight. The experimental exposure was conducted for 12 h daily from April 22 to May 9, 2023, spanning 18 days in the ZJ-WES. At GD18, dams were transferred to an environment with FA to undergo natural parturition until delivery (∼19 days) to ensure that the offspring were not directly exposed to PM2.5. After excluding pseudopregnant mice, the study included n = 4 dams per group, and no instances of abortion or preterm delivery were recorded.

At birth, each litter was randomly culled to maintain 6–9 pups. During lactation, the pups remained with their birth mother until 3 weeks of age. At 3 weeks of age, the pups were weaned, and the male pups were randomized into two groups (FA and PM) based on whether their mothers were exposed to PM2.5 and then fed with a standard diet until 14 weeks of age. At 14 weeks of age, the offspring were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group) for 13-week HFD feeding experiments: (1) FA-CD, prenatal FA exposed-offspring fed with chow diet (CD, 10% of calories from fat, 71% of calories from carbohydrates, 19% of calories from protein; Trophic, TP23302, China); (2) PM-CD, prenatal PM2.5 exposed-offspring fed with CD; (3) FA-HFD, prenatal FA exposed-offspring fed with HFD (60% of calories from fat, 21% of calories from carbohydrates, 19% of calories from protein; Trophic, TP23300, China); and (4) PM-HFD, prenatal PM2.5 exposed-offspring fed with HFD. Throughout the entire experimental duration, all male mice and their offspring were maintained in an environment with filtered air.

PM2.5 Concentration and Characterization

In order to examine the PM2.5 concentration to which mice were exposed, a Teflon membrane (37 mm in diameter, Whatman, UK) was used to collect PM2.5 in the PM2.5 chamber. Before and after sampling, the Teflon membranes were balanced for 24 h in a weighing room maintained at 22 ± 3 °C and 50% ± 10% relative humidity. The membranes were weighed with an Excellence Plus XP microbalance (Mettler Toledo, Schweiz), with each sample weighed in triplicate to calculate weight gain, and blank environmental samples were included to correct for concentration estimation errors. The formula is as follows: the average PM2.5 concentration = [12 h × daily PM2.5 concentration in the PM2.5 chamber (μg/m3) + 12 h × daily PM2.5 concentration in the FA chamber (μg/m3)]/24 h. In our study, the average PM2.5 concentration (91.03 ± 8.70 μg/m3) is comparable to levels observed in urban areas with moderate to high pollution, such as certain cities in developed countries and other industrialized regions. The primary polluting trace metals and water-soluble inorganic ions were analyzed and quantified by Pooke Testing company as previously described.

Offspring Growth Trajectory Recording

All of the offspring were kept at a density of two or three mice per cage. Body weight and food intake were recorded each week.

Body Composition

The body composition of offspring was conducted using a minispec LF50 (Bruker, Germany) after HFD feeding. As described previously, each mouse was measured twice, separated by a 10 min pause, for accurate recording. The ratio of fat or lean mass to body weight was calculated using the following formula: the ratio of fat or lean mass to body weight = [fat or lean mass (g)/body weight (g) × 100%].

Glucose Tolerance and Insulin Sensitivity

At the end of HFD feeding, a glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were conducted. Mice were given a 12 h fast the night before the GTT test. A FreeStyle Blood Glucose Meter (Abbott Diabetes Care Inc., Alameda, CA, USA) was used to measure blood glucose levels at baseline and then 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after receiving an intraperitoneal injection of dextrose (2 mg/g body weight). In the ITT, mice were given an intraperitoneal injection of insulin (0.75 U/kg) after a 4.5 h fast, and blood glucose measurements were made at the same intervals as in the GTT.

Energy Expenditure Measurement

After 24 h of acclimation, VO2 consumption, VCO2 production, respiratory exchange ratio, and heat production were measured at the end of HFD feeding using the Columbus Instruments Laboratory Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS) (Columbus, OH, USA) over 24 h.

Sample Collection

After body composition measurement, GTT, and ITT, offspring were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (20 mg/kg, ip) and euthanized, and blood was collected from the retro-orbital area for serum analysis. The liver, eWAT, and intrascapular brown adipose tissue (BAT) were quickly collected and weighed. The formula was used to calculate the organ coefficient, which is the ratio of an organ’s mass to the overall body weight: organ coefficient = [organ mass (g)/body weight (g) × 100%]. Subsequently, the bulk of the liver, eWAT, and BAT samples from each mouse were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination, while the rest were frozen at −80 °C for future use.

Histological Analysis

For Hematoxylin & Eosin staining (H&E) staining, paraffin-embedded tissue samples were cut into 5 μm and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin as described previously. The vacuolar area in liver sections and the adipocyte size in adipose tissue sections were determined by analyzing four fields per slide utilizing ImageJ Fiji 2.1.0 software (USA).

For Oil Red O staining, frozen liver sections were cut into 10 μm and then stained with Oil Red O as described previously. The images were captured from four random fields per slide, and quantitative assessment of the positively stained lipid areas with Oil Red O was conducted using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (USA).

Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were cut into 5 μm pieces for immunohistochemistry, and then, they were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and antigen-retrieve. Tissue sections were incubated with endogenous peroxidase blocking solution and nonspecific staining blocking solution (MXB, Fuzhou, China), followed by incubation with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following a phosphate buffered saline (PBS) wash, tissue slices were incubated with goat antirabbit IgG conjugated with HRP and streptavidin-peroxidase (MXB, Fuzhou, China). A 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining solution (ZSGB, Beijing, China) was then used for detection. The integrated optical density (IOD) of liver and adipose tissue sections was determined by averaging the values from four selected fields per slide, utilizing ImageJ Fiji 2.1.0 software (USA).

Biochemical Measurements

Serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were assessed with a fully automated biochemical analyzer (HITACHI, Japan). The triglyceride (TG) content in both hepatic tissue and serum was quantified using a triglyceride determination kit for tissue and liquid samples (Applygen Technology, Beijing, China). The hepatic TG levels were normalized against the protein content, which was determined with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

RNA Extraction and Real-Time qPCR

Tissues were processed to extract total RNA using the Trizol reagent at a ratio of 1 mL per 20–30 mg of tissue (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan), followed by the conversion of RNA to cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). For a 20 μL reaction volume, 1 μg of RNA was combined with 16 μL of nuclease-free water and 4 μL of PrimeScript RT Master Mix. The resultant cDNA served as a template for preparing the reaction mixtures for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), which were composed of specific primers (Table S1) and PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Real-time qPCR was executed on a QuantStudio Q7 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The relative mRNA expression levels were quantified using the ΔΔCT method and were standardized to the expression of Gapdh.

Immunoblotting

A protease inhibitor combination was added to the RIPA lysis solution (Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Santa Clara, CA, USA) in order to separate tissue proteins. Following portioning, protein lysates were performed on 10% SDS-PAGE gels. Following resolution, the proteins were blotted onto PVDF membranes and blocked using either skim milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The primary antibodies (Table S2) were incubated with the immunoblots for a whole night at 4 °C. The membranes were then washed and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Lastly, the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) was used to view the target proteins, and ImageJ Fiji 2.1.0 (USA) was used to quantify them.

Statistics

All data are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. Normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were verified prior to analyses. Two-tailed unpaired t-tests were used for two-group comparisons. In order to distinguish the effects of prenatal PM2.5 exposure from the effects of the HFD, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess main effects and interactions between variables. Posthoc comparisons were conducted using a Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to determine pairwise group differences. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

PM2.5 Concentration and Characterization

Figure A illustrates the daily PM2.5 levels throughout the exposure duration. The mean daily PM2.5 concentrations recorded in the FA chamber and PM2.5 chamber were 1.4 ± 0.48 and 91.03 ± 8.70 μg/m3, respectively (Figure B). During the exposure phase, the predominant water-soluble inorganic ions detected in PM2.5 were SO4 2–. Among the metallic elements, Aluminum (Al), Manganese (Mn), and Lead (Pb) were most prevalent, while Arsenic (As) and Selenium (Se) were the dominant nonmetallic elements. For organic compounds, benzo­[b]­fluoranthene was found to be the most concentrated.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Metabolic Disorder in HFD-fed Offspring

Pregnant mice’s body weight did not differ between the FA and PM groups during the exposure period (Figure S1A). Despite a slight decrease in body weight gain observed following 18-day exposure to PM2.5, this change did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.0763) (Figure S1B). The following parameters were evaluated depending on the prenatal treatment in order to investigate the long-term effects of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on the growth trajectory of offspring: FA-CD vs PM-CD and FA-HFD vsPM-HFD. From 5 weeks of age, the prenatal PM2.5 exposed offspring showed a significant increase in body weight (Figure A) and body weight gain (Figure B) until they were fed with HFD. The offspring on an HFD displayed a time-dependent rise in body weight, and the prenatal PM2.5 exposed offspring showed an even more significant increase in body weight (Figure A). Moreover, the PM-HFD offspring exhibited an increasing trend in body weight gain compared to FA-HFD offspring after 13 weeks of HFD feeding (P = 0.0951) (Figure C). There was no variation in the food consumption of offspring from the other groups (Figure S2A). On CD, prenatal PM2.5 exposure significantly increased the fat mass (Figure D) and the ratio of fat mass to body weight (Figure E), whereas it reduced the ratio of lean mass to body weight (Figure G) without affecting the lean mass (Figure F). On HFD, prenatal PM2.5 exposure increased the lean mass (Figure F) without influencing the fat mass (Figure D). Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not impact the whole-body energy expenditure in either CD-fed or HFD-fed offspring, including VO2 consumption (Figure S3A), VCO2 production (Figure S3B), respiratory exchange ratio (Figure S3C), and heat production (Figure S3D). It is worth noting that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated HFD-inhibited insulin sensitivity (Figure J-K) in offspring, although no difference was observed in glucose tolerance (Figure H–I) between the FA-HFD and PM-HFD groups. These findings indicated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure exacerbated HFD-induced increases in body weight and insulin resistance in offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Further Inhibited the Insulin Signaling Pathway in HFD-Fed Offspring

Due to the above-mentioned observation that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated systemic insulin resistance in HFD-fed offspring, we further examined the insulin signaling pathway in the liver and adipose tissue, the major insulin target organs. A distinct reduction in the level of phosphorylation of IRS at Ser307 (but not Ser1101) was noted in the liver of CD-fed offspring after prenatal PM2.5 exposure (Figure A,B). However, no difference in the phosphorylation of IRS at Ser307 was observed in the livers of offspring between FA-HFD and PM-HFD groups (Figure A,B). Additionally, prenatal PM2.5 exposure further repressed the phosphorylation of IRS at Ser1101 in the liver of HFD-fed offspring (Figure A,B). AKT, downstream of IRS, plays a vital role in insulin-signaling-mediated glucose uptake. The phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 was decreased in the liver of PM-CD offspring compared to FA-CD offspring (Figure A,B). In addition, the HFD induced a trend toward reduction in phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 in the liver of offspring (P = 0.0910), which was further repressed by prenatal exposure to PM2.5 (Figure A,B). Corresponding to the liver observations, prenatal PM2.5 exposure significantly decreased the phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 in both eWAT (Figure C,E) and BAT (Figure D,F) of CD-fed offspring, which was further repressed by HFD feeding. These results demonstrated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated the HFD-inhibited insulin signaling pathway in the liver and adipose tissue of offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Hepatic Lipid Deposition and Hepatic Dysfunction in HFD-Fed Offspring

To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of how prenatal PM2.5 exposure affects HFD-induced fatty livers in offspring, we measured liver morphology and hepatic TG levels. The PM-HFD offspring exhibited a significant increase in liver mass (Figure A) and a slight elevation in liver organ coefficient (P = 0.0605) (Figure B) when compared to FA-HFD offspring, while no discernible difference was found between the FA-CD and PM-CD groups (Figure A,B). Morphological staining showed that prenatal PM2.5 exposure did not increase the area of vacuoles (Figure C,D) and lipid accumulation (Figure E,F) in the liver of CD-fed offspring. H&E staining revealed an increased number of vacuoles in liver sections of the FA-HFD offspring, with approximately 40% steatosis, whereas the PM-HFD offspring exhibited marked macrovesicular steatosis involving more than 70% of hepatocytes (Figure C,D). Oil Red O staining indicated that the PM-HFD offspring had liver sections with fused lipid droplets, indicating significant lipid accumulation, whereas the lipid droplets in FA-HFD were smaller and more dispersed (Figure E,F). Correspondingly, after HFD feeding, prenatal PM2.5-exposed offspring showed a substantial rise in hepatic TG levels (Figure H), whereas serum TG levels (Figure G) did not significantly change. To examine if prenatal PM2.5 exposure regulates hepatic function in response to HFD feeding, we measured serum levels of ALT and AST. The PM-HFD offspring displayed significantly upregulated ALT levels in serum (Figure I) compared to FA-HFD offspring, while AST levels (Figure J) remained unchanged. Altogether, our current results suggested that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated hepatic lipid deposition and hepatic dysfunction in HFD-fed offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Hepatic Lipid Metabolism Disorder, Inflammation, and Senescence in HFD-Fed Offspring

To determine the impact of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on hepatic lipid metabolism, we examined the expression of critical genes involved in lipid metabolism in the liver of offspring. It is well-known that fatty acid binding protein 1 (FABP1) facilitates the transport of fatty acids and other hydrophobic molecules in the liver. Prenatal PM2.5 exposure markedly reduced Fabp1 mRNA expression in CD-fed offspring, while it increased Fabp1 mRNA levels in HFD-fed offspring (Figure A). This was confirmed by protein examination with Western blot analysis (Figure B,C). Figure A and Figure S4A showed that prenatal PM2.5 exposure upregulated the mRNA expression of enzymes involved in lipogenesis (sterol regulatory element binding protein 1 (Srebp1)) and inhibited the mRNA expression of lipid export-related enzymes (apolipoproteins B (Apob)) in the liver of CD-fed offspring. In addition, CD-fed offspring showed an increase in the protein expression of fatty acid synthase (FAS) (Figure B,C) and a decrease in the protein expression of recombinant peroxisome proliferator activated receptor alpha (PPARα) by prenatal PM2.5 exposure (Figure S4B,C). Under HFD conditions, we noticed that PM-HFD offspring displayed the markedly upregulated phosphorylation of hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) at Ser660 and the protein expression of acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1) in the liver compared to that of FA-HFD offspring (Figure B,C). Thus, prenatal PM2.5 exposure promoted fatty acid intake, lipolysis, and fatty acid β-oxidation in the liver of HFD-fed offspring.

The imbalance of hepatic homeostasis caused by inflammatory stress is a significant molecular mechanism for the development of MAFLD. The mRNA (Figure D) and protein expression (Figure E,F) of inflammatory factors in CD-fed offspring were unaffected by prenatal exposure to PM2.5. Figure D showed that the increased mRNA levels of inflammation-related genes [tumor necrosis factor alpha (Tnfα), C C motif ligand 2 (Ccl2), and nuclear factor kappa b (Nfκb)] caused by HFD were further elevated by prenatal PM2.5 exposure. Moreover, the protein expression of interleukin 1 beta (IL1β) and TNFα (P = 0.0875) in PM-HFD offspring was upregulated to some extent compared to the FA-HFD offspring (Figure E,F).

Marked upregulation of intracellular inflammatory signaling pathways is a hallmark of hepatic senescence. We examined the impact of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on hepatic senescence by measuring the expression of β-Gal, which is a marker of cellular senescence. Immunohistochemistry showed that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not lead to an increase in the expression of β-Gal in CD-fed offspring, whereas PM-HFD offspring exhibited a significant elevation in β-Gal expression compared to that of FA-HFD offspring (Figure G-H). This result was confirmed using Western blot analysis (Figure J,K). Due to the fact that cell cycle arrest plays a significant role among the mechanisms that induce cell senescence, we examined the expression of p16, p21, and p53, the key regulators for the cell cycle, in the liver of offspring. Figure I–K demonstrated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure did not increase the expression of these cell cycle regulators at the transcription and translation levels in the livers of CD-fed offspring. The upregulated mRNA expression of p21 was observed in PM-HFD offspring compared to FA-HFD offspring, whereas there was no significant difference in the mRNA expression of p16 and p53 (Figure I). Moreover, prenatal PM2.5 exposure led to a significant increase in the protein levels of p21 and p53 in HFD-fed offspring (Figure J,K).

Taken together, these findings showed that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated hepatic lipid metabolism disorder, inflammation, and senescence in HFD-fed offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Adipocyte Hypertrophy and Adipokine Dysregulation in eWAT of HFD-Fed Offspring

The eWAT acts as a vital organ for energy storage and is closely linked to metabolic disorders, so we assessed the effects of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on the eWAT of offspring. There was no change in the eWAT mass (Figure A) and organ coefficient (Figure B) of CD-fed offspring after prenatal PM2.5 exposure, whereas prenatal exposure to PM2.5 caused a trend toward an increase in the organ coefficient of eWAT (P = 0.0613) in HFD-fed offspring (Figure B). To determine the morphology of the adipocytes, we performed H&E staining and found that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 significantly increased the size of adipocytes in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring (1.6 times that of the FA-HFD offspring) but not CD-fed offspring, suggesting that the prenatal PM2.5 exposed-offspring mice were prone to develop adipose hypertrophy after HFD feeding (Figure C,D). To further delineate the effect of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on adipokines, we examined mRNA levels of Adiponectin, Resistin, and Leptin in eWAT of offspring. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not alter the expression of any gene in CD-fed offspring or Adiponectin in HFD-fed offspring (Figure E-G). The reduction in the mRNA expression of Resistin induced by the HFD was further decreased by prenatal PM2.5 exposure (Figure F), whereas the mRNA expression of Leptin in eWAT was upregulated in the PM-HFD offspring compared to FA-HFD offspring (Figure G). Collectively, our findings indicated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated adipose hypertrophy and adipokine dysregulation in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Lipid Metabolism Disorder, Inflammation, and Senescence in eWAT of HFD-Fed Offspring

Next, we explored whether prenatal exposure to PM2.5 could disrupt the lipid homeostasis in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring. Figure A-C shows that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not alter the mRNA or protein expression of lipid metabolism-related molecules in the CD-fed offspring. Contrasting with the unchanged mRNA expression of lipolysis enzymes (Atgl, Hsl) in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring (Figure S5A-C), the PM-HFD offspring displayed a significant upregulation of mRNA expression for the rate-limiting lipogenesis enzymes (Fas, Srebp1) compared to the FA-HFD offspring (Figure A). Regarding fatty acid β-oxidation enzymes, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 induced a trend toward an increase in the mRNA level of Acox1 in the eWAT of PM-HFD offspring compared to the other group (P = 0.0885) but did not affect the mRNA level of Cpt1α (Figure A). In comparison to HFD-fed offspring, offspring subjected to PM-HFD exhibited a significant elevation in the protein expression of FABP4, which is secreted by adipocytes and the key component for fatty acid intake (Figure B,C). However, the protein expression of FAS was reduced in the prenatal PM2.5 exposed-offspring mice after HFD feeding, while prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not cause changes in the protein expression of SCD1 (Figure B,C). In HFD-fed offspring, prenatal PM2.5 exposure further significantly increased the protein expression of ACOX1 induced by HFD, with no observed changes in the level of CPT1α (Figure B,C). PPARs serve as essential regulators of the lipid metabolic process. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 significantly upregulated the protein levels of PPARα and PPARγ induced by HFD (Figure S6B,C).

FABP4 has been shown to act at the interface of metabolic and inflammatory pathways. To investigate the effects of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on inflammation in eWAT, we examined the level of the inflammation-related genes. There was no significant change in the mRNA (Figure D) or protein expression (Figure E,F) of inflammation-related markers in CD-fed offspring following prenatal PM2.5 exposure. In HFD-fed offspring, prenatal PM2.5 exposure further significantly increased the mRNA expression of IL6, Tnfα, and Nfκb induced by the HFD (Figure D). Consistent with this, Western blot examination showed upregulation of IL6, IL1β, and TNFα expression in PM-HFD offspring compared to FA-HFD offspring (Figure E,F).

Given the profound association of pro-inflammatory factors with senescence, we analyzed the expression of the senescence-related molecules in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring. Immunohistochemical staining for β-Gal revealed a significant increase in β-Gal expression in the PM-HFD offspring, with a 1.2-fold increase compared with the FA-HFD offspring (Figure G,H). This was further confirmed by the observed increase in the level of protein expression of β-Gal (Figure J,K). Consistently, prenatal PM2.5 exposure increased the mRNA level of p16 and p21 (Figure I) and elevated the protein level of p21 (Figure J,K) in HFD-fed offspring, suggesting that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 could exacerbate HFD-induced senescence.

In summary, these findings indicated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated lipid metabolism disorder, inflammation, and senescence in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Adipocyte Hypertrophy in BAT of HFD-Fed Offspring

Indeed, BAT has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of metabolic disorders. In CD-fed offspring, prenatal PM2.5 exposure increased the mass of BAT (Figure A) and induced a trend toward increase in the organ coefficient of BAT (P = 0.0613) (Figure B). On the contrary, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 caused a trend toward decrease in the organ coefficient of BAT (P = 0.0950) (Figure B) in HFD-fed offspring, albeit no significant decrease in the organ mass (Figure A). The changes in the organ mass and organ coefficient of BAT induced by prenatal PM2.5 exposure in CD-fed offspring were accompanied by increased adipocyte size, indicating hypertrophy in PM-CD offspring compared to FA-CD offspring (Figure C,D). H&E staining revealed that the adipocytes in PM-HFD offspring exhibited a hypertrophic morphology, approximately doubling in size compared to those in the FA-HFD offspring (Figure C,D). Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not cause any changes in mRNA expression levels of Adiponectin (Figure E), Resistin (Figure F), and Leptin (Figure G) in either CD-fed or HFD-fed offspring. These findings revealed that prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated adipocyte hypertrophy in BAT of HFD-fed offspring.

Prenatal PM2.5 Exposure Aggravated Inflammation and Cell Cycle Arrest in BAT of HFD-Fed Offspring

BAT is the critical thermogenic tissue that regulates energy expenditure. To assess the impact of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on the BAT thermogenic capacity, the expression of thermogenic genes was measured. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 did not alter the mRNA (Figure A) or protein expression (Figure B,C) of thermogenic molecules in CD-fed offspring. Additionally, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 further enhanced the mRNA levels of uncoupling protein 1 (Ucp1) and recombinant deiodinase iodothyronine type II (Dio2) in HFD-fed offspring, while no significant alteration in the mRNA expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (Pgc1α) was observed after prenatal PM2.5 exposure (Figure A). However, the immunoblotting analysis revealed that there was no change in the protein expression of UCP1 or PGC1α for prenatal PM2.5 exposure (Figure B,C). These results indicated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure induced no further impairment of the thermogenic capacity of BAT in HFD-fed offspring.

Recently, the function of acute inflammation in the remodeling and expansion of adipose tissue has been clarified. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 increased the mRNA and protein expression of IL1β in CD-fed offspring while concurrently causing a decrease in the protein level of IL6 and TNFα (Figure D–F). Figure D illustrates significant upregulation of the mRNA levels of IL6, IL1β, and Nfκb in BAT of the prenatal PM2.5 exposed offspring after HFD feeding. Consistent with this, the protein expression of IL6, IL1β, and TNFα was significantly increased in PM-HFD offspring compared with FA-HFD offspring as well (Figure E,F). Therefore, prenatal PM2.5 exposure aggravated inflammation in BAT of HFD-fed offspring.

Chronic inflammation is associated with accelerated aging. To investigate whether prenatal PM2.5 exposure induced senescence in BAT of HFD-fed offspring, we examined and found that the offspring in the PM-HFD group did not exhibit a higher distribution of β-Gal than offspring in the FA-HFD group (Figure G,H). Correspondingly, there was no observed elevation in the protein expression of β-Gal in the PM-HFD offspring (Figure J,K), suggesting that prenatal PM2.5 exposure did not lead to an apparent increase in cellular senescence in BAT of HFD-fed offspring. Further analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of senescence-related genes, p21 and p53, were obviously upregulated only by HFD feeding (Figure I), whereas their protein expression was increased in the prenatal PM2.5 exposed-offspring after HFD feeding (Figure J,K). Thus, these findings suggested that prenatal PM2.5 exposure might cause cell cycle arrest in BAT of HFD-fed offspring.

Discussion

This research examined the impact of prenatal PM2.5 exposure on the development of MS in response to the second hit of the HFD in adult offspring and further highlighted the importance of early life exposure to environmental stressors in the development and progression of metabolic diseases. The main findings included the following: (1) prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated HFD-induced metabolic disorder, including increase in body weight, both systemic and local insulin resistance; (2) prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated HFD-induced hepatic dysfunction, hepatic lipid deposition, and adipose hypertrophy in both eWAT and BAT, accompanied by lipid metabolism disorder in the liver and eWAT; (3) the underlying mechanism may involve that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 exaggerated HFD-induced cellular senescence in both hepatic and eWAT, as well as cell cycle arrest in BAT, which ultimately leading to the activation of chronic inflammation in offspring. The current findings delineated the complex and multifaceted effects of prenatal exposure to PM2.5 on the programming of MS in adult offspring in response to the subsequent challenge of a HFD. These observations not only uncovered the susceptibility of offspring to metabolic disruptions but also illuminated the potential mechanisms by which combined exposure to multiple stressors, such as environmental pollutants and dietary factors, regulate the ontogeny of metabolic diseases. This research provides critical insights into the early life determinants of metabolic health and the interplay between environmental exposures and dietary challenges in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders.

Maternal exposure to PM2.5 during pregnancy has been correlated with numerous adverse outcomes in offspring, including low birth weight, neurodevelopmental toxicity, , and an increased risk of overweight or obesity in childhood. Studies have shown that intrauterine exposure to environmental air pollutants was associated with postnatal weight gain in Hispanic infants from 1 to 6 months of age, and PM2.5 exposure during pregnancy might increase the risk of abdominal obesity in children at 6 years of age. In this study, it was found that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 led to an increase in the body weight and body weight gain of the offspring from the age of 5 to 13 weeks. In CD-fed offspring, prenatal PM2.5 exposure significantly elevated the fat mass without affecting the overall body weight. Additionally, while prenatal PM2.5 exposure inhibited the insulin signaling pathway, it did not result in insulin resistance in CD-fed offspring. Our investigation did not observe adiposity or insulin resistance in PM-CD offspring, despite previous research demonstrating a higher body weight and glucose intolerance in prenatal PM2.5-exposed offspring. Given the similarity in PM2.5 concentrations within this study, the noted difference is likely due to the varied exposure periods, with exposure occurring only during pregnancy in this study, whereas in the other study, exposure extended from preconception through pregnancy and into the lactation period. This discrepancy indicates that the effects of maternal PM2.5 exposure on metabolism in the offspring may be dependent on exposure periods, and further studies are required to understand the implications for health outcomes in offspring. Study has demonstrated that chronic exposure to SO4 2 – significantly increases the risk of metabolic syndrome, including central obesity, hypertension, and dysglycemia. , Consistently, our compositional analysis of PM2.5 during the exposure period identified SO4 2 – as the most abundant ionic species. However, the specific bioactive constituents responsible for driving these metabolic effects remain to be fully elucidated.

The intricate relationship between maternal influences and postnatal dietary challenges, particularly the impact of high-fat diet, is a main contributing factor in the development of MS. Exposure to metformin during pregnancy resulted in enhanced body weight gain, impaired glucose tolerance, and higher fasting glucose levels in the mouse offspring during the HFD. Moreover, maternal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure reinforced dyslipidemia in response to HFD in adult offspring of mice. In a murine model, the histological markers of fatty liver disease in mature male progeny were notably elevated following prenatal exposure to gutkhaa commonly utilized smokeless tobacco preparationand HFD during their adult life. Additionally, prenatal ethanol exposure increased susceptibility to HFD-induced NAFLD in female mouse offspring. The evidence collectively indicated that prenatal exposure to adverse factors may exacerbate metabolic disorders in HFD-fed offspring. In our study, HFD was administered to prenatal PM2.5 exposed offspring for 13 weeks to simulate the “second hit” after birth, given the potential risk factors for MS posed by both PM2.5 and HFD. Prenatal exposure to diesel exhaust particles exacerbated the increase of body weight and the susceptibility to insulin resistance induced by HFD in offspring. Prenatal PM2.5 exposure induced glucose intolerance and insulin resistance in HFD feeding offspring. Consistently, this study demonstrated that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated the HFD-induced increase in body weight and insulin resistance. We showed that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated HFD-induced increase in body weight and insulin resistance. The study confirmed that both prenatal PM2.5 exposure and postnatal HFD induction were potential risk factors for MS, with prenatal PM2.5 exposure exacerbating the vulnerability to MS in offspring that were fed on a HFD, indicating a pronounced synergic action between early life environmental exposure and dietary challenge. Our study draws attention to the increased susceptibility of individuals to metabolic diseases following early life exposure to air pollution, particularly when such exposure is followed by a high-fat dietary tendency.

The liver and adipose tissue are major metabolic organs responsible for the regulation of whole-body metabolism, and therefore, they are highly involved in the pathogenesis of MS. Further reduction of phosphorylation of AKT, a well-implicated marker of insulin resistance, was observed by the second-hit of HFD challenge in the liver and adipose tissue of prenatal PM2.5-exposed offspring, accompanied by accelerated hepatic lipid deposition and dysfunction, as well as adipose hypertrophy in eWAT and BAT. Consistent with our findings, previous research has reported a significant reduction in AKT phosphorylation levels, insulin resistance, and hepatosteatosis, as well as an increase in subcutaneous fat pad weight, in the livers of HFD-fed offspring from PM2.5-exposed mothers. It is noteworthy that FABPs, which are versatile proteins known to modulate lipid fluxes, trafficking, signaling, and metabolism, may play a critical role in this context. Earlier research demonstrated that FABP1–/– mice were resistant to the development of obesity and fatty liver disease caused by HFD. In addition, interference against FABP4 to white adipocytes ameliorated obesity and insulin resistance. The enhanced levels of FABP1 in the liver and FABP4 in eWAT of HFD-fed offspring in the present study may contribute to the elevated liver TG and adipocyte hypertrophy in PM-HFD offspring by accelerating fatty acids uptake. Lipid homeostasis is reflected by a balance between lipid production and decomposition. Unexpectedly, we uncovered that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 led to an increase in hepatic lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation, concurrently inhibiting lipid synthesis and promoting fatty acid oxidation in the eWAT of HFD-fed offspring. In line with the current findings, Camilla et al. noted that overfeeding activated homeostatic compensatory mechanisms that prevented weight gain. However, these compensatory mechanisms might be insufficient to counterbalance the lipid metabolism disturbances induced by prenatal PM2.5 exposure.

A distinctive aspect of the research is that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated HFD-triggered cellular senescence in both hepatic and eWAT of offspring as well as cell cycle arrest in BAT of offspring. Consistently, prenatal PM2.5 exposure resulted in the senescence of bone marrow cells in offspring mice, which triggered inflammation. Resveratrol has been demonstrated to reverse PM2.5-induced senescence and reduce inflammatory factor secretion in human dermal papilla cells. In obese mice induced by HFD, senescent cells accumulated in adipose tissue, leading to reduced tissue regeneration and functional impairment. Moreover, senolytic cocktail (dasatinib + quercetin) treatment can ameliorate HFD-induced obesity and insulin resistance. Senescent cells are strikingly characterized by a preponderance of pro-inflammatory molecules. Consistent with this, prenatal exposure to PM2.5 aggravated inflammation in the liver, eWAT, and BAT of HFD-fed offspring, as evidenced by the increased expression of pro-inflammatory molecules. In NAFLD, Kupffer cells (KCs) not only serve as a significant source of cytokines and chemokines, including TNFα, IL1β, and CCL2 but also serve a crucial function in regulating lipid metabolism in hepatocytes, thereby contributing to the accumulation of TG within hepatocytes. Importantly, the use of neutralizing antibodies against TNFα has been shown to mitigate KC-induced liver injury. Additionally, Sadler et al. demonstrated that the administering of low-dose LPS results in adipocyte hyperplasia. Moreover, while pro-inflammatory mediators secreted by immune cells and adipocytes are known to suppress thermogenesis activation in BAT, our model demonstrated that prenatal PM2.5 exposure did not impair the thermogenic capacity of BAT in HFD-fed offspring, aligning with another study showing that maternal exposure to PM2.5 did not reduce BAT whitening. Taken together, cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence are significant occurrences in prenatal PM2.5 exposure-aggravated dysfunction of the liver, eWAT, and BAT in HFD-fed offspring, which may be the critical trigger of the insulin resistance, fatty liver, and adipocyte hypertrophy in eWAT and BAT of the HFD-fed offspring above.

The significant advantage of the study is that we highlighted the second-hit effect of the HFD secondary to prenatal PM2.5 exposure. Our findings advocate for a multifaceted approach that combines reducing prenatal PM2.5 exposure, postnatal dietary intervention, and targeted senolytic therapies to mitigate the global burden of MS resulting from the “double hit” of prenatal PM2.5 exposure and HFD in adulthood. Nevertheless, this study has a few limitations. First, while we analyzed PM2.5 composition (water-soluble ions, metals, organic compounds), the specific bioactive components driving metabolic effects remain unidentified. Second, using only male offspring precludes examination of sex-specific responses. Third, although we identified cellular senescence and inflammation involvement, the precise molecular pathways require further elucidation. These aspects represent key directions for future research on pollutant-induced metabolic programming.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results suggest that prenatal PM2.5 exposure initially aggravates HFD-induced cellular senescence or cell cycle arrest in the metabolic organs of adult offspring, priming the initiation of local inflammatory responses that further worsen metabolic dysfunction. These findings aroused attention to avoid adverse environmental exposure during the full life cycle (particularly during pregnancy), as well as target cellular senescence-mediated inflammation to mitigate the development of fetal-originated MS. This research underscores the critical role of prenatal air pollution exposure in programming metabolic dysfunction, highlighting the need for targeted interventions and policies to protect offspring health.
