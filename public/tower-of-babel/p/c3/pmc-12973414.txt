Effect of Partial Substitution of Refined Wheat Flour with Tubtim Chumphae Rice Flour on the Physicochemical Properties and Quality of Pasta
Authors: Natthaporn Subanmanee, Narissara Uthai, Racharat Yampuang, Rungtip Wongtom, Jutarat Wattanakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pasta, a globally appreciated staple food, is an excellent source of carbohydrates. Nevertheless, wheat-based pasta contains certain nutrients. Thus, a new product with enhanced nutritional value is required to overcome this nutritional limitation and enhance the health-related advantages of pasta. A feasible strategy is to partially replace wheat flour with Tubtim Chumphae rice (TCR) flour. This study aimed to manufacture tagliatelle pasta using a formulation wherein wheat flour is replaced by 0%, 15%, 30%, 45%, and 60% TCR flour. The microstructure, color, texture, total phenol and anthocyanin levels, antioxidant activity, cooking properties, and proximate composition profiles of the product, along with its sensory attributes, were evaluated. Sample analyses indicated that TCR incorporation elevated the levels of fiber, proteins, lipids, and ash content in comparison to pasta devoid of TCR flour. Furthermore, the yellowness and brightness diminished significantly compared to the control. In TCR-fortified pasta, the cooking time and yield were reduced, but cooking loss increased. However, the concentrations of total phenolics and anthocyanins, as well as antioxidant activity, were also markedly elevated. In addition, the pasta displayed an evidently undesirable texture, including surface roughness, particularly when TCR levels were more than 45%. These findings indicate that replacing wheat flour with TCR flour enhanced the functional and nutritional value of pasta, making TCR flour a feasible functional food ingredient.

INTRODUCTION

Traditional wheat-based pasta is a staple food product usually prepared using a mixture of wheat, eggs, and water. It is favored by consumers mainly due to its quick and simple preparation method (Boroski et al., 2011). It is distinguished by its elevated carbohydrate and low vitamin content; nevertheless, its nutritional and health benefits can be enhanced by the incorporation of various ingredients (Boroski et al., 2011; Sereewat et al., 2015; Betrouche et al., 2022). It possesses remarkable potential as an ideal functional food when supplemented with nutritious ingredients, including amaranth, buckwheat, corn, quinoa, rice, soybean, and sweet potato (Marengo et al., 2018; Sujka et al., 2022; Thanushree et al., 2022; Uthai et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2023). Thailand usually imports wheat to meet the significant domestic demands for baked products, noodles, and pasta, which increases costs. It cannot be cultivated locally due to unfavorable climate conditions and poor seed development, unlike rice, which is a cash crop and is exported. Therefore, using rice as the main ingredient for pasta is an excellent option that can help to reduce wheat flour imports. In addition, the prevalent concerns about health problems lead consumers to opt for more nutritious products, especially pasta with pigmented rice as the main ingredient. The pigmented varieties of rice, including black, purple, and red rice, are superior sources of fibers, minerals, phytochemicals (polyphenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and tocopherols), and antioxidant activity compared to white rice (Bae et al., 2017; Iqbal et al., 2023). Prior research on the supplementation of wheat-based products with pigment rices (black, red, or purple) has predominantly investigated its impacts on product quality. These include cookies formulated (Yee et al., 2024) and bread enriched (Iqbal et al., 2023) with red rice flour; noodles made with berry rice flour (Sirichokworrakit et al., 2015; Thongkaew and Singthong, 2020); spaghetti composed of rice flour and defatted soy flour (Sereewat et al., 2015); and brown rice pasta (Wang et al., 2016), Thai Jasmine rice pasta (Phongthai et al., 2017), and pasta enriched with black rice bran (Sethi et al., 2020). However, the research on the impacts of rice flour on the attributes of cereal-based pasta has been limited. Tubtim Chumphae rice (TCR) flour serves as a vital ingredient for fortifying pasta. TCR cultivar (RD69) is a Thai red rice cultivar that is a cross between Jasmine rice (Hom Mali rice) and Sung Yod Phatthalung rice varieties (Kammapana, 2023). It comprises a substantial quantity of phenolics, anthocyanins, flavonoids, and vitamin E (Rungruang et al., 2022; Kammapana, 2023). Additionally, it inhibits cancer cell growth; exhibits marked radical scavenging capability, as shown by 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid), and ferric reducing antioxidant power assays (Rungruang et al., 2022); and improves blood vessel relaxation (Taratima et al., 2023). Thus, the formulation of pasta products with pigmented rice flour represents a trend toward the transformation of conventional foods into functional foods, underscoring its potential for application in the food industry. However, the development of pasta enriched with TCR flour has not yet been explored. This study aimed to examine the impact of partially substituting wheat flour with TCR flour on the physicochemical attributes and qualities of pasta.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

TCR was acquired from the Khon Kaen Rice Research Center, Khon Kaen province, Thailand. The seeds were ground using an HR-06B multifunction disintegrator (Horus) and subsequently sieved to obtain flour with a particle size of less than 180 μm. The flour was preserved in a vacuum-sealed aluminum pouch and stored in a dry place at 25°C for further analysis. The ingredients of pasta, including wheat flour (United Flour Mill), salt (Prungthip), and pasteurized egg (CP), were acquired from a local market in Bangkok, Thailand.

Fresh pasta preparation

As control, fresh pasta was prepared with the following formulation: refined wheat flour (280 g), pasteurized egg (50 g), salt (4 g), and drinking water (110 g). For the test pasta samples, at 15%, 30%, 45%, and 60% (w/w) of refined wheat flour in the control formulation was replaced with the TCR flour. The dough was stirred for 3 min with a 5KSM60SPXTER stand mixer (KitchenAid), rolled to a thickness of 2 mm, and laminated into a tagliatelle form with a 04GD187 pasta rolling machine (Pedrini). The fresh pasta was cooked in boiling water for 2 min and then plunged into cold water for 2 min. The excess water was then eliminated. The fresh pasta was physiochemically analyzed and characterized 1 h after production.

Pasta color

The color of the uncooked and cooked pasta samples was assessed utilizing a CR-400 chroma meter (Konica Minolta) in accordance with the CIE L* a* b* (CIELAB) color model. The color of each pasta sample prepared with a different TCR composition was measured in five replicates. The hue angle (h°) and chroma (C*) values of the samples were determined using equations 1 and 2.

Cooking quality of pasta

Optimum cooking time: The optimum cooking time for each pasta sample was evaluated as previously indicated by Uthai (2021). Briefly, 10 g of pasta was placed into 100 mL of hot water maintained at 100°C. The pasta was subsequently randomized and squeezed between two glass plates every 10 s. The optimum cooking time was determined as the time point at which the white core of the pasta is no longer visible. All analyses were conducted in triplicate.

Cooking yield: The cooking yield was determined utilizing the technique previously proposed by Uthai (2021) with some modifications. Briefly, 10 g of the pasta sample was immersed in 200 mL of boiling water and heated for 5 min. Subsequently, the water was drained, and the sample was set aside for 15 min. Then, the sample was weighed, and the cooking yield was calculated using equation 3. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Cooking loss: The cooking loss of pasta was assessed by quantifying the residual liquid collected after boiling pasta, following the methodology established previously by Uthai et al. (2022). Briefly, 10 g of pasta was placed into 200 mL of boiling water in a beaker and cooked for a preset cooking time. Then, the sample was allowed to cool down to 25°C for 15 min and placed in a hot air oven set at 105°C until a consistent weight was achieved. The cooking loss was estimated using equation 4. All analyses were conducted in triplicate.

Texture determination

The tensile strength, elasticity, and firmness of the uncooked and boiled pasta samples were measured using a TA-XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems) as outlined previously by Uthai and Chetyakamin (2020). A 5 kg load cell was used to calibrate the instrument. The test employed an A/SPR probe (for assessing tensile strength and elasticity), a 5-kg load cell, a pre-test velocity of 3 mm/s, a test velocity of 3 mm/s, a post-test velocity of 5 mm/s, and an initial probe distance of 100 mm. To evaluate the firmness of pasta, the A/LKB probe was used with the following conditions: a pre-test velocity of 10 mm/s, a test velocity of 10 mm/s, a post-test velocity of 10 mm/s, a return to start distance of 90%, and a trigger type of 10 g. Each treatment was performed in ten replications.

Morphological analysis

The morphology of pasta was assessed with a JSM-6610LV scanning electron microscope (SEM). Each pasta sample was cut into 0.5×0.5×1.5 cm pieces. The pieces were then coated with a thin layer of gold and observed at magnifications of 75×, 500×, and 1,000×, with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

Proximate analysis

The proximate compositions of the TCR flour and the pasta prepared using it were determined per the method established previously by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2023). The moisture content was evaluated by drying the samples to a constant weight in a UF110 hot air oven (Memmert). Crude fat was extracted with petroleum ether using an E-500 Soxhlet extractor (Büchi) and then measured. The nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 981.10) with a K-439 speed digester (Büchi) and a K-360 KjekFlex (Büchi). The protein level was calculated by applying a conversion factor of 5.95 for rice flour and 6.25 for pasta. The ash content was determined after burning the sample in a muffle furnace at 550°C until the ash was obtained. The residue was obtained after digesting the pasta sample with acid and alkali, and its crude fiber content was examined using a fully automated FibertecTM 8000 analyzer (Foss). Carbohydrate content and the energy value were calculated using equations 5 and 6.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC) and the DPPH assay for assessing the antioxidant capacity

Sample extraction: The methanolic extract of the pasta was obtained as per a protocol outlined previously by Wattanakul et al. (2024), with a slight modification. The samples of uncooked and cooked pasta (2 g each) were added with 10 mL of 80% methanol and extracted by agitating for 2 h at 150 rpm on a GS-20 orbital shaker with a rotor (PP dealing). The extract was centrifuged at 1,750 g for 10 min (Jouan CR3i). The supernatant was subsequently analyzed for the TPC and for estimating DPPH radical scavenging activity (%).

Determination of TPC: The TPC was quantified with a slight modification of the technique described previously by Wattanakul et al. (2024). Briefly, 1 mL of the methanolic extract was added to 0.2 mL of the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and subsequently vortexed for 3 min. Then, 2% (w/v) Na2CO3 solution was added to the mixture and incubated for 30 min at 25°C. OD760 was measured with a Genesys 10S UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The TPC was calculated based on a curve plotted using 0－0.05 mg/mL gallic acid (GA) standard, and was represented as mg of GA equivalent per 100 g of extract.

Determination of DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was estimated using the method described previously by Wattanakul et al. (2024) with some modifications. Briefly, 1 mL of the methanolic extract was added with 1 mL of 0.15 mM DPPH in 95% (v/v) ethanol and subsequently incubated at 25°C in the dark for 30 min. OD517 of the solution was assessed using a Genesys 10S UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The absorbance values were applied to equation 7, and the results were reported in terms of percentage DPPH radical scavenging activity.

Determination of total anthocyanin content (TAC)

Sample extraction: The samples were extracted using acidified methanol according to the procedure described previously by Colombo et al. (2023) with some modifications. Briefly, 0.5 g of the sample was suspended in 10 mL of a methanol:1 M HCl solution (85:15, v/v). The mixture was shaken at 150 rpm on a GS-20 orbital shaker with a rotor for 30 min at 25°C. It was centrifuged at 1,750 g (Jouan CR3i) for 10 min. The supernatant was used for TAC analysis.

TAC estimation: The TAC of each sample was determined using the method described previously by Colombo et al. (2023) with slight modifications. The supernatant was diluted with 1 mL of 0.025 M KCl (pH 1) and 1 mL of 0.4 M CH3COONa (pH 4.5). OD520 and OD700 of the diluted solution were measured using a Genesys 10S UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The absorbance values were applied to equation 8, TA was quantified, and expressed as equivalent of mg cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G)/g.

Where DA: (A520−A700) pH 1−(A520−A700), pH 4.5; MW: molecular weight of C3G (449.2 g/mol); DF: dilution factor; 1,000: conversion factor from g to mg; V: volume of extraction solution (L); e: molar extinction coefficient of C3G (26,900 m−1･cm−1); 1: path length in cm (1 cm); W: sample weight (g).

Sensory evaluation

For sensory assessments, 50 untrained panelists (25 females and 25 males, aged 22－60 years) were chosen from universities and companies, all of whom consumed pasta or noodles at least once a week. Each member received the five test samples after boiling for 10 min. The samples were served to the assessors in a random sequence, identified with a 3-digit number. The panelists evaluated the pasta for sensory attributes-color, odor, taste, elasticity, smoothness, and overall liking-using a 9-point hedonic scale. In this scale, the likeness values were described as follows: 9: extremely, 8: very much, 7: moderately, 6: slightly, 5: neither like nor dislike, 4: slightly dislike, 3: dislike moderately, 2: dislike very much, and 1: dislike extremely. Ethical permission for human involvement in this study was issued by the Naresuan University-Network of Research Ethics Committee (COE No. 0002/2023 and NREC No. 0019/2566). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment in this study. The research was conducted ethically in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were reported as mean±standard deviation. A completely randomized design was utilized to obtain the physicochemical data, whereas a randomized complete block design was employed to obtain the sensory assessment data. An analysis of variance was performed with the statistical analysis software SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp.). When the F-value was significant (P<0.05), Duncan’s New Multiple Range Test was employed to identify the statistically significant differences among the means.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Color characteristics of pasta

Color is the fundamental characteristic that captures a consumer’s attention, based on which the person evaluates the quality of a food product. The color of pasta is determined by the type of flour utilized for its production, as it is the primary ingredient (Fig. 1). Therefore, the color variations in the flours used for pasta manufacturing were apparent in both the uncooked and cooked pasta (Table 1). Pasta of high quality usually has a bright yellow appearance. However, its nutritional enrichment often leads to color changes due to the pigments present in the added ingredients (Sujka et al., 2022). In this study, substitution with 15% TCR flour markedly reduced the lightness (L*) of the pasta [68.47－71.53 (control) to 66.38－67.34 (test)]. Furthermore, L* most significantly reduced after substitution with 60% TCR flour (from 68.47 to 58.50 for uncooked and from 71.53 to 55.50 for cooked pasta, Table 1). The yellowness parameter (b*) also exhibited a similar trend: the maximum values of 9.76－13.34 were recorded for pasta with 60% TCR flour. TCR enrichment also caused a marked decline in yellowness from 17.40 (15% TCR) to 13.34 (60% TCR) for uncooked pasta and from 11.99 (15% TCR) to 9.76 (60% TCR) for cooked pasta. Our findings aligned with those of Thongkaew and Singthong (2020), who demonstrated that rice noodles prepared using rice flour fortified with riceberry flour exhibited greater a*, but lower L* and b* values compared to the noodles devoid of riceberry flour. Moreover, Sethi et al. (2020) discovered that incorporating black rice bran into pasta remarkably reduced its lightness and yellowness, but conversely, it intensified the redness relative to the pasta lacking black rice bran.

Consequently, an increase in TCR flour proportionally reduced the L* and b* values and, subsequently, the chroma (C*) value; however, it minimally affected the a* in comparison to the control. The maximum a* value was recorded for pasta made with 60% TCR flour (9.96 in uncooked and 9.14 in cooked pasta, while the minimum value was seen in the control (2.61 in uncooked and 0.45 in cooked pasta). These alterations in color resulted from differences in the anthocyanin pigment content of the TCR flour. Hue is an attribute closely linked to our perception of conventional colors, where 0° and 360°, 90°, 180°, and 270° are related to red, yellow, green, and blue, respectively (Delgado-Pando et al., 2025). Evidently, this parameter distinctly differentiated the sample containing TCR flour from the others, as noodles comprising 30%, 45%, and 60% TCR flour exhibited significantly lower values (approaching the red hue) in contrast to the remaining samples (all of which leaned towards the yellow hue). In addition, cooking also influenced the color parameters of pasta, including L*, a*, b*, hue, and chroma values, which decreased upon cooking (Table 1).

Cooking quality of pasta

The cooking qualities of pasta were assessed based on the optimal cooking time, cooking yield, and cooking loss that were affected by the TCR flour levels in the recipe (Table 2). A shorter optimal cooking time, low cooking loss, and high cooking yield are considered good qualities (Sereewat et al., 2015). The pure wheat-based pasta (0% TCR flour) had the longest optimum cooking time of 3.5 min compared to all other samples. This finding might be attributable to the secure integration of starch granules into the gluten matrix, as seen in the SEM images (Fig. 2). However, this duration reduced markedly with enhancing proportions of TCR flour (P<0.05, Table 2). This result might be attributed to the dilution of the gluten network, facilitating a more rapid entry of water into the pasta structure. Our findings aligned with those of Uthai et al. (2022), who revealed that the cooking time of noodles enriched with 5%－20% of purple sweet potato flour (PSPF) was longer than that of the noodles without PSPF. Cooking loss is a widely prevalent measure of food quality, with a lower value signifying superior quality. Such losses for all compositions ranged from 2.46% to 3.68%, but remained within the permitted limits for commercial pasta, i.e., not exceeding 10% (Phongthai et al., 2017; Delgado-Pando et al., 2025). An increase in the proportion of TCR flour as a substitute for wheat flour enhanced cooking loss in pasta (Table 2). Enrichment with different levels of TCR flour exhibited robust variations in cooking loss (P<0.05). The pasta made from wheat flour substituted with 15% TCR flour displayed the best resistance against structural disintegration with a lower cooking loss (2.91%) compared to the pasta made from wheat flour replaced with 15%－60% TCR flour, but higher than that of the control (2.46%). Similar optimum cooking time and cooking loss values were reported in noodles made from wheat flour supplemented with PSPF (Uthai and Chetyakamin, 2020) and with buckwheat hull (Sujka et al., 2022). Such results indicate that TCR flour forms a network that demonstrates increased solid loss after cooking. In addition, the pasta enriched with higher levels of TCR flour contained more fibers, which most likely contributed to a destabilization of the starch network, and may influence gluten network formation, thereby altering the protein-starch matrix, increasing cooking loss (Rungsardthong et al., 2021). A comparable trend was noted in pasta prepared using wheat flour supplemented with mango peel powder (0%－7.5%) contributed to an increase in cooking loss from 5.84% to 8.71% (Ajila et al., 2010). Furthermore, enhanced TCR flour proportion also contributed to a decline in cooking yield. This trend was also reported by Sun et al. (2019), who demonstrated a reduction of cooking yield in noodles made from flour fortified with 30% Chinese yam powder, which can be attributed to the dilution of gluten in the pasta, thereby suppressing its water retention. Thus, it can be concluded that adding more amounts of TCR flour altered the cooking quality and other characteristics of the pasta via the dilution of gluten.

Texture analysis

The pasta texture significantly influences consumer acceptance. Texture is primarily determined by the structural network of starches, glutens, and other biopolymers (Xing et al., 2023). The firmness and tensile strength of pasta post-cooking are critical criteria for evaluating its quality. In cooked pasta, these parameters markedly diminished with increasing proportion of added TCR flour (Table 3). This result was consistent with the findings of Xing et al. (2023). Thus, the replacement of wheat flour with TCR flour adversely affected the texture of pasta. A similar finding was reported by Uthai et al. (2022), who demonstrated that cooked noodles with added PSPF exhibited a lower tensile strength. Sereewat et al. (2015) also reported that spaghetti with added rice flour exhibited reduced tensile strength. Furthermore, the inclusion of TCR flour into pasta also reduced the gluten concentrations, compromising its structural integrity and enhancing susceptibility to tearing. Such disruption and degradation of the gluten network also diminish the textural qualities of pasta (Xing et al., 2023). Finally, the firmness of pasta substantially correlates with cooking loss. Heightened firmness may limit the expansion of starch granules, partially reducing cooking loss (Phongthai et al., 2017).

Microstructure of pasta

The microscopic structure of the pasta cross-sections is presented in Fig. 2. The gluten network of pasta devoid of TCR flour maintained a dense surface structure. In contrast, substantial amounts of TCR flour remarkably disrupted the gluten network of pasta, particularly evident by the noticeable cracks and large pores present within its gluten matrix. The extent of starch swelling at the outer periphery and structural alterations in the gluten network influence the anatomical integrity of the surface, consequently influencing surface roughness (Ohmura et al., 2023). The elevated cooking loss and diminished firmness in TCR-fortified pasta may be explained further by the SEM image insights. The internal structure of the TCR flour-enriched pasta exhibited a discontinuous surface and several fractures, enabling the leaching of starch, fibers, and other solid constituents during cooking, which resulted in a marked cooking loss and reduced firmness (Table 2 and 3). However, D’Amico et al. (2015) proposed that an uneven distribution of moisture may lead to micro-cracks in the structure and an uncontrollable shrinking of gluten-free pasta. Such observations were also reported by Kim et al. (2014), who noted obvious surface cracks in rice protein-enriched rice noodles, and by Phongthai et al. (2017), who observed such cracks in rice pasta.

TPC, TAC, and antioxidant activity

The incorporation of TCR flour enhanced the phenolic and anthocyanin contents (P<0.05) of the pasta (221.85 and 4.98 mg/100 g, respectively) relative to the control (57.00 and 0.70 mg/100 g, respectively; Table 4 and 6. Other studies on pasta and/or noodles supplemented with flour or vegetable powder also presented similar findings, as evidenced by noodles made with PSPF (Uthai et al., 2022), pasta incorporated with buckwheat hull (Sujka et al., 2022), and pasta made from black rice bran (Sethi et al., 2020). Such an increase can enhance the antioxidant capability, as phenolics and anthocyanins are natural antioxidants, and can neutralize harmful free radicals and reactive oxygen species (such as DPPH), thereby protecting cells and tissues from oxidative damage. Thus, TCR flour can serve as an excellent antioxidant for enriching pasta. Boroski et al. (2011) reported that pasta fortified with oregano and carrot leaves possessed an enhanced antioxidant capacity. Similarly, Uthai et al. (2022) reported that substituting wheat flour with 5%－20% PSPF in formulations enhanced the antioxidant ability of the noodles compared to those without PSPF. Betrouche et al. (2022) revealed that the incorporation of tomato pomace and linseed meal can alter the antioxidant capacity of pasta.

Proximate composition of flour and pasta

Chemical compositions of wheat and TCR flour are shown in Table 4. The moisture levels of wheat flour and TCR flour were 9.74% and 10.34%, respectively. The proportions of nutrients in the wheat flour included 79.99 g carbohydrate, 7.12 g protein, 1.29 g fat, 1.02 g ash, and 0.84 g fiber per 100 g. The TCR flour comprised 72.69 g carbohydrate, 8.01 g protein, 1.48 g fat, 1.25 g ash, and 6.23 g fiber per 100 g. Both types of flour had an elevated carbohydrate content owing to the removal of bran, which is rich in fiber. Enriched pasta, obtained by the partial substitution of wheat with TCR flour, exhibited a notable reduction in carbohydrate content from 66.30% (0% TCR) to 56.65% (60% TCR) as shown in Table 5, which might be attributable to the carbohydrate contents of wheat flour and TCR flour. Kraithong et al. (2018) also observed a reduction in carbohydrate content between white and red pigments. Furthermore, TCR incorporation substantially enhanced the fat, protein, ash, and fiber levels in pasta (Table 4). In addition, elevated TCR flour levels reduced the energy content of pasta compared to the control samples (Table 5).

Sensory evaluation

The sensory characteristics of cooked pasta enriched with TCR flour, including color, odor, taste, elasticity, smoothness, and overall acceptance, are summarized in Table 7. The replacement of wheat flour with 15%－60% TCR flour endowed the pasta produced with a reddish-brown color (indicative of anthocyanins), significantly increasing the score for color attribute over control pasta. However, the pasta prepared from flour enriched with 60% TCR flour showed a markedly better score related to odor compared to the control. However, substitution with 15%－30% TCR flour did not impact the taste of the pasta compared to the control. The structure of pasta, made from wheat flour augmented with various proportions of TCR flour, exhibited a discontinuous and highly porous surface (Fig. 2). Consequently, increasing the relative proportion of TCR flour in the pasta recipe reduced the elasticity and smoothness and, therefore, consumer acceptability of the pasta. These findings aligned with the texture and morphology results of the TCR-enriched pasta. They also aligned with the results of a study by Evangelista and Schönlechner (2025), who reported that increasing the substitution levels of pigmented rice flour diminished the elasticity of the noodles and pasta prepared. Therefore, the substitution of wheat flour with TCR flour negatively affected the texture of pasta. In conclusion, the overall acceptability score was the highest for the products containing wheat flour substituted with 30%－45% TCR flour.

Conclusion

This study revealed that replacing wheat flour with TCR flour substantially altered the physicochemical qualities of pasta. Sample analysis revealed a rise in protein, fat, ash, and fiber levels, but a decline in the carbohydrate content. TCR flour notably changed the pasta color, particularly, reducing the lightness and yellowness parameters. Sensory evaluations indicated that substituting wheat with TCR flour adversely affected the texture of cooked pasta, due to a dilution of the gluten network, which might be attributed to a reduced compaction of starch granules. Consequently, the pasta surface showed roughness. However, the TCR flour-enriched pasta exhibited elevated TPC and TAC, enhancing the antioxidant capacity. Our findings indicate that such pasta might serve as a beneficial choice for consumers pursuing nutritious and beneficial meals with elevated fiber, protein, ash, and antioxidant levels. However, the proportion of TCR flour should be less than or equal to 45% to improve consumer acceptability. Pasta made from wheat flour infused with TCR flour exhibited satisfactory cooking and sensory characteristics, with approximately 3-fold more antioxidant capacity than the pasta devoid of TCR. However, further studies employing various concentrations of TCR flour and different food matrices, along with in vivo experiments, are needed to support these findings.
