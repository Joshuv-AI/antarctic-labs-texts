Effects of dietary nisin on growth performance, meat quality, antioxidant status, and microbial homeostasis in broilers
Authors: Xiao Xiao, Tao Wang, Xin Li, Yushan Li, Zhenglie Dai, Agnieszka Lewińska, Caimei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this research is to explore the potential effects of different doses of nisin supplementation on the growth performance, meat quality, immune function, short chain fatty acid (SCFAs), and gut microbiota of broilers. Thus, 640 yellow-feathered broilers (initial body weight: 32.35 ± 0.13 g) at 1 d of age were randomly distributed into four groups (eight replicate pens per treatment and 20 birds per pen), fed with a basal diet with 0 (CON), 250 (N250), 500 (N500), and 1000 IU (N1000) nisin/g for 56 d. Nisin supplementation showed a quadratic increase in ADG from d 29 to 56 (P = 0.040) and d 0 to 56 (P = 0.048), and effectively increased the redness (a∗) (P < 0.001), significantly decreased the hardness (P < 0.001) and chewiness (P < 0.001) of breast muscles. The contents of serum immunoglobulin Y (IgY) at d 56 was significantly increased in the N500 and N1000 groups compared to the CON group (P = 0.013). Nisin significantly decreased the contents of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (P < 0.05) in serum at d 28 and 56. Furthermore, nisin supplementation significantly increased the levels of acetic acid (P < 0.05) at d 28 and 56, and propionic acid (P = 0.034) at d 56 to enrich the SCFAs content. More importantly, the α-diversity was increased (P < 0.05) and the microbial structure were altered by nisin supplementation at d 28 and 56. Differential analysis and linear discriminant analysis with effect size (LEfSe) plots together showed that Barnesiella and Lactobacillus were enriched in the CON group, while other species, including Bacteroides, Oscillibacter, and f_Ruminococcaceae, were predominant in the nisin-supplemented groups. In conclusion, nisin supplementation in the diet showed little impact on growth performance, effectively increased the meat quality of breast muscles, and strengthened the immune and antioxidant functions as indicated by pro-inflammatory cytokines and antioxidant enzymes, increased the contents of acetic and propionic acids, and potentially changed the microbial composition of broilers. These findings suggest that nisin has the potential to be used as a natural dietary supplement for broilers.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, large-scale overuse of growth-promoting antibiotics has resulted in bacterial resistance, antibiotic residues in animal-derived products such as meat, dairy, and eggs, and environmental pollution, which have become a global concern (Cook and Wright, 2022). Developed countries such as the European Union and the United States have previously announced a full ban on the use of certain antibiotics in animals (Schmerold et al., 2023). Since July 1, 2020, China also prohibited the application of growth-promoting antibiotics as feed additives except for medicinal herbs (Wen et al., 2022). However, reduced growth performance was observed in animals that did not receive antibiotics, highlighting the challenges associated with maintaining optimal productivity under antibiotic-free production systems (Cromwell, 2002). Thus, it is urgent to develop safe, efficient and environmentally friendly alternatives to antibiotics for animal farming and feed industries.

Nisin (3.5 kDa), also known as Nisaplin, is a natural bacteriocin produced by certain Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis strains, which is composed of 34 amino acid residues (Pogány Simonová et al., 2019). Previous studies have presented that nisin can inhibit most Gram-positive pathogenic bacteria, particularly Bacillus, Staphylococcus aureus, and Listeria monocytogenes, which contributed to the fact that nisin is the most studied bacteriocin and widely used in the food industry (Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020). Gradually, nisin has been approved as a safe food additive worldwide, receiving approval in different countries such as the European Union, the United States, and China (Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2024). Up to now, nisin has been widely used as a high-efficient, innoxious, secure and non-harmful side-effects food preservative in many aspects (Khan et al., 2023; Khelissa et al., 2021). Recent reports have showed that the global market size of nisin is expected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of about 5%–6% from 2024 to 2030, indicating the expanding applications of nisin in dairy products, meat processing and preservation, and beverages (Bennett et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025a).

Beyond its widespread application in food preservation, nisin has attracted global attention as a potential alternative to antibiotics due to its antimicrobial properties (Barbosa et al., 2021). Previous studies have exhibited that wide range of nisin (0.1–2700.0 IU/g) inhibited the growth of pathogens, improved the growth performance, restored the gastrointestinal microbial homeostasis, and finally enhanced host health (Barbosa et al., 2021; Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020; Pogány Simonová et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025b; Xiao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025c). However, the regulatory effect of nisin on meat quality, antioxidant functions and intestinal health of broilers are not fully understood and need further investigation.

The current study aims to investigate the regulatory effects of nisin in the diet (250, 500, and 1000 IU/g) on growth performance, immune status, meat quality, antioxidant functions, intestinal microbial balance and short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) of broilers. This research will provide a theoretical foundation for the application of nisin as a feed additive in livestock and poultry production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The animal experiment was conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang A&F University (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China; approval No. ZAFUAC2023041).

Experiments design, animals, and diets

In total, 640 yellow-feathered broilers (initial body weight: 32.35 ± 0.13 g) at 1 d of age were randomly divided into four groups (eight replicate pens per treatment and 20 birds per pen) under a completely randomized design. According to previous studies on broilers (Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020), chickens were fed with a wide range of nisin levels (0.1–2700.0 IU/g). Although the related literature about nisin in broilers is sparse, the mid-range doses from 250 to 1000 IU/g was chosen for exploration in this study. In detail, the chicks were fed with a basal diet with 0 (CON), 250 (N250), 500 (N500), and 1000 IU (N1000) nisin/g for 56 d. Nisin (100% purity) was offered by Zhejiang Vegamax Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Huzhou, Zhejiang, China) and the detailed formulation of nisin preparation was not disclosed due to commercial confidentiality. The basic diet (Table 1) did not contain antibiotics or anticoccidials according to the previous study (Yang et al., 2023), which was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of NY/T 3645-2020 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2020) and NY/T 33–2004 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2004). The crude protein (CP) content in the diet was analyzed using an automatic Kjeldahl analyzer (K9840, Shanghai Lijin Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in accordance with GB/T 6432-2018 (China National Standard, 2018b). The calcium (Ca) was determined via titration following GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018a) and phosphorus (P) was determined spectrophotometrically with a UV spectrophotometer (UV100star, Shanghai InsMark Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) following GB/T 6437-2018 (China National Standard, 2018c). The amino acid (AA) contents were analyzed with an amino acid analyzer (L8900, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) according to the method 982.30 (AOAC, 2006). Digestible energy values and available P were calculated based on data from the China Feed Database (Center for Feed Research of China Agricultural University, 2020). The available P content was estimated from the available P values of individual feed ingredients and their proportions in the formulation.

Growth performance

For growth performance analysis, the pen was considered as the experimental unit, with eight replicate pens per treatment group (n = 8). Body weight (BW) was recorded at d 0, 28, and 56. Feed consumption was measured at d 28 and 56. Average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were further calculated based on BW and feed intake values.

Sample collection

At d 28 and 56, one broiler with a BW approximate to the replicate pen's average was selected for sampling, resulting in eight biological replicates (n = 8) for immune organs and meat quality analysis. Blood was collected from the carotid artery of each bird, centrifuged at 3000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min to separate serum. The serum samples were stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. Afterwards, the liver, spleen, bursa and thymus were separated and weighed to calculate immune organ indexes. Immune organ index was calculated as follows:Immuneorganindexg/kg=Immuneorganweightg/Bodyweightkg.

The breast muscle was collected for measuring meat quality. The individual cecal contents from per bird were collected and stored in liquid nitrogen for a short time, and then transferred to −80 °C for analyzing SCFAs and microbiota composition. For other biochemical measurements including immunoglobins, cytokines, antioxidant parameters, and fatty acids, five birds were randomly selected from eight biological broilers for later analysis, resulting in five replicates (n = 5).

Meat quality

After 24 h post-mortem, the pH of breast muscle was detected by a portable pH meter (MP511, Shanghai San-Xin Instrumentation Inc., Shanghai, China) as previously (Cao et al., 2023). The pH meter was standardized with pH 4.010 and 7.007 buffer before determination. Each sample was measured three times at different locations, and the average value was used for further analysis. The color (lightness [L∗], redness [a∗], and yellowness [b∗]) of the breast muscle was estimated with an SR-68 computer colorimeter (Shenzhen Threenh Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) as reported previously (Cao et al., 2023). The drip loss was measured according to another article (Zhang et al., 2015). Briefly, a muscle sample of approximately 30 g, with the dimensions of 3 cm (length) × 2 cm (width) × 1 cm (thickness), was initially weighed, placed in a netting and suspended in a vacuum bag at 4 °C for 24 h. Afterwards, the muscle was weighed again, and the difference between the final and initial weights was calculated to express the drip loss. Texture-related parameters (hardness, chewiness, and springiness) of the breast muscle were measured by a Rapid TA+ texture analyzer (Shanghai Tengba Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) as previously reported (Cao et al., 2023).

Immunoglobulin and inflammatory cytokines levels in serum

The serum concentrations of immunoglobulins (including immunoglobulin A [IgA; ANG-E32004C], immunoglobulin M [IgM; ANG-E32005C], and immunoglobulin Y [IgY; ANG-E32009C]) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (including interleukin-1β [IL-1β; ANG-E32218C], interleukin-6 [IL-6; ANG-E32013C], and tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α; ANG-E32030C]) were measured by ELISA kits (Nanjing Angle Gene Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) in strict accordance with the protocols provided by the manufacturer, and the absorbance was read using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Antioxidant capacity

The serum contents of the total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-1-2), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2), and superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2) were measured according to the instructions of corresponding commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), and the absorbance was read using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Measurement of SCFAs

Briefly, 0.5 g of cecal contents was weighted accurately per sample, after which 1.5 mL of ultrapure water was added for thawing and vortex shock mixing. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for a duration of 15 min. The supernatant was collected and subsequently mixed with 25% phosphoric acid at a ratio of 1:5 (m/v), followed by storage at 4 °C overnight. Then, the mixture was filtered into the special injection bottles for later detection. For the determination of SCFA concentrations in cecal contents, a 7890B Network GC System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was utilized for gas chromatography, and the operation was carried out in accordance with the procedures detailed in the prior research (Qin et al., 2024). The concentrations obtained from the chromatographic analysis were normalized to the sample weight and expressed as μg SCFAs per g of feces (μg/g).

16S rRNA analysis

The microbiota structure and composition were analyzed according to the procedures of Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). In brief, the total genomic DNA of each sample was extracted from the cecal contents using the Fecal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO Biotech Co., Ltd., Taizhou, Jiangsu, China), and the concentration and quality were detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 regions of the bacteria 16S rRNA gene was amplified by the following universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Afterwards, the PCR products were retrieved and purified. Subsequently, library quantification, normalization, and pooling were performed, and the samples were loaded for MiSeq sequencing on the Illumina Miseq PE300 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw reads with sequencing adapters, N base, poly base, and low quality were filtered. The cleaned amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table was obtained and used for subsequent analysis. Shannon, Simpson, Chao, and ACE indexes were used to evaluate the α-diversity. Principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis were conducted to evaluate the β-diversity. Bacterial abundances and differential species at phylum, family, and genus levels were further analyzed. In addition, linear discriminant analysis with effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify biomarker microbes among groups. Related analysis was conducted in the online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Statistical analysis

For growth performance, the pen was considered the experiment unit. For immune organ indexes, meat quality, serum biochemical variables and SCFAs, individual birds were considered the experiment unit. The statistical differences between treatments were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's multiple range tests using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Polynomial contrasts were performed to determine the linear and quadratic effect of dietary nisin. Different letters in the tables indicate significant difference (P < 0.05), and ∗ in the figures indicate significant differences (∗ P < 0.05, ∗∗ P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗ P < 0.001). All data were presented as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). The graphs were generated by GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The mathematical model for analysis of variance in one-way ANOVA experiments is as follows:Xij=μ+ai+eij,where Xij denotes the experimental results; μ denotes the population mean; ai denotes the i-th treatment effect; eij denotes the accumulated experimental error. The eij were independent of each other and eij ∼ (0, σ2).

Results

Nisin showed little impact on the growth performance and immune organ indexes of broilers

As shown in Table 2, IBW did not differ significantly among the four groups (P > 0.05), confirming successful randomization. Dietary nisin supplementation at three dosages showed no impact on the FBW, ADG, ADFI, and FCR at any stage according to ANOVA analysis (P > 0.05). However, a significant quadratic effect of nisin on ADG from d 29 to 56 (P = 0.040) and d 0 to 56 (P = 0.048) was observed. Compared to the CON group, ADG increased by 7.76% from d 29 to 56 and 5.48% from d 0 to 56 in the N250 group, 7.90% from d 29 to 56 and 5.66% from d 0 to 56 in the N500 group, and 4.73% from d 29 to 56 and 4.45% from d 0 to 56 in the N1000 group. Together, nisin supplementation did not significantly affect other growth performance parameters (BW, ADFI, and FCR), although a quadratic response was observed for ADG (P = 0.048). As shown in Table 3, dietary nisin supplementation at three dosages showed no impact on the liver, spleen, bursa, and thymus indexes at d 28 and 56 based on ANOVA analysis (P > 0.05). Nisin enhanced the breast meat quality of broilers

Results in Table 4 showed that dietary nisin supplementation at three dosages showed no impact on the L∗, b∗, pH, drip loss, and springiness of breast muscle at d 56 according to ANOVA analysis (P > 0.05). However, the L∗ (P = 0.047) increased linearly, whereas the b∗ (P = 0.048) and hardness (P < 0.001) decreased linearly with the increasing addition of nisin. In addition, the N500 group pronouncedly enhanced the a∗ of breast muscle at d 56 (P < 0.001), which was not affected among the CON, N250, and N1000 groups (P > 0.05). There was a quadratic influence on the a∗ (P < 0.001) in response to increased nisin level. Nisin supplementation groups significantly decreased the hardness and chewiness of breast muscle at d 56 compared to the control broilers (P < 0.05), especially the N1000 group. Similarly, Fig. 3A and B showed the increased Shannon index and decreased Simpson index at d 56, which was the same as the results of microbial diversity index at d 28. Surprisingly, the Chao and ACE indexes were also significantly enhanced (P < 0.05; Fig. 3C and D), indicating a time-dependent effect. PCoA and NMDS plots jointly indicated the significant separation between the CON and nisin treatment groups (P = 0.001; Fig. 3E and F). Thus, above results demonstrated that nisin supplementation enhanced the microbial diversity, richness and altered the flora composition of broilers with time prolonging.

The microbial composition and abundance at d 56 are showed in Fig. 4. The dominant bacteria at phylum level were constantly Firmicutes and Bacteroidota, whereas no phyla exhibited significance (P > 0.05) among four groups (Fig. 4A). The predominant bacteria at family level were Oscillosporaceae and Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the relative abundances of Oscillosporaceae, Clostridia_UCG-014, and Tannerellaceae were higher (P < 0.05), and the relative abundance of Streptococcaceae was lower (P < 0.001) in the nisin supplementation groups compared with the control broilers. In addition, the relative abundance of Christensenellaceae was reduced in the N250 and N500 groups compared with the CON group (P = 0.031; Fig. 4C). The Top10 and differential bacteria at genus level are exhibited in Fig. 4D and E. Bacteroides, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Ruminococcus_torques_group, Alistipes, and Faecalibacterium were the predominant bacteria (Fig. 4D). Among which, the relative abundances of Streptococcus and Barnesiella were decreased (P < 0.05), and the relative abundances of Alistipes, f_Ruminococcaceae, and Clostridia_UCG-014 were increased (P < 0.05) in the nisin supplementation groups. Compared to the CON group, the N1000 group increased the relative abundance of Oscillibacter (P = 0.015), the N250 and N500 groups both expressed a significant decrease of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (P = 0.028). Similarly, LEfSe analysis (LDA > 2; Fig. 4F) showed that Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, and Barnesiella et al., were enriched in the CON group. The N250 group possessed more abundance in V9D2013_group, and Papillibacter et al. The bacteria including Ruminococcus and Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group were increased in the N500 group, while the bacteria including Clostridia_UCG-014, and Oscillibacter et al., were enriched in the N1000 group. Together, these findings indicated that the beneficial effect of nisin on broilers health may be associated with alterations in gut microbiota.

Discussion

The current study demonstrated that dietary nisin addition showed no obvious effect on growth performance, improved the breast meat quality, immune and antioxidant capacity, reduced inflammatory response, improved cecal acetic acid and propionic acid, and modulated the microbial ecology of broilers.

Growth performance is one of the most important traits for evaluating the productive value of broilers. Previous study has showed that nisin supplementation ranging from 100 to 800 IU/kg showed no impact on body weight gain and feed intake of broilers, while the addition of nisin (100, 200, and 800 IU/kg) reduced the FCR from d 14 to d 35 (Kierończyk et al., 2020). Consistently, this study found that nisin addition showed a quadratic increase in ADG, with no impact on ADFI and FCR, indicating no obvious impact of nisin on the growth performance. However, another study demonstrated that nisin at doses of 100, 300, 900, and 2700 IU/g for 35 d improved body weight gain and feed intake, and reduced FCR in a dose-dependent manner, especially in the NI900 and NI2700 groups (Kieronczyk et al., 2017). Other evidence has indicated that nisin addition showed positive effect on growth performance by affecting BW, ADG, ADFI, or FCR based on different animal models (Pogány Simonová et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024). These inconsistent results were possibly due to the concentrations of nisin and experimental periods.

Immune status is regarded as an important indicator of estimating overall body health, while immune organs, immune cells, secreted cytokines and peptides are closely related to the immune functions. A previous study has reported that bursa of Fabricius, spleen, pancreas, and liver indexes of broiler chickens showed no differences between nisin addition groups and the control group (Kierończyk et al., 2020), which was in line with the current results. Similarly, another study also reported that 2000 IU/kg nisin did not affect thymus, spleen and bursa indexes of broilers (Kieronczyk et al., 2017). Immunoglobins are well-known antibodies, which is primarily produced by plasma cells reacting to antigens. These Igs recognizes and neutralizes pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and toxins to affect the immune systems (Sun et al., 2020). The current research found that nisin supplementation selectively increased IgY at d 56 to show a time accumulating effect. Consistently, one previous study in piglets also showed that nisin supplementation increased serum IgA, IgM, and IgY contents (Xiao et al., 2025). Likewise, bacteriocin-producing probiotics contributed to the increased release of IgA and IgG in mice (Bu et al., 2023). Cytokines, including pro-inflammatory IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, are closely related to inflammatory response (Chauhan et al., 2021). A previous study indicated that 100 mg/kg nisin in broilers reduced serum TNF-α, but showed no effect on IL-2 and IL-6 contents (Yuan et al., 2024). Another study exhibited that piglets supplemented with nisin (200 mg/kg) significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α contents (Xiao et al., 2025). Moreover, nisin treatment abrogated the release of IL-6 and TNF-α in the uterus and oral cavity in periodontitis-related mice (Ye et al., 2024). One type of antimicrobial peptide, called Gal-13, downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory IL-2, IL-6 and TNF-α to inhibit the inflammation of broilers (Wang et al., 2023). In line with the results of other researches, the current study also found that dietary nisin supplementation reduced serum pro-inflammatory cytokines to inhibit inflammation and maintain immune status. In summary, the current research demonstrated the profitable effect of nisin on immune status of broilers.

Meat quality directly affects consumer acceptability, which determines the economic value of the poultry industry. Nutritional strategies, except genetics, are key factors for promoting the meat quality of broilers (Song et al., 2025). In practical feeding, meat quality is accessed by various indicators, including pH, drip loss, shear force, meat color, moisture contents et al. (Bortolotto et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2023; Pogány Simonová et al., 2019). Nisin, as a natural food preservative, was found to exert antimicrobial properties and protect meat quality in the food industry (Bortolotto et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025). There was only one study reported the effect of nisin on meat quality of broilers, which showed that drinking nisin-containing water for d 28 and 42 showed no impact on pH, L∗, a∗, b∗, water content, protein content, and fat content (Pogány Simonová et al., 2019). Another study found that nisin-treated calabresa sausages showed no obvious change in protein and moisture content, but displayed an increase of instrumental color parameters (L∗, a∗, and b∗) (Bortolotto et al., 2025). Sausages treated with combined nisin-nanoparticles and propolis ethanolic extract displayed higher pH values and sensory scores with low hardness throughout keeping period (Yaghoubi et al., 2025). The current data revealed that dietary nisin improved the meat quality. The findings add to the existing literature by examining the effects of nisin on meat quality considering both dose impact and time-dependent response.

The antioxidant defense system is essential for diminishing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining the oxidative balance of host. Many internal antioxidants composing of the antioxidant network function on reducing the production of ROS to alleviate the oxidative damage, such as GSH-Px, SOD, and CAT (Halliwell, 2024). Previous study demonstrated that probiotic Paenibacillus ehimensis NPUST1 with bacteriocin-like activity in the diet increased the activity of SOD in fish (Chen et al., 2019). Another study showed that thuricin 17 (T17) and bacthuricin F4 (BF4), two bacteriocins, increased the activity of the seven SOD isozymes in soybean seedlings (Jung et al., 2011). Antimicrobial peptide Gal-13 supplementation in broilers increased the activity of GSH-Px, and reduced the MDA content in the serum and liver (Wang et al., 2023). Nevertheless, no available data has reported the effect of nisin on antioxidant functions based on broiler model. This study first found that nisin supplementation enhanced the levels of GSH-Px and T-AOC at d 56 in a time-dependent manner to increase the antioxidant capacity of broilers.

Growing evidence has emphasized the critical function of gut microbiota and their derived metabolites in maintaining intestinal balance (Gao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2021). Short-chain fatty acid, originating from carbohydrate fermentation, is the largest group of metabolites and reported to modulate immune response, barrier integrity, intestinal health, oxidative stress, and host metabolism (Flint et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2025; Hays et al., 2024). One study found that supplemented with nisin at doses of 100, 300, 900, and 2700 IU/g for 35 d reduced the proportion of propionic acid and iso-butyric acid in ileum and ceaca digesta, and increased the proportion of acetic acid in ileum digesta on broilers (Józefiak et al., 2013). Another study indicated that nisin increased the content of acetic acid in the cecal content, but reduced the content of acetic acid in the jejunal content of broilers (Kierończyk et al., 2020). Moreover, piglets supplemented with nisin (100, 200, and 300 mg/kg) promoted the levels of acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, with no impact on the levels of isobutyric, valeric, and isovaleric acids (Xiao et al., 2025). Consistently, the current study also found that nisin supplementation especially increased the levels of acetic acid and propionic acid of broilers in a time-dependent manner. As some bacteria (Bacteroides spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Clostridium spp., and Streptococcus spp.) can produce acetate to reduce the intestinal pH, inhibit the growth of harmful bacteria, and maintain intestinal health (Flint et al., 2012; Hays et al., 2024). Moreover, acetic acid and propionic acid are important metabolites that can serve energy substrate and regulate host metabolism. However, further studies are required to investigate whether the increases of acetic acid and propionic acid are associated with gut microbiota, as well as the potential effects on host metabolism and gut health.

Short chain fatty acids are critical mediators of host-microbiota crosstalk, whose change is closely related to specific bacteria and microbial balance (Gao et al., 2025). Similar to other studies (Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024), the current study also found a significant effect of nisin supplementation on the intestinal microbiota of broilers. Although no literature has reported the effect of nisin on microbial diversity in broilers up to now, the findings of this research demonstrated that nisin supplementation significantly increased the α-diversity and changed the microbial β-diversity, which was in line with the study of nisin in piglets and in mice (Xiao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024). Only two papers exhibited the effect of nisin on the selected microbial populations by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) (Józefiak et al., 2013; Kierończyk et al., 2020). In other animals, bacteriocin bifidocin A enhanced the microbial diversity to restore gut microbial balance of mice (Du et al., 2025). MccY, a novel microcin with antimicrobial activity, significantly increased Chao1 and observed-operational taxonomic units (OTUs) to upregulate the microbial alpha diversity in mice (Li et al., 2024b). In the current study, the relative abundance of Barnesiella and Streptococcus were significantly enriched in the CON group. Barnesiella was reported to maintain the strength and integrity of the intestinal barrier, protected against pathogens infection and alleviated metabolic disorders (Zhang et al., 2025d). On the other hand, Barnesiella was reduced in the probiotics-supplemented groups compared with the control broilers, which was in line with the present results (Emami et al., 2021). However, another study found that MccY significantly increased the abundance of Barnesiella (Li et al., 2024a). These findings suggested a dual and context-dependent role of Barnesiella. Certain Streptococcus species are considered opportunistic and can occasionally cause diverse diseases, which posed a threat to pigs, broilers, and humans (Zhang et al., 2025b). The protective effect of nisin on the whole health of broilers may be potentially associated with the decreased Streptococcus abundance. Surprisingly, the genus Lactobacillus was enriched in the nisin treated groups at d 28, and then enriched in the CON group at d 56. Some Lactobacillus such as L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus are lactic-acid producing bacteria, which play important roles in maintaining gut health, modulating immune functions and inhibiting pathogen infection (Huang et al., 2022; Lou et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2024). Although lactic-acid bacteria are often considered beneficial, increasing evidence indicates that certain species or strains may exert neutral or even adverse effects under specific conditions, particularly when their metabolic activities disrupt microbial balance (Cannon et al., 2005; Sanders et al., 2019). Therefore, changes in Lactobacillus should be interpreted cautiously in strain-specific and context-dependent manners. Previous study showed that MccY addition raised the proportion of Bacteroides similar to the present results, but showed no impact on Alistipes and Lactobacillus (Li et al., 2024b). In this study, the potential role of Lactobacillus on nisin-treated broilers still needs further exploration. In addition, Bacteroides and f_Ruminococcaceae are well-known bacteria for acetate and propionate production (Dou et al., 2025; Flint et al., 2012; Gałęcka et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2023). The increase of above bacteria induced by nisin supplementation may be associated with the increased acetic acid and propionic acid, which still needs further exploration. Alistipes, the crucial commensal microorganisms producing butyrate, was negatively linked to the occurrence and development of inflammatory diseases and hepatic diseases (Older et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). In contrast, some Alistipes species are pathogenic in colorectal cancer and reported to be associated with mental signs of depression (Parker et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2012). The dual-role of Alistipes still warrants further investigation under different conditions. Although the current results showed the change of microbiota and specific bacteria, many studies have reviewed the opposite effects of mentioned bacteria under different context, time, strains, and animal models. Their functions should be further verified under specific circumstances, and the inner relationship between microbial change, acetic acid increase, and host health require further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that nisin addition at 250 to 1000 IU/g in the diet showed no impact on the growth performance, enhanced the immune status as accessed by increased serum IgY content and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines levels. Moreover, nisin improved the breast meat quality, and increased the levels of GSH-Px and T-AOC to enhance the antioxidant capacity, indicating the dose and time-dependent effect of nisin. Furthermore, nisin supplementation increased acetic acid and propionic acid contents to enrich SCFAs, and shifted the microbial composition, especially the SCFAs-producing bacteria including Bacteroides, Oscillibacter, Alistipes, and Lactobacillus. However, there are several limitations for the current study. First, the inclusion level of nisin was primarily exploratory and may be too high considering the cost and commercial feasibility, future work should further investigate lower dietary levels of nisin to establish optimal concentrations for practical applications in broilers. Second, although the study found the beneficial effect of nisin on immune and antioxidant functions, the inner correlations among increased acetic acid, altered specific bacteria, and intestinal health still need further exploration. Finally, the study was conducted in a single broiler breed under controlled experimental conditions, limiting generalizability to other breeds or commercial production systems, which should also be considered.
