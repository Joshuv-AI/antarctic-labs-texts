Could Esmolol/Landiolol Infusion During Pulsed Field Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation Have Averted Takotsubo Syndrome?
Authors: John E Madias
Published: 2026

Tomura et al1 reported on the first case of pulsed field ablation (PFA)-triggered takotsubo syndrome (TTS), involving a 76-year-old woman who underwent ablation (pulmonary vein isolation) for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF). This author2 has previously summarized reported data on patients suffering TTS in the setting of undergoing AF ablation and other procedures in the electrophysiology laboratory and has proposed the implementation of periprocedural carefully titrated infusions of short-acting (esmolol: 9 minutes half-life)/ultrashort-acting (landiolol: 4 minutes half-life) β-blockers for some patients (those excessively nervous about the procedures, postmenopausal women, or patients with underlying neurological/psychiatric afflictions); such infusions can be terminated with impunity if complications attributed to β-blockade arise. The authors should be congratulated for the expert diagnosis and management of their patient and insightful discussion of their paper; they are probably correct attributing the emergence of TTS to a stimulation of the efferent to the heart sympathetic nerves and cardiac ganglionated plexi, speculating also about a greater emotional or physical stress associated with PFA than with conventional thermal ablation, and thus higher propensity to trigger TTS.1 TTS can be triggered by both local stimulation of the sympathetic nerves in proximity to the heart and via an autonomic sympathetic nervous system surge imparted by the stellate ganglia's neural input to the heart3,4; the latter could be monitored noninvasively periprocedurally via thoracic skin signals, representing a surrogate of the stellate ganglia's neural activity.5 For both, the advocated periprocedural β-blockade2 may be of value in PFA of AF and other electrophysiology procedures (EPP).

The pathophysiology of TTS is complex and still elusive; β-blockade may not prevent its emergence, if other inciting factors (eg, microvascular dysfunction, direct cardiotoxic mechanisms, other than the one via β-adrenergic receptor intense stimulation) are at play. The baseline incidence of TTS during AF ablation has not been determined, although it is not negligible,6 and a comparison of AF ablation performed employing thermo-/cryo-ablation or PFA have not been attempted, although the latter may be more prone to instigate stimulation/modification of the cardiac ganglionated plexi.1 There are also risks in employing β-blockade (eg, hypotension, bradycardia, precipitation of atrioventricular block, causation/intensification of heart failure), although the short/ultrashort half-life of the recommended agents provides a safeguard. The target population has not been determined but should include primarily elderly women, since >80% who suffered TTS in the setting of EPP were women,2,6 patients with neurological/psychiatric diseases and on psychotropic drugs, those with thyroid diseases, or who have shown/verbalized worry about the EPP, appeared nervous, or with tachycardia/hypertension and a decrease in heart rate variability.5 Of course one could consider alternative strategies to ameliorate the autonomic adrenergic surge, like anxiolytics, adequate analgesia, conscious sedation as potentially safer approaches, although β-adrenergic stimulation may still take place in the process of implementing PFA. Another issue not considered heretofore is whether EPP induce milder variants of TTS,7 something that could be explored by mandatory implementation of echocardiography after EPP. The above constitute a hypothesis, requiring validation.
