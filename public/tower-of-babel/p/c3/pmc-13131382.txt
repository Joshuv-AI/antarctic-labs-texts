A Standardized Statistical Framework for Population Surveillance Using the National Health Interview Survey
Authors: Adith S Arun, Rishi M Shah, Yuan Lu, Sanket S Dhruva, Harlan M Krumholz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNational health surveillance systems in the United States collect essential information about population health, yet differences in analytical methods often make it difficult to compare results across studies. This paper presents a clear and consistent framework for descriptive analyses using the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). The framework establishes shared definitions for sociodemographic and clinical variables, outlines methods for handling missing data, and describes use of survey weights and multivariable models. This process will generate reproducible and interpretable estimates across different NHIS-based studies.Key words: NHIS, population trends, statistical framework

The U.S. has many national health surveillance systems, but analyses are often siloed and methodologically inconsistent, resulting in difficulties of interpretation across studies. We present a common analytic framework for conducting and reporting descriptive analyses using the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). This framework provides the foundation for new analyses presented in accompanying data briefs to the flagship JACC Cardiovascular Statistics in the United States: 2026 report. Specifically, we describe our framework for segmenting sociodemographic variables, defining clinical conditions, handling missing data, and performing statistical analyses within the context of the NHIS. This approach ensures coherence across our reports and those of others, enabling interpretation of trends in exercise, sleep, health care affordability, and health care utilization by key subgroups with the same conceptual and visual vocabulary as trends in access to care. The methods presented here can inform others analyzing these datasets, enabling the research community to generate comparable and reproducible insights into the health of people in the United States.

Methods

Data source and reproducibility

Data were collected from NHIS, a cross-sectional representative survey of the U.S. civilian population.1 We downloaded data through the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) website and present data for each report alongside the code used to generate the analyses in a public GitHub repository.2 Approval from the Yale Institutional Review Board was not required because all data and analytic code are publicly available. All analyses were conducted and figures generated with R (version 4.3.1).

Definitions

All adults aged ≥18 years were eligible. A self-reported history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was defined as ever having been told by a doctor or other health professional that they had any of the following: coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Income levels were classified relative to the federal poverty level (FPL) for each year. We classified participants based on the ratio of their family income to FPL when stratifying an outcome by income: <100% FPL, 100% to 199% FPL, 200% to 299% FPL, 300% to 399% FPL, 400% to 499% FPL, and ≥500% FPL. Race/ethnicity was delineated by the following categories: Hispanic/Latino, non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black, NH Asian, NH American Indian/Alaskan Native, and other. An individual who responded “yes” to identifying as Hispanic was classified as Hispanic regardless of the race they self-reported. Age, when used to stratify an outcome, was binned into 3 categories: 18 to 39, 40 to 64, and ≥65 years.

Statistical analysis methodology

Survey weights from NHIS for each participant were applied to account for the complex NHIS sampling design, oversampling, and nonresponse to arrive at nationally representative estimates of an outcome. When projecting the annual rate of an outcome without stratification by a sociodemographic factor, we used only the survey weight for the relevant participants (ie, all participants, or, depending on the context, participants with ASCVD) each year.

When estimating annual outcome rates stratified by race/ethnicity, we binarized the variable of interest such that we could estimate a percentage. Furthermore, we used multivariable logistic regression models where covariates were survey weight, age, sex, region, urbanicity, ratio of family income to FPL, and survey year. Age and ratio of family income to FPL were input as continuous variables when included as covariates.

Specifically, for race/ethnicity-stratified estimates, we removed this variable from the covariate list and fit a multivariable regression model. When income was the relevant stratum, we estimated the annual outcome using a multivariable logistic regression model that did not have income to FPL ratio as a covariate but did add race/ethnicity to the list of covariates mentioned above. When age or income variables were the stratum of interest, they were categorized into clinically relevant subsets as defined in the previous section.

All covariates were centered at their overall sample means, such that the estimated year coefficients, combined with the model intercept, represented the adjusted logit of the annual outcome rate. Separate models were fitted for each subgroup, and annual adjusted rates were obtained by applying the inverse logit transformation to each’s year coefficient. Standard errors (SE) and 95% CIs for the transformed estimates were calculated by means of parametric bootstrapping. Analyses were restricted to respondents with complete data.

Trend analyses for statistical significance

To assess trends over time, weighted linear regression of the annual outcome rate estimates were conducted within each stratum (eg, <100% FPL), using weights equal to the inverse of the square root of the SE derived from each estimate’s 95% CI. A 2-sided P value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Discussion

We seek to leverage U.S. federal databases to gain insight into the cardiovascular health of people in the U.S. over time. To make improvements, we must measure what matters. Developing a standardized and transparent methodology enables us to consistently measure progress, identify differences, and reveal gaps that hinder improvement.

Limitations of these approaches when using NHIS include the self-reported status of health conditions (eg, ASCVD) and accuracy of sociodemographic variables.3 In addition, the stability of point estimates in strata is subject to sample size constraints. We chose to estimate yearly annual rates for a specific outcome at a given strata (eg, NH Asian) using all participants instead of a specific subset of participants (eg, those with ASCVD) to preserve the stability of estimates. Other strategies may be to pool data across years and compute odds ratios, or accept the instability in yearly condition subset outcome rates for specific strata.
