Pipecolic acid modulates physiological parameters and biotic stress resilience in Mesembryanthemum crystallinum in a photosynthetic mode-dependent manner
Authors: Emilia Gula, Agnieszka Ostrowska, Elke Reinartz, Jürgen Zeier, Ewa Pociecha, Monika Tuleja, Bartosz J Płachno, Ewa Niewiadomska, Marta Libik-Konieczny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPipecolic acid (Pip) functions as a precursor of key signalling metabolites involved in systemic acquired resistance; however, its broader role in modulating plant metabolism under biotic stress remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated how Pip influences metabolic and physiological responses to bacterial challenge in Mesembryanthemum crystallinum (ice plant), a semihalophytic species capable of shifting from C₃ photosynthesis to CAM through regulation of photosynthetic enzymes and stomatal opening rhythms under stress conditions.ResultsProlonged exogenous pipecolic acid (Pip) supplementation induced several CAM-associated traits in C₃ plants, including nocturnal malate accumulation, diurnal fluctuations in cell-sap acidity, and increased phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) levels, suggesting a partial shift toward CAM-like metabolism. In both C₃ and CAM plants, Pip treatment increased RuBisCO activity while reducing the abundance of the large RuBisCO subunit (LSU), indicating differential regulation of photosynthetic carbon assimilation. Gas-exchange measurements showed that Pip-treated plants maintained higher net photosynthetic rates (PN) and improved water-use efficiency (WUE) under bacterial stress. The effects of Pip on stomatal morphology, photosynthetic pigment content, and photosynthesis-related proteins differed between C₃ and CAM species, highlighting photosynthetic type-dependent responses. Salicylic acid (SA) accumulation following Pip treatment was detected only in C₃ plants, whereas endogenous Pip levels increased in both photosynthetic types after bacterial challenge, with a more pronounced response in CAM plants.ConclusionsOur findings identify pipecolic acid as metabolic regulator that integrates defence signalling with photosynthetic plasticity, thereby coordinating plant responses to environmental stress. This highlights Pip as a promising target for strategies aimed at enhancing stress resilience while maintaining efficient carbon assimilation and photosynthetic performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09590-8.Keywords: Ice plant, Signalling molecules, Biotic stress, Pseudomonas syringae, Chlorophyll, Photosynthesis

Introduction

Climate change increasingly exposes plants to multifactorial stress combinations (MFSC), in which abiotic factors such as drought, salinity, and elevated temperatures coincide with biotic threats like pathogen infection [1]. These simultaneous stressors frequently exacerbate plant vulnerability, leading to reduced biomass accumulation and disruption of critical physiological functions, notably photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, and water-use efficiency [2–5]. Pathogen infection triggers extensive metabolic reprogramming, suppressing photosynthesis-related genes while redirecting carbon and energy towards defence responses [6, 7]. This shift is accompanied by enhanced glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity, carbohydrate remodelling, photorespiration, reactive oxygen species (ROS) signalling, and hormonal regulation involving salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and abscisic acid (ABA), allowing plants to balance growth with immunity [8–18]. The extent and nature of pathogen-induced photosynthetic responses are expected to vary among plants with different photosynthetic strategies. C3 and CAM species possess fundamentally distinct carbon assimilation pathways and contrasting water-use efficiencies [19–24]. Therefore, differences in photosynthetic metabolism may influence how plants balance defence activation, carbon allocation, and stress acclimation during pathogen challenge.

The hemibiotrophic bacteria Pseudomonas syringae is a well-established model for studying plant immunity. Infection activates pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), initiated by recognition of conserved microbial molecules, while bacterial type III secretion system (T3SS) effectors suppress these defences until they are detected by intracellular immune receptors, triggering effector-triggered immunity (ETI) and often the hypersensitive response (HR) [25–32]. Local immune activation also induces systemic acquired resistance (SAR), a long-lasting defence state coordinated primarily by SA and N-hydroxypipecolic acid (NHP). The amino acid derivative Pip, the metabolic precursor of NHP, accumulates rapidly during infection and is essential for SAR establishment through its conversion to NHP by flavin-dependent monooxygenase1 (FMO1). Together, the Pip-NHP-SA signalling network forms a central regulatory hub coordinating local and systemic immunity across diverse plant species [33–45].

Beyond its established role in immune signalling, increasing evidence suggests that Pip also influences primary metabolism. Pip-dependent signalling regulates genes associated not only with defence but also with photosynthesis and carbon metabolism, indicating a broader role in metabolic acclimation during stress. However, whether Pip directly modulates photosynthetic performance and gas exchange, particularly in plants with contrasting photosynthetic strategies, remains largely unknown. The facultative halophyte M. crystallinum provides an excellent system to address this question because it reversibly switches from C3 photosynthesis to crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) in response to environmental stress. CAM is characterized by nocturnal CO₂ fixation, enhanced water-use efficiency, and elevated antioxidant capacity, traits that may influence plant responses to pathogen infection [46–51]. Previous work showed that exogenous Pip improves redox homeostasis during bacterial challenge, particularly in the C3 phase, whereas CAM plants exhibit comparatively minor metabolic responses, suggesting that the effect of Pip activity depends on the photosynthetic state [51]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether Pip directly regulates photosynthetic metabolism or promotes CAM-like physiological traits independently of abiotic stress.

In this study, we investigated the effects of Pip on the physiological responses of M. crystallinum to P. syringae treatment in both the C₃ and CAM metabolisms. We further examined whether prolonged Pip supplementation of non-stressed C₃ plants induces CAM-like characteristics. To address these questions, we analysed gas exchange, chlorophyll fluorescence, pigment composition, stomatal morphology, RuBisCO and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) abundance, and RuBisCO activity. These analyses provide new insight into the integration of immune signalling with photosynthetic metabolism and clarify whether Pip functions not only as a SAR regulator but also as a modulator of photosynthetic acclimation and stress resilience.

Materials and methods

Plant material, bacteria culture, and Pip supplementation

The procedures for plants and bacterial growth were previously described in detail in Gula et al. [51]. The experimental design is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Information Appendix S1). M. crystallinum seeds were obtained from the Botanical Garden of the University of Vienna (IPEN number of accession: XX-0-WU-0003530). Seeds were sown directly into a universal peat-based soil mixture. After two weeks of germination, seedlings were individually transferred into separate pots and placed in a controlled-environment growth chamber. After an additional two weeks of standard growth, half of the four-week-old plants (with fully developed leaves) were watered with a 400 mM NaCl solution for seven consecutive days to induce a transition from C₃ to CAM metabolism. The remaining plants continued to receive water only, serving as C₃ controls. Plants representing both C₃ and CAM metabolic states were divided into two groups. One group from each photosynthetic type was treated with 10 mL of 1 mM Pip solution applied to the soil, while the corresponding control group received water. One day after supplementation, half of the plants from each subgroup were inoculated with Pseudomonas syringae. This experimental design resulted in eight final treatment variants. The procedure for treating M. crystallinum with bacteria has been previously utilized in our studies, with a detailed description of the method published by Libik-Konieczny et al. [52]. Suspension of Pseudomonas syringae DC3000 strain was prepared by establishing a liquid culture from a 1-week-old bacterial culture, in an NGYA solid medium (Bacto Proteose Peptone 0.5%, yeast extract 0.3%, glycerol 2% (v/v), agar 1.5%) with rifampicin (50 mg L− 1). After 24 h of growth in the dark, the suspension was centrifuged at 5000 x g for 5 min at room temperature (RT). The pellet was collected and resuspended in 10 mM MgCl2. The culture density was measured, and bacteria were diluted to an optical density (OD600) of 0.1 (106 colony-forming units -CFUs mL− 1). For successful infection of plants with P. syringae, the bacterial solution was introduced into the leaf (one leaf of the third pair per plant) using a syringe with a needle. Bacterial inoculation was carried out at 8 a.m. in relation to a similar degree of stomatal opening in both metabolic groups (Supplementary Information Appendix S2). For the identification of P. syringae presence in plant tissue, inoculated leaves of M. crystallinum were cut into small fragments, which were then fixed in a 5% glutaraldehyde solution and dehydrated in an ethanol concentration gradient (70–100%). The pieces were then embedded in Technovit 7100 resin, sliced into 5–7 μm-thick sections, and stained with Gram staining (PREVI® COLOR GRAM, bioMérieux, France). Visualised bacteria in the plant tissue were compared with Gram-stained P. syringae collected from bacteria culture on solid medium after 24 h of growth (Supplementary Information Appendix S3, S4).

Leaf gas exchange parameters

Gas exchange was assessed using an LCpro-SD infrared gas analyzer (ADC BioScientific Ltd., Hoddesdon, UK), equipped with automated control over measurement conditions. The evaluated parameters encompassed the photosynthetic rate (PN), indicating CO2 assimilation, and leaf stomatal conductance (gs). The water use efficiency index (WUE) was derived from the PN/E relationship [53]. The conditions in the measurement chamber were as follows: carbon dioxide concentration 400 µmol CO2 mol− 1 air, air humidity equal to ambient humidity, and PAR intensity 400 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1. The measurements were taken from the second fully developed leaf of control plants, Pip-supplemented plants, and inoculated with bacteria plants, on four replicates per variant (one leaf from different plants per replicate). The parameters were assessed 24 and 72 h after bacterial inoculation, at the same time of the day (8 a.m. in relation to a similar degree of stomatal opening in both metabolic groups).

Stomata visualization and measurements

Leaves were collected and examined under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) to visualize overall stomatal morphology and compare structural differences between control and Pip-supplemented plants (Supplementary Information Appendix S5). The plant leaves were cut into fragments and fixed according to the methods described by Lustofin et al. [54] and Płachno et al. [55]. Subsequently, they were transferred to ethanol, then to acetone, and dried using supercritical CO₂. The samples were sputter-coated with gold and examined at an accelerating voltage of 15–20 kV using a Hitachi S-4700 scanning electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan) at the Institute of Geological Sciences, Jagiellonian University, Kraków, Poland.

Stomatal length and width were measured in leaves from control, Pip-supplemented, and bacteria-inoculated plants using microscopic preparations under an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E200, Japan) with the same magnification. A replica of the leaf surface was made using varnish. This method involved covering a leaf from the same pair, opposite to the leaf directly treated with a layer of varnish. After drying, the varnish was removed with tweezers, applied to a glass slide, and used for microscopic observations [56]. Samples were always collected at the same time of the diurnal cycle (Supplementary Information Appendix S2), and the measurement was taken primarily from the underside of the leaf, between the leaf veins. The samples were collected 24 h after Pip supplementation, and 72 h after bacterial inoculation (3 days pbt). Approximately 40 stomata from each variant were analysed each time. The length and width of the stomata were measured using the ImageJ computer program (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Supplementary Information Appendix S6). The measurements were made according to Savvides et al. [57].

Fast kinetics chlorophyll a fluorescence

PSII efficiency was estimated based on measurements of parameters of chlorophyll a fast fluorescence, which were performed using a Plant Efficiency Analyser-FluorPen (PSI Photon, Drasov, Czech Republic). Before the measurement, the leaves (one leaf from third pair) of control, Pip-supplemented, and inoculated with bacteria plants were dark-adapted for 25 min to permit the fast fluorescence quenching mechanisms to relax [58]. OJIP fluorescence transients were measured in dark-adapted leaves, and the corresponding parameters describing PSII performance and energy fluxes were derived from the recorded fluorescence curves [59, 60]. The PSII efficiency was measured on the second fully developed leaf in four replicates per variant (one leaf from different plants per replicate).

Chlorophyll a, b and carotenoid content

For photosynthetic pigments content, 0.2 g of leaf tissue was collected from control plants, Pip-supplemented plants, or plants inoculated with bacteria. Then, collected material was frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground with a mortar and pestle with the addition of a small amount of CaCO3, and placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf. Homogenates were suspended in pure acetone and centrifuged at 3000 × g at 4˚С for 10 min. The supernatant was poured into clean Eppendorf tubes. Chlorophyll absorbance was measured at 644 and 662 nm, and carotenoid absorbance was measured at 440 nm using 100% acetone as a blank. Pigment concentration was calculated using a method from Coşkun [61], based on Wettstein [62].

Analysis of PEPC and RuBisCO LSU protein levels

Preparation of protein extracts and determination of protein concentration

To isolate fractions of soluble proteins, 1 g of fresh plant tissue was collected from leaves of control plants, supplemented with Pip or inoculated with bacteria, and homogenized at 4˚C with a mortar in a 2.5 mL buffer (17.9 g L− 1 Tricine, 0.74 g L− 1 MgSO4, 0.155 g L− 1 DTT, 1.14 g L− 1 EDTA (pH 8.0)). Non-soluble material was removed by centrifugation for 5 min at 3000 x g. The protein concentration was then determined according to Bradford [63] using the BioRad protein assay with Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) as a standard [52].

Immunoblotting- visualization of PEPC and RuBisCO LSU protein on nitrocellulose membranes

Samples containing isolated proteins were diluted in SDS loading buffer at a ratio of 3:1. The SDS loading buffer contained 200 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 400 mM DTT, 8% SDS, 40% glycerol, and 0.1% bromophenol blue. Protein denaturation was performed at 70˚C for 5 min. The same amounts of protein (5 µg) were loaded on the gel and separated by (10% gel) SDS-PAGE electrophoresis [64]. After separation, proteins were transferred using a semi-dry method (30 min, 25 V/1A) with WBB buffer (24.2 g L− 1 Tris, 112.7 g L− 1 glycine) from which two different buffers were prepared: the cathode buffer (20 mL 10x WBB, 1 mL 20% SDS, distilled water to 200 mL), and the anode buffer (10 mL 10x WBB, 30 mL methanol, distilled water to 100 mL) (PVDF membranes (0.45 μm), Bio-Rad Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System- Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

After protein transfer, the membranes were washed with TBS (Tris Buffered Saline) buffer (pH 7.5, 10 mM Tris, 0.9% (w/v) NaCl) for 5 min and blocked with TBS milk (10 g low-fat milk powder, 500 mL TBS) for 1.5 h at room temperature (RT) with agitation. Next, the membranes were washed twice for 5 min with a TBS buffer at RT with agitation, and then they were incubated in the primary antibody (Anti-PEPC (AS09 458), dilution 1:1000; or Anti-RbcL (AS03 037), 1:1000, Agrisera, Vännäs, Sweden) at 4˚C overnight.

The membranes were washed four times for 5 min with a TBS buffer. Then they were incubated with the secondary antibody (HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H&L (AS09 602), dilution 1:5000, Agrisera, Vännäs, Sweden) for 1.5 h at RT with agitation. The membranes were then washed two times for 5 min in TBS buffer, and four times for 5 min in AP buffer (Alkaline Phosphate buffer with pH 9.5, 12.1 g L− 1 Tris, 5.81 g L− 1 NaCl, 1.02 g L− 1 MgCl2). The protein bands were visualized using the staining solution (BCIP/NBT tablets (5- bromo- 4- chloro-3-indolyl phosphate/nitroblue tetrazolium) in AP buffer) (Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland). Dilutions of the antibodies were selected based on the previous optimization process and the protocol of the manufacturer.

Determination of PEPC and RuBisCO LSU protein amount visualized on the nitrocellulose membranes

Membranes after immunostaining of PEPC or RuBisCO LSU proteins were scanned with the Epson Perfection V700 Photo scanner (Epson, America). The intensity of the bands corresponding to PEPC or RuBisCO LSU amount was determined through densitometric analyses of visualised bands using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

The values are presented as arbitrary units (A.U.), corresponding to the area under the densitometric curves. Three biological replicates were used for analyses, and three measurements were performed on each replicate.

Assay of RuBisCO activity

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase activity was analysed according to the method described by Jurczyk et al. [65] based on the method of Sharkey et al. [66]. Leaf samples (known leaf area − 4cm2) collected from control plants, Pip supplemented plants, and inoculated with bacteria plants were homogenized at 4˚С in 2 mL of extraction buffer (5 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM Bicine pH 7.8, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.002% BSA (w/v)) with the addition of insoluble PVPP and centrifuged at 16.000 x g for 1 min.

The obtained crude extract was further used to measure RuBisCO activity by the spectrophotometric method. The procedure of this enzyme activity determination is based on a coupled enzyme assay in which CO₂ fixation by RuBisCO leads to the formation of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA) from ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). The resulting 3-PGA is converted to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate through a coupled reaction system, during which NADH is oxidized to NAD⁺. The rate of decrease in absorbance at 340 nm, corresponding to NADH oxidation, was used as a measure of RuBisCO activity. Assays were performed in 50 mM Bicine buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1 mM EDTA, 15 mM MgCl₂, 18.5 mM NaCl, 9.2 mM DTT, 0.6 mM RuBP, 9.2 mM NaHCO₃, 0.4 mM NADH, 0.5 mM ATP, and 4.6 mM phosphocreatine, supplemented with phosphocreatine kinase (1.3 U), phosphoglycerate kinase (47 U), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Reactions were initiated by the addition of crude leaf extract and monitored at 340 nm for 2 min using an Ultrospec 2100 pro spectrophotometer (Amersham Biosciences, Sweden).

Determination of selected leaf metabolite levels by GC/MS

To assess metabolic responses to bacterial treatment, chromatographic analysis was used to quantify the endogenous levels of selected metabolites, including Pip, NHP, SA, and SA glucose conjugates. They were quantified exclusively in non-supplemented plants to avoid confounding effects arising from exogenous Pip application; exogenously supplied Pip contributes directly to the total Pip pool, its inclusion would preclude discrimination between endogenous biosynthesis and externally derived compound, thereby compromising interpretation of metabolite homeostasis. Accordingly, only non-supplemented Pip plants were used for absolute quantification of endogenous content of selected metabolites. Metabolite analysis was performed by gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) following tissue extraction of lyophilized M. crystallinum leaf samples and analyte derivatisation by trimethylsilylation, as previously described for the analysis of shock-frozen leaf material of Arabidopsis thaliana [45]. Briefly, ~ 15 mg of lyophilized leaf sample was extracted twice with 1 ml of MeOH/50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 6.0) (80:20, v/v). In the first extraction step, the extraction buffer was supplemented with 1 µg of deuterium-labelled Pip and SA (D9-Pip and D4-SA) as internal standards [41]. Aliquots of 600 µl of the combined extracts were evaporated to dryness in a vacuum centrifuge. Then, 20 µl of pyridine, 20 µl of N-methyl-N-trimethyl-silyltrifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) containing 1% trimethylchlorosilane (v/v), and 60 µl of hexane were added, and the sample mixture was incubated at 70˚С for 30 min. Finally, 2 µl of the sample was analyzed by GC-MS with an Agilent 7890 A/5975 C system equipped with a Phenomenex ZB-35 (30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 μm) capillary column and MSD ChemStation software version E.02.01.1177 (Agilent Technologies). The temperature of the GC oven was set as follows: 70˚С for 2 min, gradient to 250˚С at 10˚С /min, gradient to 260˚С at 2˚С/min, gradient to 320˚С at 10˚С/min, and 320˚С for 3 min. For the quantitative determination of analytes, selected ion chromatograms were used: Pip (m/z 156), D9-Pip (m/z 165), SA (m/z 267), D4-SA (m/z 271). For absolute quantification, peak areas of Pip (SA) were related to those of D9-Pip (D4-SA) and to the dry weight of the leaf samples. Experimentally determined correction factors for the analyte/internal standard pairs were considered in the calculation.

Prolonged Pip supplementation

In a separate set of experiments, prolonged Pip supplementation was conducted by applying 10 mL of 1 mM Pip every 24 h for 10 consecutive days into the soil of pot-cultured M. crystallinum plants. Four-week-old C3 plants were supplemented with 1 mM Pip solution to verify whether the metabolism of these plants could be changed from C3 to CAM by Pip supplementation alone. The potential change in metabolism was verified using pH indicator papers (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) to determine the acidity of cell sap collected from the leaves of the studied plants after 10 days of Pip supplementation in the morning (9:00 a.m.) and evening (9:00 p.m.). The concentration of malate in the cell sap using the spectrophotometric method was also studied [67]. Daily differences in PEPC protein levels were determined using the methodology as described in the next paragraphs. Differences in the analysed parameters in samples collected at dusk and dawn indicated a switch from C3 to CAM metabolism.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses (two-way ANOVA, post-hoc Duncan’s tests) were conducted using Statistica 13.1 software (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA). The significance of differences was calculated with Duncan’s multiple range test at a 0.05 probability level (values marked with the same letters did not significantly differ). The Tukey test was additionally performed to confirm the significance pattern; the test yielded the same conclusions.

Data from control and bacterial-treated plants were analyzed separately. Therefore, two-way ANOVA was performed independently for each of those groups, with type of metabolism and Pip-supplementation as fixed factors.

Results

To investigate the effect of pipecolic acid (Pip) on key physiological parameters of M. crystallinum during the C3 and CAM stages and under biotic stress caused by Pseudomonas syringae, plants from both metabolic groups, either with or without 1 mM Pip supplementation, were compared under non-bacteria inoculation conditions as well as after bacterial exposure, including plants treated with both Pip and bacteria.

Gas exchange parameters

Differences in gas exchange parameters, particularly transpiration (E) and net photosynthesis (PN), were observed in both metabolic groups (C3 and CAM) following supplementation with Pip and after bacterial treatment. In plants not treated with bacteria, Pip supplementation affected PN only in the C3 group (increase of PN) (Fig. 1A). Simultaneously, E statistically significantly increased only in the CAM control group, with no detectable effect in the C3 group after Pip supplementation (Fig. 1C).

A, C, E -values for plants not treated with bacteria, and B, D, F-values for plants 3 days post-bacterial treatment (pbt). According to ANOVA, means (n = 6 per treatment ± SE) values marked with the same letters (within each cultivar separately) are not significantly different (Duncan test, p ≤ 0.05)

Despite the observation of lower values of PN and E parameters in all the groups treated with bacteria, compared to the control groups, during bacterial treatment Pip-supplemented plants from both photosynthetic types maintained higher PN values compared to non-supplemented ones (Fig. 1B). However, by 3rd day post-bacterial treatment (pbt), E levels in Pip-supplemented C3 plants were lower than in their non-supplemented counterparts. In contrast, in CAM plants, transpiration rates remained unchanged by Pip supplementation (Fig. 1D).

To further quantify these responses, WUE was calculated. In control conditions, Pip led to a statistically non-significant increase in WUE in C3 plants, but a decrease in CAM plants (Fig. 1E). Notably, under Pip supplementation, the WUE of C3 plants reached a level on par with that of CAM plants, suggesting that Pip application triggered a physiological shift in C3 plants toward a CAM-like state. Although Pip initially reduced WUE in the CAM control group, on the 3rd day pbt, both C3 and CAM plants supplemented with Pip exhibited statistically significantly higher WUE values compared to non-supplemented ones. This highlights the positive effect of Pip on gas exchange efficiency, particularly under biotic stress conditions (Fig. 1F).

Stomata measurements

Measurements conducted on leaf tissue preparations from four independent plant cultivations (all plant cultivations were carried out under the same conditions described in Materials and methods 2.1) allowed us to identify consistent trends regarding the impact of Pip supplementation on stomatal apparatus dimensions. Although slight variability in stomatal size was observed across cultivations, the overall trends remained consistent.

Data from all independent cultivations revealed no statistically significant effects of Pip supplementation on C3 stomatal length and width (Fig. 2A). In contrast, CAM plants exhibited measurable changes in stomatal dimensions following Pip supplementation, with a specifically Pip-induced increase in stomatal length (Fig. 2A). Additional measurements were taken from plants 3 days pbt, with or without previous Pip supplementation. In the C3 group, after bacterial inoculation and in the presence of Pip, stomatal length increased, whereas width decreased. In CAM plants, neither stomatal parameter showed statistically significant differences in the context of Pip supplementation after bacterial exposure (Fig. 2B).

Size of stomata [µm] presented as length or width in A control plants, and B plants 3 days post-bacterial treatment (pbt). According to ANOVA, means (n = 4 per treatment ± SE) values marked with the same letters are not significantly different (Duncan test, p ≤ 0.05)

When considered alongside the gas exchange data (Results 3.1), a few notable relationships emerge. In bacterial-exposed C3 plants, Pip supplementation led to a statistically significant increase in stomatal length but a concurrent reduction in stomatal width by the 3rd day pbt. This reduction in width reflects stomatal closure. Consequently, this narrow aperture restricted water loss, directly linking the modified stomatal morphology to the observed decrease in transpiration rate under biotic stress. In contrast, a different dynamic was observed in CAM plants (unexposed to bacterial inoculation). In these plants, Pip supplementation was associated with an enlargement of stomatal length and a concurrent increase in transpiration rates. This indicates that under non-stress conditions in CAM plants, the Pip-induced expansion of stomatal dimensions directly facilitated greater water loss. These morphological changes are visually supported by the microscopy images (Supplementary Information Appendix S5).

Fast kinetics of chlorophyll a fluorescence

Analysis of fast chlorophyll a fluorescence kinetics indicated that neither Pip supplementation nor bacterial treatment caused significant changes in key photosynthetic parameters in either C3 or CAM plants. Although minor variations were observed in some parameters, further detailed analysis revealed that these differences lacked biological significance. In particular, no meaningful effects were observed on the maximum quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (Fv/Fm) (Supplementary Information Appendix S7, parts A and B). Small fluctuations were recorded in control plants following Pip supplementation rather than in response to bacterial treatment. For instance, slight changes in the relative proportion of closed PSII reaction centres at the J-step of fluorescence induction (VJ) parameter were observed (Supplementary Information Appendix S7, parts C and D); however, these alterations did not affect overall photosynthetic performance. Collectively, these findings indicate that neither Pip supplementation nor bacterial exposure adversely affected PSII photochemistry or primary photochemical reactions. The absence of significant changes in chlorophyll a fluorescence parameters, despite observed physiological differences, can be explained by the high stability and strong regulatory capacity of the photosynthetic apparatus.

Chlorophyll a, b, and carotenoid content

Pip supplementation influenced the levels of photosynthetic pigments: chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids in both C3 and CAM plants. In control plants, Pip supplementation led to an overall increase in the content of all measured pigments, with chlorophyll showing the most significant enhancement (Fig. 3A). This suggests that Pip may promote photosynthetic pigments biosynthesis.

According to ANOVA, means (n = 3 per treatment ± SE) values marked with the same letters (within each cultivar separately) are not significantly different (Duncan test, p ≤ 0.05)

By 3 days pbt, plants in the C3 group that were not supplemented with Pip showed a statistically significant increase in pigment content compared to plants not treated with bacteria, possibly reflecting a stress-induced compensatory response, where pigment accumulation may help maintain photosynthetic performance or provide photoprotection. In contrast, pigment levels declined in Pip-supplemented C3 plants at the same time, despite their initially elevated baseline. It may indicate early priming of protective mechanisms, whereas the subsequent decline suggests a disruption or feedback regulation in pigment maintenance under prolonged bacterial exposure (Fig. 3B). In CAM plants, pigment levels in the non-Pip-supplemented group remained relatively stable at 3 days pbt relative to plants not exposed to bacteria, whereas Pip-supplemented plants exhibited a marked decline in chlorophylls and carotenoids, consistent with the pattern observed in the C3 group (Fig. 3B).

These observations suggest that Pip initially promotes the accumulation of photosynthetic pigments in both C3 and CAM plants, potentially enhancing photosynthetic potential under non-stress conditions. However, as the bacteria treatment lasts, this effect diminishes or reverses. The subsequent decline in pigment levels in Pip-supplemented plants during bacterial exposure may reflect an accelerated shift toward stress responses such as energy conservation, senescence, or redirection of metabolic resources, ultimately impacting the plant’s ability to sustain photosynthesis under prolonged biotic stress.

Changes in RuBisCO LSU and PEPC protein levels

The analysis of RuBisCO LSU and PEPC protein content revealed coordinated yet distinct responses to Pip supplementation and bacterial treatment in both C3 and CAM plants. In control plants, Pip supplementation statistically significantly reduced RuBisCO LSU protein levels in both metabolic groups (Fig. 4A), while simultaneously increasing PEPC accumulation (Fig. 5A). This inverse relationship between RuBisCO LSU abundance and enzyme activity (Results 3.6) suggests that Pip may enhance RuBisCO efficiency by creating cellular conditions that promote post-translational modifications or activation mechanisms of the enzyme, compensating for the lower protein amount.

During bacterial treatment, RuBisCO LSU level in C3 plants was higher in plants previously supplemented with Pip, and plants without previous supplementation showed a statistically significant decrease compared to the control. The same tendency was observed in the CAM plant group; higher level of LSU during bacterial treatment in the group previously supplemented with Pip (Fig. 4B). Similarly, 3 days pbt, PEPC protein levels, in both metabolic groups (C3 and CAM), were higher in the previously Pip supplemented plants; reaching higher levels in CAM than in C3 plants (Fig. 5B).

The coordinated modulation of RuBisCO LSU and PEPC protein levels by Pip likely enhances carbon fixation capacity, especially under stress. Elevated PEPC in CAM plants may improve nocturnal CO₂ capture, increasing CO₂ availability around RuBisCO during the day, while in C3 plants, enhanced PEPC supports anaplerotic metabolism, aiding stress resilience. Together with the sustained or increased RuBisCO activity, these changes support the maintenance of photosynthetic carbon assimilation, aligning with observed gas exchange and stomatal adjustments, and ultimately contributing to improved water use efficiency and tolerance to bacteria.

Changes in RuBisCO activity

Measurement of RuBisCO enzyme activity across all four experimental variants revealed that Pip supplementation significantly influenced enzyme activity in both C3 and CAM plants. In control plants, Pip supplementation resulted in more than a twofold increase in RuBisCO activity in both metabolic types (Fig. 6A), despite a previously observed decrease in the abundance of the RuBisCO LSU protein in these plants (Results 3.5). This discrepancy suggests that post-translational modifications or changes in enzyme activation state may enhance RuBisCO catalytic efficiency independently of protein abundance, a hypothesis explored further later in this study.

By 3 days pbt, RuBisCO activity remained at similar levels in the tested groups as without bacterial treatment, except for the C3 group without Pip supplementation (decrease in activity). Additionally, thus supporting the conclusion from the control groups that the plants that were Pip supplemented (both C3 and CAM) maintained higher RuBisCO activity compared to their non-supplemented counterparts, and after treatment with bacteria, this tendency was maintained (Fig. 6B).

Determination of leaf metabolite levels by GC/MS

Among the targeted metabolites, only Pip and SA were detected at quantifiable levels in the analysed samples. Measurements were performed on four variants of plants; non Pip-supplemented control plants from both C3 and CAM metabolic groups at baseline (untreated) and three days post-bacterial treatment (Fig. 7). The basal levels of Pip were clearly detectable in both C3 and CAM plants, with CAM plants exhibiting markedly higher basal Pip levels than C3 plants. Moreover, Pip levels increased significantly at 3 days pbt in both groups compared to their respective controls, with a particularly pronounced rise in CAM plants (Fig. 7). Overall, CAM plants showed a more robust Pip accumulation, which may indicate their inherently greater stress resilience. In contrast, the basal levels of SA were low in the leaves of both C3 and CAM plants. Further, the levels of SA did not increase in CAM plants and increased only marginally, although statistically significantly, in C3 plants following bacterial inoculation (Fig. 8).

Long-term Pip supplementation of C3 plants

Long-term supplementation with Pip of M. crystallinum plants at the C3 stage affects the diurnal variation in leaf cell sap acidity and malate concentration. Lower pH and higher malate levels, after supplementation, were observed in the morning compared to the evening, suggesting that CO₂ fixation occurs during the night, followed by its conversion to malate and storage in vacuoles (Supplementary Information Appendix S8, parts A and B). Analysis of PEPC levels revealed daily fluctuations in this enzyme in the leaves of Pip-supplemented plants. The increased abundance of PEPC in the evening confirms the plant’s capacity for nocturnal CO₂ fixation, contributing to malate synthesis and its accumulation during the night (Supplementary Information Appendix S8, parts C and D).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that exogenous Pip differentially modulates photosynthetic performance in C3 and CAM Mesembryanthemum crystallinum, affecting gas exchange, carbon metabolism, and immune-associated physiological responses during bacterial challenge.

Analysis of gas-exchange parameters revealed that the effects of Pip on physiological performance is clearly dependent on the photosynthetic mode (C3 or CAM). Under control conditions, Pip increased PN in C3 plants without statistically significantly affecting E despite reduced stomatal aperture (Figs. 1 and 2), suggesting enhanced photosynthetic efficiency beyond stomatal regulation. In contrast, CAM plants showed increased E without changes in PN (Results 3.1). These responses are consistent with the contrasting carbon assimilation and water-use strategies of C3 and CAM metabolism, where C3 plants typically exhibit higher photosynthetic rates, whereas CAM plants prioritize water conservation [68–73]. During bacterial challenge, Pip supplementation maintained higher PN in plants representing both C3 and CAM photosynthetic pathways. However, transpiration responses were pathway-dependent: Pip reduced E in C3 plants, whereas E remained stable in CAM plants, suggesting differential regulation of gas exchange and water-use strategies associated with distinct photosynthetic mechanisms. These functional responses were accompanied by changes in stomatal morphology. Several previous studies have demonstrated that stomatal traits are shaped by the combined effects of abiotic environmental factors and pathogen-mediated selective pressures, and their morphology plays a critical role in stress adaptation and stress responses, with stomatal size and density influencing both water-use efficiency and stress tolerance [74–78]. In our studies, Pip supplementation of C3 plants did not statistically significantly affect stomatal size under control conditions, whereas CAM plants showed increased stomatal length associated with higher transpiration (Figs. 1 and 2). Under bacterial challenge, Pip-treated C₃ plants exhibited increased stomatal length and reduced stomatal width, accompanied by lower transpiration (Results 3.2). However, because stomatal conductance was not significantly affected (data not shown), the reduced transpiration cannot be attributed solely to changes in stomatal morphology. In contrast, stomatal traits in CAM plants remained largely unchanged under bacterial stress, consistent with their stable transpiration and conservative water-use strategy. Together, these findings indicate that Pip induces photosynthetic-state-dependent stomatal plasticity, with morphological responses to bacterial challenge occurring predominantly in C3 plants.

Chlorophylls and carotenoids are essential components of the photosynthetic apparatus, contributing to light harvesting, energy transfer, and photoprotection, and their abundance is often modulated during plant responses to environmental stress and pathogen attack [79–81]. In the present study, Pip supplementation increased pigment content under control conditions (Results 3.4), suggesting adjustments in the photosynthetic antenna system. However, these changes were not accompanied by alterations in chlorophyll a fluorescence parameters or fluorescence induction kinetics in either C3 or CAM plants (Results 3.3), indicating that PSII photochemical efficiency remained stable following Pip supplementation and bacterial challenge. The maintenance of PSII function despite increased pigment content is consistent with the inherent plasticity of the photosynthetic apparatus, which preserves excitation energy balance through coordinated photoprotective mechanisms [82–85]. Similar pigment accumulation has been reported as an adaptive response supporting photosynthetic performance under stress in other plant species [86, 87]. Summarising, these findings suggest that Pip promotes acclimatory changes in pigment composition and carbon metabolism while maintaining stable PSII photochemistry, thereby enhancing photosynthetic plasticity rather than directly affecting the primary photochemical reactions.

Coordinated changes in key carbon fixation enzymes indicate that Pip influences photosynthetic metabolism through regulation of enzyme activity rather than protein abundance alone. The contrasting responses between RuBisCO large subunit (LSU) abundance (Fig. 4) and RuBisCO activity (Fig. 6; Results 3.5, 3.6) suggest that carbon fixation capacity is controlled at multiple regulatory levels. RuBisCO activity depends not only on enzyme abundance but also on activation state, carbamylation, substrate availability, and interactions with regulatory proteins such as RuBisCO activase [88–91]. Therefore, differences between RuBisCO content and activity likely reflect changes in enzyme activation or post-translational regulation rather than alterations in protein quantity alone [92, 93].

In M. crystallinum, the transition between C3 and CAM metabolism requires coordinated regulation of PEPC- and RuBisCO-mediated carbon assimilation through changes in enzyme abundance, activity, and metabolic flux [94, 95]. PEPC is essential for CAM by enabling nocturnal CO₂ fixation and is regulated through reversible phosphorylation according to metabolic demands [94]. In C3 plants, PEPC primarily contributes to anaplerotic carbon metabolism and metabolic adjustment under changing environmental conditions [96, 97]. The increase in PEPC abundance (Fig. 5), together with altered RuBisCO activity in Pip-treated plants, suggests that Pip influences carbon allocation and metabolic flexibility in a photosynthetic-state-dependent manner.

Importantly, prolonged Pip supplementation of C3-stage M. crystallinum induced CAM-like characteristics, including nocturnal CO2 fixation, malate accumulation, and increased evening PEPC abundance (Supplementary Information Appendix S8). These findings indicate that Pip can promote photosynthetic reprogramming beyond short-term physiological adjustments. Similar roles for PEPC-mediated metabolic flexibility have been reported in other species, where regulation of PEPC expression contributes to drought tolerance in Dendrobium and expression of CAM-type PEPC enhances salt and drought resilience in C3 plants [98, 99]. Together, these results suggest that Pip modulates primary carbon metabolism and promotes adaptive metabolic flexibility, linking defence signalling with photosynthetic acclimation in both C3 and CAM plants [40, 100].

Although the present study demonstrates Pip-associated changes in carbon fixation capacity, the precise mechanisms remain unresolved. Future isoform-specific transcriptomic and proteomic analyses will be required to determine whether Pip regulates these pathways through transcriptional control, post-translational modification, or changes in enzyme turnover. The role of Pip in plant immunity has been extensively characterized in A. thaliana and several crop species, where it functions as a key regulator of systemic acquired resistance (SAR), defence amplification, and immune priming [29, 39, 101–103]. In A. thaliana, pathogen-induced accumulated Pip is converted into the immune-active derivative NHP, which acts as a mobile signal promoting systemic defence responses. NHP activity is further regulated through metabolic modifications, including reversible glycosylation, which contributes to balancing immune activation and growth maintenance [104]. In contrast, NHP remained below the detection limit in M. crystallinum under both C3 and CAM conditions. This may indicate that NHP does not accumulate to detectable levels in this species under the conditions tested. Its concentration may have remained below the sensitivity of the GC-MS method employed, preventing reliable detection. Although this does not exclude transient accumulation, rapid turnover, or tissue-specific NHP production, the strong accumulation of Pip suggests that Pip metabolism in M. crystallinum may differ from the canonical Pip-NHP pathway described in Arabidopsis. SA levels showed only minor changes following bacterial inoculation, with a slight increase observed in C3 plants but not in CAM plants. In many plant species, strong accumulation of SA and NHP is associated with immune activation during pathogen challenge [41, 44]. The moderate disease symptoms observed in the M. crystallinum-P. syringae interaction, together with the high stress tolerance and metabolic flexibility of this species [105–107], may explain the limited activation of the SA-NHP amplification pathway. However, the differential SA responses between C3 and CAM plants indicate that photosynthetic state influences immune signalling, potentially through differences in carbon allocation, redox regulation, and primary metabolism.

Previous studies have shown that Pip acts upstream of SA accumulation during SAR while also activating SA-independent defence pathways [40, 102, 103]. Consistent with these findings, Pip accumulated in both C3 and CAM plants following bacterial infection, with stronger accumulation in CAM plants (Fig. 7), whereas SA responses differed between photosynthetic states (Fig. 8). Together, these results suggest that Pip-mediated defence in M. crystallinum involves conserved immune functions combined with species- and metabolism-dependent regulation. The strong endogenous accumulation of Pip, together with the effects of Pip supplementation on photosynthetic physiology and carbon metabolism, supports a role for Pip as an interface between immune signalling and metabolic adjustment. This interpretation is supported by studies of Arabidopsis ald1 mutants, in which disruption of Pip/NHP biosynthesis affects both defence responses and photosynthetic physiology following bacterial challenge [40].

Furthermore, our previous study demonstrated that Pip supplementation exerts a multifaceted influence on the antioxidant system, helping to mitigate the effects of oxidative stress induced by bacterial infection. This was evidenced by higher total antioxidant activity and elevated activity of the enzyme superoxide dismutase, as well as increased levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as proline and glutathione, especially in the C3 metabolic type of M. crystallinum [51]. These responses are consistent with the defence priming model proposed by Bernsdorff et al. [40], in which Pip promotes ROS-scavenging capacity to limit oxidative damage during immune activation. Together, the present and previous findings support a broader role for Pip in coordinating defence activation, redox homeostasis, and photosynthetic metabolism across contrasting photosynthetic states. Future studies should elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Pip-mediated regulation of RuBisCO activity and its potential interaction with stomatal signalling, while evaluating the effects of Pip across diverse crop species exposed to combined biotic and abiotic stresses. Although a chromosome-level genome assembly is now available for M. crystallinum [108], functional annotation of photosynthesis-related gene families remains incomplete, and the presence of multiple isoforms together with the highly dynamic CAM transcriptional programme complicates isoform-specific analyses. Consequently, comprehensive time-course transcriptomic and proteomic approaches will be required to resolve the molecular basis of Pip-dependent regulation of photosynthetic metabolism. Overall, our findings identify Pip as a promising regulatory molecule linking immune signalling with photosynthetic acclimation, highlighting its potential as a target for improving stress resilience while maintaining carbon assimilation.

Conclusion

Our study identifies Pip as a regulator connecting defence responses with photosynthetic acclimation in C3 and CAM M. crystallinum. Beyond its established role in systemic acquired resistance, Pip modulated physiological and metabolic responses to bacterial challenge in a photosynthetic-state-dependent manner. In C3 plants, Pip enhanced photosynthetic performance and intrinsic water-use efficiency while altering stomatal traits and pigment composition, whereas CAM plants exhibited distinct physiological adjustments consistent with their contrasting carbon and water-use strategies. Pip-induced changes in carbon fixation, including altered RuBisCO activity and PEPC abundance, together with stable PSII photochemistry, indicate that Pip primarily promotes metabolic adjustment rather than direct modification of photochemical efficiency. Differences in endogenous Pip accumulation and salicylic acid responses between C3 and CAM plants further highlight the influence of photosynthetic metabolism on immune regulation. Although NHP was not detected under the conditions examined, the observed responses suggest that Pip-associated signalling contributes to the coordination of defence, redox balance, and photosynthetic plasticity through canonical and/or alternative pathways. Overall, these findings reveal Pip as a potential integrator of immune activation and metabolic flexibility, enhancing stress acclimation across contrasting photosynthetic states.
