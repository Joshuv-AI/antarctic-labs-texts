EndoEx: Benchtop and cadaveric evaluation of a novel endograft removal device
Authors: Brandon Look Fong, Rishik Puppala, Zain Moin, Alayne Morrel, Maham Rahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm repair has improved short-term patient outcomes as compared with open techniques. However, endograft complications, particularly infection and endoleaks, may necessitate the removal of the graft and fixation spines. Current explanation techniques are limited, often relying on repurposed tools like modified syringes, which can damage the aortic wall or nearby vessels during removal and lead to serious complications, such as aortic dissection, renal injury, and catastrophic bleeding, with mortality rates of up to 30%. In response, the EndoEx device was developed, designed specifically for safe and controlled graft removal, especially regarding the suprarenal fixation spines. The device uses specific geometry and features designed to limit the mechanical damage associated with vessel trauma and improve ease of use. This study compared EndoEx with the current standard of care, a modified syringe, through benchtop hydrogel testing, ex vivo bovine aorta models, and cadaveric explantation of suprarenal Cook stent grafts. EndoEx showed a smaller damaged surface area in benchtop hydrogel testing as compared with the modified syringe (0 ± 0 cm2 vs 3.97 ± 0.2 cm2), elimination of the snow-plowing effect during animal tissue testing, and less gross damage during cadaveric explantation. This study demonstrates that the EndoEx device significantly reduces mechanical damage in experimental, animal tissue, and preliminary human cadaveric models. This reduction in damage may confer a reduction in aortic wall injury during graft explantation, potentially leading to a safer and more effective solution that may substantially decrease morbidity and mortality in complex explant procedures of endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm repair devices.

Recent movement toward endovascular methods of abdominal aortic aneurysm repair (EVAR) over an open surgical approach has significantly improved 30-day and 6-month patient outcomes.1 However, EVAR introduces a risk of long-term complications.1 Notably, endovascular stent grafts used to manage abdominal aneurysms pose risks of graft infection (0.2%-5.0%)2 and endoleaks (19.0%)3 caused by feeding vessels. These complications are associated with substantial mortality (11%)4 and morbidity (37%).5 Type Ia endoleaks typically require urgent management, and type II endoleaks are particularly challenging to manage. Various embolization techniques have been developed to control these leaks,6 but failed management leads to surgical explantation,7,8 which is complicated by the suprarenal fixation struts used to stabilize the graft.9 Additionally, with infection, removal of the fixation struts may be preferred, depending on institutional preference. Removing the struts during explantation can be associated with aortic dissection and tearing, ultimately leading to catastrophic bleeding, as a primary complication, in addition to permanent dialysis owing to injury to renal arteries.7,10 Postoperative complications are associated with a mortality rate of up to 30%.9 Consequently, multiple investigators over various institutions have explored device solutions to aid in endograft removal, such as repurposing surgical syringes5 or proctoscopes.10 These devices highlight the lack of available instruments to remove endografts safely and the need to minimize damage to the aortic wall during EVAR device explantation.10

The current standard of care involves the off-label use of surgical syringes, wherein the hub of a 20- to 50-mL syringe is removed, yielding a hollow cylinder (Fig 1). The cylinder is advanced around the limbs of the endograft and rotated upward to collapse the body of the endograft and both dislodge and cover the fixation hooks. Once the hooks are successfully sheathed within the lumen of the cylinder, the syringe-endograft assembly is retracted and removed as one unit. The leading edge of the syringe can cause dissection flap creation into renal arteries, resulting in kidney failure or superior mesenteric artery collapse leading to catastrophic mesenteric ischemia.Fig 1Current standard of care.

Current standard of care.

A propriety device to solve this issue, the EndoEx, has been prototyped and developed. This device uses a two-part hinging assembly for ease of attachment around the endograft, a ball bearing-based proximal edge for mechanical force reduction, 5-mm ball bearings, a conical geometry at the leading edge for collapsing the endograft, and an ergonomic thumb style grip for ease of use during the operation with maintained dexterity. The internal lumen of the device measures 7 mm in diameter, with the outer body ranging from 8 to 22 mm.

The device requires a four-step process for the removal of an endograft. Initially, the device is prepared by opening the securement pin and expanding the device. Then, the device is positioned beneath the endograft and secured around it, just above the legs, while simultaneously compressing the body of the endograft to fit within the lumen of EndoEx (Fig 2, A). The securement pin is then reengaged with the EndoEx securely attached. The device is advanced toward the fixation spines, while simultaneously using the conical geometry of the device to facilitate the collapse of the incoming endograft (Fig 2, B). As the device moves distal to the fixation spines, the conical design begins to dislodge the spines and, with further advancement, encloses them within the device's lumen (Fig 2, C). Finally, the EndoEx-endograft assembly is extracted as a single unit (Fig 2, D).Fig 2EndoEx functions. Device attachment (A), Advancement (B), Capture (C), and Retraction (D).

EndoEx functions. Device attachment (A), Advancement (B), Capture (C), and Retraction (D).

This novel tool for endograft removal greatly reduces risk of aortic dissection and tears in the aortic wall from the struts of endograft. Therefore, this device improves morbidity and mortality associated with EVAR device explantation procedures. This study analyzes and quantifies the damage associated with endograft explantation using the novel EndoEx method compared with the current syringe method.

Methods

This study was designed to analyze the mechanical force between the explantation tool and endothelial lining. To standardize trials, a testing apparatus was created (Fig 3). This device uses two linear actuators and a servo to replicate the mechanical forces encountered during explantation procedures. The horizontal actuator advances the endothelial sample against the explantation tool secured on the vertical axis through the electronic servo. The platform controls the speed, angle, force, and distance traveled for each tool during each trial. Additionally, an Arduino microprocessor is used for automation and repeatably of trials; both explantation tools are placed with the leading edge 16 mm ahead of the mount to reduce variability in pressure and maintain a consistent angle between trials.Fig 3Linear testing platform.

Linear testing platform.

Initial trials used simulated endothelium comprising a hydrogel base. Agar, gelatin, and silicon were analyzed at different concentrations to determine the most accurate composition for simulation of a diseased aorta. This concentration was subjectively determined based on expert opinions of vascular surgeons. Different percentages of each substance were placed in wells and brought to a vascular surgeon in a blinded format. The consensus of the most representative to the endothelial lining was the 5% agar. These 5% gels were then prepared in 9 × 23 × 3 mm three-dimensional printed polylactic acid (PLA) testing wells. To reduce the agar shrinking and maintain full contact with either device, different volumes of gels were placed in the test well. In the end, 9 mL of 5% agar was determined to be the optimal volume and was used for all subsequent trials. Gels were mass prepared in batches of 20 and allowed to rest at 4°C for 3 hours before mechanical testing. Gels exhibiting surface bubbling or shrinkage resulting in delamination from the walls of the PLA wells were excluded from the study.

Mechanical force testing between the simulated endothelium gels and explantation tool began with placing the well into a secured position on the horizontal actuator of the testing apparatus. The program then executed a homing sequence to position the explantation tool, secured on the vertical actuator, into the same position on each test. The servo motor then set the angle of each tool to 20° relative to the horizontal platform, and the tool was lowered to contact the surface of the gel. Each test was done with a constant amount of pressure. Once the tool was placed at the correct height and pressure, the horizontal platform performed a sweep at 5 cm/s for a distance of 15 cm. After the sweep was complete, the explantation tool was returned to the home position, and the horizontal plate was moved away from the tool to allow switching of the gel samples.

Later trials comprised of ex vivo mechanical testing on harvested bovine aorta (Animal Technologies). These aortic tissues were cut into 25 × 20 mm samples, shaped to a uniform thickness of 2 mm, and stretched across a three-dimensional-printed PLA plate developed to interface with the testing platform and uniform tension across the sample. Once prepared, the tissue samples underwent the same sweep pattern as described elsewhere in this article (Fig 4). Some of the trials were recorded for video analysis of the leading edge with the endothelium.Fig 4Experimental Platform Setup and function.

Experimental Platform Setup and function.

Analysis of the simulated endothelial gels used image J to determine the surface area lost during each trial. Each gel was pictured with a ruler for scale. The gel images were then uploaded to ImageJ and the scale was set. The gel surface was magnified and the freehand selection tool was used to outline the grossly apparent damage (Fig 5).Fig 5Surface area analysis of damaged simulated endothelium.

Finally, trials of EndoEx and the syringe method were performed on a human cadaveric model (Fig 6). The model was procured, and the experiments were performed. Here, the abdominal aorta was exposed and a Cook stent graft (Cook Medical) with suprarenal fixation was placed at the level of infra renal aorta. The EndoEx device was used to explant the graft, and subsequent damage was grossly analyzed. This section of the abdominal aorta was removed, and the process was repeated for the syringe method.Fig 6Cadaveric explantation of stent using the EndoEx device.

Results

There were 17 trials conducted on agar for the EndoEx device and 16 trials conducted on agar for the syringe. Three of the EndoEx trials and two of the syringe trials were discarded because the agar had shrunk below the wall of the well. The EndoEx mean surface area lost was 0 cm2. The standard error for the trials was 0 cm2. In contrast, the syringe mean surface area lost was 3.97 cm2. The standard error for the trials was 0.05 cm2.

These findings indicated a significant difference in damage between using the EndoEx device compared with the syringe during an explant of an endograft from an aorta with a loss of surface area of 0 ± 0 cm2 and 3.97 ± 0.2 cm2, respectively (Fig 7).Fig 7Surface area lost graph of the EndoEx vs a syringe.

Surface area lost graph of the EndoEx vs a syringe.

Video analysis of the trials yielded a clear difference between EndoEx and the syringe with respect to the snow-plow effect. The EndoEx did not induce bunching of the endothelium ahead of the leading edge across the entire length of the samples, whereas the syringe induced significant bunching throughout the test (Fig 8).Fig 8Snowplow effect.

Snowplow effect.

Damage to the bovine endothelium is grossly apparent on photographs. Long excoriations parallel to the direction of travel were present on five of the five samples using the syringe method and none of the five using the EndoEx (Fig 9). Damage to the abdominal aorta endothelium was greatly reduced when using the EndoEx device. No excoriations or dissection was grossly present after the procedure for the EndoEx device, but fragments of scraped endothelium and a dissection flap were present after use of the syringe method (Fig 10).Fig 9Tissue damage with the EndoEx (A) vs a syringe (B).Fig 10Cadaveric tissue damage with the EndoEx (A), with no evidence of flap vs the syringe technique (B), with dissection of the flap.

Discussion

Our study showed a significant reduction of mechanical damage when using the EndoEx as compared with the syringe methodology. The addition of the ball bearings to the leading edge of the explantation device provides a significant reduction in friction, and thus mechanical forces, caused by interactions with the inner layer of the aorta and explantation tool. As shown in the gel tests, this reduction of mechanical forces is significant, with the EndoEx resulting in no gross damage of the simulated endothelium. Ex vivo tissue testing corroborated this finding, with greatly decreased gross tissue damage with the EndoEx as compared with the syringe. However, the addition of ball bearings focuses the compressive forces between the EndoEx device and endothelial surface across the smaller surface area of the individual ball bearings instead of distributing it across the larger surface area of the body of the device. This technique introduces greater compressive forces as compared with the syringe, and the EndoEx did not result in gross damage in either the gel or tissue tests. The addition of the ball bearings also reduces resistance to advancement of the device, as seen in the video analysis showing an elimination of the snow-plow effect. The features of the EndoEx device significantly reduce observed damage on the experimental models. This reduction may confer a reduction in the risk of arterial dissection with visceral aortic occlusion.

The EndoEx device's reduction in simulated aortic injury may represent drastic improvements in ease of use, aortic wall injury with suprarenal fixation tearing aortic wall, and aortic dissection causing visceral aortic branch occlusion. Additionally, we hypothesize this device may confer a reduction in morbidity and mortality owing to shorter operative and ischemic times and less blood loss associated with tears during explantation. However, this claim requires further investigation and more advance models. If true, the EndoEx method may have advantages over an alternative method of explantation, including open explantation under direct visualization.

Conclusions

The study has shown the effectiveness of the EndoEx device compared with the current standard of care in reducing mechanical damage during benchtop, animal tissue, and early cadaveric studies. The reduction in mechanical damage may translate to a reduction in visceral aortic dissection and improved safely during endograft strut removal during explantation. Additionally, this reduction in damage may improve overall morbidity and mortality rates associated with the procedure. Current study limitations include limited testing on human tissue; however, preliminary data and results on agar gel testing, in vitro animal tissue testing, and human cadaveric testing show promising results. Moreover, the cadaveric study simulated only an acute explantation; further chronic animal models are needed to simulate the inflammation and fibrosis that occur after implantation.
