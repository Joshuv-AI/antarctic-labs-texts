Short-interval longitudinal analysis of immune cell dynamics in heavy draft foals after colostrum intake
Authors: Sakura YOSHIDA, Akiko TAKEYAMA, Yosuke MAEDA, Yasuo NAMBO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal cells and antibodies are present in equine colostrum, but their relationship with immune cells in foals after colostrum ingestion remains unclear. This study longitudinally analyzed immune cells in the peripheral blood of neonatal foals at short intervals and investigated their relationship with colostral immune cells. Eleven clinically healthy draft mares and their foals were examined. Foal blood was collected before colostrum intake (0 hr) and at 2–3, 6, 12, 36–48 hr, and 3 and 7 days after ingestion. Mare blood and milk (0, 36–48 hr; 0 hr milk defined as colostrum) were also collected. Mononuclear cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using cluster of differentiation (CD) 5, CD4, CD8, CD21, CD172a, and CD45RO, with double-staining for major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II). Total leukocytes and CD4+, CD5+, CD8+, and CD45RO+ cells significantly increased during the first week of life, whereas MHC II+ CD5+, MHC II+ CD8+, and MHC II+ CD45RO+ cells increased earlier at 12 hr. CD5+, CD4+, and CD8+ cells were more abundant in colostrum than in milk. CD8+ cells were more abundant in colostrum than in blood, whereas CD21+ and CD45RO+ cells were more abundant in blood than in colostrum, with no correlation. An early increase in CD8+ MHC II+ T cells before gut closure is a novel finding and suggests possible migration of colostrum-derived CD8+ T cells into neonatal circulation.Keywords: cell-mediated immunity, colostral leukocytes, colostrum, neonatal foal, T-cell activation

INTRODUCTION

Foal loss caused by infectious diseases remains a significant concern in equine reproduction. Horses possess a diffuse epitheliochorial placenta, which prevents the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies to the fetus during pregnancy [25]. Consequently, foals are born agammaglobulinemic, lacking circulating immunoglobulin (Ig) at birth. Thus, immune protection depends entirely on the timely ingestion and absorption of colostrum after birth. Failure of passive transfer (FPT) of colostral antibodies increases susceptibility to infectious diseases, leading to high mortality and substantial economic losses due to treatment costs [15].

The adaptive immune system comprises humoral and cellular immunity. Both are essential for host defense: Ig plays an important role in humoral immunity, whereas cellular immunity eliminates infected or abnormal cells that cannot be controlled by humoral mechanisms. Previous studies of the immune components of equine colostrum have mainly focused on humoral immunity, particularly immunoglobulin G (IgG), whereas studies of cellular immunity, especially lymphocytes, are limited. Equine colostrum consists predominantly of T cells (approximately 93% of total leukocytes), with smaller populations of other immune cells, such as monocytes and B cells [29]. The proportion of cluster of differentiation (CD) 8+ T cells in colostrum is significantly higher than that in maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), whereas CD4+ T-cell levels are similar [29]. Additionally, equine colostrum contains high concentrations of T-helper 1 (Th1)- and Th17-inducing cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-17 and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), as well as inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). In contrast, Th2-type cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-10 are present at low levels [5, 29, 33]. These characteristics are similar to those observed in humans [31] and cattle [10]. Therefore, colostrum from mares is thought to enhance foal immune function through multiple mechanisms, including humoral immunity (IgG), cellular immunity (cytotoxic T lymphocytes; CTL/ Th1 cells), inflammatory responses (Th17 cells), and inflammatory cytokines [1, 4, 24].

Specialized epithelial cells in the small intestinal mucosa of neonatal foals actively and indiscriminately absorb large molecules, including IgG [17]. In cattle, pigs, and sheep, labeled maternal leukocytes in colostrum have been observed to migrate into Peyer’s patches of neonates [21, 34, 37]. Several studies have reported elevated serum IgG concentrations in foals after colostrum ingestion [3, 19, 38]. However, clear evidence of immune-cell transfer from colostrum to foals’ immune system has not yet been demonstrated. The duration of macromolecular absorption varies among species. In pigs and sheep, gut closure occurs gradually in response to cumulative macromolecular absorption, allowing relatively long absorption periods [1, 20, 28, 35]. In contrast, gut closure in foals depends primarily on time rather than macromolecule intake, and IgG absorption decreases markedly when colostrum is ingested beyond 12 hr after birth [32]. Current knowledge regarding immune cells in equine colostrum remains limited. Few studies have examined whether these cells are transferred to foals, or, if so, what roles they play in neonatal immunity. Because the opportunity for colostrum ingestion in horses is extremely brief and limited, elucidating the dynamics of colostral immune cells is essential for understanding the establishment of early immunity in foals.

Evaluating the immune memory capacity of colostral leukocytes is important but has rarely been addressed. If immune cells with high memory capacity are transferred to foals through colostrum, they may enhance neonatal immune responsiveness. CD45RO is a tyrosine phosphatase expressed on the surface of leukocytes that plays an important role in T-cell activation. CD45RO is converted from CD45RA following antigen encounter and is a known marker of memory T cells in humans [23]. Furthermore, major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) is a molecule essential for antigen presentation by antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells [26]. In humans and sheep, MHC II is also expressed on activated T cells and is therefore widely used as a marker of T-cell activation [13, 18]. In contrast, in horses, MHC II expression on T cells occurs independently of activation status [9, 22]. Its expression increases with age, suggesting that MHC II may reflect antigen exposure and could potentially serve as a marker of memory T cells [22]. However, few studies have evaluated the utility of CD45RO and MHC II as markers of memory T cells in horses. This study aimed to elucidate the role of immune cells in equine colostrum during the initial immune development of neonatal foals. The specific objectives were: (1) to characterize lymphocyte subset dynamics in foal PBMCs at short intervals following colostrum intake using flow cytometry, (2) to compare immune cell populations between colostrum ingested by foals and maternal PBMCs to assess their relationship, and (3) to evaluate CD45RO expression as a potential marker of memory cells in horses. This study provides new insights into the physiological significance of immune cells in equine colostrum.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

This study was conducted on a heavy draft horse farm in Hokkaido, Japan. Samples were collected from 11 pregnant mares (Percheron, Belgian, and Breton crossbreeds) and their foals between January and July 2024. The mean age of the mares was 8.6 years (range, 4–13 years), including eight multiparous mares (average parity 2.8) and three nulliparous mares. The mares were kept in groups of 3–4 within paddocks (20 m × 30 m) and were regularly monitored. All foaling occurred in the paddocks and was completed within 30 min without dystocia. The condition of all mares and their foals (four colts and seven fillies) was diagnosed as normal based on routine veterinary examinations. All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (approval no.24–155) and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the institution.

Sample collection

Blood samples were collected by jugular venipuncture using 20G × 1 1/2-inch needles, and approximately 20 mL of blood was dispensed into EDTA, heparin, and plain tubes. Milk samples (approximately 50 mL) were collected manually into conical tubes after thorough disinfection of the teats. Blood samples from mares were collected within 1 hr postpartum (0 hr) and again 36–48 hr after colostrum ingestion. Milk samples were also collected at the same time points: colostrum within 1 hr postpartum (before suckling, 0 hr) and milk at 36–48 hr postpartum. Blood samples from foals were collected within 1 hr of birth (before colostrum intake, 0 hr) and at 2–3, 6, 12, and 36–48 hr, as well as 3 and 7 days after first colostrum ingestion. Samples containing EDTA were used for complete blood counts (CBCs), whereas those containing heparin and all milk samples were used to determine lymphocyte subpopulations. Samples in plain tubes were incubated at 37°C for 1 hr and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min. The serum obtained was used to analyze total protein and IgG concentrations.

Sample analysis

CBCs were performed to measure the number of white blood cells (WBCs) and the counts and percentages of lymphocytes (LY, LY%), monocytes (MO, MO%), eosinophils (EO, EO%), and granulocytes (GR, GR%) using an automated hematology analyzer (Celltac-alpha MEK-6450; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). PBMCs were isolated from heparinized blood by density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, heparinized blood was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 20 min. The buffy coat was collected, diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), and layered over 4.5 mL of Lymphocyte Separation Medium 1077 (PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany). After centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 20 min and several washes with PBS, the resulting fraction was used as the PBMC fraction.

Milk samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The pH was measured using a pH meter (HALO2; Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA), and Brix values were measured using a refractometer (PAL-1, ATAGO, Tokyo, Japan). To isolate the cells, milk was diluted 4-fold with PBS and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 20 min. The pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of PBS, filtered through sterile gauze, centrifuged again, resuspended, and filtered again. The final cell suspension was washed with PBS and diluted for further analysis.

Total serum protein (TP) was measured using a TP meter (PAL-11S; ATAGO), and IgG concentrations were determined using a radial immunodiffusion assay (JJJ Diagnostics, Bellingham, WA, USA). In brief, 5 μL of serum was dispensed into wells on the plate and incubated at room temperature for 48 hr. The diameters of the precipitate rings were measured independently by two operators using a stereomicroscope (SZX10; Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and image analysis software (cellSENS Microscope Imaging Software 1.18; Olympus Corp.).

Flow cytometry

Lymphoid subpopulations in foal and mare PBMCs and milk were characterized by fluorescent antibody double-staining and analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX S System; Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA). The primary monoclonal antibodies (mAb) used were CD5, CD4, CD8, CD21, CD172a, and CD45RO. For each of these markers, double-staining was performed with an anti–MHC II mAb. IgG1 isotype mAbs were used against CD5 (HT23A), CD4 (HB61A), CD8 (HT14A), and CD172a (DH59B), all obtained from the WSU Monoclonal Antibody Center (Washington State University, Pullman, WA, USA), and the IgG1 mAb against CD21 (B-ly4), obtained from BD Pharmingen (San Diego, CA, USA). An IgG2a isotype mAb was used against CD45RO (HB32A) and an IgG2b isotype mAb against MHC II (H34A), both obtained from the WSU Monoclonal Antibody Center. PBMCs or cell suspensions were incubated at 4°C for 1 hr with primary mAbs diluted 1:100. Following incubation, the cells were PBS-washed and further incubated with the following secondary mAbs: phycoerythrin-labeled anti-mouse IgG1 (1:4,000 dilution; RMG1-1; BioLegend, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), anti-mouse IgG2a (1:4,000 dilution; RMG2a-62; BioLegend, Inc.), and fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled anti-mouse IgG2b (1:2,000 dilution; SouthernBiotechnology Associates, Inc., Birmingham, AL, USA) at 4°C for 45 min. Negative controls were used to confirm the absence of non-specific binding. To exclude non-specific binding mediated by Fc receptors, control samples stained with secondary antibodies alone and isotype control antibodies were analyzed. No significant fluorescence signal was detected in these controls, indicating that non-specific binding was negligible. Data were analyzed using the Kaluza Flow Cytometry Analysis Software (Beckman Coulter, Miami). For blood samples, the absolute number of each surface antigen–positive cell population (cells/μL) was calculated by multiplying the cytogram positivity rate obtained from flow cytometry by the total WBC count determined by CBC analysis. The total leukocyte counts per unit volume could not be determined for milk samples; hence, the proportion of each surface antigen–positive cell population was expressed as the percentage of positive mononuclear cells based on the cytogram positivity rate.

Statistical analysis

Values from CBC and percentages of antigen-positive cells determined by flow cytometry are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which provides a graphical user interface for R (the R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Owing to missing data at certain time points, linear mixed-effects models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with individual animals included as random effects and time as a fixed effect. Multiple pairwise comparisons among all time points were performed using Tukey’s method. Comparisons between colostrum and milk were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The relationship between colostrum at 0 hr and maternal blood was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlations were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. For all non-parametric analyses, P<0.05 was considered significant. For IgG concentrations determined by radial immunodiffusion (RID), values were calculated from a standard curve prepared using control serum, and group comparisons were analyzed using the linear mixed-effects model described above.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we conducted a short-interval longitudinal analysis of immune cells in the peripheral blood of neonatal foals. The results showed that the proportions of CD8+ T and CD8+ MHC II+ cells increased as early as 6–12 hr after colostrum ingestion. These findings provide new insights into the early postnatal dynamics of equine immune cells, which have not been sufficiently investigated, and suggest a possible association with the T cells present in colostrum.

The WBC count of the foals increased until 1 week of age. However, no significant difference was observed between 6 and 12 hr and 1 week, suggesting a transient increase prior to gut closure. Although the overall number of lymphoid cells in the blood did not change markedly, the composition of fluorescently labeled T-cell subsets did. CD5+ T cells increased within 7 days after foaling, and CD5+ MHC II+ cells were already elevated by 12 hr. CD8+ and CD45RO+ cells also tended to increase, with CD8+ cells rising earlier, at 6 hr. In contrast, the CD4+ cells did not increase significantly at 6–12 hr, although their levels did not differ from those at 7 days. B cells (CD21+) and mononuclear phagocytes (CD172a+) remained unchanged. These results indicate that specific immune cell counts increased during the first week after birth. However, this study also showed that MHC II+ T cells, particularly CD8+ and CD45RO+ cells, had already increased from immediately after foaling to 12 hr after colostrum ingestion. MHC II is a molecule involved in antigen presentation by antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells and B cells [26]. In this study, its expression was also confirmed in B cells and mononuclear phagocytes. Although slight differences were observed between total surface antigen–positive subsets and MHC II double-positive subsets, no statistically significant differences were detected at any time point. These discrepancies may reflect heterogeneity in MHC II expression levels and/or the influence of gating thresholds in flow cytometric analysis. Furthermore, MHC II is constitutively expressed on T cells in horses, and its expression intensity increases with accumulated antigen exposure [22]. Therefore, although MHC II expression is detectable in neonatal foals, their T-cell population is considered predominantly naïve, and expression levels are markedly lower than those in adult horses [9, 22]. Indeed, MHC II expression increases in an age-dependent manner and is reported to reach mature levels at approximately 4 months of age [9]. In the present study, an increase in MHC II+ T cells was also observed; however, the early increase detected at 6–12 hr after colostrum ingestion is difficult to explain solely by the differentiation of naïve T cells in the foal. Therefore, this rapid increase in MHC II+ cells suggests the possible transfer of antigen-experienced maternal T cells via colostrum. Furthermore, the predominance of CD8+ cells within the increased cell population supports this hypothesis and is consistent with the immune cell profile of colostrum.

Previous studies in cattle have reported similar findings. Calves that received colostrum from bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV)-immune cows showed enhanced lymphoproliferative responses to the BVDV antigen on days 1 and 2 [7]. This effect was not observed in calves fed frozen or cell-free colostrum [7]. Although IgG transfer was comparable, only calves that received viable maternal cells showed enhanced cellular immune function [7]. These findings indicate that viable maternal cells in colostrum enhance neonatal cellular immunity, with the peak effect occurring around gut closure [7]. In the present study, the number of immune cells also peaked before gut closure. This suggests that colostral immune cells may similarly migrate into the foal and enhance immune function. However, detailed studies on MHC II expression in T cells of foals less than 1 day old remain limited. In the present study, we primarily evaluated the presence or absence of MHC II expression and did not perform a quantitative assessment of expression levels. Therefore, it remains unclear to what extent the observed MHC II expression corresponds to levels reported in previous studies. Furthermore, it is also uncertain whether the increase observed at 12 hr reflects normal physiological development or the effect of colostrum ingestion. Neonatal foals are abruptly exposed to a large amount of environmental antigens after birth, transitioning from a sterile intrauterine environment; thus, the possibility that immune responses are induced by such exposure cannot be excluded. In addition, the potential for maternal–fetal cellular transfer during gestation has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, further studies are warranted, including comparisons with foals that do not receive fresh colostrum and long-term follow-up evaluations. It also remains unclear whether the increased cells are derived from mares or represent foal-derived cells influenced by colostral immune components. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with caution, as they only suggest the possible transfer of maternal immune cells via colostrum and do not provide direct evidence. If functional maternal CD8+ T cells were transferred into the foal circulation, alloimmune responses based on MHC I mismatches, such as graft-versus-host–like reactions, could theoretically occur. However, such phenomena are not commonly observed in clinical settings, suggesting the presence of regulatory mechanisms. Several possibilities may explain this discrepancy. First, colostral lymphocytes may exhibit reduced or regulated functional activity compared with peripheral blood lymphocytes [6]. Second, if the transferred cells predominantly consist of memory T cells, their antigen specificity may be restricted, and nonspecific cytotoxic responses against foal tissues may not necessarily be induced. Third, the neonatal immune environment is generally tolerogenic and may involve regulatory T cells and cytokine-mediated mechanisms that suppress excessive immune responses against exogenous cells [8, 11]. Nevertheless, these explanations remain speculative and were not directly examined in the present study. Therefore, these points should be considered as limitations of the current study.

We also compared immune cells in mare PBMCs and colostrum. CD8+ cells were more abundant in colostrum, whereas CD21+ and CD45RO+ cells were higher in PBMCs. These characteristics are consistent with previous findings that colostrum collected within 24 hr contains abundant T cells, predominantly CD8+ cells, indicating a CTL-type profile in draft horses [30]. However, no correlation was observed between colostrum and blood, suggesting selective recruitment of immune cells into the mammary glands. The blood–milk barrier (BMB) plays a key role in this process. It comprises endothelial cells, connective tissue, basement membranes, and mammary epithelial cells [36]. Tight junctions in epithelial cells regulate selective permeability [36]. Permeability is primarily controlled by hormones and increases during milk production, mastitis-related inflammation, and mammary involution [14]. Thus, immune cells clearly migrate into the mammary glands. However, whether selective recruitment of specific immune subsets occurs during colostrum formation in horses remains unknown. In humans, cattle, and mice, lymphoid migration is regulated by adhesion molecules (MAdCAM-1 and VCAM-1) and chemokines. Therefore, selective recruitment is likely to occur, and the present results support this hypothesis [12, 36].

The immunological function of T cells that increase in foals and are present in colostrum requires further investigation. In cattle, colostral lymphocytes exhibit mitogenic and antigen-specific responses, although their activity is weaker than that of blood lymphocytes [6]. In lambs, absorbed colostral lymphocytes maintain immune function after intestinal uptake [34]. Therefore, colostral lymphocytes may function as memory cells rather than rapidly acting effector cells. In the present study, we evaluated CD45RO as a memory cell marker. However, memory cell characteristics are complex and develop in multiple stages, making it difficult to identify memory cells using CD45RO alone [23]. RNA-seq data from horses indicated that CD45RO expression was relatively low, suggesting the need for combined markers [27]. Although CD45RO dynamics follow T-cell behavior, it remains uncertain whether these cells truly represent memory cells. Instead, their expression may reflect the activation or differentiation of T-cell subsets. In cattle, CD45RA is dominant on CD8+ cells, whereas CD45RO is dominant on CD4+ and γδ T cells [2, 16]. If memory cells are selectively transferred into the colostrum, their proportion should be higher than that in the blood. However, in the present results, CD45RO+ cells were more abundant in blood and did not match CD4+ and CD8+ distributions. This suggests that CD45RO expression in horses may reflect γδ T-cell variation. Memory cells are generally few in circulation, as many reside in tissues [23]. Therefore, memory cells may still appear relatively abundant in blood, despite some being transferred to the mammary glands. Further studies using equine flow cytometry are required to clarify the utility of CD45RO antibodies.

Colostrum also contains bioactive molecules such as cytokines and hormones. These factors are likely to enhance immune competence via multiple mechanisms. Therefore, comprehensive evaluation of these interactions is required.

In summary, this study revealed relationships among immune cell subsets in foals receiving colostrum. In particular, CD8+ MHC II+ T-cell increases before gut closure represent a novel finding. This suggests the possible transfer of CD8+ T cells from colostrum into foal circulation. However, whether these cells are maternal or foal-derived remains unclear. Therefore, further studies are warranted. These findings contribute to our understanding of acquired immunity in neonatal foals and may support improved foal management strategies.
