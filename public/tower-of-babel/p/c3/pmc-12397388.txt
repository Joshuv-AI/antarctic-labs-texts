Thermodynamic properties of tetragonal silicene nanoribbons under the influence of bias voltage and magnetic field
Authors: Somayeh Behzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis theoretical study provides a comprehensive analysis of the tunable influence of combined electric bias and magnetic fields on the thermoelectric properties of Tetragonal Silicene nanoribbons (T-SiNRs). The analysis focuses on both symmetric and asymmetric lattice configurations of T-SiNRs. The findings reveal that the application of a bias voltage induces an energy gap, transforming metallic T-SiNRs into semiconductors. In contrast, a magnetic field produces distinct effects: the Zeeman effect preserves the metallic nature by enhancing the density of states near the Fermi level, while the Peierls phase can induce a small band gap under specific field strengths. The thermodynamic properties, including thermal conductivity κ(T), heat capacity C(T) and Lorenz number L(T) exhibit distinct responses to these external perturbations: bias voltage significantly reduces these properties, particularly at higher temperatures, whereas a magnetic field enhances them. The emergence of a zero-intensity region in temperature-dependent functions, attributed to the suppression of charge carrier excitation, expands with increasing bias voltage but diminishes under a magnetic field. Moreover, the simultaneous application of bias voltage and a magnetic field enhances thermodynamic properties across various temperature ranges, compared to the unperturbed cases. Symmetric T-SiNRs exhibit higher thermal conductivity and heat capacity at low temperatures, while asymmetric structures dominate at higher temperatures. The behavior of the Lorenz number is particularly sensitive to external fields, with its peak position and intensity increasing under bias voltage but decreasing with the magnetic field. These findings highlight the potential of T-SiNRs for advanced nanoelectronic and thermophotonic applications, where precise modulation of thermal and electronic properties is critical for optimizing device performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15844-6.Keywords: Tigth binding model, Tetragonal silicene nanoribbon, Themal conductivity, Heat capacitySubject terms: Electronic properties and materials, Electronic properties and materials, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

In recent years, two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials have attracted considerable attention from researchers across various fields due to their unique electronic, optical, and thermal properties1,2. Among these, Graphene with its zero-energy band gap3, exhibiting massless Dirac fermion behavior with a linear energy dependence on wave number, around the Dirac point4.

Silicene and germanene, which possess graphene-like structures, are inherently stable with a non-planar buckled configuration and exhibit a zero-band gap under ideal conditions5,6. The application of a perpendicular electric field induces a band gap, allowing for tunable electronic properties and making them promising candidates for high-performance transistors and reconfigurable electronics7,8.

Due to attractive properties of various 2d nanostructures composed of different elements9,10, research has expanded to explore other derivatives of these materials, such as nanoribbons, nanowires, and nanotubes11,12. Studies on Silicene nanoribbons (SiNRs) have distinct electronic properties based on their edge configurations. Armchair-edged SiNRs exhibit a direct band-gap with three family band gap reduction as the ribbon width increases, whereas zigzag-edged SiNRs demonstrate uniform band gap decrease with increasing width13. Their intrinsic buckling14, creates unique electronic and optical characteristics under bias voltage15. Moreover, the influence of doping with elements such as B, N, P, and As, along with the impact of structural defects, on the electronic properties of SiNRs has been extensively studied16–20. Additionally, strain engineering21–23 and external electric and magnetic fields15,24 also significantly influences the electronic and thermodynamic properties of SiNRs. The exploration of nanoribbon derivatives has been extended to a variety of emerging two-dimensional materials, such as α-graphyne, C3N, and α-carbon phosphide nanoribbons, which exhibit intriguing electronic and spin-dependent transport properties25,26. Notably, studies on their edge functionalization and structural configurations have demonstrated significant potential for band structure engineering and the realization of novel transport phenomena, including promising thermoelectric performance27.

Beyond all above mentioned nanostructures, recently discovered novel graphene allotropes with special structural configurations exhibit distinct electronic and thermodynamic properties, expanding the range of achievable characteristics in 2D materials. For instance, tetragonal-graphene (T-Geraphene) retains a zero-band gap with two Dirac cones at the Fermi level28,29. T-graphene also exhibits massless Dirac fermions with a high Fermi velocity comparable to graphene and can be realized in the form of nanotube and nanoribbon geometries30–32.

Similar to graphene, the possibility of new allotropes with tetragonal structures based on different atomic species has also been explored. For instance, structures such as tetragonal Silicene (T-Si) and Germanene (T-Ge) exhibit intrinsic buckling33, enabling tunable band gap in the presence of an external fields 34. Consequently, due to this modification in electronic properties, their thermoelectric performance decreases with increasing bias voltage but enhances under an applied magnetic field35. Additionally, their properties can be further tuned through mechanical strain and hydrogenation36,37. Electronic transport studies on T-Si have demonstrated a higher thermal conductivity compared to graphene and a superior figure of merit, making it a promising candidate for thermoelectric applications38. Similarly, T-Ge demonstrates strong responses in its thermoelectric and optical properties under external fields, which can be further modulated via strain engineering, offering significant performance enhancements for potential device applications39,40.

Similar to T-graphene, which can form nanotubes and nanoribbons, tetragonal SiNRs (T-SiNRs) have been the subject of limited studies. It has been shown that they exhibit significant structural and electronic differences, displaying metallic behavior in contrast to corresponding SiNRs41,42. Compared to hexagonal GNRs and SiNRs, T-SiNRs exhibit notable advantages in thermodynamic behavior, primarily due to their buckled structure and high sensitivity to external bias voltage. This buckling feature enables efficient and controllable band gap tuning, which is absent in planar GNRs. Additionally, the presence of multiple conduction and valence band touch points at the Fermi level, which can be modulated by tuning the ribbon width, enhances carrier excitation rates at moderate temperatures. These unique characteristics make T-SiNRs promising candidates for next-generation thermoelectric and nanoelectronic applications.

While research on the electrical properties of T-SiNRs is limited, their thermodynamic properties have not yet been investigated. This study specifically focuses on the diverse thermodynamic characteristics of T-SiNRs using theoretical tight-binding model, Green’s function approach, and the Kubo formula. Specifically, it examines electrical conductivity and heat capacity under various external factors including voltage bias and magnetic fields. Additionally, the influence of chemical potential on these properties is also examined. Ultimately, this study aims to elucidate the temperature dependence of these functions and identify strategies for optimizing thermodynamic properties by tuning external parameters and their effects on the nanoribbon structure.

Analysis and discussion

Electronic properties

Figure 2 presents the DOS spectrum of a T-SiNR with N = 6, under varying bias voltage U. All bias voltage values in the calculations are expressed in electron volts (eV). In the absence of an electric field, the structure exhibits metallic behavior, as evidenced by non-zero DOS intensity around the Fermi level. Moreover, several distinct peaks observed in the DOS spectrum correspond to the discrete energy levels in the valence and conduction states, which are symmetrically distributed on either side of the Fermi level. A particularly notable feature of this system is the presence of a zero-energy gap in the unbiased case, arising from the contact between the highest valence band and lowest conduction band states at specific points at the Fermi level. Upon applying a bias voltage, substantial modifications in the DOS spectrum become evident. Notably, an energy gap emerges in the band structure, resulting in a region of zero DOS intensity around the Fermi level, which indicates a transition from metallic to semiconducting behavior. As the bias voltage increases, both the energy gap and the zero-intensity DOS range broaden. Additionally, the DOS peaks shift further from the Fermi level with increased intensity. This behavior contrasts with multilayer structures such as boron nitride (BN) and silicon carbide (SiC), where external bias reduces the band gap45. The mechanism underlying this distinct feature can be attributed to the induced potential difference between neighboring atomic sites under the applied bias voltage. Consequently, variations in the on-site potential among neighboring atoms lead to the formation of an energy gap in the band structure, which is reflected in the corresponding DOS spectrum.

Effects of the bias voltage (in eV) on the DOS spectrum of the T-SiNRs across different energy ranges. The emergence of an energy gap with increasing U indicates a transition from metallic to semiconducting behavior.

Figure 3 presents the impact of an external magnetic field (B) on the electronic properties of T-SiNRs under both biased and unbiased conditions in the vicinity of the Fermi energy. The application and subsequent increase of the magnetic field strength preserves the metallic nature of the structure while inducing significant modifications in the DOS spectrum near the Fermi level, including variations in the intensity, spacing and position of the DOS peaks across different energy regions. For instance, at B = 0.1, the DOS intensity in regions closer to the Fermi level is lower compared to that at B = 0.15, whereas the trend reverses at energy regions farther from the Fermi level. These variations in DOS spectrum under an applied magnetic field originate from the Zeeman effect, which lifts the spin degeneracy of each energy subband in the valence and conduction bands. This leads to enhanced energy splitting between states that shift in opposite directions as the magnetic field strength increases. While the application of a bias voltage induces an energy gap and shifts the valence and conduction DOS peaks away from each other, the influence of a magnetic field on a biased structure exhibits an opposite effect, reducing band gap and shifting the DOS peaks toward the Fermi level. This result demonstrates that the tunability of the energy gap in T-SiNRs through the application of bias voltage and magnetic field provides a powerful approach for engineering tunable electronic properties which can be crucial for applications in nanoelectronic devices and high-performance sensors.

Under an applied dimensionless magnetic field, T-SiNR remains metallic, exhibiting notable modifications in the DOS peaks near the Fermi level. In biased structures, increasing the magnetic field strength B leads to a reduction in the band gap.

Thermal conductivity

In this study, the thermodynamic properties of T-SiNRs, including thermal conductivity κ(T) and heat capacity C(T), are investigated as functions of temperature under the influence of various parameters including external fields (electric and magnetic) and chemical potential. Although thermal transport of materials generally involves both electronic and phononic contributions, the present study focuses specifically on the electronic component, while neglecting the phononic part. This approach is consistent with several previous studies based on DFT and tight-binding methods, which have similarly emphasized the electronic contributions to thermal transport46–50.

Before analyzing the results, we first provide a physical description of the observed behavior in this structure. As temperature increases, the thermal energy of charge carriers rises, enabling their excitation from the valence to the conduction bands. Consequently, the thermodynamic properties show a strong dependence on temperature variations.

Figure 4a and b illustrate the thermal conductivity behavior of T-SiNR under varying bias voltages. In the absence of external fields, the structure exhibits metallic characteristics, with κ(T) increasing linearly from zero as temperature rises (red line in Fig. 4a). This behavior stems from the absence of an energy band gap, which causes the thermal energy received by the charge carriers in the valence band to increase and they are excited to the conduction bands without restrictions.

(a) Effect of bias voltage (in eV) on the thermal conductivity κ(T) of T-SiNR in the absence of a magnetic field. The application of bias voltage suppresses κ(T) across the entire temperature range. (b) Influence of the dimensionless magnetic field B on the thermal conductivity of biased T-SiNR. Increasing B enhances κ(T) due to band gap reduction.

With the application of bias voltage, an energy gap appears in the band structure, acting as a potential barrier and preventing the thermal excitation of charge carriers at low temperatures. For instance, at U = 1.5 eV, the thermal energy gained by charge carriers below T < 500 K is insufficient to overcome the energy gap, resulting in near-zero thermal conductivity in this temperature range. Consequently, at higher temperatures above this region, κ(T) becomes non-zero and begins to increase. As shown in the inset of Fig. 4a, the zero-intensity range in κ(T) exhibits a strong dependence on the applied bias voltage and increases with it. Furthermore, the results indicate that at constant temperature, κ(T) decreases with increasing bias voltage. This behavior arises because some of the thermal energy gained by charge carriers is used to overcome the energy gap barrier.

Figure 4b illustrates the effect of a magnetic field on biased T-SiNRs. Unlike the reducing effects of U on κ(T), the application of magnetic field B significantly enhances thermal conductivity across all temperature ranges. Specifically, it reduces the zero-intensity region of κ(T) while enhancing its intensity in the nonzero regions. The reason for this phenomenon is the reduction of the energy gap of the biased structure in the presence of a magnetic field, leading to an increase in the density of thermally excited carriers from lower to higher energy states over a smaller temperature range. For example, as shown in Fig. 4b, applying B to the biased T-SiNR results in a more rapid increase in κ(T) with temperature. This behavior arises from the inverse relationship between the energy band gap and B. These findings indicate that the combined application of B and U enables precise tuning of κ(T) across various temperature regimes, enhancing the thermoelectric properties of T-SiNR. This tunability makes T-SiNR a promising candidate for potential applications in nanoscale thermal management and energy harvesting devices.

Figure 5 illustrates the temperature dependence of κ(T) under various magnetic field strengths. In contrast to the biased case, a distinct pattern emerges for κ(T) in the presence of B. For all selected B values, κ(T) remains nonzero across all temperature range, as applying B does not induce an energy band gap. At temperatures below 100 K, κ(T) at B = 0.15 exhibits the same intensity as at B = 0 and above 150 K, its intensity increases significantly. This behavior at low temperatures is also observed for stronger magnetic fields. For instance, κ(T < 150) at B = 0.25 exhibits a lower intensity compared to B = 0.15, but as temperature increases, this trend reverses, with κ(T) at higher B values surpassing those at lower B values. As shown in Fig. 5b, κ(T) at B = 0.25 exceeds that at other selected magnetic field strengths, although the difference between them diminishes with increasing temperature.

Thermal conductivity κ(T) as a function of temperature for different dimensionless magnetic field strengths across various temperature ranges. Higher magnetic field strengths lead to increased κ(T) at high temperatures.

This reduction in thermal conductivity under stronger magnetic field at different temperature ranges can be interpreted by analyzing the DOS spectrum of the structure for different values of B. As shown in the inset of Fig. 5b, near the Fermi level within |E|< 0.05 eV, the DOS intensity at B = 0.25 is lower than at B = 0. This results in a higher charge density at B = 0 to be excited from the valence to the conduction bands with less thermal energy. Consequently, at the low temperature range, κ(T) at B = 0 exhibits higher intensity. In energy ranges further from the Fermi level, the DOS spectrum at B = 0.25 exceeds that at B = 0, leading to greater excitation of charge carriers at higher temperature range for B = 0.25 due to increased thermal energy. A comparative analysis of the influence of different magnetic field strengths on κ(T) shows that: (a) κ(T,B ≠ 0) does not display a zero-intensity temperature range and (b) k(T,U ≠ 0) is significantly lower than κ(T,B ≠ 0), with the intensity difference between them increases as temperature rises.

One of the key factors affecting thermal conductivity behavior is the effect of the chemical potential, as shown in Fig. 6. The results indicate that the dependence of κ(µ) on µ exhibits different patterns in the presence of various external fields and with increasing temperature. At T = 300 K, κ(µ ,U = 1 eV) shows a zero-intensity range around µ = 0 which arises from the presence of a finite energy gap acting as a potential barrier, preventing carrier excitation. As the temperature increases, the zero-intensity region gradually disappears due to the enhanced thermal energy of charge carriers, enabling them to overcome the energy gap barrier. Additionally, at a constant µ, the intensity of κ(µ) at a higher temperature is greater than at a lower temperature, which indicates the crucial role of temperature in enhancing thermal conductivity. In the presence of B ≠ 0, κ(µ) exhibits non-zero intensity across all µ regions, which contrasts with the U ≠ 0 case and is independent of temperature [Fig. 6b]. With increasing temperature, this effect is enhanced, leading to higher intensity of κ(µ), particularly around µ = 0.

Dependence of thermal conductivity κ(μ) on chemical potential μ under different external field conditions: (a) U = 1.0 eV, (b) B = 0.1 and (c) combined U = 1.0 eV and B = 0.1. The application of bias voltage introduces a zero-intensity region, which disappears with increasing temperature or the addition of a magnetic field.

As shown in Fig. 6c, the combined influence of bias voltage and magnetic field on the T-SiNR demonstrates that, despite the presence of bias voltage U = 1 eV, applying B ≠ 0 eliminates the zero-intensity region and produces a distinct thermal conductivity pattern with higher values. In this case, the decreasing behavior of κ(µ) as a function of µ is also reduced. In all patterns with different field strengths, κ(µ) reaches its maximum at µ = 0 for any fixed temperature. Additionally, κ(µ) exhibits a decreasing trend due to the varying influence of chemical potential, which occurs under different field conditions over certain µ ranges. This feature can be explained as follows: at constant temperature, increasing µ enhances the charge carrier concentration, which due to the occurrence of quantum effects such as collisions and scattering between charge carriers in ground and excited states, causes κ(μ) to decrease with increasing μ.

Heat capacity

In addition to thermal conductivity, the behavior of heat capacity of T-SiNRs undergoes significant modifications under the influence of external fields and variations in chemical potential, as depicted in Figs. 7–9. Since, according to Eq. 6, the heat capacity C(T) is directly influenced by variations in the DOS and we examine its modification based on the dependence of DOS on external parameters.

Temperature dependence of heat capacity C(T) (a) under different bias voltages (in eV) and (b) considering the combined effects of bias voltage and dimensionless magnetic field. In biased structures, heat capacity remains negligible at low temperatures due to the absence of thermally excited carriers. This effect diminishes with the addition of a magnetic field due to allowing thermal excitation at lower temperatures.

Variation of heat capacity C(μ) as a function of chemical potential μ at different temperatures and external field conditions: (a) U = 1.0 eV, (b) B = 0.1 and (c) combined U = 1.0 eV and B = 0.1. The zero-intensity region observed under bias voltage vanishes with rising temperature and the application of a magnetic field.

As shown in the Fig. 7a, in the absence of external fields, C(T) increases uniformly with temperature because, due to the absence of an energy gap in the pure structure, all thermal energy is directly used to increase the internal energy. A similar behavior has been reported for two-dimensional materials with metallic properties such as Silicene, Germanene and graphene45. Under the application of a specific bias voltage, the intensity of C(T) becomes zero within a certain temperature range T < T0, where the value of T0 is proportional to the magnitude of the applied bias [Fig. 7a]. This behavior is similar to the results observed in κ(T) and is attributed to the formation of an energy gap induced by bias voltage, which acts as an energy barrier, suppressing the thermal excitation of charge carriers with energies below kBT. Beyond the temperature threshold T > T0​, the charge carriers gain sufficient thermal energy to overcome the potential barrier, leading to non-zero values of C(T) that increase with temperature. As the bias voltage U increases, the rate of increase in C(T) with respect to temperature decreases because more of the received thermal energy is used to overcome the band gap [Fig. 7a].

Additionally, C(U = 0.5 eV) exceeds C(U = 0) for T > 600 K and this intriguing behavior can be explained based on the DOS of the selected structure under varying bias voltages. The inset of Fig. 7a shows that, at U = 0.5 eV, the DOS intensity for ∣E∣ > 0.1 eV is significantly higher than in the unbiased case. This leads to a greater concentration of thermally excited charge carriers at higher U, a trend consistent across different bias values over various temperature ranges.

Figure 7 (b) examines the combined effects of bias voltage and magnetic field on the temperature dependence of C(T). Applying a magnetic field B reduces the zero-intensity temperature range of C(T) for the biased structure and increases its slope with temperature. These findings can be explained by the effect of B on reducing the energy gap of the biased structure, which enhances carrier excitation at lower temperatures. The observed differences in the behavior of C(T) between the cases [U = 1,B = 0.2] and [U = 1 eV,B = 0] are related to the corresponding modifications in DOS under these conditions [inset of Fig. 5b].

The impact of different magnetic field strengths on C(T) across two temperature ranges is illustrated in Fig. 8. Unlike the case with applied bias voltage, C(T) under different B values exhibits two notable differences: (i) the absence of a zero-intensity temperature range and (ii) a consistent increase in C(T) with respect to temperature. In different temperatures regions below T < 300 K, C(T) at B = 0 exhibits both lower and higher intensities compared to B = 0.1 and B = 0.15. These variations in thermal behavior are directly related to changes in the DOS intensities induced by different magnetic field strengths. In contrast, for T > 400 K, C(T) at stronger magnetic fields exceeds that at weaker fields, with C(B = 0.25) exhibiting the highest values. As shown in the inset of Fig. 8b, this trend remains consistent with increasing temperature.

In the presence of a magnetic field, the zero-intensity region does not induce due to the absence of a band gap. Inset (a) illustrates how an increase in DOS near the Fermi level enhances heat capacity as temperature rises.

The dependence of C(μ) on μ at different temperatures and in the presence of varying values of B and U is illustrated in Fig. 9. Analysis of different cases reveals that C(μ) displays a symmetric pattern for positive and negative values of μ. At T = 300 K, applying a bias voltage U = 1 eV induces a zero-intensity region for C(µ) which the emergence and increase of this region is temperature-dependent and disappears above 600 K. In all investigated cases, increasing the temperature enhances C(μ), where can be attributed to two factors: (i) the increase in thermal energy of charge carriers and (ii) the increased density of thermally excited charge carriers at higher μ values. This pattern persists over a wide range around µ = 0. Within the selected μ range shown in Fig. 9, C(μ) exhibits a decreasing trend near μ = ± 0.5 eV. This reduction is attributed to the increasing influence of quantum effects, such as scattering and collisions between excited carriers which become more pronounced due to the higher density and thermal energy of excited carriers.

The effects of U and B on the temperature dependence of Lorenz number L(T) are shown in Fig. 10. Although thermal conductivity increases with temperature, the behavior of L(T) as a function of temperature exhibits three distinct regions characterized by constant, increasing, and decreasing trends. These regions are determined by variations in the ratio of electrical to thermal conductivity parameters. Under applied bias voltage U, the range of L(T) with a constant value depends on the magnitude of U and expands with increasing U. Furthermore, higher U values enhance the overall L(T) magnitude and shift its maximum peak to higher temperatures. This behavior is attributed to the enhanced semiconducting properties induced by the bias voltage. In contrast, applying a magnetic field B to biased-T-SiNR shifts the maximum peak of L(T) toward lower temperatures with reducing its intensity. The results indicate that the intensity of L(T) in the absence of B is greater than B ≠ 0.

Structural dependence

In addition to external factors such as applied fields and chemical potential, the thermodynamic properties of T-SiNR structures are also significantly influenced by their structural symmetry. Previous DFT studies have revealed significant differences in the electronic band structure between symmetric and asymmetric T-SiNRs, particularly near the Fermi level41. Based on these differences, Fig. 11 illustrates the heat capacity and thermal conductivity for both symmetric and asymmetric T-SiNR configurations. In the absence of bias voltage, the symmetric structure exhibits higher values of C(T) and κ(T) than the asymmetric structure at temperatures below 150 K. However, as the temperature rises beyond this threshold, this trend reverses and the difference between two configurations becomes more pronounced. Upon applying a bias voltage, the thermodynamic properties of the asymmetric structure become greater than those of the symmetric structure and this trend persists across the entire temperature range. Moreover, as the bias magnitude increases, the difference between the thermodynamic properties of the two structures decreases, becoming nearly negligible at sufficiently high bias voltages. These findings indicate that, without bias voltage, structural symmetry plays a critical role in determining the thermodynamic behavior, especially at higher temperatures. However, the application of bias voltage effectively reduces this symmetry-induced dependence.

Conclusions

In this theoretical study, the combined effects of an external magnetic field and bias voltage on the electrical behavior, thermal conductivity κ(T), heat capacity C(T) and Lorenz number L(T) of T-SiNRs are investigated using the tight-binding formalism, Green’s function method and Kubo formula. The pristine T-SiNRs exhibits metallic behavior, convert to a semiconducting state upon application of a bias voltage. The application of a magnetic field leads to a dual impact on T-SiNRs’ electronic properties of T-SiNRs: the Zeeman effect maintains their metallic nature by enhancing the DOS near the Fermi level, whereas the Peierls phase, under certain field strengths, can induce a small energy gap. In the absence of external fields, the thermodynamic properties change uniformly with variations in temperature and chemical potential. Due to the metallic nature of the structure under a magnetic field, thermal conductivity κ(T) remains nonzero across all field strengths, with its intensity modulated by both temperature and magnetic field strength. The application of a bias voltage significantly reduces these properties, with the reduction becoming more pronounced as temperature. This behavior is directly correlated with the creation and increasing of the energy gap. Furthermore, the bias voltage induces a zero-intensity region in the temperature dependence of these thermodynamic functions, which arises from the inhibition of charge carrier excitation in the low temperature regime due to the energy gap. This zero-intensity region increases with increasing bias voltage and decreases with the application of a magnetic field. The simultaneous application of bias voltage and a magnetic field enhances thermodynamic properties in different temperature regimes compared to the unperturbed case. The Lorenz number L(T) exhibits a characteristic peak whose position and intensity are determined by the strength of the applied fields, increasing with bias voltage and decreasing under a magnetic field. These findings demonstrate that the precise and simultaneous control of bias voltage and magnetic field can effectively tune the thermal and electronic transport properties of T-SiNRs, offering valuable insights for optimizing nanoelectronic and thermophotonic device designs.

With the initial application of Q, a noticeable reduction in the DOS intensity around the Fermi level (EF) occurs. Specifically, at Q = 0.04, a small band gap emerges at EF​. As Q increases to 0.08, the band gap expands significantly and the separation between the conduction and valence band DOS peaks becomes more distinct, indicating a transition toward semiconducting-like behavior. Notably, while the DOS intensity decreases near EF, increasing the parameter Q simultaneously enhances DOS intensity at energies further away from the Fermi level. This behavior markedly contrasts with the Zeeman effect, where the DOS around the Fermi level typically remains non-zero, preserving the metallic nature of the structure. Although the effect of Q on band gap formation is qualitatively similar to that of applying a bias voltage U, the magnitude of the gap created by the selected Q values is considerably smaller compared to those induced by typical bias voltages.

Electronic density of states (DOS) for T-SiNRs under varying values of the dimensionless magnetic flux parameter Q. The results show the emergence and increase of an energy gap around the Fermi level with increasing Q, indicating a transition towards semiconducting behavior.

Figure 13 presents the specific heat capacity C(T) of the T-SiNR structure under various Q values. At lower temperatures, specifically below approximately 150 K, the application of Q reduces C(T). This decrease is directly attributable to the formation of the band gap and the consequent reduction in DOS intensity near the Fermi level, as observed in Fig. 12. Increasing the parameter Q further enhances this suppressive effect on C(T) in this low-temperature regime. In contrast, at temperatures above 150 K, this trend reverses. Under these conditions, C(T) exhibits higher intensities for larger Q values compared to smaller ones. This contrasting behavior at higher temperatures stems directly from the increased DOS intensity at energies further from the Fermi level when larger Q values are applied. As temperature increases toward higher values, such as 800 K, the difference between C(T) for different values ​​of Q decreases and all curves converge toward similar values. Furthermore, our results indicate that above room temperature, C(T) under the influence of Q (due to the Peierls phase effect) is slightly greater than C(T) under the influence of a magnetic field B (due to the Zeeman effect) , although both converge near 800 K.

Temperature dependence of the electronic heat capacity C(T) for T-SiNRs at different values of the dimensionless magnetic flux parameter Q.

Figure 14 displays the temperature dependence of thermal conductivity κ(T) in T-SiNRs for various values of Q. For applied Q values up to approximately 0.08, κ(T) remains zero at temperatures below approximately 100 K. Beyond this range, κ(T) becomes finite and increases with rising temperature. This behavior of thermal conductivity at low temperatures stems directly from the formation of an energy gap in the electronic structure by the selected Q values, as highlighted in the discussion of Fig. 12. This induced gap acts as a potential barrier, effectively preventing the thermal excitation of charge carriers at low temperatures. For instance, at Q = 0.08, κ(T) remains zero below 100 K because the kinetic energy gained by charge carriers from thermal energy (kB​T) is insufficient to overcome the induced energy gap. Above this critical low-temperature region, κ(T) becomes finite and shows an increasing trend with temperature. A significant observation is that stronger Q values lead to a smaller κ(T) strength. This behavior is consistent with the larger band gap and reduced DOS intensity near the Fermi level observed at higher Q values (Fig. 12). Comparing the effect of the Peierls phase (induced by Q) with the Zeeman effect reveals that the intensity of κ(T) under the Zeeman effect is consistently larger than κ(T) under the influence of Q. Moreover, the variation in κ(T) with Q is more pronounced than that under the Zeeman effect. While the application of Q has a suppressive effect on the magnitude of κ(T), this effect is considerably weaker than the reduction in κ(T) caused by the application of bias voltage U.
