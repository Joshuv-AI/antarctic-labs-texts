Physics-informed deep learning enables reliable and scalable organoid quantification for drug screening via OCT
Authors: Bin Li, Mingen Xu, Yuhang Bai, Yang Liu, Xiao Liang, Ling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) hold transformative potential for personalized medicine by recapitulating patient-specific drug responses. While Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) is ideal for monitoring these responses, its translation into high-throughput screening (HTS) is hindered by a segmentation accuracy-throughput bottleneck. Existing solutions fail to meet clinical demands: accurate 3D approaches are prohibitively slow, while emerging foundation models lack sensitivity to minute, low-contrast OCT targets. Conversely, fast 2D models suffer from background noise and unstable performance across varying scales. To bridge this gap, we propose DICE-2DSeg, a physics-informed, graph-enhanced framework. By synergizing OCT-inspired intra-slice coherent enhancement with graph-based inter-slice context aggregation, our method ensures robust quantification. Validated on 93 volumes across diverse cancer types and drugs, DICE-2DSeg demonstrates exceptional robustness. Specifically, our high-throughput variant achieves a 14-fold speedup over nnUNet3D while retaining 93.65% of its accuracy. Crucially, it exhibits superior multi-scale consistency, establishing a new state-of-the-art for challenging drug-responsive remnants (0–100 μm) while maintaining high fidelity for massive clusters (> 100 μm). By resolving the conflict between precision, scalability, and scale-invariance, DICE-2DSeg provides a technical enabling step for automated, large-scale PDO drug screening.

Introduction

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a powerful preclinical model, as they faithfully recapitulate the structural, molecular, and genetic characteristics of their tissue of origin. This fidelity allows them to reproduce patient-specific drug responses in vitro1. By culturing PDOs in parallel within 96- or 384-well plates, the efficacies of dozens of drugs can be evaluated within a clinically relevant timeframe, presenting tremendous potential for personalized medicine by facilitating the rapid identification of optimal treatment strategies2. However, conventional evaluation methods are subject to significant limitations. End-point techniques, such as biochemical viability assays and fluorescence staining, provide assessments at only a single time point, thereby failing to capture transient or dynamic drug effects. Moreover, while microscopy methods like bright-field and high-content imaging offer high-resolution structural detection, they cannot resolve the intrinsic three-dimensional (3D) morphology of PDOs3. With its key attributes of high resolution (1–15 μm), deep tissue penetration (1–3 mm), and non-invasive, rapid 3D imaging capabilities, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) provides an ideal technological platform for monitoring the dynamic changes in PDO clusters in response to drug treatment4–6.

The application of 3D OCT morphological analysis to high-throughput screening (HTS) of PDOs introduces a dual challenge for segmentation algorithms, demanding a balance between accuracy and throughput. On one hand, segmentation accuracy is severely compromised by image degradations inherent to OCT imaging, such as speckle noise, shadowing artifacts, and low contrast7–12. These issues pose significant challenges to the precision, robustness, and stability of any segmentation algorithm. On the other hand, HTS imposes stringent demands on processing efficiency. A single seven-day drug sensitivity experiment on a 96-well plate can generate 672 3D volumes. Given the limited computational resources typically available in clinical settings, algorithms must be highly efficient to enable timely decision-making. This creates a distinct trade-off: high-throughput algorithms often lack robustness against variations in image quality and exhibit instability when faced with drug-induced changes in the optical properties of PDO clusters. Conversely, high-accuracy models typically entail substantial computational costs. Therefore, this study aims to strike a critical balance among segmentation accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency, thereby providing a reliable segmentation method for OCT-based HTS of PDO clusters.

Contemporary medical image segmentation algorithms are broadly categorized into 2D and 3D approaches, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages when applied to challenging OCT images of PDO clusters characterized by artifacts, complex backgrounds, and ambiguous object boundaries. Benefiting from their 3D receptive fields, 3D algorithms effectively integrate spatial context, generally achieving more robust segmentation results than 2D methods on low-quality volumetric OCT data with poor contrast or missing features. However, the high inference cost of accurate 3D algorithms severely restricts their application in HTS tasks. In contrast, 2D algorithms offer significant computational advantages, but their performance is highly dependent on single-slice image quality. They frequently suffer from incomplete segmentation of large-scale objects, misclassification or omission of small-scale targets, and false positives from background noise. Crucially, such background mis-segmentation can severely undermine the reliability of downstream growth analysis, effectively stripping the quantitative assessment of drug responses of its accuracy and credibility. To this end, our research explores a novel mechanism that fuses intra-slice feature enhancement with inter-slice receptive field expansion, aiming to preserve the efficiency of 2D algorithms while elevating the accuracy and robustness of PDO cluster segmentation in OCT images.

In the realm of intra-slice feature enhancement, two inherent problems must be addressed: speckle noise, which is intrinsic to coherent imaging, as well as structural artifacts and signal attenuation arising from light interaction with the organoid and matrix material. Existing methods primarily focus on improving single-slice OCT image quality at either the spectral or image level. Spectral-level approaches, such as multi-view reconstruction7–9, correct for structural artifacts and signal decay by fusing spectral information from different angles, thereby enhancing imaging depth and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, these methods increase hardware costs and require acquiring several times more data, resulting in significant storage and processing burdens as well as dramatically extended scan times, which can compromise organoid viability and render them unsuitable for HTS applications. At the image level, denoising algorithms based on multi-frame averaging or deep learning can smooth speckle noise and restore blurry boundaries to some extent10–12. Yet, they are fundamentally post-processing techniques and struggle to effectively recover information in deeper regions where the SNR is low due to insufficient light penetration or high tissue attenuation, leading to irreversible information loss. Consequently, a key challenge is to design a segmentation network that can, while maintaining computational efficiency, actively perceive and compensate for image degradation, thereby achieving more robust and complete segmentation of PDO clusters, especially in regions with missing or ambiguous structures.

Regarding the expansion of the inter-slice receptive field, researchers have explored various mechanisms to incorporate spatial context into 2D networks. For instance, hybrid architectures like H-DenseUNet13 integrate a 3D branch to extract volumetric features but inevitably inherit the high computational load of 3D networks. Transformer-based attention mechanisms have been used to model long-range dependencies efficiently; however, whether using axial attention (e.g., After-UNet14) or memory attention modules (e.g., MedSAM215), the computational complexity scales quadratically with the feature map size. This dependency typically restricts their application to the low-resolution feature maps at the network’s top layers, thereby limiting the guidance of inter-slice information for the high-resolution features in the lower layers and hindering the full utilization of multi-level feature fusion across different layers.16,17. Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)-based methods, such as SeqCorr-EUNet18, leverage recursive state transitions to correlate consecutive slices. Yet, these approaches generally only support information exchange between the current slice and the hidden state, failing to achieve comprehensive contextual aggregation across multiple slices. Thus, the central challenge lies in designing an efficient inter-slice interaction mechanism that can fuse contextual information across multiple network levels (not just at the top-level low-resolution features or within sequential hidden states) to simultaneously enhance fine-grained segmentation quality and overall 3D spatial consistency.

In this paper, we introduce DICE-2DSeg, a high-efficiency framework designed to bridge the gap between 2D computational speed and 3D segmentation accuracy. Central to our approach is the Dual-Stage Interaction and Complementary Exchange (DICE) module, which creates a synergy between physics-informed priors and data-driven learning. By simulating the optical interference mechanism of OCT imaging, the framework actively suppresses noise and enhances intra-slice features, while simultaneously employing a lightweight graph network to aggregate global volumetric context across slices. This design allows diverse 2D backbones to achieve an equivalent 3D receptive field and robustness without the prohibitive computational cost of 3D convolutions. Consequently, DICE-2DSeg serves as an architecture-agnostic solution that balances precision and efficiency, providing a technical enabling step for the high-throughput morphological quantification of organoid drug responses.

Results

Dataset characteristics and challenges

The patient-derived organoid (PDO) clusters originated from seven patients: one with pancreatic cancer (P1), five with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (P2–P6), and one with hepatocellular carcinoma (P7). SS-OCT was used for imaging PDOs from patients P1–P6, while SD-OCT was used for those from patient P7, resulting in a total of 1124 unlabeled 3D OCT volumes.

To establish a challenging benchmark, our dataset was curated by experts who specifically selected difficult cases, such as those exhibiting well-plate artifacts, reflection noise from the culture medium surface, OCT speckle noise that obscures organoid boundaries, and reduced organoid visibility due to drug treatment. This resulted in a total of 93 labeled volumes, comprising 35,180 labeled B-scans and 447,910 labeled organoids. Patient-derived organoid (PDO) clusters exhibit multi-scale tissue morphologies. To characterize this scale heterogeneity, the organoids were stratified into four distinct size groups based on diameter: 0–40 μm, 40–100 μm, 100–150 μm, and > 150 μm. These scale characteristics are closely linked to organoid function and tumor heterogeneity19–21. In applications involving OCT-based dynamic monitoring and analysis of PDOs, the misidentification of background elements (e.g., stripe artifacts, well-plate structures, detached debris) as organoids (a phenomenon known as false positive hallucination) can severely interfere with downstream quantitative biological analyses22,23, such as the precise assessment of organoid growth or mortality rates, potentially leading to erroneous conclusions.

To explore the translational value of organoid OCT image segmentation performance in downstream quantitative biological analyses, we constructed a coupled morpho-metabolic drug response test subset (see the rightmost column of Table 1). Extracted from the intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC, P2–P6) test set, this subset comprises 7 independent patient-derived organoid groups subjected to individual drug interventions (e.g., Control, Icaritin, Cisplatin, and 5-Fluorouracil). For this evaluation, longitudinal OCT monitoring was conducted over three consecutive days at 24-h intervals, yielding 21 independent 3D OCT volumes. These longitudinal scans were directly paired with a destructive endpoint adenosine triphosphate (ATP) assay performed immediately after the final imaging time point. As the recognized gold standard for quantifying cellular metabolic activity 24, this independent ATP measurement provides a robust metabolic reference to biologically corroborate the treatment-induced morphological changes tracked by OCT.

The results in Table 2 demonstrate that the proposed DICE module is an efficient, high-throughput, and architecture-agnostic component. It can be seamlessly integrated into existing 2D segmentation frameworks to enhance their performance on the challenging task of 3D segmentation of complex organoid OCT images. The DICE-GDNet advantage is striking: while the baseline GDNet lagged behind complex architectures, the enhanced model demonstrated superior segmentation accuracy, achieving the top score on three out of four key accuracy metrics, maintained a background mis-segmentation rate below 7%, and preserved GDNet’s original high-throughput advantage (68.43 FPS), far exceeding the efficiency of other baseline 2D networks. This demonstrates a high degree of synergy between performance and efficiency. Consequently, we selected GDNet as the backbone for our proposed DICE-2DSeg model.

Component-level analysis of proposed modules

To validate the necessity of each component within the complementary design of the DICE module, we conducted a series of ablation experiments. As detailed in Table 3, starting with GDNet as the baseline, integrating either the “Intra-Slice Coherent Enhancement” or the “Inter-Slice Graph Convolution” module alone improved performance. However, the most significant performance gain was observed when both modules were used in combination, confirming their synergistic effect. This result not only confirms a significant synergistic effect between the two modules but also validates the efficacy of our complementary design strategy. Furthermore, while embedding the modules at multiple feature levels yielded marginal gains on specific metrics, it introduced a substantial computational burden. In contrast, deploying the module only at the highest-level feature layer (level 3) allowed DICE-2DSeg to achieve an AVG 3D Dice score of 65.35%, indicating that its 3D segmentation accuracy is comparable to that of the multi-level embedding configuration (reaching 97.84% of its performance). At the same time, this single-level configuration achieved the lowest background mis-segmentation rate (6.51%) and the fastest inference speed (68.43 FPS). This non-linear relationship highlights two critical conditions for the effective use of inter-slice context. First, a narrow context (e.g., three slices) may introduce noise from adjacent slices when dealing with highly noisy data, thereby interfering with segmentation. Second, a sufficiently wide context (e.g., seven slices) is necessary to achieve a performance gain, as it enables the inter-slice graph convolution module to effectively aggregate information and suppress noise. Therefore, this experiment not only confirms that sufficient inter-slice information is crucial for 3D segmentation performance but also independently validates the core role of the “intra-slice coherent enhancement” module in high-resolution feature extraction through its performance with a single-slice input. The high-quality single-slice features provided by the “intra-slice coherent enhancement” module are what allow the inter-slice module, once given a broad 3D field of view, to drive comprehensive improvements across all key metrics.

Comparison with baseline models

Correlation analysis between model-derived morphological metrics and endpoint metabolic activity

Based on the aforementioned advantages in scale-dependent segmentation accuracy and mitigation of background mis-segmentation, we evaluated the Spearman rank correlation coefficients between the morphological changes extracted by various models and the endpoint ATP assay results, within the longitudinal drug response validation set (Table 7, Fig. 3). The results indicate that the two variants of DICE-2DSeg (DICE-2DSeg-H and DICE-2DSeg-S) exhibit high positive correlations (ρ ≥ 0.893, P < 0.01) with ATP-derived metabolic readouts across two longitudinal morphological metrics: absolute growth (V3 − V1) and relative growth rate ((V3 − V1)/V1). Further analysis reveals that the strength of these biological correlations is associated with the scale-dependent segmentation accuracy and the mitigation of background mis-segmentation achieved by the models. Regarding the control of background mis-segmentation, models with higher mis-segmentation rates tend to include non-biological structures into the organoid volume, causing the extracted morphological metrics to lose their monotonic relationship with the actual number of viable cells. When the average background mis-segmentation rate of a model exceeds 10%, the correlation between the four morphological indicators discussed in this study and the ATP assay results decreases significantly (ρ < 0.5). For instance, although nnUNet3D performs well in overall 3D Dice (3DDice = 69.78%), its background mis-segmentation rate reaches 10.44% (Table 6), resulting in a correlation between relative growth rate and ATP-derived metabolic readouts of only ρ = 0.393. In terms of scale-dependent segmentation accuracy, higher segmentation accuracy for small-scale targets helps preserve minute structural information during longitudinal monitoring, thereby reducing the loss of morphological information. As shown in Table 5, for organoids with a diameter of less than 100 μm, the segmentation accuracy of all models declines significantly; however, DICE-2DSeg-H achieves suboptimal performance in the two most challenging scale intervals of [0 μm, 40 μm) and [40 μm, 100 μm), with scores of 38.70% and 57.88% respectively, while maintaining the background mis-segmentation rate at the lowest level (AVG 6.51%). Discussion

As a non-invasive 3D imaging modality, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) has immense potential for dynamically monitoring patient-derived organoid (PDO) drug responses, holding promise to guide personalized treatment through continuous growth analysis1,4–6. The accuracy and robustness of organoid segmentation in OCT images are critical for downstream quantitative morphological analysis. In particular, the background mis-segmentation rate can undermine the reliability of growth analysis, stripping the quantitative assessment of drug responses of its accuracy and credibility. Furthermore, the massive volume of OCT volumetric data generated by high-throughput organoid drug screening imposes requirements on segmentation efficiency and computational cost for practical applications.

Existing methods are generally constrained by an inherent trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency. While traditional 3D models like nnUNet3D can leverage global spatial context to ensure high segmentation accuracy, their prohibitive computational costs render them unsuitable for high-throughput analysis scenarios (Table 6). In contrast, high-throughput 2D models such as GDNet achieve high processing speeds at the expense of inter-slice information, making their segmentation results highly dependent on single-slice image quality and extremely vulnerable to interference from speckle noise and background artifacts (Fig. 2b). More complex 2D methods, including MedSAM and MambaUNet, introduce long-range dependency modeling to more effectively capture global context within a slice, leading to some improvement in 3D macroscopic accuracy. However, their uncertain performance for minute objects and ill-defined boundaries results in suboptimal performance in scale-dependent 2D Dice and average 2D Dice evaluations. Moreover, their computational efficiency remains far inferior to that of high-throughput 2D models (Tables 2, 6). In specific local regions of organoid OCT images (Fig. 1), inter-slice correlation methods have shown localized promise: H-DenseUNet yielded superior segmentation in selected instances for organoids in the [40 μm, +∞) range, while AFTer-UNet performed best for those in the [0 μm, 40 μm) range. This preliminarily validates the effectiveness of inter-slice approaches for segmenting complex organoid OCT images. Yet, methods that rely solely on a 3D network branch (H-DenseUNet)13 or on unidirectional information transfer between slices (AFTer-UNet, MedSAM2)14,15 cannot fully leverage multi-slice contextual information, leading to a significant performance decline in extensive testing scenarios (Table 5).

To address the aforementioned challenges, our proposed DICE-2DSeg utilizes an intra- and inter-slice dual-stage coupled enhancement mechanism (Table 3). This design enables it to achieve the lowest background mis-segmentation rate while balancing overall accuracy and throughput (Table 6). By simulating the physical processes of constructive and destructive interference from OCT imaging in the wave domain, it enables a 2D segmentation network to actively perceive and compensate for intra-slice image degradation (Fig. 4). This significantly reduces the dependency of a high-throughput method like GDNet on image quality, without introducing additional hardware costs7 or image reconstruction overhead10 (Table 3, “Intra-Slice Coherent Enhancement” module alone). Concurrently, the framework constructs 3D spatial context by aggregating cross-slice information in the graph domain. This ensures that multi-slice contextual information is fully utilized, overcoming the limitation of conventional sequential models14,15 where information can only flow unidirectionally, preventing the perception of global context. It also effectively circumvents the high computational costs associated with 3D network branches13 or the repetitive feature computation of adjacent slices14,18. Consequently, this synergy enables our method to transcend single-scale limitations. As highlighted in our results, DICE-2DSeg exhibits superior multi-scale consistency: it establishes a new state-of-the-art for challenging small-to-medium drug-responsive remnants (0–100 μm), a capability essential for capturing drug efficacy, while retaining high-fidelity segmentation accuracy for massive clusters (>100 μm).Fig. 4DICE-2DSeg framework.The DICE module enhances a 2D segmentation network by integrating the intra-slice wave-domain interaction and the inter-slice graph-domain interaction; arrows indicate information flow, and dashed boxes outline the DICE interaction stages and the segmentation network.

Beyond achieving robust segmentation performance, the practical utility of our framework lies in its ability to support reliable morphological quantification for downstream biological evaluation. To this end, we evaluated the reliability of longitudinal morphological metrics derived from the segmentation models within a longitudinal test subset featuring real drug interventions. As demonstrated by the correlation analysis results (Table 7, Fig. 3), the dynamic features (Absolute Growth and Relative Growth Rate) extracted by DICE-2DSeg exhibit a high degree of monotonic consistency (ρ ≥ 0.893) with the biological endpoint metabolic activity (measured via ATP assay). This biological cross-validation indicates that the model’s dual advantages in suppressing background mis-segmentation and recognizing minute targets effectively mitigates non-biological noise interference and minimizes the omission of drug-fragmented organoid remnants. Consequently, it enables a more accurate quantification of drug-induced changes in the metabolic activity of organoid clusters.

Notably, the inherent heterogeneity of organoids as biological entities significantly impacts their appearance in OCT images and the subsequent segmentation performance. The contrast in OCT imaging fundamentally originates from refractive index discontinuities within biological tissues25,26. Consequently, varying biological states alter the tissue’s optical properties, affecting image quality and, by extension, the performance of segmentation algorithms. For instance, during lumen formation, a pronounced refractive index gradient emerges between the central cavity and the peripheral viable cell layer, manifesting as a highly reflective ring-like structure in OCT images. Conversely, treatment-induced fragmentation leads to the dynamic disintegration of organoid boundaries, resulting in time-dependent structural alterations that manifest in OCT as discrete, low-contrast granular signals heavily obscured by speckle noise. Such variations in image quality, driven by underlying biological states, are the direct cause of fluctuations in segmentation accuracy, posing significant challenges to both the precision and robustness of segmentation algorithms.

By adhering to a morphology-agnostic principle, DICE-2DSeg effectively mitigates the impact of such structural variations. Rather than relying on memorizing specific phenotypic templates, its structural extraction is governed by a network architecture constrained by physics-informed priors, specifically through the simulation of underlying optical interference mechanisms and the enforcement of inter-slice spatial coherence. This physics-driven structural design provides a solid physical foundation for maintaining segmentation accuracy and stability (Table 2) when encountering temporal morphological changes and macroscopic morphological variations across diverse cancer types (e.g., pancreatic cancer, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, as shown in Table 1). However, it must be emphasized that since DICE-2DSeg relies on physics-informed optical interference mechanisms, its efficacy is fundamentally bounded by the inherent information capacity of the OCT signal. In extreme cases, such as cellular fragmentation falling below the optical diffraction limit or dense necrotic cores causing severe signal attenuation, the underlying coherent structural information is irreversibly lost. Consequently, the model’s physical priors fail to distinguish these sub-resolution remnants from inherent speckle noise, leading to a systematic underestimation of residual organoid volume. This establishes a hard physical boundary for physics-driven segmentation approaches, indicating that the drug response might be slightly overestimated when relying solely on morphological readouts.

Beyond the aforementioned limitations related to image quality, our method is also constrained by the intrinsic physical boundaries of structural OCT imaging. Relying solely on the physical features of structural OCT means that, at a single time point, the model cannot directly differentiate between viable tissues and necrotic cell clusters that retain their structural architecture but have lost metabolic activity. Currently, our study indirectly reflects overall viability through longitudinal morphological metrics. To further elucidate the micro-physiological functional states within the organoids, functional imaging modalities must be introduced. For instance, dynamic OCT speckle analysis has been reported for evaluating cell viability and toxic responses27–29. Furthermore, the dynamic OCT technique27 developed by our team, based on the power spectrum analysis of temporal signals, has enabled the quantitative assessment of hepatocyte spheroid viability. Integrating the structural segmentation method proposed in this study with such functional imaging technologies holds the promise of achieving a more comprehensive characterization of organoid structure and function under label-free conditions, serving as a natural extension for our future research.

Alongside these modality-specific constraints, the clinical generalizability of our framework requires further validation. While our dataset (Table 1) incorporates unseen patients, independent drug groups, and cross-device heterogeneity within the test set to preliminarily validate the generalization capability of the method, all PDO samples were generated and cultured at a single institution. Variations in organoid culture protocols and matrix gel batches can potentially impact image characteristics. Therefore, to comprehensively evaluate the robustness of our framework across diverse laboratory conditions, systematic independent validation within broader multicenter institutions will be necessary in future work.

In conclusion, this study positions DICE-2DSeg as a critical technological enabler for large-scale organoid analysis, rather than a clinically validated screening platform. Although DICE-2DSeg achieves significant progress in balancing segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, the current version cannot directly support clinical treatment decisions due to the intrinsic physical boundaries of structural imaging and the limited validation scope of single-center data. Moving forward, we will continue to advance multicenter validation and the integration of multimodal technologies based on this framework, progressively driving its translation into practical applications for preclinical research and the future evaluation of personalized treatment strategies.

Overview

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the proposed DICE-2DSeg framework integrates a novel enhancement module, DICE (Dual-Stage Interaction and Complementary Exchange), with a baseline 2D segmentation network (e.g., CNN, Mamba, Transformer). The DICE module consists of two sequential stages designed to enhance segmentation accuracy and robustness for complex patient-derived organoid (PDO) OCT data. First, the Intra-Slice Interaction in Wave Domain stage (see the “Intra-Slice Interaction in Wave Domain” section) maps features into a wave space, implementing a physics-inspired interference mechanism to perceive and compensate for image degradation. Subsequently, the Inter-Slice Interaction in Graph Domain stage (see the “Inter-Slice Interaction in Graph Domain” section) uses a graph convolutional network to efficiently aggregate contextual information from adjacent slices, establishing a cross-slice receptive field that emulates a 3D network. This dual-stage design allows a 2D network to achieve segmentation robustness comparable to computationally expensive 3D models while retaining its efficiency.

Intra-slice interaction in wave domain

This module is founded on the concept of mapping image features into complex-valued waves30. Under the supervision of segmentation labels, it leverages the interference effects of waves to achieve adaptive feature enhancement and suppression. Specifically, it employs constructive interference to amplify organoid signals and destructive interference to attenuate background noise, thereby actively compensating for and enhancing ambiguous or missing feature structures in images with low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) and poor contrast.

Residual fusion and semantic reconstruction of wave-enhanced features

Inspired by ref. 31, we employ a content-adaptive weighting module to achieve dynamic fusion of multi-branch features. Compared to fixed residual connections, this method can dynamically adjust the contribution of different features based on image content, effectively enhancing the discriminability and adaptability of the fusion process. This improves the stability and expressive power of the final feature representation. Specifically, we first apply global average pooling to the three feature branches to extract channel-wise contextual information. Then, a lightweight learnable module generates attention weights for each channel across the three branches. After normalization via a softmax operation, we obtain weighting coefficients (αx, αy, αc). Multi-level feature fusion and segmentation prediction

Data acquisition

In this study, three-dimensional (3D) imaging data were acquired using two distinct types of optical coherence tomography (OCT) systems: a swept-source OCT (SS-OCT; Bio-architect Tomography-Eagle, Regenovo) and a spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT; Bio-architect Tomography-Lark, Regenovo). Each C-Scan volume has dimensions of 512 × 800 × 800 (Z × X × Y). The image resolution for the SS-OCT system is 5.9 × 5.4 × 5.4 μm3 (Z × X × Y), and for the SD-OCT system, it is 7.0 × 5.0 × 5.0 μm3 (Z × X × Y). These PDOs were constructed in a standardized, high-throughput manner directly within 96-well plates using 3D bioprinting technology13. The printed PDOs underwent continuous multi-day OCT scanning. Alongside the imaging data, endpoint cellular metabolic activity was quantified using a standard chemiluminescence-based adenosine triphosphate (ATP) assay 5,24. These metabolic readouts served as the biological reference for Spearman correlation analysis against the model-derived volumetric metrics.

Following data acquisition, the image annotation process employed a three-tier review protocol (with an average inter-annotator Dice coefficient of 0.7887 ± 0.0850), proceeding sequentially through independent initial annotation, secondary quality control, and slice-by-slice expert review to resolve boundary discrepancies. The annotations relied primarily on the biological interpretation provided by experts and incorporated cross-validation with bright-field microscopy images. By integrating the specific morphological features inherent to OCT, organoids were delineated based on explicit exclusion criteria. Specifically, non-biological debris, isolated cellular detritus, and matrix-related artifacts were identified and excluded according to their size (typically < 20 μm), morphology (lacking continuous boundaries), intensity distribution (low signal-to-noise ratio), and spatial context (absence of a coherent 3D structure). Conversely, genuine drug-fragmented organoid remnants that retained coherent structural features were meticulously annotated to ensure the accurate longitudinal assessment of treatment-induced disintegration. Additionally, histological staining (H&E and immunofluorescence) of samples from the same batch was utilized as a supplementary biological reference to evaluate the OCT morphological characteristics.

Baseline models

We compared DICE-2DSeg against a diverse set of baselines, including: 2D CNNs (FRNet34, GD-Net35); a large foundation model (MedSAM36); hybrid and pure 3D models (H-DenseUNet13, 3D-TransUNet37, nnUNet3D 38); State Space Models (MambaUNet39) and various inter-slice methods like AFTer-UNet14 (axial attention), SeqCorr-EUNet18 (RNN), and MedSAM215 (memory attention). This selection of models, covering 2D, 3D, graph-based, sequence-based, Transformer-based, SSM-based, and large model-adapted approaches, ensures a comprehensive and rigorous comparative analysis.

Implementation details

All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5. A combination of the Dice coefficient and cross-entropy was used as the segmentation loss function. For DICE-2DSeg, the number of adjacent slices was set to six according to the ablation study (see the Results section, “Context-level analysis of multi-slice fusion”). For evaluation on the test set, we calculated the accuracy metrics using a batch size of 1. Inference time per volume was measured using Python’s time library and GPU memory usage was recorded using PyTorch’s max_memory_allocated function. The best-performing checkpoint on the validation set was selected and used for testing.

Evaluation metrics

High-throughput organoid drug screening imposes stringent requirements on the time efficiency and computational cost of segmentation. We conducted a comprehensive performance evaluation of segmentation models on OCT images derived from high-throughput organoid screening by assessing three key aspects. First, we measured accuracy using Diameter-based Dice (2D) to assess scale-dependent fidelity by grouping objects by size, in addition to 2D Dice for slice-level consistency and 3D Dice for volumetric overlap. Second, we measured model robustness via the Background Mis-segmentation Rate. This metric is the ratio of false positive pixels (FP), where the background is mistaken for organoids, to all pixels classified as organoids (FP + TP). Finally, we benchmarked efficiency based on peak GPU memory, inference time (s) per 3D volume, and throughput (FPS).

To quantitatively evaluate the biological translational value, we systematically extracted four types of morpho-kinetic features based on the longitudinal volumetric measurements of organoids over three consecutive days (denoted as V1, V2, and V3, respectively): Endpoint Volume (V3), Absolute Growth (V3 − V1), Relative Growth Rate ((V3 − V1)/V1), and Cumulative Area Under the Curve (Cumulative AUC, ∑Vi). Furthermore, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was employed to quantify the monotonic association between these morphological features and the endpoint ATP levels. This correlation analysis aims to investigate the relationship between the longitudinal morphological metrics derived from the segmentation models and the metabolic activity of organoids, thereby assessing how improvements in segmentation performance impact the downstream quantitative analysis of drug responses.
