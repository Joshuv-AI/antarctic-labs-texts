Active Polymers Containing Silver Nanoparticles Combined With Formulations Based on Essential Oils: Quality Effect on Cereals and Dairy Products
Authors: Tofa Begum, Peter A Follett, Stephane Salmieri, Lana Moskovchenko, Monique Lacroix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTBioactive nanocomposite diffusion films containing plant essential oils (EOs) and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were fabricated and tested against foodborne pathogens, spoilage organisms, and insects in packaged foods. Films were based on chitosan (CH), poly(butylene adipate‐co‐terephthalate) (PBAT), and polylactic acid (PLA), containing cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), plasticizers such as glycerol (Gly) or polyethylene glycol (PEG), and three active mixtures of EOs/AgNPs (AC‐1, AC‐2, or AC‐3). Films were characterized by their mechanical properties, water solubility (WS), water vapor permeability (WVP), in vitro antibacterial/antifungal and insecticidal properties, and release kinetics in food simulants. PBAT and PLA films displayed higher barrier properties, and no solubility compared to CH films. Bioactive CH and PBAT films showed stronger antibacterial/antifungal, and insecticidal properties against Escherichia coli O157:H7, Salmonella Typhimurium, Aspergillus niger, Penicillium chrysogenum, Mucor circinelloides, and rice weevil Sitophilus oryzae, compared to bioactive PLA film. Challenge tests were conducted on rice and yogurt inoculated with selected bacteria and fungi by applying the diffusion films. Rice was treated with bioactive CH and PBAT films, in combination with gamma (γ)‐irradiation (0.75 kGy) and stored for 8 weeks. Yogurt was treated with PBAT and PLA films for 8 weeks. Results showed that bioactive films significantly reduced the growth of bacteria/fungi in both food samples. The growth of insects inoculated in rice treated with bioactive films in combination with irradiation (0.1–0.3 kGy) was controlled from the first week of storage. A combined treatment of irradiation at 3 kGy with bioactive films increased significantly the insect mortality up to 96%–100%, from Day 7 to 14 compared to treatment with bioactive films alone.Practical ApplicationsNanocomposite bioactive diffusion films developed in this study could be applied as diffusion devices of active natural compounds not only for the control of bacteria/fungi and pest insects in packaged rice (with a possible combined treatment with γ‐irradiation), but also for that of bacteria/fungi in yogurt. The active formulations could be used in the food industry for controlling insect proliferation in the sector of stored cereals but also to prevent bacterial and fungal contamination in cereals and dairy products such as yogurts and their derived products.Keywords: antimicrobial, bioactive films, essential oils, insecticidal, nanocomposites, nanoemulsion, silver nanoparticles

Introduction

Rice, Oryza sativa L., is an important staple food for more than half the world's population (Das et al. 2021b; Liu et al. 2020). Rice contains mainly carbohydrates and some proteins, with practically no fat (Das et al. 2021b). Rice is sensitive to biodeterioration by pathogenic bacteria, spoilage fungi, and pest insects, which can cause discolored, off‐flavor, unpleasant smell, reduced nutrition, and also secreted mycotoxins and excrete of insects (Das et al. 2021a; Hossain et al. 2014a). The rice weevil, Sitophilus oryzae L. (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), is one of the most destructive pests of rice worldwide (Hossain et al. 2019b). Otherwise, due to its nutritional values, rice is affected by many pathogenic bacteria and fungi, including Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Mucor, which also produce harmful mycotoxins (Guo et al. 2025). The presence of Enterobacteriaceae in rice, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., has also been reported. This microbial growth in rice grains generally affects the sensory quality of the product and makes it unfit for consumption (Myoda et al. 2019).

Dairy products including yogurt are consumed throughout the world because of their high nutritional value (Yangilar and Yildiz 2018). Yogurt is typically acidic (pH = 4.4) which allows for extended shelf life, but may still be susceptible to spoilage from growth of fungi and yeasts that are able to grow under acidic conditions (Viljoen et al. 2003). The most common spoilage organisms in yogurt are Aspergillus niger and Penicillium sp., Candida parapsilosis, Candida maltosa, Debaryomyces castelli, Debaryomyces hansenii, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Kluyvermyces (Gougouli and Koutsoumanis 2017; Lee et al. 2014; Moreira et al. 2001). The fungus Mucor circinelloides is responsible for swelling and bloating in yogurt during storage (Snyder et al. 2016a). Yogurt is also susceptible to bacterial contamination by food‐borne illness organisms including E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella Typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus (Siddiqui and Nadeem 2007; Lee et al. 2014).

Plant‐derived essential oils (EOs) are getting increasing attention in the food industry because of their antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, insecticidal, and antioxidant properties (Burt 2004; Turgis et al. 2012). EOs are secondary metabolites of plants composed of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and their oxygenated derivatives (alcohols, aldehydes, esters, ethers, ketones, phenols, and oxides) and they are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Commission (Hossain et al. 2016b; Turgis et al. 2012; Vishwakarma et al. 2016). Despite their strong bioactivity, their uses in food industry are limited because they may cause changes in the taste and aroma of foods at high concentrations. Therefore, methods to reduce the concentration of EOs, such as combination treatments with other antimicrobial agents, could reduce the negative sensory impact on foods (Mohammadzadeh 2017; Tardugno et al. 2019).

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are an important category of antimicrobial/antifungal agents widely used in packaging, pharmaceutical, food storage, and processing industries (Ghosh et al. 2013; Weisany et al. 2019). The combined use of EOs and AgNPs may improve the characteristics of both: (i) AgNPs improve the stability of volatile EOs by inorganic core, and (ii) EOs improve the stability and compatibility of the AgNPs by conjugating EO compounds (Gherasim et al. 2020). AgNPs showed bioactivities against a wide range of bacteria, fungi, and insects at low concentrations, and the treated biological entities cannot become resistant because of their high surface area and multifaceted mode of action (Ghosh et al. 2013).

Encapsulation of active agents into biopolymeric matrices can reduce the limitation of application of active agents in food products. Polymers such as chitosan (CH), poly(butylene adipate‐co‐terephthalate) (PBAT), and polylactic acid (PLA) are commercially available biodegradable polymeric materials (Khan et al. 2012; Su et al. 2020). CH, a polysaccharide produced by deacetylation of chitin, is used as a food ingredient approved by the FDA as biocompatible, nontoxic, and biofunctional, as well as an encapsulation matrix to preserve active ingredients (Hossain et al. 2019a; Khan et al. 2012). PBAT is an aliphatic‐aromatic copolyester, flexible, and compostable certified by the European Bioplastics (EN13432 standard criteria) and the Biodegradable Polymers Institute (ASTM D6400 standard specification; Morelli et al. 2016; Shankar and Rhim 2018). PLA is a thermoplastic polyester with good mechanical properties, transparency, and easy processibility prepared from renewable sources (Salmieri et al. 2014a, 2014b). Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) are used as reinforcing agents in polymeric matrices for improving mechanical and barrier properties and also improving the controlled and extended release of active ingredients during storage (Haafiz et al. 2016; Khan et al. 2013; Salmieri et al. 2014b). Combining bioactive packaging films with ionizing radiation (gamma‐rays) could result with lower doses for controlling foodborne pathogenic bacteria, spoilage fungi, and pest insects in stored cereal grains if the two act synergistically (Follett et al. 2013; Hossain et al. 2014b).

The goals of the current studies were to (i) develop nanoemulsions of active combinations (Eos + AgNPs), (ii) develop bioactive CH‐, PBAT‐, and PLA‐based nanocomposite films, (iii) characterize the bioactive films by measuring their mechanical properties, water solubility (WS), water vapor permeability (WVP), in vitro release profile and in vitro antibacterial, antifungal, and insecticidal properties, (iv) determine antibacterial, antifungal, and insecticidal properties of the diffusion films via challenge tests against foodborne pathogens in stored rice and yogurt, and (v) evaluate the synergistic effect between gamma (γ)‐irradiation and these bioactive nanocomposite diffusion films when applied on rice. The innovative aspect lies in the development of sustainable, bioactive diffusion films presenting antimicrobial as well as insecticidal properties in two different food models. These bioactive films aimed to prevent the proliferation of bacteria/fungi and pest insects in commercial rice packaging in combination with γ‐irradiation and to prevent the proliferation of bacteria/fungi in yogurt.

Materials and Methods

Essential Oils and Silver Nanoparticles

EOs of cinnamon, lavang, citrus, Asian formulation, Mediterranean formulation, and three types of AgNPs (AGPPH, AGPP, and AGC0.5) were used as active agents. Asian and Mediterranean formulations were bought from BSA (Montreal, QC, Canada), and citrus, lavang, and cinnamon EOs were purchased from Zayat Aroma (Bromont, QC, Canada). AgNPs were provided by NanoBrand (Laval, QC, Canada) and each sample was used at 1000 ppm silver concentration. Three active combinations (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) were prepared using AgNPs and mixture of EOs were developed according to Begum et al. (2022a). In the composition of formulations, AC‐1 was composed of AGPPH and cinnamon EO (ratio of 0.1:6 v/v), AC‐2 composed of AGC 0.5, Mediterranean formulation and citrus EO (ratio of 0.1:12:6), and AC‐3 composed of AGPP, cinnamon EO, Asian formulation, and lavang EO (ratio of 0.1:12:6:6). The major components of the EOs, Asian and Mediterranean formulations were provided in our previous study, based on the gas chromatography/mass spectroscopy analyses of the manufacturer (Begum et al. 2022b).

Bacterial and Fungal Culture Preparation

For the in vitro and challenge experiments, two pathogenic bacterial species (E. coli O157:H7 1931, S. Typhimurium ATCC SL 1344) and three food spoilage fungal species (Aspergillus niger ATCC 1015, Penicillium chrysogenum ATCC 10106, M. circinelloides ATCC 56649) were selected. E. coli O157:H7 1931 was provided by the US Department of Agriculture—Western Regional Research Center (USDA‐ARS; Albany, CA, USA); other strains were from Cedarlane (Burlington, ON, Canada). The bacterial and fungal species were preserved in trypto soy broth (TSB) and in potato dextrose broth (PDB) with 10% (v/v) glycerol (Gly) at −80°C, respectively (Ghabraie et al. 2016). Prior to experiment, the cultures were passed through two consecutive cycles in TSB for 24 h at 37°C and in PDB for 48 h at 28°C. Bacterial cultures were centrifuged and washed with saline water to obtain the bacterial concentration to 105 CFU/mL for each experiment. Fungal cultures concentration was adjusted to 105 spores/mL by collecting spores using saline water and filtered through sterile screen (40‐µm nylon screen).

Insect (S. oryzae) Culture

The Cereal Research Centre (Agriculture and Agri‐Food Canada, Winnipeg, MB, Canada) provided the cultures of rice weevil (S. oryzae L.) and the adults (200 insects, sex ratio of male and female 1:1) were reared in a well‐ventilated 2‐L plastic container. The container was previously filled with 1/3 quantity of sterile rice (super quality basmati rice; Saint‐Laurent, QC, Canada). The container was then incubated at 28°C/65% relative humidity (RH) for 12:12 h light/dark conditions. Gamma (γ)‐irradiation at 3 kGy was used to sterilize the commercial rice prior to each experiment, using a cobalt‐60 UC‐15A irradiator at a dose rate of 7.1 kGy/h (Sterigenics U.S. LLC, Laval, QC, Canada; Hossain et al. 2021).

Ethical Statement

The use of rice weevils was carried out in accordance with our institution's ethical guidelines. We regularly inspected the insects’ housing and food supply under optimal daytime conditions of temperature, RH, and light, as recommended by Agriculture and Agri‐Food Canada and the USDA. We ensured the natural reproduction of the weevils under these conditions, and no individuals were killed, thus ensuring a continuous supply of insects. As our study was conducted in collaboration with the USDA, it is believed our protocol complied with the guidelines recommended by any Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethical Review Board (ERB).

Development and Characterization of Bioactive Nanoemulsions

An oil‐in‐water (o/w) emulsion was prepared using a quantity of 2% (w/w) active combination (AC‐1, AC‐2) with 1% (w/w) Tween 80 as an emulsifier (MilliporeSigma Canada Ltd, Oakville, ON, Canada) and 2% (w/w) AC‐3 with 2% (w/w) Tween 80. Then o/w emulsions were treated with an Ultra‐Turrax T25 disperser (IKA Works Inc., Wilmington, NC, USA) at 15,000 rpm for 1 min, for getting homogenous mixtures (coarse emulsions of active combinations). Afterward, the coarse emulsions of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 were processed through a Microfluidizer M‐110P (Microfluidics, Westwood, MA, USA) at 6000, 8000, 10,000, and 15,000 psi, combined to 1, 2, 3, and 4 cycles, to obtain more stable nanoemulsions. The pressure and number of cycles of microfluidization for each active combination were selected based on a previous study (Supporting Information, Table 1). The developed coarse and nanoemulsions were characterized by determining their zeta potential (mV), Z‐average particle size (nm), and polydispersity index (PDI) by dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Saint‐Laurent, QC, Canada) at 25°C, followed a method of Ben‐Fadhel et al. (2022). Briefly, the emulsions were diluted in deionized water in the ratio of 1:50 for preventing light scattering effect. Preparation of Bioactive Diffusion Films

Three types of nanocomposite film matrices were prepared in this study: CH‐, PBAT‐, and PLA‐based films in the presence of CNC. CH (Chitoscience 85/2500; degree of deacetylation 82.6%–87.5%, viscosity 151–350 mPa s) was obtained from Heppe Medical Chitosan GmbH (Halle, Saale, Germany). PBAT resin (TH801T; decomposition temperature > 300°C) was from Xinjiang Blue Ridge Tunhe Polyester Co., Ltd (Changji, Xinjiang, China). PLA (Ingeo Biopolymer 3051D, injection grade, melt temperature 150–165°C, D‐lactide content 3.7%–4.6%) was bought from NatureWorks LLC (Northford, CT, USA). CNC was provided by the University of Maine (Process Development Center, Orono, ME, USA). The active combinations AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 were encapsulated into PBAT + CNC, PLA + CNC, and CH + CNC nanocomposite matrices to obtain a design of nine bioactive films respectively: CH‐1, CH‐2, CH‐3, PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, PBAT‐3, PLA‐1, PLA‐2, PLA‐3.

Bioactive diffusion CH films were fabricated using a solvent casting method. The composition of the CH films and their active formulations were selected based on a procedure by Begum et al. (2024, 2026). A quantity of 2 g CH was dissolved in 2% (v/v) acetic acid solution, followed by 0.12 g CNC and 0.5% polyethylene glycol (PEG; MilliporeSigma Canada Ltd) as a plasticizer. A volume of 1% stable nanoemulsion of AC‐1, AC‐2, or AC‐3 was added into the film‐forming solution and homogenized for 6 h using an IKA RW‐20 overhead stirrer (IKA Works Inc.) at 1500 rpm and room temperature. After mixing, 15 mL of film‐forming solution was poured onto a Petri dish and let them dry for 24 h at room temperature. Begum et al. (2024) reported that the reinforcement with CNC in these films provided enhanced mechanical properties, a higher water vapor barrier property and a controlled release of active components for an extended period during storage.

Bioactive diffusion PBAT and PLA films were fabricated using a compression molding method (thermoforming). The composition of the PBAT and PLA films and their active formulations were selected based on a procedure by Begum et al. (2025, 2026). For PBAT film, 2 g of PBAT were mixed with 0.375% CNC, 300 µL of active combination (AC‐1, AC‐2, or AC‐3), and 0.5% Gly (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Saint‐Laurent, QC, Canada) and then hot‐pressed using a Carver C hydraulic press (model 3912; Carver Inc., Wabash, IN, USA), under a pressure of 2.5–3.5 ton metric, at 140–150°C for 1–2 min, to form films loaded with AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3. For PLA films, a quantity of 1% CNC, 300 µL active combination (AC‐1, AC‐2, or AC‐3), and 1% Gly were incorporated into 2 g of PLA polymeric pellets and films were also formed by compression molding. As observed above for CH films, Begum et al. (2025) reported that the reinforcement with CNC in the PBAT and PLA films provided a higher flexibility, a higher water vapor barrier property and a slower controlled release of active components for an extended period.

Characterization of Bioactive Diffusion Films

Mechanical properties, WVP, in vitro release profile of active combinations from the optimized films, and in vitro antibacterial, antifungal and insecticidal properties were examined. The tested films were, namely, CH control (neat CH film composed of CH + PEG), CH‐1, CH‐2, CH‐3, PBAT control (neat PBAT film composed of PBAT + Gly), PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, PBAT‐3, PLA control (neat PLA film composed of PLA + Gly), PLA‐1, PLA‐2, PLA‐3. Tests are described briefly in the following sections.

Mechanical Properties

Tensile strength (TS), tensile modulus (TM), and elongation at break (Eb) of the films were measured using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) H5KT (Tinius Olsen Testing Machine Co., Inc., Horsham, PA, USA), equipped with a 100 N‐load cell (type FBB) and 1.5‐kN specimen grips, based on the ASTM D638‐99 Method (2015). The thickness of the film was measured by a Mitutoyo 543‐792B Digimatic Indicator (model ID‐S112EX; BDI Canada Inc., Laval, QC, Canada) at five random positions. The width of films was measured using a Traceable Carbon Fiber Digital Caliper (resolution: 0.1 mm; accuracy: ±0.2 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific). TS (MPa), TM (MPa), and Eb (%) values were automatically collected after film break due to elongation, using Test Navigator program (ver. 7.02.11). Measurements were taken for at least six specimens of each composition to obtain average values and standard deviations.

Microbiological Challenge Studies

Challenge Test on Antibacterial and Antifungal Properties of the Diffusion Films in Stored Rice

Bioactive diffusion films with higher bactericidal and fungicidal properties along with satisfactory mechanical properties, WS, WVP, and release profiles were selected for a challenge test in rice against two pathogenic bacteria (E. coli O157:H7, S. Typhimurium) and two spoilage fungi (A. niger, P. chrysogenum; Begum et al. 2022a). Furthermore, combining the bioactive films with γ‐irradiation at 0.75 kGy were applied to evaluate the possible synergistic effect for controlling the growth of bacteria and fungi in stored rice. Briefly, 500 g of sterile rice was poured into an inoculation bath of 105 CFU/mL of bacteria/fungi and mixed well for 30 s at room temperature. Then, the rice was filtered using a sterile screen to drain out the excess water and dried for 2 h under sterile conditions. After drying, 50 g of rice was taken out and placed into a sterile plastic bag containing bioactive films (1 g/cm2) at 28°C. For irradiation treatment, the inoculated rice samples with and without bioactive nanocomposite films went under irradiation treatment at 0.75 kGy using a cobalt‐60 UC‐15A irradiator at a dose rate of 7.1 kGy/h (Sterigenics U.S. LLC). The control sample was designed as rice inoculated with pathogens without bioactive film and irradiation control corresponded to 0.75 kGy irradiation alone. Microbiological analyses were performed weekly for 8 weeks (Begum et al. 2022a).

Challenge Test on Insecticidal Properties of the Diffusion Films in Stored Rice

The insecticidal properties of the nine bioactive diffusion films were tested against rice weevils (Hossain et al. 2014b, 2019b). The samples without bioactive films were designed as control. A total of 15 adult rice weevils were added into a plastic bag containing 30 g of sterile rice. Two (2) pieces of active films were inserted into the plastic bag and stored for 14 days at 28°C. Samples were divided into three groups: (i) one set containing only insecticidal film, (ii) a second set combining films with γ‐irradiation at doses of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 kGy, and (iii) a third set treated by irradiation alone at 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 kGy, to determine the insecticidal effects of these treatments during 14 days of storage. Challenge Test on Antibacterial and Antifungal Properties of the Diffusion Films in Stored Yogurt

Bioactive diffusion films with higher barrier properties and lower WS along with satisfactory biological properties were selected for a challenge test in yogurt against two pathogenic bacteria (E. coli O157:H7, S. Typhimurium) and three spoilage fungi (A. niger, P. chrysogenum, M. circinelloides). Briefly, diffusion films were cut 7 cm in diameter and fixed in the inner side of the lid of yogurt container. The yogurt was inoculated with 105 CFU/mL of bacteria/fungi, and the microbial analysis was performed every week for 8 weeks at 4°C. The control sample was designed as the yogurt inoculated with pathogens without bioactive film.

Statistical Analysis

Experiments were carried out in triplicate (n = 3). For each replicate, three samples were analyzed. Data were subjected to one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0; IBM Canada Ltd., Markham, ON, Canada). Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and differences between samples was assessed using Duncan's test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Development and Characterization of Bioactive Nanoemulsions

The particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and EE of the nanoemulsions AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 were tested and compared with their corresponding coarse emulsions (Table 1). Results obtained from Supporting Information (Table 1) were oriented in function of the highest EE (%) obtained. Indeed, the highest significant EE values (p ≤ 0.05) were obtained with 8000 psi/2 cycles for AC‐1, 10,000 psi/3 cycles for AC‐2, and 6000 psi/4 cycles for AC‐3, with respective values of 85.6%, 78.5%, and 82.0%. These EE percentages were significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than those of coarse emulsions, with respective values of only 32.0%, 19.1%, and 31.6%. Results in Table 1 show that the microfluidization at 8000 psi/2 cycles reduced the droplet size of AC‐1 from 162.0 to 76.2 nm (p ≤ 0.05; reduction by 53%). In a similar progression, microfluidization at 10,000 psi/3 cycles reduced significantly (p ≤ 0.05) the droplet size of AC‐2 from 122.7 to 90.0 nm (by 26%), and process at 6000 psi/4 cycles decreased that of AC‐3 significantly (p ≤ 0.05) from 257.3 to 85.0 nm (by 67%). These results suggest that microfluidization generated a strong reduction of particle size, converting coarse emulsions into nanoemulsions. The microfluidization pressure also had significant effect (p ≤ 0.05) on the zeta potential and PDI of emulsions. The zeta potential was −33.7, −36.8, and −35.3 mV for the nanoemulsion of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3, respectively, while that of coarse emulsions was −14.2, −18.7, and −21.5 mV, respectively, suggesting a notable, more electrostatically stable emulsion after microfluidization. PDI describes the degree of “nonuniformity” of a size distribution throughout the emulsion. Hence, lower the PDI, smaller the size distribution (i.e., higher homogeneity or more uniform sample). As observed with other DLS parameters, microfluidization significantly enhanced the stability of emulsions as PDI decreased from 0.6 to 0.19, from 0.54 to 0.22, and from 0.67 to 0.19 between coarse and nanoemulsions of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3, respectively.

To develop stable nanoemulsions, Microfluidizer machines (high pressure homogenizers) have several advantages such as obtaining ultrafine nanoemulsions, maintaining the droplet size distributions in a uniform range, and easy scale‐up. To get stable nanoemulsions, the pressure and number of cycles are important factors (Llinares et al. 2018, 2021). Several studies have demonstrated that higher zeta potential (≤ −30 mV or ≥ 30 mV), lower droplet size (≤ 100 nm), lower PDI (≤ 0.3), and higher EE is an indication of stable and stronger nanoemulsion by preventing aggregation, creaming, and coalescence (Asmawati et al. 2014; Ben‐Fadhel et al. 2020; Begum et al. 2025, 2026; Cheng et al. 2016; Hossain et al. 2019a, 2019b). In the current study, results showed suitable values of zeta potential for AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 between −33.7 and −36.8 mV meaning the obtained nanoemulsions were electrostatically stable. Further, these nanoemulsions were encapsulated into polymeric matrices to develop bioactive diffusion films for controlling the growth of pathogenic bacteria/spoilage fungi in stored rice and dairy products, but also pest insects in stored rice.

Characterization of Bioactive Diffusion Films

Twelve diffusion films were fabricated and coded as described in Section 2.6. All films were characterized by their mechanical properties (tensile strength, TS; tensile modulus, TM; elongation at break, Eb), WS, WVP, in vitro bioassay (antibacterial, antifungal, and insecticidal properties) and cumulative release of active combinations in food simulants for further applications in real foods. Results are described in Sections 3.2.1 to 3.2.6.

The structural properties of CH films by scan electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and UV–visible spectrophotometry were studied previously in our laboratories (Shankar et al. 2021). SEM images showed a smooth, compact surface of the films. However, in the presence of CNC, the films showed a rough surface with evenly distributed particles and more aggregation. FTIR analysis showed a new band at 1700–1750 cm−1 (C = O stretching vibration) relatively to ester, aldehyde, ketone groups of EO‐based formulation, and a shift from 1542 to 1555 cm−1 (NH– bending and stretching) after EOs and/or AgNPs addition, suggesting strong interactions with the Amide II bond of CH. UV–visible analysis also showed interactions between the active compounds and CH matrix, with two additional peaks at 315 and 355 nm (phenolic groups from the components of EOs), a broader peak at 430 nm (AgNPs), and a new peak at 385 nm (interactions between EOs and AgNPs). Besides, CH films containing CNC were characterized by FTIR, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), X‐ray diffraction (XRD), and SEM (Khan et al. 2012). Results showed the appearance of a sharp peak at 3342 cm−1 relatively to hydrogen bonding between CH and CNC, but also a higher intensity of the peaks at 1538 (Amide II), 1340 cm−1 (C–H bending), and 1054/1032 cm−1 (C–O stretching in CNC). TGA measurements showed a weight loss of 27.8% after CNC addition compared to 26.7% for control. A major weight loss occurred at 280°C in all films suggesting a negligible influence of CNC. XRD analysis indicated a sharp peak at 2ϴ = 25° supporting the combination of amorphous and crystalline peaks toward a transcrystallization effect. Finally, the results obtained by SEM showed results similar to those observed in our previous studies (Shankar et al. 2021), with a rougher, denser surface and more aggregation with CNC content (appearing like white dots).

Another recent study in our laboratories focused on the structure characterization of PBAT films in the presence of CNC and EOs/AgNPs‐based formulation by FTIR and TGA (Mahmud et al. 2026). FTIR analysis highlighted an increase of intensity in the broad band at 3630–3100 cm−1 (O–H stretching) and at 2960–2875 cm−1 (C–H stretching) with an increasing concentration of CNC in PBAT matrix. The increased peak at 1060–1018 cm−1 is related to C–O stretching mode in the cyclic structure (C–O–C) and typical C–OH linkage of CNC as also observed in our previous studies (Khan et al. 2010, 2012). The presence of CNC in PBAT matrix induced physical changes observed in TGA analysis. Indeed, the onset temperature of film decomposition (T onset) increased from 352 to 373°C after CNC addition, along with an increase of 90%‐weight loss from 367 to 384°C and no variation of maximum decomposition temperature (T max) in the range of 414–415°C, suggesting a high thermal stability of the PBAT–CNC film. However, the addition of EOs and AgNPs induced supplementary changes such a decrease of the T onset from 352 to 302°C, a slight decrease of T max to 313°C, globally suggesting that the degradation of films started at a lower temperature (reversed effect to that of CNC addition; Mahmud et al. 2026).

Films based on PLA were also characterized in our laboratories (Ardjoum et al. 2021). In this study, we have investigated the effect of EO addition on the thermal properties of the PLA films. Results showed an increase of the peak at 3300 cm−1 (O–H stretching) from the hydroxyl groups of EOs and the appearance of new bands at 800 and 817 cm−1 (aromatic ring C–H vibration) due to the aromatic and phenolic components of EO. The analysis of differential thermogravimetric (DTG) thermograms showed two weight loss derivatives with two stages: between 80 and 190°C (due to water removal and degradation of volatile bioactive compounds) and between 290 and 340°C (due to degradation of aromatic ring from EO and decomposition of PLA). Furthermore, a decrease in T onset of films with a T max of PLA at 328°C. was observed.

CH‐Based Films

The TS and TM of the bioactive CH films increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) with the incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions compared to the CH control film (neat CH). In current study, TS values were found to be 83.7, 96.7, and 88.2 MPa in CH‐1, CH‐2, and CH‐3 film, respectively, which was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than CH film (TS = 75.3 MPa). Significantly higher TM values (p ≤ 0.05) were also recorded for CH‐1 (1480 MPa), CH‐2 (1635 MPa), and CH‐3 films (1544 MPa) as compared to CH film (1279 MPa). Moreover, the flexibility (Eb) of the bioactive CH films was significantly lower than CH control films (p ≤ 0.05). Indeed, Eb values were 61%, 55%, 48%, and 44% for CH, CH‐1, CH‐2, and CH films, respectively. These values are in accordance with our previous studies that have delineated the enhancement of the TS and TM values of the CH nanocomposite films after incorporating CNC and EOs (Begum et al. 2025; Hossain et al. 2019a; Khan et al. 2012). de Mesquita et al. (2010) stated that the TS values of CH nanocomposite films were increased due to the interaction between the cationic amino groups in CH polymer and the anionic sulfate groups in CNC that might increase the interface between the filler and the polymeric matrix and caused higher mechanical strength of the resulting nanocomposite. Moreover, the mean‐field mechanical force may also be responsible for higher TS values because of the uniform distribution of the CNC throughout the CH–polymeric matrix without interactions (Khan et al. 2012). It has been observed that the incorporation of CNC into CH polymer increased the brittleness of the films which is a very common phenomenon in filler‐reinforced films observed by several authors (Hossain et al. 2019a; Khan et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2004). Hossain et al. (2019c) reported a 15% increment of TM values of methylcellulose (MC) nanocomposite films when incorporated 7.5% (w/w) CNC. The reduction of Eb in the nanocomposite films might be occurred due to the strong interactions between polymeric matrix and CNC that limited the movement of the matrix and thus lowered the Eb values of the CH nanocomposite films (Hossain et al. 2019a; Lee et al. 2004).

PBAT‐Based Films

The incorporation of CNC and ACs into PBAT matrix significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) the TM of the films. Indeed, the TM values were 76, 70, and 64 MPa for the PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3 films, respectively, while that of PBAT control film was 77 MPa. A similar behavior was observed in the case of TS values, with 24.4 MPa for PBAT control, compared to 19.1, 12.5, and 13.2 MPa for PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3, respectively. An opposite tendency was observed in the case of the Eb values. Eb increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) in PBAT‐1 films but decreased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) in PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 films compared to PBAT control (670%, 584%, and 619% compared to 627%). These observations are most probably due to a difference of composition between AC‐1 and AC‐2/AC‐3. Li et al. (2015) observed a TS, TM, and Eb values of 22.0 MPa, 90.4 MPa, and 817.8% of pure PBAT film, which supported the results obtained in the current study.

This study also showed that PBAT films (TM: 76.8 MPa) are flexible while the PLA films (TM: 1253 MPa) are rigid (Table 2). The incorporation of CNC and ACs into PBAT matrix acted as a plasticizer and reduced the intermolecular forces between polymeric chains, thus improving further the flexibility and extensibility, and lessening the rigidity compared to the neat PBAT control film (Begum et al. 2025).

PLA‐Based Films

The TS of the neat PLA film was found to be 66.7 MPa, which was reduced significantly (p ≤ 0.05) by 23%–45% after incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions into PLA matrix (51.3, 42.8, and 36.8 MPa in PLA‐1, PLA‐2, and PLA‐3, respectively). A similar TS value of the neat PLA control film (62 MPa) was found by Wang et al. (2016). Besides, the TM values of the PLA films were also significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) by 23%–37% after incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions compared to PLA control film, with respective values of 896, 968, and 788 MPa compared to 1253 MPa. As observed in CH films, the Eb values PLA‐1, PLA‐2, and PLA‐3 films increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) compared to neat PLA control film. The Eb values were 68.7%, 92.8%, 82.9%, and 36.3% for PLA‐1, PLA‐2, PLA‐3, and PLA control film, respectively. The global plasticizing effect of CNC + nanoemulsions could weaken the intermolecular interactions of the polymeric chains, thus, raising the mobility of the polymeric motion with lesser rigidity as well as higher flexibility and extensibility of the nanocomposite PLA and PBAT films (Begum et al. 2025; Cardoso et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2004). A similar trend has been observed by Sharma et al. (2020) and the authors prepared the bioactive PLA/PBAT composite polymeric films by incorporating clove EO (1%–10%). They found that the TS and Eb values of the PLA/PBAT composite films were 1.35 MPa and 5.63%, respectively. However, after the incorporation of clove EO into PLA/PBAT matrix, the clove components acted as a plasticizer, and thus, the flexibility of the films was raised significantly (Eb from 26% to 40%) and the TS values were reduced (TS from 0.79 to 0.94 MPa; Sharma et al. 2020).

Water Solubility

The WS values of the films are presented in Table 2. WS measurement of the film is a very important parameter in food packaging as it is an index of the solubility to water or high moisture content food. Incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions into CH matrix significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) WS by 24% in CH‐1 film (WS = 24.6%), by 17% in CH‐2 film (WS = 26.8%), and by 26% in CH‐3 film (WS = 23.7%) as compared to the CH control film (WS = 32.2%). In the case of PBAT and PLA films, the incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions did not significantly affect their WS (p > 0.05) because of their insoluble polyester nature in water, with a very low range between 0.59% and 0.87%. The significant reduction of WS in bioactive nanocomposite CH films might be due to chemical hydrophobic interactions and possibly crosslinking within polymer network. The results are consistent with some findings reported by other authors. Sani et al. (2019) have developed CH active films by encapsulating Melissa EO and ZnO nanoparticles, and they found a significant WS reduction (p ≤ 0.05) of these films compared to pure CH film. In the current study, the WS reduction of CH films could be due to the incorporation of hydrophobic EOs preventing water molecules from binding to the polymer via –OH groups. Herein, a transverse coupling reaction might have occurred between EOs and esters or amino groups of CH, causing a WS reduction (Abdollahi et al. 2012).

Moreover, interactions between polymer matrix and CNC could reduce the polymer solubility as CNC is stabilized in polymer network via hydrogen bonding (Sani et al. 2019). As a result, CNC might have increased the cohesion of the film which also reduced the free access to –OH groups to interact with water molecules and thus, reduced WS of the films (Escamilla‐García et al. 2022).

Water Vapor Permeability

WVP is an important parameter of the packaging films because water vapor from the atmosphere can play an important role in food degradation by accelerating chemical and enzymatic reactions, thus promoting microbial growth in foods and deteriorating food quality during storage (Hossain et al. 2019a). WVP results of the films are presented in Table 2. WVP of the bioactive CH films was significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) by 29.4%–32% after incorporating CNC + nanoemulsions, with lower values between 5.8 and 7.2 g mm/m2 day kPa. Moreover, the WVP of PBAT and PLA control films was 2.35 and 3.45 g mm/m2 day kPa, respectively, and the values were in agreement with previous studies (Begum et al. 2024, 2025; Morelli et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016). Incorporation of CNC + nanoemulsions into PBAT matrix induced a significant reduction (p ≤ 0.05) of WVP by 12% (2.07 g mm/m2 day kPa), 6.4% (2.20 g mm/m2 day kPa), and 8% (2.16 g mm/m2 day kPa) in PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3 films, respectively. On the other hand, an opposite phenomenon was observed in the case of PLA films after adding CNC + nanoemulsions. As observed, WVP was significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) by 8% (3.74 g mm/m2 day kPa), 4% (3.58 g mm/m2 day kPa), and 6% (3.65 g mm/m2 day kPa) in the PLA‐1, PLA‐2, and PLA‐3 films, respectively. Similar observations were obtained by several authors (Begum et al. 2025; Hossain et al. 2019a, 2019c; Salmieri et al. 2014a; Shankar and Rhim 2018). Salmieri et al. (2014a) who developed bioactive nanocomposite films based on PLA‐containing CNC and nisin for meat preservation found that the WVP values of bioactive films (WVP = 3.8 g mm/m2 day kPa) were comparatively higher than the PLA‐CNC control film (WVP = 3.2 g mm/m2 day kPa) at Day 0 (17% increment) even though these values were relatively in a low range.

However, several factors such as vapor diffusion, adsorption, desorption, and water affinity may affect the WVP of the films (Srisa and Harnkarnsujarit 2020). The presence of CNC throughout the bioactive PBAT and CH nanocomposite films could create a tortuous pathway which decreased the diffusion process and hence, slower the water vapor transmission rate of the films (Begum et al. 2025; Khan et al. 2012, 2013). Moreover, the presence of hydrophobic EOs in active formulations might also effectively prevent the diffusion of vapor from the bioactive PBAT and CH films and result in improved WVP of the films (Srisa and Harnkarnsujarit 2020). It can be assessed that a percolated network is created when CNC is incorporated into polymeric matrices by H‐bonding, which can reduce the molecular mobility and free volume of polymeric matrices and create more obstacles to water vapor diffusion, and thus reduce WVP (Begum et al. 2024; Pereda et al. 2012). Khan et al. (2012) studied CH films by CNC reinforcement for improving their mechanical and barrier properties. The authors found that the WVP of the CH films by incorporating 5% CNC into the polymer decreased by 27% compared to the control.

On the other hand, the bioactive PBAT films showed significant higher IC values (p ≤ 0.05) ranging from 62.9% to 88.3% against all tested microorganisms. The in vitro insecticidal properties of the films were tested against rice weevil using a fumigation toxicity test for 24, 48, and 72 h of exposure time. Results are presented in Table 4. The vapor effect of active formulations from the active nanocomposite films showed significantly higher insect mortality (p ≤ 0.05) compared to their control and increased the insect mortality during the incubation period. Bioactive CH films caused 2.6%–6.5% insect mortality at 24 h of incubation, followed by 23%–28% after 48 h, and from 31% to 40% after 72 h. Bioactive PBAT films caused from 0.5% to 38% insect mortality after 24 h, from 15% to 20% after 48 h, and from 29% to 38% after 72 h. PLA films caused 0.0% (no insect mortality) to only 2.5% insect mortality after 24 h, followed by 1.5%–5.8%% after 48 h, and from 18% to 26% after 72 h.

Several authors revealed that EOs, their components and nanoparticles showed strong fumigation toxicity effect against several stored product insects (Abdelgaleil et al. 2009, 2016; Benelli 2018; Chand et al. 2017; Hossain et al. 2014b; Khoobdel et al. 2017). The nanoemulsions with smaller droplet sizes have better insecticidal properties than coarse emulsions (Adak et al. 2020; Anjali et al. 2012; Choupanian et al. 2017; Suresh Kumar et al. 2013). Adak et al. (2020) have developed a nanoemulsion of eucalyptus EO that was evaluated against S. oryzae and Tribolium castaneum. Their results showed that the nanoemulsion showed stronger insecticidal properties with LC50 values in a range of 0.51–0.58 µL/cm2 and 0.86–1.86 µL/cm2 against S. oryzae and T. castaneum, respectively, while it generated higher concentrations of LC50 values measured at 0.83 and 4.17 µL/cm2, respectively (Adak et al. 2020). In the current study, results showed that bioactive CH and PBAT films (containing the nanoemulsion) had better insecticidal properties after 72 h than bioactive PLA films. El‐Bakry et al. (2019) developed an insecticidal formulation using Ag‐loaded 4A‐zeolite (ZAg) along with Rosmarinus officinalis (RO) EO against S. oryzae (L.) and Rhyzopertha dominica (F.). The authors observed that ZAg + RO EO accomplished a 100% mortality of insects after 14 days, while the application of ZAg (1 g/kg) alone took longest exposure time (21 days) to kill 100% and 96.7% of S. oryzae and R. dominica, respectively (El‐Bakry et al. 2019). A repellent study of a single, binary, and ternary combination of EOs (anise, eucalyptus, and peppermint EOs) and their major chemical components correspondingly (tans‐anethole, 1,8‐cineole, and L‐menthol) on S. oryzae was studied by Hategekimana and Erler (2020). The authors found that the binary and ternary combinations required a lower concentration of EOs to show RC50 (repellency concentration: the concentration required to show 50% repellency) as compared to the single EOs and their major chemical components (Hategekimana and Erler 2020). The results obtained in the current study suggest that the mixtures of EOs and AgNPs (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) have strong fumigation activities against S. oryzae when released from the polymeric matrices.

Release of Bioactives From PBAT and CH Films in 10% Ethanol

Figure 1 shows the cumulative release (%) of bioactive compounds from PBAT (Figure 1A) and CH films (Figure 1B) in a 10% ethanol food simulation, during a period of 70 h.

PBAT‐Based Films

Results showed that after 0.5 h, the release % of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 were 5.21%, 0.86%, and 7.54% from the PBAT films. Afterward, a release stabilization was observed after 5, 24, and 56 h for AC‐3, AC‐1, and AC‐2, respectively. In total, 26.3%, 28.8%, and 34.5% cumulative release of the active compounds was observed in final steady state (70 h) of the respective three active formulations. It is to be noted that AC‐2 formulation presented a slower release but finally generated the higher release % at the end of the simulation, via two inflexion points of the curve, compared to the two other formulations (presenting similar fuse curves).

CH‐Based Films

After 0.5 h, the release % of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 was respectively 0.58%, 1.47%, and 4.12% from the CH films. Compared to PBAT films, the release of compounds was much slower and increased gradually and constantly. Thereafter, only films containing AC‐1 initiated a plateau (release stabilization), at 36 h whereas the PBAT films tended to generate a continue but lower release %. In total, 31.5%, 26.0%, and 36.9% cumulative release of the active compounds were obtained in final, approximate steady state (70 h) of the respective three active formulations.

Release of Bioactives From PBAT and PLA Films in 3% Acetic Acid

Figure 2 shows the cumulative release (%) of bioactive compounds from PBAT (Figure 2A) and PLA films (Figure 2B) in a 3% acetic acid food simulation, during a period of 70 h. It was generally observed that the cumulative release % of active compounds were higher in 3% acetic acid than in 10% ethanol.

PBAT‐Based Films

At 0.5 h, the release % of AC‐3, AC‐2, and AC‐1 was 2.4%, 2.8%, and 4.4% respectively from the PBAT films. Afterward, the release of the active compounds (%) from the films increased rapidly (higher slope between two inflexion points) after 18 h and finally initiated a plateau (release stabilization) for AC‐1, and 48 h for AC‐2 and AC‐3. In total, 42.0%, 36.5%, and 46.0% cumulative release of the active compounds was obtained in final steady state (70 h) of the respective three active formulations. It is to be noted that, contrary to the difference in curves established in 10% ethanol medium, the three bioactive formulations presented similar trends in the same fuse in acetic acid medium.

PLA‐Based Films

After 0.5 h, the release of active compounds (%) of AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1 was 3.1%, 8.2%, and 12.6% respectively from PLA films. Compared to PBAT films, the release of compounds from PLA films was much higher and stabilized rapidly, in a same fuse. Hence, all films initiated a plateau (release stabilization) at an earlier period: 6 h for AC‐1, 12 h for AC‐2, and 36 h for AC‐3. In total, 69.0%, 68.0%, and 58.9% cumulative release of the active compounds were obtained in final steady state (70 h) of the respective three active formulations. It is to be noted that AC‐3 formulation presented a slower release before reaching the same plateau of release (%) of the two other active formulations.

Overall, these results show that in 10% ethanol simulant, PBAT‐2 films have a lower release during the 12 first h, with a very different profile compared to the other films (PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐3). This trend is then inversed between 12 and 70 h as the release of PBAT‐2 films continues increasing while those of the PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐3 films reach a plateau. In comparison, CH‐1 and CH‐2 films have a lower release than CH‐3 films, with a lower trend maintained up to 70 h. Finally, PBAT‐1, PBAT‐3, CH‐1, and CH‐2 films generate the lowest release after 70 h in 10% ethanol. In 3% acetic acid simulant, PBAT‐1 films have the lowest release percentage after 70 h, rapidly reaching a plateau after 12 h of release. Besides, PLA‐3 followed a similar trend of low release after 70 h, by reaching a plateau after 18 h of release. These release behaviors can be explained by the molecular interactions between film matrices and the bioactive compounds of the nanoemulsions and by the internal composite structure of films. Indeed, CH as a biopolymer, and PBAT and PLA as polyesters are highly polar compounds (hydroxyl, acetyl, and amino groups in CH; esters groups in PBAT and PLA). The polarity of these functional groups can influence these interactions between polymer matrix and bioactive compounds (Hossain et al. 2019b).

The Korsmeyer–Peppas parameters n, k, and R 2 were calculated for analyzing the release kinetics of AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 from the PBAT and CH films in 10% ethanol medium (Table 5) and from the PBAT and PLA films in 3% acetic acid medium (Table 6). The higher R 2 values (≥ 78%) for all tested films indicate that the kinetics model is suitably fitted for the release of the three active formulations from all films. In the case of PBAT films in 10% ethanol, the values of the coefficient n range between 0.26 and 0.32. These low values (< 0.5) indicate that the release of active compounds occurred through a Fickian or quasi‐Fickian diffusion mechanism. In comparison, the release kinetics from CH films are characterized by n values ranging from 0.54 to 0.63, therefore suggesting a release mechanism through polymer relaxation and a diffusion via anomalous transportation. The release of compounds from PBAT and PLA films in acetic acid medium generally occurred though anomalous transportation (0.56 < n < 1.02) except for PBAT‐3 that had a coefficient n of 0.43 (Fickian diffusion). In particular, the release of PLA‐2 showed a high value of n = 1.02, indicating this film induced a non‐Fickian transportation mechanism via the deterioration polymeric chains and release occurred both diffusion and relaxation process (Begum et al. 2025; Ben‐Fadhel et al. 2020; Requena et al. 2017). The constant of apparent release k was found to vary more between PBAT (k = 0.36–0.46) and CH films (k = 0.28–0.35) in 10% ethanol (Table 5) whereas it was similar between PBAT‐ (k = 0.27–0.35) and PLA films (k = 0.30–0.34) in acetic acid (Table 6). These results suggest that the release of compounds in CH matrix was more gradual in a controlled way compared to PBAT and PLA films, most probably due to its colloidal hydrophilic nature (Begum et al. 2026; Ben‐Fadhel et al. 2024).

Release of active compounds from polymeric matrices depend on several factors such as polymer morphology, polymer‐active combination interactions, molecular weight distribution, polarity of the food simulants, temperature, solubility of the active compounds in food simulants (Da Rosa et al. 2020). The current results are in accordance with the results obtained by several authors as they observed the release of active ingredients or EOs from polymeric matrices occurred by a diffusion mechanism (Begum et al. 2024, 2025, 2026; Ben‐Fadhel et al. 2020; Bustos et al. 2016; Petchwattana and Naknaen 2015; Requena et al. 2017; Tawakkal et al. 2016). Requena et al. (2017) studied the release kinetics mechanism of the eugenol and carvacrol from poly(hydroxybutyrate‐co‐hydroxyvalerate) (PHB‐PHV) films in different food simulants (10% ethanol, 50% ethanol, 3% acetic acid, and isooctane). Their results showed that the release of eugenol and carvacrol occurred through Fickian and quasi‐Fickian diffusion through PHBV matrix. It was also suggested that the incorporation of CNC/Gly into polymeric matrices improved the interactions with active formulations which reduced their migration by the restriction of compound mobility in polymer network (Requena et al. 2017). Based on our results, the bioactive PBAT films had a greater ability to retain active compounds longer than CH films in 10% ethanol but also longer than PLA films in acetic acid medium. From these considerations, further challenge tests would be conducted with PBAT and CH films in stored rice for controlling pathogenic bacteria, fungi, and insects but also with PBAT and PLA films in stored yogurt to control bacteria and fungi.

PBAT‐Based Films Against Bacteria

The results of the efficiency of PBAT films against bacteria are presented in Figure 3A,B. Results showed that the vapor effect of active nanoemulsions (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) significantly reduced the growth of both bacteria (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All nanoemulsions were generally antimicrobial with a same efficiency except for E. coli where AC‐2 was significantly more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) compared to AC‐1 and AC‐3 formulations. Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of E. coli was 3.47 log CFU/g for control (inoculated rice without film) compared to 2.54, 2.13, and 1.76 log with films containing AC‐3, AC‐1, and AC‐2 (Figure 3A). This corresponded to reductions by 0.93, 1.34, and 1.71 log, respectively. A noticeable significant reduction of E. coli (p ≤ 0.05) was observed all along storage with AC‐2 while no significant effects were observed between AC‐1 and AC‐3 up to 6 and 7 weeks of storage (p > 0.05). A similar trend was observed in the case of S. Typhimurium. A significant reduction of bacterial population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 2 weeks and remained lower during all storage, with a significant higher efficiency of AC‐1 and AC‐2 compared to AC‐3 (p ≤ 0.05). Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of S. Typhimurium was 3.73 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.78, 2.13, and 2.89 log with films containing AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 (Figure 3B). This corresponded to reductions by 1.95, 1.60, and 0.84 log, respectively.

The treatment of γ‐irradiation combined with bioactive PBAT films containing AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1 caused 86.2%, 77.8%, and 75.2% reduction of E. coli, respectively, after 8 weeks of storage (Figure 5A). This combined treatment also induced 89.8%, 86.9%, and 84.4% reduction of S. Typhimurium, respectively, after 8 weeks. However, so significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between the three combined treatments against both bacteria. These combined treatments were significantly much more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) than control (irradiation treatment without film) as the latter reduced the growth of E. coli and S. Typhimurium by only 21.8% and 28.6%, respectively.

PBAT‐Based Films Against Fungi

The analysis of PBAT films against fungi showed that their effect was more noticeable as the growth rate of fungi in control sample was much higher (Figure 3C,D) compared to bacterial growth (Figure 3A,B). Overall, the vapor effect of active nanoemulsions (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) significantly reduced the growth of both fungi (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All nanoemulsions were generally antifungal with a same efficiency except for P. chrysogenum where AC‐2 was significantly less efficient (p ≤ 0.05) compared to the other ones (AC‐1 and AC‐3). After 8 weeks, the level of A. niger reached 7.00 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.26, 2.70, and 3.21 log with films containing AC‐2, AC‐1, and AC‐3 (Figure 3C). This corresponded to reductions by 4.74, 4.30, and 3.79 log, respectively. Thus, a noticeable significant reduction of A. niger (p ≤ 0.05) was observed all along storage with all nanoemulsions compared to control but no significant difference was observed between them (p > 0.05) as they followed a same fuse. A similar trend was observed in the case of P. chrysogenum. A significant reduction of fungal population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 3 weeks and remained lower during all storage, in the presence of all nanoemulsions. However, a significant, least reduction of P. chrysogenum was observed (p ≤ 0.05) in the second part of storage (4–8 weeks) with AC‐2 while no significant effects (p > 0.05) were observed between AC‐1 and AC‐3 up to 8 weeks. Hence, after 8 weeks, the level of P. chrysogenum was 6.68 log CFU/g for control compared to 3.09, 3.45, and 4.22 log with films containing AC‐3, AC‐1, and AC‐2 (Figure 3D). This corresponded to reductions by 3.59, 3.23, and 2.46 log respectively showing a significant mold reduction in samples treated with AC‐3 as compared to AC‐2 (p ≤ 0.05).

The treatment of γ‐irradiation combined with bioactive PBAT films containing AC‐2, AC‐1, and AC‐3 caused 88.4%, 86.3%, and 83.4% reduction of A. niger, respectively, after 8 weeks of storage (Figure 5A). This combined treatment also induced 65.6%, 73.3%, and 78.0% reduction of P. chrysogenum, respectively, after 8 weeks. However, so significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between the 3 combined treatments against A. niger but AC‐2 was measured with a significant inhibiting property (p ≤ 0.05) against P. chrysogenum compared to AC‐3. As observed for bacteria, these combined treatments were significantly much more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) than irradiation treatment alone as the latter reduced the growth of A. niger and P. chrysogenum by only 31.0% and 27.0%.

CH‐Based Films Against Bacteria

The analysis of CH films (Figure 4A) showed that the vapor effect of active nanoemulsions (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) significantly reduced the growth of both bacteria (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All nanoemulsions were generally antimicrobial with a same efficiency. Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of E. coli was 3.47 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.76, 2.23, and 2.77 log with films containing AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐2 showing a significant lower level of microorganisms with AC‐2 as compared to AC‐1 (Figure 4A). This corresponded to reductions by 1.71, 1.24, and 0.70 log for AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1, respectively. A similar trend was observed in the case of S. Typhimurium. A significant reduction of bacterial population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 2 weeks and remained lower during all storage. Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of S. Typhimurium was 3.73 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.85, 2.46, and 2.89 log with films containing AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1 (Figure 4B). This corresponded to reductions by 1.88, 1.27, and 0.84 log respectively. AC‐2 exhibited the highest antimicrobial efficiency, with a significant difference compared to AC‐1 (p ≤ 0.05).

The treatment of γ‐irradiation combined with bioactive CH films containing AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1 caused 92.2%, 84.4%, and 81.8% reduction of E. coli, respectively, after 8 weeks of storage. This combined treatment also induced 85.5%, 79.6%, and 69.4% reduction of S. Typhimurium respectively, after 8 weeks (Figure 5B). A significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) was observed between the AC‐2 and AC‐1 combined treatments against both bacteria. These combined treatments were also significantly much more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) than irradiation treatment alone as the latter reduced the growth of E. coli and S. Typhimurium by only 21.8% and 28.6%.

CH‐Based Films Against Fungi

Similarly to the trend observed in PBAT films, the efficiency of CH films against fungi was more noticeable since the growth of control increased much more with a positive slope (Figure 4C,D) compared to bacterial growth (Figure 4A,B). The vapors effect of the three active nanoemulsions (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) significantly reduced the growth of both fungi (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All nanoemulsions showed a similar antifungal efficiency except for A. niger where AC‐2 was significantly more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) compared to the other ones (AC‐1 and AC‐3). After 8 weeks of storage, the level of A. niger reached 7.00 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.48, 3.21, and 3.44 log with films containing AC‐2, AC‐1, and AC‐3 (Figure 4C). This corresponded to reductions by 4.52, 3.79, and 3.56 log respectively with no difference between the three treatments (p > 0.05). Moreover, a noticeable significant reduction of A. niger (p ≤ 0.05) was observed all along storage with all nanoemulsions compared to control as they followed a same fuse. A similar trend was observed in the case of P. chrysogenum. A significant reduction of fungal population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 3 weeks and remained lower during all storage, in the presence of all nanoemulsions. However, no significant effects (p > 0.05) were observed between nanoemulsions up to 8 weeks. Hence, after 8 weeks, the level of P. chrysogenum was 6.68 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.89 and 2.11 and 2.74, log with films containing AC‐2, AC‐3, and AC‐1 (Figure 4D). This corresponded to reductions by 4.79, 4.57, and 3.94 log, respectively.

The treatment of γ‐irradiation combined with bioactive CH films containing AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 caused 85.0%, 82.6%, and 79.7% reduction of A. niger, respectively, after 8 weeks of storage (Figure 5B). This combined treatment also induced 88.9%, 87.7%, and 83.1% reduction of P. chrysogenum, respectively, after 8 weeks. However, so significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between the three combined treatments against both fungi. As observed for other tests with irradiation, these combined treatments were significantly much more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) than irradiation treatment alone as the latter reduced the growth of A. niger and P. chrysogenum by only 31.0% and 27.0%.

Begum et al. (2022a, 2024, 2025) found strong antibacterial and antifungal properties of active formulations AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 in vapor state via an agar diffusion assay using a modified Gompertz model. These formulations generated strong reduction of colony diameters due to their volatile components. Globally, they were able to decrease the colony diameter of E. coli from 2.4 to 0.6–0.7 cm, that of S. Typhimurium from 2.3 to 0.5–0.7 cm, that of A. niger from 3.1 to 0.2–0.7 cm, and that of P. chrysogenum from 3.2 to 0.1–0.2 cm. In the current study, two types of bioactive PBAT and CH films were used containing 300 µL of active formulation, with CNC and plasticizer. Furthermore, the active compounds present in AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 were released from the films which prevented efficiently the growth of these pathogenic bacteria and spoilage fungi in rice. Polymeric film matrices loaded with reinforcing agent CNC could control the release of active compounds and maintain their bioactivity over the storage time and thus, prevented the microbial growth in stored foods (Boumail et al. 2013; Deng et al. 2017; Hossain et al. 2019a, 2019c; Salmieri et al. 2014b). In accordance with our study, Hossain et al. (2019c) developed bioactive MC‐based films using oregano/thyme EO and CNC and applied the films in stored rice for controlling the growth of spoilage fungi (A. niger, A. flavus, A. parasiticus, and P. chrysogenum). The authors observed a fungal reduction growth in rice by 2 log at 28°C after 8 weeks. Moreover, the incorporation of CNC into polymeric matrix reduced the release of active compounds by 35% after 12 weeks in stored rice (Hossain et al. 2019c). A trinary biocomposite film composed of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), tragacanth, and beeswax loaded with AgNPs inhibited the growth of pathogenic bacteria Bacillus cereus, S. aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, L. monocytogenes, E. coli, S. Typhimurium, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Bahrami et al. 2019). A study by de Souza et al. (2022) allowed the development of a bioactive PBAT film using PLA‐Cinnamon EO capsules. The film was fabricated by solvent casting method for the preservation of strawberries; the authors observed the film potentially extended the shelf life of strawberries without compromising fruit quality and dehydration. Polyvinyl alcohol/CH‐based nanocomposite films reinforced with CNC and carvacrol could modulate the mechanical properties of films and increase their antioxidant properties, thus controlling the microbial growth and preventing the oxidation of stored products (Luzi et al. 2017).

Furthermore, combining ionizing radiation along with bioactive packaging films drastically reduced the bacterial and fungal growth in stored rice as compared to the treatment alone (either by bioactive film or by γ‐irradiation alone). Ionizing radiation of pathogenic bacteria and spoilage fungi can damage their cellular DNA by breaking chemical bonds, alter the membrane permeability of the biological entities, and dysfunction cell activity. When irradiation is applied with bioactive films, irradiation can increase contact between the microbial cell, activate the components released from packaging and facilitate cell destruction (Ayari et al. 2012; Begum et al. 2020, 2024, 2026; Caillet et al. 2005; Caillet and Lacroix 2006). The release of active components from AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3 combined with γ‐irradiation might have affected the cell integrity, accelerated the release of bacterial and fungal cell ingredients, and unbalance the internal pH and ATP synthesis (Caillet et al. 2005; Oussalah et al. 2006). Shankar et al. (2021) have done works on strawberry with preservation by using active CH nanocomposite films loaded with EO/AgNPs in combination with 1‐kGy γ‐irradiation. They stated that the combination between active film and irradiation significantly prolonged the shelf life of strawberries by 4 days. Results in the present study is also supported by those obtained by several authors (Caillet et al. 2005; Hossain et al. 2014a, 2019a, 2019c; Lacroix and Follett 2015). The combined application of irradiation (either γ‐rays or X‐rays) and oregano/thyme EO drastically reduced the growth of E. coli O157:H7, S. Typhimurium, and L. monocytogenes in rice (Begum et al. 2020). Moreover, the authors found that the D 10 values were 0.326, 0.266, and 0.236 kGy for the bacteria E. coli O157:H7, S. Typhimurium, and L. monocytogenes, respectively, when treated with a high dose rate of γ‐irradiation alone (9.1 kGy/h), while the corresponded D 10 values were significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) to 0.274, 0.218, and 0.219 kGy in combined treatment between the vapor of EOs and γ‐irradiation (Begum et al. 2020). Therefore, it could be stated that the combined treatment between irradiation and bioactive PBAT and CH films could be an effective strategy to control the pathogenic bacteria and spoilage fungi in stored food products. Moreover, the current study could be an alternative of chemical preservatives and petroleum‐based synthetic nondegradable polymer packaging, as it contributes to sustainable packaging goals.

The results of the effect of bioactive films show that all films showed a wide range insecticidal properties against rice weevils (Figure 6). The highest insecticidal properties after 14 days were shown by the PBAT and CH films, with significant higher anti‐insect properties (p ≤ 0.05) compared to PLA films. The PBAT films generated an insect mortality of 77.7%, 73.3%, and 58.0% with PBAT‐3, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐1 with no significant difference (p > 0.05) between PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 while the CH films led to a mortality of 68.9%, 64.4%, and 60.0% respectively for CH‐1, CH‐2, and CH‐3 with no significant difference (p > 0.05) between the three formulations. Moreover, the level of insect mortality increased throughout storage. In comparison, the mortality level did not increase during storage, showing only 2%–6% mortality during the period from 7 to 14 days with no significant difference over time (p > 0.05).

In the present study, the insecticidal properties of the different types of films were more similar in PBAT and CH films than in PLA films. These differences might depend on the diffusion rate of the bioactive compounds which mostly relied on several factors such as the chemical structure of the molecules, the type of polymer, the binding affinity between polymer matrix and active compounds, the volatility of compounds, their solubility, the temperature, the storage time, as well as the degree of polymer crystallinity (Begum et al. 2026; Hossain et al. 2019b; Sharma et al. 2021). Moreover, in the current study, CNC was incorporated into polymer matrix as a reinforcing agent and to improve the controlled release of active formulations throughout storage (Boumail et al. 2013; Deng et al. 2017; Hossain et al. 2019a, 2019c; Salmieri et al. 2014b). Hossain et al. (2019b) developed insecticidal CH‐, MC‐, and PLA‐based nanocomposite films using a mixture of EOs (oregano: thyme) as an insecticidal agent and CNC as a reinforcing agent to control rice weevils in stored rice for 14 days. The authors observed that the bioactive CH/CNC film had the highest insecticidal properties with a mortality of 43% at 14 days, then followed by MC/CNC‐ (27%) and PLA/CNC‐based film (20%), and these results strongly support our current findings (Hossain et al. 2019b). Hence, it is suggested that the encapsulation of active formulations (mixture of EOs and AgNPs) within polymeric matrices could control the release of active ingredients progressively from the films and protect stored cereals from infestation by insects for a longer storage time.

Hence, a new hurdle technology was introduced to prevent infestation of stored rice by combining bioactive films with γ‐radiation. However, numerous experiments performed on the use of EOs or AgNPs to protect stored grains from infestation by insects were reported, but very few studies aimed the combination of ionizing radiation with bioactive films for insecticidal purposes. In this study, the effect of γ‐irradiation at different doses (0.1–0.3 kGy) on insect mortality was applied alone as well as in combined treatment with the bioactive films, and results are presented in Table 7. It is shown that the insect mortality increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) with radiation dose as well as the incubation period. When γ‐irradiation was applied alone to control S. oryzae for 14 days, an increment of mortality (%) was observed from 3 days of incubation with higher doses (0.2–0.3 kGy) and mortality increased progressively during storage. A mortality of 71% was observed after a treatment of 0.3 kGy at Day 14, showing a significantly higher mortality (p ≤ 0.05) as compared to samples treated at 0.1 and 0.2 kGy for which mortality levels of 51% and 62% were observed. The control sample (no bioactive film, no irradiation dose) showed only 6% mortality on Day 14, which remained very low. Interestingly, the mortality observed in this study was slightly lower than previously reported by Hossain et al. (2019b), who irradiated rice weevils in rice at 0.1–0.3 kGy and recorded mortality rates of 65%, 79%, and 100% at Day 14. Moreover, Tunçbilek (1995) reported that 0.090 kGy γ‐irradiation eradicated 100% of S. oryzae after 2 weeks of incubation. So, insecticidal effectiveness shown by γ‐irradiation alone may vary depending on several factors such as atmosphere conditions, the age of insects (Begum et al. 2020; Hossain et al. 2021).

In the combined treatment of γ‐irradiation with CH films, the mortality of insects was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) as compared to one treatment alone (either by γ‐irradiation or by bioactive CH nanocomposite film; Figure 6 and Table 7). In the case of combined treatment with bioactive CH films, the mortality increased more rapidly and significantly (p ≤ 0.05) during storage as compared to control, with initial values ranging between 27% and 63% at Day 3 and reaching 100% at earlier stages of incubations compared to 0% for the control. Indeed, CH‐3 film combined with 0.3 kGy reached 100% at Day 7 (compared to 22% for control), while CH‐1 film treated at 0.3 kGy, CH‐2 film treated at 0.2 kGy, and CH‐3 film treated at 0.2 kGy reached 100% at Day 10 (compared to 41% and 55% for controls at 0.2 and 0.3 kGy, respectively).

Similar trends were found in the case of the bioactive PBAT and PLA films. But results obtained in this study indicate that PBAT had a more efficient response in combined treatment than CH films, whereas PLA films had the least efficiency. When bioactive PBAT film was treated in combined treatments, the mortality increased quite rapidly and significantly (p ≤ 0.05) during storage, with initial values ranging between 19% and 74% at Day 3 and reaching 100% at earlier stages of incubations from Day 3. Indeed, PBAT‐2 films treated at 0.3 kGy and PBAT‐3 treated at 0.2 kGy reached 100% at Day 7, while PBAT‐1 treated at 0.3 kGy and PBAT‐2 treated at 0.2 kGy reached 100% at Day 10. In comparison, the level of mortality with films alone was significantly lower (p ≤ 0.05) at the same days: only 49%–51% with PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 films at Day 7, followed by 40%–62% for PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐2 films at Day 10. The combined treatment with PLA film, showed a significant lower efficiency level (p ≤ 0.05) since 100% of mortality was obtained after 14 days of storage with PLA‐2 film treated at 0.2 kGy and PLA‐3 film treated at 0.3 kGy. The films alone showed a significant lower (p ≤ 0.05) mortality level with 38% for PLA‐2 and 44% for PLA‐3 films, after 14 days of storage.

Hence, results show a significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) level of insects mortality during the period from 7 to 14 days when bioactive PBAT films combined with 0.2–0.3 kGy were applied to rice, followed by CH‐2 or CH‐3 films combined with 0.2–0.3 kGy whereas bioactive PLA films showed a delayed response with total mortality after only 14 days when combined mainly with 0.2–0.3 kGy (Table 7). These results suggest that a dose of 0.1 kGy combined with films was not sufficient to reach 100% mortality at earlier stages (before Day 14). Insects have varying levels of ionizing radiation tolerance. Doses between 0.4 kGy and 1 kGy are recommended for insect disinfestation but a dose of 0.120 kGy can sterilize the rice weevil S. oryzae after 2 weeks of storage (Follett et al. 2013).

It has also been well‐established by several authors that the combined treatments of irradiation with bioactive films or plant‐derived EOs can enhance the radiosensitization of foodborne pathogenic bacteria, spoilage fungi, and stored products pest insects (Ayari et al. 2012, 2020; Begum et al. 2020, 2026; Caillet et al. 2005; Hossain et al. 2014b, 2019b, 2021). Hossain et al. (2021) worked with tea tree and eucalyptus EO along with γ‐radiation and X‐ray against S. oryzae and the authors stated that the application of EOs and irradiation treatment caused the mortality of rice weevil by 3–6 times higher than the treatment alone. In accordance, Ahmadi et al. (2013) reported a synergistic effect of the treatment between EOs (R. officinalis EO and Perovskia atriplicifolia EO) and γ‐irradiation on major insect T. castaneum found in stored products. R. officinalis EO at 4.2 µL/mL showed only 1% T. castaneum mortality and doses of 0.13 and 0.23 kGy of γ‐irradiation caused 1% and 5% mortality respectively when applied alone; however, combined treatments of EO with irradiation at 0.13 and 0.23 kGy increased the insect mortality up to 20% and 30%, respectively (Ahmadi et al. 2013). According to Hossain et al. 2014b, rice weevils became 4.1 and 5.3 times more sensitive to γ‐irradiation at 0.120 kGy in combined treatment with basil EO vapor at 0.12 and 0.24 µL/mL, respectively. This demonstrated that the control of stored products pests such as rice weevils by using irradiation could be enhanced by EO vapor with a high radiosensitization level.

Moreover, the application of such bioactive nanocomposite films in stored rice can release active ingredients slowly during the storage period and kill the adult weevils, although fumigation of EOs cannot negatively affect their eggs and larvae because of their location inside kernel. But application of low doses of irradiation could pass through kernel and affect the development of insects in all stages (Follett et al. 2013). Thus, combining two treatments showed a synergistic effect to control insect pests in stored rice. Furthermore, the application of films or irradiation separately required higher radiation doses and higher concentrations of EOs, which represents not only an expensive but also a time‐consuming process.

PBAT‐Based Films Against Bacteria

The efficiency of PBAT films against bacteria (Figure 7A,B) showed that the vapor of PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3 films significantly reduced the growth of both bacteria (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All diffusion films showed antimicrobial properties with a same efficiency for PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐2 films (p > 0.05) and a significant higher efficiency for PBAT‐3 (p ≤ 0.05). Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of E. coli was 5.55 log CFU/g for control compared to 0.85, 2.14, and 2.41 log with PBAT‐3, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐1 (Figure 7A). This corresponded to reductions by 4.70, 3.41, and 3.14 log, respectively. A noticeable significant reduction of E. coli (p ≤ 0.05) was observed all along storage with PBAT‐3 while no significant effects were observed between PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐2 (p > 0.05). A similar trend was observed in the case of S. Typhimurium. Indeed, a significant reduction of bacterial population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 3 weeks and remained lower during all storage, with a significant higher efficiency of PBAT‐3 compared to PBAT‐1 and PBAT‐2 (p ≤ 0.05). Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of S. Typhimurium was 5.86 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.14, 2.26, and 2.57 log with PBAT‐3‐, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐1 films (Figure 7B). This corresponded to reductions by 4.72, 3.6, and 3.29 log, respectively.

PBAT‐Based Films Against Fungi

The efficiency of PBAT films against fungi showed a significant vapor effect of PBAT‐1, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3 to reduce the growth of the three fungi (p ≤ 0.05) after 8 weeks of storage (Figure 7C–E). However, the rate of destruction was different between microorganisms. All diffusion films had antifungal properties but did not show a same efficiency except for A. niger where bioactive films generated a common fuse of activity and for which PBAT‐3 was significantly more efficient (p ≤ 0.05) after 7 weeks. After 8 weeks, the level of A. niger reached 5.55 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.56, 2.60, and 2.78 log in the presence of PBAT‐3, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐1 (Figure 7C). This corresponded to reductions by 3.99, 2.95, and 2.77 log respectively showing a significant difference between PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 (p ≤ 0.05). A different trend was observed in the case of P. chrysogenum. In this case, the action of PBAT‐1 generated an immediate inhibiting action with a level of 1 log CFU/g at Day 1 compared to 2.86–3.14 log with control, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3 (Figure 7D). Thereafter, PBAT‐1 followed a same increasing slope as the control whereas PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 films generated a constant decreasing curve with almost total inhibition of the molds at Day 8. Hence, after 8 weeks, the level of P. chrysogenum was 5.83 log CFU/g for control compared to 0.23, 0.62, and 3.33 log in the presence of PBAT‐3, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐3. This corresponded to reductions by 5.60, 5.21, and 2.50 log, respectively. In the case of the inhibition of M. circinelloides, all diffusion films were antimicrobial with a same efficiency (common fuse) up to 5 weeks (p > 0.05) and then, a significant higher efficiency of PBAT‐3 (p ≤ 0.05) was noted compared to the other films (Figure 7E). Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of M. circinelloides was 5.44 log CFU/g for control compared to 1.01, 2.00, and 2.58, log with PBAT‐3, PBAT‐2, and PBAT‐1. This corresponded to reductions by 4.43, 3.44, and 2.86 log, respectively. It is also to be noted that the initial level of fungi was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than that of control and was significantly lower (p ≤ 0.05) after 4 weeks—with a reduction from 4.12 log in control to 3.43–3.52 log with PBAT‐2 and PBAT‐3 and a continuous decrease with the three films, hence suggesting a higher resistance of M. circinelloides after inoculation in dairy medium.

PLA‐Based Films Against Bacteria

Results on PLA films are shown in (Figure 8A,B). Results showed that the vapor effect of PLA‐1‐, PLA‐2‐, and PLA‐3 films significantly reduced the growth of both bacteria (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. All diffusion films were generally antimicrobial with a same efficiency, and with a slightly significant higher efficiency of PLA‐2 and PLA‐3 (p ≤ 0.05) at the end of storage. Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of E. coli was 4.52 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.61, 2.87, and 3.36 log in the presence of PLA‐3, PLA‐2, and PLA‐1 (Figure 8A). This corresponded to reductions by 3.28, 1.65, and 1.16 log, respectively. A similar trend was observed in the case of S. Typhimurium. A significant reduction of bacterial population (p ≤ 0.05) was observed after 2 weeks and remained lower during all storage. Indeed, after 8 weeks, the level of S. Typhimurium was 5.86 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.74, 3.02, and 3.36 log with PLA‐3, PLA‐2, and PLA‐1 (Figure 8B). This corresponded to reductions by 2.50, 1.78, and 1.50 log, respectively.

PLA‐Based Films Against Fungi

The efficiency of PLA films against fungi, showed a less efficiency but a more constant effect during storage (Figure 8C–E) compared to PBAT films (Figure 7C–E). The vapors effect of all diffusion films significantly reduced the growth of the 3 fungi (p ≤ 0.05) during 8 weeks of storage. No significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between them at the end of storage with a plateau reached from 7 weeks (Figure 8C–E). After 8 weeks, the level of A. niger reached 5.55 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.82, 3.38, and 3.75 log with PLA‐3, PLA‐2, and PLA‐1 (Figure 8C). This corresponded to reductions by 2.73, 2.17, and 1.80 log, respectively. Very similar trends were observed with P. chrysogenum and M. circinelloides (Figure 8D,E). A significant reduction of fungal population (p ≤ 0.05) was measured after 4 weeks and remained lower during all storage, in the presence of all diffusion films. However, no significant effects (p > 0.05) were observed between them up to 8 weeks. Hence, after 8 weeks, the level of P. chrysogenum was 5.83 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.04, 2.28, and 2.41 log with PLA‐2, PLA‐3, and PLA‐1 (Figure 8D). This corresponded to reductions by 3.79, 3.55, and 3.42 log, respectively. The level of M. circinelloides was 5.44 log CFU/g for control compared to 2.45, 2.70, and 3.11 log with films containing PLA‐3, PLA‐2, and PLA‐1 (Figure 8E). This corresponded to reductions by 2.99, 2.74, and 2.33 log, respectively.

Several authors used EOs to extend the shelf life of yogurt by incorporating EOs directly into the yogurt (El Omari et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2011; Yangilar and Yildiz 2018); but this study is the first to focus on the release of bioactive compounds from yogurt pot lids. El Omari et al. (2020) extended the shelf life of Lebanese yogurt using Micromeria barbata EO (0.125 and 0.25 µL/100 mL). Thus, M. barbata EO (0.125 µL/100 mL) could significantly reduce the growth of fungi as compared to oil free yogurt without compromising the growth of starter lactic acid bacteria and organoleptic properties. Vázquez et al. (2001) used eugenol and thymol in Spanish cheeses to control the production of citrinin from P. citrinum. Authors found that 200 µg/mL eugenol could completely inhibited the fungal growth on cheese. According to Ahmed et al. (2018) the application of low‐density polyethylene (LDPE) film containing bimetallic nanoparticles (4% Ag–Au NPs) with cinnamon EO showed strong antibacterial activities against S. Typhimurium, L. monocytogenes, and Campylobacter jejuni in meat at 4°C for 21 days storage. It was also observed that combined Ag–Au/cinnamon EO was capable of a 100% reduction of S. Typhimurium and C. jejuni in meat during storage. Dehkordi et al. (2019) have evaluated the antibacterial properties of AgNPs and eugenol, alone and in combination, against S. aureus and S. Typhimurium in meat and milk. The authors found that combined treatment eugenol/AgNPs achieved important log reductions of S. Typhimurium and S. aureus in LB broth. Eugenol/AgNPs optimal inhibitory combination generated a 5‐log bacterial reduction after 3 h and 9‐log reduction after 24 h whereas single treatments induced reductions by 2 log after 3 h and 4 log and 5 log after 24 h against S. Typhimurium and S. aureus, respectively.

Conclusion

In total, nine bioactive nanocomposite diffusion films were developed using CH, PBAT, and PLA in the presence of plasticizers. CNC and bioactive nanoemulsions were loaded in each polymer matrix. Three active nanoemulsions (AC‐1, AC‐2, and AC‐3) were developed using mixture of EO‐based nanoemulsions and AgNPs. Optimized parameters of microfluidization (ranges of 6000–8000 psi/2–4 cycles) could significantly reduce the droplet size of emulsions to 76–90 nm, increase their stability (low PDI and zeta potential), and increase their EE (%) up to 78%–86%. Bioactive diffusion films were characterized with variations in mechanical properties: (i) an increase of TS/TM and decrease of Eb in CH films, (ii) a decrease of TS/TM in PBAT and PLA films and mostly decrease of Eb in PBAT (originally more flexible) as opposed to an increase of Eb in PLA (originally more rigid). A significant reduction of the WS (by 17%–26%) and WVP (by 29%–43%) was also observed in CH films after incorporation of bioactive nanoemulsions. All films showed strong in vitro antibacterial/antifungal properties whereas CH and PBAT films demonstrated higher insecticidal properties. Depending on the type of film, the release kinetics of nanoemulsions followed different diffusion modes after 70 h, with a slow‐release rate varying from medium with PBAT‐1, PBAT‐3 (27%–42%) and CH‐1, CH‐2 (29%–31%), to high release with PLA films (59%–69%). Kinetics model fitted for the three formulations with a Fickian profile for PBAT, a relaxation model for CH and mostly a non‐Fickian diffusion for PLA films.

Application of PBAT and CH diffusion films was carried out in stored rice for controlling the growth of spoilage bacteria/fungi and rice weevils along with irradiation. Results showed that the combination of bioactive films and irradiation acted in synergy and an increase of the microbicidal and insecticidal effects were observed compared to single treatments, after 8 weeks of storage. Application of PBAT and PLA films was performed in stored yogurt for controlling the growth of spoilage bacteria/fungi. Results indicated that bioactive films eliminated drastically the growth of microorganisms at 4°C, after 8 weeks.

It can be concluded that the application of bioactive PBAT and CH films combined with irradiation on cereal grains and the application of bioactive PBAT and PLA films on dairy products are effective and safe technologies to protect cereals and dairy products during long‐term storage as well as contributing to “green consumerism” and “sustainable packaging” in agriculture and food sectors.
