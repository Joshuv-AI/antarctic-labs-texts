Does DRG-based payment lead to unintended effects on care quality? A case under global budget with price adjustment in China
Authors: Xinyue Dong, Jing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe DRG-based payment system has gained widespread adoption globally, yet evidence about its impact on care quality remains scarce. In 2020, China implemented its inaugural national DRG payment initiative, the China Healthcare Security-Diagnosis Related Group (CHS-DRG), driven by the pressing need to regulate healthcare provider behaviors.ObjectiveTo evaluate the changes in process and outcome quality associated with the CHS-DRG under a global budget with price adjustment in Tianjin, China.MethodsA retrospective, population-based analysis was conducted on 932 354 inpatient cases from pilot hospitals and 608 940 from non-pilot hospitals between November 2021 and September 2022, utilizing social medical insurance claims data and DRG payment enrollment files. A difference-in-differences approach was employed to examine the impact on diagnostic and therapeutic technology utilization, patient care experience, and moderately to extremely severe outcomes. The corresponding primary outcomes were the proportion of laparoscopic surgeries, waiting time for surgery, 30-day readmission rates and all-cause mortality. Robustness checks and common trends tests were performed to validate the findings.ResultsThe improved efficiency in diagnostic and therapeutic practices was evidenced by a 19.17%-point increased likelihood of performing laparoscopic surgery (0.83%, P < 0.001) and a 53.20%-point reduction in the use of low-value drugs (-1.90%, P < 0.001), potentially contributing to a substantial relative decline of 2.57%-point in 30-day readmissions (-0.22%, P = 0.033) and 5.66%-point emergency department visits (-0.36%, P = 0.320). However, the CHS-DRG system was associated with longer waiting time for surgery by 5.96%-point (0.18 days, P < 0.001) and a 9.32%-point increase in the proportion of outpatient visits within three days before admission and after discharge (3.33%, P < 0.001), possibly compromising the patient care experience. Additionally, these changes were accompanied by a 6.88%-point increase in 30-day all-cause mortality (0.28%, P = 0.024). Notably, effect heterogeneity with respect to hospital characteristics and patient clinical severity was observed.ConclusionsThe observed positive and negative effects highlight the imperative for continued research into the mechanism design of DRG payment systems to safeguard process and outcome quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13625-5.

Introduction

A value-driven payment system can effectively align economic incentives, fostering sustainable health development by enhancing both the quality and efficiency of healthcare [1, 2]. Restructuring from traditional fee-for-service (FFS) reimbursement towards bundled payments provides a promising path forward [3, 4].

Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG)-based payment is a widely adopted model in which hospitals receive a fixed amount for services rendered and are financially accountable for the costs of each admission based on DRG classification [3, 5, 6]. Originally implemented in developed countries, this approach has recently been piloted by an increasing number of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), adapted to their national contexts to regulate medical costs [7]. China, as one such country, launched its nationwide DRG-based bundled payment program in 2019, covering the entire disease spectrum and all therapeutic regimes. This adaptation, branded as the China Healthcare Security-Diagnosis Related Group (CHS-DRG), was officially announced in 2020 under a universal coverage system by the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA), formerly the Bureau of National Health Insurance [8, 9].

Amid yardstick competition for optimal cost reduction, concerns persist that an undue focus on financial metrics may inadvertently compromise health outcomes [10]. Policymakers, however, have predominantly concentrated on the cost-containment potential of DRG-based payment systems, often overlooking their impact on care quality. Emerging evidence highlights the advantages of DRG-based payment — including greater transparency, improved efficiency, and cost reduction — while its effects on care quality remain insufficiently explored, inconclusive, and largely confined to high-income regions [11–14]. Notably, research on process quality across diverse disease categories is strikingly scarce, with most studies limited to specific clinical conditions or procedures [15]. For instance, Bäuml and Kümpel (2021) in Germany observed improved mortality rates among patients receiving high-intensity treatments under the DRG mechanism [16]. A similar decline in mortality for sepsis hospitalizations was noted in California’s MS-DRG system, while early evidence from Hong Kong indicated a reduction in in-hospital mortality in acute care settings [17, 18]. Hospitals in Taiwan, China also reported decreased cumulative medical costs and improved event-free survival rates for breast cancer patients under full-cycle care bundles, compared to the conventional FFS model [19]. In contrast, several studies have shown negligible or negative impacts on quality metrics [20, 21]. For example, analyses of the Korean DRG system found no significant trends in postoperative complications for treatments like adenotonsillectomy [22]. Similarly, the Swiss-DRG system showed no effect on ICU mortality, and Japan’s DRG-like grouping system exhibited a negative, albeit statistically non-significant, impact on mortality [23, 24]. Following the introduction of Swiss-DRG, patients with acute myocardial infarction in Switzerland experienced an increase in 30-day readmission rates, a pattern also observed in private hospitals in France [25, 26].

Variations in payment model design — encompassing goals, incentives, participant eligibility, implementation context, and whether it was determined prospectively or retrospectively — significantly influence outcomes. In Tianjin, China, a DRG-based payment program, set to launch in November 2021, is structured within a global budget framework that incorporates dynamic price adjustments (Appendix A) [27]. Given the mixed findings and limited evidence from developing countries, rigorous evaluations are needed to resolve inconsistencies and elucidate the relationship between care quality and DRG payment, accounting for institutional settings.

This study aims to examine changes in clinical process quality and health outcomes associated with DRG-based payment over the first one year of program in Tianjin, China, while exploring the often-overlooked hospital- and patient-level effect heterogeneity.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE and RECORD guidelines for observational studies using routinely collected health data.

Institutional background and settings

With a per-capita disposable income of 43 854 CNY — 136.24% of the national average — Tianjin ranks among China’s largest and most economically advanced provincial cities [28]. Its DRG payment model, adapted from CHS-DRG, marks a shift from retrospective FFS to a system that redefines hospital profit strategies. By prioritizing cost efficiency over service volume expansion, this transition aims to curb soaring medical expenses through predefined payment caps for specific diagnoses and procedures. A total of 646 patient classifications were established in Tianjin.

Due to the complexity of the reform, the most capable local providers — tertiary hospitals — were selected for intervention. Launched on November 1, 2021, the program initially involved 18 (34%) tertiary hospitals across the city on a semi-mandatory basis. That is, hospitals were directly mandated by the government to participate, but implementation was carried out in a staggered manner. Selection of pilot hospitals took into account geographic distribution, ensuring that each administrative district of Tianjin was represented by at least one participating facility. Once designated, hospitals were not permitted to opt out of the reform under any circumstances.

Concerns persist that DRG-based algorithms may undermine care quality by incentivizing hospitals to increase admissions and limit the use of high-value innovative technologies. Two featured practices in Tianjin may help mitigate these issues (Appendix A). First, the DRG payment system operated within a global budget framework with price adjustments, where a surge in admissions could expose hospitals to the risk of reduced per-case reimbursement, discouraging unnecessary hospitalizations [29, 30]. Second, a portion of cases is exempt from DRG and reimbursed separately via FFS, alleviating hospitals’ reluctance to adopt high-value innovative technologies. These intricate design elements further obscure the policy’s impact on quality.

Moreover, as a supply-side reform, only the funding allocation model to hospitals has changed, while insurance coverage, deductibles, copayment rates, and premiums on the demand side remain unaffected, thereby largely avoiding patient selection bias.

Data sources and study periods

This study utilized two primary data sources. The first is a comprehensive, case-level claims dataset that includes all health services covered under Tianjin’s Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance Scheme (UEBMI) and the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance Scheme (URRBMI) (Appendix B). The second is a classification file mapping each case to its corresponding DRG group. Due to data availability constraints, the analysis of post-discharge mortality was restricted to in-service employees under UEBMI.

The study spanned the period from 1 November 2020 to 30 September 2022, encompassing both pre-implementation (November 2020-October 2021) and post-implementation phases (November 2021-September 2022). This article specifically examines the first 11 months of the program. To the best of our knowledge, no pilot policies pertaining to cost or quality overlapped with the DRG-based payment reform. Other concurrent external events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, that may introduce potential confounders to our effect estimates would be discussed later in the paper.

Sample selection

Tianjin’s program provides a unique opportunity to examine the impacts of CHS-DRG using a quasi-experimental design, with admissions in pilot hospitals serving as the intervention group and those in non-pilot hospitals as the natural control group.

To ensure optimal comparability between the intervention and control groups in terms of healthcare practices and vulnerability to external shocks, the sample was initially restricted to tertiary hospitals [31, 32]. Among the selected hospitals, we first excluded psychiatric hospitals, rehabilitation centers, and nursing facilities participating in other payment models, as well as traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) hospitals due to their limited eligibility for DRG classification. Hospitals with fewer than 30 discharges during the study period were removed after matching for bed capacity, location, type, and grade (Appendix C). This resulted in a final sample of 41 hospitals, 18 in the intervention group and 23 in the control group, encompassing 1 541 294 cases (mean per hospital: 37 592 [IQR: 10 828 − 49 681]).

Procedures and outcomes measurement

Two dimensions were identified to assess public benefits: process quality and outcome quality.

Process quality was defined by two categories of indicators: the use of diagnostic or therapeutic technologies and patient care experience. The proportion of laparoscopic surgeries, a validated evidence-based proxy, was used to measure the adoption of advanced therapeutic technologies [33–35]. Additional prespecified measures encompassed the utilization rate of low-value drugs (including 20 nationally monitored nutritional and auxiliary drugs) and the proportion of grade 4 or minimally invasive surgeries, as all defined by the “National Tertiary Public Hospital Performance Appraisal Manual” from China’s National Health Commission [36]. Specifically, low-value drugs were identified based on a nationally endorsed list comprising auxiliary, nutritional, and clinically non-essential medications. This list was developed through a rigorous, standardized, multi-stage process involving centralized initiation, provincial-level selection, expert panel review, systematic evaluation, and consensus-based decision-making, thereby ensuring its credibility and applicability (further details are provided in Appendix D) [36, 37]. Patient care experience was assessed by examining waiting time for surgery (the interval between admission and procedure), the volume of examinations and diagnostic tests, and the proportion of outpatient visits occurring within three days before admission and after discharge. Peri-hospitalization outpatient visits, in particular, reflect potential cost-shifting from inpatient to outpatient settings — a strategy commonly employed by physicians to circumvent DRG-related financial constraints, though it may increase patient inconvenience due to additional hospital visits.

Outcome quality indicators were ranked based on severity, from moderate to extreme. For moderately severe outcomes, recurrence was appraised through the proportions of unplanned readmissions and emergency department (ED) visits to any hospital for the same conditions within 30 days post-discharge. Additionally, 30-day ambulatory care visits (and their associated costs) for all causes post-discharge were analyzed as proxies for recovery and symptom alleviation, with ambulatory care defined as all types of outpatient services provided at hospitals throughout the city. For extremely severe outcomes, 30-day all-cause mortality post-discharge was the primary endpoint, followed by the reoperation rate within half a month of discharge. Lastly, the association between this program and in-hospital mortality was examined (Appendix D).

Key focal points (primary outcomes) include the proportion of laparoscopic surgeries, waiting time for surgery, 30-day readmission rate for identical conditions, and all-cause mortality within 30 days post-discharge.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by prior research [15, 31]. Hospital characteristics were defined by bed capacity, accreditation level, geographic location, and type (general or specialized). Individual demographics included age and sex, while patient-level socioeconomic factors encompassed personnel type and health insurance type. Health status indicators incorporated primary disease diagnoses (ICD-10 code), admission type (emergency or outpatient), transfer status, annual inpatient encounters, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), including its interaction with age [38, 39]. Additionally, differential changes in these variables between groups from pre- to post-program were analyzed.

Validity tests and robustness analyses

Identification in the DID framework hinges on the critical parallel trends assumption, which posits that, in the absence of payment reform, pilot and non-pilot hospitals would have followed a common trajectory in their outcomes. We employed three distinct tests using pre-implementation data (Appendix F). First, unadjusted visual analyses were conducted to compare outcome changes between intervention and control groups. Second, we tested whether the interaction term coefficient between the intervention group indicator and a linear time variable spanning 12 months was zero. Finally, we augmented Eq. (1) by replacing the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Post$$\end{document} dummies with year-month dummies, employing the event study approach [43].

Additionally, we conducted a series of robustness checks to address uncertainty in the results (Appendix F). While placebo and sensitivity tests can enhance the credibility of the findings, residual confounding from exogenous factors, such as COVID-19 dynamics, remains possible and should be kept in mind when interpreting the results. Five sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the stability of the findings across different methodologies. First, we extended the DID approach by incorporating a linear time variable to relax the parallel trends assumption, allowing for distinct temporal trends between the intervention and control groups [13]. Second, we combined DID with propensity score matching (PSM), multi-group matching, and entropy balancing (EB) to mitigate potential biases from the non-random assignment of reform initiation and hospital participation [44, 45]. Third, a triple-difference (DDD) analysis was employed to control for unobserved confounders, enhancing the reliability of causal estimates through an additional layer of comparison [46]. Fourth, in October 2022, the government designated 15 additional tertiary hospitals to join the pilot. However, only two months of data were available following their inclusion, limiting our ability to adequately assess their performance. Therefore, we incorporated the second batch using a staggered DID (SDID) framework as a sensitivity test, instead of as part of the main analysis, to ensure that observed policy effects reflected genuine changes in care delivery rather than shifts in hospital characteristics [47]. Fifth, for regressions on continuous variables with excess zeros and high variance, we applied zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) and negative binomial (NB) models to correct biases in parameter estimation.

We also assessed the robustness of our results with respect to model specification and covariate selection. Sequentially, we included a socioeconomic status (SES) index, the squared term of age, disease-specific fixed effects (FEs), interactions between district FEs and health insurance type, interactions between hospital FEs and health insurance type, as well as hospital FEs and their interactions with district FEs in the regression model.

Subsequently, we conducted subgroup analyses based on hospital inclusion, case selection, and study timeframes to examine potential variations in results across individuals, hospitals, and time periods, with particular attention to minimizing time-varying confounding related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, analyses were stratified by hospital and patient characteristics to explore effect heterogeneity.

Furthermore, analyses of patient care experience, moderately severe outcomes, and extremely severe outcomes were re-conducted using alternative measurements (Appendix D) to mitigate potential biases arising from indicator construction.

Finally, placebo tests were performed to validate the robustness of the findings. First, a pseudo-policy implementation timeline was created by shifting the actual event date monthly from nine months prior to nine months post-implementation, verifying whether estimates peaked at the true program start. Study results

Among these, 578 859 patients and 932 354 admissions were in the intervention group, while 400 216 patients and 608 940 admissions constituted the control group. The proportions of admissions to general hospitals were comparable across groups — 63.39% for intervention and 64.47% for control group. Detailed characteristics are in Table 1.

No meaningful differential trends were observed between the intervention and control groups in most characteristics at the hospital and case levels pre- and post-program. For instance, the average age of admitted patients remained stable, increasing from 59.59 to 59.97 years in the intervention group and from 49.05 to 49.46 years in the control group (P = 0.793). Similar trends were observed for the proportions of females and UEBMI beneficiaries, with P-values of 0.150 and 0.827 respectively. The proportion of admissions to general hospitals slightly decreased in both groups (P = 0.707). Divergent trends were found in the CCI (P = 0.012) and emergency admissions (P < 0.001), raising concerns about atypical selection favoring healthier patients. This variation was further explored and addressed through subgroup analyses.

Quality of care process

Table 2; Fig. 2 present the DID regression results regarding process quality. In terms of diagnostic and therapeutic technology use, the proportion of laparoscopic surgeries in pilot hospitals decreased from 6.69% to 5.67%, compared to a 1.35% reduction in non-pilot hospitals. These findings suggest no significant compromise in care quality, as participation in the DRG-based payment program was associated with a 19.17%-point increase (approximately 0.83%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the utilization rate of low-value drugs decreased by 1.90% (95% CI: -2.05%, -1.75%), while the proportion of grade 4 or minimally invasive surgeries showed a slight decline of 24.12%-point (95% CI: -35.72, -12.51).

Regarding patient care experience, the unadjusted per-admission waiting time for surgery in the intervention group increased slightly from 2.98 to 3.02 days post-program, while in the control, it decreased from 2.34 to 2.25 days. The adjusted DID analysis attributed a 5.96%-point relative increase to the program, corresponding to a 0.18-day rise in waiting time (95% CI: 0.13, 0.22). Additionally, the volume of examinations and diagnostic tests grew modestly by 0.28 to 0.96% points compared to nonparticipants (P < 0.001). Following the reform, the proportion of outpatient visits within three days before admission and post-discharge rose by an average of 9.32%-point (3.33%, P < 0.001), implying more frequent hospital encounters for patients.

Patient health outcomes

Table 2; Fig. 2 also highlight the analytical results related to patient health outcomes. For moderately severe outcomes, both groups demonstrated an upward trend in the proportion of unplanned same-condition readmissions within 30 days post-discharge from November 2020 to August 2022. These increases plateaued after program implementation but steeply declined to below pre-program levels by September 2022, with a more pronounced reduction in the intervention group. The DID analysis revealed a significant relative decrease of 2.57% points (95% CI: -4.93, -0.21; P = 0.033), corresponding to an estimated reduction of 11 533 recurrent cases over the 11-month study period among 448 761 admissions in the pilot hospitals. Additionally, while the payment reform led to an economically meaningful but statistically insignificant reduction of 5.66%-point in the proportion of ED visits for the same conditions within 30 days post-discharge, a substantial decrease of 6.87%-point (-0.14%, P < 0.001) was observed in ambulatory care visits for all causes.

In cases of extremely severe outcomes, 30-day all-cause mortality increased post-program in both the intervention and control groups. Regression analysis showed that the intervention group experienced a significantly greater increase by 6.88%-point (0.28%; 95% CI: 0.04%, 0.51%; P = 0.024) compared to the control group. Moreover, the reoperation rate within half a month of discharge rose by approximately 1.04%-point following the payment reform; however, the DID estimate showed no statistically significant effect (0.03%, P = 0.675). Lastly, comparison of in-hospital mortality reported a small-sized increase by 0.01%, but the effect did not reach statistical significance.

Robustness checks

None of the modifications in the robustness checks materially altered the conclusions drawn from the main analyses (Appendix F).

First, consistent results were observed across various econometric methods. Similar patterns emerged with extended DID, DID combined with different matching techniques, DDD, and staggered DID, partially alleviating bias stemming from the non-completely random selection of pilot hospitals (Tables F2, F3, F4, F6, F7). Additionally, Zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) and negative binomial (NB) models yielded findings consistent with the main results (Table F8).

Second, re-evaluation with alternative model specifications and additional confounder controls reaffirmed the robustness of the estimates, which align with the main analyses (Table F9).

Third, our results remained robust to alternative definitions of patient care experience, as well as moderately and extremely severe outcomes related to the program’s process and outcome quality (Table F10).

Fourth, sensitivity analyses accounting for variations in study duration, population composition, and hospital inclusion/exclusion further corroborated the statistically significant associations identified in the primary analyses. Notably, although the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, multiple sources of evidence suggested that its impact on the findings was minimal and unlikely to bias the observed associations (Table F11, Figure F3).

Fifth, subgroup analyses stratified by hospital and patient characteristics largely confirmed previous findings, with consistency observed in 76%, 81%, 64%, and 84% of scenarios for the proportion of laparoscopic surgeries, waiting time for surgery, 30-day unplanned readmissions and 30-day all-cause mortality respectively (Table F12).

Finally, placebo test results presented in Figures F4 and F5 affirmed that the observed effects of the DRG payment reform on process and outcome quality were not driven by omitted factors. Notably, the most significant estimates emerged around or after November 2021, coinciding with the payment shock. And the distribution of estimated coefficients from 500 random assignments of the intervention group, based on the virtual group’s proportion in the full sample, centered around zero, indicating no effect for the randomly assigned groups.

Effect heterogeneity

We identified hospital- and patient-level heterogeneities in effects through subgroup analyses (Table F12). Seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR) were employed to assess group differences, accounting for potential correlations in error terms.

At the hospital level, general hospitals showed an increase in waiting time for surgery by 7.04%-point (P < 0.01), while specialized hospitals demonstrated no significant change (P = 0.443), resulting in an 8.51%-point difference (P < 0.001). The program also significantly reduced 30-day readmissions in specialized hospitals of 8.37% points (P < 0.001), with rates 11.44%-point lower (95% CI: 5.80, 17.07) compared to general hospitals. Geographically, hospitals on the city’s periphery saw a 26.35%-point decrease (-5.25%, P < 0.001) in laparoscopic surgeries and suburban hospitals experienced a 21.45%-point decrease (-1.12%, P < 0.001), whereas hospitals in the city center had a significant increase (1.63%, P < 0.001). For hospital size, those with more than 1 200 beds demonstrated substantial improvements in laparoscopic surgery utilization (27.16%-point, P < 0.001) and reduced surgical waiting time (-2.01%-point, P = 0.029). Smaller hospitals, by contrast, showed the opposite trend, with highly significant differences between the two groups (55.82%-point, 95% CI: 45.12, 66.51; -11.35%-point, 95% CI: -14.50, -8.20). Overall, better-managed hospitals — characterized by professionalization, larger size and central location — achieved superior health outcomes.

At the individual level, the proportion of patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery declined among those aged 19–40 and 41–60, while an opposite trend was observed in other age groups. Waiting time for surgery increased significantly by 9.52 (P < 0.001), 5.77 (P < 0.001) and 8.57%-point (P < 0.001) for the 19–40, 41–60 and 61–80 age groups respectively. In contrast, patients aged ≤ 18 experienced a significant reduction of 13.30%-point (P = 0.022), while those > 80 showed a negligible change (P = 0.897). By admission type, 30-day unplanned readmissions increased for emergency admissions by 5.74%-point (P = 0.034) but diminished for elective admissions of 5.20%-point (P < 0.001). Waiting time and 30-day all-cause mortality differed markedly between the two groups with estimated differences of -21.18%-point (95% CI: -25.88, -16.48) and − 19.57%-point (95% CI: -31.88, -7.26) respectively, indicating negative changes for emergency admissions and positive changes for elective admissions. Furthermore, 30-day readmissions decreased significantly among patients with higher CCI scores (P < 0.001) and more frequent annual inpatient encounters (P < 0.001), but had minimal impact on those with a CCI of zero (P = 0.785) and ≤ 1 annual inpatient encounter (4.38%-point, P < 0.001), with respective differences of -5.63%-point (95% CI: -11.11, -0.14) and − 0.76%-point (95% CI: -5.57, 4.05). In sum, payment reforms had minimal impact on clinically severe patients — typically younger or older, with higher CCI scores, more inpatient encounters, and emergency admissions — who, nonetheless, showed better clinical outcomes.

Discussion

As the first comprehensive evaluation of the CHS-DRG payment system, we employed a DID approach leveraging a large-scale database of insured patients in Tianjin, China, contributing real-world evidence on its cumulative effects on care quality.

Main findings and interpretations

Following the DRG program, the hospital’s reengineering of diagnostic and treatment protocols demonstrated both beneficial and detrimental effects on quality.

Performance in the pilot hospitals improved for moderately severe outcomes, with a reduction in post-discharge ambulatory care visits, leading to a significant relative decline in monthly outpatient care costs by 40.47% points (P = 0.049) and reimbursements by 49.18% points (P = 0.009) per hospital (Appendix F, Table F10). This, in turn, alleviated patients’ time and financial burdens associated with frequent hospital visits. Additionally, the DRG program was linked to a 2.57%-point decrease in 30-day readmissions for the same conditions, with no adverse effects on ED visits. These results contrast with previous studies, which generally reported no significant changes or even worsened outcomes, including higher readmission rates and more frequent ED encounters [15, 48–50].

Improvements in process quality likely underpin the aforementioned advancements. On one hand, a diminution in the utilization of low-value drugs under special surveillance by 53.20% points (-1.90%, P < 0.001), aligning with mechanisms documented in prior study [51], and a significant increase in the proportion of laparoscopic surgeries by 19.17% points (0.83%, P < 0.001) indicated that providers facing financial risks enhanced efficiency by curbing unnecessary services rather than limiting the adoption of advanced technologies [52–54]. Notably, Tianjin’s exemption mechanism, which allows critically ill patients or those requiring novel technologies to be excused from DRG payment, mitigated hospitals’ reluctance to offer advanced services. On the other hand, staff time for patients was not excessively compressed, averting adverse effects on clinical practices. This is evident in three key aspects (unpublished data; X.D., X.H., J.W.; November 2024). First, the DRG program led to a significant reduction in admission volume, likely driven by synergy with the global budget under a floating payment model. Second, no substantial reduction in length-of-stay was observed. Third, the percentage of cases transferred to other institutions showed a modest decline. These factors collectively reduced the incidence of insufficient medical activities, as highlighted in existing literature [29].

Extremely severe outcomes revealed some adverse effects. While no significant changes in in-hospital mortality were observed, the quality of care appeared unaffected, consistent with findings by Gluckman et al. (2020) [55], Wu et al. (2022) [18] and Bäuml and Kümpel (2021) [56] on the DRG-based payment model. However, 30-day all-cause post-discharge mortality increased significantly by 6.88%-point (0.28% absolutely), coupled with a modest, non-significant rise in reoperation rates within 14 days post-discharge. These diverge from prior assessments which predominantly endorsed DRG-based payment as an effective approach for improving outcomes and controlling costs [57], suggesting a partial impairment in recovery following prolonged and severe health events. Given that this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, part of this divergence may be attributable to pandemic-related factors. Disruptions such as the reallocation of healthcare resources [58], delays in care-seeking behavior [59] and infection-related mortality [60] may have independently contributed to elevated post-discharge mortality. This interpretation is supported by the observed increase in mortality in both the intervention and control groups (Fig. 1), as well as by the results of a sensitivity analysis excluding patients most vulnerable to COVID-19 — specifically, those diagnosed with COVID-19 or respiratory diseases. In this restricted sample, although the overall conclusions remained unchanged, the estimated effect on mortality was no longer statistically significant (Table F11).

The observed unintended outcomes can also be attributed to a decline in process quality, partially driven by cost-control pressures. From the providers’ perspective, a notable increase in diagnostic tests and examinations was observed, while the use of critical medications and high-complexity treatments — such as grade 4 or minimally invasive surgeries — was markedly reduced, presumably due to their higher costs. Moreover, patient care experience did not improve, as deviations from optimal treatment protocols by some clinicians altered clinical pathways. For instance, outpatient visits prior to admission and after discharge, as well as waiting time for surgery increased by 9.32%-point and 5.96%-point respectively, thereby exacerbating treatment burdens. Another explanation is that pilot hospitals may have failed to adequately adapt their long-term internal management to align physician incentives with the new payment model, ultimately undermining treatment efficacy [61, 62].

Furthermore, the discrepancies observed between short- and long-term outcomes — such as the differing impacts on in-hospital versus 30-day post-discharge mortality — suggested that factors beyond the immediate quality of care may be involved. Downstream influences, like post-acute care and home health care, which have been widely discussed in the evaluation of episode-based payment reforms [63, 64], may play a role. For instance, under cost-control pressures, providers may have inadvertently reduced attention to discharge planning, or some patients may have received suboptimal inpatient care and lacked access to adequate post-discharge support. The underlying mechanisms merit further exploration.

Notably, subgroup analyses revealed substantial effect heterogeneity, with more favorable outcomes observed among patients at both age extremes, those with higher CCI scores, emergency admissions, frequent hospitalizations, and those treated at larger, specialized, urban-centered hospitals. These patterns suggest that the impact of DRG reform may be not uniform but rather contingent upon both institutional capacity and patient characteristics. From an institutional perspective, the superior performance of resource-rich hospitals may be attributable to scale efficiencies and enhanced administrative capabilities. Such facilities typically possess advanced health information systems, dedicated coding and billing teams, and stronger managerial oversight, all of which facilitate accurate DRG classification and efficient resource allocation. In addition, these hospitals are more likely to have established clinical pathways and greater standardization of care, enabling consistent adherence to evidence-based treatment protocols. This organizational readiness allows them to contain costs while maintaining, or even improving, quality of care. From a patient-level perspective, better outcomes among clinically vulnerable groups may be explained by a combination of clinical prioritization and financial protection mechanisms. First, a risk-averse response to DRG incentives may have prompted providers to prioritize limited inpatient resources for patients with higher disease severity, cases in which discretionary cost-cutting is less feasible and adherence to clinical guidelines is more strictly required. As a result, these patients likely received more appropriate and timely care, leading to relatively improved outcomes under the DRG payment system. Second, Tianjin’s DRG exemption policies, which excluded high-need patients from strict DRG-based cost ceilings, likely reduced financial barriers to accessing necessary services. In contrast, lower-risk patients, whose care trajectories are more discretionary, may have been subject to strategic adjustments such as longer waiting time, premature discharge or reduced intensity of care, in efforts to create fiscal space under fixed-payment constraints. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for heterogeneity in the effects of DRG reform across both patient and provider dimensions. They also align with previous evidence suggesting that individuals with poorer functional status or higher clinical risk tend to derive greater health benefits from inpatient care compared to their healthier counterparts [65].

Policy implications

Understanding hospital responses and their potential effects — both positive and negative — on quality within the context of DRG bundles is of considerable interest to both policymakers and clinicians, especially as payment reforms evolve. In the transition toward value-based care, prioritizing patient benefits must remain the foremost objective.

For providers, ensuring high process quality is essential. Key strategies include enhancing care coordination among nurses and physicians across specialties and applying clinical pathways to standardize diagnostic and treatment practices. Prioritizing cost-effective approaches — such as minimizing low-value procedures, utilizing advanced surgical techniques and investing in early diagnostics, treatment and rehabilitation — is also critical. Additionally, regular shared-learning initiatives, including webinars, in-person meetings and regional collaborations, can foster collective improvements. Finally, performance-based bonuses should reward physicians or clinical teams for achieving equivalent or improved outcomes.

Governments must adopt quality improvement strategies to complement the DRG payment system. First, surveillance and assessment should be strengthened through monitoring systems integrated with routine data-collection networks and regular feedback on hospital performance and patient outcomes, as seen in Germany, Switzerland, and Austria [66]. The heterogeneity of results in this study underscores the need for targeted interventions in specific hospitals and populations. For instance, particular attention should be paid to strategic behaviors targeting patients with mild conditions and their potential adverse consequences, while technical and managerial support should be provided to resource-limited hospitals. Second, incorporating pay-for-performance incentives — rewarding high-quality practices with additional compensation and penalizing poor performance through budget clawbacks — should be considered within DRG frameworks. Objective quality metrics, such as the process and outcome indicators used in this study, like the utilization rate of low-value drugs, waiting time for surgery, 30-day readmission, 30-day all-cause mortality post-discharge, etc., can form the basis for evaluating provider performance. Evidence from higher-income countries, including the UK’s CQUIN framework and Germany’s pay-for-quality contracts, shows that such mechanisms effectively motivate providers to uphold high-quality care in order to secure full reimbursement [67, 68].

Strengths and limitations

This study advances the existing literature in several key areas. First, it offers the first empirical evidence on the impact of CHS-DRG payment system within a novel global budget framework incorporating price adjustments, proposing valuable insights for LMICs. Second, amid limited research on care quality, it integrates both process and outcome indicators to assess both desirable and undesirable efforts, deepening our understanding of changes driven by exogenous payment policies. Third, by utilizing unique provincial administrative claims data, which encompass inpatient and outpatient records for all socially insured beneficiaries across diverse clinical conditions, the study presents a comprehensive view of real-world practices, enhancing statistical power and precision. Fourth, leveraging an extensive quasi-natural experiment in Tianjin, we employed a DID strategy after carefully constructing a comparable control group, ensuring robust causal inferences. Finally, rigorous robustness checks validated the key identification assumption and affirmed the reliability of the findings, meanwhile effect heterogeneity was comprehensively explored through subgroup analyses.

Despite its strengths, this study had several limitations that merit attention. First, complete randomization for DRG program participation was unfeasible in the context of a natural experiment. As a result, pilot hospitals may differ systematically from later adopters (serving as controls in this study) in terms of institutional size, administrative capacity, and resource availability. Nevertheless, this potential bias was mitigated by the validation of the DID model through three common trends tests and consistent results across robustness checks, such as re-analyses using PSM-DID and DDD. Additionally, geographically determined participation and the lack of patient awareness regarding hospital payment models minimized self-selection bias at both institutional and individual levels. Meanwhile, the consistency of results after excluding switchers who sought care from both pilot and non-pilot hospitals eliminated the influence of patient interactions and reinforced the clear distinction between intervention and control groups. Second, the scarcity of medium- and long-term evidence highlights the need for ongoing monitoring of care quality post-reform [13, 69]. Although the use of time-varying participation through staggered DID affirmed the robustness of our findings, prior research suggests that effects may evolve over time as hospitals adapt to new regulations. Therefore, further validation is necessary to comprehensively evaluate provider performance after the inclusion of the second batch of pilot hospitals. Third, several data-related caveats should be acknowledged. Future studies should incorporate patient-reported outcomes, physician-centered measures of symptom management and external audits or confirmatory data (e.g., electronic health records) to provide a more comprehensive assessment. Fourth, further research is warranted to better understand the potential causal pathways linking process quality to outcome quality, such as the possible associations between longer waiting time for surgery, increased peri-hospitalization outpatient visits, and elevated 30-day all-cause mortality. Fifth, external factors such as the COVID-19 pandemic may present challenges to causal inference. However, as these factors affected all hospitals concurrently, their potential confounding effects were likely mitigated through the DID design and a series of sensitivity analyses. Future research should further validate the policy effects in non-pandemic contexts. Finally, this study was based on a within-region comparison in Tianjin, a municipality with a high level of urbanization and economic development, which likely facilitated smoother DRG implementation. Caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other settings. In less developed provinces or other LMICs, DRG adoption may encounter substantial barriers, such as incomplete electronic health records and limited clinician training, potentially diminishing its quality-related benefits. Future multi-site studies are needed to explore how DRG performance varies across different institutional and socioeconomic contexts.

Conclusions

The CHS-DRG payment reform in China has yielded both positive and negative effects on care quality under a global budget framework with price adjustments. Short-term improvements in moderately severe outcomes may be attributed to more judicious use of diagnostic and therapeutic technologies. However, the worsening of extremely severe outcomes coincided with a partial decline in patient care experiences. These findings underscore the urgent need for regulatory measures and a robust supervision system to monitor both process and outcome quality when structuring effective incentives within a DRG-based payment system. Notably, longer-term follow-up — particularly during non-COVID-19 periods — is warranted to strengthen the validity of causal inference and clarify the potential relationship between the pandemic and the observed mortality patterns.
