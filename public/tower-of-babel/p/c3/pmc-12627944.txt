Compromised lipid metabolism, mitochondria respiration and neuroprotective effects in iPSC-derived astrocytes from a Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome patient
Authors: Keiji Kawatani, Samantha K Baker, Jazmine D W Yaeger, Ruthellen H Anderson, Yingxue Ren, Zonghua Li, Hanmei Bao, Xianlin Han, Kevin R Francis, Takahisa Kanekiyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome (SLOS) is a rare, autosomal recessive disorder characterized by congenital malformations, intellectual disability, and behavioral abnormalities. SLOS results from mutations in the DHCR7 gene, leading to impaired cholesterol biosynthesis due to dysregulation of 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase. Cholesterol plays crucial roles in neurophysiology, including synaptic formation and neurotransmitter receptor regulation, which likely contribute to neurological manifestations in SLOS patients. While astrocytes are the main cholesterol producing cells in the brain, their specific role in SLOS pathogenesis remains unclear. In this study, we utilized induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived astrocytes from a SLOS patient with DHCR7 c.C278T mutation and the isogenic control. We found decreased lipid droplet formation in SLOS iPSC astrocytes compared to controls, accompanied with diminished efflux of cholesterol and apolipoprotein E. Lipidomics revealed reduced cholesterol and cholesterol esters, as well as altered profiles of other lipids in SLOS iPSC astrocytes. While RNA-sequencing identified various genes and pathways affected by the disease status, those related to mitochondria functions were top-ranked. Mitochondrial electron transport chain oxidative phosphorylation gene expression decreased in SLOS iPSC astrocytes, alongside impaired mitochondrial respiration. Furthermore, SLOS iPSC astrocytes less effectively mediated neuroprotection on iPSC neurons than control astrocytes in serum-starvation conditions. SLOS iPSC astrocytes also poorly contributed to synaptic networks when co-cultured with iPSC neurons. Overall, our findings provide mechanistic insights into how DHCR7 disruption impacts astrocyte function, contributing to SLOS neuropathology by dysregulating lipid metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and impaired neuroprotection.Keywords: Astrocyte, iPSC, DHCR7, Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome, lipids

Introduction

Smith-Opitz-Lemli syndrome (SLOS) is a rare autosomal recessive condition accompanied with congenital malformations and intellectual disability [1]. Patients with SLOS present with devastating behavioral problems, cardiac defects, and growth restrictions [1, 2]. SLOS is also often characterized by anatomical abnormalities, including microcephaly, characteristic facial malformations, abnormal external genitalia, and syndactyly of digits [3, 4]. The prevalence of SLOS in North America is approximately 1/40000, though cases may be underestimated due to overlapping clinical features with other diseases and heterogenous clinical phenotypes impacting presentation and disease severity [3, 5]. SLOS is caused by inherited mutations in the DHCR7 gene, coding for the enzyme 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase [6–8]. Over 150 different DHCR7 variants have been identified in SLOS patients [9]. Interestingly, compound heterozygous DHCR7 mutations have been reported to induce severe phenotypes comparable to homozygous mutations, whereas single heterozygous mutations typically do not exhibit clinical presentation [10].

DHCR7 catalyzes the final step of cholesterol biosynthesis in the Kandutsch-Russell pathway through the reduction of the Δ 7,8 bond of 7-dehydrocholesterol [11, 12]. Since DHCR7 is the only enzyme capable of performing this catalysis step, patients with complete loss of function DHCR7 mutations are unable to produce cholesterol from 7-dehydrocholesterol, resulting in embryonic or early postnatal lethality [5, 13, 14]. Despite diversity in DHCR7 variants, SLOS is consistently characterized by low levels of serum cholesterol and elevated 7-dehydrocholesterol, establishing 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase loss of function as the defining feature of the condition [3]. Cholesterol depletion is detrimental to mammalian development [15] as cholesterol is the most abundant lipid in the plasmid membrane and modulates cellular functions such as trafficking and cellular dynamics [15, 16]. Cholesterol also regulates vital developmental pathways, including acting as a negative regulator of Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling [17, 18], and positive regulator of Wnt signaling [19]. The distinctive role that cholesterol plays during early development likely accounts for congenital abnormalities in SLOS patients. However, cholesterol is indispensable for neurophysiological functions. Dysregulated cholesterol metabolism is associated with several neurological diseases, including Alzheimer disease [20], Parkinson disease [21], and schizophrenia [22, 23]. This can be partially attributed to cholesterol’s role in receptor membrane organization and dynamics. Previous studies have illustrated the need for cholesterol in regulating function and location of neurotransmitter receptors, including the serotonin1A receptor [24, 25]. Similarly, neurons require glial-produced cholesterol to form synapses [26–28]. Given the importance of lipid metabolism in neurophysiology, behavioral, cognitive, and intellectual disabilities are neurological hallmarks of SLOS.

Astrocytes are the main cells synthesizing cholesterol in the brain and largely responsible for supplying metabolic products to neurons [29]. Astrocyte-synthesized cholesterol is vital for homeostatic neuronal functions, such as the regulation of ion permeability in neurons and the formation of synapses [27, 30]. Our previous work demonstrated Dhcr7 inhibition in mouse astrocytes resulted in neuroinflammatory signaling and impaired astrocyte function. However, how astrocytic cholesterol metabolism contributes to SLOS pathogenesis requires further elucidation. Thus, we investigated the impact of DHCR7 on astrocyte signaling and function using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models of SLOS. We demonstrated that SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes exhibit distinct functional abnormalities, including heightened proliferation capacities, dysregulation of lipid species, distinct mitochondrial dysfunction, and, most notably, diminished neuroprotective effects.

Results

Altered metabolism of cholesterol and other lipids in the astrocytes derived from SLOS iPSCs

To model SLOS within an isogenic in vitro system, iPSCs previously generated from a SLOS patient harboring a DHCR7 c.C278T mutation on one allele underwent CRISPR/Cas9 mediated-genetic correction. Following clonal selection and expansion, the isogenic iPSCs maintained expression of pluripotent markers SSEA4, OCT4, TRA-1-60, and β-catenin (Fig. 1A). Genetic correction of the c.C278T mutation was confirmed by sequencing of the DHCR7 allele (Fig. 1B). Functional correction of DHCR7 activity was demonstrated by GC–MS analyses of cholesterol and 7-dehydrocholesterol content within iPSCs maintained in cholesterol-deficient mTeSR1 media. While parental SLOS iPSCs exhibited reduced cholesterol levels and elevated 7-dehydrocholesterol, the isogenic iPSC line was biochemically normal (Fig. 1C). To analyze DHCR7 impacts on astrocyte biology, SLOS and isogenic control iPSCs were first differentiated into neural precursor cells (NPCs) and subsequently astrocytes using established methods [31, 32]. At day 45 of differentiation from NPCs, we confirmed successful generation of astrocytes from control and SLOS iPSCs by immunostaining for astrocyte markers S100β and GFAP (Fig. 1D). When iPSC-derived astrocytes were subjected to EdU proliferation assays, we observed a difference in proliferation rates between the two groups with SLOS astrocytes dividing at over twice the rate of control astrocytes (Fig. 1E). To investigate lipid droplet formation, astrocytes were immunostained with BODIPY. We observed less intracellular accumulation of neutral lipids detected by BODIPY in SLOS astrocytes compared to isogenic controls (Fig. 1F). While there was no difference in the uptake of BODIPY-conjugated cholesterol (Fig. 1G), the release of BODIPY-conjugated cholesterol into the culture media from SLOS astrocytes was lower than control astrocytes using recombinant APOA1 as an acceptor (Fig. 1H). Endogenous APOE were also reduced in the culture media from SLOS astrocytes in comparison to controls (Fig. 1I). These results suggest that cholesterol efflux capacity is diminished in SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes, likely as compensation for the reduction of intracellular cholesterol storage.

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of how altered cholesterol metabolism influences the overall lipid profile of SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes, we performed lipidomic analysis in control and SLOS astrocytes. We confirmed that total cholesterol (TC), free cholesterol (FC), and cholesterol esters (ce) were all reduced in SLOS astrocytes, although total and subclasses of triacylglyceride (TAG) and phosphatidylglycerol (PG) were increased in comparison to controls (Fig. 2A and B). Higher levels of subclasses of phospholipids including phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) were detected in SLOS astrocytes (Fig. 2B). We also found significantly lower levels of acylcarnitine (CAR), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) in SLOS astrocytes than controls (Fig. 2A and B). Moreover, weighted correlation network analysis of lipidomics results identified one downregulated module (MEbrown) and one upregulated module (MEblue) (Fig. 2C). The top 10 hub lipids included subclasses of phosphatidylinositol (PI), LPE, and LPC as well as CE_22:5 and CAR_20:1 in MEbrown, (Fig. 2D), while MEblue was enriched for TAG subclasses (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that DHCR7 mutation manifests perturbed cholesterol metabolism pathways and broadly alters the lipid profile of SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes.

Altered transcriptome profiles in astrocytes derived from SLOS iPSCs

Two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) through bulk-RNA sequencing of control and SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes revealed clustering of gene expression between the groups (Fig. 3A). By evaluating the fold change of gene expression in SLOS astrocytes compared to controls, we observed a remarkable abundance of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Several homeobox genes including HOXA3, HOXB3, HOXB4, and HOXB5 were upregulated in SLOS astrocytes, while NKX2–1 coding NK2 homeobox 1 was downregulated (Fig. 3B). In addition, downregulated DEGs included DNAJC6, GALNT3, and IAH1 (Fig. 3B). Transcriptional regulators, such as AFF3, ZFN558, and MIR214 were upregulated in SLOS iPSC astrocytes (Fig. 3B). Downregulated Gene Ontology (GO) pathways in SLOS astrocytes included ‘positive regulation of MAPK cascade’, ‘nuclear receptors meta pathway’, and ‘enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway’ (Fig. 3C). Upregulated GO pathways were enriched for those related to cellular organization and developmental processes. Specifically, SLOS astrocytes exhibited an upregulation of genes associated with ‘extracellular matrix organization’, ‘tube morphogenesis’, ‘embryonic morphogenesis’, and ‘elastic fiber formation’ (Fig. 3D). The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified two downregulated (MEturqoise and MEblack) and four upregulated gene modules (MEblue, MEmagenta, MEbrown, and MElightgreen) by comparing SLOS astrocytes with controls (Fig. 3E). In the downregulated modules, the top pathways in MEturqoise were characterized by those related to mitochondrial membrane assembly and electron transport chain proteins (Fig. 3F) and MEblack related to immune response and Th1 cell cytokine production (Fig. 3G). In the upregulated modules, the top pathways included ‘cell cycle’ and ‘nuclear lumen’ in MEblue (Fig. 3H), ‘mitochondrial matrix’ and ‘organelle envelope’ in MEmagenta (Fig. 3I), and ‘cell periphery’, ‘plasma membrane’, and ‘cell adhesions’ in MEbrown, (Fig. 3J) and MElightgreen (Fig. 3K). Collectively, these findings highlight diverse transcriptome alterations driven by DHCR7 mutation in SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes.

Impaired mitochondrial respiration in the astrocytes derived from SLOS iPSCs

Since the WGCNA identified a downregulated module relating to mitochondrial membrane, we focused on the expression of mitochondrial electron transport chain oxidative phosphorylation genes in the bulk RNA-seq dataset. We observed decreased expression of genes coding for complex I, III, IV, and V proteins, but not complex II, in SLOS astrocytes compared to controls (Fig. 4A). Consistently, RT-qPCR confirmed the reduction of mRNA levels of genes coding NADH-dehydrogenase 5 (MT-ND5) for complex I, cytochrome b (MT-CYB) for complex III, cytochrome C oxidase I (MT-CO1) for complex IV, and ATP synthase F1 subunit gamma (ATP5F1C) for complex V, except for N succinate dehydrogenase assembly factor (SDHAF1) for complex II, in the SLOS iPSC astrocytes (Fig. 4B-F). To investigate their effects on mitochondria respiration, we measured oxygen consumption rates (OCR) in control and SLOS astrocytes using the Mito Stress Kit through Seahorse KFe96 Extracellular Flux analyzer (Fig. 4G). We observed decreased basal respiration, ATP-linked production, maximal respiratory capacity, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial consumption rates, but not proton leak, in SLOS astrocytes compared to controls (Fig. 4H-M). Overall, we provide evidence that SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes exhibit reduced mitochondria electron transport chain gene expression, resulting in lower mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.

Diminished neuroprotective effects of the astrocytes derived from SLOS iPSCs

To investigate the neurotrophic capacity of SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes, neurons derived from an unaffected control iPSC model were co-cultured with astrocytes derived from SLOS or isogenic control iPSCs for one week in a trans-well chamber in serum free DMEM/F12 media. While survival and growth of iPSC-derived neurons were substantially disturbed in nutrient depleted conditions compared to supplemented neural media, neuron co-culture with isogenic control astrocytes ameliorated neuronal loss compared to nutrient depletion alone (Fig. 5A, B). In contrast, SLOS astrocyte co-culture did not prevent neuronal loss in serum-depleted conditions (Fig. 5A and B). These results indicate SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes provide attenuated neuronal support, possibly through reduced neurotrophic factor supplies, compared to isogenic control iPSC-derived astrocytes.

To further explore the impact of control vs SLOS astrocytes on synaptic formation, iPSC-derived NPCs from an unaffected individual were differentiated into neurons for one week and co-cultured with SLOS or isogenic control astrocytes on microelectrode array (MEA) plates. When the frequency of spontaneous firing (Fig. 6A, C) and the incidence of synchronized burst firing (SBF) (Fig. 6B, D) were monitored for 6 weeks after the differentiation, they increased in a time-dependent manner. In the presence of isogenic control astrocytes, co-cultured neurons demonstrated an increased frequency of spikes and synaptic bursts over time compared to monoculture of neurons, demonstrating a clear role for astrocytes in the formation and maintenance of synapses (Fig. 6C and D). However, the spike frequency of neurons cultured with SLOS astrocytes remained comparable to that observed in monocultured neurons, demonstrating SLOS astrocytes provide impaired synaptic support compared to control astrocytes (Fig. 6C). Co-culturing neurons with isogenic control astrocytes induced SBF within 2 weeks of differentiation, whereas monoculture neurons and neurons co-cultured with SLOS astrocytes did not exhibit SBF until 5 weeks of differentiation (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, the number of spikes and the duration in SBF was impaired in neurons co-cultured with SLOS astrocytes compared to isogenic controls (Fig. 6E and F). These findings indicate the supportive effects of astrocytes to facilitate synaptic network formation and transmission are compromised in SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes.

Discussion

Our understanding of SLOS pathophysiology has been propelled by the generation of both genetic [33] and pharmacological [34] animal models. In particular, studies utilizing DHCR7 knockout (DHCR7−/−) mice have provided evidence that oxysterol accumulation, a metabolite of 7-dechydrocholesterol and a byproduct of DHCR7 deficiency, drives premature neurogenesis [35]. Previous work has demonstrated that primary astrocytes from Dhcr7−/− mice exhibit heightened immune signaling, dysregulation of glutamate and calcium signaling, and altered microglial crosstalk [36]. Additionally, recent in vitro studies have demonstrated the role of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in SLOS cellular dysfunction, such that photoreceptor cells treated with DHCR7 inhibitor AY9444 exhibited increased oxidative stress transcriptional pathways [37]. Nevertheless, there is a limited body of research exploring how DHCR7 mutations drive phenotypes in a cell-specific manner in SLOS. Since astrocytes substantially contribute to cholesterol synthesis in the brain, this study stood as the inaugural attempt to delineate the role of human astrocytes in driving SLOS cellular phenotypes. We uniquely utilized iPSC-derived astrocytes from a SLOS patient harboring DHCR7 c.C278T to gain insight into the complex interactions between cholesterol metabolism dysregulation and loss of cellular homeostasis as it relates to astrocyte functionality.

Astrocyte specific SREBP2 knockout mice exhibit impaired neural development and systemic metabolic deficits [38], both of which are present in SLOS patients. Furthermore, it has been illustrated that developmental synaptogenesis is promoted by astrocyte-derived cholesterol and APOE [39]. Because DHCR7 is highly expressed in astrocytes during early postnatal development, astrocytic DHCR7 deficiency may drive early neurodevelopmental defects in SLOS by disturbing cholesterol metabolism [40, 41]. Our results also showed that SLOS iPSC astrocytes store less intracellular cholesterol and exhibit less efflux of cholesterol and APOE. Our lipidomic analyses revealed that cholesterol levels were not completely depleted, indicating either involvement of alternative cholesterol biosynthetic pathways, such as the Bloch pathway, or residual DHCR7 function. In SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes, the metabolism of various lipid classes was impacted downstream of cholesterol synthesis inhibition, corroborating previous findings of broad changes in lipid classes, including phospholipids and TAGs in total brain tissue of Dhcr7 mutant mouse models [42]. Interestingly, our identification of PI species as upstream regulators of total lipid changes via network analyses was unique to our study. Future comparative analyses of the impact of DHCR7 loss on global lipid expression in additional cell types will be important for confirming the cell type-specificity of DHCR7 impacts.

Lipidomic changes were also accompanied by disturbances in mitochondrial respiration. Mitochondrial respiration and cholesterol metabolism have a bidirectional relationship. Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation influences fatty acid oxidation and lipid metabolism while mitochondria biogenesis and membrane maintenance are dependent on cholesterol [43]. Consistent with our findings, mitochondrial dysregulation has been previously documented in SLOS dermal fibroblasts [44]. The mechanism of this dysfunction may be attributed to the accumulation of 7-dehydrocholesterol, which has been shown to enhance free radical mediated lipid oxidation [45]. As 7-dehydrocholesterol is more rapidly oxidized than cholesterol, alterations in the lipid profile may exacerbate oxidative phosphorylation dysfunction in SLOS astrocytes. Notably, we also found decreased CAR in SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes in comparison to isogenic controls. Since CAR plays an essential role in delivering fatty acids into the mitochondria for β-oxidation [46], insufficient CAR supply may disturb mitochondria function in SLOS. In addition, although bulk-RNA sequencing revealed decreased expression of numerous mitochondrial electron transport chain genes in SLOS astrocytes, expression for genes responsible for complex II coding succinate dehydrogenases (SDHs) was increased. Since complex II is involved in regulating both the electron transport chain and TCA cycle [47], increased expression of SDHs may be a compensatory mechanism in response to intracellular lipid depletion in SLOS astrocytes. While we detected higher concentrations of TAG and PG in SLOS astrocytes, this finding may result from diminished mitochondrial lipid catabolism. Indeed, mitochondrial dysfunction has been reported to enhance TAG accumulation in 3 T3-L1 cells [48].

Astrocytes play a critical role in supporting neural homeostasis and synaptic plasticity. This is evidenced by the coupling of lipid metabolism between neurons and astrocytes, which allows for neural network development and synaptic activity [49]. Therefore, lipidomic changes and mitochondrial dysregulation detected in SLOS astrocytes may account for the observed diminished neuroprotective effects. Fatty acid oxidation serves as the primary route for energy expenditure within the brain, predominantly occurring in astrocytes [49]. Coordinated fatty acid metabolism between neurons and astrocytes safeguards neurons from fatty acid-induced toxicity during increased heightened synaptic activity. Unlike neurons, astrocytes are relatively resilient to impaired oxidative phosphorylation, as they rely heavily on glycolysis for energy. Blocking the electron transport chain in astrocytes does not impact their survival but leads to gliosis and neuronal death [50]. Correspondingly, we found that SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes were not effective in rescuing neurons from serum-starvation induced damage. When cocultured with SLOS iPSC astrocytes, neurons exhibited impaired formation and maintenance of synaptic networks. Thus, SLOS astrocytes may have an inferior ability to provide neurotrophic factors to facilitate synaptic maturation. In conjunction with providing neurons with cholesterol and growth factors, astrocytes also exhibit the capacity to transfer intact and functional mitochondria to neurons under conditions of heightened stress [51]. Functional mitochondria are needed for proper neurotransmission and synaptic development. Neurons shuttle mitochondria to the synapse, where they serve as local ATP sources and buffer calcium [52, 53]. Although further studies are needed, the disturbed transfer of functional astrocyte mitochondria to neurons may attribute to the phenotype.

In summary, our study demonstrates that SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes exhibit perturbed lipid homeostasis accompanied by dysregulation of mitochondrial respiration and diminished neurotrophic and neuroprotective functions. Our future studies will confirm the results obtained herein by further analysis of astrocytes derived from a cohort of SLOS subjects as well as additional disorders of cholesterol biosynthesis such as Lathosterolosis [19]. In addition, our data suggests DHCR7 mutation may impair the maturation of iPSC-derived astrocytes. Further profiling of astrocyte signaling and functional maturation at the single cell level will further detail developmental differences between isogenic control and SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes. Our findings provide critical insight into the connection between cholesterol metabolism and astrocyte signaling, while also supporting the amelioration of astrocyte dysfunction in SLOS as a novel therapeutic strategy to ameliorate neurological symptoms within this rare disease.

Materials and methods

Maintenance of human induced pluripotent stem cells

Previously generated unaffected control (BJ 3F1) and patient-derived iPSCs (CWI 4F2) [19, 54] were propagated on CF-1 mouse embryonic feeders (MEFs) (Life Technologies, A34181) in DMEM-F12, 20% Knockout Serum Replacement (Life Technologies, A3181502), glutamine (2 mM), 1x non-essential amino acids, 2-mercaptoethanol (100 μM), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (7.5 ng/mL; Miltenyi Biotec, 130–093-840). iPSCs were passaged with dispase (1 U/ml; Stem Cell Technologies, 07923).

Generation of neural progenitor cells from iPSCs

Neural induction utilized a modified rosette-based method as previously published [19, 55, 56]. MEFs were manually removed and iPSCs collected using StemPro accutase (Life Technologies, A1110501) supplemented with 10 μM Y27632 (Reagents Direct, 53-B85). For neural induction, neurospheres were generated from iPSCs in Aggrewell 800 plates (Stem Cell Technologies) following manufacturer recommendations in the following neurosphere media: DMEM, B-27 supplement with vitamin A (Life Technologies, 17 504 044), N2 supplement (Life Technologies, 17 502 048), LDN193189 (100 nM; Reagents Direct, 36-F53), SB431542 (10 μM; Reagents Direct, 21-A94), bFGF (20 ng/mL), EGF (20 ng/ml, Miltenyi Biotec, 130–097-749), glutamine (2 mM), and penicillin–streptomycin (50 μg/ml). Y27632 was added for the first 24 h of differentiation/sphere formation. 50% media changes were performed daily beginning on day 1 of differentiation. On day 7, neurospheres were plated to poly-L-ornithine (PLO; Sigma Aldrich, P3655; 20 ng/ml) and natural mouse laminin (Sigma Aldrich, L2020-1MG; 10 μg/ml) coated tissue culture treated dishes. On approximately day 12, rosette structures were manually isolated using a p1000 pipette tip and plated to a new PLO-laminin coated dish. Isolated rosettes were dissociated to single cells with accutase upon reaching confluency to generate neural progenitor (NPC) lines for directed differentiation and cryopreservation. NPCs were either cryopreserved in StemMACS cryo-brew (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–109-558) or expanded in neural precursor media containing DMEM, B-27 without vitamin A, bFGF (20 ng/ml), EGF (20 ng/ml), glutamine (2 mM), and penicillin–streptomycin (50 μg/ml). To allow SLOS NPC formation and proliferation [19], sterile-filtered human LDL (Lee Biosolutions, 360–12) and human HDL (Lee Biosolutions, 361–12) were each added to a final concentration of 15 μg/ml. NPCs also received 1:400 dilution of chemically defined lipid concentrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11 905 031).

Genetic correction of c.C278T mutation in SLOS patient-derived iPSCs

Genetic correction of the mono-allelic c.C278T missense mutation within the previously generated CWI 4F2 iPSC line [19] was performed using CRISPR/Cas9 targeting by Applied Stem Cell, Inc. as previously reported [57]. CWI 4F2 iPSCs were transfected with plasmids expressing Cas9, a DHCR7 targeting sgRNA (5′-gcggctttcctcattataggtgg-3′), and a repair template (5′-tcggctctcggacatctgggccaagactcctccaattacgaggaaagccgcccagctctataccttgtg-3′). Cells were sorted, screened, and individual clones expanded and cryopreserved. Clonal cell lines were confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma. A non-corrected clone of the parental CWI 4F2 which underwent the gene editing process was used for all comparisons to gene corrected clones. Allelic correction of the c.C278T mutation was validated by Sanger sequencing of the PCR-amplified DHCR7 hg38 locus. Primers used to sequence the target locus for allelic correction were F: 5′-cccagtgtgactgcctgcatccg–3′; R: 3′-acgctccccacctgctgtgtccc–5′. DHCR7 functional improvement was validated by GC–MS analyses of iPSCs following culture in cholesterol-deficient mTeSR1 (Stem Cell Technologies) for 7 days.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS)

iPSCs were collected, flash frozen on dry ice, reconstituted in 1 ml of H2O and lysed by successive freeze/thaw cycles in dry ice and a 50°C bead bath. Protein quantification (Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23 235) was performed using 50 μL from each sample. 1 mL of saponification buffer (7% KOH, 92% ethanol, 10 μg/ml coprostan-3-ol) was added to the remaining cell lysate (~900 μl). Samples were saponified at 60°C for 1 h. Separation of the aqueous phase was initiated with 3 mL ethyl acetate and vortexing before centrifugation for 5 min at 2200 rpm. Following organic phase extraction in 2 mL water, samples were concentrated to dryness at 50°C under nitrogen flow. Sterols were then dissolved in 50 μl pyridine (CHROMASOLV Plus, Sigma) and derivatized in 50 μl N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide with 1% trimethylchlorosilane (BSTFA +1% TMCS, Thermo Fisher Scientific TS-38831) at 50°C for 1 h. Sterol analysis was performed by GC–MS. 1 μl of each derivatized sample was injected by autosampler into an Agilent 7890 GC using a split injection port (4 mm ID × 78.5 mm quartz wool liner, Restek 23 309) connected to a 0.18 mm ID × 20 m 1,4-bis(dimethylsiloxy)phenylene dimethyl polysiloxane column (Restek, 43 602). The carrier gas helium flowed at a linear rate of 46.9 cm/sec. The method used: 0.5 min at 170°C, oven temperature was raised to 250°C at 18°C/min, followed by an increase to 280°C at 3°C/min, and finally to 320°C at 20°C/min where the temperature was held for 7 min. A mass spectrometer (Agilent 5977B) was operated in electron impact mode at 70 eV at an ion source temperature of 275°C.

Sterol quantification by GC–MS

TMS-derived ethers of natural sterols were identified through comparison to commercially available standards and through comparison to MS spectra available through the National Institute of Standards and Technologies Standard Reference Database. MassHunter software was used for GC–MS analysis. Normalization of sterol abundance was performed using both the internal standard (coprostanol) and protein concentration (Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23 235). Data was analyzed as relative to control samples using GraphPad Prism.

Differentiation of iPSC-derived NPCs into astrocytes

NPCs from isogenic control and SLOS iPSCs were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well cultured on Matrigel-coated 6 well-plates in astrocyte differentiation medium composed of astrocyte medium (ScienCell) with CNTF (10 ng/ml; Stem Cell Technologies), BMP4 (10 ng/ml; Stem Cell Technologies), and Heregulin-β1 (10 ng/ml; Stem Cell Technologies) for 1 week. The culture medium was switched to astrocyte maintenance medium composed of astrocyte medium with CNTF (20 ng/ml) 1 week after the differentiation. The medium was changed every 2 days, and the cells were passaged every 5–7 days with dissociation using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Astrocytes were used for experiments at day 45 after differentiation [31, 32].

Immunocytochemistry

Immunocytochemistry was performed as previously described [58] with slight modifications. Briefly, iPSC-derived astrocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. After blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Roche) in PBS, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against S100β (1:100, ab52642, Abcam), GFAP (1:200, ab279291, Abcam), or βIII-tubulin (1:500, ab78078, Abcam). Cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 2 h with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by nuclei counterstaining with DAPI (300 nM, D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were taken with an LSM880 inverted confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Cell-proliferation assay

Click-it EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to measure cell proliferation. At 1 day before adding the thymidine analogue EdU, iPSC-derived astrocytes were dissociated with TrypLE Express and 5 × 103 cells/well were plated into a Matrigel-coated 96-well plate (Cellvis). On the day of EdU treatment, cells were cultured with EdU (10 μM) for 8 h, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and permeabilization with PBS containing Triton X-100 (0.5%). Cells were immunostained as described by the manufacturer, images were acquired using Keyence fluorescence microscopy (BZ-X810, Keyence), and EDU-positive cells and stained nuclei were counted.

BODIPY staining and quantification

To quantify cholesterol accumulation, astrocytes derived from isogenic control and SLOS iPSCs (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on a Matrigel-coated 96-well plate and cultured for 1 day. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and stained with S100β as described above. The cells were then incubated with 4,4-difluoro-4-bora-3a,4a-diaza-s-indacene (BODIPY, Avanti Polar Lipids) 493/503 in PBS for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the images were taken with an LSM880 inverted confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). The integrated fluorescence intensities of BODIPY-Green co-localized with S100β were measured with ImageJ.

Cholesterol uptake and efflux assay

Cholesterol efflux was investigated as described [59]. Briefly, iPSC-derived astrocytes (6 × 104 cells/well) were plated to each well of a black glass bottom 96 well plate and treated with solubilized 1X Top Fluor BODIPY-cholesterol (AVANTI Polar Lipids 810 255) for 30 min at 37°C. Cells were then equilibrated with cAMP (Sigma-Alderich C8988). After incubation for 18 h, cells were treated with 10 μg/ml of APOA1 (Fitzgerald 118–30-1047S) at 37°C for 30 min prior to analysis. Fluorescence intensity of BODIPY-cholesterol effluxed into culture media was analyzed on SYNERGY H1 microplate reader (BioTek) and normalized to intracellular BODIPY cholesterol fluorescence intensity from cell lysate. For cholesterol uptake assay, cells were treated BODIPY cholesterol in the presence of Sandoz 58–035 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min prior to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. After washing with PBS, the images were taken with an LSM880 inverted confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) and quantified through ImageJ.

ELISA for APOE

iPSC-derived astrocytes were washed three times using PBS and cultured in serum-free astrocyte medium for 1 day. The conditioned medium was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min and the supernatant was filtrated through a 40 μm cell strainer (Falcon). Total protein concentration of the medium was determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The corrected medium was preserved at—80°C before being used for the experiments. ELISA for APOE was performed as previously described [53]. A 96-well plate was coated with an APOE antibody (1:1000, AB947, Millipore) in carbonate buffer at 4°C overnight. On day 2, the plate was blocked with 1% Block Ace (Bio-Rad) in PBS and then washed 3 times with PBS. Recombinant APOE3 (Biosynth) were used as a standard. Samples were diluted and incubated at 4°C overnight. On day 3, the plate was washed and incubated with anti-APOE antibody (1:4000, 74810B, Meridian Life Science) for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation with Streptavidin Poly-HRP40 Conjugate (1:4000, 65R-S104HRP, Biosynth) for 1.5 h at room temperature, the plate was developed by adding tetramethylbenzidine Super Slow substrate (Sigma-Aldrich). The reaction was stopped with H2SO4 and read at 450 nm with Synergy HT Microplate reader (BioTek). The measurements were normalized by the protein concentration of each cell lysate.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from iPSC-derived astrocytes with RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). RT-qPCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). The ∆∆Ct values relative to GAPDH were measured by qRT-PCR. All primer sequences are as follow; GAPDH (F) 5’GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG3’ (R) 5’ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA3’, MT-ND5 (F) 5’TTCATCCCTGTAGCATTGTTCG3’ (R) 5’GTTGGAATAGGTTGTTAGCGGTA3’, SDHAF1 (F) 5’TGCTGCGCATCGAGTACCTGTA3’ (R) 5’GTCGTCAGGCTGGGAACCCAC3’, MT-CYB (F) 5’ATCACTCGAGACGTAAATTATGGCT3’ (R) 5’TGAACTAGGTCTGTCCCAATGTATG3’, MT-CO1 (F) 5’GACGTAGACACACGAGCATATTTCA3’ (R) 5’AGGACATAGTGGAAGTGAGCTACAAC3’, ATP5C1 (F) 5’TGAGCAGAGTGCCAGGATGACA3’ (R) 5’TGTGATGACAGCTTGGCGGGTA3’.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) evaluation

iPSC-derived astrocytes (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on Matrigel-coated Seahorse XF96 Cell Culture Microplate (Agilent) and cultured for 1 day. OCR was measured using Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions in Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent). The XF assay medium was made with glucose (10 mM), glutamine (2 mM), and pyruvate (1 mM). Oligomycin (2.5 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM) were used in Mito Stress Test. After OCR was measured, astrocytes were fixed and stained with DAPI and the fluorescence intensity was measured using SpectraMax M5 (Molecular Devices) to assess the cell density. OCR in each well was normalized to cell density.

Co-culture with iPSC-derived neurons and astrocytes in trans-well plate

iPSC-derived NPCs from a healthy individual (MC0192, 2.0 × 104 cells/well) [31] were seeded onto Poly-L-ornithine (PLO; Sigma-Aldrich)/Laminin (Sigma-Aldrich)-coated 96 well plates (Cellvis) and cultured in BrainPhys Neuronal Medium (Stem Cell Technologies) for 1 week to be differentiated into neurons. iPSC-derived astrocytes (1.5 × 104/well) were then plated on cell culture inserts with 0.4 μm diameter pores (Corning) [31]. The neurons were co-cultured with the insert containing astrocytes in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) without any supplements. After 7 days co-culture, neurons were fixed and immunostained with the neuronal marker βIII-tubulin and DAPI as described above. Images were taken with an LSM880 inverted confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). βIII-tubulin positive cells were counted using ImageJ.

Microelectrode array (MEA) electrophysiology

iPSC-derived NPCs from a healthy individual (MC0192, 1.0 × 105 cells/well) [31] were seeded onto Poly-L-ornithine (PLO, Sigma-Aldrich)/Laminin-coated plates and cultured in BrainPhys Neuronal Medium for 1 week. iPSC-derived astrocytes (1.5 × 104/well) were then plated on the culture plate. The neurons were co-cultured with astrocytes for an additional 5 weeks. Spontaneous spikes were recorded for 10 min with MED64 Presto (Alpha Med Scientific) weekly 1 week after the differentiation into neurons. Spikes were detected when the recorded signal exceeded a threshold of ±5 σ, where σ was the standard deviation of the baseline noise during quiescent periods. SBFs were detected using the 4-step as previously described [56]. Spikes separated by interspike intervals of 20 msec were attributed to the same SBF in the first step. Secondary datasets with a maximum number of spikes in the SBF below 20 spikes/SBF were eliminated from the analysis. SBFs separated by inter-SBF intervals shorter than 500 msec were combined in the third step. A SBF was defined when it contained over 500 spikes/SBF in the fourth step. Recorded spikes and SBFs were analyzed with MEA Symphony and Burst Analysis Table software (Alpha Med Scientific).

RNA-sequencing

RNA-sequencing was performed at Mayo Clinic sequencing core using Illumina HiSeq 4000 as described previously [58]. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38. Mayo Clinic RNA-Seq analytic pipeline (MAP-RSeq Version 3.1.3) was used [60] to generate raw gene read counts and sequencing quality control metrics. Raw gene counts were corrected for gene length differences, GC bias, and global technical variations through Conditional Quantile Normalization (CQN) [61]. Based on the bi-modal distribution of the CQN normalized and log2-transformed reads per kb per million (RPKM) gene-expression values, genes with an average of log2 RPKM ≥ 3 in at least one genotype group were included in the analysis. Using this threshold, 13 361 genes were identified for downstream analysis. PCA and differentially expressed genes (DEG) analysis were performed using Partek Genomics Suite (Partek Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA). An ANOVA model was used to compare gene expressions between SLOS iPSC-derived astrocytes and isogenic controls. Pathway analyses of differentially expressed genes were performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen) [62]. WGCNA was performed using CQN normalized and log2-transformed RPKM values. The power of 9 was chosen to build scale-free topology using signed hybrid network [63]. Hybrid dynamic tree cutting was utilized based on a minimum module size of 60 genes and a minimum height for merging modules of 0.3. Modules were annotated using the WGCNA R function GOenrichmentAnalysis. Genes with high connectivity in the respective modules were considered hub genes. Gene–gene connections among top hub genes were visualized using VisANT version 5.51 [64].

Lipidomics

Shotgun lipidomic analysis was conducted through the multidimensional mass spectrometry system as described previously [58]. Samples were homogenized with Precellys Lysing Kit (Bertin Instruments) using Cryolys Evolution homogenizer (Bertin Instruments) and subjected to lipid extraction through modified Bligh and Dyer procedure [65]. Each lipid extract was reconstituted in organic solvent composed of chloroform and methanol (1:1 volume ratio, 400 μl/mg protein) and diluted to a final total lipid concentration of ~ 500 fmol/μl. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Altis, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which were connected with an automated nanospray device (TriVersa NanoMate, Advion Inc) [66]. Amounts of lipid species were normalized to the protein concentrations [67, 68]. For weighted correlation network analysis in the lipidomics data, a power of 14, a minimum module size of 10 lipids, and a minimum height for merging modules of 0.25 were employed to construct an unsigned network. Modules were annotated through R package. Hub lipids were defined based on the connectivity in each module.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses other than RNA-sequencing and lipidomics were performed using EZR software and GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons were performed using two-tailed student’s t-test, One-way ANOVA with Tukey test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
