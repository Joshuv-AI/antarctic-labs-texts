Reply to Arroyo et al.: Universality and diversity in thermal performance curves
Authors: Jean-Francois Arnoldi, Andrew L. Jackson, Ignacio Peralta-Maraver, Nicolas Payne
Published: 2026

We thank Arroyo et al. for their engagement with our work (1). We believe that much of the apparent disagreement reflects a difference in perspective rather than a fundamental contradiction.

Our contribution (2) is not to propose yet another mechanistic model for thermal performance curves (TPCs), nor to compare a small set of models and retain those that “work.” Rather, we provide a general argument for why a broad class of models and datasets tend to exhibit similar behavior. Specifically, we show, using elementary mathematics, that the exponential dependence of biological rates on temperature generically leads to the emergence of a common universal curve. In this sense, the approximate nature of our derivation is not a limitation but the essence of the result: universality concerns leading-order behavior that is largely independent of mechanistic detail (3).

From this perspective, the diversity of models documented in the literature—including the absence of a single best-performing model highlighted by recent large-scale comparisons (4)—is not in contradiction with our findings. On the contrary, it is precisely what one would expect if many distinct formulations converge, at leading order, to a similar functional form.

Regarding the model proposed by Arroyo et al. (equation 4 in ref. 5), we note that, despite its ambition as a general first-principles theory, it does not reproduce the characteristic left-skewed shape that is widely observed in empirical TPCs and captured by many existing models (2–6). While this is not a central result of our study, it provides a useful counterexample that helps clarify the conditions under which convergence toward the universal curve occurs. This discrepancy is not resolved by their critique of our approach; rather, it points to a limitation in the outcome of their formulation, independently of our analysis.

In this context, the fact that their model does not fall within the class of models admitting the UTPC as a limit is not evidence against our results, but an indication that an important ingredient may be missing. Notably, we showed that introducing a high-temperature cutoff into their model restores behavior consistent with our predictions (6), suggesting that a reconciliation between the two perspectives is possible.

Finally, we agree that not all temperature responses are strictly left-skewed, and our claim is not that every system must conform exactly to a single curve. Rather, we emphasize that a broad and biologically relevant class of TPCs exhibits this structure, and that its origin can be understood through simple and general arguments. Our work is complementary to mechanistic approaches: It identifies constraints that any successful model should satisfy at leading order. As argued by Pawar and Kontopoulos in their recent perspective on our work (7), it is precisely by studying deviations from this limit form that deeper mechanistic insight can be gained.

More broadly, we view the convergence of seemingly contrasting results as a source of insight, illustrating how different approaches can illuminate the same underlying structure. Scientific progress often arises when such apparent tensions are resolved into a deeper understanding.
