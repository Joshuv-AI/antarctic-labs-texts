Evaluation of bamboo biomass pellets: impact of binder type and age of bamboo on pellet quality and performance
Authors: V M Ilorkar, P D Raut, Savi R Nimbarte, Aarti P Deshmukh, K Pandiyan, A D Bhosale, S U Kaple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to identify the energy alternative by using different kinds of de-oiled cakes which are considered as waste and produce pellets. The bamboo biomass dust used in this study was Bambusa balcooa and the binders are de-oiled cakes of Sal (Shorea robusta), Karanj (Millettia pinnata), Mahua (Madhuca longifolia) and Rice bran. Different aged bamboo biomass dust viz., one-year-aged, two-year-aged and three-year-aged was used in the pellet production. The experiment was designed in Factorial Randomized Block Design in which two factors are involved i.e., different binders are considered as Factor A and different ages of bamboo biomass dust was considered as Factor B. Based on the results obtained from the study for physical properties, proximate analysis, combustion properties and economics of the pellets showed that the pellets would be the proposed new biomass solid fuel (suitable for burning purpose) and can be used for commercial purpose. In case of bamboo biomass dust, the analyzed parameters are moisture content (%), ash content (%) and gross calorific value (Cal g-1). The data showed that, as the age of bamboo biomass increases, the moisture content (%) and ash content (%) decreases but the gross calorific value (Cal g-1) increased. The depicted results of different parameters for bamboo pellets are studied and resulted that, in particle density (g cm-3) and combustion rate (g s-1), the highest values were showed by 2-year-old bamboo biomass followed by 3-year-old bamboo biomass and lowest value was observed in 1-year-old bamboo biomass. Meanwhile, the properties such as bulk density, percentage volatile matter and percentage ash content showed gradual decrease in values as the age of bamboo biomass increased.Keywords: Bamboo pellets, Binding agents, Bamboo ageSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

India is the second richest country in the world in terms of bamboo genetic wealth after China. Half of the world bamboo plants which are found in almost every were in India except Himalayan region. In India, the Western Ghats and Northeastern India, the bamboo is most frequently grown. About 4.6 million tones of bamboo are produced annually, with an average of 0.33 tones per ha and a range of 0.2 to 0.4 tones per ha, 13% of the country’s total forest sector is made up of bamboo. The estimated size of the nation’s total bamboo producing area is 160,037 km2. Based on the India State of Forest Report (ISFR) 2019, the area for bamboo cultivation has increased by 3,229 km2. According to Forest Survey of India (FSI) report, Maharashtra state has a bamboo bearing land of 11,465 km2, while Vidarbha region producing more than 90% of the total bamboo biomass1.

Biomass is a beneficial type of biofuel because it is sustainable, lean, carbon-neutral and renewable. However, because of its low energy density, consistent burning rate, high particle emissions and difficulties in storage and transportation, biomass is not directly used. The process of pelletization is observed to be a successful way to enhance the capacity of biomass for storage and transportation2.

The conversion of bamboo biomass through the process of pelleting could provide sustainable alternative biomass to replace wood and help to address the nation’s energy challenges. It will also be crucial to help in solving problems associated with deforestation and help reduce pollution and emission related to fuel wood collection3. When pellets are compared with briquettes and chips as a biomass energy source, have more advantages than others, especially in uniformity and density of shape. A decrease in moisture and a higher density leads to decreased transportation costs and increased energy conservation efficiency. Due to low moisture content the storage duration is also increased4.

Binders and lubricants may add to produce higher quality pellets. It increases pellet density and durability. The use of natural additives depends on the biomass composition and the mass proportion between cellulose, hemicelluloses, lignin, and inorganics. Excess heat generated during pelletization produces friction, making the pellets soft and hot (about 70–90°C). They need to be cooled and dried before they are stored or packaged. Pellets can be stored indefinitely, but they must be kept dry to prevent deterioration5. Binders also help to reduce combustion problems and better bonding to the pellets6.

Sal (Shorea robusta), Karanj (Pongamia pinnata), Mahua (Madhuca indica) and rice bran deoiled cakes (DOC) are important agro-forest residues that can be efficiently converted into pellets to enhance their handling, storage, and utilization efficiency. These by-products are typically characterized by low bulk density and irregular particle size, making pelletization an effective method for densification and value addition. The presence of inherent binding agents such as proteins, residual oils, and carbohydrates facilitates good pellet formation, particularly under elevated high temperatures during pelletization. However, certain DOCs, notably mahua and karanj, contain toxic constituents such as saponins and Karanj in; therefore, their pellets are more appropriate for bioenergy applications or for agricultural use only after adequate composting or detoxification. Rice bran DOC enhances pellet strength and durability due to its fine particle size and residual starch content, while sal and karanj DOCs contribute to improved calorific value and structural integrity of the pellets. Blending these deoiled cakes in appropriate ratios further improves pellet density, mechanical stability, and combustion performance.

The demand for bamboo pellets has unexpectedly increased in recent years. Prior to large-scale production, biomass pellet sales were dominated by Europe, Asia, and America. The manufacturers started to produce and transact in Latin America and Asia as they realized the advantages they had in terms of economy and environment. Throughout, 30% of the profit share in 2018 proved by the demand for bamboo pellets in the Asia Pacific region, and it is believed that this demand would continue to grow throughout the projected period. Currently a number of government programs promotes the production of rattan and bamboo in order to help farmers generate sustainable income. These government policies are expected to open up new opportunities for bamboo pellets’ market expansion. The value of the bamboo pellet for the environment and the economy has grown over the last two decades.

Biomass pellet production has risen in recent years due to bioenergy goals and policies in the European Union (EU). In 2025, global biomass pellet production reached 38.0 million tonnes. Large-scale use of pellets for power generation can boost local economies, promote industrial growth, and generate employment through biomass collection, transportation, and processing5.

Determination of age of Bambusa balcooa

1-year-old bamboo culm

At 1-year age of bamboo culm, the culm sheath is present at 2-3 basal nodes of culm. On the culm nodes, a branching bud breaks and thick, sturdy branch emerges. One year old bamboo culm contains brown to whitish pubescent ring on the basal 6-9 nodes. At this age prominent and ridged nodes are observed8.

2-year-old bamboo culm

Culm sheaths are usually absent or may present on 1-2 basal nodes of bamboo culm. At 4-5 basal nodes, all the culm node buds develop strong, stout branches. Axillary branches undergo transformation into a curled, thorn- like structure up to 5-6 basal nodes8.

3-year-old bamboo culm

When culm become 3-year-old, culm sheath is completely absent. Dying of bud on basal 3-5 culm nodes occurred. At 2-3 basal nodes of the culm, dead adventitious root rings may observe. Basal nodes become thick and stout8.

Pellet quality

The simplest way to perform the quality pellet test was to place a pellet in glass of water. If the pellet sinks at the bottom of the pellet has a high density and was formed under sufficient pressure. Combustion properties

Gross calorific value

The gross calorific value is the amount of energy per unit mass released upon complete combustion. The calorific value of bamboo pellets was tested using bomb calorimeter. Before testing the gross calorific value of bamboo pellets, the calorimeter was calibrated with tablets of benzoic acid whose calorific value was 22,465 Jg-1. In this test, about 1g bamboo pellets was introduced into the calorimeter, which was charged slowly with pure oxygen (>99.95 V%) to a pressure of 3.0 ± 0.2 MPa without displacing the original air. No combustion aids were used and five samples was tested in each condition. The final experimental result was the average value of five samples6.

Estimation of physical properties of pellets

Pellet dimensions

Weight

Interactions results showed that weight was not influenced by combination of different binders and age of bamboo biomass. Among the binders, the treatment Mahua + Rice bran (A6) recorded the highest mean pellet weight (2.80 g), which was significantly superior to the other treatments (Fig. 1). This was followed by Karanj DOC (A2) with a mean weight of 2.67 g. The lowest pellet weight (2.25 g) was observed in pellets prepared using Rice bran (A3) alone. The combination of Mahua + Rice bran (A6) resulted in the highest pellet weight, likely due to the synergistic effect of the natural oil content in Mahua and the starch and protein fractions in rice bran, which enhance binding efficiency during pellet formation11.

Effect of different binder on weight (g) of bamboo pellets.

Similarly, for the age of bamboo biomass (Factor B), the effect was found to be significant. The highest mean pellet weight (2.54 g) was obtained from pellets prepared using one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1), followed by three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3) with a mean weight of 2.50 g. The lowest pellet weight (2.44 g) was recorded for two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2). The higher pellet weight observed in three-year-old bamboo (B3) may be related to its elevated lignin and cellulose content, which improve pellet durability and density12.

Length

With respect to the length of bamboo pellet, the interaction was non-significant. Among the binders, the treatment Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) produced the longest pellets (38.42 mm), which was significantly higher than the other treatments (Fig. 2). This was followed by Rice bran (A3) with a mean length of 38.02 mm. The shortest pellets (34.81 mm) were obtained from the Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) treatment. The combination Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) produced significantly longer pellets, possibly due to the complementary binding characteristics of Mahua oil, which provides lubricity and plasticity, and the high protein and fat content in Karanj DOC, which enhances binding and compaction during pellet formation11.

Effect of different binder on length (mm) of pellets.

With respect to the age of bamboo biomass (Factor B), significant differences were also recorded. The highest mean pellet length (37.20 mm) was obtained from pellets prepared using three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3), followed by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (36.67 mm). The lowest pellet length (35.93 mm) was observed for one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1). The three-year-old bamboo (B3) biomass produced longer pellets due to its higher lignin content, which enhances binding under pressure and contributes to better structural integrity13.

Diameter (mm)

The diameter of pellet was depended upon the diameter of pellet machine. Hence the diameter of all the pellets was 8 mm.

Bulk density

Among the binders, the highest mean bulk density (862.91 kg m⁻3) was observed in pellets prepared using Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5), which was significantly superior to the other treatments (Fig. 3). This was followed by Karanj DOC (A2) with a mean bulk density of 835.89 kg m⁻3. The lowest mean bulk density (693.67 kg m⁻3) was recorded in pellets prepared using Mahua + Sal DOC (A4). The combination of Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) produced pellets with the highest bulk density, likely due to the synergistic effect of the oil and protein content in both materials, which enhanced plasticity and compaction during pelletization14.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets bulk density.

Regarding the age of bamboo biomass (Factor B), the highest mean bulk density (797.70 kg m⁻3) was recorded in pellets prepared from one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1), followed closely by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (794.97 kg m⁻3). The lowest mean bulk density (769.88 kg m⁻3) was recorded in pellets made from three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3). The one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1) produced the densest pellets, possibly due to its higher flexibility and fiber structure, which allowed better packing and compression during densification15. The interaction effect was non-significant.

Particle density

Interaction results showed that the particle density was not influenced by combination of different binders and age of bamboo biomass. Among the binders, the highest mean particle density (0.54 g cm⁻3) was recorded in pellets prepared using Sal DOC (A1) (Fig. 4). This was followed by Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5), which recorded a mean density of 0.53 g cm⁻3. The lowest particle density (0.46 g cm⁻3) was observed in pellets prepared from Karanj DOC (A2). The highest particle density observed in Sal DOC (A1) can be linked to its relatively higher protein and fiber content, which enhances particle adhesion and compaction during densification11.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets particle density.

With respect to the age of bamboo biomass (Factor B), the highest mean particle density (0.52 g cm⁻3) was recorded in pellets prepared from two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2), followed by three-year-old bamboo (B3) with 0.51 g cm⁻3 and one-year-old bamboo (B1) with0.49 g cm⁻3.The two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) exhibited the highest density, which could be due to an optimal balance between lignin content and fiber flexibility allowing better compression and reduced porosity during pelletization16.

Durability

Among the different binders, pellets prepared using Sal DOC (A1) recorded the highest mean durability (99.28%), followed by Rice bran (A3) (99.00%) (Fig. 5). The lowest mean durability (97.89%) was obtained in pellets prepared from Mahua + Sal DOC (A4). Pellets made with Sal DOC (A1) exhibited the highest durability, likely due to the high lignin and protein content of Sal DOC, which act as natural binders during the pelletization process, enhancing particle adhesion and reducing micro-cracks17.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets durability.

Regarding the effect of bamboo biomass age (Factor B), a significant variation was observed. The highest mean durability (98.95%) was recorded in pellets prepared from three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3), followed by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (98.40%), while the lowest mean durability (97.84%) was observed in pellets made from one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1).Three-year-old bamboo (B3) produced the most durable pellets, likely because lignin softens under heat and pressure during pelletization, acting as a natural binder that strengthens inter-particle adhesion15.

Proximate analysis of bamboo pellets

Percentage volatile matter

The interaction effect was non-significant. However, among the binders, pellets prepared with Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) exhibited the highest mean volatile matter content (84.87%), followed by Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) (82.24%) (Fig. 6). The lowest mean volatile matter (72.91%) was recorded in pellets prepared using Rice bran (A3). The Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) binder combination showed the highest volatile matter, likely due to the presence of oil residues, proteins, and organic extractives in both Mahua and Karanj materials. These compounds are thermally labile and decompose at lower temperatures, contributing to a higher proportion of volatile constituents14.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets volatile matter percent.

Regarding the effect of bamboo biomass age, the highest mean volatile matter (79.61%) was observed in pellets made from one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1), followed by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (76.18%), while the lowest mean value (76.10%) was recorded in three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3).Younger bamboo biomass is characterized by higher cellulose and hemicellulose contents and relatively lower lignin concentration, both of which enhance the release of volatiles upon heating13.

Percentage ash content

Among the binders, pellets prepared using Rice bran (A3) recorded the highest mean ash content (4.31%), followed by Mahua+ Rice bran (A6) (4.02%) (Fig. 7). The lowest mean ash content (2.66%) was observed in pellets prepared with Mahua + Sal DOC (A4). The Rice bran (A3) binder resulted in the highest ash content, likely due to its high intrinsic mineral and silicacontent, particularly from the bran layer of rice grains18. Interaction effect was non-significant.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets ash percent.

With respect to the age of bamboo biomass, the highest mean ash content (4.36%) was observed in pellets prepared from one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1), followed by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (3.84%), while the lowest ash content (2.71%) was recorded in three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3). Older bamboo (three years and above) tends to accumulate more structural carbon compounds (lignin), resulting in lower inorganic residue after combustion19.

Percentage moisture content

Among the binders, pellets prepared using Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) exhibited the highest mean moisture content (10.78%), followed closely by Sal DOC (A1) (10.68%) (Fig. 8). The lowest mean moisture content was recorded in pellets prepared with Rice bran (A3). High moisture content reduces calorific value, promotes microbial growth during storage, and increases energy required for ignition14. Pellets prepared with Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) and Sal DOC (A1) exhibited higher moisture content, likely due to the hygroscopic nature of these oilseed cake residues, which can absorb and retain water from the environment11.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets moisture content.

Regarding bamboo biomass age, the highest mean moisture content (11.88%) was observed in pellets made from three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3), followed by two-year-old bamboo biomass (B2) (9.05%), while the lowest moisture content (6.31%) was recorded in one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1). Interaction effect was non-significant. Increased porosity and microstructural changes in mature bamboo, allowing higher water absorption20.

Percentage fixed carbon

Among the binders, pellets prepared using Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) recorded the highest mean fixed carbon (81.24%), followed by Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) (78.58%) (Fig. 9). The lowest mean fixed carbon (68.25%) was observed in pellets prepared with Rice bran (A3). Higher fixed carbon contributes to longer burn times and higher calorific value, making it a critical characteristic for energy applications17. Interaction effect was non-significant.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets fixed carbon percent.

Regarding bamboo biomass age, the highest mean fixed carbon (75.18%) was observed in pellets prepared from one-year-old bamboo biomass (B1), followed by two-year-old bamboo (B2) (72.36%), while the lowest mean fixed carbon (71.63%) was recorded in three-year-old bamboo biomass (B3). Pellets made from younger bamboo (B1) had higher fixed carbon than older bamboo. This could be associated with higher cellulose and hemicellulose content in young bamboo, which after pyrolysis leaves a greater proportion of fixed carbon. In contrast, older bamboo (B3) with increased lignification and structural rigidity may yield slightly lower fixed carbon upon densification19.

Estimation of combustion properties

Gross calorific value

Among the binders, pellets prepared using Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) exhibited the highest mean gross calorific value (4341.11 cal g⁻1), followed by Mahua + Karanj DOC (A5) (4254.44 cal g⁻1) (Fig. 10). The lowest mean calorific value (4159.11 cal g⁻1) was observed in pellets prepared with Mahua + Rice bran (A6). Pellets prepared with Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) exhibited the highest GCV, likely due to the high carbon and fixed energy content in Mahua and Sal oilcake residues11. Interaction effect was non-significant.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets gross calorific value.

Regarding bamboo biomass age, pellets prepared from three-year-old bamboo (B3) showed the highest mean calorific value (4229.00 cal g⁻1), followed by two-year-old bamboo (B2) (4218.51 cal g⁻1), while the lowest calorific value (4175.61 cal g⁻1) was recorded in one-year-old bamboo (B1). This can be attributed to increased lignification and fixed carbon contentin mature bamboo culms, which enhances energy density. Younger bamboo, with higher moisture and volatile matter, tends to yield slightly lower energy per unit mass13.

Combustion rate

Interaction effect was non-significant. Among the binders, pellets prepared using Sal DOC (A1) recorded the highest mean combustion rate (0.00418 g s⁻1), followed closely by Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) (0.00414 g s⁻1) (Fig. 11). The lowest mean combustion rate (0.00367 g s⁻1) was observed in pellets prepared with Mahua + Rice bran (A6). Mahua + Rice bran (A6) pellets showed the lowest combustion rate, possibly due to higher density and lower volatile content, which slows the combustion process11,19.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets combustion rate.

Regarding bamboo biomass age, although the differences were non-significant, pellets from two-year-old bamboo (B2) showed a slightly higher mean combustion rate (0.00403 g s⁻1), followed by three-year-old bamboo (B3) (0.00390 g s⁻1) and one-year-old bamboo (B1) (0.00386 g s⁻1). The non-significant effect of bamboo biomass age (Factor B) indicates that culm maturity does not substantially affect the combustion rate, although minor differences in density and volatile content may exist.

Heat release rate

Among the different binders tested, pellets produced with Mahua + Sal DOC (A4) recorded the highest mean heat release rate (5.97 J s⁻1), followed closely by Sal DOC (A1) at 5.90 J s⁻1 (Fig. 12). The lowest mean release rate (5.23 J s⁻1) was observed in pellets made with Karanj DOC (A2). The superior performance of A4 and A1 pellets can be attributed to their higher volatile matter and lower density, which facilitate faster combustion. In contrast, the denser structure and lower volatile content of A2 pellets likely contributed to their reduced heat release rate11.

Effect of different binder and culm age on pellets heat release rate.

In relation to bamboo biomass age, pellets from three-year-old bamboo (B3) exhibited the highest mean heat release rate (5.68 J s⁻1), followed by one-year-old bamboo (B1) at 5.57 J s⁻1, while the lowest value (5.52 J s⁻1) occurred in two-year-old bamboo (B2). The slightly higher rate in older bamboo (B3) may result from its greater lignin and fixed carbon content, which enhance combustion efficiency, whereas the younger bamboo samples (B1 and B2) generated marginally lower values.

The study demonstrates that different binding materials can be effectively used for bamboo pellet production, regardless of the bamboo culm age or type of binder. This represents a value addition to locally available resources such as Karanj, Sal, Mahua DOC, and rice bran. These materials are readily accessible in nearby forest areas and offer an opportunity to establish small-scale businesses using locally available raw materials. Moreover, promoting the use of bamboo and tree-born oilseed residues for pellet production can help reduce pressure on forests for fuelwood, while also minimizing human-animal conflicts.

Conclusion

The findings of the study indicate that the quality of bamboo biomass pellets is strongly influenced by both the type of binder used and the age of the bamboo dust. These factors notably affect the physical and combustion characteristics of the pellets. The densification of bamboo biomass into strong, compact pellets emerges as an efficient strategy to fulfill the growing demand for sustainable raw materials in biofuel production.
