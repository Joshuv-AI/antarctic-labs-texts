Validation of a standardized question and a numerical rating scale for assessment of patient satisfaction after knee arthroplasty at one and two years
Authors: Nick D. Clement, Donia Karimaghaei, Irrum Afzal, Fares S. Haddad, Deiary F. Kader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThe aim was to validate the use of a standardized satisfaction question with a numerical rating scale (NRS) response following knee arthroplasty (KA) at one and two years.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted over six years and included 7,644 KAs in 6,690 patients. Patient demographic details, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, and preoperative and postoperative Oxford Knee Scores (OKS), and EQ-5D-3L) scores were obtained from a local arthroplasty register. Patient satisfaction was assessed at one and two years postoperatively using a standardized questionnaire with possible responses ranging from 0 (not satisfied) to 100 (very satisfied) on a NRS.ResultsDuring the study period, 5,893 and 4,879 KA had completed outcomes at one and two years’ follow-up, respectively. The median satisfaction score was 90 (IQR 80 to 100). There were moderate correlations between the postoperative OKS at one year (r = 0.554) and two years (r = 0.441) with satisfaction, supporting construct validity. Responses to the satisfaction question were skewed, with a 34.1% ceiling effect. The test–retest analysis showed an intraclass coefficient of 0.934 (95% CI 0.886 to 0.962) for single measures and 0.966 (95% CI 0.940 to 0.981) for average measures, suggesting good to excellent reliability. Longitudinal agreement between one and two years showed an intraclass coefficient of 0.71 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.74, p < 0.001). Level of satisfaction was not influenced by preoperative variables or had a negligible to weak influence on satisfaction. Suggested thresholds in satisfaction score were very satisfied ≥ 90, satisfied as 80 to 89, neutral 60 to 79, dissatisfied 40 to 59, and very dissatisfied < 40.ConclusionThe NRS was a valid, reliable, and universally applicable measure of satisfaction following KA, with a smaller ceiling effect than Likert scales. One year appears optimal for assessing satisfaction, as no further improvement occured beyond this, and follow-up rates decline. The defined thresholds will help identify satisfied and unsatisfied patients.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):916–925.

Introduction

The level of patient satisfaction with their knee arthroplasty (KA) is a key outcome measure, providing insights into pain relief, joint-specific functional improvement, and quality of life.1 The rate of dissatisfaction after KA is commonly quoted to be approximately 20%,2 although more recent reviews suggest this may be between 10%3 and 14%.4 There is a wide variation in the reported rate of satisfaction after KA, which ranges from 69% to 99%.5 The reason for this variation is unclear, but it may partly result from the lack of a validated question with a standardized definition of satisfaction.6 Kahlenberg et al7 reviewed 208 studies and only found 13% to have used a validated satisfaction survey to assess the outcome of total KA. Klem et al5 also demonstrated variability in both the satisfaction questions employed, which has been shown to affect the rate of satisfaction,8 and the threshold values used to define satisfaction with KA across the 43 studies they assessed.

Several instruments exist for measuring satisfaction, with the visual analogue scale (VAS), numerical rating scale (NRS), and ordinal Likert scales being among the most commonly employed.7 NRS and VAS demonstrate a resistance to bias from confounding factors, are more effective at reducing the ceiling effect, and require 28% less time to complete than Likert scales.9 To the authors’ knowledge, only Brokelman et al10 have validated the VAS for assessment of satisfaction following total hip arthroplasty, with no similar study assessing KA. One limitation of the VAS score is the requirement for manual measurement (e.g. ruler measurement of distance from ‘0’), which introduces potential for human error,11 whereas the NRS provides direct numerical values, making it quicker and less prone to scoring inconsistencies. Therefore, the NRS may be a preferred measure when assessing patient satisfaction compared with the VAS. A limitation of both the NRS and VAS assessments is the variation in threshold values to define the level of satisfaction. Some authors use 80 to define very satisfied,12 whereas others have used 50,13 60,14 and 707 or more to define satisfied patients. There is also variation in the timepoint of assessment, which has been shown to influence the patients’ response according to the question being asked.15

The primary aim of this study was to validate the use of the NRS for assessment of patient satisfaction at one and two years following KA when using a standardized question. Secondary aims were to assess whether satisfaction changes between one and two years, and to define threshold values for the level of patient satisfaction according to the established, commonly employed five-point Likert scale.16,17

Methods

A single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted over six years from January 2016 to December 2021. The study centre has an established arthroplasty register that prospectively records patient demographic details and routine pre- and postoperative patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). There was no additional patient contact, and as such, this project did not require ethical approval (Supplementary Material). The project was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki18 and the guidelines for good clinical practice.19 Patients undergoing primary KA for arthritis were included, and those undergoing revision were excluded. Patient demographic details were routinely recorded, and ASA grade was assigned by the anaesthetist on the day of surgery, which was recorded prospectively in an electronic hospital surgical database.20,21

Patient satisfaction was assessed at one and two years postoperatively with their KA using a standardized question (Figure 1) with possible responses ranging from 0 (not satisfied) to 100 (very satisfied) on a NRS. PROMs were assessed preoperatively and at one and two years postoperatively. The Oxford Knee Score (OKS) was used to assess the joint-specific outcome of the KA.22,23 The responses to each of the OKS questions were scored from 0 to 4. A summative score of 48 is the best possible score (least symptomatic), and 0 is the worst possible score (most symptomatic). Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was assessed using the EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D) general health questionnaire, which ranges from -0.594 (worst health) to 1 (best health).24 The EQ-VAS for general health was also used, where zero is the worst health, and 100 is the best health-related quality of life.24 Patients who did not return the questionnaire or missed responses to specific questions were routinely contacted via telephone to complete the questionnaire. This has been shown to be a valid way to collect PROMs.25

The validity of the satisfaction question and NRS in terms of construct assessment was evaluated through its correlation with widely validated PROMs, specifically OKS and the EQ-5D/VAS. Construct validity comprises two key components: convergent and discriminant validity.26 Convergent validity pertains to the degree to which different measurement methods of the same characteristic correlate with each other. Discriminant validity, on the other hand, ensures that a given measure does not exhibit excessive correlation with measures designed to evaluate distinct traits. An essential element of content validity is the presence of floor and ceiling effects, which occur when patients consistently achieve the lowest or highest possible scores. While a significant ceiling effect in satisfaction measurement may suggest an optimal surgical outcome, it can also reduce the usefulness of the scores, as it limits the ability to differentiate between patients based on this outcome alone.27

Reliability signifies that the same result would be obtained if the test is repeated under unchanged conditions. Test-retest reliability was assessed by asking patients to complete a paper-based NRS for satisfaction and then contacting them again within two weeks to complete the same measure. To ensure no change (condition has not improved or worsened) had occurred in the interval, the Global rating of change scale was also asked, with only patients answering “no change” or “almost the same” being included.28 Patients were unaware that they would be contacted a second time to affirm their original response. To detect an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.75 with a null hypothesis of 0.5, using two ratings per subject, at least 46 participants were needed for 80% power at α = 0.05.29 In total, 49 patients were contacted, of whom 47 had “no change” or “almost the same” in their condition and were included for test-retest reliability analysis. Longitudinal agreement refers to the consistency of the assessment of satisfaction over an extended period. Unlike test-retest reliability, which typically assesses short-term consistency, longitudinal agreement is concerned with whether individuals maintain similar scores across longer intervals, i.e. whether they remain stable over time.

To define thresholds in the NRS for the level of satisfaction, the cohort was divided into percentages according to what was previously defined by Bourne et al17 and Goodman et al16 using a five-point Likert for satisfaction: very satisfied (51% and 75%), satisfied (29% and 15%), neutral (8% and 2%), dissatisfied (4% and 4%), and very dissatisfied (8% and 4%), respectively. Where the two studies reported differing percentages, an average value was used to account for the variation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v. 17.0 (SPSS, USA). Simple descriptive analysis was undertaken according to mean or median and SD or IQR for parametric and non-parametric data, respectively. A Mann-Whitney U test or a Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables between groups, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data. The association between age/PROMs and satisfaction were assessed using a Spearman’s rank test. Correlation coefficients were classified as negligible (< 0.10), weak (0.10 to 0.39), moderate (0.40 to 0.69), strong (0.70 to 0.89), and very strong (≥ 0.90).30 The reliability (test-retest and longitudinal agreement) was analyzed using the intraclass correlation coefficient, which serves as a measure of agreement for continuous data and ranges from 0 to 1. An intraclass correlation coefficient below 0.4 is deemed poor, between 0.4 and 0.75 is considered moderate, and above 0.75 is classified as good to excellent.31 A p-value of < 0.05 was defined as significant.

Results

There were 5,893 (77.1%) and 4,879 (60.1%) PROMs and level of satisfaction questionnaires completed at one- and two-year follow-up, respectively (Table I). Of the patients lost to follow-up, 70 (0.9%) and 138 (1.8%) had died before the one- and two-year reviews, respectively. There were no significant (p > 0.1) differences in sex, age, or preoperative OKS or EQ-5D/VAS scores between those alive and lost to follow-up and the study cohort. The median NRS for satisfaction was 90 (IQR 80 to 100) for both one- and two-year reviews.

Construct validity

There were moderate correlations between the satisfaction score and the postoperative OKS at one (r = 0.554) and two years (r = 0.441) (Tables II and III), suggesting construct validity with knee-specific symptoms. The change in the OKS was not as strongly correlated with patient satisfaction at one (r = 0.401) or two years (r = 0.301). The EQ-5D and EQ-VAS also demonstrated moderate correlation with patient satisfaction, suggesting construct validity with HRQoL. In contrast, changes in the EQ-5D and EQ-VAS demonstrated weaker correlations at one year (Table II) and two years (Table III), suggesting poor construct validity when assessing improvement in HRQoL.

Reliability

The test–retest analysis showed an intraclass coefficient of 0.934 (95% CI 0.886 to 0.962, p < 0.001) for single measures and 0.966 (95% CI 0.940 to 0.981, p < 0.001) for average measures, suggesting good to excellent reliability. The longitudinal agreement, measured between one and two years, showed an intraclass coefficient of 0.71 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.74, p < 0.001), demonstrating moderate reliability. There was a moderate correlation (r = 0.512, p < 0.001, Spearman) between satisfaction scores at one and two years (Figure 4), indicating some variation at the individual level. However, neither the overall distribution (Figure 5) nor the median satisfaction score (90) changed significantly (p = 0.287, Wilcoxon test) between one and two years.

Sensitivity analysis

The level of satisfaction at one (Table II) and two years (Table III) was not significantly influenced by preoperative ASA grade. In contrast, male sex, older age, and better preoperative OKS, EQ-5D, and EQ-VAS were associated with an increased level of satisfaction. However, the level of association between these variables (age, OKS, EQ-5D, EQ-VAS) and satisfaction were negligible to weak (Tables II and III).

Discussion

This study has validated the use of a satisfaction question with a NRS response at both one and two years after KA, which did not change between these timepoints, but a ceiling effect was observed. There was excellent reliability in the test–retest measure, and moderate longitudinal agreement between one- and two-year assessments. Sensitivity analysis of preoperative factors influencing satisfaction showed that preoperative variables had negligible effects, supporting universal employment across all patient groups. The suggested threshold values in the NRS for categorical grades of satisfaction were established in line with traditional percentage distributions, but these will need further validation.

The satisfaction tool validated by Mahomed et al32 includes four questions to assess patient satisfaction, which assess the results of surgery, pain, work, and recreational activities. A combined mean score from all four questions is then calculated to give an overall satisfaction score that ranges from 25 (least) to 100 (most). Goodman et al16 subsequently validated a modified version of the Mahomed et al32 satisfaction tool, which included a “neither satisfied nor dissatisfied”. There may be drawbacks to composite satisfaction scores that involve different questions, as it is recognized that the focus of the satisfaction question influences the individual’s level of satisfaction.8 Use of a single-item question to assess patient satisfaction following KA, as used in the current study, is supported by the International Society of Arthroplasty Registries Patient-Reported Outcome Measures working group.33 However, they suggested using a categorical Likert scale for available responses, in preference to the NRS, to enable a clear definition of satisfied and dissatisfied patients.33 There are limitations of a single-item question, which include the potential effect of preoperative characteristics on patient responses, and the inability to assess the magnitude of change and the cost-effectiveness of KA.33 A single-item satisfaction question may also be influenced by factors unrelated to the arthroplasty, such as their hospital experience, whereas traditional PROMs are thought to be more objective.34 However, the opposite argument could also be made in that traditional PROMs may not fully capture patient-relevant satisfaction with their surgery. For instance, 40% of patients classified as having high or chronic postoperative pain based on their OKS still report being satisfied with their outcome.35

Using the NRS to assess patient satisfaction after KA, to the authors’ knowledge, has not previously been validated, although it is well established in the assessment of perioperative pain.36 However, previous authors have used a NRS to assess satisfaction following KA. Galea et al37 is one such study that also categorized the responses into ‘very satisfied’ (0 points), ‘satisfied’ (2.5 points), ‘moderately satisfied’ (5.0 points), ‘doubtful’ (7.5 points), and ‘dissatisfied’ (10.0 points). The use of an unvalidated tool and then further categorizing the responses using randomly assigned cut-off values to define levels of satisfaction is not uncommon.2,5,6 The definition of which patients are satisfied and which are dissatisfied is critically important, as variation in this will directly affect the rate. For example, a recent review reported the rate of dissatisfaction in the study by Bourne et al17 to be 11.6%, but the original publication stated a rate of 19%.3 This difference was due to the removal of those patients who defined their level of satisfaction as neutral from the dissatisfied group.3 Authors using VAS or NRS scales to assess satisfaction have used differing cutoff values, with some using 50 and others using 60 or more to define satisfaction,14,38 whereas others have split the same scale into thirds, with the bottom third being dissatisfied.37 The thresholds in the NRS defined in the current study will aid interpretation of the patient’s response, categorization of the level of satisfaction, and comparison in relation to traditional five-point Likert scales. A ceiling effect was observed with the NRS, affecting 34% of responses. While this represents a limitation, it is substantially lower than the ceiling effects reported with the traditional Likert scale, which range from 55% to 75%.16,17,32

The timing of patient satisfaction assessment following KA varies considerably across studies, ranging from six months39 to several years postoperatively.15 This variation may influence the reported levels of satisfaction.15,37 Early assessments may capture transient factors such as postoperative pain, stiffness, or delayed functional recovery, leading to lower satisfaction scores that may not reflect the eventual outcome once rehabilitation is complete.40,41 Conversely, later assessments, beyond one or two years, may be influenced by adaptation, recall bias, or the onset of new comorbidities and age-related health changes,42 which could diminish satisfaction independently of the surgical result. Therefore, the timing of evaluation is a critical methodological consideration when interpreting or comparing satisfaction data after KA.6 The current study found a moderate correlation between the one and two-year satisfaction scores, indicating that some variation occurred at the individual level. However, overall satisfaction within the group remained stable over time, with median values and the distribution of satisfaction scores unchanged. This is supported by Galea et al37 who assessed the change in patient satisfaction from one to three years after KA. They found that 6% declined and 7% improved, but the overall distribution of satisfaction categories remained stable. There was, however, a 17.2% (n = 1,014/5,893) loss to follow-up between one and two years in the reported cohort. As loss to follow-up increases over time and thereby reduces clinical value,43,44 and given that PROMs typically peak one year after KA,45 assessment of satisfaction at one year may represent the optimal timepoint.

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the content validity, arguably the most important property of a PROM, was not formally assessed, and the extent to which the instrument fully captures patient satisfaction remains uncertain. In addition, ‘overall satisfaction’ is a broad construct that may be interpreted variably, limiting clarity around what aspect of outcome is being measured. Second, although NRS data are discrete, they were analyzed as continuous. While common, this approach relies on assumptions that may not fully hold, particularly given the pen-and-paper ‘circle’ response format used. Third, thresholds derived by mapping NRS to Likert categories were based on assumed comparable distributions to prior studies, without direct supporting evidence; these should therefore be considered exploratory. Although ceiling effects were observed, they relate to interpretability rather than validity. The Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Status Measurement Instruments criteria mandate that patients should be involved in the development or testing.46 Future studies should engage patients in the development process to ensure the question aligns with their experiences and expectations. The absence of categorical anchors to define thresholds of satisfaction may have contributed to variability in responses, making it difficult to establish clinically meaningful threshold values. Future research should explore the use of well-defined categorical descriptors to enhance interpretability and applicability in clinical settings.

In conclusion, the NRS response to a standardized question for assessment of patient satisfaction following KA was a valid, reliable, and universally applicable measure. It did, however, demonstrate a ceiling effect, although this was less than that observed with Likert satisfaction scales. One year appears to be the optimal timepoint for assessing satisfaction, as no further improvement was observed beyond this, and follow-up rates begin to decline. The defined threshold values will help to establish those patients who are satisfied and those who are dissatisfied, but these should be affirmed in future studies.

Take home message

- A standardized numerical rating scale provides a valid, reliable, and widely applicable measure of patient satisfaction after knee arthroplasty, with one year representing the optimal assessment timepoint.
