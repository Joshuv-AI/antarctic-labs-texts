Joint Associations of Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity and Depressive Symptoms on Cognitive Function Among Chinese Middle-Aged and Older Adults
Authors: Zhiyao Xin, Li Wang, Keying Wu, Xiuwen Li, Lan Guo, Wanxin Wang, Jie Liu, Bin Jiang, Chenyao Kang, Qian Li, Xiaojie Wang, Ciyong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCognitive decline in middle-aged and older adults globally is a pressing health concern, linked to two significant factors: cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) and depressive symptoms. This study aimed to investigate the independent, joint, and interactive effects of CMM and depressive symptoms on cognitive function in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.Patients and MethodsA total of 341 middle-aged and older adults were enrolled from the memory clinic at Qianhai Shekou Free Trade Zone Hospital in Shenzhen between May 2022 and June 2025 for this cross-sectional study. A composite z-score, computed based on performance on the Trail Making Test-A (TMT-A), Trail Making Test-B (TMT-B), and Animal Verbal Fluency Test (AFT), served as the measure of global cognition. CMM describes a constellation of at least two cardiometabolic diseases, including diabetes, stroke, and heart disease. Assessment of depressive symptoms was conducted using the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D). Linear regression was used.ResultsThis study enrolled 341 individuals over 45 years of age. The sample had a mean age of 62.4 years (SD 9.1), and 52.8% were male. A significant link was observed between CMM and lower global cognitive scores (β= −0.37, 95% CI = −0.57 to −0.17). A higher number of CMDs was positively associated with the severity of cognitive impairment (β=−1.08, 95% CI=−0.28 to −0.08). Depressive symptoms were inversely associated with cognitive scores (β=−0.66, 95% CI=−1.03 to −0.30). Participants with co-existing CMM and depressive symptoms exhibited the lowest cognitive scores (β=−1.31, 95% CI=−2.11 to −0.52) relative to those without either condition. This pattern remained consistent in all subgroups.ConclusionIn this cross-sectional study, the association between co-existing CMM and depressive symptoms and poorer cognitive function highlights the need for integrated management of physical and mental health to preserve cognition in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.Keywords: cardiometabolic multimorbidity, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, middle-aged and older adults

Introduction

Against the backdrop of population aging, cognitive decline has emerged as a pressing public health concern.1 Progressive cognitive decline is a recognized risk factor for dementia.2 Global dementia cases are predicted to experience a steep rise, with projections indicating an increase from 57 million in 2019 to 153 million in 2050.3 The cognitive impairment imposes a heavy burden on individuals and society. Global dementia-related healthcare costs amounted to roughly 850 billion US dollars in 2019.4 Effective treatments for late-stage dementia remain limited. This reality underscores the urgency of identifying modifiable risk factors for reduced cognitive performance prior to its progression to dementia.5 This urgency is particularly pronounced in China, which is experiencing accelerated population aging. The country’s large middle-aged and older adult population faces the dual challenge of a growing burden of chronic diseases and an increasing risk of cognitive impairment, making this group a critical target for research and intervention.

Cognitive functions are usually evaluated through neuropsychological tests such as the Trail Making Test, covering multiple aspects including memory, executive function, attention, language, and information processing speed. Numerous factors, including lifestyle, chronic disease, demographics, and psychosocial elements, are known to influence cognitive function.6,7 Cardiometabolic diseases (CMD), notably diabetes, heart disease, and stroke, represent a major global economic challenge.8 An upward trend has been observed in the prevalence of cardiovascular metabolic multimorbidity (CMM)—defined as having two or more cardiometabolic diseases.9,10 According to previous reports, the prevalence of CMM in China is estimated at 5.94%,10 indicating that CMM was widespread among Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Furthermore, previous studies have established a link between CMM and both poorer and faster declining cognitive function in middle-aged and older adults.11–13

Depressive symptoms adversely impacts cognitive function14–16 and affects approximately 320 million people worldwide.17 Depressive symptoms, a significant risk factor for cognitive decline, frequently co-occur with CMM.7 Studies reported inconsistent findings regarding the impact of having both CMM and depressive symptoms on cognitive function. A recent longitudinal study found that individuals with both CMM and depressive symptoms experienced greater cognitive deterioration than those with neither condition.18 Similarly, a cohort study result indicated that the presence of CMD in depressed patients was associated with accelerated cognitive decline.19 While a cross-sectional analysis of male participants revealed comparable cognitive function between depressed patients with and without CMD (including diabetes, heart disease, and stroke).20 This inconsistency may be due to the different age characteristics of the population and the relatively small sample sizes, which make the mechanism of the synergistic effect of comorbidities on cognitive function unclear. Clarifying how CMM and depressive symptoms jointly affect cognitive function is therefore critical for identifying windows of opportunity and designing targeted interventions to prevent or delay reduced cognitive performance.

Given the high and growing burden of both CMM and depressive symptoms in China’s aging population, clarifying how these conditions independently and jointly influence cognitive function is essential for identifying timely intervention opportunities and designing targeted strategies to prevent or delay cognitive decline.

To address this gap, the present study was conducted to assess the individual associations of CMM and depressive symptoms with cognitive function, and to evaluate their joint effect, among Chinese middle-aged and older adults. These findings could inform the development of targeted interventions to reduce the risk of cognitive impairment in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.

Material and Methods

Study Participants and Data Collection

We recruited middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years or older from the memory clinic at Qianhai Shekou Free Trade Zone Hospital in Shenzhen for this cross-sectional study between May 2022 and June 2025. These participants were conscious and cooperative in completing the cognitive function assessment. Consecutive eligible patients who visited the clinic for memory concerns or routine cognitive screening during the study period were invited to participate. We excluded the following participants: (1) previous or current history of major head trauma or any intracranial surgery, (2) rheumatic diseases, blood system diseases, or severe infections. (3) extravertebral symptoms or mental illness affecting cognitive function assessment, (4) intracranial space-occupying lesion, acute cerebral vascular disorders. After applying the exclusion criteria, a final sample of 341 individuals was included in the analysis.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Qianhai Shekou Free Trade Zone Hospital (Approval No. 2022k-013). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurements

CMM

We defined cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) as follows: diabetes (fasting blood glucose ≥7 mmol/L, self-reported diagnosis, or use of glucose-lowering drugs.21; heart disease, eg, heart failure or myocardial infarction (self-report, physician diagnosis, or related medication); and stroke (self-report, physician diagnosis, medical history, or neurological exam evidence). CMM was considered present when a participant had two or more of these CMDs.9

Depressive Symptoms

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 20-item Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression (CES-D) Scale, which has been previously validated in Chinese populations22 Participants rated the frequency of symptoms over the preceding two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). Total scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater severity. In this study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.903). A score greater than 20 was used to define the presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms.23

Cognitive Function

Under the guidance of trained investigators, participants completed a face-to-face neuropsychological assessment. A global composite score was calculated based on their performance on the Trail Making Test-A (TMT-A), Trail Making Test-B (TMT-B), and Animal Verbal Fluency Test (AFT). In both TMT-A and TMT-B, performance is quantified by the time taken to sequentially connect numbered circles, with longer completion times corresponding to poorer cognitive performance.24 In the Animal Verbal Fluency Test (AFT), participants generated animal names for one minute (1 point/item).25 All test scores were standardized to z-scores (mean=0, SD=1) by subtracting the sample mean and dividing by the standard deviation. The composite score was derived by summing these z-scores, with positive values indicating above-average performance and negative values indicating below-average performance relative to the study population.

Potential Covariates

The potential covariates include age, sex (male vs female), education level (illiterate, primary school, and high school or higher), main occupation (nonmanual vs manual), marital status (married vs others), family monthly income (<10,000, 10,000–20,000, and >20,000 Yuan), current smoking (yes vs no), current drinking (yes vs no), moderate to vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA, yes vs no), and sleep disorders (yes vs no). Dyslipidemia was defined based on elevated lipid levels (LDL ≥140, TG ≥150, TC ≥220 mg/dL, or HDL ≤35 mg/dL), self-report, physician diagnosis, or lipid-lowering medication use. Hypertension was defined based on elevated blood pressure (SBP ≥140 or DBP ≥90 mmHg), self-report, physician diagnosis, or antihypertensive medication use.26

Statistical Analyses

The analytical approach proceeded as follows. First, descriptive statistics were computed to summarize sample characteristics, stratified by CMM status. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests; categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests.

Second, multiple linear regression was employed to evaluate the independent associations of CMM and depressive symptoms with cognitive function. Covariate selection was informed by prior literature and univariate analyses (Table 1, p < 0.10). Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, occupation, monthly family income, smoking, and drinking; and Model 3 further incorporated hypertension and dyslipidemia. Results are reported as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess multicollinearity in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF) were calculated, with a GVIF > 5 indicating potential collinearity.

Third, participants were classified into four exposure groups based on the presence of CMM and/or depressive symptoms: neither, CMM only, depressive symptoms only, or both. The joint association of CMM and depressive symptoms with cognitive function was examined using the same three modeling tiers, with the neither group as the reference. An multiplicative interaction term (CMM × depressive symptoms) was included to assess effect modification.

Finally, subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of associations across strata defined by age, sex, education level, main occupation, family monthly income, current smoking, current drinking, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; The R Foundation). A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Sample Characteristics

As summarized in Table 1, the study included 341 participants with a mean age of 62.35 years (SD = 9.05). Depressive symptoms were present in 4.99% of the sample. Based on CMD status, 46.63% (n=159) were CMD-free, 34.02% (n=116) had one CMD, and 19.35% (n=66) had CMM. Compared to individuals without CMD, those with CMM were more likely to have a lower number of AFTs and require significantly longer completion times on both TMT-A and TMT-B.

Independent Associations of CMM and Depressive Symptoms with Cognitive Function

As shown in Table 2, after full adjustment for covariates in Model 3, both depressive symptoms (β = −0.66, 95% CI= −1.03 to −0.30) and CMM (β = −0.37, 95% CI= −0.57 to −0.17) were significantly associated with lower global cognitive z-scores. In subsequent analyses categorizing participants by CMD burden (CMD-free, single, two, or three CMDs), individuals with comorbid diabetes and stroke demonstrated significantly poorer cognitive performance (β = −0.44, 95% CI= −0.68 to −0.19). Furthermore, the number of CMDs showed a significant dose-response relationship with cognitive z-scores in the fully adjusted model, with β values of −0.35 (95% CI= −0.57 to −0.14) for two CMDs and −1.05 (95% CI= −1.73 to −0.36) for three CMDs. This trend of increasing CMD burden correlating with poorer cognitive performance was statistically significant (P for trend < 0.001). The multicollinearity evaluation (Supplementary Table S1) revealed no significant multicollinearity across the variables, with GVIF for each covariate being < 5.

Joint Associations of CMM and Depressive Symptoms with Cognitive Function

Regarding the joint associations of CMM and depressive symptoms with cognitive function (Table 3), the fully adjusted model (Model 3) showed that both CMM alone (β= −0.35, 95% CI = −0.55 to −0.15) and depressive symptoms alone (β = –0.59, 95% CI= –0.99 to –0.19) were significantly associated with lower global cognitive z-scores. Notably, participants with both conditions exhibited the strongest decline in cognitive performance (β = –1.31, 95% CI = –2.11 to –0.52). Furthermore, a significant additive interaction was observed between CMM and depressive symptoms (β = –1.20, 95% CI = –2.01 to –0.39), indicating a synergistic detrimental effect on global cognition.

Subgroup Analyses

As detailed in Table 4, the co-occurrence of CMM and depressive symptoms was significantly associated with lower global cognitive z-scores in both middle-aged (β = −0.36, 95% CI= −0.62 to −0.09) and older adults (β = −0.52, 95% CI= −0.77 to −0.27). Furthermore, subgroup analyses across strata defined by age, sex, education level, main occupation, family monthly income, current smoking, current drinking, hypertension, and dyslipidemia consistently revealed significant negative associations. The lack of significant interaction effects (all p > 0.05) indicates that this relationship is robust and not modified by these sociodemographic or clinical factors.

Discussion

The main findings indicate that CMM and depressive symptoms independently contribute to cognitive decline in Chinese middle-aged and older adults, with the risk escalating as the number of cardiometabolic diseases increases. Critically, the presence of both CMM and depressive symptoms demonstrated a significant additive interaction, resulting in the strongest association with cognitive decline. These results collectively highlight a synergistic detrimental effect on cognitive function.

In line with our findings, Friedmann et al identified a strong link between CMM and an increased incidence of dementia (HR = 5.55, 95% CI = 3.39 to 9.08).27 Similarly, a study of 17,913 dementia-free participants in the Swedish Twin Registry reported that individuals with CMM have an increased risk of developing dementia (HR = 2.10, 95% CI = 1.73 to 2.57).12 A meta-analysis demonstrated a comparable link, showing an elevated risk of dementia (pooled RR = 2.21, 95% CI = 1.78 to 2.73) for those with at least three cardiovascular risk factors relative to those free of such factors.28 In addition, an analysis of 203,038 dementia-free UK Biobank participants of European ancestry aged over 60 years by Tai et al demonstrated that CMM independently elevated dementia risk across all genetic risk strata, with hazard ratios of 3.53 (low), 4.65 (moderate), and 5.74 (high).27 A 12-year follow-up of 2,577 adults aged 60 or older without baseline dementia found that CMD multimorbidity was linked to a heightened hazard of developing cognitive impairment no dementia (CIND) (HR=1.73) and of progressing to dementia (HR=1.86). It also accelerated the time to CIND onset by 2.3 years and to dementia by 1.8 years.29 Furthermore, an analysis of 4,744 participants (≥65 years) from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) identified a robust link between CMM and an increased risk of motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR).11 Furthermore, the subgroup with both CMM and depressive symptoms was very small (n=3). Although a significant interaction was observed, this estimate is unstable and requires validation in larger studies.

Furthermore, our study identified a graded association between the number of CMDs and the risk of reduced cognitive performance. This pattern is corroborated by several prior studies: a cross-sectional study of rural older adults in Shandong Province, China, reported a dose-response relationship between CMD count and the prevalence of MCR.30 Similarly, Jin et al documented a dose-dependent reduction in cognitive function scores with increasing CMD number (for 1, 2, and 3 diseases: β = −0.15, −0.37, −0.57, respectively).13 In the same vein, Lyall et al concluded that the cognitive risk attributable to CMDs accumulates additively with each additional condition.31

Our study revealed a significant additive interaction between CMM and depressive symptoms, indicating a synergistic detrimental effect on global cognition, with the highest risk observed in individuals experiencing both conditions. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to specifically investigate this interaction in a Chinese middle-aged and older population. The participants were recruited from the memory clinics in a rapidly urbanizing area. This discovery not only expands the evidence from similar international studies but also provides crucial data for understanding the specific risk patterns of cognitive decline in the Chinese population. In China, the healthcare model, perceptions of illness, and concepts of mental health differ systematically from those in Western contexts. This study highlights the importance of incorporating cognitive and mental health dimensions into aging policies and chronic disease management, thereby offering precise targets for interventions aimed at achieving healthy aging. Our findings align with international research. A recent COSMIC consortium study of 30,382 older adults reported that co-existing CMM and depressive symptoms were associated with poorer cognitive performance and accelerated decline.18 Similarly, Zhong et al documented an elevated risk of cognitive decline in patients with depressive symptoms and comorbid CMDs.19 These results underscore the importance of early identification and targeted interventions for this high-risk subgroup to preserve cognitive health.

The associations between CMM, depressive symptoms, and cognitive dysfunction may be explained by several interrelated mechanisms. First, both are related to brain structural damage and may mutually aggravate each other in terms of pathology.32 CMM is associated with markers of cerebral small vessel disease, such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH),27 and lower volumes in critical regions like the hippocampus, which are also implicated in depressive symptoms and cognitive decline.33 Secondly, systemic inflammation may serve as a common pathway to explain why the two conditions coexist and result in a significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment. CMM is associated with a chronic low-grade inflammatory state.34 Depressive symptoms can also trigger and maintain the inflammatory cascade reaction. The accumulation of such inflammatory environments may accelerate the neurodegenerative and vascular processes, thereby jointly leading to more severe cognitive impairments. Moreover, the inflammatory response can directly damage the small blood vessels in the brain, reduce cerebral blood flow, and further deteriorate cognitive function.35

This study provides an exploratory analysis of the associations between CMM, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the key subgroups is relatively small, which may affect the statistical power and the stability of the results. Second, cognitive function is multidimensional, yet our assessment focused primarily on sustained attention and executive function, highlighting the need for more comprehensive evaluations in future research. Third, the recruitment of participants exclusively from a memory clinic may have introduced selection bias, as this population likely has a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic and depressive disorders than the general community. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Finally, the regional sampling from Guangdong Province may limit the generalizability of our findings. Notwithstanding these limitations, a key strength of this study lies in its use of validated measures to examine the independent and joint effects of CMM and depressive symptoms on cognition. Our results provide valuable insights for early identification of at-risk populations and inform preventive strategies for cognitive decline.

Conclusions

In summary, this study elucidates the intricate relationship between CMM, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Both of these conditions were independently associated with poorer cognitive function, and the strongest statistical correlation was observed when both were present simultaneously, suggesting a synergistic impairment effect. As one of the first studies to explore this interaction in a Chinese population, our findings provide novel empirical evidence supporting the need for integrated management of cardiometabolic and mental health in this demographic. Our findings, while preliminary, highlight the potential necessity of adopting integrated intervention strategies in the future to maintain cognitive functions. However, further longitudinal studies are needed to verify the above correlations and clarify the underlying mechanisms.
