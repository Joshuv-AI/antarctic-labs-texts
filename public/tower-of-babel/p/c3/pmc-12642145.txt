Post-thoracic aortic aneurysm procedural mental wellbeing: A scoping review
Authors: Jasper C Murphy, Natalie K Kolba, Ian T Winkeler, Lichun He, Deborah M Li, Claire D Kim, Jonathan D Price, Thomas V Bilfinger, Henry J Tannous, Allison J McLarty, A Laurie Shroyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractUnlabelled Image

Introduction

Thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAA) are pathological conditions where at least part of the thoracic aorta is dilated. Although TAAs may remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period, the potential exists for an acute aortic syndrome resulting in rupture-related or dissection-related mortality rates exceeding 90 % [1]. Compared to the abdominal aortic aneurysm prevalence [0.92 % (95 % CI: 0.65–1.30)], the reported TAA prevalence is substantially lower [0.16 % (95 % CI: 0.12–0.20)] [2,3].

Over the past decades, the TAA post-repair 30-day operative mortality rates have steadily improved. For ascending thoracic aortic aneurysms, a sixfold reduction in the 30-day mortality hazard ratio was reported in Southwest Finland, comparing 1968–1980 to the 2010–2014 periods (HR 6.0, p = 0.002) [4]. For descending thoracic aortic aneurysms, the operative mortality decreased from 10.3 % in 1998 to 3.1 % in 2008 [5]. In the past few years, moreover, post-repair operative mortality rates were different between ascending aortic repair (5.96 %) [6], descending aortic repair (4.2 %) [7], and aortic arch procedures (7.7 %) [8].

Although significant progress has been made in lowering mortality and lengthening survival periods, complications when they occur due to the central nature of the organ operated on tend to have a significant impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL); several complications have been specifically associated with decreased HRQoL (see Table 1). Historically, TAA patients' perioperative complications have often led to death. This resulted in a survival-related emphasis being placed upon TAA patients' post-procedural physical wellbeing [9].

For coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) procedures, mental health-related HRQoL assessments are increasingly reported. Several CABG studies documented that post-CABG depressive symptoms are not uncommon. Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale findings of post-CABG depression rose from 20.7 % (assessed preoperatively) to 28 % (assessed at 6 months post-CABG) [10]. Generally, these perioperative depressive symptoms resolve within the first year; however, perioperative recovery period delays and increased infectious complication rates are associated with persistent high scores [11]. Additionally, processes of care (e.g., prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time) may exacerbate these post-CABG findings [12].

Given increasing TAA procedural volumes and prolonged post-procedural survival, a new interest in TAA patients' post-procedural mental health wellbeing is now gaining attention; to-date, however, post-repair TAA patients' mental health status is not widely reported.

Publications only recently emphasize measuring and improving TAA post-repair survivors' HRQoL mental well-being. For example, the association between specific surgical techniques (e.g., cardiopulmonary bypass time or the use of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest) and post-repair HRQoL mental wellbeing in adult TAA patients is only recently being investigated. In contrast, congenital heart surgery research has documented a longstanding interest in children's neuro-developmental assessments. Based on the published literature, this first-in-kind scoping review documents the published patterns in TAA patients' post-repair mental health well-being.

Methods

This scoping review was performed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (Appendix A) [13]; the scoping review protocol and the Stony Brook University Institutional Review Board written exemption for this “not human subjects” research project have been placed online at the https://commons.library.stonybrook.edu/ website.

Search strategy

Between January 1, 1990, and September 16, 2024, a comprehensive systematic literature search was performed using four electronic databases (Pubmed, Embase, Cochrane, and PsycINFO) to identify the original TAA post-repair research articles reporting HRQoL outcomes as assessed by a validated mental health inventory. Utilizing medical subject headings (MeSH) search terms and keywords related to TAA procedures, mental disorders, and mental health outcomes (see Appendix B), this general search strategy was uniquely adapted for each database to ensure comprehensive literature coverage. For comprehensiveness, a Web of Science (WoS) backward search (based on “included” PMID numbers) was also applied.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Appendix B lists this review's inclusion and exclusion criteria, which were designed to select peer-reviewed, original research articles published in English that reported TAA patients' post-repair mental health-related outcomes (without acute aortic syndromes) using a validated mental health questionnaire. As the American and European most commonly used HRQoL instruments, mental wellbeing was assessed using the summary scores and domain-based sub-scores from RAND SF-36 HRQoL and utility scores from the EQ-5D-5L.

Search outcomes and data collection

The original search strategy initially identified over 400 articles. Two reviewers independently screened all articles' titles/abstracts to assess eligibility. For the 5 articles originally included, these two reviewers conducted a reverse WoS citation search and manually evaluated these references; this WoS backward search strategy yielded 6 additional articles.

Two trained reviewers used a standardized data extraction process explicitly developed for this scoping review to extract data from these 11 included studies. These two independent reviewers performed all data extraction in duplicate. For each PMID, extracted data included key elements such as study design, sample size, participant characteristics, TAA repair intervention(s) studied, surgical center, pre−/post-operative mental health assessments, and outcome measures.

Data analysis

Of the 11 included studies, 10 used the SF-36, and 1 used the EQ-5D-5L. As a standardized HRQoL assessment, the SF-36 has four mental component sub-scores: 1) Vitality, 2) Social Functioning, 3) Emotional Role Function, and 4) Mental Health. In several articles, the Mental Component Summary (MCS) and Physical Component Summary (PCS) scores were reported; in all but 3 of the included studies that used the SF-36, TAA patients' mental and physical sub-scores were reported, enabling direct comparison. The EQ-5D-5L is a mental health survey that scores HRQoL in 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. These scores are then combined into an overall utility score for a given respondent. For the four SF-36 mental sub-scores as well as MCS and PCS scores, the metrics of central tendency (i.e., means or medians) and variability (i.e., standard deviations or standard errors) were reported. For the SF-36 thresholds documenting “clinically significant changes,” an individual cardiac surgical patient must have had a change of at least 5.7 points from their preprocedural PCS and 6.7 points from their preprocedural MCS [14]. Wherever appropriate, clinically relevant changes and statistically significant findings have been reported.

For this scoping review, the data extracted were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4). SF-36 population size, surveys sent, and response rate were compared between European and US publications by a Wilcoxon Two-Sample Test, while sub-scores were compared by the Kruskal Wallis test. A protocol-driven p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant; however, all p-values have been reported for purposes of independent reviewer interpretation.

The number of SF-36 surveys initially distributed for 2 of these 7 publications was not reported; thus, response rates could not be calculated for these publications. For the 5 articles with SF-36 survey response rates, the median number of TAA patients sent an SF-36 survey was 103 (IQR ranged from 48 to 124). The average number of SF-36 surveys received back was 69.9 (+/− 45.8); the median was 60 (IQR from 39 to 122). Given that the average response rate of these 5 publications was 85.8 % (+/− 12.1 %) with a median of 84.5 % (IQR from 81.0 to 98.0 %), these included studies had overall high SF-36 response rates.

Interestingly, 6 papers documented a significant decrease compared to normative standards with at least one of the SF-36 sub-score categories: Vitality, Social Functioning, Emotional Role Function, and Mental Health. All studies compared postoperative patients to age and gender-matched standard populations except Meltzer et al., which compared their TAA patients' preoperative SF-36 measurements to their postprocedural measurements [16]. At 1-month follow-up, Meltzer et al. demonstrated that TAA patients' mental health-related quality of life declined post-TEVAR for both Vitality and Social Functioning sub-scores; however, by 6 months [18]. these sub-scores had bounced back to be comparable to the preoperative SF-36 sub-score. In the paper by Dick et al., TEVAR patients scored lower in Vitality levels, but their SF-36 sub-scores fell within the normal range for other sub-score values [15]. In Crawford et al., TAA patients scored lower across all SF-36 mental sub-score domains. Additionally, their TAA patients' Mental Health sub-score had the most significant difference compared to a standardized population [16]. Hamiko et al. reported a statistically significant decline in vitality and emotional role functioning for open TAA repair patients compared to the normal population; however, when patients were stratified by the surgical technique employed in their procedure, only those who underwent a David, Bentall, or Wheat procedure experienced a significant decline [24].

Other publications (e.g., Di Luozzo et al.) also identified lower Vitality scores following TAA procedures [19]. Interestingly, Immer et al. found that TAA patients who underwent deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) had significantly impaired emotional role function compared to non-DHCA patients [23]. Compared to a standardized cardiac patient population, however, four publications did not find any statistically significant differences in their post-procedural TAA patients' HRQoL sub-scores [17,[20], [21], [22]].

In distinct contrast to the CABG literature previously cited, however, none of these TAA papers documented the rates of pre-procedural versus post-procedural mental illness diagnoses (e.g., postprocedural anxiety or depression); thus, the TAA patients' self-reported HRQOL mental sub-scores could not be directly compared to mental illness-related new complication rates (e.g., delirium).

In profiling SF-36 respondents from the 10 papers, the average age was 65.27 years (STD = 11.37 years) with a median age of 63.55 years (IQR ranged from 61.58 to 70.92 years). Broken down by United States-based publications (n = 4) versus European publications (n = 6), the proportion of male respondents varied across the included studies. The US-based publications reported 42.41 % female respondents, whereas European publications reported 25.79 % female respondents (p < 0.0001). In general, the US-based publications' respondents were older, reporting an average age of 71.98 years (STD = 13.21 years) as compared to the European publications reporting an average age of 61.82 years (STD = 8.38 years), p-value = 0.0005. Compared to North American publications, European publications had higher SF-36 response rates (p = 0.0497). Across the four mental health SF-36 sub-scores, European TAA patients had near-significant increased social functioning (median European SF-36 Social Functioning sub-score was 83.05 with IQR ranging from 71.35 to 91.80 as compared to North American TAA patients' SF-36 Social Functioning sub-scores having a median of 46.10 with IQR ranging from 44.10 to 62.30; p = 0.0518) and emotional role sub-scores (median European SF-36 Emotional Role sub-score was 78.95 with IQR ranging from 73.05 to 82.00 as compared to North American TAA patients' SF-36 Social Functioning sub-scores having a median of 41.80 with IQR ranging from 40.10 to 50.50; p = 0.0518) (Table 5). Unfortunately, the European publications did not report any summary PCS or MCS scores; thus, similar PCS or MCS intercontinental comparisons were impossible.

Only three papers reported both a mean PCS and a mean MCS score [16,18,21] in addition to the eight individual physical and mental health domain sub-scores. In Meltzer et al., TAA patients experienced an initial clinically significant reduction in their PCS and MCS one month postoperatively compared to baseline; however, no clinically relevant differences were found at six months [18]. Unfortunately, clinically relevant differences for changes in mental health sub-scores were unavailable in other studies to coordinate a similar comparison.

For CABG patients' preoperative PCS and MCS scores, Rumsfeld et al. previously characterized the changes in six-month postoperative PCS and MCS scores [26]. To evaluate TAA patients postprocedural HRQoL outcomes in the larger context of cardiothoracic surgery, the mean PCS and MCS scores reported by the three papers [16,18,21] were compared to those reported in Rumsfeld et al., 2004. The mean SF-36 postprocedural follow-up time for these three papers was 46.5 months. A Wilcoxon rank sum test found no significant differences between PCS (p = 0.4370) and MCS scores (p = 1.000) following thoracic aortic repair and CABG procedures.

Sharples et al. was the only included study that used the EQ-5D-5L survey. Patients were assessed at baseline and 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, and 48 months postoperatively (assuming survival), providing a longitudinal picture of post-TAA repair HRQoL. Compared to baseline, those who underwent open aortic repair (OAR) experienced a significant decline in the Mobility, Self-care, Usual Activities, and Pain/Discomfort domains that persisted beyond 6 weeks after surgery; TEVAR patients reported near-preoperative scores in all domains following this early recovery period. Interestingly, despite persistent negative impacts in all other domains, OAR patients' scores reflected a significant improvement in the Anxiety/Depression domain. The preoperative utility score was the strongest predictor of the postoperative utility score [25].

To assess for risk of bias, each included cohort study was assessed according to the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) [27]. For cohort studies, the total NOS ranges from 0 to 9, with those studies rating 0–2 (poor quality), 3–5 (fair quality), and 6–9 (good/high quality) [28]. Of the nine papers analyzed, 8 scored a “7,” 1 scored a “6,” and 2 scored an “8.” The deduction of NOS points was due to the self-reported nature of the SF-36 and EQ-5D-5L surveys, the lack of preoperative mental health data captured, and the lack of comparable information for the control groups. All the studies identified in this scoping review had an NOS of at least 6 (with >70 % response rate at the time of follow-up and a minimum follow-up time of 1 year), falling into the “good/high quality” category.

Discussion

Mental health post-procedural assessments are gaining importance as quality indicators after major surgical procedures. Studies have shown that a new diagnosis of depression following an intervention leads to cognitive impairment and delayed recovery and may be associated with increased subsequent mortality [29]. This scoping review reveals substantial heterogeneity in the results reported for an exceedingly small sampling of TAA post-procedural HRQoL articles; thus, the generalizability of this scoping review's results is inherently limited. However, these early findings have identified a critically important knowledge gap in TAA post-repair mental health-related outcomes as well as the lack of direct comparisons performed for mental health status between open surgical versus percutaneous repair procedures.

For 7 of the 11 included studies, the SF-36 survey response rate was high, with a mean response rate of 85.8 % (±12.1 %). A comparison between European and North American studies revealed that European publications reported significantly higher response rates. Compared to North American studies, the only near-significant differences in SF-36 sub-scores was documented for the SF-36 social functioning and emotional role sub-scores, with European patients reporting higher scores for both. However, none of the European publications reported summary MCS or PCS values. Differences in social functioning and emotional role scores may reflect these patients' real-world varying viewpoints of their social and emotional role functioning following a thoracic aortic aneurysm procedure. Alternatively, these differing post-procedural social functioning perceptions may be driven by population-based expectations or cultural norms.

Regarding higher European-reported survey response rates, European patients may have different values of the importance of healthcare surveys, leading to higher survey participation rates. Differences in the healthcare infrastructure, access to healthcare, and patient-doctor relationships in these regions also play a role. Further, no socio-economic indicators were available to be evaluated.

Of the 6 studies that found statistically significant worse post-procedural mental outcomes compared to normative population-based standards, 5 demonstrated decreased functioning in post-procedural vitality (i.e., an SF-36 general metric gauging energy/fatigue) [30]. The effect of open surgery or percutaneous interventions notwithstanding, post-procedural fatigue is a common symptom experienced by many TAA patients [31].

Immer's study suggested that patients who underwent DHCA had lower scores in Emotional Role Function than those who did not [23]. At the same time, Dick et al. found that patients who underwent TEVAR scored worse in vitality compared to those who had regular or open surgery [15]. These findings suggest that specific TAA repair techniques may be more commonly associated with reporting poor mental health outcomes.

Considering that the SF-36 is a self-reported tool that requires a capable and motivated patient, it is likely that certain groups are over or underrepresented. Age and male sex are two important TAA patient risk characteristics associated with frailty, and these were consistently reported across the 11 included studies [32]. 10 studies provided demographic data stratified by procedural type; only three publications included TEVAR patients. Preliminary analysis of the pooled TEVAR and open surgical repair data from these 10 studies indicated that TEVAR patients tended to be older (71.97 years vs. 64.93 years) and were more likely to be male (71 % vs. 66 %) compared to open surgery patients, implying that TEVAR patients were at increased risk of frailty. In addition to adjusting for TAA morphology, future research should focus on solidifying the association between processes of care (e.g., a DHCA approach or TEVAR) with mental health-related outcomes, as well as identification of other TAA patient subgroups that may have an increased risk of new perioperative mental health diagnoses (e.g., depression and post-traumatic stress disorder).

The observed lack of concordance across this review's small sample size may be due to various factors. Substantial variations in study design, sample sizes, and assessment tools across these 11 studies exist; controls based on a standard population or overall healthy individuals in the population do not fully account for confounding variables such as stressors related to hospitalization/surgery (e.g. access barriers to care). Additionally, the timing of these post-procedural TAA patients' assessments may play a role, given that TAA patients' mental health outcomes may fluctuate over time.

Interestingly, Zierer et al. demonstrated that TAA patients younger than 70 years old had lower MCS scores compared with older patients (47 ± 9 versus 52 ± 9, p < 0.03), while age had no impact on PCS scores [21]. However, the younger TAA patients may have had other factors (e.g., increased comorbidity score or increased severity of TAA disease) that were not documented in this publication.

Post-TAA procedure, several studies documented non-statistically significant differences in HRQoL mental health-related outcomes. Eide et al. found no differences in TAA patient's SF-36 Emotional Role sub-scores (p = 0.54) [16]. Immer's study reported that TAA patients had no different Vitality and Social Functioning sub-scores [15], suggesting that some patients may undergo positive emotional and outlook changes following survival of a life-altering operation.

Only one other systematic review on TAA patients' mental health outcomes was identified by surveying the existing literature. An initial review performed by Jarral et al. identified 30 articles relevant to the overall quality of life of patients who had undergone a surgical procedure of the aorta [33]. Most of these articles did not apply to the current scoping review, given that this publication did not separate aneurysm repair from other TAA-related procedures, such as aortic valve repair, root replacements, and dissection repair.

Although many studies did not fit this scoping review's inclusion criteria, some excluded studies warrant mentioning. Benke et al. found a significant difference in trait anxiety between healthy individuals and patients with Marfan syndrome after acute life-saving surgery (p < 0.01), which included both dissection and aneurysm repairs [34]. Other studies, such as Oda et al. and Olsson et al., showed statistically significant SF-36 differences in mental health outcomes following thoracic aortic surgery as compared to a standardized patient population; however, these studies did not separately report the SF-36 findings for their TAA patient cohort [35,36]. Interestingly, however, all studies supported this scoping review's finding for worsened mental health outcomes after thoracic aortic surgery.

Although the SF-36 is an extensively used and validated generic health-related quality-of-life questionnaire that comprehensively measures an individual's perceived physical and mental well-being [37], it has many inherent limitations. Designed as a self-reported assessment, survey findings are subject to potential bias. As a subjective assessment, moreover, it may not align with clinical assessments (i.e., documented symptoms or diagnoses). To overcome these limitations, subjective assessments (e.g., a battery of validated psychiatric or psychological assessments) and objective evaluations (i.e., independent documentation of DSM-5 psychiatric diagnoses) may complement these SF-36 assessments. Given measurement is the first step in any quality improvement project, future research should report TAA patients' baseline and post-procedural HRQoL (i.e., both physical and mental health-related) results to be able to identify changes over time. With each TAA patient's baseline HRQoL assessment serving as their own control, the intervention-based impacts of TAA repair can be importantly compared across patient sub-groups (e.g., different TAA baseline morphology or TAA repair approaches); in future clinical decision-making professional/patient discussions, a TAA patient's interventional care can be customized for their risk profile uniquely.

Limitations

Reported TAA patients' post-procedural mental health for non-aortic syndromic indications is extremely scarce. More research is desperately needed. Across these 11 studies, four publications featured TAA patients receiving ascending TAA repair. Compared to the repair of a descending or thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, an ascending aortic aneurysm differs in the elective procedural selection criteria, the detailed surgical techniques employed, the post-interventional mortality/complication risk [38]. Disparities in the periprocedural experiences between ascending and descending TAA patients likely resulted in different post-surgical HRQoL, as reflected by their SF-36 scores. As TAA's morphologic characteristics may have contributed to SF-36 score differences, TAA patients' HRQoL findings should ideally be separately reported based on TAA pathology, detailed morphology (e.g., size), and repair procedure (e.g., open surgical versus percutaneous) approach. Based on the results reported by Hamiko et al., techniques specific to open repair of the ascending aorta (e.g., David procedure versus supra coronary replacement) may differentially influence postoperative HRQoL [24]. Stratification by technique should be considered for future studies covering the repair of ascending aortic aneurysms. Given mixed population publications did not report HRQoL findings separately for TAA morphological features (e.g., ruptured or dissected status) or by other TAA patient characteristics (e.g., elective or urgent/emergent procedure) associated with the risk of a post-repair procedural adverse event, this scoping review simply summarized TAA patients' HRQoL findings.

This scoping review included articles using different HRQoL reporting approaches; thus, challenges arose when summarizing the available information. For example, some original articles described continuous variables (e.g., age) using median and IQR, while others reported mean values with a 95 % CI. Additionally, some studies reported HRQoL tables containing domain-specific sub-scores along with 95 % CI, but others relied solely on HRQoL summary graphical representations without providing sub-score-specific details. Despite best data extraction efforts using publication-specific graphs provided, data inconsistencies may have arisen; thus, this scoping review's comparison of HRQoL results across the 11 included studies was limited in part by the completeness and consistency of the data available.

Conclusion

Publications reporting HRQoL-related mental health outcomes for patients undergoing TAA repair procedures are currently rare; even rarer still are publications that reported both baseline and post-repair HRQoL mental health. Of the 11 included studies, this review's findings were not homogenous; this heterogeneity is likely due to differences in TAA patients' risk characteristics, TAA morphological characteristics, and repair-related procedural details. Despite publication-specific reporting differences, worsening post-repair for at least one domain of mental well-being was very commonly reported. Warranting future research, post-TAA repair mental health status deterioration represents an important concern; thus, the risk characteristics and processes of care that may place TAA patients at risk for an adverse post-TAA HRQoL outcome should be identified. With risk-adjusted HRQoL prediction models, a rigorous comparison of the effectiveness of differential TAA procedures may be performed while holding other factors (e.g., patient characteristics or TAA morphology or provider) constant. By applying risk-adjusted predictions, moreover, future TAA patient care may be customized to optimize their long-term HRQoL – so they can live better, while living longer.
