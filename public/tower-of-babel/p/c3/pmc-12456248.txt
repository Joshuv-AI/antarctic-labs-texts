First Reports of Canine Coccidioidomycosis in the Paso del Norte Region, Mexico–USA: A 10‐Case Series
Authors: Carlos A Rodríguez‐Alarcón, Ramón Wong‐Saavedra, Javier A Garza‐Hernández, Diana B Rios‐Arias, Edgar A Lomelí, Erick DJ De Luna‐Santillana, Ramón Rivera‐Barreno, Alvaro Medina‐Melchor, Abihail Pérez‐Mireles, Marianna I Quintana‐García, Jaime R Adame‐Gallegos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPresent a series of cases of dogs with coccidioidomycosis residing in Juarez, city in northwest Mexico and part of the binational Mexico–USA metropolitan area ‘Paso del Norte’ which connects with El Paso, TX, and Donna Ana, NM, highlighting the variations observed in the clinical manifestations.Materials and MethodsData on coccidioidomycosis cases in dogs were collected from the University Veterinary Hospital of the Universidad Autónoma de Ciudad Juárez and a private pathology laboratory. The study spanned from January 2020 to September 2022, examining ten cases in total.ResultsAll 10 dogs in this case series showed systemic signs of coccidioidomycosis, with 80% (n = 8) displaying bone involvement and 30% (n = 3) exhibiting respiratory signs. Neurological symptoms, skin lesions, blindness, epistaxis, and orchidomegaly were also observed.Clinical SignificanceThis manuscript reveals the primary scientific evidence of canine coccidioidomycosis in Juarez and the Paso del Norte metropolitan region, emphasising dogs as crucial disease sentinels in accessible areas prone to transmission. Most cases showed bone involvement, possibly attributed to veterinarians associating radiographic signs with coccidioidomycosis. Despite respiratory manifestation being prevalent in research, the study emphasises the need for veterinarians to consider the disease in dogs with respiratory symptoms. The overall prognosis of this disease is poor, with the recurrence of signs leading to euthanasia. This emphasises the clinical challenges associated with canine coccidioidomycosis.Keywords: canine mycosis, coccidioidomycosis, dogs, infectious disease, Mexico, vectors

Introduction

Coccidioidomycosis, also known as San Joaquin Valley Fever, is an emergent systemic mycotic disease with an increased incidence considered endemic to America. The disease has been reported from the United States to Argentina (Boro et al. 2022). The infection occurs through the inhalation of arthroconidia of two main species of Coccidioides (Onygenales: Onygenaceae): Coccidioides immitis and Coccidioides posadasii, which infect humans, other mammals, reptiles, and birds (Reyes‐Montes et al. 2016). C. immitis is primarily found in California's southern Central Valley, though it has also been reported in Utah and, more recently, in Washington. In contrast, C. posadasii occurs in Arizona, with particularly high incidence in Pima, Pinal, and Maricopa counties, as well as in Texas and other southwestern US states with similar climates (Barker et al. 2012; Nguyen et al. 2013). It is also present in Mexico and various regions of Central and South America (Boro et al. 2022; Lockhart et al. 2021). Both fungi are common in environments with arid and alkaline soils with low precipitation, high summer temperatures, and few days of winter frosts; they are found in the southwestern United States, particularly in California and Arizona in the Sonoran Desert. However, in Texas and New Mexico, in the Chihuahuan Desert, cases of coccidioidomycosis are also prevalent and very common (Laniado‐Laborin 2007; Nguyen et al. 2013).

Historical data regarding the incidence of human coccidioidomycosis in Mexico reveals a distinct distribution in the northern border states; however, current epidemiological data are limited. In the latest report of 1994, the Mexican states of Nuevo León, Tamaulipas, Chihuahua, Baja California, and Sonora reported the highest incidence rates of coccidioidomycosis, ranging from 0.7 to 2.6 per 100,000 inhabitants (Baptista and Riquelme 2007). Although coccidioidomycosis in dogs is reported in highly endemic areas of the United States, there is limited scientific information on canine coccidioidomycosis in the Paso del Norte region, where this issue remains understudied

As in humans, coccidioidomycosis in dogs can produce subclinical to severe respiratory infections. However, in certain dogs, the fungus can spread to the skeletal system, central nervous system, eyes, skin, lymphatic system, and pericardium (Caceres and Lindsley 2022; Davidson et al. 2019; Laniado‐Laborín et al. 2019; Ortiz‐Encinas et al. 2015). Symptoms include cough, lethargy, anorexia, and lameness secondary to osteomyelitis. Dogs with the latest symptoms may exhibit hyperglobulinaemia, monocytosis, and hypoalbuminaemia (Graupmann‐Kuzma et al. 2008; Johnson et al. 2003; Kelley et al. 2021).

This paper aims to discuss the clinical presentations of coccidioidomycosis in dogs living in the Paso del Norte metropolitan area and to present a series of cases highlighting the observed variations in clinical manifestations.

Material and Methods

Geographic Area

This region is classified as BWks in the Köppen climate, characterised by a warm and arid climate with hot and dry summers. Winters are generally mild and dry. During the summer months (June–August), average high temperatures reach 36°C, while average lows are around 20°C. In winter, average high temperatures are 13°C, with average lows of –2°C in January. The region's highest recorded temperature is 45.5°C, while the lowest is –22°C. Precipitation follows an intermediate pattern between summer and winter, with an average annual precipitation of 251.69 mm (Tobías‐Ramírez and Hernández‐Pérez 2019).

Study Population

Data for five dogs were collected from the records of the University Veterinary Hospital of the Universidad Autónoma de Ciudad Juárez, through a retrospective review of individual medical records. This allowed us to obtain detailed information on the clinical presentation and progression of each case. The remaining five pieces of data were obtained from a private pathology laboratory analysing biopsies from private clinics. For these cases, clinical signs and case presentation were derived from the pathology sample submission forms provided by the respective veterinarians. The study covered January 2020 to September 2022.

In this series of cases, enzyme immunoassay (EIA) tests were performed at a laboratory with Mexican national certifications. Specifically, anti‐Coccidioides IgG/IgM ELISA tests, validated for veterinary use, were conducted.

Results

In this series of cases, all dogs displayed systemic coccidioidomycosis. A total of 80% (n = 8) of the dogs showed bone involvement, with respiratory symptoms following in 3 instances. Moreover, certain dogs exhibited neurological indicators, skin irregularities, loss of sight, nosebleeds, and enlarged testicles, as detailed in Table 1. The report noted respiratory signs in 40% (n = 4) and identified spinal involvement in 30% (n = 3).

Case 1

An 8‐year‐old male Miniature Poodle was presented to UVH‐UACJ for evaluation. The dog had a previous surgical history of dehiscence following the excision of a cutaneous mass, which the referring veterinary clinic did not analyse. The physical examination revealed bilateral submandibular and popliteal lymphadenopathy and an enlarged and inflamed right testicle (7.2 cm x 4.3 cm x 2.5 cm). This dog had always lived in Juarez.

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was conducted to investigate the presumptive diagnosis of testicular neoplasm further. Laboratory findings indicated normochromic normocytic anaemia, leucocytosis with neutrophilia, lymphocytosis, and monocytosis. The ultrasound revealed mixed echogenicity with variably sized cavitations in the right testicle, while the left testicle appeared unaffected. A bilateral orchiectomy was performed, and histopathological examination of the testicular tissue revealed cells indicative of a late inflammatory reaction, surrounded by capsulated spherules (25 to 30 µm in diameter) containing endospores less than 0.5 µm in diameter, consistent with Coccidioides spp. Additionally, an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) study confirmed the presence of antibodies against Coccidioides spp., indicating this dog's exposure

Itraconazole was inconsistently administered for 6 months 10 mg/kg/24 h PO, and the keeper refused thoracic radiography. The dog developed lameness in the left thoracic limb after 160 days. Palpation revealed pain in the left antebrachium. Radiography showed increased radiopacity in the ulna, indicating chronic osteomyelitis. Carprofen was added, but itraconazole administration continued to be intermittent. On day 437, the dog experienced seizures, exhibited pulmonary stertors, a cough reflex, and was percussion positive. Thoracic radiographs revealed the absence of a hilar pattern. The keeper refused cerebrospinal fluid collection, so only symptomatic treatment was given. Re‐establishing itraconazole yielded a positive response from the dog. Finally, due to the dog's unresponsive osseous problems and compromised quality of life, euthanasia was chosen 2 months after the last hospitalisation.

Case 2

A 1‐year‐old male Doberman Pinscher was rescued from the Humane Society of El Paso and referred to UVH‐UACJ with lameness in the right thoracic limb and left pelvic limb, anorexia, abscesses in the shoulder area, and mucopurulent nasal secretion. The dog's travel history was unknown. Physical examination revealed stertors and a low body condition score of 2/9 according to the Lafflamme scale (1997). In the laboratory tests, a complete blood count (CBC) and serum chemistries were performed, revealing a slight leucocytosis and neutrophilia

Radiography of the affected limbs indicated changes consistent with osteosarcoma (Figure 1), leading to a presumptive diagnosis by referring veterinarians. Palliative treatment with carprofen was administered, and the dog was referred for further evaluation. The thoracic radiographs did not reveal findings consistent with coccidioidomycosis. Additionally, a bone biopsy could not be performed due to the dog's poor condition.

Craniocaudal radiographic view of the scapula of dog number 2, demonstrating severe bone lysis.

An enzyme immunoassay (EIA) for Coccidioides spp. came back positive. Consequently, fluconazole was prescribed for 3 months. The dog responded well, with the resolution of lameness, abscesses, nasal secretion, and improved body condition. However, symptoms recurred upon discontinuing the treatment, leading to a yearlong administration of fluconazole at a dosage of 10 mg/kg/12 h PO.

When the treatment was stopped again, lameness reappeared, prompting further investigation. Radiographs revealed a periosteal reaction, suggesting possible osteomyelitis. Bone biopsy confirmed osteomyelitis, but no fungal spherules or endospores were observed. The dog was treated with fluconazole at a reduced dose.

The dog remained relatively stable, experiencing intermittent lameness. However, the dog developed paraparesis 995 days after the Coccidioides spp. diagnosis. Physical examination revealed proprioceptive deficits, hyperreflexia, and lumbar pain. Thoracolumbar spine radiographs showed a periosteal reaction with a fusion of the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae. The neurological condition progressed, resulting in pelvic limb paralysis and the decision to euthanise. While a necropsy was not performed, bone and lung biopsies were obtained as positive controls. Macroscopic examination of the lung parenchyma revealed a white–yellow dotted pattern highly suggestive of coccidioidomycosis pneumonia (Alves et al. 2021).

Case 3

In the laboratory tests, the dog established hyperglobulinaemia, increased serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP), normochromic microcytic anaemia (non‐regenerative), severe leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Left humeral radiography revealed a periosteal reaction compatible with osteomyelitis. Additionally, thoracic radiography revealed a periosteal reaction on the sixth left rib and an interstitial pattern. A bone biopsy (humerus) was performed to differentiate osteomyelitis from osteosarcoma. In addition, bone marrow polymerase chain reaction (PCR) ruled out E. canis infection. Histopathological diagnosis of the bone biopsy revealed osteitis with spherules, consistent with Coccidioides spp.

In this case, treatment with fluconazole was initiated for 90 days. The keeper was informed that the dog would not regain vision. Orthopaedic signs improved, but the dog developed skin lesions from injuries due to blindness. Subsequently, the dog had a follow‐up blood count revealing more severe anaemia. As a result, the keepers opted for euthanasia.

Case 4

Three weeks post‐treatment, the dog exhibited weight loss, lethargy, and ataxia in all limbs. Neurological examination revealed pain in the cranial cervical vertebrae, non‐ambulatory paraplegia, opisthotonos, proprioceptive deficits, and loss of deep pain sensation.

A radiographic examination of the cervical region revealed osteogenic changes at the level of the external occipital protuberance of the C1 and C2 vertebrae (Figure 2). A bone biopsy of the cervical vertebrae was recommended. The dog was discharged with a prescription of prednisone and gabapentin for 7 and 10 days, respectively. After 7 days, the dog showed significant improvement, regaining proprioception and the ability to walk. However, 3 days later, the dog experienced a relapse, leading the keepers to decide on euthanasia and necropsy. During histopathological evaluation, spherules morphologically consistent with Coccidioides spp., measuring 100 to 200 µm, were identified in the liver, diaphragm, and brain, along with endospores measuring 2.0 to 5.0 µm in diameter.

Left laterolateral radiographic view showing osteogenic alterations affecting the external occipital protuberance, as well as the C1 and C2 vertebrae.

Case 5

Suspecting bacterial bronchitis, the dog received symptomatic treatment, including antibiotic therapy with cephalexin, meloxicam, and dextromethorphan. The dog initially showed improvement, with a better appetite and reduced coughing. However, after 2 months, a relapse occurred, and the dog returned to UVH‐UACJ with hypoxia, fever, and severe cough. Epistaxis and lymphadenopathy were observed 34 days after the initial check‐up. Fine‐needle aspiration of the lymph nodes did not reveal any abnormalities. An enzyme immunoassay (EIA) test for C. immitis yielded a positive result.

Treatment with itraconazole was implemented. After two months of treatment, the dog presented laboratory studies within ranges, did not present a cough or epistaxis, gained weight, and had a good appetite.

Discussion

This case series highlights the presence of coccidioidomycosis in dogs within the Paso del Norte binational region, emphasising its potential underrecognition in veterinary medicine. Although the area is considered the most humid desert in North America (Kirkland et al. 2022), its hot, dry, and alkaline climate supports the persistence and spread of Coccidioides spp., the causative agents of coccidioidomycosis. The presence of documented human cases suggests an endemic status (New Mexico Health City of El Paso Department of Public Health 2022; Department 2017), yet limited epidemiological and serological studies exist for dogs, possibly due to the disease's non‐notifiable status in Texas (Peterson et al. 2022). These findings highlight the need for increased awareness and surveillance to better understand its impact on canine populations. Human cases of coccidioidomycosis have been reported in this particular region, with higher prevalence in counties near the Mexico–USA border. Between 2008 and 2010, a notably higher rate of cases was recorded (City of El Paso Department of Public Health 2022; New Mexico Health Department 2013). While some authors consider Coccidioides spp. a potential fungal zoonosis (Amorim et al. 2011; Boovalingam 2023; Kundu et al. 2024), it is not classified as such due to the lack of direct transmission between animals and humans. Nonetheless, the disease remains highly significant, surpassing vector‐borne diseases in importance (City of El Paso Department of Public Health 2022). It is the sixth most reported notifiable disease in El Paso, Texas (City of El Paso Department of Public Health 2022b). In Mexico, northern states report the highest number of human cases, with Sonora showing a higher incidence compared to the national average, while Chihuahua has had the lowest (Manzanares 2018). In Juárez, no documented cases exist, but since the 1960s, the city's soil and climate have been considered favourable for the development of the fungus (Maddy and Coccozza 1964). To the authors’ knowledge, no reports of coccidioidomycosis in dogs exist in this binational region. However, local veterinarians recognise the disease. For instance, the author (CARA) presented a case of vertebral osteomyelitis caused by Coccidiodes spp. in a dog at a 1992 veterinary congress in Mexico (unpublished). Given this recognition and the present case report, it is recommended to conduct a well‐designed epidemiological study to establish canines as sentinel species for human disease. Defining this region as endemic for canine coccidioidomycosis is crucial, as dogs face an increased exposure risk due to their close contact with contaminated soil. In particular, stray dogs, such as those in Juárez, could serve as valuable sentinels for human cases (Grayzel et al. 2017).

The variation in reported human cases within the Paso del Norte region can be attributed to differences in disease reporting regulations. Various countries do not require the reporting of coccidioidomycosis (Ashraf et al. 2020). In the United States, reporting mandates vary by state: Texas does not require reporting to the CDC, while New Mexico and Arizona do. This variability is also reflected within the Paso del Norte region. In Texas, coccidioidomycosis is not reportable at the state level (Peterson et al. 2022), but the city of El Paso considers it ‘notifiable within 72 hours’ (City of El Paso Department of Public Health 2022). In contrast, New Mexico has seen an increase in cases, as reporting is mandatory there (Tsang et al. 2013). In Mexico, reporting is not required under NOM‐017‐SSA2‐2012 (Diario Oficial de la Federación, Secretaría de Salud 2013), and it has not been mandatory since 1994 (Baptista and Riquelme 2007). Similarly, the Mexican Official Standard for veterinary medicine does not designate coccidioidomycosis as a mandatory reportable disease (Diario Oficial de la Federación 2021). Considering dogs’ proximity to the ground and their higher susceptibility to this mycosis, it is proposed that coccidioidomycosis in veterinary medicine should be a compulsory reportable disease. The importance of routinely reporting cases of coccidioidomycosis in dogs was previously established by Ferguson et al. (2024), who also highlighted the value of dogs as naturally occurring models for studying new treatment options and preventive measures. In this case series, all dogs exhibited systemic signs of coccidioidomycosis. Bone involvement was the predominant manifestation, observed in 8 dogs (80%), followed by respiratory signs in 3 cases. Additionally, some dogs presented neurological signs, skin lesions, blindness, epistaxis, and orchidomegaly, as indicated in Table 1. These findings contrast with previous reports where pulmonary involvement is the most common form of canine coccidioidomycosis, characterised by acute to chronic cough, varying fever, anorexia, and weight loss (Davidson et al. 2019). Radiographic lesions include lobar pneumonia, nodular lesions, and hilar lymphadenomegaly (Johnson et al. 2003). In this report, respiratory signs were observed in 4 dogs (40%). Disseminated coccidioidomycosis in dogs may manifest as skin lesions, osteomyelitis, and central nervous system disease. Spinal involvement can cause nonspecific pain, paresis, or paralysis (Graupmann‐Kuzma et al. 2008). These types of lesions were observed in 3 dogs (30%) in this manuscript. Sykes (2014) reported that specific organs, such as the testicles and eyes, are less commonly affected by coccidioidomycosis in dogs. Our study observed orchitis in one dog (10%) and uveitis in another (10%). Furthermore, our study's high prevalence of osteomyelitis cases may be attributed to 5 dogs being exclusively diagnosed in the animal pathology laboratory. Veterinarians performed biopsies on these dogs due to lameness and radiographic evidence of osteolysis. Nevertheless, veterinarians in the region must consider chronic cough, whether dry or moist, as the most common sign of canine pulmonary coccidioidomycosis (Graupmann‐Kuzma et al. 2008).

There are challenges in diagnosing coccidioidomycosis, categorised as (Boro et al. 2022) technical efficiency of laboratory tests and (Reyes‐Montes et al. 2016) supplier knowledge and performance (Williams and Chiller 2022). Culture, histopathology, and serology have traditionally been key laboratory tests for coccidioidomycosis diagnosis (Ampel 2011). In research, immunohistochemistry and molecular techniques play a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis of coccidioidomycosis (Ajithdoss et al. 2011; Alves et al. 2021). Culture was traditionally considered the gold standard diagnostic method, but diagnosis can also be made based on the morphological characteristics of the agent in affected tissues (Alves et al. 2021; Guarner and Brandt 2011). Wheat and Knox (2014) reported the histopathological/cytopathological identification of spherules, ranging in diameter from 20 to 200 µm and filled with 2‐ to 4‐µm‐diameter endospores, as the gold standard for coccidioidomycosis diagnosis. Cytology has a sensitivity of 15%–75% for demonstrating spherules, compared to 23%–84% for histopathology, with high specificity for both methods (Williams and Chiller 2022). In the current case series, spherules of the microorganism were identified (Figure 5) in the bone of 6 dogs (60%) and in testicular tissue (case 1). Two dogs in this study were diagnosed using EIA, which offers the advantages of rapid testing and does not require referral to a reference laboratory. The presence of isolated IgM and IgG reactivity by EIA in symptomatic patients (as in our cases) suggests a high likelihood of coccidioidomycosis. However, asymptomatic patients may also show IgM+/IgG− reactivity (Blair et al. 2013). A limitation of this study was the inability to differentiate C. immitis from C. posadasii as causative agents, which could be resolved through molecular screening using PCR. However, due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, these tests could not be conducted. Although the literature suggests that C. posadasii may be more common in the region of this study (Barker et al. 2012; Nguyen et al. 2013), these distinctions do not have clinical significance in terms of treatment (Kollath et al. 2019)

Dogs with pulmonary infections typically require 6–12 months of antimycotic therapy, while those with disseminated disease often need long‐term treatment (Jaffey et al. 2023). The prognosis for dogs with coccidioidomycosis remains uncertain, with some references indicating a lack of recovery in dogs with multiple bone involvement. In a study, 8 out of 9 dogs still had IgG titres ≥ 1:8 one year after diagnosis, indicating the necessity of extended azole therapy. Only 1 dog remained asymptomatic 62 days after fluconazole treatment cessation. Additionally, the potential for relapse or recrudescence after prolonged treatment in dogs is currently unknown (Shaver et al. 2022). Based on the data from our study, the prognosis for dogs with systemic coccidioidomycosis in the Paso del Norte region is guarded. Four of the 5 dogs followed up for an extended period were euthanised. Dog number one experienced a relapse with neurological signs (seizures), coughing, and lameness 13 months after diagnosis. Dog number two had a relapse with paraparesis caused by osteomyelitis in the lumbar vertebrae nearly 33 months after diagnosis. Dog number three was euthanised 3 months after diagnosis due to failure to recover from blindness and anaemia. Dog number four was euthanised 3 weeks after the examination due to osteomyelitis in the cervical vertebrae, which was confirmed through post‐mortem examination.

The clinical cases presented in the paper were from a teaching hospital. Patients at such facilities may be more severely ill due to being referred from other practices or having visited multiple veterinarians before arriving at the teaching hospital, which can result in delayed diagnoses. Considering that the dissemination rate of coccidioidomycosis in dogs can be as high as 25% in the United States (Johnson et al. 2003; Graupmann‐Kuzma et al. 2008), it suggests that many of the reported cases might represent a delayed presentation of the disease, potentially not initially considered, leading to diagnostic delays. This factor must be considered.

Conclusions

This manuscript provides the first scientific evidence of canine coccidioidomycosis in the Paso del Norte metropolitan region. This finding is of great significance given the pivotal role of dogs as disease sentinels, particularly when they have unrestricted access to recreational areas in locations conducive to disease transmission. For instance, in the Samalayuca Protected Natural Area in Juarez, sand dust storms, rodent burrows, and Larrea tridentata plants create favourable conditions for disease spread.

Notably, most dogs in this study had bone involvement, and some additional organs were affected. This could be due to veterinarians suspecting coccidioidomycosis upon observing radiographic signs of osteomyelitis. However, research has indicated that respiratory manifestation is the most prevalent form of the disease. Thus, it is recommended that veterinarians in this region regularly consider coccidioidomycosis as a possible diagnosis in dogs presenting respiratory symptoms. In summary, the prognosis of canine coccidioidomycosis is generally poor, as most dogs experience recurrences that eventually require euthanasia.
