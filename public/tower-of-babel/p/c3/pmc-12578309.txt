Clinical management of liver cancer patients with immune checkpoint inhibitors treatment
Authors: Johnny C W Yau, Landon L Chan, Stephen L Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment paradigm of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has evolved with the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Two ICI-based regimens have gained regulatory approval worldwide in unresectable HCC as first line treatment based on the IMBrave150 and HIMALAYA trial. Other regimens such as camrelizumab-rivoceranib and ipilimumab-nivolumab also demonstrated improvement in overall survival as compared to multi-targeted agents in recent phase III clinical trials. With the growing amount of evidence, it is imperative for clinicians to decide the most suitable ICI therapy for each patient based on their disease status and tolerability. Furthermore, ICI in combination with locoregional treatment such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has been shown to prolong the progression-free survival as compared to TACE alone. In early-stage HCC, the role of ICI has been studied in both the adjuvant and neoadjuvant setting. In this narrative review, we will highlight the major advancement of ICI in different stages of HCC and their implication in real world practice. The unmet need in the special population of liver cancer patients and the management of immune-related hepatitis will also be addressed.Keywords: Liver neoplasia, immune checkpoint inhibitors, systemic therapy, neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment, immune related hepatitis

Introduction

Liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for over 80% of the caseloads [1,2]. A significant proportion of HCC patients already present with advanced stage disease at diagnosis according to BCLC staging [3]. These patients have poor prognosis with a median overall survival (OS) around 10 to 13 months in the era of multikinase inhibitors (MKIs), e.g., sorafenib/lenvatinib [4–6].

While MKIs suppress tumor proliferation by inhibiting growth factor signaling and angiogenesis [7], immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) is another emerging class of drug in HCC works by a different mechanism [8]. ICIs primarily target the two principal pathways involved in regulating T-cell activation, namely cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed death 1 (PD-1) [9–10]. Through binding with their respective ligands expressed on tumor and antigen-presenting cells, T-cell activation is downregulated, and subsequently allows cancer cells to escape the immune response. ICIs, such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, exert their effect by blockade of the inhibitory signals and thus restoring anti-tumor immunity [11].

Early clinical trials with tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4) and nivolumab (anti-PD-1) have shown promising safety and efficacy of immunotherapy in HCC [12,13]. Following FDA’s approval of atezolizumab-bevacizumab (atezo/bev) based on IMbrave150 trial in 2020 [14,15], ICIs have become the mainstay of systemic therapy in the field of liver cancer. Dual ICIs, or ICI in combination with anti-angiogenic agents or MKIs, are widely studied in major clinical trials, with impressive results of median OS around 2 years [16–20]. Recently, long term data from HIMALAYA trial indicates that response to ICIs can be durable up to years of follow-up in some patients [21]. Nevertheless, questions still remain in identifying patients who can benefit from ICI treatment, as predictive or prognostic biomarkers with proven clinical utility are lacking. In patients who are non-responders and progress after first-line ICI treatment, optimal second-line treatment remains undefined and is an active area of research with studies ongoing.

In addition to advanced stage disease, efforts are ongoing to expand the use of ICIs in earlier stages of HCC. ICIs containing regimen, in combination with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) have been shown to delay disease progression in intermediate stage HCC, compared to TACE plus placebo [22,23]. The use of immunotherapy with an aim to “downstage” the tumor for curative conversion therapy is another emerging strategy for both TACE suitable and unsuitable patients [24,25]. For resectable disease, the role of neoadjuvant and adjuvant ICIs is under exploration. The IMbrave050 trial has been shown to improve recurrence-free survival in primary interim analysis but the benefit is not sustained on longer follow-up [26,27]. In the neoadjuvant setting, most of the evidence comes from phase I/II studies and remains an investigational approach at this moment [28].

In this narrative review, we will give an overview of the evolving evidence of ICIs in different stages of HCC patients, with their implication in clinical practice. Advancement concerning predictive and prognostic biomarkers in ICI treated HCC patients will also be reviewed. Moreover, liver cancer patients with comorbidities and rare histological subtypes are often excluded from major clinical trials, which constitute a substantial proportion of patients in real-world settings [29]. The role of ICIs in these populations will be discussed. Finally, immune associated hepatitis represents a considerable concern in HCC patients given concomitant underlying liver disease [30], a brief overview on the new data regarding their association and management will be provided.

ICI in advanced-stage HCC

Before exploring the robust evidence of ICI plus MKI/anti-antiangiogenic agent and dual ICIs in advanced HCC, a basic understanding of the scientific rationale behind can provide insight to the observed benefit in clinical trials.

Anti-angiogenic agent enhances immunotherapy efficacy through counteracting immunosuppressive functions [31]. Pro-angiogenic factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietins, hepatocyte growth factor and platelet-derived growth factor exert local and intratumoral effects through regulatory T cell proliferation [32], dendritic cell maturation [33], attraction of myeloid-derived suppressor cells [34] and upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules [35]. On the other hand, VEGF also promotes endothelial cell energy through downregulation of adhesion molecules, thus limiting trafficking of effector T cells [36]. Apart from the direct effect on immune cells, angiogenesis inhibition reverses the hypoxic tumor microenvironment by vascular normalization, a process which involves the removal of nonfunctional vessels in the tortuous tumor vasculature [37]. MKIs, when compared to anti-VEGF agents, theoretically provide additional benefit of intracellular pathway inhibition, but conflicting results have been reported in clinical trials [17–19].

The synergistic effect of dual blockade of PD-1 and CTLA-4 is a complex interplay [38]. While the underlying mechanism is unclear, it is thought to be distinct from either monotherapy and not a simple additive effect. In a preclinical model of colon adenocarcinoma, combination immunotherapy considerably increased the frequency of activated effector CD8+ cells while simultaneously decreasing the frequency of exhausted effector CD8+ cells. Such findings were echoed in the same study with peripheral blood from melanoma patients [39]. In another study evaluating gene expression of blood and tumor tissue from patients with different tumor types receiving ICIs, non-overlapping changes in signatures related to proliferation and chemokines were observed in the combination blockade group [40].

Based on the evidence demonstrated in preclinical studies and other tumor types, combination immunotherapy has become an appealing strategy in HCC [41]. Randomized, phase III international studies involving different regimens of ICIs and MKI/anti-VEGF agents have demonstrated benefit in advanced HCC patients, and will be discussed below (Table 1).

IMbrave150

Atezolizumab in combination with bevacizumab has been shown to prolong the progression-free survival (PFS) and OS over sorafenib in the IMbrave150 study (Table 1) [15]. In this global, open-label, phase III trial, 501 patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma were randomly assigned to receive atezolizumab (1200 mg) plus bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) or sorafenib (400 mg twice daily) in a 2:1 ratio. At primary analysis, OS at 12 months was 67.2% in atezo/bev and 54.6% in sorafenib. Median PFS was 6.8 months and 4.3 months in the respective groups. The median OS of atezo/bev arm reached 19.2 months with an objective response rate of 30% observed in the updated analysis [42].

The most common treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) with atezo/bev were proteinuria (29%), hypertension (28%), increased aspartate aminotransferase (16%), and fatigue (16%), with grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 43% of patients (Table 1) [42]. Grade 3 or 4 bleeding events occurred in 9% of patients with six grade 5 gastrointestinal bleeding events in the atezo/bev arm, but only one was considered to be attributed to study treatment, which occurred within 3 months of first dose of bevacizumab.

IMbrave150 included patients with high risks features, i.e., Vp4 portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT), bile duct invasion and ≥50% tumor involvement of liver, which are often excluded in other trials [5,16–20]. In an exploratory analysis, consistent benefit was observed in atezo/bev over sorafenib in this population. The hazard ratio for survival was 0.62 in both patients with Vp4 PVTT and high-risk group [43]. Not surprisingly, the median OS was much shorter in the high risk population, with only 7.6 months in atezo/bev (n = 48) and 5.5 months in sorafenib (n = 25). GI bleeding events in the Vp4 population occurred more frequently in the atezo/bev arm (n = 10 vs. 0 in the sorafenib arm), but the overall incidence of any grade/Grade 3–4 TRAEs were lower.

HIMALAYA

Dual checkpoint blockade with PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors is another strategy proven to be effective in advanced HCC. With the evidence from a phase II trial that a single high priming dose of CTLA-4 inhibitor with regular durvalumab could maintain efficacy while limiting toxicity [44], the combination is further validated in the HIMALAYA trial [16]. In this global phase III randomized trial, 1171 patients with similar eligibility in IMbrave150, excluding those with tumor invasion of the main portal vein, were assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive one of three regimens: tremelimumab (300 mg, one dose) plus durvalumab (1500 mg every 4 weeks); STRIDE, durvalumab (1500 mg every 4 weeks), or sorafenib (400 mg twice daily) (Table 1). The primary objective of median OS was met with a hazard ratio of 0.78 for STRIDE (16.4 months) versus sorafenib (13.8 months). For the secondary endpoint, durvalumab was non-inferior to sorafenib (hazard ratio 0.86) with a median OS of 16.6 months.

The long-term benefit of dual checkpoint blockade is recently demonstrated in the four and five-year OS update from HIMALAYA study [21,45]. In this longest data available among all phase III advanced HCC trials, the survival rate was 25.2% and 19.6% for STRIDE at 48 months and 60 months respectively. Although the study was not designed to compare STRIDE and durvalumab monotherapy, it was observed that there is a delayed separation in survival curve of the two arms starting at 24 months with benefit maintained in 36 and 48 months.

The safety profile of STRIDE regimen was manageable with grade 3–4 TRAEs occurring in 25.8% of patients (Table 1). As expected, more immune related adverse events (irAEs) occurred in combination than monotherapy arm for all grades (35.8% vs 16.5%) and grade 3 or 4 (12.6% vs. 6.4%). IrAEs leading to treatment with high-dose glucocorticoids were required in 20.1% of the STRIDE arm, with most common events being hepatitis (7.5%), colitis (5.2%), and dermatitis (3.1%).

CheckMate 9DW

Nivolumab plus ipilimumab is an emerging choice of dual blockade in the first-line setting. In Checkmate 9DW, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive nivolumab 1 mg/kg plus ipilimumab 3 mg/kg every 3 weeks (up to 4 cycles) followed by nivolumab 480 mg every 4 weeks or investigator’s choice of lenvatinib 8 mg or 12 mg daily or sorafenib 400 mg twice daily (Table 1) [20]. Dosage in combination arm was chosen based on the demonstrated efficacy in the second line CheckMate 040 study [46]. Similar to HIMALAYA, patients with VP4 portal vein thrombosis were excluded.

In the recently published result, median OS was 23.7 months in the nivolumab + ipilimumab arm and 20.6 months in the control arm [20]. 85% of the control arm received lenvatinib while 15% received sorafenib. The objective response rate (ORR) was 36% in the combination arm with complete response observed in 7% of patients, which is higher than HIMALAYA and IMbrave150. Respective overall survival rates of combination and control arm were 49% versus 39% at 24 months and 38% versus 24% at 36 months. For safety analysis, grade 3–4 TRAEs occurred in 41% of patients in the experimental arm with 18% leading to discontinuation (Table 1). The early crossing of the survival curves suggest that this regimen may have a detrimental effect to a subset of patients. Balancing the remarkable response rates and high rates of toxicity, patients receiving this regimen are preferred to have preserved liver function, good functional status, and no significant comorbidities [47].

CARES-310

Camrelizumab plus rivoceranib is the first combination of ICI and an oral MKI showing both improved PFS and OS over MKI alone in an advanced HCC population (Table 1) [19]. In the CARES-310 trial, which predominantly recruited patients from China with HBV etiology, the median OS in the experimental arm has reached 23.8 months, which is the longest among all major phase III HCC randomized trials [48]. The co-primary endpoint of PFS was also met with 5.6 months in the camrelizumab-rivoceranib group versus 3.7 months in the sorafenib group.

Grade 3 or higher TRAEs events occurred in 81% patients in the camrelizumab–rivoceranib group and dose reduction was required in nearly half of patients. Despite the high rates of the overall grade 3 or higher TRAEs, irAEs of grade 3–4 occurred in 17% of patients which is comparable to other major studies previously mentioned (Table 1). Only 3.7% of patients required discontinuation of both agents. The small number indicates the toxicities are generally manageable. At the moment, the combination is only approved by China National Medical Products Administration.

COSMIC-312 and LEAP-002

Before CARES-310, two studies on ICI in combination with MKI have reported negative outcomes. The first one was COSMIC-312 [17], which compared atezolizumab plus cabozantinib with sorafenib. Although PFS was 2.6 months longer in the combination group, the OS is shorter with 15.4 months vs 15.5 months in the sorafenib group. The ORR was only 11% in the experimental group, which is significantly lower than that of IMbrave150 and CARES-310 (Table 1) [15,19]. The ORR in sorafenib group (4%) was also observed to be lower than in previous phase III trials.

With the promising result from a phase Ib study showing 36% response rate of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab [49], together with proven efficacy in advanced renal cell carcinoma [50] and endometrial carcinoma [51], this combination is evaluated in the LEAP-002 study with lenvatinib alone as the control arm (Table 1) [18]. In this double blinded, randomized phase III trial, the median OS was 21.2 months in the combination arm versus 19.0 months in the monotherapy arm. Median PFS was 8.2 months and 8 months in respective groups. Disappointingly, both primary endpoints of PFS and OS were not met. Being the first phase III trial to use lenvatinib as the control, the prolonged median OS was unexpected. This could be explained by the increasing availability of second line choices compared with the era in REFLECT trial. Approximately half of the lenvatinib plus placebo group patients went on to have second-line treatment in LEAP-002, with 23% receiving immunotherapies. Other proposed reasons include double blinded design of the trial, and better experience with management of lenvatinib related toxicity.

What could have explained the mixed result between CARE-310 and the two negative trials? Firstly, rivocertinib has a more potent and selective VEGFR-2 inhibition than lenvatinib or cabozantinib [52]. In a molecular analysis performed with tumor samples obtained in GO30140 study, high expression of VEGFR-2, but not of VEGFA, was associated with added bevacizumab benefit and improved clinical outcomes [53]. Secondly, 76% of patients in the experimental arm of CARES-310 had HBV-related HCC, while it was only 49% and 29% in LEAP-002 and COSMIC-312 respectively. Preclinical model [54] and retrospective study [55] have shown that viral etiology HCC may derive a better response to immunotherapy. Although clinical benefits with camrelizumab–rivoceranib across geographical regions and etiologies are consistent in subgroup analysis, more prospective data is needed to explore the impact of etiology of HCC to immunotherapy response.

Prognostic and predictive biomarkers in ICI treated HCC patients

Despite the promising results of ICIs in prolonging overall survival of advanced HCC patients, responders only account for approximately 30% as reported in clinical trials [15,16,19,20]. Effort has been ongoing to identify biomarkers to predict response as well as prognosis of patients receiving ICIs in HCC, with most of these studies focused on atezo/bev regimen.

Hepatic function reserve is known to impact the HCC prognosis [56,57]. Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score is an established tool to provide an objective assessment of liver function in HCC patients [58]. Consistent with previous studies on sorafenib [59] and lenvatinib [60], ALBI score is shown to have prognostic implication in the exploratory analysis of IMbrave150 [61]. In patients with ALBI Grade 2 or modified ALBI G2a or G2b, OS benefit was not seen with atezo/bev versus sorafenib, which the median OS was 11.7 months and 12.2 months respectively. While for patients with ALBI grade 1, both OS and PFS benefits were observed in combination arm versus sorafenib.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has been evaluated to predict outcome in HCC patients. In a Japanese multicenter study, 85 patients with unresectable HCC receiving atezo/bev were recruited [62]. Pretreatment plasma with cfDNA was quantified, and a custom made panel detecting 25 frequently mutated genes in HCC was generated for ctDNA analysis. The most frequent mutations were TERT promoter (31%), followed by TP53 (22%), CTNNB1 (15%) and PTEN (7%). In particular, patients with TERT mutation were found to have remarkably shorter OS than those without TERT mutation (HR 3.25; p value 0.028) in multivariate analysis. When further stratifying patients into three groups based on TERT mutation status and an AFP level cutoff of 400 ng/mL, differences in prognosis were clearly demonstrated; which patients who had high AFP level and TERT ctDNA mutation had the shortest OS. Indeed, previous studies had shown that TERT promoter gene is a key step in driving carcinogenesis of cirrhotic nodules and frequently mutated in patients with cirrhosis [63,64]. In view of the majority of HCC patients being diagnosed radiologically, these findings together may suggest the potential of liquid biopsy in predicting survival of HCC patients.

A study reporting the molecular analyses of tumor samples from IMbrave150 provided further insight about predictive biomarkers in HCC patients receiving ICI treatment [53]. It was found that genes representing preexisting immunity, expression of CD274 (PD-L1 messenger RNA) and T-effector signature (CXCL9, PRF1 and GZMB) were associated with better clinical outcomes. Multivariate analysis confirmed that patients with high expression of these markers showed improved PFS and OS when receiving atezo/bev compared with sorafenib. On the other hand, reduced clinical benefit was associated with high regulatory T cell (Treg) to effector T cell (Teff) ratio and expression of oncofetal genes (GPC3, AFP). It is noteworthy that patients with high expression of these biomarkers still showed a favorable HR for OS (GPC3: HR = 0.69; AFP: HR = 0.62) with combination treatment versus sorafenib; though a larger OS treatment benefit was observed in the low-expression group (GPC3: HR = 0.29; AFP: HR = 0.32). PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden, while having been proven as predictive biomarkers in other tumor types [65,66], the association was non-significant with no demonstrated survival benefit in the current study.

Second-line treatment after first-line ICI

The optimal subsequent therapy after first line ICI in HCC represents a great challenge since a large proportion of patients progress within 3–9 months of ICI initiation [67]. Currently, there is no high-level evidence to inform the best strategy upon progression, and decision is primarily based on expert recommendations and clinician’s experience. International guidelines suggest MKIs as the choice of treatment [68–70], with several retrospective and prospective studies reporting their efficacy.

Lenvatinib and sorafenib are the most commonly administered agents in second-line settings. The largest cohort comes from a retrospective study that included 1,141 HCC patients treated with first-line atezo/bev in five Asia-Pacific countries [71]. About half of the patients received subsequent treatment, with 53.9% of them receiving sorafenib and 25.6% receiving lenvatinib. Lenvatinib had longer PFS (4.0 vs. 2.3 months) and OS (8.0 vs. 6.3 months) compared with sorafenib. In another multinational cohort with 464 HCC patients progressed after atezo/bev included, the most common agent being selected was lenvatinib (36%) followed by sorafenib (18.4%), median OS were 17.0 months and 14.2 months respectively [72].

The efficacy of lenvatinib is further validated in two recently published prospective studies, both including patients with progression after atezo/bev [73,74]. In the single-arm phase II trial by Kim et al. [73], 50 patients from 13 centers in Korea received second line lenvatinib. Median PFS and OS were 5.4 months and 9.8 months respectively. ORR was 14% with a median duration of response of 9.4 months. Interestingly, post hoc analysis showed that survival outcomes did not differ according to time to tumor progression (TTP) on prior atezo/bev therapy, but were well stratified by the ORR with lenvatinib. Another study, LEVIATHAN, directly compared survival outcomes of lenvatinib versus sorafenib as second line treatment [74]. 230 patients from 26 tertiary care centers across Europe, the USA and Asia were included, lenvatinib was shown to have superior PFS (5.5 vs 2.6 months) and OS (11.9 vs 7.4 months) than sorafenib, which were consistent in the propensity score matched analysis. These findings together suggest lenvatinib to be an effective choice in second-line setting.

Small scale prospective studies also evaluated the efficacy of other MKIs in the post ICI treated population. In a multicenter phase II study on cabozantinib with 47 patients from Hong Kong and Korea, the overall median PFS was 4.1 months, and the median OS was 9.9 months [75]. In the 17 patients who received first-line regimens with proven benefits in phase III clinical trials, the median PFS and OS were 4.7 and 14.3 months respectively. In the REGONEXT phase II trial, 40 patients from Korea were treated with regorafenib in the second line after progression on atezo/bev [76]. The median PFS and OS were 3.5 and 10.5 months respectively. In contradiction to the prospective study on lenvatinib discussed earlier [73], subgroup analysis in this trial revealed that patients with the shortest TTP (<2.3 months) on prior atezo/bev were associated with significantly poorer OS (3.6 vs. 15.0 months) and ORR (0% vs. 13.3%). Given different MKI agents were used with a small number of patients enrolled in both studies, the correlation between TTP of first-line ICI and efficacy of second line treatment requires further exploration.

The utility of continuing or switching ICI beyond progression is another outstanding question. A phase II trial evaluating the combination of pembrolizumab and regorafenib after progression on first-line ICI was reported in ASCO 2024 [77]. Two cohorts of patients were recruited with cohort 1 received prior atezo/bev (n = 68) and cohort 2 received other ICI regimens (n = 27). OS data is not yet mature, but median PFS in Cohort 1 and 2 were 2.8 months and 4.2 months respectively. Other ongoing study includes IMbrave251 (NCT04770896), which investigates continuation of atezolizumab in combination with MKIs (lenvatinib/sorafenib) versus MKIs alone. The result is eagerly awaited to shed further light on this approach. In addition, introduction of anti-CTLA-4 after progression on anti-PD-1/PD-L1 have been tested in small retrospective cohorts showing response rate of 20–30% [78,79]. Concerning the toxicity of dual-ICIs with patients being generally more frail in second-line settings, this approach may be less preferable in patients with advanced disease.

A limitation of aforementioned studies is that the majority of patients received atezo/bev as first line treatment. With STRIDE and nivolumab/ipilimumab gaining popularity, the role of MKIs after dual-ICIs would be of high relevance in real-life practice. MKIs may be expected to have better efficacy in patients with no prior exposure to targeted agents, but clinical evidence is lacking currently. Apart from MKIs and continuation of ICIs, other strategies such as molecular-targeted agents [80] and locoregional therapies in oligoprogressive disease [81,82] have also been described after ICI progression. Although the benefit may be only limited to a selected group of patients, they represent potential strategies to be considered when standard of care options are exhausted.

ICI in early-stage and intermediate-stage HCC

Intermediate-stage HCC

TACE has been the standard of treatment for intermediate stage HCC for around 20 years and is widely recommended by international guidelines [83]. Nonetheless, the PFS is only around 7 months [84] with meta-analysis showing ORR around 35% [85]. Based on the rationale that TACE induces tissue hypoxia and upregulation of VEGF [86–88], which may in turn contribute to neovascularization and tumor proliferation, clinical trials combining TACE with MKIs or anti-VEGF agents have been conducted [89–92]. However, these results were disappointing with no significant improvement in delaying progression.

Preclinical and clinical studies have shown locoregional therapies in HCC can induce specific tumor immune response through antigen presentation, which in turn activates T cell expansion and cytokine release [93]. TACE enhances this response through the use of chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin, which releases intracellular content and changes the composition of cell surface during killing of tumor cells, a process referred to as immunogenic cell death [94]. Combining TACE with ICIs thus becomes an appealing way to intensify this immune response and improve outcomes in patients with intermediate stage HCC.

The initial proof-of-concept study of combining ICI with locoregional therapies was conducted by Greten et al. which evaluated the safety and efficacy of tremelimumab in combination with local ablation or drug-eluting beads-TACE (DEB-TACE) [95]. Thirty-two patients with BCLC-B or C HCC were enrolled and given tremelimumab every 4 weeks for 6 doses, followed by 3-monthly infusions until off-treatment criteria were met. On day 36, patients underwent subtotal radiofrequency ablation or chemoablation, with subtotal ablation defined as complete treatment of a single lesion in the setting of multifocal disease. Treatment was tolerated and both primary endpoints of feasibility and safety were met. Of the 19 patients with evaluable disease, five (26.3%) achieved a confirmed partial response with duration of response lasting from 7 to 19 months. With efficacy demonstrated by CTLA-4 inhibition, a number of studies have also investigated the use of anti-PD-1 agents in conjunction with ablation, transarterial radioembolization (TARE) and TACE [96]. Although most of these studies are single arm in nature with a limited number of patients recruited, results show that this strategy is safe and effective, with improved objective response compared with locoregional therapy alone.

Recently, results from the randomized phase III EMERALD-1 and LEAP-012 study (Table 2), both evaluating the synergistic effect of TACE, anti-angiogenic agent and immunotherapy, were published simultaneously [22,23]. EMERALD-1 study [22] enrolled 616 participants with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma amenable to TACE and Child A-B7 liver function, who were randomly assigned in 1:1:1 ratio to TACE plus either durvalumab plus bevacizumab, durvalumab plus placebo, or placebo alone (Table 2). Median PFS was 15 months with durvalumab plus bevacizumab, 10 months with durvalumab plus placebo, and 8.2 months with placebo alone. Most common grade 3–4 adverse events were hypertension in durvalumab and bevacizumab group (6%), anemia in durvalumab and placebo (4%), and post-embolization syndrome in placebo alone (4%). Grade 3 to 4 TRAE occurred in 27%, 6% and 6% of patients in respective groups.

LEAP-012 study enrolled 480 patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma amenable to TACE and Child A liver function, who were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive TACE plus either lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab or matched dual placebo (Table 2) [23]. LEAP-012 also showed significant PFS benefit with immunotherapy plus an anti-VEGF therapy plus TACE versus TACE alone. Median PFS was 14.6 months with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab and 10.0 months with placebo (hazard ratio 0.66). OS data is not yet mature, but there is a numerical benefit in 24-month OS at interim analysis. Grade 3 or above TRAE occurred in 71% in the lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab group and 32% in the placebo group, the most common of which were hypertension (24% vs 7%) and platelet count decreased (11% vs 6%).

Several key differences between the two studies need to be considered while both of them reported positive outcomes. Firstly, EMERALD-1 included a broader population of patients, such as those with portal vein invasion (Vp1 or Vp2), hepatoma arterial embolization prognostic score D, and Child-Pugh B7 scores. Secondly, LEAP-012 allowed at most two TACE procedures per tumor while EMERALD-1 allowed up to four TACE procedures in total. Last but not least, the timing in adding lenvatinib or bevacizumab was different (Table 2). Lenvatinib was initiated 2–4 weeks before the first TACE procedure in LEAP-012, while in EMERALD-1, bevacizumab was started when all TACE sessions had been completed at week 17. This difference likely provides an explanation for the early separation of the PFS curves in LEAP-012, compared to a delayed separation in the EMERALD-1 trial. This suggests that anti-VEGF agents are needed early to delay disease progression.

While the results from EMERALD-1 and LEAP-012 are encouraging, a few considerations need to be taken before implementation in clinical practice. Both studies utilize PFS as the primary endpoint, with OS being the secondary endpoint and pending mature results. In intermediate HCC, although new options of post-progression therapies and crossover may confound the effect of specific treatment to OS [97], whether PFS can be a potential surrogate endpoint remains undetermined. The schedule of TACE is another concern when translating the results of EMERALD-1 and LEAP-012 in real-life settings [98]. As TACE is often performed as on-demand rather than a fixed regimen as in the trials [99]; how such difference will contribute to patient outcome is unknown. The scenario grows even more complex with the improving accessibility of other modalities of treatment like TARE, stereotactic body radiotherapy and hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) in intermediate stage HCC [100]. Results from ongoing studies, e.g., TALENTACE (atezo/bev plus on-demand TACE versus on-demand TACE; NCT04712643), EMERALD-3 trial (STRIDE regimen with or without lenvatinib plus TACE versus TACE; NCT05301842) and EMERALD-Y90 (TARE followed by Durvalumab and Bevacizumab; NCT06040099) are much-anticipated and hopefully can address some of the unanswered questions.

With IMBrave150 showing atezo/bev achieved longer PFS and OS in intermediate-stage (OS, 25.8 months; PFS, 12.6 months) than advanced-stage (OS, 17.5 months; PFS, 6.5 months) disease [15], trials are exploring the direct comparison of systemic therapy alone versus locoregional therapy in intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, which includes REPLACE (regorafenib and pembrolizumab) and ABC-HCC (atezo/bev) studies. The REPLACE trial allows TARE as a choice of control arm treatment in addition to TACE [101]. Either trials excluded patients who received previous locoregional or systemic therapy. While REPLACE uses PFS as the primary endpoint as per other HCC trials, ABC-HCC utilizes a novel endpoint, time to failure of treatment strategy, which is defined progressive disease and any of the following: loss of clinical benefit, unacceptable toxicity, liver function deterioration, and therapy not further applicable for other reasons [102].

For intermediate stage HCC patients who are initially ineligible for TACE therapy, the upfront use of ICIs based therapy also provides an opportunity to “downstage” the tumor and receive subsequent therapies aiming for cure. This approach was demonstrated in a proof of concept study in Japan, in which 110 patients with TACE-unsuitable intermediate-stage HCC received atezo/bev as first-line treatment was recruited [25]. Clinical or pathological CR was observed in 38 patients (35%) with curative conversion performed in 35 patients after 4–6 cycles of systemic treatment (mainly those who had partial response/static disease). The modality of conversion therapy included TACE, local ablation and resection. Although the study is observational in nature and further validation is required, the result is encouraging as a proportion of patients who were initially considered incurable could eventually achieve a drug-free status (23%). The study also highlights the importance of a tailored approach when managing intermediate stage HCC patients given the heterogeneity of disease. With appropriate sequencing and choice of therapy, some patients may be able to achieve a cure.

Early-stage HCC

Improving the recurrence free survival (RFS) is a major challenge in the early stage HCC population. The 5-year recurrence rate can reach 50–70% even in patients with low risk disease, across both Western and Asian populations [103–105]. Nevertheless, results from clinical trials evaluating adjuvant therapies in HCC have been disappointing. Before the era of immunotherapy, the largest evidence came from the phase III STORM study, in which sorafenib did not show improvement in RFS versus placebo [106]. Other agents including retinoid [107], vitamin K2 [108], and HAIC [109] all failed to show benefit as adjuvant treatment in HCC.

IMBrave050 is the first trial to report adjuvant immunotherapy outcomes in HCC. In this international randomized phase III study, 668 patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive atezo/bev or managed with active surveillance [26]. The risk of disease recurrence or death was 28% lower with adjuvant atezo/bev than with active surveillance (hazard ratio 0.72) at primary analysis. However, upon median follow-up at 35.1 months, the initial benefit was not sustained, with median RFS was 33.2 months in the experimental arm versus 36.0 months in the control arm [27]. OS data is not yet mature but there was a slightly higher number of deaths in the experimental arm. A number of reasons have been proposed to explain the negative result of IMbrave050, which includes heterogeneous pattern of recurrence, unclear definition of high risk disease and limited efficacy of immunotherapy in the context of minimal tumor burden [110]. Moreover, controversies still exist regarding the use of immunotherapy in the adjuvant context, such as the concordance between RFS and OS, toxicity concerns and impact on subsequent immunotherapy response [111].

Evidence regarding neoadjuvant and perioperative use of ICIs in HCC is limited, when compared with NSCLC [112,113], breast cancer [114] and melanoma [115]. Mechanistic advantage of neoadjuvant over adjuvant ICIs has been proposed in melanoma, in which a larger tumor burden enables greater expansion of T cell and amplification of a diverse tumor-specific T cell repertoire [116,117]. However, considerable challenges exist in translating this preclinical finding into clinical benefit in HCC patients. Given the paucity of perioperative HCC trials before the era of immunotherapy, it is unsure whether pathological response, as a surrogate endpoint, can be validated to reflect RFS, which is still considered as the standard endpoint in early stage HCC [118]. Consensus are also lacking in defining major pathological responses. With the four published neoadjuvant trials of ICI/ICI combinations in resectable HCC, two uses 70% as the cutoff while two uses 90% [119–122]. Recently, a pooled analysis of five phase I and II clinical trials with 111 patients who received ICI therapy before liver resection identified a threshold of 90% as the optimal cutoff of pathological tumor regression to predict improved relapse-free survival, but further validation in randomized trials will be required [123].

Special populations

Despite the robust evidence that ICI use is applicable to the majority of HCC patients, it is unclear whether certain groups of underrepresented patients in clinical trials can also benefit. In patients who have major cardiovascular risk factors, history of recent thromboembolic events and significant bleeding risks, given their contraindication to MKIs and antiangiogenic agents, single agent ICI may be a reasonable alternative [124]. This is also relevant in elderly patients, who have been shown to be less tolerant to MKIs with higher discontinuation rates [125]. Currently, with no single agent ICI approved for use in the first line setting, durvalumab may be considered given the non-inferiority result with manageable toxicity profile demonstrated in HIMALAYA [16]. Other options include tislelizumab and nivolumab, with similar OS and ORR shown in the RATIONALE-301 (126] and Checkmate 459 (127] studies.

Patients with Child-Pugh B or worse liver function are excluded from pivotal HCC trials due to the confounding risks of cirrhosis as a competing cause of death [128]. It is also unknown how the impaired hepatic function will affect the efficacy and safety of ICI. In cohort 5 of CheckMate 040 study, 49 patients with Child-Pugh B7-8 cirrhosis was treated with nivolumab single agent and reported a median OS of 7.6 months, the safety profile was favorable and similar to patients with Child-Pugh A liver function [129]. A recent meta-analysis has also shown that the safety of ICI is comparable in patients with HCC with or without advanced liver disease, despite survival outcomes are still inferior in patients with worse liver function [130]. Phase IIIb SIERRA trial is ongoing to evaluate STRIDE regimen in patients with Child-Pugh B7-8 liver function and estimated to be completed in 2026 [131].

Rare primary liver cancers include fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLHCC), mixed hepato-cholangiocellular carcinomas (mixed HCC/CCC), hepatic epithelioid hemangioendothelioma, and some distinct histological subtype like carcinosarcoma, liver squamous cell carcinoma [132]. Each subtype only comprises 1–2% of all primary liver cancers and these patients are often excluded from clinical trials, with limited data regarding the efficacy of ICI use. In a retrospective cohort study by Chen et al. objective responses were only observed in 3 out of 19 FLHCC patients treated with ICI therapy, with median PFS of 5.5 months [133]. It was also observed that FLHCC has low tumor mutational burden and PD-L1 expression, which may potentially explain the low efficacy. While for mixed HCC/CCC, a retrospective cohort with 25 patients receiving ICIs treatment in Korea has reported an ORR of 20% [134]. In this single center study, most patients (n = 22) were treated with single agent ICI and had previous exposure to systemic treatment, the median PFS was 3.5 months and the median OS was 8.3 months. In another case series reported in Japan, six patients with mixed HCC/CCC were treated with atezo/bev in the first (n = 4) and second setting (n = 2), with three partial responses and one stable disease observed [135]. These results suggest that ICIs may be an option in rare populations of liver cancers.

Immune related hepatitis in HCC

HCC often develops in a background of underlying liver disease, causing immune related hepatic events to occur more frequently than in other solid tumor types [29]. In the prospective multicenter study by Cesa et al. [136], two separate cohorts of patients treated with ICIs for HCC or other oncological indications were compared regarding the characteristics and outcomes of immune related liver injury (irLI). The incidence of any grade irLI was 11.4% in the HCC group versus 2.6% in non-HCC group despite the median treatment exposure being longer. The majority of irLI observed were grade 1–2 in both cohorts. Notably, in the HCC cohort, alpha-fetoprotein level was significantly higher in patients who developed irLI, while other baseline characteristics including BCLC stage, etiology of disease and baseline ALT level were similar.

For clinical outcomes in the HCC cohort, patients who developed any grade of irLI had a median OS of 19.7 months (95% CI 15.0-not reached) compared to 15.4 months (95% CI 13.0–19.6) in patients who did not experience irLI. Treatment discontinuation rate related to irLI was 7%, with no death directly attributed to irLI event. Grade 1–2 irLI was significantly associated with longer survival (hazard ratio 0.53). These findings are consistent with previous retrospective studies from Korean [137] and Japanese [138] groups, suggesting mild immune related hepatitis may be an indicator of good prognosis. Indeed, this was postulated to be related to liver tumor cells lysis or T cell infiltration leading to an overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines by intrahepatic immune cells [12]. Therefore, instead of injury, the numerical rise of liver enzyme level can be seen as a response. However, this must be interpreted with caution as disease progression due to increase in tumor burden can also present the same picture. Lastly, the study reported only 16% of patients with irLI in HCC cohort required corticosteroid use (10 mg prednisolone or equivalent) with most cases resolved with observation, this is of high clinical relevance as patients can be spared from unnecessary steroid use and their associated side effects. One point to be noted is that, patients in all these studies were receiving atezo/bev, it is unclear whether the findings are applicable to other ICI regimens.

Conclusions

ICIs have become a pivotal first-line treatment in HCC with unprecedented progress (Figure 1). With this strong armamentarium available, clinicians need to have a comprehensive understanding on efficacy and toxicity profile when treating patients with ICIs in real-life practice. In the advanced disease setting, the benefit of ICIs combination regimen is consistent across patients with different subgroups and maintained on long term follow-up. As a single agent, ICI also has a role for patients who are less fit and intolerant to anti-angiogenic agents. The treatment paradigm is growing even more complex with mounting evidence of ICIs in intermediate stage HCC, but whether these approaches will become standard of care requires further validation. Overall, the use of ICIs is full of opportunities and challenges in HCC patients and we look forward to more advancement in the field with research ahead.

Future perspective

In spite of the proven efficacy of ICIs in prolonging survival of HCC patients, only less than half of patients can benefit from it. Prognostic and predictive biomarkers to guide the selection of responders and optimal ICI regimen are urgently needed. In ICI non-responders, a better understanding of resistance mechanisms from translational and preclinical studies can provide insight to drug development. Expansion of immunotherapy use to earlier stages of HCC further complicates the picture. With a number of locoregional therapies available in the intermediate stage setting, well-designed clinical trials is crucial to inform the best strategy and maximize the synergistic effect in combination with ICIs. Lastly, clinical trials involving lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) inhibitors (NCT06320080, NCT05337137), anti-TIGIT antibody (NCT05904886) and glypican-3 directed CAR-T cell therapy (NCT03198546) are ongoing, which are potential therapeutic options for HCC patients in future apart from PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibition.
