The Richmond Crown Returns: A Laser‐Assisted Approach to Restoring a Grossly Fractured Maxillary Central Incisor
Authors: S. Padma Swetha, Abdul Mujeeb, S. Chidananda, S. Seema, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtensive coronal destruction following trauma or caries presents a recurring challenge in prosthodontic rehabilitation, particularly in the esthetic zone. A multidisciplinary approach combining traditional post‐core systems with contemporary surgical adjuncts ensures optimal long‐term outcomes. This paper reports the management of a grossly fractured maxillary central incisor using a Richmond crown following diode laser‐assisted crown lengthening. A 21‐year‐old male patient presented with pulpal necrosis and apical periodontitis in tooth 21. After nonsurgical endodontic therapy, post space preparation was performed while preserving a 5 mm apical seal. Diode laser gingivectomy allowed for biological width preservation and adequate ferrule creation. An indirectly fabricated Richmond crown was luted using glass ionomer cement. Six‐month follow‐up confirmed the restoration's stability, periodontal health, and patient satisfaction. This report highlights the relevance of combining historically proven techniques like the Richmond crown with minimally invasive adjuncts, offering a conservative, cost‐effective, and esthetically superior alternative to implants. It emphasizes the need for clinicians to revisit classical restorative methods, especially in cases with significant structural compromise.

Introduction

Modern dental paradigms emphasize the preservation of natural dentition, functional rehabilitation, and the reestablishment of esthetic harmony. In clinical scenarios where extensive coronal destruction, often exceeding two‐thirds of the clinical crown, results from caries or traumatic injury, conventional restorative approaches prove insufficient. Particularly following endodontic therapy, such compromised cases necessitate the incorporation of post and core systems to re‐establish structural integrity and ensure long‐term retention and resistance form [1]. Among the available restorative strategies, the Richmond crown represents a historically validated and biomechanically sound solution, especially indicated when the remaining coronal tooth structure is inadequate to support separate core and crown assemblies. This monoblock restoration integrates the post and crown into a unified cast, thereby offering enhanced reinforcement in structurally debilitated teeth [2]. Failure to address such compromised anatomy appropriately may predispose the tooth to restorative failure, including post‐operative fractures, loss of marginal seal, prosthetic dislodgement, or encroachment upon the biological width, potentially resulting in periodontal sequelae. Despite its traditional roots, the Richmond crown remains a dependable modality in select clinical circumstances where preservation and reinforcement are paramount [3].

First introduced in 1878, the Richmond crown is a monolithic, post‐retained prosthesis characterized by the inclusion of a porcelain facing for enhanced esthetics [4]. This restoration exemplifies a custom‐fabricated, castable design that seamlessly unites the post and crown into a singular structure, thereby eliminating the need for multiple restorative interfaces. The incorporation of a post within a cast metal framework facilitates superior mechanical stability and provides a reliable solution for achieving sustained intraradicular retention in endodontically treated teeth [5].

Case History/Examination

Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

A 21‐year‐old male patient reported to the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics with the primary complaint of a fractured tooth in the maxillary left anterior region, persisting for approximately 5 months. The patient denied any episodes of pain, and his medical and dental histories were non‐contributory.

Clinical examination revealed residual root stumps in relation to tooth 21, accompanied by mild generalized dental fluorosis (Figure 1a). The patient's smile line was assessed during the initial examination to guide esthetic planning of the anterior restoration. The involved tooth demonstrated a negative response to both percussion and pulp vitality testing. Radiographic assessment via periapical imaging revealed a radiolucent lesion involving enamel, dentin, and pulp tissues, along with evidence of periapical radiolucency indicative of chronic pathology (Figure 1b).

(a) Pre‐operative photograph, (b) pre‐operative radiograph, (c) working length determination, (d) master cone, and (e) obturation.

Based on clinical and radiographic findings, a definitive diagnosis of pulpal necrosis was established, with an associated periapical diagnosis of asymptomatic apical periodontitis for tooth 21. The proposed treatment protocol included nonsurgical root canal therapy followed by definitive prosthetic rehabilitation using a Richmond crown.

Endodontic Intervention

Following detailed patient counseling regarding the diagnosis, treatment options, and prognosis, informed consent was obtained. Local infiltration anesthesia was administered using 2% lignocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine (Lignox A; India). Rubber dam isolation was employed to establish a sterile operative field.

Endodontic access was achieved in tooth 21. Working length determination was carried out using an electronic apex locator (Root ZX, Morita, Tokyo, Japan) with a size 15K‐file and corroborated radiographically (Figure 1c). Biomechanical instrumentation of the canal was performed utilizing the ISO ProTaper rotary file system (Dentsply Sirona, Switzerland), advanced to file size F5 following a crown‐down technique. Intermittent irrigation with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite was performed between each file to ensure disinfection.

A calcium hydroxide‐based intracanal medicament (Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd., India) was placed, and the access cavity was sealed provisionally using a temporary restorative material (Cavit G; 3M ESPE, Germany).

Canal Disinfection and Obturation

At the subsequent visit after 1 week, the intracanal medicament was removed using sonically activated irrigation with the Endo Activator system (Dentsply Sirona, Switzerland). A master cone radiograph was taken to validate the apical fit of the gutta‐percha cone.

Final irrigation was performed in sequence with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite, 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and normal saline. The canal was then dried with sterile paper points. Obturation was completed using the single‐cone technique in combination with a zinc oxide‐eugenol‐based sealer. The access cavity was temporarily sealed with Cavit G (3M ESPE, Germany) (Figure 1d,e).

Post Space Preparation and Apical Seal Preservation

During the subsequent clinical appointment, post space preparation was initiated 1 week after obturation to allow adequate sealer setting and stabilization of the apical seal. A 5 mm apical seal of gutta‐percha was meticulously retained to ensure the integrity of the apical seal. The remaining gutta‐percha material was removed, and the post space was carefully prepared using a Peeso reamer up to size 4 (Mani Inc., Japan) (Figure 2a), in accordance with standard endodontic post preparation protocols.

(a) Post space preparation, (b) gingivectomy done using diode laser (labial), (c) palatal view, and (d) crown preparation.

Crown Lengthening Procedure

Crown lengthening was performed immediately after post‐space preparation using a 940 nm diode laser, providing excellent hemostasis and allowing uninterrupted restorative procedures (Figure 2b,c). Only a soft‐tissue gingivectomy was carried out (1.0 W, comfort pulse mode) to re‐establish biologic width; no osseous recontouring was required, as radiographs confirmed adequate supracrestal bone. Preoperatively, 1.5–2 mm of sound coronal tooth structure was present, and the procedure gained an additional 1.5 mm, yielding a total of 3–3.5 mm of clinical crown height for effective ferrule creation. Laser parameters (average power 1.0 W, peak 5.0 W, 10 μs pulse, 40 μs interval) enabled precise gingival contouring, preservation of biologic width, and minimal postoperative discomfort. A unilateral approach was deliberately chosen to maintain the health of the adjacent tooth while achieving optimal esthetic and functional outcomes.

Tooth Preparation and Post‐Core Fabrication

Subsequent to crown lengthening, tooth preparation was performed to accommodate a Richmond crown (Figure 2d). Tooth preparation was carried out with a circumferential shoulder finish line having rounded internal angles, placed equigingivally at the newly established crown lengthened level. A porcelain‐fused‐to‐metal Richmond crown design was selected, with porcelain coverage extending to both labial and palatal surfaces to enhance esthetics and functional integration. This preparation allowed the creation of an adequate ferrule, reduced cervical stress concentration and facilitated harmonious esthetic blending in the anterior region. An indirect technique was utilized for the fabrication of the post and core assembly. The canal was first coated with light‐body elastomeric impression material. An orthodontic wire segment, similarly coated with light‐body material, was gently positioned into the canal to replicate its internal configuration.

Final Impression and Model Fabrication

The final impression was made after a healing period of 2 weeks, which allowed sufficient gingival margin stabilization and soft tissue maturation for accurate recording.

The prepared tooth structure was coated with additional light‐body material. A putty‐consistency impression material was then placed into a stock tray and seated intraorally to record the final impression with adequate detail and stability. Die stone was poured into the impression to create a definitive working model for prosthesis fabrication.

Richmond Crown Fabrication and Cementation

A Richmond crown was fabricated using the indirect laboratory technique (Figure 3a). Proper marginal fit and placement were confirmed with intraoral periapical radiography (IOPAR). After clinical verification of esthetic integration and marginal integrity, the crown was luted with conventional glass ionomer cement (Shofu Inc., Osaka, Japan) (Figure 3b,c). The tooth surface was cleaned with water spray and gently blot‐dried prior to cementation. Glass ionomer cement was chosen for its chemical adhesion to dentin, fluoride release, and lower technique sensitivity compared with adhesive resin cements, which were not used in this case due to their higher technique sensitivity and comparable clinical outcomes in cast post‐core restorations.

(a) Richmond crown, (b) post‐operative photograph, (c) post‐operative radiograph, and (d) 6‐month follow‐up.

Results

The patient underwent successful nonsurgical endodontic treatment of tooth 21, followed by laser‐assisted crown lengthening and post‐endodontic rehabilitation using an indirectly fabricated Richmond crown. Clinical procedures were well tolerated, and no intraoperative or postoperative complications were reported.

Laser gingivectomy using a 940 nm diode laser achieved optimal exposure of sound tooth structure and preserved biological width, thereby enabling accurate crown preparation and impression making within the same appointment. Post space preparation was completed while maintaining a 5 mm apical gutta‐percha seal, and the final Richmond crown demonstrated precise marginal adaptation and satisfactory esthetic blending.

Immediate postoperative intraoral periapical radiography confirmed proper placement and seating of the restoration. At the 6‐month follow‐up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no signs of periapical pathology, soft tissue inflammation, or prosthetic dislodgment (Figure 3d). Both clinical and radiographic evaluations confirmed the functional stability, periodontal health, and esthetic success of the rehabilitation. The patient expressed high satisfaction with the treatment outcome.

Extraoral photographs were obtained to assess the patient's smile both preoperatively (Figure 4a) and postoperatively (Figure 4b,c), demonstrating harmonious integration of the restoration within the patient's smile. A 10‐month follow‐up evaluation (Figure 4d) revealed no loss of esthetics or evidence of gingival recession, with the crown maintaining excellent adaptation.

Discussion

In cases of extensive coronal tooth structure loss—resulting from deep caries, previous endodontic treatment, or prosthetic failure, strategic restorative planning is crucial to restore function and longevity [6]. The post‐and‐core technique remains a foundational method in such cases, consistently delivering favorable long‐term outcomes [7].

A critical component in achieving biomechanical stability is the establishment of a ferrule, which necessitates adequate clinical crown height. This may be accomplished through crown lengthening procedures or orthodontic extrusion. Among crown lengthening modalities, laser‐assisted techniques have emerged as superior, offering precise tissue removal, excellent hemostasis, reduced postoperative morbidity, and facilitating immediate restorative procedures—advantages that are particularly beneficial when time and esthetics are paramount [8, 9].

The Richmond crown, a full‐coverage restoration with an integrated cast post and core, offers a uniquely effective solution for severely compromised anterior teeth, particularly when residual coronal structure is minimal or incisal guidance is steep. Its monoblock design enhances fracture resistance by reducing stress concentration at the cervical margin and minimizing flexural distortion during occlusal loading [7].

Unlike modular post‐core systems that require precise adaptation and adequate tooth volume, the Richmond crown requires minimal tooth preparation, conserving residual dentin. Its design supports predictable axial alignment, retention, and esthetic contouring—especially in areas with reduced interocclusal clearance. In anterior cases with steep incisal guidance or economic limitations, this method remains an exceptionally viable yet underutilized option [10].

Retrospective studies indicate that Richmond crowns still account for a small fraction (2%–5%) of post‐endodontic restorations in Indian academic settings, especially in government institutions prioritizing affordability and function. Their continued relevance in select cases, however, is often overshadowed by the dominance of newer, more technique‐sensitive modalities [2, 6].

Rationale for Choosing the Richmond Crown

Modern alternatives such as fiber‐reinforced posts, though popular, demand sufficient ferrule and tooth volume and are susceptible to failure in grossly damaged teeth. Moreover, when failures occur, they often result in catastrophic root fractures, limiting retreatment possibilities. In contrast, cast post systems like the Richmond crown demonstrate up to 2.5 times higher fracture resistance and more restorable failure patterns [11, 12].

Implant‐supported prostheses, while widely considered the gold standard, were excluded in this case due to surgical morbidity and financial limitations. Implant placement would have required bone augmentation and complex prosthetic work, neither feasible nor acceptable to the patient, who preferred a conservative and non‐invasive solution [12].

In the present case, the patient exhibited a Class I occlusion with no signs of traumatic contacts, bruxism, or other parafunctional habits, which are known to negatively influence the prognosis of post‐core restorations. Periodontal health was satisfactory, with no clinical attachment loss or pocketing, providing a stable foundation for restorative intervention. Although orthodontic extrusion can be a valid alternative for achieving adequate ferrule effect, it was not pursued here due to the patient's preference for a shorter treatment duration, reduced cost, and minimally invasive approach. Instead, diode laser‐assisted crown lengthening was selected as it allowed precise gingival contouring, preservation of biologic width, and immediate progression to prosthetic rehabilitation.

Given these considerations, the Richmond crown augmented by diode laser‐assisted crown lengthening offered a highly efficient and minimally invasive route. This combination allowed for biologic width re‐establishment, optimal ferrule creation, and immediate prosthetic rehabilitation while avoiding aggressive surgical intervention.

Revisiting a Forgotten Yet Valuable Technique

Despite its proven biomechanical strength and conservative design, the Richmond crown is largely overlooked in contemporary literature and clinical curricula. The profession's current emphasis on newer technologies and materials has eclipsed time‐tested techniques that, in certain clinical contexts, remain not only relevant but optimal. This case reaffirms the Richmond crown's resurgence of value, not as a relic of the past, but as a solution perfectly aligned with present‐day demands for cost‐effective, biologically respectful, and structurally sound restorations.

Comparison With Reported Cases and Limitations

Recent reports continue to highlight the Richmond crown as a viable option for the rehabilitation of grossly compromised anterior teeth, particularly where esthetics and function must be balanced with cost‐effectiveness [2]. Case reports from Indian and international centers confirm favorable outcomes in maxillary anterior teeth when sufficient apical seal and ferrule are maintained. Although limited literature exists specifically on combining Richmond crowns with diode laser‐assisted procedures, several studies have demonstrated that laser crown lengthening provides superior precision, hemostasis, and patient comfort compared with conventional scalpel techniques [8, 9]. The present case therefore illustrates a contemporary integration of a historically reliable restorative design with modern soft‐tissue management [13, 14].

Despite these advantages, certain limitations warrant discussion. Retreatment, if required, can be technically challenging due to the monoblock design of the Richmond crown, which complicates post removal. Over time, stress concentration at the root may predispose one to vertical root fracture, particularly in cases of heavy occlusal loading or parafunctional activity. Furthermore, long‐term prognosis depends on patient‐specific factors such as oral hygiene, periodontal maintenance, and compliance with recall visits. These considerations highlight the importance of careful case selection and emphasize the need for continued follow‐up to monitor functional and biological performance [15].

Clinical and Educational Implications

This case illustrates the power of integrating traditional methods with modern adjuncts. The use of diode laser technology for precise soft‐tissue management, followed by the placement of a Richmond crown, reflects a multidisciplinary, minimally invasive approach that prioritizes tooth preservation and long‐term function. Clinically, it offers a blueprint for managing anterior teeth with severe coronal compromise. Pedagogically, it underscores the need to recontextualize classical techniques within evidence‐based contemporary practice, enriching restorative protocols and student training alike.

Ultimately, successful rehabilitation depends not merely on adopting the latest materials but on selecting the most biomechanically appropriate and contextually feasible solution. Regardless of the system used, the amount and integrity of residual dentin remain the cornerstone of post‐endodontic restorative success [10].

Conclusion

Although dental implants have witnessed a substantial rise in clinical preference in recent years, post and core restorations continue to occupy a pivotal role in the rehabilitation of extensively compromised dentition. Their enduring relevance is attributable not only to their relatively reduced procedural duration and economic feasibility but also to their capacity to deliver superior esthetic integration in specific clinical contexts. The Richmond crown, in particular, is exceptionally well‐suited for cases characterized by constrained incisal clearance, wherein the cumulative thickness of the core, luting agent, and coronal restoration must be judiciously minimized without compromising structural integrity. Its monoblock design affords enhanced retention and marginal seal, making it an indispensable option in the restorative armamentarium for teeth with severely diminished coronal structure.

While 10‐month follow‐up confirmed stability and patient satisfaction, the case will be monitored annually to further assess long‐term prognosis, given the documented durability of Richmond crowns when adequate ferrule and apical seal are maintained.
