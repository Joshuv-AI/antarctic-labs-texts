Decreased expression of BCL2 protein on the diagnostic bone marrow T cells in adult acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Zong-Yan Shi, Kai Sun, Ya-Zhe Wang, Dai-Hong Xie, Zhao-Yu Li, Qian Jiang, Hao Jiang, Ya-Zhen Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractB-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2) is an anti-apoptotic molecule. Previous studies on its function focused on leukemic cells in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), whereas its effect in lymphocytes is unclear. We performed multi-parameter flow cytometry to test BCL2, immune checkpoint and cytotoxic molecules on T and NK cells in bone marrow samples of 80 newly diagnosed adult AML patients and 7 healthy donors (HDs). BCL2 expression was evaluated on T-cell differentiation subsets in a subset of 28 AML patients and 6 additional HDs. AML patients had lower BCL2+ frequencies on CD4+T and CD8+T cells and similar frequency on NK cells than HDs (p = 0.001, 0.001 and 0.34). Effector memory T cells had lower BCL2+ frequency than naïve T, stem central memory T and central memory T cells (all p < 0.05) in AML. Lower BCL2+ frequency on NK cells predicted higher 1-course complete remission rates (p = 0.016). Lower BCL2+ frequency on T cells was related to poor relapse-free survival (all p < 0.001) and that on CD4+T cells was an independent predictor (p = 0.008). BCL2+ cells had lower PD1+ and TIM-3+ frequencies than BCL2− cells on CD4+T cells, and lower PD1+ but higher TIM-3+, perforin+ and Granzyme B+ frequencies compared to BCL2− cells on CD8+T and NK cells (all p < 0.05). Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), which serves as the most common acute leukemia in adult, is characterized by the accumulation of myeloid blasts with uncontrolled proliferation and lack of differentiation1. In addition to AML blasts, dysregulated bone marrow microenvironment which includes abnormity in human leukocyte antigens, expression of checkpoint molecules, and dysfunction of T cells and NK cells also contributes to the development of AML2–6. B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2) protein is the founding member of BCL2 family. It is located to the mitochondria and exerts anti-apoptotic effect by preventing the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria and subsequently inhibiting caspase activation7–10. BCL2 is involved in occurrence, growth and resistance to treatment in tumors. Up-regulation and abnormal activation of BCL2 protein was firstly identified in follicular lymphoma with t(14;18) translocation11,12, and subsequently reported in various malignancies such as breast cancer, lung cancer and leukemia13–15.

In AML, leukemia stem cells were reported to have aberrantly high BCL2 expression, which promotes its survival and AML maintenance16. Venetoclax is the first commercial BCL2 inhibitor with high selectivity, and its combination with hypomethylating agents has been approved to be frontline therapy in elderly patients or those who cannot tolerate aggressive chemotherapy regimen17. Previous studies on BCL2 expression mainly focused on AML blasts, which was reported to be related to response to venetoclax, chemotherapy resistance and adverse prognosis18–20. In comparison, whether BCL2 is abnormal expressed in the bone marrow T and NK cells and whether this is related to their malfunction in AML remains unexplored to date. Recently, the venetoclax was demonstrated to be able to modify T cell subsets and alter PD-1 and TIM-3 expression on specific T cells in AML21. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the expression pattern of BCL2 on T and NK cells in order to clarify the function of venetoclax on them.

In the current study, we performed multi-parameter flow cytometry (MFC) to measure BCL2 expression on T and NK cells in the bone marrows of newly diagnosed adult AML patients to assess its expression pattern and prognostic significance. Moreover, we simultaneously measured TIM-3, PD1, perforin (PFP) and Granzyme B (GZMB) to evaluate their association with BCL2 expression.

Methods

Patients and treatment

A total of 80 non-APL adult AML patients were enrolled in this study. They were consecutively diagnosed in our institute from August 2022 to March 2023 and had residual bone marrow specimens after clinical immunophenotype testing. All patients’ diagnosis was based on bone marrow morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics and molecular biology.

The induction chemotherapy was conducted based on the standardized Chinese therapeutic guidelines22. IA (idarubicin and cytarabine), DA (daunorubicin and cytarabine), HAA (homoharringtonine, cytarabine and aclarubicin) and CAG (cytarabine, aclarubicin and G-CSF) served as induction chemotherapy regimen for fit patients, and those unfit for the above regimen received azacitidine combined with venetoclax or Sorafenib. The consolidation included chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy followed by allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). The indications, conditioning regimen and graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis for allo-HSCT were described previously23.

The study was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University People’s Hospital.

Flow cytometry testing of BCL2, immune checkpoint and cytotoxic molecules expression in T and NK cells

Bone marrow samples were collected from 80 AML patients at diagnosis and 7 healthy donors (HDs). MFC was performed to evaluate the expression of BCL2, immune checkpoint molecules (PD1 and TIM-3) and cytotoxic molecules (PFP and GZMB) on T and NK cells using fresh samples. In addition, BCL2 expression on T-cell differentiation subsets was assessed in a subset of 28 patients and another 6 HDs.

Bone marrow samples were washed twice by PBS and then incubated with directly conjugated monoclonal antibodies for 15 min at room temperature. FACS lysing solution (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to lyse red blood cells. Next, Fix&Perm Cell Permeabilization kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States) was used and followed by intracellular staining of BCL2, PFP and GZMB. After washed by PBS, cells were resuspended for acquisition. MFC data were collected using FACSCantoTM II (BD Biosciences, San Jose CA, USA) or Navios (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis IN, USA), and analyzed by Kaluza 2.0 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The monoclonal antibodies used included CD45-V500 (BD Biosciences, Clone HI30), CD3-APC-H7 (BD Biosciences, Clone SK7), CD8-PE-Cy7 (BD Biosciences, Clone SK1), CD4-BV605 (Biolegend, Clone OKT4), CD56-FITC (Biolegend, Clone QA17A16), BCL2-PE (Biolegend, Clone 100), TIM-3-APC (Biolegend, Clone F38-2E2), PD1-PE-Dazzle594 (Biolegend, Clone EH12.2H7), PFP-PepCP/Cy5.5 (Biolegend, Clone dG9), GZMB-BV421 (Biolegend, Clone QA18A28), CD127-BV510 (Biolegend, Clone A019D5), CD45RO-Percp/Cy5.5 (Biolegend, Clone UCHL1) and CD95-BV421 (Biolegend, Clone DX2).

The gating strategy was shown in Fig. S1. After removing doublets and cellular debris, lymphocytes were defined as CD45-high/SSC-low in nucleated cells (NCs), CD4+T cells and CD8+T cells were defined according to CD4 and CD8 expression in CD3+T cells, and NK cells were defined as CD3−CD56+. BCL2+ populations were gated in total CD4+T cells, CD8+T cells and NK cells. BCL2 combined with TIM-3, PD1, PFP, GZMB were further gated in CD4+T cells, CD8+T cells and NK cells, respectively. In addition, BCL2+ populations were gated in T-cell differentiation subsets. The naïve T cells (TN), stem central memory T cells (TSCM), central memory T cells (TCM) and effector memory T cell (TEM) and terminally differentiated effector T cells (TEFF) were defined as CD45RO−CD127+CD95−, CD45RO−CD127+CD95+, CD45RO+CD127+, CD45RO+CD127−, CD45RO−CD127−, respectively.

Definitions and statistical analysis

Complete remission (CR) referred to hematological relapse, which included less than 5% blasts in bone marrow, no extramedullary disease, > 1 × 109/L neutrophils and > 100 × 109/L platelets in peripheral blood. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was measured from date of CR achievement to relapse or the last bone marrow examination. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from diagnosis to death.

Continuous variables were compared by Mann-Whitney U test, and pairwise analysis used Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was performed to determine the optimal cutoff values for each indicators. Kaplan-Meier method was used to perform survival functions and log-rank test was used for comparison. Variables with p < 0.05 were thought to be statistically significant difference in univariate analysis, and those with p < 0.20 were enrolled in multivariate analysis using Cox proportional hazard models. The SPSS Statistics 26.0 software packages (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), GraphPad Prism 9.4.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) and R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software were used for the data analysis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics and outcomes

Of all enrolled 80 AML patients, the median age at diagnosis was 47 (range, 16–68) years and 39 (48.8%) were males. The patients’ subtypes according to 2022 WHO criteria and 2022 ELN genetic risk classification were summarized in Table 1. 57 (71.3%) patients received treatment at our institute and were followed up with a median follow-up time of 24.3 (range, 0.2–32.4) months. 52 (91.2%) patients achieved CR, 43 (82.7%) after 1-course induction and 9 (17.3%) after 2-course inductions. Thereafter, 38 (73.1%) patients received chemotherapy alone as consolidation regimen and 20 of them experienced hematological relapse, and 14 (26.9%) patients received chemotherapy followed by allo-HSCT as post-remission therapy and 3 of them relapse. In addition, 2 (3.5%) patients received allo-HSCT at the time of not achieving CR by induction. 48 (84.2%) patients were alive at the last follow-up. The 2-year RFS and OS rates were 51.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 34.7%−65.2%) and 83.1% (70.0%−90.8%), respectively.

The expression patterns of BCL2 on T and NK cells as well as T-cell differentiation subsets

AML patients had significantly lower BCL2+ frequencies on both CD4+ T cells (named as BCL2+CD4+T cells) and CD8+ T cells (named as BCL2+CD8+ T cells) compared with HDs (p = 0.001 and 0.001), whereas no significant difference existed in the frequency of BCL2 expression on NK cells (named as BCL2+NK cells) between AML patients and HDs (p = 0.34, Fig. 1A).

The expression patterns of BCL2 on CD4+T, CD8+T, NK cells (A) and T-cell differentiation subsets of CD4+T cells (B) and CD8+T cells (C) in adult AML patients and HDs. The median (range) values of the individual groups were shown in tables.

The BCL2 expression was further analyzed on T-cell differentiation subsets in which 28 patients enrolled and shown in Fig. 1B and C. The comparison of BCL2+ frequency in CD4+T cells between 28 AML patients and HDs was the same as the comparison using all 80 AML patients (p = 0.006), whereas BCL2+ frequency in CD8+T cells between 28 AML patients and HDs was not significant (p = 0.98). AML patients had significantly lower frequencies of BCL2+TN (p = 0.010), BCL2+TSCM (p = 0.023), BCL2+TCM (p = 0.047), BCL2+TEFF (p = 0.001) and similar BCL2+TEM on CD4+ T cells (p = 0.28) compared to HDs. However, the frequencies of BCL2+ cells were not significantly different on all differentiation subsets in CD8+T cells between AML and HDs (all p > 0.05), and this result might be affected by small sample size. Comparisons were further performed among differentiation subsets. For both AML patients and HDs, TEM in both CD4+T and CD8+T cells had significantly lower BCL2+ frequency than TN, TSCM and TCM (all p < 0.05). Furthermore, TEM in CD4+T and CD8+T cells had significantly lower BCL2+ frequency than TEFF in HDs (all p < 0.05) but not in AML patients.

The impact of BCL2+ frequency on T and NK cells on achieving CR

As shown in Fig. 2, in all follow-up patients, the frequencies of BCL2+CD4+T and BCL2+CD8+T cells were similar between patients achieving CR after one course of induction and those not (p = 0.51 and 0.77), whereas patients achieving CR after 1-course induction had significantly lower BCL2+NK frequency than those failed (p = 0.016).

Determination of the optimal cutoff values for BCL2+ frequencies on T and NK cells for survival analysis

To evaluate the impact of BCL2+ frequency on CD4+T, CD8+ T and NK cells on survival, patients were first grouped by their individual quartiles for preliminary assessment of RFS. As shown in Fig. 3, BCL2+CD8+T frequency was significantly related to RFS (p < 0.001), but no relations were shown in BCL2+CD4+T and BCL2+NK cells (p = 0.33 and 0.85). Next, ROC curves of these indicators were performed based on relapse and were shown in Fig. S2 (AUC = 0.59, p = 0.28; AUC = 0.55, p = 0.57; AUC = 0.59, p = 0.25). Then the optimal cutoff values were individually determined as 83.6%, 84.4% and 92.9% for BCL2+CD4+T, BCL2+CD8+T and BCL2+NK cell frequencies by the maximal Youden index, and patients were categorized as high and low BCL2+ frequency groups accordingly.

RFS of patients grouped by quartile of BCL2+ frequency on CD4+T (A), CD8+T (B) and NK cells (C). RFS and OS grouped by optimal cutoff values of BCL2+ frequency on CD4+T (D, G), CD8+T (E, H) and NK cells (F, I).

The impact of BCL2+ frequency on T and NK cells on RFS and OS

As shown in Fig. 3, both low BCL2+CD4+T and low BCL2+CD8+T frequencies were significantly related to poorer RFS (2-year RFS rate: 0 vs. 58.0% [95% CI: 39.8%−72.5%], p < 0.001; 15.6% [95% CI: 2.6%−38.9%] vs. 66.5% [95% CI: 45.3%−81.1%], p < 0.001), and BCL2+ NK cells had no significant relation with RFS (2-year RFS rate: 42.5% [95% CI: 21.8%−61.8%] vs. 56.0% [95% CI: 27.0%−77.3%], p = 0.16).

Low frequencies of BCL2+CD4+T significantly related to lower OS (2-year OS rate: 51.4% [95% CI: 11.8%−81.3%] vs. 87.2% [95% CI: 73.6%−94.0%], p = 0.019, Fig. 3G). BCL2+CD8+T and BCL2+ NK frequencies had no significant relation with OS (75.6% [95% CI: 47.1%−90.1%] vs. 86.4% [95% CI: 70.3%−94.1%], p = 0.34, Fig. 3H and 87.5% [95% CI: 66.1%−95.8%] vs. 77.9% [95% CI: 57.1%−89.4%], p = 0.30, Fig. 3I).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of RFS and OS

In addition to low BCL2+ frequencies on T cells (p < 0.001), high WBC count was significantly related to low RFS (p = 0.002) and chemotherapy alone as consolidation regimen tended to have lower RFS than allo-HSCT (p = 0.057). Low BCL2+ frequencies on CD4+T cells, high platelet counts and ELN risk classification were significantly related to low OS (p = 0.019, 0.032 and 0.011, Table 2). Variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. As a result, low BCL2+CD4+T frequency (HR: 6.5 [95% CI: 1.6–26.3], p = 0.008), chemotherapy alone (HR: 4.9 [95% CI: 1.1–21.3], p = 0.036), intermediate and adverse ELN classification (HR: 7.2 [95% CI: 2.0–25.4], p = 0.002; HR: 4.7 [95% CI: 1.4–15.7], p = 0.012, favorable as reference) were independent adverse prognostic factors for RFS, and no indicators was independent prognostic factor for OS.

Discussion

A growing body of evidence demonstrates that the bone marrow microenvironment significantly contributes to the pathogenesis, disease progression, and clinical outcomes of AML, offering a promising avenue for refining prognostic stratification and therapeutic interventions. Increasing studies showed that bone marrow microenvironment plays an important role in occurrence, progression and outcomes of AML, which might be an effective perspective to improve prognostic classification and curative effect. In this study, we demonstrated that low BCL2+ T cell frequency of bone marrows at diagnosis predicted adverse outcomes, while patients achieving CR exhibited lower BCL2+NK cell frequency. Moreover, we investigated the association of BCL2 expression with the immune checkpoint and cytotoxic molecules.

Previous study reported elevated BCL2 expression in blasts in AML patients compared to healthy donors in transcript levels24, and showed overexpression of BCL2 in CD34+ AML cells by flow cytometry18,25. Our current study for the first time revealed that BCL2 expression on CD4+T and CD8+T cells of AML patients was lower than that in HDs in protein level. It implied that BCL2 expression on lymphocytes and blast cells were regulated by different mechanisms. Liu et al. showed that BCL2 hardly expressed on peripheral blood T cells of HDs, but BCL2+ frequency significantly increased in chronic lymphocytic leukemia patients compared to healthy donors26. The inconsistencies might be attributed to high expression of BCL2 in bone marrow of HDs26.

We further evaluated BCL2 expression on T-cell differentiation subsets. We found that BCL2 expression was highest on TN cells and lowest on TEM cells in both CD4+T and CD8+T cells. Liu et al. reported that the ability of BCL2+ cells to differentiate to TEFF was impaired in both CD4+T and CD8+T cells, and most of them were blocked in TN phase in peripheral blood of chronic lymphocytic leukemia26. It is reported that BCL2 played an indispensable role in survival of TN cells, while it was not required for the maintenance of TEM cell27. Several studies reported that TN cells were highly venetoclax sensitive, but TEM and TEFF showed less sensitivity to venetoclax, leading to increased TEFF proportions after treatment27–29. Our results provided a clue for discrepant sensitivities to venetoclax among T lymphocyte differentiated subsets.

The prognostic significance of BCL2 expression on AML blasts has already been reported. Several studies found that high BCL2 expression on AML cells was related to decreased CR rates, chemotherapy resistance and poor survival18,30–32. Our study for the first time demonstrated that low BCL2+CD4+T cells were independent adverse prognostic factors for RFS. BCL2 inhibits apoptosis and promotes cell survival, with low BCL2 expression indicated more T cell apoptosis and impaired T cell immunity within the microenvironment. These results implied the role of T cell mediated adaptive immunity in eliminating residual leukemia cells after AML patients achieving CR. On the contrary, low BCL2 frequency on NK cells predicted high CR rates, and the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

T cell mediated immunity is an essential part of anti-tumor immune response, alterations of T cells in AML included diminished Th cell function, increased Treg frequency, and T cell exhaustion3. Consistently expressed immune checkpoint transmitted immunosuppressive signals and served as an important characteristics of T cell exhaustion5. To further illustrate the reason for the prognostic significance of BCL2 expression on T cells, we explored the relationship between BCL2 expression and immune checkpoints. We found that PD1 expression was negatively correlated with BCL2 expression on T and NK cells in AML patients, indicating that more BCL2− T and NK cells inclined to be exhausted. PD1 was demonstrated to contribute to dysfunction of cytotoxic T cells and inhibit immune response in AML33. In agreement with its function, it was reported that high PD1 expression on CD8+T cells independently predicted poor OS and event occurs34, and patients failed to achieve CR had significantly higher PD1+TIM-3+CD3+T cells in bone marrow and PD1+TIM-3+CD4+T cells in peripheral blood than those achieved CR35. In addition, we observed that the relationship between BCL2 and TIM-3 expression was similarly negative in CD4+T, but that was positive in CD8+T and NK cells in AML patients, which both served as cytotoxic cells. Ndhlovu reported that TIM-3 functions not only as an exhaustion marker but also as an indicator of cellular maturation and robust cytotoxic activity36. Rakova et al. reported that TIM-3 expression on NK cells positively correlated with cytotoxicity and related to improved RFS and OS37. Our observed phenomena may be explained by the dual role of TIM-3 as both an immune checkpoint biomarker of T cell exhaustion and a functional marker associated with lymphocyte maturation and potent cytotoxic effector functions.

We further evaluate relation of BCL2 with PFP and GZMB in CD8+T and NK cells. Our results showed that both molecules were expressed at higher levels in BCL2+ cells compared to their BCL2− counterparts, suggesting that BCL2+ cells possessed stronger cytotoxic functions. We speculate that BCL2− represents a dysfunctional state of T and NK cells. Taken together, low BCL2 expression led to an increased proportion of surviving CD4+ T cells in an exhausted state, alongside a marked reduction in the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells and NK cells, which collectively contributing to adverse clinical outcomes.

Hyperleukocytosis was thought to be an adverse prognostic significance in AML. It was mainly composed of early death caused by a higher risk of complications such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, leukostasis as well as tumor lysis syndrome, and a higher rate of relapse and death in the long term38–40. Our study showed that BCL2+CD4+T frequency was significantly negatively related to WBC count, and low BCL2+CD8+T frequency had similar tendency, which was consistent with adverse prognostic significance of high WBC counts. However, the causal relationship between BCL2 expression and WBC levels remained unclear.

In conclusion, our study showed that low BCL2+ frequency on T cells in the diagnostic bone marrows was associated with high relapse, while low BCL2+ frequency on NK cells was related to CR by induction in adult AML. These findings may be explained by the association between BCL2 expression and enhanced cytotoxic activity as well as altered immune checkpoint molecules expression in T and NK cells. Our findings suggested that T and NK cells with low BCL2 expression play a role in malfunctional antitumor immune responses in AML microenvironment, and the current study provided additional theoretical basis for the application of BCL2 inhibitors in AML.
