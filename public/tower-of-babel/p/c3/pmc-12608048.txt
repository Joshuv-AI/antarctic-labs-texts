Pathogenic Variants in ATP1A3
Authors: Kathleen J. Sweadner, Elena Arystarkhova, Ihtsham U. Haq, Allison Brashear, Laurie J. Ozelius
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dominant pathogenic variants in ATP1A3 can occur anywhere in the coding sequence and can cause a wide range of clinical presentations. A practical problem is that sequencing services use one of 3 different mRNA transcripts with different lengths to number the candidate variants. Genetic reports often identify an ATP1A3 variant as a VUS when it is actually well validated. Only 1 transcript, the MANE Select transcript that encodes a protein of 1,013 amino acids, is well supported by evidence. Misidentification of variants in ATP1A3 is disadvantageous when it prevents a confident diagnosis. We illustrate the differences among the 3 transcripts and their merits. Sequencing services should use the MANE Select transcript.

ATP1A3 is one of 4 genes encoding Na,K-ATPase catalytic subunits. In the brain, single-cell transcriptome sequencing identified it in neurons at various stages of differentiation,1 where it plays a crucial role in sodium homeostasis.2,3 Its heterozygous pathogenic variants cause neurologic disease with a wide spectrum of symptoms, ages at onset, and severity.4 Currently, at least 150 of its amino acids have missense or indel variants that we consider pathogenic based on laboratory validation or recurrence with typical symptoms. Of these, more than 100 residues have variants in multiple independent clinical cases or corresponding pathogenic variants in ATP1A1 and/or ATP1A2. Many variants are associated with particular neurologic syndromes. These include rapid-onset dystonia-parkinsonism (RDP), alternating hemiplegia of childhood (AHC), early infantile epileptic encephalopathy, early neurodevelopmental abnormalities (polymicrogyria), and cerebellar hypoplasia with dystonia and other manifestations without hemiplegia. Specific variants are associated with unique syndromes: p.Arg756, linked to relapsing encephalopathy with cerebellar ataxia/fever-induced paroxysmal weakness and encephalopathy (RECA/FIPWE); p.Glu818Lys, with cerebellar ataxia, areflexia, pes cavus, optic nerve atrophy, and sensorimotor deafness (CAPOS); or p.Pro775Leu, with spasticity and intellectual disability.4,5 Clinicians and patients should be able to benefit from knowing the relevant features of each pathogenic variant.

The ongoing problem is that commercial and academic DNA sequencing services have made different choices regarding which of 3 putative mRNA transcripts to use for numbering the variants (Table). The 3 different mRNA transcripts encode real or hypothetical splice forms that differ in the number of amino acids only at the N-terminus. The end result is that in both the literature and the clinic, patients with the identical codon change can be reported to have different variants, and often the variants are called VUSs (variants of unknown significance) even when there is a substantial literature about symptoms, experimental validation, emergency interventions, and attempts to therapy. There are no numbering issues for ATP1A1 or ATP1A2 variants. In ClinVar, one can find other genes where 2, 3, or more alternative numberings are given based on known or predicted transcripts. We assume that this is because deposits have been made that are not annotated with a standardized transcript, and in fact, there may be no clear evidence yet for a MANE Select transcript in some cases because it is chosen on the basis of known biology.

ATP1A3 Transcripts in Current Use for Interpretation of Genetic Sequence

Figure 1 illustrates the sequence differences between the ATP1A3 transcripts used by sequencing services. Panel A shows the traditional diagram of exons and introns for exons 1, 2, and 3 from a total of 23 exons. The top transcript is the MANE Select transcript,6 established as the most biologically significant. It is consistent with 4 decades of research on ATP1A3 since its cloning in 1985 (rat)7,8 and 1988 (human).9 It is also the only reviewed protein sequence in UniProt (P13637). It encodes 1,013 amino acids. The 2 other transcripts both originated as single deposits to NCBI in 2007, AK3160691 (from thalamus) and AK2965571 (from hippocampus), from a Japanese project searching for protein splice forms.10 The second transcript has the same sequence as the MANE Select transcript but also includes 39 bases of intron before the second exon due to use of a different splice acceptor site. It would encode a protein of 1,026 amino acids. This transcript has been detected at low levels in normal brain samples in GTEx (GTEx Portal)11 and at higher levels in a database that combines GTEx data with those from cancerous tissue (Home-RJunBase).12 Its expression as a protein is not yet supported by any laboratory investigation that we are aware of. The third transcript does not use the known ATP1A3 promoter, but it is predicted to encode a protein of 1,024 amino acids starting in an alternative first intron, where it is surrounded by much repetitive DNA. It does not appear in GTEx or RJunBase. All 3 transcripts include exon 2 through exon 23 of ATP1A3 without further differences. As an example of the clinical genetics problem that results from lack of a single consensus transcript, a person with the most common pathogenic variant, p.Asp801Asn, may be told that they have p.Asp812Asn or p.Asp814Asn, misinterpreted in either case as a VUS. For clinicians, incorrect variant annotation undermines the ability to associate genotype with phenotype, explain prognosis, or consider future mechanism-informed therapeutics. Misclassification also impairs accurate enrollment into natural history or interventional trials—particularly problematic given the rarity and severity of ATP1A3-related diseases.

Should the longest transcript (containing SGGSYRIATSQ) be chosen as the default transcript for clinical genetics despite not being MANE Select? In fact, there is reason to believe that variants in SGGSYRIATSQ will not be pathogenic. Figure 2 shows that there are also sequence differences at the N-termini for the Na,K-ATPase genes ATP1A1 and ATP1A2; the N-terminus is one of several sites of paralog-specific differences that are assumed to underlie functional differences. It is important to note that those amino acids are invisible in X-ray crystallography or cryoelectron microscopy, indicating that they are unstructured. Unstructured protein segments are intrinsically flexible because they lack stable interactions with other parts of the protein. Unstructured segments typically interact with regulatory or cytoskeletal proteins and play no role in catalysis; the 2 amino acids shown in red in ATP1A1 are known sites of modulatory phosphorylation. Resolved structure in Na,K-ATPase begins where sequence homology begins, shown by asterisks in Figure 2, downstream of both of the alternative sequences in the other transcripts.

Looking toward the future, there is no current evidence ruling out the expression of the transcript containing the intron-derived sequence SGGSDSYRIATSQ in other tissues or in cancer (where ATP1A3 expression is frequently found). Its position in the unstructured N-terminus, however, makes it unlikely for missense or indel variants to cause ATP1A3-associated syndromes. Small variants there (i.e., setting aside premature stops and frameshifts) would not inactivate the enzyme or make it leaky because the N-terminus plays no direct role in ion transport. SGGSDSYRIATSQ is rich in possible phosphorylation sites though and there is detectable homology between species, so it might have regulatory roles yet to be discovered. Nonetheless, numbering pathogenic ATP1A3 variants by the MANE Select transcript best serves the interests of clinical genetics.

Conclusion

For the convenience of clinicians confronted with a new sequence report, or puzzled by conflicting residue numbering found in the literature, a complete Ensembl translation of the MANE Select ATP1A3 transcript is included in the supplementary data (eAppendix) for reference. We encourage all sequencing services to use the MANE Select transcript; authors to check the numbering of variants before publication and also those in any earlier case report they wish to cite; and ATP1A3-related foundations to update their records and assist in educating the community. Ideally, publications should also include the genome position and build. Consistency is critical as we move into potential therapeutics for the disabling diseases caused by ATPA1A3 variants.
