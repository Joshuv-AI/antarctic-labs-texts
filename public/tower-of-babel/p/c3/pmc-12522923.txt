Prediction of UGT-mediated phase II metabolism via ligand- and structure-based predictive models
Authors: Ludovica Bono, Filippo Lunghini, Emanuela Sabato, Akash Deep Biswas, Angelica Mazzolari, Alessandro Pedretti, Andrea R Beccari, Giulio Vistoli, Serena Vittorio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prediction of drugs metabolism by in silico techniques is gaining a growing interest due to the possibility to process large datasets allowing the stability and safety of new drug candidates to be evaluated during the early stages of the drug discovery process. To date, in silico models for metabolism prediction mainly exploits the ligand-based (LB) properties of the training molecules to predict the occurrence of a given metabolic reaction and/or the reactive site involved in the biotransformation. However, recent reports highlighted that structure-based (SB) modeling can be conveniently integrated with LB methods for drug metabolism prediction purpose, with the advantages to predict if a given molecule can fit the enzyme active site and which moiety approaches the catalytic residues. Herein, we developed machine learning models for UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT)-mediated metabolism by using both LB and SB methods. In particular, this study was focused on UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 isoforms which are involved in the clearance of many drugs as well as in clinically relevant drug-drug interactions. First, molecular dynamics (MD) and docking simulations were combined to explore the binding mechanism of cofactor and substrate within the catalytic pocket of the studied UGT isoforms exploiting their AlphaFold structures. The analysis of the MD trajectories allowed an appropriate conformation of both UGT isoforms to be identified for the development of binary classification models. For this purpose, Random Forest algorithm and the metabolic data extracted from the MetaQSAR database were used. SB models were trained on a set of scoring functions and protein–ligand interaction fingerprints derived from docking, while the LB models were built on a set of physicochemical and constitutional descriptors. When the single models were evaluated, the LB classifiers outperformed the SB models. However, the application of a consensus strategy led to an improvement of the prediction accuracy if compared to the individual models, highlighting that LB and SB approaches convey complementary information whose aggregation allowed us to achieve better predictions than the single models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-025-01097-y.Keywords: Drug metabolism, Metabolism prediction, Machine learning, Glucuronidation, UGT

Introduction

The in silico prediction of drug metabolism is attracting increasing interest in recent years due to its capacity to analyze even huge datasets of ligands thus allowing the pharmacokinetic profiling to be anticipated at early stages of drug discovery pipeline. As a result, such an early pharmacokinetic evaluation allows a remarkable reduction of the related attrition by discarding those molecules with a predicted unfavorable profile. The increasing role of drug metabolism prediction has ben also promoted by both the general increase of the availability of experimental data and the incessant development of artificial intelligence algorithms [1, 2].

Despite the beneficial effects of the increasing availability of scientific data, it should be emphasized that the performances of all predictive models strongly depend on the accuracy of the data by which they are trained, and few inaccurate data can undermine their predictive power [3]. To optimize the accuracy of the metabolic data to be used in novel predictive studies, some of us proposed the MetaQSAR database, a manually curated resource collected by meta-analysis of the recent specialized literature [4]. MetaQSAR implements a hierarchic classification of the collected biotrasformations which subdivide them in 3 major classes, 21 classes and 101 subclasses thus allowing a precise grouping of the included metabolic reactions. Thus, MetaQSAR represents a fruitful source of highly accurate datasets of metabolic data to be used in predictive analyses, which proved successful in several recent studies [5–8].

From a general point of view, the metabolism prediction can be subdivided into two major factors: (1) the prediction of the chemical reactivity which describes the intrinsic susceptibility of a given molecule (and more specifically of each atom) to undergo a specific metabolic reaction and (2) the prediction of the recognition between the substrate and the involved enzyme to assess the capacity of a molecule to assume a pose conducive to the catalysis [9].

The former factor can be primarily treated by ligand-based (LB) approaches involving both physicochemical descriptors (e.g., log P) which encode the overall susceptibility of a given molecule to drug metabolism processes, and stereo-electronic descriptors which account for the intrinsic reactivity of the entire molecule and of each analyzed atom. In this context, atom typing and fingerprints can be also effective in recognizing specific substructures which are commonly involved in the predicted biotransformations.

Although it can also utilize pharmacophoric models [10, 11], the prediction of the enzyme–substrate recognition mostly involves structure-based (SB) approaches. Docking simulations are well suited for this task since the generated poses allow us to predict: (1) if a molecule can fit the catalytic pocket and (2) which molecular substructure approaches the catalytic site. Hence, the proper combination of the outcomes deriving from ligand- and structure-based simulations should reveal if a given molecule is a substrate of a given biotransformation and which is the site of metabolism [12].

Several computational approaches exploited this combination of LB and SB approaches. For example, Kingsley et al. combined SMARTCyp reactivity descriptors with ensemble docking simulations to generate improved QSAR models [13]. Also, Zaretzki et al. combined reactivity parameters with flexible docking calculations by Vina to develop a Docking-Regioselectivity-Predictor by machine learning methods [14]. Similarly, Mukherjee et al. proposed a method which comprises docking simulations and molecular orbital analysis to successfully predict the site of metabolism by CYP450 [15].

The examples reported above emphasize that most proposed projects are focused on the phase I redox reactions with special emphasis on the biotransformations catalyzed by CYP-450, while conjugation reactions are scantly considered by predictive studies although they play key role in detoxifying processes. In particular, the conjugations with glucuronic acid are very frequent reactions and play crucial detoxifying roles by conjugating aromatic hydroxyl functions and preventing their oxidation to electrophilic quinones. The reactions are catalyzed by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes, which are membrane-bound proteins which catalyze the glucuronidation of substrates containing a nucleophilic O-, N-, S- group and utilize the cofactor uridine-5′-diphospho-α-d-glucuronic acid (UDPGA) [16]. In humans, UGTs are classified into four families: UGT1, UGT2, UGT3, and UGT8. The drug metabolizing enzymes belong to UGT1 and UGT2 families and are characterized by a binding pocket which can accommodate structurally heterogeneous substrates. In contrast, UGTs from UGT3 and UGT8 retain substrate specificity and are not involved in drug metabolism [17].

Some LB predictive models for glucuronidation were recently published [18–20], while the lack of SB studies can be explained by considering the scarcity of UGT resolved structures. Currently, there are not complete resolved human UGTs since the two deposited structures comprise only the cofactor binding domain of UGT2B7 [21] and UGT2B15 [22]. Unfortunately, the few resolved homologous proteins are plant glycosyl transferases which show a limited degree of similarity with human UGTs [23]. Hence, they can be utilized as templates with difficulty as confirmed by the few UGT homology models hitherto reported. The situation is completely changed in the last few years due to the outstanding advancements that deep learning methods had in the ab initio protein modelling. In detail, the AlphaFold method can generate satisfactory theoretical models for any protein regardless of the availability of homologous templates [24]. Indeed, AlphaFold generated reliable and complete models for both UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 by combining the resolved structures with the modelled substrate binding domain.

On these grounds, the primary objective of the study was to develop predictive models for glucuronidation by using the AlphaFold-based models for UGT2B7 and UGT2B15. Initially, SB modelling was exploited to get more insights about the binding mechanism of cofactor and substrate within UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 catalytic pockets. Machine learning models aimed at predicting the occurrence of glucuronidation by combining LB descriptors with the docking simulations on the two cited UGT isoforms were generated. The simulations involved the datasets extracted from MetaQSAR and the predictive models were developed by using the Random Forest (RF) classification algorithm which proved successful in recent studies on metabolism prediction. In a more general perspective, this study aimed to verify if the theoretical models generated by Alphafold can be successfully used in SB metabolism predictions to expand these predictive studies on other metabolizing enzymes, the structure of which was not yet resolved.

Material and methods

Protein preparation

The 3D structural models of human UGT2B7 (AF-P16662-F1) and UGT2B15 (AF-P54855-F1) were taken from AlphaFold2 repository (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/, accessed on April 2022) [25]. The residues located at N- (1–23) and C-terminus (485–529 in the case of UGT2B7 and 484–530 in UGT2B15) were removed due to the low prediction accuracy. Hydrogen atoms were added to the protein structures by means of H++ webserver (http://newbiophysics.cs.vt.edu/H++/) [26] according to the physiological pH value of 7.4.

Molecular docking studies

All molecular docking simulations were carried out using the software PLANTS [27] implemented in VEGA ZZ, [28] setting ChemPLP as scoring function and speed 1 as search mode. A RMSD threshold of 2 Å was used for ligand poses clustering. For each ligand, 10 poses were generated. The docking search was focused on a 10 Å radius sphere around the anomeric carbon of UDPGA.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using Amber18 [29]. Two binary complexes were initially generated for each UGT isoform. The first, named E-C, involved the enzyme in complex with the cofactor UDPGA, the second, called E-S, consisted of the enzyme bound to the substrate. UDPGA was added to both UGT isoforms by overlap with the uridine-5'-diphosphate-2-deoxy-2-fluoro-alpha-d-glucose position found in the x-ray structure of VvGT1 (PDB ID: 2C1Z) [23] as they share similar nucleotide–sugar binding sites [21]. (R)-denopamine and (S)-4-hydroxyphenytoin were selected as representative substrates for UGT2B7 and UGT2B15, respectively [17]. The identification of the substrate binding pocket was conducted by considering the binding mode of kaempferol in the above-mentioned experimental structure of VvGT1.

Two 250 ns MD simulations were performed on each binary complex. In all simulations, the complex was solvated into a box of TIP3P water molecules, counter ions (Na + and Cl−) were added to neutralize the systems, reaching the physiological ionic concentration of 0.15 M. The ligand was parametrized using antechamber with the AM1-bcc charges and GAFF force-field [30], while the protein was described by the ff14SB force-field [31]. The protocol used to run the simulations has been described elsewhere [32]. The analysis of the resulting trajectories was performed by means of the Cpptraj module of AmberTools 18 [33].

Cluster analysis allows the identification of the most persistent conformation of each system that was subsequently used as structural coordinates for docking studies aimed at introducing the substrate and UDPGA into the E-C and E-S complexes, respectively, to obtain the corresponding ternary adducts. The so generated ternary complexes underwent 500 ns MD simulation to explore the conformational changes induced by the addition of the substrate or cofactor using the same procedure described above.

MM-GBSA calculations

Binding free energy calculations were performed using the MM-GBSA method by means of the MMPBSA.py tool [34] implemented in AMBER18, setting a solvent dielectric constant of 78.5 and an ionic concentration of 0.15 M.

Data collection

The molecules composing the initial dataset were collected from the manually-curated MetaQSAR database containing experimentally validated substrates covering different chemical classes of xenobiotics classified basing on the specific metabolic reaction [4]. The positive samples were selected according to the metabolic reaction classes 21, 22.01 and 22.02 encoding for O-glucuronidation, N-glucuronidation of amines and N-glucuronidation of amides, respectively. Instead, the negative samples consist of molecules giving metabolic reactions other than those coded 21 and 22.01-02. The so obtained dataset was refined removing metabolites and duplicates resulting in 373 substrates and 1922 non-substrates.

To avoid some structural biases that might affect the ability of the models to discriminate between substrates and non-substrates, the negative samples were filtered to comprise only substrates containing the functional groups that can undergo glucuronidation reaction. In addition, to balance the two classes, the non-substrates set was further filtered by means of the Diversity Picker tool of RDKit (https://www.rdkit.org/). The resulting dataset included 373 substrates and 361 non-substrates.

The collected molecules were ionized considering the physiological pH value of 7.4 and optimized by semi-empirical calculations using MOPAC 2016 [35] setting the external dielectric constant at 80, the Hamiltonian operator PM7 and calculating the super-delocalization parameters by using the keyword “SUPER”.

Chemical space analysis

Chemical space distribution was investigated through principal component analysis (PCA) performed by means of OriginPro 2017 (https://www.originlab.com/) basing on 26 physicochemical descriptors as computed by VegaZZ program [28].

Structure- and ligand based descriptors calculations

SB descriptors included a set of scoring functions and protein–ligand interaction fingerprints (PLEC) [36] derived from docking calculations of our dataset. The docking simulations were performed by using the same protocol described above employing the representative UGT structures as obtained by cluster analysis of the MD simulations performed on the ternary complexes in which the substrate was inserted after the cofactor and assumes a pose conducive to the metabolic reaction. The resulting poses were subjected to a rescoring procedure by means of the Rescore + tool implemented in VEGA ZZ, which allowed the calculation of twenty-five scoring functions (Table S1) including (i) the Elect and ElectDD scores for the electrostatic interactions; (ii) the MLP interaction scores describing the hydrophobic contacts [37]; (iii) the different components of PLANTS and Xscore scoring functions; (iv) CVFF and CHARMM Lennard-Jonnes components for the evaluation of the Van der Waals interactions and (v) the Contacts score which account for the number of the residues surrounding the bound ligand [38].

PLEC (protein–ligand extended connectivity) fingerprints were computed on the complexes obtained from docking using protein depth 4, ligand depth 2 and FP size of 16,348.

Twenty-five LB attributes (Table S2) encompassing constitutional and 2D- and 3D-physicochemical descriptors, which have been proven to be successful in the development of predictive models for metabolism prediction in previous studies [5, 6], were computed by VEGA ZZ program basing on the conformations obtained from docking simulations.

LB and SB features were used to train machine learning models considering for each compound the mean value computed over all the generated poses according to the properties and binding space concepts [39–41].

Models training and validation

The classification models reported in this study were generated by means of a Konstanz Information Miner (KNIME) [42] workflow as follow. The initial dataset extracted from MetaQSAR was split into 80% training and 20% test set using a stratified sampling approach. Data pre-processing was carried out by removing high correlated features (correlation coefficient > 0.99). For the models trained on PLEC fingerprints a low variance filter was applied to filter out attributes with variance lower than 0.1. Feature selection was performed employing a "Forward Feature Selection" strategy which is an iterative approach in which a new variable is added at each iteration until the addition of a new feature does not increase the model performance. Concerning UGT2B7, features selection led to 7 and 11 variables selected for LB and Rescore + models (Table S3), respectively, and 1762 PLEC fingerprints. Regarding UGT2B15, attributes selection led to 8 and 6 variables selected for LB and Rescore + models (Table S3), respectively, and 1413 PLEC fingerprints. All models were trained by means of RF algorithm using the Random Forest Learner node implemented in KNIME by applying the default settings as parameters’ tuning did not afford classifiers with better performances. All generated models were subjected to an internal validation by performing ten-fold cross-validation on the training set and further evaluated on the hold-out set.

The following statistical parameters were employed to estimate the models performances: Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), area under the curve of receiver operating curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), sensitivity and specificity.

Structural similarity analysis

The relationship between structural similarity and models’ accuracy was evaluated as follow. The Tanimoto similarity (TS) between each compound of the test set and the first near neighbor (1-NN) within the training set was calculated basing on ECFP4 fingerprints, as computed by CDK, [43] and the obtained values were binned according to the TS. The accuracy value within each bin was calculated and plotted against these bins to analyze the correlation between structural similarity and models performances.

Results and discussion

Structural insights about cofactor and substrate recognition by UGT2B7 and UGT2B15

Despite the catalytic process of UGTs have been described in many reports [21, 44–46], the binding mechanism governing the interplay between enzyme, cofactor and substrate is still unclear. To better understand how the three molecular entities cooperate, the binding order of cofactor and substrate to UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 was investigated through a multistep procedure including MD simulations and docking. To this aim, the respective 3D models retrieved from AlphaFold repository were used. (R)-denopamine (DNP) and the (S)-4-hydroxyphenytoin (p-HPPH) were chosen as representative substrates of UGT2B7 and UGT2B15, respectively [17].

In the first step of our computational analysis, two binary complexes including (i) enzyme–substrate (E-S) and (ii) enzyme-UDPGA (E-C) were generated as detailed under Material and Methods section, and underwent two replica 250 ns MD simulations to allow the enzyme’s active pocket to adapt to the positioned substrate or cofactor. RMSD analysis was performed to check the stability of the complexes throughout the trajectories as detailed in Supplementary Information (Figure S1). Cluster analysis allowed the identification of the most persistent conformations of each trajectory (Figure S2) that were subsequently used to dock the substrate and the cofactor into the E-C and E-S complexes, respectively. As a result, two ternary complexes with different coordinates were obtained for (i) the systems in which the cofactor is the first to bind to the enzyme (EC + S) and (ii) those in which is the substrate the first to access the binding site (ES + C). The so generated ternary complexes were subjected to 500 ns MD simulations, enabling the mutual adaptability between enzyme, cofactor and substrate. Also in this case, the stability of the simulated systems was monitored through RMSD analysis as shown in Figures S3 and S4. Regarding UGT2B7 isoform, in the EC + S complexes, DNP assumed a stable behavior in both simulations. Instead, the cofactor exhibited a stable RMSD profile in the first run (Figure S3B), while showing some fluctuations in the second MD (Figure S3B). In the ES + C complexes, the cofactor displayed a higher mobility adopting RMSD values of ~ 2.60 Å in the first run (Figure S3C), while assuming a quite unstable behavior in the second MD (Figure S3D). A more stable profile was observed for the substrate in the first MD trajectory (Figure S3C), while in the second run an increase of DNP RMSD was detected at about 200 ns after which it remained stable for the rest of the simulation time. UGT2B7 showed quite stable RMSD profiles in all ternary complexes, exhibiting only some small fluctuations throughout the trajectories. Similarly, also in the ternary adducts involving UGT2B15, the enzyme displayed a quite stable behavior in all simulated systems (Figure S4) as well as the cofactor except for the second MD run involving the EC + S complex in which an increment of the RMSD values was detected at about 100 ns (Figure S4B). Overall, p-HPPH showed quite stable RSMD profiles in all simulated systems (Figure S4). Also in this case, cluster analysis was carried out to identify the most persistent conformation occurring during the simulations. Regarding UGT2B7, in the EC + S complexes (Fig. 1A and 1B), UDPGA maintained in both simulations the same orientation observed in the binary complexes, in agreement with literature data [21, 47]. Instead, two different binding modes were adopted by DNP within the catalytic pocket in the two simulations. Nonetheless, a similar interaction pattern could be identified. Indeed, in both binding conformations, the 3,4-dimethoxyphenyl ring occupies a sub-pocket lined by F396, F119, M116 and F174 eliciting hydrophobic contacts with the surrounding residues and pi-stacking interactions with F119. The phenol moiety is involved in hydrophobic contacts with I153 in both trajectories but, while in the first MD run the hydroxyl group of the phenol ring forms a H-bond with the glucuronic acid of UDPGA, in the second run it establishes the same interaction with D151. Finally, the secondary alcohol elicits a H-bond with D151 in the first MD run (Fig. 1A) and with the glucuronic acid of the cofactor in the second MD run (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, the obtained binding modes corroborate experimental data according to which in humans the glucuronidation of DNP by UGT2B7 occurs on the secondary alcohol [48]. Indeed, in both proposed binding modes the secondary alcohol is located in proximity of the catalytic H35 implicated in the deprotonation of the substrate. Moreover, in the most persistent conformer of the second MD run, the reactive site approaches the sugar anomeric carbon which undergoes the nucleophilic attack by the substrate (Fig. 1B).

Most persistent conformations obtained from the MD simulations of the ternary complexes: A UGT2B7-UDPGA + DNP (MD run 1), B UGT2B7-UDPGA + DNP (MD run 2), C UGT2B7-DNP + UDPGA (MD run 1) and D UGT2B7-DNP + UDPGA (MD run 2)

The situation dramatically changes in the ES + C adducts (Fig. 1C, D). In both trajectories, the cofactor is unable to assume the binding mode described above for this class of enzymes, especially in the second simulation (Fig. 1D) in which it is oriented far from the substrate.

Similarly to UGT2B7, also in the EC + S adducts involving the UGT2B15 isoform the cofactor retained the same position observed in the respective binary complexes. Two different binding modes were observed for p-HPPH. In the first MD run (Fig. 2A), the substrate conveniently orients the phenol ring towards the catalytic H35 and elicits hydrophobic contacts with F397 and L396, while the phenyl moiety is engaged in hydrophobic and pi-stacking interactions with I314 and H375, respectively. Instead, the hydantoin ring is implicated in a network of H-bonds involving the backbone of M313 with the phosphate group and the glucuronic acid moiety of the cofactor. In the second trajectory (Fig. 2B), the phenol ring establishes pi-stacking interactions with Y33 and F397, while the phenyl moiety is inserted into a sub-pocket lined by M313, L314, L90, L93 and L94, eliciting hydrophobic contacts with the side chains of these residues. The hydantoin ring is involved in H-bonds with M313 and the sugar moiety of UDPGA. Regarding the ES + C complexes (Fig. 2C, D), in none of the simulations the cofactor was able to accommodate within the enzyme active pocket assuming a pose that is in agreement with literature data [21, 47].

Altogether the outcomes obtained from the MD study performed on both UGT isoforms suggested that the cofactor is the first entity to access the catalytic pocket. This hypothesis was further supported by binding free energy calculations executed on all ternary complexes. Basing on the above reported results, the representative structures of the EC + S complexes obtained from cluster analysis were exploited for docking simulations to develop structure-based predictive models for UGT2B7- and UGT2B15-mediated metabolism. For this purpose, based on the orientation of the substrate and the residues within the catalytic pocket, we selected the representative conformation obtained from the second MD run in the case of UGT2B7 and the structure derived from the first MD simulation in case of UGT2B15.

Chemical space analysis

PCA analysis was carried out to characterize the chemical space of the dataset used in this study basing on a set of 26 physicochemical descriptors. As displayed in Fig. 3, no substantial differentiation between positive and negative class can be detected. This result was quite expected as the non-substrate set includes molecules bearing the same functional groups of substrates thus sharing similar physicochemical properties. Similarly, there is no difference between molecules belonging to training and test set thus underlining the reliability of the latter for validation purposes.

Classification models for the prediction of UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 mediated metabolism

LB and SB classification models were built by means of RF algorithm. In more details, for each UGT isoform one LB classifier was trained on a set of physicochemical and constitutional descriptors, while two SB models were built on (i) a set of scoring functions obtained from docking and rescoring calculations (Rescore +) and (ii) on the PLEC fingerprints. The use of both scoring functions and PLEC fingerprints as SB attributes allowed us to take into account the two crucial information derived from docking, namely binding affinity estimation and protein–ligand interaction prediction. It is worth mentioning that some of the LB descriptors used for model training rely on the 3D structure of the molecules; for this reason, these attributes were computed using the conformations obtained from docking simulations. This implies that two LB models specific for the two UGT isoforms were here developed. Moreover, for each LB and SB feature the mean value over all the generated poses was computed and used to train the respective models according to the property and binding space concepts developed by some of us [39–41].

It is important to emphasize that in our initial dataset the non substrates were filtered to comprise only compounds bearing the functional groups that can be subjected to glucuronidation to avoid structural biases that can affect models’ performances. This makes the discrimination between the two classes more challenging but less biased especially for the LB models. Nonetheless, our models displayed good capabilities to discern between positive and negative classes, as reported in Table 2 which summarizes the results obtained from the ten-fold cross-validation performed on the entire training set. The LB models exhibited satisfactory performances for both UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 isoforms with MCC values in both cases of 0.52 and AUC values of 0.87 for UGT2B7 and 0.81 for UGT2B15. The SB classifiers also showed a good ability in discriminating between substrates and non substrates with MCC values ranging from 0.41 to 0.49. Overall, each model displayed similar capabilities in correctly classifying the two classes, as expressed by similar sensitivity and specificity values, except for the PLEC classifier trained on UGT2B7 for which a lower sensitivity value (0.69) was observed in respect to its specificity (0.78).

To better rationalize the obtained results, the PCA analysis, performed as previously described, was mapped according to the predictions yielded by the different methods on both UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 as shown in Figures S5 and S6, respectively. The results revealed that both LB and SB models are able to cover all analyzed chemical space without the formation of well-defined clusters of molecules correctly predicted by a specific approach.

Figure S7 reports the Venn diagram displaying the number of training and test set molecules correctly predicted by each approach. Concerning UGT2B7, 306 out of 587 molecules were properly classified by all models in cross-validation (Figure S7A). Overall, the LB model was able to correctly predict about 76% of the instances, while both SB classifiers correctly recognized about 73% of the molecules. When validated on the test set, the LB classifier rightly predicted about 76% of the molecules followed by the Rescore + and PLEC models which correctly classified about 72% and 71% of the instances, respectively (Figure S7C). In this case, 67 out of 147 compounds were properly classified by all models. Regarding UGT2B15, in cross-validation the LB model correctly predicted 76% of the training molecules, while and PLEC the Rescore + models were able to rightly classify about 74% and 71% of the instances, respectively. In this case, 284 out of 587 compounds were correctly predicted by all methods. In the external validation, 68 molecules out of 147 were rightly classified by all models, with both LB and PLEC models correctly predicting about 72% of the instances and the Rescore + model properly classifying about 67% of the molecules. Overall, this analysis revealed that all models provided the same predictions for about 50% of the instances and about 30% of compounds were correctly classified by two models out of three. In light of this, to enhance the prediction accuracy of the single classifiers, a consensus strategy was applied by combining the predictions yielded by each model. In more details, a given molecule was classified as substrate if at least two models out of three predicted it as substrate. As shown in Table 4, the results revealed an increment of the performances in comparison with the single models in both cross- and external validation for both UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 isoforms. The improvement obtained through the combination of LB and SB approaches suggested that the applied methodologies provide complementary information whose integration leads to better predictions with respect to the single models.

Structural similarity analysis and applicability domain evaluation

To analyze the applicability domain (AD) of our models, the similarity between training and test molecules was evaluated basing on ECFP4 fingerprints and computing the TS of the 1-NN. As displayed in Fig. 4, a common trend was observed for the LB models in the two studied UGT isoforms, with the models accuracy strongly correlating with the TS. Concerning the docking-based models, both classifiers also showed a positive correlation with the TS but lower if compared to the LB models. In addition, for the SB classifiers we could not identify a common trend between the two isoforms; indeed, in UGT2B7 the PLEC model displayed the worst correlation with the TS (Fig. 4A), while in the case of UGT2B15 the worst correlation was observed for the Rescore + model (Fig. 4B). Overall, these results are in good agreement with our previous work [49] showing that the AD of the docking-based models is less restricted if compared to the LB models as the performances are less dependent on the structural similarity with the molecules of the training set.

Relationship between the 1-NN TS respect to the training set and the accuracy values for UGT2B7 (A) and UGT2B15 (B) predictive models

Binding modes of UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 substrates

UGT2B7 catalyzes the glucuronidation of a variety of xenobiotics including various therapeutic agents. As an example, the top-ranked docking poses of two drugs metabolized by UGT2B7, gemfibrozil and chloramphenicol, are reported in Fig. 5. Gemfibrozil is a fibrate hypolipidemic agent which undergoes glucuronidation reaction on its carboxylic group [50, 51]. It was included in our test set and was correctly predicted by all developed models. According to the docking outcomes (Fig. 5A), gemfibrozil might bind UGT2B7 active site with the carboxylic group placed in proximity of the catalytic H35 and the sugar moiety of UDPGA. Instead, the 2,5-dimethylphenyl ring and the carbon linker are stabilized by hydrophobic contacts involving M312, F396, A397, Y33 and I153.

Top-ranked docking pose of A Gemfibrozil and B Chloramphenicol within UGT2B7 active site, and C (S)-oxazepam and D (R)-Naringenin within UGT2B15 catalytic pocket

Chloramphenicol is a broad spectrum antibiotic primarily eliminated through glucuronidation which occurs at its primary alcohol yielding the 3-O-glucuronide, which represents the major glucuronide, and at its secondary alcohol to give the 1-O-glucuronide [52, 53]. Chloramphenicol was included in our training set and was classified as substrate by the LB and Rescore + models in ten-fold cross-validation. Docking studies revealed that chloramphenicol might establish a network of hydrogen bonds within UGT2B7 active site involving N378, Y33, Q399, M312 and the sugar moiety of UDPGA (Fig. 5B). In addition, the aromatic ring might elicit hydrophobic contact with M312. Interestingly, in the top-scored docking pose the primary alcohol is placed close to H35 and the glucuronic acid portion of the cofactor in good agreement with the experimental studies reporting that the 3-O-glucuronide is the major metabolite of chloramphenicol [53].

UGT2B15 is implicated in the clearance of endogenous compounds, natural substances such as flavonoids, and several important drugs like benzodiazepines [17]. In Fig. 5C, the top-scored docking poses of (S)-oxazepam and (R)-naringenin, which are metabolized by UGT2B15, are shown. Both compounds were included in our training set and predicted as substrate by all models in ten-fold cross-validation. (R,S)-oxazepam is a benzodiazepine anxiolytic drug primarily eliminated by glucuronidation. Experimental studies pointed out that the conjugation of oxazepam catalyzed by UGTs is stereoselective, with (S)-oxazepam selectively glucuronidated by UGT2B15, while R-oxazepam is glucuronidated by multiple UGT isoforms [54]. In the predicted binding mode, the hydroxyl group of (S)-oxazepam, which undergoes glucuronidation, is oriented towards the catalytic H35 while the aromatic rings are engaged in hydrophobic contacts with I227, V177, F175, L154, A153 and L173. (R)-naringenin is a flavanone contained in Citrus fruits endowed with multiple pharmacological properties such as antiviral, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [55, 56]. In the top ranked docking solution (Fig. 5D), (R)-naringenin is involved in H-bonds with Y121, F175 and the sugar moiety of the cofactor. Moreover, the phenol ring elicits pi-stacking interactions with Y120. Remarkably, the hydroxyl group at position 7 is located nearby the sugar moiety of UDPGA and H35 in line with the experimental data regarding the conjugation of naringenin by UGTs which can occur at the hydroxyl groups at positions 7 and 4’ [57].

The above reported examples revealed that docking simulations using AlphaFold structures can provide poses consistent with literature data in which the reactive atom is placed in proximity of the catalytic histidine and the cofactor. Therefore, if a given compound has been predicted as substrate by the machine learning model, the analysis of docking poses could be useful to get more insights about the structural motifs that are most likely to undergo glucuronidation thus confirming that docking simulations can have a predictive role in elucidating the sites of metabolism.

Conclusions

In this study, LB and SB methods were successfully integrated to predict the occurrence of glucuronidation reaction by UGT2B7 and UGT2B15.

SB approaches were first exploited to investigate the binding interplay between enzyme, UDPGA and substrate within the catalytic pocket of the two analyzed UGT isoforms. The outcomes pointed out that the cofactor is the first to access the binding site as suggested (i) by the binding mode assumed by UDPGA in absence of the substrate, which is similar to that observed in plant glucosyltransferases, and (ii) by binding free energy calculations which provided lower ΔG values in the ternary complexes in which the cofactor is the first to access the active site. Through cluster analysis a representative conformation of UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 was extracted to develop SB classification models. To this aim, metabolic data concerning the glucuronidation reaction were retrieved from the MetaQSAR database and used to train both LB and SB models. LB classifiers were built on a set of constitutional and physicochemical descriptors as computed on the conformations obtained from docking. Concerning the SB classifiers, two models were developed. The first one was trained on a set of scoring functions computed by docking and rescoring procedures (as carried out using Rescore +), while the second one was built on the PLEC fingerprints. The best performances were achieved by the LB models in both ten-fold cross-validation and when validated on the test set for both UGT2B7 (MCCcross = 0.52, MCCtest = 0.52) and UGT2B15 (MCCcross = 0.52, MCCtest = 0.45). Remarkably, except for the Rescore + model of UGT2B15 which moderately performed on the test set (MCC = 0.33), the SB models showed a good ability in discerning between the two classes yielding in some cases performances comparable to that of the corresponding LB model as shown by the PLEC model of UGT2B15 (MCCcross = 0.49, MCCtest = 0.43). To improve the prediction accuracy, a consensus approach was used basing on the predictions yielded by the single models. The results revealed an improvement of the performances in respect with the individual models highlighting that the employed LB and SB descriptors provide complementary information, and their integration allows the development of more accurate predictors in comparison to the single models. The analysis of the applicability domain by structural similarity revealed that the AD domain of the SB models is less restricted as less dependent on the structural similarity with the training molecules if compared to the LB classifiers. In addition, once that a given molecule has been predicted as substrate, the analysis of docking poses could furnish useful hints about the reactive site as exemplified by gemfibrozil and chloramphenicol for UGT2B7 and S-oxazepam and (R)-naringenin for UGT2B15, whose docking poses are consistent with literature data. However, some aspects must be considered. The first one concerns the definition of the negative class as the non-substrates include compounds that were not reported as substrates without any confirming experiments, introducing some noise in the training set. Furthermore, in this study the non-substrates bear the chemical functionalities susceptible to glucuronidation making the discrimination between positive and negative class more challenging but less biased especially for the LB models. Another aspect to consider is that induced-fit effects were neglected in the development of the SB models as the protein was kept rigid during the docking calculations of our dataset compounds. Accounting for protein flexibility could be helpful to further improve the accuracy of the SB models.

In conclusion, this study provides a new computational strategy to incorporate SB information with LB properties in metabolism prediction enhancing the performances of the individual approaches. Furthermore, the outcomes of this work confirmed the reliability of AlphaFold structures to develop predictive SB models for metabolism assessment.
