Breast milk antibody Fc signatures track with HIV transmission during breastfeeding in ART-naïve mothers
Authors: Nadege Nziza, Wonyeong Jung, Yunjoo Lee, Jeff C Hsiao, Tina Chen, Fan Li, Caroline Atyeo, Douglas A Lauffenburger, Louise Kuhn, Nicole H Tobin, Grace M Aldrovandi, Galit Alter, Boris Julg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBreastfeeding shapes early life immunity, but without antiretroviral therapy (ART), it poses a risk of HIV transmission. The contribution of breast milk antibodies to this risk remains unclear. Using systems serology, we profiled milk antibodies from transmitting and non-transmitting mothers in the ART naive Zambia Exclusive Breastfeeding Study. Transmission was associated with elevated gp41-specific IgG1 and enhanced effector functions—including complement deposition and neutrophil phagocytosis—likely driven by higher milk viral loads. In contrast, non-transmitting mothers exhibited functional p24-specific antibody responses that correlated with lower milk viral loads, suggesting improved viral control. Fc glycosylation analysis showed that transmitting mothers had higher levels of digalactosylated IgG, a profile linked to increased neutrophil inflammatory cytokine release. Overall, these data indicate that milk antibodies have multifaceted roles in postnatal HIV transmission and identify immune signatures associated with increased risk or protection, providing mechanistic insights relevant for vaccine development and Fc-engineered antibody-based prevention strategies.Subject areas: immunity, virology

Introduction

In historical antiretroviral therapy (ART)-naïve cohorts, postnatal HIV transmission via breastfeeding occurred at roughly 1% per month, with cumulative risks of 10–20% over 12–18 months.1,2,3 These estimates mainly reflect chronic maternal infection; when mothers acquire HIV during pregnancy or lactation, postpartum transmission risk is several-fold higher, with pooled rates approaching ∼25% in historical cohorts.4 Even modest month-to-month risk in chronic infection makes breastfeeding a major contributor to pediatric HIV in high-incidence settings, and its relative contribution has grown as in utero and intrapartum infections have declined with widespread ART.5,6

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, followed by continued breastfeeding with complementary foods, is still recommended in resource-limited settings when effective maternal or infant ART prophylaxis is in place, as the survival benefits outweigh residual transmission risk.7,8,9 Understanding the biological drivers of both transmission and protection in the breastfeeding dyad is critical for optimizing interventions for women with chronic versus incident infection.6,10

Mechanistic studies indicate that both cell-free HIV and infected leukocytes in breast milk can cross mammary and infant mucosal barriers via endocytosis or transcytosis, with local inflammation or epithelial disruption enhancing viral passage.11,12 Maternal plasma and breast milk viral loads, low CD4+ counts, and advanced disease are strong predictors of transmission, while clinical and subclinical mastitis further increase viral shedding and postnatal infection risk.13,14,15,16,17

HIV-specific IgG and IgA from breast milk can inhibit viral transcytosis in vitro and ex vivo,18 suggesting a protective role; however, cohort studies show inconsistent associations between breast milk antibodies19,20,21—including ADCC, envelope-specific IgA, and sIgA—and transmission (multiple cohort studies). HIV also remodels IgG Fc glycosylation,22 affecting Fc-mediated effector functions, including ADCC, which may contribute to viral control. The role of Fc profiles and other antibody-dependent functions in postnatal transmission remains to be clarified. This is particularly relevant given recent advances in the evaluation of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) for perinatal and postnatal prophylaxis23,24,25 (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT02256631, NCT06517693). For example, subcutaneous administration of bNAbs with enhanced breadth, potency, ADCC, and phagocytosis has been suggested to eliminate HIV transmission through breastfeeding,26 suggesting Fc-mediated effector functions as potentially relevant mechanisms worth investigating in the role of antibodies during HIV transmission.

Here, we comprehensively analyzed the breastmilk humoral immune profile, including antibody titers, Fc receptor binding, Fc-mediated effector functions such as antibody-mediated complement deposition (ADCD), antibody-mediated cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and antibody-mediated neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP), and Fc glycosylation in mothers who did nor did not transmit HIV to their infants in the Zambia Exclusive Breastfeeding Study (ZEBS).27 This historical cohort, recruited prior to the routine use of ART, received a single dose of nevirapine, per local standard of care, thus enabling us to investigate the interplay between breast milk virus and anti-viral immunity during transmission.

Results

Since infants can acquire HIV in utero, during delivery, or through breastfeeding, we analyzed two sub-cohorts from the ZEBS study.27 The postpartum transmission cohort (n = 34; 17 transmitters and 17 non-transmitters (NTs)) included mothers whose infants were HIV PCR-negative at 1 week and 1 month but later became infected through breastfeeding. The early mucosal transmission cohort (n = 66; 22 transmitters and 44 NTs), included infants PCR-negative at 1 week but PCR-positive at 1 month (Figure S1A). Hereafter, transmitting and non-transmitting mothers are referred to as T and NT, respectively. Breast milk samples were collected at 1 week, 1 month, 4 months, and 4.5 months postpartum (Figure 1A). Transmission timing was defined by the earliest positive infant PCR, with a median of 3 months in the postpartum cohort. Because the exact route of infection cannot be determined for infants positive by 1-month, primary analyses focused on the postpartum cohort, with the early mucosal cohort used for exploratory confirmation. Cohort characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2. As breastfeeding cessation alters milk antibody profiles28,29,30 and was associated with elevated titers after weaning in our cohort (Figure S1B), mothers who stopped breastfeeding before 4.5 months postpartum were excluded.

We utilized systems serology to comprehensively assess the breast milk humoral profiles of the cohort. HIV-specific antibody types (total IgG, IgG1-3, IgM, IgA1, IgA2, sIgA), Fc receptor binding profile (Fcγ2A, Fcγ2B, Fcγ3A, Fcγ3B, Fc⍺R, and FcRn), and Fc-effector functions, including ADCD, ADCP, and antibody-mediated neutrophil-phagocytosis (ADNP) were measured. For the early mucosal transmission cohort, we additionally measured antibody-mediated natural killer cell (NK cell) activation (ADNKA) by quantifying degranulation marker (CD107a) expression and macrophage inflammatory protein 1 beta (MIP-1β) release.

Postpartum differences in milk viral load and gp41-targeting antibody responses between transmitters and non-transmitters

It is well established that breast milk viral load is associated with transmission.31,32,33 Consistently, in this cohort, we observed that milk viral load was similar between groups at earlier timepoints but was significantly lower in NT compared to T by Month 4, the timepoint at which 14 out of 17 transmissions occurred (Figure 1B). To characterize HIV-specific antibody responses in breast milk, we selected gp120 and gp41 because they represent two major and functionally distinct regions of the HIV-1 envelope, mediating receptor binding (gp120) and membrane fusion (gp41), and we included p24 as an internal capsid antigen to capture responses against non-envelope structural proteins. At month 4, the transmitting mothers showed significantly higher gp41-targeting IgG1 in breastmilk compared to the non-transmitting mothers, primarily driven by the decline in gp41-targeting IgG1 among the non-transmitting group; no such differences were observed for gp120-or p24-specific IgG1 (Figure 1C). At earlier timepoints, there were no significant differences between NT and T (Figure S2A). Additionally, gp41-specific ADCD and ADNP were significantly higher in T at month 4 (Figure 1D).

Postpartum transmitters have more potent functional antibodies

We next asked which antibodies drive effector functions by assessing the correlation between gp41-specific antibodies and effector functions (Figure 2A). In both NT and T, IgG1 was positively correlated with ADCD and ADNP, whereas IgA1 was either uncorrelated or negatively correlated with ADCD and ADNP. Thus, it is possible that IgG1 drives the effector functions in T. To further assess the time-dependent differences in effector functions between NT and T groups (Figure 1D), we plotted the temporal dynamics of antigen-specific IgG1 and associated effector functions and compared the area under the curve (AUC) between the two groups (Figure 2B). Only gp41-specific IgG1 levels, ADCD, and ADNP were significantly different between NT and T. While these features showed a decreasing trend in NT over time, they remained stable in T. Thus, we next asked whether qualitative differences in the measured IgG1 contributed to the differences in effector functions between NT and T over time. To address this, we normalized effector functions by antigen-specific IgG1 levels and compared the results between the NT and T groups. For gp120 and p24, there were no significant differences in normalized functions (Figure 2C). For gp41, significant differences in functions were observed at Month 4, at which transmitting mothers showed significantly higher gp41-targeting ADNP and ADCD.

p24-specific responses are associated with lower viral load in non-transmitting mothers

IgA is the most abundant antibody in breast milk and the main isotype that mediates protection on the mucosal surface.34 A previous study showed that envelope protein-specific IgA and sIgA are associated with reduced transmission.21 Thus, we next compared antigen-specific IgA in NT and T. There were no significant differences in IgA1 and IgA2 responses for all antigens (Figures 3A and S2B), while gp41-specific sIgA was higher in T (Figure S2C). To further dissect protective antibody features, we evaluated the correlation between breast milk viral load and antibody features. In transmitting mothers, the viral load in the breast milk and antibody features did not show a strong association except for a few positive correlations (Figure 3B). However, in NT, positive associations between gp120, g140, gp41, and gag-specific IgA2 and sIgA were observed at Month 1. Additionally, negative associations between viral load and p24-specific features, including IgG1, FcR binding, and antibody-mediated functions such as ADCD and ADNP, were observed at Month 4. At this time point, p24-specific IgA1 and IgG1 were positively correlated only in NT (Figure S3).

Early mucosal transmitters exhibit elevated functional antibody profiles

To better understand the generalizability of these findings and determine whether similar immune mechanisms operate across different modes and timing of transmission, we extended our analysis to the early mucosal transmission cohort (Figure 4A). While it is unclear if the HIV transmission occurred at the mucosal surface during birth or during breastfeeding, this subcohort offers an opportunity to evaluate whether the functional antibody profiles observed in postpartum transmission also emerge in earlier transmission events. Similar to the postpartum transmission cohort, the breast milk viral load was higher in transmitting mothers, specifically at the timepoint at which transmission happened (i.e., month 1) (Figure 4B). Overall, the difference in antibody titers between NT and T was not statistically significant at week 1 and month 1 (Figures 4C and 4D). Since there were no specific antigens that were preferentially targeted in either of the NT or T groups in this cohort, we selected gp120 as the target antigen for functional profiling due to its prominent role in mucosal HIV entry and its accessibility to antibody recognition during early transmission events. While no differences were observed at Week 1, functional responses, including ADNP, ADCP, ADNKA (CD107a), and ADNKA (MIP1b), were significantly higher in Ts at Month 1 (Figure 4E).

To enable a more detailed comparison between the two subcohorts, we constructed pooled multivariable models with transmission status as the outcome. NTs from both subcohorts were combined to generate a shared reference group. We then applied multinomial logistic regression comparing NTs from both subcohorts with transmitters (T) from each subcohort (Figure S4; see STAR Methods). Given the observed differences in baseline CD4 T cell counts between NTs from the two subcohorts (Figure S4A), as well as the previously noted differences in breast milk viral load distribution, both baseline CD4 count and breast milk viral load were included as covariates in the models. The two subcohorts exhibited distinct patterns of feature associations with transmission, suggesting that underlying biological differences between the subcohorts may contribute to the limited overlap in features associated with transmission and non-transmission (Figures S4B and S4C).

Early mucosal transmitters possess digalactosylated, neutrophil-activating IgGs

In both sub-cohorts, we observed higher antibody functionality among T. Thus, we next sought to identify which antibody drives the observed effector functions mechanistically. For this analysis, we focused on the early mucosal transmission group, for which we had sufficient numbers of samples and month 1 data, the time point at which transmission occurred for this sub-cohort. To further understand the antibody characteristics that drive the effector functions, specifically ADNP, which showed differences between NT and T in both sub-cohorts, we first depleted IgG and IgA from the breast milk in the early mucosal transmission group and measured ADNP driven by the breast milk (Figures 5A and 5B). In both NT and T, gp120-specific ADNP was affected by IgA and IgG levels, but only IgG depletion resulted in statistically significant differences, demonstrating that IgG drives ADNP, which is in line with findings from correlation analysis for the postpartum transmission cohort (Figure 2A). Additionally, we measured the effects of IgG and IgA depletion on other effector functions, including proinflammatory chemokine (IL-8, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5) and enzyme (myeloperoxidase; MPO, matrix metalloproteinase-9; MMP-9) release from neutrophils (Figure 5C). In NT mothers, neither IgG nor IgA depletion had a significant effect, suggesting that either IgG or IgA can drive chemokine and enzyme release in neutrophils. In contrast, T mothers showed a significant reduction in chemokine release following IgG depletion but not IgA depletion. These findings suggest that in T mothers, breast milk IgGs mediate neutrophil-driven chemokine release. Since antibody functionality has been shown to be driven by antibody Fc glycosylation,35,36 we next compared the glycosylation profile of IgG Fcs in both groups (Figure 5D). Compared to T, NT had higher levels of agalactosylated Fc (Figure 5D, lower left panel), whereas T had higher levels of digalactosylated Fc (Figure 5D, lower right panel), which were shown to enhance ADCC and ADNK in previous studies.36,37

Discussion

The rationale of this study was to delineate breast milk humoral immune signatures associated with infant HIV transmission risk in breastfeeding mothers not receiving ART. Using systems serology, we evaluated antibody quantity, quality, effector function, and Fc glycosylation to define antibody features associated with higher or lower transmission risk in the context of variable milk viral load. Our analyses revealed distinct temporal and functional antibody patterns that were associated with viral load and transmission status, offering novel insights into how breast milk antibodies might influence or reflect HIV transmission dynamics.

In the postpartum transmission cohort, transmitting mothers exhibited higher gp41-specific IgG1 and more sustained gp41-linked ADCD and ADNP compared to NTs. These responses persisted in transmitters but declined in NTs, reflecting ongoing antigenic stimulation from higher milk viral loads. Correlation analyses indicated that gp41-specific IgG1 was the primary driver of these effector functions, whereas IgA1 showed no similar associations and, in some cases, was negatively correlated. To compare qualitative differences, we examined functional activity in relation to antigen-specific IgG1 levels; features linked to gp41 showed greater functional efficiency in transmitters at the key transmission timepoints. Taken together, these observations suggest that both antibody magnitude and quality align with periods of high milk viral load in transmitters. While functional antibody levels were also higher in transmitters in the early mucosal transmission group, the overall patterns of feature associations with transmission differed across subcohorts. These findings suggest that antigen specificity and the timing of exposure influence which antibody features are most discriminating, with associations observed later in the postpartum period being attenuated or shifted in the earlier exposure window.

Whether the identified functional antibody responses are protective or pathogenic remains unclear. Antibodies may serve dual roles: promoting immune clearance on one hand, while fueling inflammation and tissue damage on the other. Our depletion experiments in the early mucosal transmission cohort demonstrated that IgG, but not IgA, was responsible for driving neutrophil activation and release of proinflammatory mediators, including IL-8, CCL3, CCL4, MPO, and MMP-9, with IgG depletion significantly reducing these responses only in transmitting mothers. Inflammatory chemokines have previously been linked to viral shedding and epithelial barrier disruption in the context of mastitis, raising the possibility that elevated antibody function under some conditions could facilitate, rather than prevent, viral transmission by exacerbating mucosal inflammation.

The underlying mechanism of transmission may involve differences in antibody Fc glycosylation. We observed that transmitting mothers had higher levels of digalactosylated Fc structures, a glycoform previously associated with increased NK cell activity.36,37 Differences in Fc glycosylation, therefore, provide a plausible biochemical basis for the heightened effector potential observed in transmitters, although the same properties could also amplify inflammatory signaling at the mucosal surface.36,37

Notably, the role of IgA remains controversial. Mucosal and plasma IgA from highly exposed, persistently seronegative individuals have been shown to inhibit HIV-1 transcytosis across epithelial barriers.38 Previous studies have reported conflicting results on the association of breastmilk IgA and transmission: Pollara et al.21 found enrichment of IgA and sIgA in NTs, whereas Becquart et al.18 and Mabuka et al.19 found no association. In our dataset, gp41-specific secretory IgA was elevated in transmitters. Similar null results have been reported in ART-treated individuals in the IMPAACT PROMISE trial.39 In the ZEBS cohort, Kuhn et al.20 previously reported no association between HIV-1 envelope-specific IgA/sIgA and transmission. By profiling antigen-specific antibodies in ZEBS, we found that gp41-specific sIgA was elevated in transmitting mothers, indicating that IgA responses are not uniformly protective and may, in some contexts, track with higher viral burden or local inflammation.

The discrepancies observed between cohorts may be attributable to differences in maternal viral loads, CD4+ counts, viral clades, ART exposure, sample size, antigen specificity profiled, effector readouts used, and sampling schedules— all of which likely influence both antibody responses and transmission outcomes. Notably, the dynamic nature of virus-antibody interactions suggests that cross-sectional antibody profiling may fail to capture the temporal nuances essential for identifying correlates of protection. These results underscore that longitudinal profiling is essential, since cross-sectional snapshots may miss critical temporal inflection points.

In our study, p24-specific IgG1 levels and associated effector functions were negatively correlated with viral load exclusively in NTs. Because p24 is an internal capsid protein, these antibodies are unlikely to mediate direct viral neutralization. Notably, we observed a correlation between p24-specific IgA1 and IgG1 responses, suggesting that p24-specific IgA1 may act as a surrogate marker for p24-specific IgG1 activity, which has previously been linked to viral control in HIV subtype C.40 However, without contemporaneous cellular immune measurements, the mechanistic role of p24-specific antibodies remains unresolved. Future studies integrating antibody and cellular immune profiling will be required to determine whether these responses reflect or contribute to T cell- or NK cell-mediated viral control.

Finally, the similarities and differences between the postpartum and early mucosal transmission cohorts further support the hypothesis that viral replication drives functional antibody responses in breast milk. Both sub-cohorts showed higher viral loads and elevated antibody functions in transmitters, whereas the specific antigen associations varied by window, with gp41-linked features distinguishing transmitters later postpartum and gp120-linked functions being more prominent earlier. These cohort-specific patterns may reflect differences in antigen availability, mucosal compartmentalization, feeding dynamics, or inflammation at the time of exposure. Infant factors are also critical: Transplacental IgG sets the neonatal baseline, while milk antibodies, particularly secretory IgA, act at mucosal surfaces. Neonatal effector systems differ from those of adults in cytotoxicity, neutrophil function, complement activity, and Fc receptor expression, and their changing dynamics over the first months of life may influence how milk antibodies translate into protection or inflammation. Incorporating neonatal effector cells into functional antibody assessments might address these limitations, but it is technically challenging given their limited availability.

Our data suggest several prevention strategies: (1) reduce mucosal inflammation via early mastitis treatment and by ensuring maximum maternal viral suppression; (2) tailor maternal therapeutic HIV vaccination to boost neutralizing antibodies and protective Fc profiles in milk while avoiding inflammatory antibody signatures; (3) consider infant or maternal administration of Fc/glycan engineered bNAb to balance protection and inflammation; and (4) for future correlates work in ART-treated cohorts, stratify by transient viremia, breast inflammation, and cell-associated virus in breast milk to evaluate Fc-mediated mechanisms.

With ART, the HIV transmission risk through breastfeeding significantly decreases.41 Because ART both lowers milk viral load and reshapes humoral immunity,42,43,44 our findings would not necessarily apply to an ART-naïve setting. Notably, Hompe et al. showed that maternal humoral immune responses do not predict postnatal transmission risk in an ART-treated cohort (IMPAACT PROMISE trial). As ART implementation is now widespread and transmission rates have substantially decreased, understanding these immune landscapes and the relevant maternal and infant factors is still necessary for developing adjunctive maternal vaccine and antibody approaches for preventing transmission in contemporary settings.

In summary, breast milk antibody profiles in mothers not on ART differed between transmitters and NTs across time, with functional and glycosylation features that co-varied with viral load and transmission status. The integration of maternal and infant factors, together with targeted strategies that shape antibody specificity and Fc biology while minimizing mucosal inflammation, may inform adjunctive vaccine and antibody approaches for preventing postnatal transmission in contemporary settings.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. The modest sample size reduces statistical power and generalizability, limiting further stratification of NTs and exploration of interactions between viral load, antibody features, and transmission timing. Clinical confounders such as subclinical mastitis, which can influence HIV shedding and antibody profiles, were not accounted for, though close matching of transmitters and NTs by demographic and sampling criteria, as well as the availability of the data on maternal viral load and CD4, which are the known risk factors for transmission, helps mitigate this. Our longitudinal analyses are observational and largely correlational, so elevated antibody responses in transmitters may reflect, rather than drive, transmission. Future studies with higher-resolution sampling and concurrent assessment of breast inflammation and cell-associated virus are needed to clarify causal mechanisms. Additionally, Fc glycosylation and inflammatory chemokine release were not assessed in the postpartum transmission group, nor was the impact of functional antibodies on infant mucosal integrity, which warrants further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the underlying study from which the clinical specimens were derived was obtained from the human subjects committees of all participating institutions in Zambia and the United States. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was additionally reviewed by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (#4116).

Study overview and participant samples

Samples were collected from women living with HIV, enrolled in the Zambia Exclusive Breastfeeding Study (ZEBS)27 (NCT00310726). The ZEBS was conducted between 2001 and 2004 among women living with HIV in Lusaka, Zambia, to evaluate the effects of exclusive breastfeeding for 4 months followed by abrupt weaning on postnatal HIV transmission and mortality through the first 2 years of life.27 The analyzed cohort was predominantly of African ancestry. All participants were antiretroviral therapy-naive, except for a single peripartum dose of nevirapine, administered per Zambian government guidelines. Two sub-groups from ZEBS were selected for the current study based on the availability of breast milk samples at 1 week, 1 month, 4 months, and 4.5 months post-partum. The first sub-group comprised n = 17 women who transmitted HIV-1 to their infants through breastfeeding (defined as infant HIV-1 DNA negativity at 1 month of age followed by HIV-1 DNA positivity at a subsequent visit) along with 17 matched controls (ratio 1:1) (‘postpartum transmission’ cohort, total n = 34). The second sub-group comprised n = 22 women who transmitted HIV-1 to their infants during early infancy (defined as infant HIV-1 DNA positivity at 1 month of age) along with 44 matched controls (ratio 1:2) (‘early mucosal transmission’ cohort, total n = 66). Infant HIV-1 DNA positivity was assessed by heel-stick samples that were batch-tested for HIV-1 DNA by PCR in the US, with positive results confirmed on repeat testing and negatives verified by β-globin amplification to exclude inadequate samples.27 Each sub-cohort was matched for age, maternal CD4 count, maternal viral load, child death, and maternal death. Maternal milk samples were prospectively collected throughout lactation as described.45 Because all participants were breastfeeding mothers, there was no variability in biological sex; therefore, sex was not included as a covariate in multivariable analyses. The median transmission time was 3 months for the postpartum transmission cohort. For the early mucosal transmission cohort, since infants tested positive by 1 month, it is not possible to definitively determine whether infection occurred during delivery or through early breastfeeding. We therefore performed our primary analysis for the postpartum transmission cohort since the route of transmission was clearly by breastfeeding and the early mucosal transmission cohort to confirm findings in an exploratory manner. Cohort characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2. There were several cases for which breastmilk viral load was not available, especially at week 1 after birth. Thus, week 1 data was excluded from some of the analyses. The number of available breastmilk viral load data at each timepoint is shown in Table S1.

Method details

Breast milk processing

Breastmilk samples were centrifuged at 500g for 10 min at room temperature to separate the lipid and cellular components.32 The aqueous middle layer (lactoserum), located below the lipid layer and above the cell pellet was collected and used for all antibody measurements and functional assays.

Antigens

Antigens used in this study included recombinant gp120 (ConC, HIV-1 consensus clade C), gp140 (clade C, isolate C.ZA.1197 MB), gp41 (ectodomain, isolate C.ZA.1197 MB), and Gag (clade C, isolate HIV-1 ZM651), all purchased from Immunetech.

Cells

THP-1 cells used in phagocytic assays were grown in RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS, 5% penn/strep, 5% L-glutamine, 5% HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) and 0.5% 2-Mercaptoethanol, and maintained at 2.5 × 105 cells/mL.46

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis was assessed using a flow cytometry-based phagocytic assay.46 Briefly, 1.0 μm, yellow-green fluorescent (505/515) FluoSpheres NeutrAvidin (Thermo Fisher) were coated with biotinylated antigens and incubated with breastmilk samples diluted 1:50. The ability of breastmilk samples to drive uptake of antigen-coated beads by THP-1 cells was assessed by flow cytometry using the IntelliCyt iQue Screener Plus VBR (Sartorius). Phagocytic scores were determined by (%yellow-green+ cells x yellow-green MFI)/100 = % phagocytosed. Samples were run in duplicate. IVIG (Sigma) and 1xPBS alone were used as controls. Additional beads were coated with HA or Ebola protein and run with just control samples, as assay technical positive and negative controls. Data was averaged and reported as area under the curve for the two dilution points.

Antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP)

Antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis was measured by a flow cytometry-based assay.47 Briefly, breastmilk samples were diluted 1: 50 and 1:100 and added to plates at 10 μL/well plus 10 μL/well of 1.0 μm yellow-green fluorescent (505/515 nm) FluoSpheres NeutrAvidin (Thermo Fisher) beads coated with biotinylated antigens incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Neutrophils were obtained from ACD-treated whole blood using ACK Lysing Buffer (GIB) at room temperature (1:10). After lysis, remaining cells were counted and resuspended at 2.5 × 105 cells/mL in complete RPMI media. Cells were then incubated with bead/Ab mixture for 1h at 37°C. Cells were then stained with pacific blue-conjugated anti-CD66b (Biolegend) in PBS. Finally, cells were fixed in 4% PFA and phagocytosis of beads by CD66b+ positive cells was measured by flow cytometry using the IntelliCyt iQue Screener Plus VBR (Sartorius). IVIG (Sigma) and 1xPBS alone were used as negative controls. Samples were run in duplicate, and two biological replicates were run for each, each consisting of two whole blood donors. Additional beads were coated with HA or Ebola protein and run with just control samples, as assay technical positive and negative controls. Data reported is an average of all data points collected. Data was averaged and reported as area under the curve for the two dilution points.

Antibody-dependent NK cell activation (ADNK)

NK cell activation was evaluated based on surface CD107a expression and intracellular levels of IFN-γ and MIP-1β, following a previously established protocol.48 NK cells were isolated from whole blood of healthy donors through negative selection using RosetteSep (STEMCELL Technologies). The isolated NK cells were then exposed to gp120 (60 μg/mL) and co-cultured with T lymphoblast CEM-NKr cells at a 1:5 ratio. Purified IgG, anti-CD107a-PE-Cy5 (BD), brefeldin A (10 μg/mL) (Sigma), and GolgiStop (BD) were added to the culture, followed by a 5-h incubation at 37°C. Following incubation, surface staining was performed using anti-CD16-allophycocyanin (APC)-Cy7 (BD), anti-CD56-PE-Cy7 (BD), and anti-CD3-Alexa Fluor 700 (BD), then stained intracellularly with anti-MIP1b-PE (BD) after treatment with Fix and Perm A and B solutions (Invitrogen). Fixation was carried out with 4% paraformaldehyde, and samples were analyzed via flow cytometry. NK cells were identified as CD3-negative and either CD16-positive and/or CD56-positive. The percentage of NK cells expressing each marker was quantified.

Glycan analysis

A capillary electrophoresis-based method was used to analyze the glycosylation profiles of gp120-specific and total breastmilk IgG, following a previously established protocol.49 Magnetic Streptavidin beads were coated with biotinylated antigens. To obtain an adequate quantity of antigen-specific IgG (≥1 mg), the beads were incubated overnight at 4°C with 300 μL with milk diluted at 1:10. The Fc portion of IgG was then enzymatically cleaved from the antigen-bound Fab fragments using IdeZ protease (NEB). N-linked glycans were enzymatically released from IgG using PNGase F, fluorescently labeled via reductive amination with 8-aminopyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid (APTS), and separated from excess dye. The labeled glycans were then analyzed using an Agilent 3130XL ABI DNA sequencer. Peak identities were determined through exoglycosidase digestion and compared with glycan standards. The relative abundance of each glycan species was analyzed by capillary electrophoresis on a 3500xL Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Glycan structures, including galactosylation (G), sialylation (S), and the presence or absence of core fucose (F) or bisecting GlcNAc (B), were identified by comparing retention times with known glycan standards, utilizing Glycan Acquisition Software Version 3500 v1.0.3 and Glycan Analysis Software v1.1, following previously established protocols.

Neutrophil activation assays

Neutrophils were isolated from ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes of healthy donors using the EasySep Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The isolated neutrophils were stimulated with immune complexes as previously described,50 and supernatants were collected after 4 h. The levels of MPO, lactoferrin, and MMP-9 were determined using ELISA kits (Biolegend and Abcam) while the concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, IL-1R, and GM-CSF in undiluted supernatants were measured using a custom multiplex cytokine Luminex kit (Thermo Fisher).

IgA and IgG depletion

Human breastmilk samples were depleted of IgA using the CaptureSelect IgA Affinity Matrix (Thermo Fisher). The affinity matrix was washed three times with PBS before being incubated overnight with plasma samples diluted 1:5 in a low protein-binding MultiScreen filter plate (Millipore). The depleted breastmilk samples were collected by centrifugation of the filter plate. As a control, non-depleted breastmilk samples underwent the same procedure without the affinity matrix. For IgA isolation, the affinity matrix was washed five times with PBS, and the bound IgA was eluted using a Tris-glycine buffer system. The eluted IgA was then buffer-exchanged into PBS using ZebaSpin desalting plates (Thermo Fisher).

Isotype and subclass assay

Microspheres were incubated with plasma diluted at 1:50, overnight at 4°C, with shaking at 900 RPM. After incubation, samples were washed by magnetic bead separation, and then incubated with IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, or IgM secondary PE-conjugated antibody (Southern Biotech) for 1 h, with shaking at 900 RPM at RT. The beads were then washed and read on the IntelliCyt iQue Screener Plus VBR (Sartorius). Results are reported as the average median fluorescent intensity of the two replicates. Negative controls consisted of Ebola virus specific glycoprotein antigen, pooled serum of intravenous IgG (Sigma), and 1xPBS alone as background controls. Positive controls consisted of pooled influenza HA (Immunetech).

Fc receptor binding assay

Fc receptor (FcR) binding profiles were measured by Luminex.51 Briefly, breastmilk samples were diluted at 1:100 or 1:1000 and incubated with antigen coated as in the subclass/isotype assay. Next, secondary reagents were prepared using the recombinant advi-tagged human FcR protein (provided by the Duke university protein production facility), incubated with PE-conjugated streptavidin at RT in the dark. Excess streptavidin was quenched with biotin (Agilent). Samples were incubated with secondary reagents at RT for 1 h with shaking. Between each incubation, beads were washed with 5%BSA/0.5% tween in PBS and sonicated to resuspend. The IntelliCyt iQue Screener Plus VBR (Sartorius) was used to measure and calculate a Median Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) of PE for each bead type. Results are reported as the average median fluorescent intensity of the two replicates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed with R (version 4.1.0). Antibody titers, and Fc receptor binding data were log-transformed using log10. For multivariate analysis, data were centered and scaled after the log-transformation.

Univariate analysis

Two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to compare each feature from the transmitter and non-transmitter groups at each time point. P-values were multiple test-corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. We used a single p-value threshold (p < 0.05) to simplify the interpretation.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rho was used as a measure of correlation, and p-values were multiple-test corrected for all comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.52 The difference between correlations was calculated by Fisher Z-transformation.53

Logistic regression model

To evaluate the association between T and NT status and antibody features while controlling for breastmilk viral load and baseline CD4 T cell count, we fitted a logistic regression model: Full model T vs. NT ∼ breastmilk viral load + baseline CD4 T cell count + antibody features. The p-value of the coefficient for all antibody features were multiple test-corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.
