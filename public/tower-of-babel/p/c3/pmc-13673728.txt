Early sensory differences in toddlers at elevated likelihood for autism and association with later autistic traits: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Shihuan Wang, Cong You, Leran Xue, Feixia Zhang, Yu Xing, Linru Liu, Yijie Li, Shaoli Lv, Qianying Ye, Yuanyuan Zou, Kaiyun Chen, Hongzhu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a core diagnostic criterion for autism spectrum disorder (ASD), sensory differences may have profound implications for the quality of life among autistic individuals. However, little is known about the developmental trajectories of sensory features in toddlers at elevated likelihood (EL) for ASD. The associations between early sensory patterns and later autistic traits also remains unclear.MethodsA total of 127 toddlers were enrolled in a China cohort study. At 24 months, 125 toddlers were classified into three groups: EL toddlers diagnosed with ASD (EL-ASD, n = 18), EL toddlers not meeting diagnostic criteria for ASD (EL-nonASD, n = 38), and low likelihood toddlers without ASD (LL, n = 69). Prospective longitudinal assessments of sensory patterns were conducted using the Toddler Sensory Profile-2 (TSP-2). Sensory patterns were compared among groups from 12 to 24 months by Linear Mixed-effects Models and Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons. The associations between early sensory patterns and later autistic traits were also examined.ResultsA significant group × time interaction for sensory sensitivity across 12–24 months (F = 9.87, p < 0.001), and a marked increase in sensory sensitivity in EL-ASD toddlers between 18 and 24 months (p < 0.001). EL-ASD toddlers exhibited significantly higher sensory registration, auditory processing and touch processing scores at 12 months compared to LL group. Elevated sensory registration scores at 12 months were positively associated with social affect (B = 0.14, p = 0.036), restricted repetitive behaviors (RRBs; B = 0.12, p = 0.003), and total scores (B = 0.25, p = 0.005) at 24 months among EL-ASD toddlers. Additionally, elevated sensory avoiding scores at 18 months were positively associated with social affect (B = 0.33, p = 0.045) and total scores (B = 0.30, p = 0.046) at 24 months.ConclusionsDistinct sensory patterns emerge by 12 months in elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism, with sensory sensitivity increasing significantly between 18 and 24 months. Notably, early hypo- and hyper-responsivity predict later social communication difficulties and RRBs, suggesting that sensory differences may exert a “cascading effect”, thereby contributing to autistic outcomes.Clinical trial numberwww.chictr.org.cn/; No: ChiCTR2100049811; Date:2021.08.10.

Background

Historically, sensory differences have been viewed as less important than social communication and restricted repetitive behaviors in defining autism. The inclusion of sensory differences as a diagnostic domain in autism [1] has since catalyzed a paradigm shift, stimulating research into behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms underlying these patterns in autistic individuals. Sensory differences are a hallmark feature of autism, affecting up to 90% of autistic individuals [2, 3]. Crucially, sensory differences may carry profound implications for the quality of life and developmental outcomes of autistic individuals, influencing adaptive functioning and family functioning [4].

Empirical studies have identified three primary sensory patterns in autistic children: hyper-responsivity (exaggerated reactions), hypo-responsivity (diminished/no responses) and sensory seeking [5, 6]. However, longitudinal evidence characterizing early sensory differences in autism remains scarce. Prospective longitudinal research with infants at elevated likelihood (EL) for autism is essential for mapping early sensory differences and their associations with emerging autism phenotypes [7, 8]. This approach enables the identification of preclinical sensory markers of autism at earlier developmental stages. Understanding sensory development during infancy and toddlerhood is critically important for early autism identification, particularly within the critical developmental window from 12 to 24 months. Evidence from prospective longitudinal studies have demonstrated that sensory differences emerge during the first year of life in infants later diagnosed with autism [9, 10], suggesting such differences may constitute early precursors to autism. However, significant gaps remain regarding trajectory of early sensory development in autism. Only a limited number of longitudinal studies have examined sensory trajectories in infant siblings. For instance, Green and colleagues have detected no significant changes in sensory hyper-responsivity in autistic toddlers during 18–33 months [11]. In contrast, Wolff and colleagues have demonstrated escalating sensory hyper-responsivity from 12 to 24 months in elevated likelihood toddlers who later diagnosed with autism [12]. These discrepancies highlight methodological limitations in prior research, including heterogeneous age groups, sample sizes, cross-sectional designs and limited follow-up intervals, which highlight the necessity of prospective investigations to assess the trajectory of early sensory development in autism between 12 and 24 months of age.

Previous cross-sectional studies have established associations between sensory patterns and core autistic traits [13] and co-occurring conditions such as anxiety and attention difficulties [14]. A critical unresolved question is whether early sensory patterns can predict later autistic traits. Prior research has predominantly relied on retrospective designs to examine the associations between early sensory differences and later autistic traits [13, 15]. By contrast, emerging prospective studies of toddlers at elevated likelihood are beginning to employ longitudinal approaches to track these developmental associations. For example, Damiano-Goodwin and colleagues have documented that elevated sensory seeking at 18 months in toddlers at elevated likelihood for autism were associated with reduced social orienting and predicted subsequent social traits at 36 months [16], suggesting that sensory seeking may produce cascading effects on social development in autism. Grzadzinski and colleagues have found that observed sensory hypo-responsivity at 14 months in children at elevated likelihood for autism were associated with higher autistic severity at 3–5 years, and parent report of hyper-responsivity at 14 months was positively associated with later repetitive behaviors [17]. Chen and colleagues further demonstrated that change rates of sensory hyper- and hypo-responsivity better differentiated children with an autism diagnosis from those with other developmental conditions in a community sample, and elevated hyper- and hypo-responsivity at 6–19 months followed by steeper increases differentially predicted more autistic traits at 6–7 years [18]. Collectively, these findings yield initial longitudinal evidence that early sensory differences predict subsequent autistic manifestations. Although prior longitudinal work has examined sensory developmental trajectories and their correlations with later core traits from Western populations, there is currently no investigation of this issue among Chinese population. Given potential cultural and contextual disparities, rigorous verification of such predictive relationships necessitates additional prospective longitudinal studies across different populations.

The present study employed a prospective cohort design, aiming to: (1) characterize trajectory of sensory development from 12 to 24 months in toddlers at elevated likelihood for autism, and identify between-group differences at each time point; (2) examine associations between early sensory patterns and later autistic traits. We hypothesized that: (1) elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism would show distinct sensory trajectories relative to non-autistic toddlers, with sensory differences emerging by 12 months; and (2) early sensory patterns would predict later autistic traits.

Methods

Study design

This study is part of the ongoing Longitudinal Chinese Autism Study of Early Development (LCAS-ED), a prospective cohort study implemented at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Tianhe Campus) and Lingnan Hospital (Huangpu Branch) in Guangzhou, China. Infants were enrolled at age of 6–18 months and followed longitudinally at 12, 18, 24 months of age, with required follow-up at all scheduled time-points to minimize attrition. Follow-up windows were operationally defined as 1 month for assessments conducted at 12–18 months of age and 2 months for evaluations at 24 months of age. Participants were recruited using multi-modal enrollment strategies, included: (1) direct referral from pediatricians in the specialty clinic, where physicians identified eligible infants and introduced the study to families; (2) parent advocacy groups (e.g., local autism family support network), which disseminated study information through their established family networks; and (3) social media campaigns targeting families with infants via platforms (e.g., WeChat). Interested families contacted the research team via telephone or email, after which a trained research assistant conducted a preliminary eligibility screening and scheduled the baseline assessment.

Participants

Toddlers at elevated likelihood (EL) for autism defined as those with at least one older sibling, who has a confirmed clinical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [1]. Toddlers at low likelihood (LL) for autism were those with older siblings meeting criteria for neurotypical development (confirmed by Ages and Stages Questionnaires, ASQ) and no first-degree relatives with documented developmental disorders (ASD, intellectual disability, or specific learning disorder etc.).

In this study, all toddlers satisfied the following inclusion criteria: gestational age of 36–42 weeks, birth weight greater than 2500 g and Mandarin Chinese as their native language. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Syndromic autism (e.g., Rett syndrome, fragile X syndrome, Down syndrome, Williams syndrome) confirmed via clinical genetics testing or medical record review; (2) Active neurological/medical conditions impacting development: untreated epilepsy, congenital heart disease, inborn errors of metabolism, or history of central nervous system infection; (3) organic sensory dysfunction (e.g., hearing loss, visual impairment);4) Documented perinatal brain injury (hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, intraventricular hemorrhage) or teratogenic exposure (e.g., alcohol, valproic acid) with clinical suspicion of neurodevelopmental impact; 5) Twins.

Participant attrition and missing data

Longitudinal attrition was monitored across all assessment time points. A total of 127 toddlers were enrolled at study entry, with valid data available for 79 (62.2%) participants at 12 months, 104 (81.9%) participants at 18 months, and 125 (98.4%) participants at 24 months of age. The relatively high proportion of missing data at 12 months was attributable to the study enrollment window spanning 6–18 months of age. The primary reasons for missing data at 18 months included family travel, acute illness, and competing work commitments. Notably, these participants remained enrolled in the study and completed subsequent assessments, which were classified as missed visits rather than formal withdrawals. By 24 months, only two families had withdrawn from the study, citing transportation difficulties. Finally, the participants’ sample comprises 56 EL toddlers and 69 LL toddlers.

Measures

Toddler sensory profile-2 (TSP-2)

The TSP-2 is a standardized measure of sensory patterns in daily life for toddlers aged 7–35 months, comprising 54 items [19]. Caregivers rate children’s responses to sensory experiences on a 5-point scale (from “almost never” to “almost always”), anchored in Dunn’s theoretical framework of sensory processing, which posits two core constructs: neurological threshold (stimulus responsiveness) and self-regulation style (passive vs. active engagement with stimuli). Combining these constructs yields four sensory patterns: Registration: high threshold + passive regulation (under-responding to sensory input); Seeking: high threshold + active regulation (intentionally seeking intense sensations); Sensitivity: low threshold + passive regulation (notice or detect sensory input more readily); Avoiding: low threshold + active regulation (actively avoiding sensory experiences). The TSP-2 also assesses five sensory modalities: auditory, visual, touch, movement and oral processing. These axes delineate four quadrants that elucidate how toddlers process sensory information through dynamic interactions between stimulus-driven reactivity and adaptive self-regulatory strategies. Dunn’s sensory patterns quadrant model remains the predominant framework for classifying sensory profiles in autism [20]. Sensory avoiding and sensitivity represent distinct manifestations of hyper-responsivity, differentiated by the presence or absence of active behavioral regulation [21]. By distinguishing sensory sensitivity (passive responding) from sensory avoiding (active responding), this model permits investigation of their differential developmental trajectories, helping to reconcile prior inconsistent findings linking hyper-responsivity to autistic traits [22].

The TSP-2 demonstrates robust psychometric validity, with evidence of temporal stability and cross-cultural applicability [23]. Chinese version shows high internal consistency (α = 0.80) and excellent test-retest reliability (interclass correlation coefficient = 0.94) for subscales, discriminating between typical and atypical sensory patterns in Chinese children [24]. These properties make the TSP-2 a validated tool for identifying sensory processing difficulties in preverbal and early-verbal Mandarin-speaking children. The toddlers underwent the TSP-2 assessment at 12, 18, 24 months of age. Higher TSP-2 scores indicated greater sensory differences. For the registration domain specifically, higher scores reflected lower sensory registration.

Autism diagnostic observation schedule- toddler module (ADOS- T)

The ADOS-2 is a standardized, semi-structured observational assessment of autistic symptoms, evaluating core domains including communication, social interaction, imaginative play, Restricted Repetitive Behaviors (RRBs) [25]. The ADOS-2 Toddler Module (ADOS-T) is specifically designed for children aged 12–30 months, ranging from nonverbal to those using simple two-word phrases. Two age- and language-level-specific algorithms are provided for the toddler module to account for developmental variability [26]. The ADOS- T demonstrates excellent diagnostic reliability for ASD (Cohen’s kappa ≥ 0.80) [27]. In this study, standardized scores [28] for social-affect, RRBs and total scores were used, with higher scores indicating more autistic traits. The toddlers were administered ADOS-T assessment at 24 months of age.

Mullen scales of early learning (MSEL)

The MSEL [29] is a standardized, observation-based assessment of early child development. Widely used in early developmental screening, the MSEL exhibits strong psychometric properties for measuring developmental abilities in autistic children, including robust reliability and validity [30, 31]. T-scores were used to quantify performance in fine motor, visual reception, receptive language, and expressive language domains, with higher scores reflecting better developmental proficiency. The toddlers were assessed by MSEL at 24 months of age.

Clinical diagnosis protocol

Participants undergo comprehensive developmental evaluation at 24 months of age by a multidisciplinary team (developmental pediatricians, child neurologist, child psychotherapist), including: ADOS-T, MSEL and systematic behavioral observation/ history collection. Toddlers at elevated likelihood for autism were classified based on Clinical Best Estimate (CBE) diagnosis made by experienced clinicians using DSM-5 criteria and algorithm cutoffs for ASD on standardized ADOS-Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS).

Diagnostic verification was conducted by two specialists. Based on previously established grouping criteria [12], this independent review process classified participants into three groups at 24 months of age: toddlers at elevated likelihood diagnosed with ASD (EL-ASD group; n = 18), which met ASD diagnosis and ADOS-CSS ≥ 4; toddlers at elevated likelihood not meeting the diagnostic criteria for ASD (EL-nonASD group; n = 38); and toddlers at low likelihood without ASD (LL group; n = 69). Specially, all participants in the LL group met ADOS-CSS <‌ 4, and were classified as typically developing based on clinical diagnostic evaluation.

Quality control

Toddlers with ASD were independently diagnosed by two board-certified specialists (developmental pediatricians or child neurologist) with ≥ 10 years of clinical experience, using a diagnostic consensus conference to ensure inter-rater reliability and adherence to DSM-5 criteria. During cohort follow-up, quarterly quality control reviews and data reconciliation were conducted by research staffs, with all data securely recorded in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).

ADOS-T assessments were administered by certified clinical pediatricians who completed standardized training and achieved ≥ 90% inter-rater reliability on practice videotapes before data collection. Examiners followed strictly scripted administration protocols and used calibrated scoring guides to minimize observer bias. Parents completed the TSP-2 after receiving standardized written instructions from research coordinators, with clarifications provided via telephone if needed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean (M) and standard deviation (SD). Normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed variables were transformed via natural logarithm transformation to meet parametric assumptions. The three group differences in normally distributed continuous variables were evaluated using ANOVA. Non-parametric variables (e.g., parental education, family incomes) were compared via the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical data were reported as frequencies (%) and analyzed using Chi-squared test.

To examine longitudinal trajectories of the four sensory patterns and five sensory modalities, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted to the repeated TSP-2 data. We initially specified a maximal random-effects structure, including both random intercepts and random slopes for time (age in months) within participants. However, given the modest sample size and restricted number of repeated measurements, models incorporating random slopes for time encountered convergence issues and produced fit warnings. Consequently, we adopted a random-intercept-only model, which appropriately accounts for within-individual correlation while maintaining model stability. This decision was supported by likelihood ratio tests (LRT) and comparisons of Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, which indicated that the random-intercept model provided adequate fit without significant loss of explanatory power relative to the more complex structure.

The final models included random intercepts for participants to account for within-subject correlation, with fixed effects for group (EL-ASD vs. EL-nonASD vs. LL group), time, and the group × time interaction. Sex was included as a covariate to account for sex distribution differences, particularly among EL-ASD toddlers. All models were estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML). Given a prior hypothesis regarding group differences at specific developmental periods [12], we conducted planned comparisons at each timepoint regardless of interaction significance, with Bonferroni correction applied to control familywise error. To estimate effect sizes, partial η² was computed for the group × time interaction, with larger values indicating stronger effects. Common interpretation criteria are as follows: small effect: partial η² =0.01; medium effect: partial η² =0.06; large effect: partial η² = 0.14.

To examine the predictive associations between early sensory patterns and later autistic traits in EL-ASD toddlers, hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted. Separate models were fitted for each ADOS-T outcome (social affect, RRBs, and total scores) at 24 months. Sex was entered in Block 1 as a covariate; the four sensory patterns (sensitivity, avoiding, seeking, and registration) at 12 and 18 months were separately entered in Block 2 as predictors. To account for multiple comparisons and minimize the risk of false positive findings, we applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction [32] separately within each outcome, with statistical significance set at q < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and behavioral characteristics

As the cohort study protocol permitted a flexible assessment window of ± 1–2 months, chronological age at the three time points were examined. No significant between-group differences in age at assessment were observed across the 12-, 18-, and 24-month visits (all p > 0.05). EL-ASD toddlers showed a significant difference in sex distribution (χ² = 19.36, p = 0.036), consistent with well-documented sex differences in autism. Parental education levels did not differ significantly among the three groups (all p > 0.05). MSEL data indicated that EL-ASD toddlers exhibited significantly lower T-scores in fine motor, visual reception, expressive language, and receptive language domains at 24 months (all p < 0.05). ADOS-T data further revealed that EL-ASD toddlers obtained significantly higher scores in social affect, RRBs, and total scores at 24 months (all p < 0.05), compared to EL-nonASD and LL toddlers (see Table 1).

Trajectory differences of sensory patterns in EL-ASD toddlers

LMMs indicated significant main effects of time (F = 8.88, p < 0.001) and group (F = 12.07, p < 0.001) in sensory sensitivity. Critically, a significant group × time interaction emerged from 12 to 24 months (F = 9.87, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.187), suggesting divergent developmental trajectories of sensory sensitivity across groups during toddlerhood. Development curve further showed a marked increase in sensory sensitivity among EL-ASD toddlers between 18 and 24 months, whereas the development curve remained relatively stable for both EL-nonASD and LL toddlers (see Fig. 1a). Bonferroni post hoc comparisons indicated that sensory sensitivity differed between EL-ASD and EL-nonASD toddlers at 18 months, with EL-ASD toddlers scoring higher than EL-nonASD toddlers (MD = 5.88, SE = 2.25, p = 0.031; see Table 2).

Notably, post hoc comparisons revealed that EL-ASD toddlers exhibited significantly poorer registration than LL toddlers at 12 months (MD = 6.73, SE = 2.41, p = 0.020), as indexed by higher TSP-2 scores. However, group × time interactions were not significant for sensory avoiding (F = 2.09, p = 0.088) and sensory seeking (F = 0.57, p = 0.687) over time. No between-group differences were detected at any timepoint (all p > 0.05).

For auditory processing, LMMs showed a significant main effect of group (F = 22.38, p < 0.001), whereas the main effect of time did not reach significance (F = 2.13, p = 0.123). Critically, a significant group × time interaction was observed from 12 to 24 months (F = 4.13, p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.097), indicating divergent developmental trajectories across groups. Touch processing exhibited a comparable pattern: a significant group main effect (F = 49.72, p < 0.001), a non-significant time main effect (F = 1.36, p = 0.260), and a significant group × time interaction over the same period (F = 2.65, p = 0.034, partial η² = 0.045), again suggesting divergent trajectories in touch processing. The developmental curves illustrated that EL-ASD toddlers exhibited an accelerated upward trajectory in auditory processing between 18 and 24 months, whereas touch processing showed minimal change from 12 to 24 months (see Fig. 2a, c). Bonferroni post hoc comparisons also indicated that EL-ASD toddlers scored higher auditory processing (MD = 7.29, SE = 1.57, p < 0.001) and touch processing (MD = 4.06, SE = 1.13, p = 0.001) than the LL group at 12 months (see Table 3). The group × time interaction for visual processing was not significant from 12 to 24 months (F = 0.88, p = 0.479, partial η² =0.018), and no between-group differences were detected at any timepoint (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

Development trajectory differences of early sensory patterns in elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism

In this prospective cohort study, we identified that EL-ASD toddlers exhibited significant sensory differences at 12 months compared with LL toddlers, manifesting as low sensory registration and increased difficulties in auditory and tactile processing. These sensory patterns were not mutually exclusive in autism; they frequently co-occurred as diverse profile combinations, reflecting substantial heterogeneity in early sensory responses among autistic individuals [33].

This study demonstrated that EL-ASD toddlers exhibited low sensory registration at 12 months, a profile that remained relatively stable through 24 months. These findings offer novel evidence characterizing the specificity of early sensory differences in autism, aligning with developmental psychology frameworks positing that dysregulation in sensory registration—the initial stage of information processing—may cascade into disruptions in subsequent cognitive integration and social interaction [34]. Low sensory registration reflects a combination of high neurological thresholds and passive response styles, characterized by reduced detection of sensory input and limited active compensatory behaviors; this construct overlaps conceptually with sensory hypo-responsivity [35].

From a developmental perspective, sensory hypo-responsivity (i.e., low registration) presents as elevated thresholds for environmental stimuli (e.g., requiring louder verbal input to elicit attention). Notably, sensory hypo-responsivity in autistic toddlers did not diminish significantly with age, consistent with follow-up studies of autistic children aged 2–12 years indicating that hypo-responsivity to sensory stimuli remains a core differentiating feature among autism, neurotypical development, and developmental delay [36]. This developmental stability not only supports sensory hypo-responsivity as a potential early behavioral marker for autism but also provides longitudinal evidence for the hypothesis that early sensory differences may constitute primary drivers of neurodevelopmental divergence [37]. These findings suggest that sensory hypo-responsivity may operate not merely as a comorbid feature but as a cascade initiator, indirectly contributing to secondary social communication difficulties by disrupting early multimodal sensory integration [38]. These findings carry significant clinical implications: incorporating sensory hypo-responsivity into 12-month developmental assessments may enhance the sensitivity of early autism identification.

Longitudinal analyses revealed significant increases in sensory sensitivity among EL-ASD toddlers between 18 and 24 months, whereas non-autistic toddlers generally exhibited decreasing or stable trajectories. Sensory sensitivity represents a manifestation of hyper-responsivity, arising from low neurological thresholds combined with active response strategies that produce intense or rapid reactions to sensory input [21]. This divergent developmental trajectory aligns with the period of expanding environmental exposure during the second year of life, during which autistic toddlers demonstrated heightened reactivity to sensory inputs, particularly auditory and tactile stimuli. This temporal alignment raises the possibility that the interaction between neurodevelopmental differences associated with autism and the escalating complexity of sensory input during this period may contribute to the amplification of sensory hyper-responsivity. These findings resonate with prior evidence of elevated hyper-responsivity in autistic individuals [39], alongside recent work identifying sensory sensitivity as a robust predictor of autistic diagnosis in toddlers [40].

Sensory hyper-responsivity in autism is not simply increased but may be modulated by factors such as environmental predictability [41]. The predictive coding framework provides a complementary theoretical lens through which to consider sensory hyper-responsivity in autism [42]. This framework posits that suboptimal predictive processing in autism may reduce the precision of prior expectations and disrupt the filtering of irrelevant sensory information, potentially contributing to sensory hyper-responsivity. Early research suggested that inflexibly high precision of prediction errors could generate strong error signals from sensory inputs, potentially causing sensory hyper-responsivity in autism [43]. Integrating these findings, a plausible psychological mechanism for significant increases in sensory sensitivity observed in EL-ASD toddlers is proposed: The timing of sensory hyper-responsivity exacerbation between 18 and 24 months coincides with increased environmental exploration and multisensory integration demands. During this period, autistic toddlers may experience heightened stress due to impaired filtering of irrelevant stimuli, leading to cumulative sensory overload. This aligns with the neurodevelopmental model [44], which posits primary neural deviations in autism—not secondary anxiety or intolerance of uncertainty—drive sensory hyper-responsivity. Consistent with the model linking sensory hyper-responsivity and anxiety, emerging evidence indicated that sensory hyper-responsivity was perceived as significantly more of a cause of anxiety than an effect. Consistent with theoretical models linking sensory hyper-responsivity and anxiety [45], autistic adults perceive sensory hyper-responsivity as a cause rather than an effect of anxiety [46]. However, we emphasize that the present study did not measure anxiety or predictive processing parameters directly, and therefore any mechanistic interpretation remains speculative.

Notably, the concurrent increases in sensory sensitivity and auditory processing difficulties observed in EL-ASD toddlers between 18 and 24 months align with recent advances in cross-modal sensory interactions—differences in one sensory modality may disrupt integration across modalities via neural plasticity mechanisms [47]. These emerging sensory patterns may provide evidence for developing prediction models based on sensory phenotypes in autism.

Prediction of early sensory differences on later autistic traits in elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism

Growing evidence indicates that sensory differences serve not only as early markers for autism, but also as critical predictors of later autistic traits. Departing from the predominantly cross-sectional designs characterizing prior research, our longitudinal findings demonstrated that early sensory differences exhibit domain-specific predictive pathways toward later autistic traits. Specifically, sensory low registration (i.e., hypo-responsivity) at 12 months predicted greater social affect difficulties, RRBs, and autistic severity at 24 months among EL-ASD toddlers. Additionally, heightened sensory avoiding at 18 months—reflecting hyper-responsivity—positively predicted later social affect difficulties and autistic severity. These findings highlight the need to examine sensory-behavioral associations separately across core symptom domains, rather than treating autistic traits as a unitary construct.

Social affect domain

Sensory differences have been documented as early as 6 months of age in infants later diagnosed with autism [48, 49], and such differences may initiate cascading developmental effects on subsequent social communication [50]. Hypo-responsivity during infancy is particularly disruptive to social development, as toddlers who fail to orient toward and engage with social information (e.g., speech, facial expressions) subsequently miss critical learning opportunities that scaffold reciprocal social interaction. This potential mechanism is supported by longitudinal evidence indicating that sensory hypo-responsivity observed between 12 and 18 months was associated with later social communication difficulties in autistic toddlers [51]. Similarly, hyper-responsivity may also constrain social participation by triggering overwhelming aversive responses to everyday stimuli (e.g., noisy environments, unexpected touch), prompting toddlers to withdraw from social contexts and thereby limiting social practice. Among toddlers at elevated likelihood for autism, those exhibiting hyper-responsivity at 12 months subsequently demonstrated more pronounced social affect difficulties, alongside the highest autism diagnosis rate at 36 months [52]. Collectively, these findings suggest that early sensory hypo- and hyper-responsivity may affect social development through distinct mechanisms, yet both are associated with less favorable social outcomes by 24 months.

RRBs domain

The relationship between early sensory differences and later RRBs appears to be more complicated and subtype-specific. In the present study, increased sensory hypo-responsivity at 12 months predicted elevated RRBs at 24 months in EL-ASD toddlers. By contrast, sensory avoiding at 18 months predicted autistic severity but was not associated with RRBs at 24 months. This finding aligns with the hypothesis that sensory hypo-responsivity may drive repetitive sensory-motor behaviors (e.g., stereotyped body rocking, hand flapping) [53]. Such repetitive motor behaviors may function as self-regulatory strategies when environmental sensory input is insufficient or poorly registered. Consistent with our findings, Woff and colleagues have found that sensory hypo- and hyper-responsivity at 12 months was significantly associated with RRBs at 24 months in elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism [12]. This association is further supported by prior research, which demonstrated that sensory hypo- and hyper-responsivity were associated with repetitive motor and insistence on sameness behaviors, and the relationships were mediated by intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety [54].

This study did not find any associations between early sensory seeking and later autistic traits in EL-ASD toddlers, consistent with findings from two longitudinal cohort studies [12, 52]. A meta-analysis revealed that sensory seeking is significantly more pronounced among autistic children aged 6 to 9 years [5]. It is plausible that the association between sensory seeking and autistic traits emerges at different developmental stages. Notably, we observed that the mean scores of sensory seeking in EL-ASD toddlers were slightly lower than non-autistic toddlers. A potential explanation for this discrepancy relates to rater bias: EL-ASD toddlers have at least one older autistic sibling, and it is likely that these parents have a different experience of autism and may underrate the sensory seeking behaviors of their children.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first longitudinal investigation of early sensory development trajectories in EL siblings within the Chinese context, extending prior research to include LL toddlers. However, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, parental-report of TSP-2 may cause subjectivity and potential bias, and future research should integrate objective psychophysical measures with observational assessments to achieve a more comprehensive understanding early sensory processing in autistic toddlers. Secondly, the modest sample size, especially for the EL-ASD group, limits statistical power and estimate reliability, potentially precluding detection of small-to-medium group differences. Large-scale, multi-site replication with diverse racial and ethnic populations is essential to validate these findings. Thirdly, the MSEL was not administered at baseline, precluding control for developmental level. Consequently, we cannot rule out the possibility that baseline group differences in developmental level confounded the observed associations between early sensory patterns and later autistic outcomes. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive developmental assessments at the initial visit to disentangle the unique contributions of sensory processing and general developmental functioning. Finally, this study delineates sensory development patterns in autistic toddlers during the first two years of life, generalization to broader age ranges (2–6 years) remains unconfirmed. To address this, we will continue tracking sensory development trajectories in EL siblings, providing critical scientific evidence to support the extrapolation of our findings across the early childhood continuum.

Conclusion

This prospective study demonstrates that distinct sensory patterns emerge by 12 months in elevated likelihood toddlers later diagnosed with autism, with sensory sensitivity increasing significantly between 18 and 24 months. Notably, early hypo- and hyper-responsivity predict later social communication difficulties and RRBs. Clinically, these findings underscore the value of sensory difference for identifying autism in early childhood, and suggest that targeted sensory interventions may improve developmental trajectories.
