Prescription and use of ward‐delivered nasal high‐flow oxygen and non‐invasive ventilation for people with acute respiratory failure
Authors: Aiden Cushnahan, Amelia Harrison, Catherine Buchan, Matthew T. Naughton, Eli Dabscheck, Natasha Smallwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNasal high‐flow therapy with oxygen entrained (HFNO) and non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) are important therapies for managing acute respiratory failure (ARF) in the ward‐based setting. The European Respiratory Society released guidelines in 2022 recommending the use of HFNO over conventional oxygen therapy and NIV in patients with hypoxic ARF. We conducted a 12‐month retrospective audit of ward‐based HFNO and NIV use at our hospital to determine prescribing practices for these therapies and to assist with the safe implementation of the recent European Respiratory Society guidelines. We found significant variation in prescribing practices for both HFNO and NIV.

Acute respiratory failure (ARF) occurs when the respiratory system fails to either adequately oxygenate the blood or prevent carbon dioxide retention and is a major reason for hospital admission in Australia. 1 Severe ARF has a mortality rate of ~40% to 45%. 2 The role of ward‐based respiratory therapies in managing ARF is critical as the healthcare system seeks to reduce the burden on precious intensive care resources.

Ward‐based respiratory supports include conventional oxygen therapy (COT), nasal high‐flow therapy with oxygen entrained (HFNO) and non‐invasive ventilation (NIV). 3 HFNO therapy provides a high flow of heated humidified air to which 100% oxygen can be entrained and has the ability to deliver a small amount of positive end‐expiratory pressure (5 cmH20 when flow is 60 L/min), reduce dead space ventilation, improve mucociliary clearance and provide uninterrupted titratable airflow and oxygen therapy, which is well tolerated. 4 Systematic reviews have demonstrated that HFNO may reduce the need for intubation and NIV, reduce mortality (for selected patient populations) and improve dyspnoea in patients with hypoxic ARF due to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, asthma, acute lung injury or pulmonary oedema. 5 NIV provides bilevel positive airway pressure delivered via a non‐invasive interface and avoids the risks associated with invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV). 6 Based on substantial evidence over many years, clinical practice guidelines recommend NIV as first‐line therapy for the treatment of hypercapnic ARF in people with exacerbations of COPD. 7 , 8 Compared with NIV, there is some evidence that HFNO may reduce the rate of intubation, mortality and intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay and improve comfort in patients with hypoxic ARF. 5 In light of the benefits of HFNO in 2022, the European Respiratory Society (ERS) released guidelines recommending the early use of HFNO over COT and NIV in patients with hypoxic ARF. 9

Models for delivery of ward‐based NIV have long been described. 10 , 11 Evidence shows that early implementation of NIV in appropriately selected patients with hypercapnic ARF is safe and reduces mortality. 11 The provision of HFNO for hypoxic ARF outside of the critical care environment has increased due to the need to provide safe, high‐quality care to all patients (including those who might not be considered suitable for ICU admission) and the profound pressures placed on the healthcare system by the COVID‐19 pandemic. 5

Our study aimed to examine the use of ward‐based HFNO and NIV at an Australian metropolitan teaching hospital over a 1‐year period pre‐pandemic to determine the prescribing and implementation practices for these therapies, identify any gaps in care and to assist with the safe implementation of the latest ERS guidelines. 9 We chose the year before the pandemic to examine routine practice, as opposed to models of care that were adapted to address pandemic‐driven healthcare pressures. As NIV has been used for longer, we hypothesised that prescribing of NIV would be more complete than HFNO prescribing.

Ethics approval for this study was granted by the Alfred Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (approval No: 291/21). The Alfred Hospital is a quaternary metropolitan teaching hospital in Melbourne. People with ARF may receive HFNO or NIV in the emergency department (ED), ICU, respiratory ward or on any acute medical or surgical ward as required. When NIV or HFNO are provided outside of the ED and ICU, these therapies are supervised by a dedicated respiratory support service comprising a respiratory physician, respiratory advanced trainee and respiratory clinical nurse consultants.

A retrospective, electronic medical record review was conducted of all consecutive patients admitted to the Alfred Hospital between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2019 and who required ward‐based NIV or HFNO for treatment of ARF. Patients who commenced NIV or HFNO in the ED, ICU or theatre recovery and who required ongoing support with either therapy on the ward were included. However, patients who received HFNO or NIV solely in the ED, ICU or theatre were excluded, as the focus of this study was to examine the delivery of respiratory support therapies outside of high‐intensity ward areas. Similarly, patients who received continuous positive airway pressure therapy rather than NIV were excluded.

Data were collected from hospital administrative databases and electronic medical records and included patient demographics, admission illness, comorbidities, NIV or HFNO prescription and use and patient outcomes. The Thoracic Society of Australia and New Zealand (TSANZ) oxygen guidelines 12 and ERS NIV guidelines 7 were referenced to define the key elements required for comprehensive prescription of each respiratory support therapy. For HFNO, a complete prescription was defined as including HFNO device flow rate, fraction of entrained inspired oxygen (FiO2) and desired target oxygen saturation (SpO2) range on treatment. For NIV, a complete prescription was defined as including inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP), expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP), target SpO2 range on treatment and time until next medical review and arterial blood gas (ABG). For both treatments, we also expected resuscitation status to be clearly documented as per hospital policy. Patient outcomes included escalation to NIV (from HFNO) or IMV (from HFNO or NIV), discharge destination and mortality.

Results are reported descriptively using counts and frequencies or using median values with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐parametric data. Tests of association were used to examine relationships between baseline variables (including age, sex, smoking status, comorbidities, baseline lung function and reason for hospital admission) and pre‐defined outcomes of mortality, escalation of therapy to NIV or IMV and direct discharge home after the acute hospital admission. Student t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, Χ2 test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney test for non‐parametric data. Statistical analyses were performed using XL‐STAT statistics software. Statistical significance was indicated by P < 0.05.

Of 1314 patients who received ward‐based HFNO or NIV in the hospital, 238 met eligibility criteria, with 111 patients receiving NIV and 127 patients receiving HFNO. Patients were predominantly male, with a median age of 72 years (IQR: 61.5–81 years) in the HFNO group and 67 years (IQR: 58.5–81.5 years) in the NIV group (Table 1). Receiving ward‐based NIV was associated with a history of cigarette smoking (P = 0.031), any underlying respiratory disease (P < 0.001), COPD (P < 0.001), previous lung transplantation (P = 0.029) and worse lung function parameters (P < 0.001).

Patients who were prescribed NIV were most frequently admitted for an exacerbation of COPD (27.9%) and had hypercapnic ARF (median arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide = 63 mm Hg (IQR: 50–72.5 mm Hg)) with acidosis (median pH = 7.31 (IQR: 7.25–7.36)). HFNO was most frequently prescribed for ARF secondary to pneumonia (20.5%).

Rates of complete initial device prescription were low (NIV = 30% and HFNO 19%). The most frequently missing elements of device prescription were flow rate (HFNO), time until next ABG and medical review (NIV) and resuscitation status (HFNO and NIV), with the most incomplete prescriptions missing multiple elements. Clinician seniority was significantly associated with complete HFNO prescription, with consultant physicians providing more complete prescriptions than junior medical staff (P = 0.014) (Fig. 1). Complete NIV prescription was also significantly associated with treating medical team, being highest when completed by the respiratory team (P = 0.030). ABGs were more frequently performed prior to commencement of NIV when treatment was recommended by the respiratory unit (P < 0.001) and within business hours (P = 0.029).

Of 127 patients receiving ward‐based HFNO, 29 (23%) died, 62 (49%) were directly discharged home, 36 (28%) required further admission in a subacute setting and 13 (10%) were escalated to NIV (9%) or IMV (1%). Of 111 patients receiving ward‐based NIV, 20 (18%) died, 81 (73%) were discharged directly home, 10 (9%) required further admission in a subacute setting and two (2%) were escalated to IMV. Death was associated with age older than 65 years (P = 0.025) in patients receiving HFNO. Age and gender were not associated with mortality in patients receiving NIV.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first paper reporting prescribing practices and prescription completeness for HFNO and NIV. We identified significant variation in prescribing practices for respiratory support therapies and rates of complete initial prescription were low for both HFNO and NIV. This is not an isolated finding as poor prescription of COT for the treatment of ARF is also a well‐recognised issue. 13 Prescription of respiratory therapies, including conventional oxygen therapy, is not only recommended in respiratory guidelines 7 , 8 , 9 , 12 but is crucial to ensure correct implementation and to safeguard patients from potential harms, particularly when these therapies are provided in lower acuity ward settings. 14

There is a large body of literature examining strategies to improve oxygen prescription in hospitals. From this evidence, it is clear that a multi‐faceted approach is required to achieve sustained improvements in oxygen prescribing practices. 15 , 16 , 17 Improving HFNO and NIV prescription practices likely requires a similar approach. Education on oxygen prescription for hospital staff is important; however, as a standalone intervention, it has been shown to be ineffective and needs to be coupled with other interventions for meaningful improvement. These strategies include the incorporation of HFNO and NIV prescribing templates and decision assistance prompts into the electronic medical record, providing visual cues such as stickers on respiratory devices and above oxygen taps and posters in work areas where these devices are likely to be used. It is also important that local hospital policies for use of respiratory devices (including COT) are standardised and kept up to date to reflect advice from international guidelines. Successful implementation of these measures requires input from both senior and junior medical staff, nurses and allied health and, ideally, nomination of ‘HNFO/NIV champions’. 18 Regular HFNO and NIV prescription audits are then required to assess changes in practice and allow for feedback.

Mortality rates were high among patients requiring HFNO or NIV for ARF, which is consistent with other studies 19 , 20 , 21 and expected given the seriousness of the illness. Additionally, patients receiving ward‐based respiratory supports are older with major comorbidities, thus may have treatment limitations that contribute to decisions to provide treatment outside of the ICU. Incomplete prescription of these respiratory devices has the potential to result in delay to treatment initiation, titration of device settings, timely re‐assessment and escalation of therapy, as well as issues with staff handover of device settings and goals of treatment. There is evidence, particularly for the treatment of hypercapnoeic ARF with NIV, that effective treatment in the first 24 h (and particularly the first 2 h) is critical for treatment success. 22 Meticulous early monitoring, with clear guidance of when to reassess physiological parameters, is key. 23 Our study aimed to understand how non‐invasive respiratory therapies were being used on the wards to support patients with ARF and what implementation practices were adopted, and so we were unable to comment further on whether prescription completeness was associated with patient outcomes.

Study limitations include the single‐centre, retrospective (pre‐COVID pandemic) design, which may limit generalisability to other institutions, and data quality was reliant on accurate clinical coding and documentation within electronic medical records. This also limited our ability to capture patients who were switched to HNFO for periods of NIV breaks. While we purposely chose to examine routine care during a 1‐year time interval prior to the pandemic (for which complete data were available), it will be important to examine HFNO and NIV prescription and implementation over the past few years (as the pandemic has eased in Melbourne) to determine whether practice has changed.

In conclusion, this study identified significant variation in prescribing practices and care delivery for both HFNO and NIV when implemented to treat ward‐based patients with ARF. Understanding that poor prescription practices are not limited to conventional oxygen therapy but apply broadly to non‐invasive, ward‐based respiratory therapies highlights the need for change and to adopt new approaches to facilitate guideline‐concordant care.
