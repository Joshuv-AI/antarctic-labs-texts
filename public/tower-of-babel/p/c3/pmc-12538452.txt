Basithoracic ancient schwannoma: A case report of an unusual localization of a rare tumor
Authors: Rabti Souphia, Ben Hassine Basma, Ben Marzouk Sawssen, Tlili Karima, Mohamed Hedi Mannai, Khalifa Mohamed Bechir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAncient schwannomas are rare benign tumors originating from Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system. These tumors are characterized by degenerative changes that can mimic malignancy. Although schwannomas frequently develop in the head and neck region, they can appear in various anatomical locations. Introduction

Schwannomas are rare benign tumors of the peripheral nervous system, derived from the Schwann cells that form the sheath of peripheral nerves [1]. These tumors are usually encapsulated, slow-growing and can occur in a variety of anatomical locations [2]. Although around 25 %–50 % of schwannomas occur in the head and neck region, this tumor can rarely develop in any region of the body [1]. While schwannomas have been reported in various anatomic regions, very few cases involve the basithoracic location, making this presentation particularly noteworthy [3,4].

Among its variants, ancient schwannoma is characterized by specific histological degenerative changes that can lead to confusion with malignant tumors [5]. These changes include calcification, hyalinization and cystic necrosis [5]. The term “ancient” refers to degenerative changes that occur over time in the tumor [6].

The clinical presentation of schwannomas depends on their location, and patients are usually asymptomatic, although they may present with pain and a positive Tinel's sign, which is a tingling sensation along the distribution of the affected nerve [1,7]. Diagnosis is based on imaging, supplemented by histopathology and immunohistochemistry, which remain the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis [6].

Case report

This work has been reported in line with the 2025 SCARE criteria [8].

A 35-year-old male construction worker, nonsmoker, with BMI of 24.5 kg/m2 and no significant past medical, surgical, or family history presented with persistent right basithoracic parietal pain for 3 months. The patient denied weight loss, fatigue, or other systemic symptoms. The history revealed no associated fever, nausea or vomiting. The patient initially noticed the mass during self-examination after experiencing localized pain.

Clinical examination revealed a palpable mass measuring approximately 5 cm, painful to palpation, located at the right inferior costal margin. Tinel's sign was absent upon examination. The pain was localized without radiation to adjacent dermatomes. Vital signs and general, respiratory and cardiovascular examinations were all normal. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no guarding or rigidity.

Biological tests, including tumor markers, were all within normal limits. A thoracoabdominal CT scan showed a homogeneous, well-demarcated, oval mass at the right thoracic base, with no evidence of invasion of adjacent structures. MRI completed the work-up, showing a well-defined, hypervascularized mass with homogeneous enhancement after contrast injection, and low signal intensity in T1 and T2 (Fig. 1). Differential diagnoses considered preoperatively included neurofibroma, soft tissue sarcoma, and metastatic disease.

Axial T2-weighted MRI with gadolinium enhancement showing well-defined, hypervascularized mass in the right basithoracic region.

In view of these radiological findings, complete surgical excision was decided upon. The tumor was excised via a right subcostal incision using an open anterior approach. Operative time was 85 min, with minimal blood loss (approximately 120 ml). Intraoperative neuromonitoring was used to avoid injury to adjacent nerve structures. The procedure was uncomplicated, with complete excision of the mass and no damage to surrounding structures. No surgical drain was required, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2.

Pathological examination of the excision specimen showed a mass composed of spindle cells with a focal palisade arrangement, including hypercellular areas corresponding to Antoni A fibers and hypocellular areas corresponding to Antoni B fibers (Fig. 2). Degenerative changes were also present, with myxoid remodeling and degenerative nuclear atypia. Mitotic activity was low (<1/10 HPF), and Ki-67 index was below 3 %, confirming the benign nature of the lesion. Immunohistochemistry revealed uniform and intense positivity for S100 protein (Fig. 3). A final diagnosis of ancient schwannoma was made on the basis of these results.

Spindle cell mass with focal palisade arrangement, including hypercellular areas (Antoni A fibers) and hypocellular areas (Antoni B fibers) (magnification ×100).

Tumor cells show uniform and intense positivity for S100 protein (magnification ×200).

At 3-month follow-up, the patient was doing well, with no signs of recurrence. Although early follow-up showed no recurrence, annual MRI surveillance is planned for the next 5 years given the potential for delayed recurrence of nerve sheath tumors.

Discussion

Ancient schwannomas, first described by Ackerman et al. in 1951, are a rare variant of schwannomas, which are benign tumors arising from Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system [1,5]. Ancient schwannomas have been reported in various regions of the body, most commonly in the head and neck region [1]. We present a case of ancient schwannoma arising in the basithoracic region, a rare location for this tumor with only a few cases reported in the literature [3,4,9].

When compared to other reported thoracic schwannomas, our case shares similar imaging characteristics with retroperitoneal and mediastinal schwannomas, but differs in its superficial basithoracic location, which facilitated surgical access and reduced operative complexity [10].

Histologically, ancient schwannomas are characterized by encapsulated regions with atypia and nuclear hyperchromatism. As they grow, they may show various degenerative changes such as cystic degeneration and hemorrhage, which can be confused with malignant neoplasms such as sarcoma [1,11]. Ancient schwannomas may mimic malignancy due to cystic degeneration and nuclear atypia, leading to preoperative diagnostic uncertainty. These degenerative changes contribute to the growth and aging of the tumor, hence the term “ancient” schwannoma [12,13]. In addition, ancient schwannomas show areas of high and low cellularity, called Antoni A and Antoni B areas respectively [14]. On immunohistochemistry, ancient schwannomas are positive for S100 [14].

On radiological examination by CT scan, ancient schwannomas appear well-defined, with enhancement after injection of IV contrast. MRI is the most useful modality for further evaluation of ancient schwannomas. Ordinary schwannomas show T1 hypointensity and T2 hyperintensity, corresponding to Antoni B areas [7]. Ancient schwannomas, however, show heterogeneous signal intensity, reflecting the variable amounts of Antoni A and Antoni B areas seen in ancient schwannomas [15,16].

Treatment options vary depending on tumor size, location, and patient symptoms. For smaller, asymptomatic lesions (<3 cm), watchful waiting with annual imaging surveillance may be considered. Radiotherapy may be indicated for incomplete excision in patients not suitable for re-operation, though complete surgical excision remains the gold standard treatment [17]. In selected cases, minimally invasive resection techniques may be considered, though open access was necessary in our case due to tumor size and location.

Complete surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment and offers a favorable prognosis [18]. Recurrence is the most common complication, often following incomplete excision [19,20]. Long-term follow-up is essential as recurrence can occur years after initial treatment.

Conclusion

This case report illustrates the rarity of ancient schwannoma in an unusual basithoracic location, and the value of a rigorous diagnostic approach. The successful surgical management of this rare tumor highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach combining clinical assessment, advanced imaging, and expert surgical technique. Diagnosis is based on clinico-radiological and histopathological correlation. Ancient schwannomas, a rare variant of schwannomas, are benign tumors arising from Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system. Various imaging modalities can be used to detect ancient schwannomas, but MRI remains the most effective. Histopathological evaluation is essential, and is characterized by degenerative changes and areas of Antoni A and B fibers. Complete surgical excision is the treatment of choice and offers an excellent prognosis, though alternative management strategies including watchful waiting may be considered for smaller, asymptomatic lesions. This case report emphasizes that long-term surveillance is recommended given the potential for delayed recurrence.
