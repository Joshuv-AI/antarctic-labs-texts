Empathy and burnout in healthcare professionals: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Rebecca Jane Edwards, Keith Nockels, Jeremy Howick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPrevious reviews have investigated the relationship between empathy and burnout. However, these are now out of date, did not capture the effect of the pandemic, did not include healthcare professionals other than doctors and nurses or medical students, did not assess the impact of differences in profession and did not pool the data, which made estimating the strength of the association unclear. We therefore aim to address these shortcomings in an up-to-date, rigorous, systematic review and meta-analysis.Methods and analysisFindings will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and flowchart.We will search American Psychological Association (APA) PsycINFO, APA PsycArticles, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), The Cochrane Library, PubMed and Scopus. We will also search ResearchSquare and Social Science Research Network (SSRN) for preprints; ProQuest Dissertations and Theses and Electronic Theses Online Service for relevant theses. Forward and backward citation searches will identify additional studies. Two independent reviewers will screen titles, abstracts and full texts and extract data. Two independent reviewers will assess risk of bias using Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) for randomised controlled trials, Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) for non-randomised interventional studies and Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Exposures (ROBINS-E) for observational studies.For all included studies, we will summarise the study characteristics, including number of participants; health profession, specialty and career stage; country and gender. If data are suitable, we will pool results and conduct subgroup analyses (including by health profession, career stage and clinical specialty). We will also explore the relationships between subscales of empathy and burnout. We will use metaregression to explore the impact of theoretically derived factors (such as study design and profession) on the strength of the association. Sensitivity analyses will assess the impact of low-quality research. In our discussion, we will summarise results, the limitations and provide a general interpretation of the results and implications.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this review, as primary data will not be collected. The review will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and presentation at conferences.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251075618.Keywords: Empathy, Burnout, Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, Health Workforce

Introduction

Rationale

Empathy in healthcare refers to the process of generating understanding between a clinician and a patient through communication combined with helpful action.1 Empathic healthcare benefits medical students and clinicians by reducing burnout2,5 and patients6 by increasing patient satisfaction7 and reducing avoidable patient harm.8 9

Meanwhile, evidence suggests that clinician burnout causes adverse outcomes for patients10 and clinicians,11 with high levels of burnout impacting the quality of patient care,10 doctor–patient relationships12 and patient safety.13 Burnout is often a response to chronic stressors at work and is characterised by feelings of emotional and physical exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy, lack of accomplishment and detachment from the job.14 The link between empathy and burnout is also supported by the fact that the decline in empathy as medical students progress through their studies15 is accompanied by an increase in burnout.16

Three systematic reviews have investigated the relationship between empathy and burnout.2 4 17 Wilkinson et al’s 2017 study identified 10 studies and found a clear inverse relationship between empathy and burnout. However, this review is now out of date, with numerous studies published since then.18,22 Additionally, the authors may have missed studies because they excluded mixed-methods studies with potentially relevant data; they did not search the grey literature; they excluded non-English publications; and it is unclear whether their search strategy included subject index terms.

A more recent review published in 2023 did pool the data,17 and they aimed to investigate the associations between the components of empathy and burnout. This left the nature of the overall relationship unclear. While 23 studies met their eligibility criteria, only five were included in their meta-analysis due to a lack of available data on the association between subscales, and three were excluded because they only reported overall empathy. Therefore, we aim to complement this review with a broader research question and wider population to describe the relationship between empathy and burnout, both overall and between subscales. Additionally, they may have missed studies because the authors excluded non-English and non-Spanish papers; did not search grey literature and did not include ‘empathy’ nor ‘burnout’ in their search terms. Indeed, our scoping search revealed several potentially relevant studies that might have been included.23,25

Even more recently, Cairns et al examined the relationship between empathy and burnout in medical students.4 They found that greater empathy, especially cognitive empathy, is associated with lower burnout. While this review provided useful information for the medical school setting, it lacks direct relevance to practising clinicians.

None of the reviews considered differences between professions, specialties and career grade. For example, while Wilkinson et al’s2 and Delgado et al’s17 populations of interest were health professionals, they pooled only doctors and nurses across specialties and career stages. Yet, evidence suggests that rates of empathy and burnout vary across clinical specialties26 27 and career stages.28,30 Moreover, there appears to be a relationship between empathy and specialty preference in medical students,31 and empathy training may differ between specialisations. For example, psychiatry communication skills training often involves empathic components,32 with empathy playing a crucial role in the therapeutic relationship.33 In contrast, surgery may lack formal training in empathic communication.34 This highlights the need to consider the empathy–burnout relationship across all healthcare professions, as current evidence may not generalise. The development of knowledge around these factors in relation to empathy and burnout could drive curriculum development in undergraduate and postgraduate medical education, ensuring empathic skill development across the healthcare setting.

An updated review with meta-analysis can address limitations in existing syntheses of the empathy–burnout relationship.

Eligibility criteria

GP, general practitioner.

Study designs

We will include studies quantifying the relationship between empathy and burnout, including mixed-methods (with suitable quantitative data), observational and interventional study designs. Studies reporting only qualitative methods will be excluded.

Participants

We will include studies investigating empathy and burnout in any healthcare professionals. Informal caregivers or individuals who do not provide healthcare services within a professional healthcare setting will be excluded.

Exposures

The term ‘exposures’ in systematic reviews is generally applied to exposure–outcome relationships in epidemiological studies, but we adopt it here to structure our review of correlational evidence where no causal relationship is implied. The exposures of interest are empathy and burnout, measured by any standardised and validated scale (eg, Jefferson Scale of Empathy and Maslach Burnout Inventory).

Comparators

For studies that have interventions with comparators, the comparators will be usual care (eg, no empathy or well-being intervention).

Outcomes

The outcome of interest is the relationship between empathy and burnout, reported using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) or other statistic that can be converted to r. If sufficient studies report Spearman’s ρ, separate analyses will be performed for this subset but will be excluded from the primary analysis.

Setting

Participants must be practising health professionals who provide care in a healthcare setting. This may include, but is not limited to, hospitals, general practitioner surgeries, care delivered in the home and other therapeutic settings. This does not include informal caregivers, even if care is provided in a professional healthcare setting (eg, a parent caring for a child in a hospital setting).

Language

There will be no restrictions on language.

Information sources

We will search APA PsycINFO (via EBSCO database platform), APA PsycArticles (via EBSCO), CINAHL (via EBSCO), The Cochrane Library, PubMed and Scopus. To ensure inclusion of grey literature, we will search ResearchSquare and SSRN for preprints and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses and Electronic Theses Online Service for relevant theses. We will perform forward and backward citation searches to identify additional studies.

Study records: data management

Literature search results will be collated in EndNote 2136 and imported to The Evidence Research Accelerator (TERA).37

We will use TERA’s The Deduplicator38 tool to identify duplicates. All studies identified as potential duplicates will be checked by a member of the research team before removal.

Study records: selection process

The review team will develop and pilot initial screening questions and/or forms based on the inclusion criteria.

Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts using Rayyan.39 The AI tool MechaScreener37 will be used to check human decisions. Full reports will be obtained for all titles that appear to meet the inclusion criteria or where there is uncertainty around whether they meet the inclusion criteria. Two independent reviewers will screen the full-text reports and decide whether the identified studies meet the inclusion criteria. Additional information will be sought from study authors where necessary to resolve eligibility uncertainty.

Where studies are reported in a language other than English, the screening team will translate them if the language is known or use a translation tool (eg, Google Translate40 or DeepL).41

Disagreements will be resolved by consensus between reviewers or, if required, through consultation with JH. Rationales for study exclusion will be recorded.

Study records: data collection process

Extraction forms will be developed with feedback from piloting within the review team. The form fields will be based on the inclusion criteria table (table 1), study characteristics and methodology, demographic information (profession, clinical specialty and career stage), outcomes and attributes of interest and intervention details.

Data will be extracted by two independent researchers. For up to 30 papers, all data entries will be checked. For a larger review of up to 100 papers, 30 randomly selected data entries will be checked; for over 100, 50 random entries will be checked. Entries for checking will be selected using a random number generator. If a large number of significant errors (judged by RJE) are identified, an additional 10% of reviews will be checked, and this process will be repeated.

To avoid double-counting, prioritised outcomes (see section ‘Outcomes and prioritisation’) for each research question and subgroup analysis will be labelled in a separate column during extraction.

Outcomes and prioritisation

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome will be the correlational relationship between empathy and burnout, reported as Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), then transformed to Fisher’s z for analysis.42

If Pearson’s correlation is reported without an r value, r will be calculated from reported t values and sample sizes or df. Where alternative measures of correlation (eg, Phi coefficient, Standardised beta) are reported, they will be converted to r if possible and reasonable. Other calculable correlation measures (and statistics used to calculate r) include:

Bivariate correlation (direction of association, p value, sample size)

Bivariate correlation (direction of association, p value, sample size)

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)/regression with one continuous predictor (F value, sample size)

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)/regression with one continuous predictor (F value, sample size)

Simple linear regression (beta coefficient)

Simple linear regression (beta coefficient)

If r cannot be calculated, the study will be excluded.

Where multiple measurements of empathy and burnout are available, such as in an interventional study, we will prioritise preintervention measurements or the first measurement in a time series. Where multiple measurements are provided at the first time point (eg, parallel group design), we will pool baseline measurements (if they are preintervention), ignoring arm allocation. In the case of multiple publications of the same trial that share a control group, the number of participants for the control group will be divided by the number of published studies of that trial included in the review. This will avoid double-counting participants and avoid arbitrarily inflating the weight of one publication over another.

Empathy may be reported ‘objectively’ (ie, patient-observed empathy) or ‘subjectively’ (ie, self-reported by clinicians). Where multiple measurements are provided, we will extract all data but prioritise observed empathy for primary analysis. Given sufficient data, we will perform subgroup analysis for observed and perceived empathy and include these factors in meta-regression analyses.

Measures reporting relationships between overall or global scores for both empathy and burnout will be included in the primary analysis. This will avoid conflation between overall constructs and subscales, preventing overestimation of prevalent subscales and improving interpretability. Where scales report domains of work-, client- and personal burnout (eg, Copenhagen Burnout Inventory), work-related burnout will be prioritised. This domain aligns with the context of this work and aligns with the purpose of many burnout scales. For example, the Maslach Burnout Inventory and Oldenburg Burnout Inventory explicitly reference work within their items. Prioritisation of domains within a scale that may represent a global aspect of empathy or burnout, and require a decision of prioritisation, will be discussed within the review team, with a final decision from a content expert (JH) where consensus cannot be reached. We will extract relationships between subscales for the secondary analysis of empathy and burnout domains and the metaregression.

We will consider whether missing data impact the suitability of study data to be included in analysis (eg, if a study reports a profession but not specialties, they may be excluded from the specialty subgroup analysis but included in other analyses). Where outcomes are reported across groups identified for the subgroup analysis, we will exclude these from the subgroup analysis (eg, studies reporting across newly qualified doctors and nurses will be included in the overall analysis but excluded from the profession subgroup analysis). We will classify career stage and specialty within each profession, guided by wider literature, professional guidelines and subject expert opinion.

Secondary outcomes

Where Spearman’s ρ is reported and a sufficient number of studies are identified, we will convert these values to Fisher’s z and analyse separately to the Pearson’s r studies, as this index cannot reasonably be converted to Pearson’s r.

We will conduct additional meta-analyses to explore the relationships between the domains of empathy (cognitive, affective and behavioural) and burnout (emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and personal accomplishment). Similarly to Cairns’4 approach, for empathy, we will classify overall scales and subscales as either overall, cognitive, affective, behavioural or a combination of these facets. Studies reporting only overall scores or combined facets for empathy will be excluded from this analysis. For burnout measured by scales other than the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), where possible, we will map similar components of these alternative burnout tools to the facets of MBI. If enough studies report domains of work-related, personal and emotional burnout, we will repeat analyses for these domains of burnout. Studies will be excluded from this analysis if facets cannot be related or only overall scores are reported. Similarly to Cairns’,4 outcomes of these analyses will be presented in a table. Given sufficient studies, we will conduct subgroup analyses for profession, specialty and career grade.

We will use metaregression to explore the impact of identified subgroup factors (ie, profession, specialty, career grade), study design and other factors identified as potentially contributing to heterogeneity (eg, subscales). If sufficient studies are identified, we will repeat this for Spearman’s ρ studies.

Effect measures

We plan to analyse correlational outcomes by converting correlation coefficients to Fisher’s z for meta-analysis. Where available, we will include 95% CIs provided by the included literature. Otherwise, we will calculate 95% CIs from the Fisher’s z values. For the summary of effects, Fisher’s z values will be converted back to the original correlation coefficient for interpretability.

Risk of bias assessment

For each selected study, risk-of-bias will be assessed independently by two reviewers, prior to data synthesis. Where possible, we will use tools recommended by Cochrane for each study type,43 44 including the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Exposures (ROBINS-E) tool45 for observational studies, Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) Version 246 for non-randomised interventional studies and Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2)47 for randomised-controlled trials. If the selected tools are inappropriate for some identified studies, the approach to risk of bias assessment for these studies will be discussed by the research team, and whether we can adapt a Cochrane-recommended tool or if using an alternative tool would be most appropriate. We intend to adapt the ROBINS-E tool for use with cross-sectional studies investigating correlations between variables (empathy) without exposure to an independent variable and use this adaptation where appropriate.

Disagreements will first be addressed through discussion to reach consensus; where consensus cannot be reached, a third researcher will consult to reach a decision. We will create a graphic representation of potential bias within and across studies using robvis.48

Synthesis methods

Synthesis

A random-effects meta-analysis, with Restricted Maximum Likelihood to estimate between-study variance, will be the primary model. This approach accounts for expected heterogeneity resulting from, for example, differences in study designs, analytic methods and participant characteristics between studies.

All quantitative analyses will be performed with R,49 using either the metafor50 or meta51 packages. We will consider a two-tailed p value of 0.05 as significant for all hypothesis testing, except for 0.10 for the test of heterogeneity and reporting biases. We will use the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach52 53 to guide presentation and interpretation of findings.

Exploring heterogeneity

We will investigate heterogeneity via both visual (forest plots) and statistical methods. We will report the heterogeneity of variance (τ2) and inconsistency (I2). To establish the appropriateness of quantitative synthesis, we will consider the threshold of I2 >75% as signs of considerable heterogeneity and also consider the evidence of this heterogeneity through uncertainty intervals and localisation on forest plots. Heterogeneity in the primary outcome will be explored through subgroup, sensitivity and metaregression analyses.

Summary of findings

We will present meta-analyses and metaregression outcomes with forest plots and a table for the subscale analysis. Important sensitivity analysis outcomes will be presented in-text, with full results provided in online supplemental materials. Publication bias will be presented visually with funnel plots.

We will include a summary of findings table to collectively report each element of the proposed analysis in line with Cochrane standards (adapted for use with correlational non-intervention studies) and using the GRADE approach.52 As this review investigates correlational relationships and not interventional impact, we will adapt some elements of the Cochrane criteria to best reflect the nature of this work and the presented evidence.

Qualitative summary

If pooling is not appropriate, we will include a systematic narrative synthesis of findings and tables to summarise and explain the characteristics and findings of the included studies. The narrative synthesis will explore the relationship between empathy and burnout within and between included studies and differences/similarities in the correlational relationship of empathy and burnout between the identified subgroups (profession, specialty and career stage).

Metabias assessment

Reporting bias will be explored with funnel plots, including across included studies, within identified subgroups and between peer-reviewed and grey literature, where ≥10 studies are available.

We will evaluate selective reporting of outcomes through the risk of bias tools, including investigation of disparities between measured and reported outcomes and comparing trial published outcomes to trial protocols or registries. We will also investigate whether the inclusion of all variables was justified, and whether posthoc/exploratory results are presented as a-priori decisions.

We will also evaluate the potential impact of conflicts of interest on differences in study design and results. Concerns will be noted during the risk of bias assessment and reported in the characteristics of included studies table.

Certainty assessment

We will adapt GRADE for prognostic factors52 54 to assess certainty across the body of evidence for key outcomes, as this approach focuses more on observational data and summarisation of an effect within a population rather than studies of interventions. We will provide ratings of either high, moderate, low or very low confidence that the observed effect estimates are close to true effects. As with evidence from prognostic factors, it is likely that observational studies provide the best estimate of effect within a population, as randomised-controlled trials often impose restrictions on participation.55 To establish the initial rating, we will assess the rates of different study designs within the analysis. If observational studies are predominant and other study designs are unlikely to substantially affect the observed estimate (evidenced by sensitivity analyses), we will start with ‘high’ certainty in the evidence.54 For each domain, we will judge whether there are ‘not serious’ (no change in quality), ‘serious’ (downgrade one level) or ‘critical’ concerns (downgrade two levels) to reach a final judgement of the overall quality of evidence. Reasons for downgrading will be indicated in the summary of findings table.

Rating of risk of bias will be informed by study-level assessments appropriate to each study design (ie, ROBINS-E for observational data and RoB 2 for randomised-controlled trials). We will also consider whether the removal of high risk of bias studies impacts the estimate of the effect.

Inconsistency will be assessed based on the variability of the magnitude and direction of reported associations across studies. We will consider I2, with consideration of the sample sizes and consistency of study effect sizes and CIs through evaluation of forest plots, as I2 is often large for observational studies with large sample sizes. We will also consider the extent to which heterogeneity is explained through subgroup, sensitivity and metaregression analyses. We will downgrade the evidence if substantial heterogeneity remains.

Imprecision will be evaluated from CIs of the pooled estimates and the total sample size.

Indirectness will be judged by the relevance of study populations and settings to the review question. We will not consider measurement instruments, as we are only including standardised and validated measures of empathy and burnout. We will consider whether the sample is likely representative (eg, are all health professions represented?).

We will assess publication bias and small-study effects by inspecting funnel plots of Fisher’s z-transformed correlations for asymmetry and using Egger’s test, where ≥10 studies are available. We will consider whether small studies tend to report larger effects and investigate their impact with sensitivity analyses. Evidence of asymmetry or small-study effects will be used to downgrade certainty in this domain.

We will not rate certainty upward based on criteria related to causal inference (eg, dose-response), and findings will be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Certainty ratings will reflect confidence in the presence, magnitude and consistency of the association across studies.
