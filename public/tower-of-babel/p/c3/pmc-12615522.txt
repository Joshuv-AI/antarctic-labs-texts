Advancing Precision Medicine in Inflammatory Skin Disease
Authors: Michelle Yuan, Jinwoo Lee, Mark Taylor, Raymond J. Cho, Jeffrey B. Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The growing availability of targeted immunomodulatory therapies has transformed the treatment landscape for chronic inflammatory skin diseases. However, treatment selection remains largely empirical, often guided more by trial-and-error and insurance mandates than by an individual patient’s underlying disease biology. This disconnect between therapeutic strategy and the need to address and calibrate for patient molecular heterogeneity undermines clinical outcomes and contributes to inefficiency in care delivery. Precision medicine offers a solution by tailoring diagnosis and treatment to the molecular and cellular features of each patient’s skin disease. In this Current Opinion, we outline key clinical contexts where precision approaches can be transformative: diagnostic ambiguity, selecting treatments for an established diagnosis, and selecting treatments without a defined diagnosis or disease mechanism. We highlight advances in precision techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics that enable more refined skin disease classification and accurate prediction of drug response. Although several challenges remain before these techniques can be widely adopted, such as limited biomarker validation, high costs, and a lack of breadth in research cohorts, we argue that their potential benefits, for patients, clinicians, and the broader field of dermatologic care, substantially outweigh the associated costs. We advocate for expanded funding, population-based research, and scalable diagnostics to successfully integrate precision medicine into dermatology. By combining molecular phenotyping with traditional clinicopathologic diagnosis, precision medicine can reduce therapeutic inefficiency, improve patient outcomes, and redefine care paradigms in chronic inflammatory skin disease.

Introduction to Precision Medicine for Inflammatory Skin Disease

Medications inhibiting specific inflammatory pathways are now so commonly prescribed to treat chronic skin conditions that precisely matching drug to disease presentations is deterministic in patient outcomes. Despite a growing arsenal of targeted immunomodulatory therapies, treatment selection remains largely empirical. Within well-defined disease categories, molecular heterogeneity leads to varying drug responses. In other cases, overlapping clinicopathologic features complicate diagnosis and medication selection. As a result, therapeutic decisions are often shaped more by trial-and-error and insurance constraints than by underlying biology. This disconnect between disease heterogeneity and therapeutic strategy not only undermines clinical outcomes, it also contributes to escalating costs, as biologic medications represent a substantial share of prescription drug expenditures [1].

Precision medicine offers a framework to address these challenges by tailoring diagnosis and treatment to the key molecular features of each patient’s disease. We identify three clinical contexts where such approaches can be transformative. First, overlapping features of distinct conditions (such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis) complicate diagnosis in some cases and increase the risk of erroneous drug selection (Fig. 1A). Second, as in most cases where the diagnosis is clear (e.g., psoriasis), therapeutic response varies. While many patients with psoriasis respond to either tumor necrosis factor (TNF) or interleukin (IL)-23 inhibitors, a subset responds to neither. Identifying these individuals in advance could avoid delays in effective care (Fig. 1B). Finally, for clinical presentations lacking a clear diagnosis or diseases without a defined mechanism, molecular testing can provide partial yet actionable insight into disease presentations whose etiology is unclear. In this Current Opinion, we consider the emergence of precision approaches to solve these dilemmas in chronic inflammatory cutaneous disease. We argue for the development and increasing utilization of clinically tractable precision medicine assays to improve patient outcomes and decrease wasted drug expenditures.

Diagnostic Ambiguity in Inflammatory Skin Disease

Cases of inflammatory skin disease frequently display morphologic and microscopic features corresponding to more than one standard disease category [2]. With multiple targeted treatments now available to treat defined diseases such as atopic dermatitis and plaque psoriasis, correctly classifying such ambiguous cases takes on greater importance. For instance, precision medicine can enable early targeted intervention, helping patients become ‘super-responders’ who achieve rapid, complete, and sustained skin clearance [3–5]. Yet the very atypical features and heterogeneity of ambiguous cases typically exclude them from investigation in pharmaceutical drug trials and government-funded research initiatives, which both demand well-defined disease.

Such ambiguous cases likely represent at least two major phenomenological classes. Some simply illustrate atypical presentations of a canonical disease, resisting accurate diagnosis in the absence of more accurate clinical tests. Other cases with contradictory features may represent authentically complex and mixed pathology, arising from multiple molecular abnormalities, rather than the narrow derangements underlying a classic disease definition.

Precision diagnostics have been proposed for the frequent cases that show overlapping features of atopic dermatitis and psoriasis. In 2014, microarray analysis, an oligonucleotide probe-based technique used to measure RNA expression for many genes at once, was applied to patients diagnosed with psoriasis, who also harbored some form of endogenous or reactive eczema [6]. By comparing messenger RNA profiles from disparate lesions in the same patient, transcripts specific to each condition were identified: elevated inducible nitric oxide synthase/nitric oxide synthase 2 (iNOS/NOS2) expression in psoriasis and CCL27 expression in eczematous disease [6]. This minimalist signature distinguished disease state in a small cohort of ambiguous cases. The NOS2 result was replicated in a 2021 study applying conventional RNA sequencing, a sequencing-based technique used to measure gene expression by quantitating RNA expression levels for all genes at once across a submitted tissue or cell sample, to tape stripped lesional skin differentiating atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, in addition to expected elevations of IL-17A in psoriasis and IL-13 in atopic dermatitis [7]. Similar findings for elevated IL-17A and IL-13 in psoriasis and atopic dermatitis-affected skin, respectively, were identified utilizing RNA in situ hybridization, a technique that uses labeled nucleic acid probes to localize and detect the expression of a sequence of RNA in a tissue section [8]. However, another group performed immunohistochemical analysis, which visualizes the presence and location of specific proteins in tissue sections by using antigen-specific antibodies, of T helper (Th)-2 and Th17 cells in psoriasis or atopic dermatitis and found that only interleukin (IL)-36 elevation in psoriasis could reliably distinguish the two states [2]. These small cohort studies suggest potential biomarkers that could be used to guide diagnosis and treatment in clinically ambiguous cases. Larger scale validation studies supporting widespread clinical utilization have yet to be reported.

Clinically ambiguous cases may also represent authentic instances of complex inflammatory disease with hybrid combinations of aberrant signaling pathways rather than simply atypical presentations of a known class. Prospectively identifying such cases would nominate them for treatment by emerging drug classes that act more generally than a single-cytokine blockade. For example, small-molecule drugs such as Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors more broadly inhibit inflammatory signaling in a range of T and myeloid cells [9]. Bi-specific and combination antibody treatments are being tested as means to raise the efficacy ceiling in diseases such as atopic dermatitis [10, 11]. In skin, OX40/OX40L inhibitors promise the ability to more widely restrain the skin-resident T-cell populations whose pathogenic reprogramming appears central to both psoriasis and atopic dermatitis [12].

Role of Single-Cell RNA Sequencing in Precision Diagnostics

The emergence of single-cell technologies in the early 2020s introduced far higher resolution in distinguishing cutaneous disease states. Our group has utilized single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), a sequencing-based technique that quantitates numerous transcripts on a single-cell level allowing assignment of expression changes for specific cell types, to develop disease-specific multi-gene signatures in skin-resident T cells (Fig. 2) [13, 14]. We applied these signatures in a case study of a patient with overlapping clinicopathologic features of both psoriasis vulgaris and atopic dermatitis [15]. The patient had not responded to empirical treatment with standard drugs for psoriasis (adalimumab, a TNF inhibitor) as well as atopic dermatitis (dupilumab, an IL-4 receptor- α inhibitor). However, scRNA-seq analysis for this patient identified a skin resident memory T-cell transcriptomic signature matching that of plaque psoriasis. In another recent case, we reported evidence for mixed pathology in a patient with resistant hand dermatitis with features of both atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, who had not improved with TNF, IL-23, or IL-4 receptor-α blockade. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, a tissue section-based technique that measures RNA expression for many genes at once while preserving spatial context, was performed on lesional biopsies and revealed mixed transcriptomic patterns of both diseases. The patient was therefore treated with topical JAK inhibition given its broader inhibitory effect on both atopic dermatitis and psoriasis-related inflammatory cytokines, leading to rapid disease improvement [16]. Thus, these “multi-pathway” disease instances (e.g., with upregulation of both Th2 and Th17 pathways) represent an opportunity to guide drugs advancing a broader molecular blockade to specific patients.

The rapid adoption of scRNA-seq technology has expanded precision studies past the psoriasis-atopic dermatitis dilemma. IL-18 has emerged as a key biomarker in dermatomyositis across several studies [17, 18]. In a related advancement, transcriptomic comparisons were able to differentiate histopathologically indistinguishable cases of cutaneous lupus erythematosus and dermatomyositis based on an IL-18-containing 5-gene signature in the latter, shown to reside in keratinocytes by scRNA-seq [19]. These findings underscore IL-18 as a potential diagnostic marker for dermatomyositis and highlight the broader role of molecular profiling in resolving ambiguous skin diseases.

In 2024, a scRNA-seq analysis of lesional parapsoriasis, a psoriasiform dermatitis long debated to represent cases of eczema in transition to malignancy, revealed greater levels of T-cell clonality in larger plaque disease, mirroring the pattern observed in early-stage mycosis fungoides [20]. This report suggested not only that clinical concern for smaller plaque psoriasis might be downgraded, but also nominated an analytic approach to clarify ambiguous cases.

Role of Precision Medicine in Selecting Targeted Treatments for Established Diagnosis

In chronic skin diseases such as psoriasis vulgaris and atopic dermatitis, both of which are treated by multiple US Food and Drug Administration-approved targeted drugs, patients in the United States often face insurance-mandated treatment protocols that require trying older, less expensive medications first. For example, patients with psoriasis are commonly required to demonstrate poor response to TNF inhibitors such as adalimumab before accessing newer, more effective options such as IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors. These aging, stepwise approaches force patients to endure side effects or inadequate responses before they can receive optimal treatment. Ideally, clinical tests would predict which patients are unlikely to respond to certain drug classes before therapy begins, sparing them from unnecessary exposure and treatment delay.

Early efforts to identify predictive biomarkers in psoriasis focused on disease-linked alleles such as HLA-C*06:02. However, follow-up studies have yielded conflicting results, without a consistent predictive trend [21–23]. Other genetic polymorphisms, including variants in GLYR4, ZNF816A, CTNNA2, IL12B, MAP3K1, GBP6, and ZNF816A have been associated with responses to TNF inhibitors, but findings varied depending on the timing of the treatment response assessment [24, 25]. More recent work has identified cellular biomarkers, such as high levels of LPS-induced NF-κBp65 phosphorylation in type-2 dendritic cells, which correlate with poor responses to adalimumab [26]. Our recent studies suggest that failure of an IL-23 blockade may arise from persistently active T17 skin-resident memory cells, providing a rationale for downstream IL-17 inhibition as a salvage therapy [27].

In atopic dermatitis, emerging biomarkers linked to a dupilumab therapeutic response include increased IL-13 expression in lesional skin detected by RNA in situ hybridization [28], as well as low baseline blood eosinophil counts and serum lactate dehydrogenase levels [29]. Predictive biomarker analyses were incorporated into the phase IIb trial of the IL-13 inhibitor tralokinumab, in which elevated baseline serum levels of IL-13 signaling markers such as dipeptidyl peptidase-4 and periostin correlated with a greater clinical improvement [30]. However, both the above psoriasis and these atopic dermatitis findings have yet to be validated in larger cohorts.

In clinical oncology, clinically useful molecular tests usually rely on multi-transcript signatures derived from genome-wide analyses, rather than single-gene markers. For example, the MammaPrint assay, a 70-transcript signature test, predicts recurrence and metastasis risk in breast cancer [31]. Until recently, similar transcriptome-wide biomarker approaches in inflammatory skin diseases were limited by the technical challenges of profiling distinct pathogenic cell types such as T cells, antigen-presenting cells, and keratinocytes at sufficient resolution. High-resolution technologies such as scRNA-seq now herald a transformative shift. By sequencing at single-cell resolution, scRNA-seq has revealed abnormalities in immune and stromal cell subpopulations that were previously obscured by bulk profiling, as recently demonstrated in skin-resident T cells (Fig. 2) [32]. These insights may finally enable the identification of drug response-relevant endotypes, or molecularly defined disease subtypes within chronic skin conditions, an aspirational model to subdivide patients based on a suite of objective molecular features [33].

Utilization of Precision Techniques to Select Treatment Without a Diagnosis or Disease Mechanism

For rarer inflammatory skin diseases, understanding of disease etiology is often limited. Moreover, some cases of skin inflammation diverge clinically from established categories, defying formal diagnosis and further complicating treatment decisions. In such scenarios, molecular approaches can sometimes identify targetable abnormalities, nominating existing drugs for treatment in a subset of these challenging cases. An increasing number of case reports illustrate the feasibility and promise of this precision medicine strategy.

For example, RNA in situ hybridization implicated IL-13 overproduction in eosinophilic dermatosis of hematologic malignancy, a rare and difficult-to-treat rash associated with hematologic disorders [34]. Separately, a case report described a patient with eosinophilic dermatosis of hematologic malignancy who responded to dupilumab, supporting the role of Th2 cytokines and targeting of IL-13 as a rational treatment option [35]. In another instance, cutaneous scRNA-seq profiling of a patient with an atypical, refractory drug hypersensitivity rash, unresponsive to high-dose prednisone, intravenous immunoglobulin, and the TNF inhibitor etanercept, revealed upregulation of the druggable JAK3/STAT1 pathway in a discrete T-cell subpopulation [36]. Treatment with the JAK inhibitor tofacitinib led to successful resolution of the patient’s symptoms. Similarly, in the inherited skin condition autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis (loss-of-function), standard bulk RNA-sequencing showed increased JAK-STAT signaling in affected skin. Based on these findings and the limited treatment options available, the patient was trialed on tofacitinib, resulting in a significant improvement in erythema, fissuring, itch, and transepidermal water loss [37].

Two recent molecular profiling studies highlighted aberrant IL-1B signaling in subsets of patients with Sweet’s syndrome and pityriasis rubra pilaris (PRP). Transcriptomic microarray profiling of affected skin from a patient with Sweet’s syndrome refractory to corticosteroid-sparing agents revealed elevated IL-1B production, with subsequent remission achieved using the IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra [38]. Although the exact mechanisms and inflammatory pathways in Sweet’s syndrome remain unclear, this patient’s transcriptomic profile differed from that of seven other patients with Sweet’s syndrome, limiting the ability to generalize treatment based on this data. Targeted bulk RNA sequencing of PRP lesional skin, compared to psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and healthy controls, revealed an increased expression of IL-1B and CCL20 unique to PRP. The underlying dysfunctional pathways in PRP remain unknown, and the disease is notoriously difficult to treat. Thus, three patients were trialed on anakinra leading to a 50% improvement in cutaneous disease and normalization of the aberrant transcriptional signature within 3 weeks [39].

These cases underscore the growing utility of molecular precision medicine to identify specific, targetable dysregulated inflammatory pathways and elicit clinical improvement in cases that previously would likely have eluded empiric treatments. Such uncommon and diagnostically challenging cases are particularly well suited to global profiling techniques capable of uncovering unexpected molecular abnormalities.

Considerations for Advancing Precision Medicine in Dermatology

While precision medicine shows transformative potential in the clinical contexts previously described, several challenges must be addressed before these approaches can be integrated broadly into dermatologic practice.

Lack of Established Biomarkers

Currently, most dermatological conditions lack robustly validated biomarkers, limiting the broad applicability of precision medicine. Databases such as CENTRAL, Embase, LILACS, and MEDLINE provide valuable resources for identifying biomarkers linked to treatment response [40]. However, inconsistent study designs, selective outcome reporting, and inadequate control for confounders undermine the clinical utility of many proposed biomarkers. Initiatives such as the BIOMarkers in Atopic Dermatitis and Psoriasis (BIOMAP) project aim to overcome these challenges by curating and analyzing large-scale clinical and molecular datasets, enabling earlier disease intervention and more personalized care [41]. Complementing these efforts, a recent transcriptional cross-comparison study of inflammatory skin diseases analyzed functional gene and pathway groups (immune modules) to build a molecular map of disease pathogenesis [42]. By proposing core immune modules driving disease, this such meta-analyses generate testable hypotheses regarding disease progression mechanisms.

More Scalable Alternatives

Given the high cost, technical complexity, and limited accessibility of advanced methods such as scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, economical alternatives such as targeted polymerase chain reaction gene panels or immunohistochemistry assays remain more realistic for widespread clinical practice. Reformatting approaches assessing thousands of genes to smaller marker sets subclassifying inflamed skin into targetable pathways could prioritize targeted medications currently available only after failure of older drugs. Strategies such as value-based pricing models, government subsidies, and streamlined prior authorization could help mitigate the financial and logistical burdens associated with widespread utilization of future molecular approaches.

Generalizability

With rapid technological advances, both private and public funding bodies must integrate sophisticated biomarkers into clinical trials evaluating immunomodulatory drug responses. To maximize clinical utility, these molecular assays must reliably detect disease-related perturbations across different age groups and ancestral backgrounds. Because many major disease-associated genes were identified primarily in European populations, expanding molecular studies to non-European populations is essential for refining treatment strategies within specific subgroups [43, 44]. This challenge can be addressed by having a broader genetic recruitment in clinical trials, expanding biobanks to include more data from overlooked populations, and developing ancestry-specific panels and therapeutic tools. For instance, the All of Us Research Program works to improve participation in biomedical research by enrolling over one million individuals across the USA from disparate backgrounds [45]. This initiative has generated a genomic biobank with more than 1 billion genetic variants identified, including over 275 million previously unreported variants, enabling more generalizable biomarker and assay development that can be integrated into clinical trials.

Financial Considerations

An underlying dilemma in addressing atypical presentations of canonical skin disease is the lack of economic incentive for pharmaceutical companies to develop precision diagnostics to accompany therapeutics. In molecular oncology, the deadly highly heterogeneous nature of cancers compels the matching of drugs to patient-level disease. However, in chronic inflammatory disease, which range from the skin to the gut to the joint, the consequences of empirical drug therapy failures remain less visible. Drug manufacturers may have limited strategic cause to limit such empiric failures of relatively safe drugs, which contribute to overall usage [46–48]. Thus, we argue that advancement of precision diagnostics in inflammatory disease should now represent cardinal priorities of governmental funding agencies, whose missions naturally encompass cost efficiency in treatment delivery. Dissemination of such tools would not only reduce wasted expenditure, but also alleviate the invisible yet vast patient morbidity arising from failed empirical drug treatment.

Looking Forward Toward a More Precise Future in Dermatology

To realize the full promise of precision dermatology, coordinated efforts across research, clinical practice, regulatory policy, and funding priorities are essential. Emphasizing broad participation, developing scalable diagnostics, and validating robust biomarkers will ensure that precision medicine not only improves outcomes but also becomes accessible and beneficial for the wide range of patients with inflammatory skin diseases.

Furthermore, we argue that the broader adoption of precision diagnostics in clinical practice hinges on the integration of molecular phenotyping with traditional clinicopathologic subtyping. While classification based on clinical morphologic and histopathologic features, disease progression, and symptoms remains important for identifying disease subtypes, combining this with molecular biomarkers provides the precision needed to refine prognosis and personalize treatment. By uniting these complementary approaches, clinicians can tailor interventions to each patient’s unique disease profile, optimizing efficacy, minimizing unnecessary exposure to ineffective therapies, and ultimately delivering improved and individualized dermatologic care.

Conclusions

The expanding array of immunomodulatory therapies has led to treatment variability shaped largely by insurance policies and drug marketing rather than biology, especially in the USA. This metastable landscape risks perpetuating inefficiency and variations in the quality of care. Dermatologists must advocate for a deliberate shift toward precision medicine, investing in molecular diagnostics and rational drug selection, to ensure that treatment decisions for chronic inflammatory skin diseases are evidence based, personalized, and consistent for all.
