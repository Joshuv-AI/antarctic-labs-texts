Colorimetric sensing of fatty acids in organic solvents using polydiacetylene-based nanocomposites
Authors: Nannapat Kaewnukulkit, Nisanart Traiphol, Rakchart Traiphol
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorimetric detection of long chain fatty acids by polydiacetylenes (PDAs)-based sensors is quite a challenge, mainly due to their relatively low solubility in aqueous media. In this study, we developed PDA/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites in various organic solvents for detecting fatty acids. Oleic acid and steric acid were used as representatives of fatty acids with bent and linear structures, respectively. The nanocomposites dispersed in ethanol exhibited blue-to-red color transition upon increasing the concentration of oleic acid. The use of other solvents including 1-butanol, 1-hexanol, 1-octanol, 1,4-dioxane, hexane, octane and toluene affected the sensitivity of nanocomposites. Furthermore, incorporating cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) into the structure of the nanocomposites resulted in a systematic increase of sensitivity. The use of organic solvents simplified the method and allowed direct sensing of the fatty acids. Paper-based sensor was fabricated and utilized as a mobile test kit. This class of colorimetric sensors can also distinguish the bent and linear structures of fatty acids. Our study extends the utilization of PDA-based materials as colorimetric sensors of fatty acids in food industries.Keywords: Polydiacetylene, Colorimetric sensors, Smart materials, Fatty acids, Colloids

Introduction

One of the three major compositions of food is fatty acid [1,2]. Their structures constitute carbon chains in backbone with a methyl group at one end and a carboxylic group (–COOH) at another end [3]. Fatty acids are mainly divided into two groups, saturated and unsaturated, determined by the backbone structures. The most common fatty acids have a chain length of 16–22 and a double bond with the cis-configuration [4]. The trans-fatty acids in food are mainly generated from ruminant animals (i.e., meat and milk) and oil products [5]. The content of fatty acids and the ratio between unsaturated and saturated fatty acids is an important parameter for determination of nutritional value of certain oil [6]. The newest trend in the food processing industry requires notification for the content of each individual fatty acid [6]. Furthermore, food industry in the last few decades has been shifting from using high content of trans-fatty acids to low or non-trans- products [7]. Fatty acids may cause many effects on human health such as cardiovascular disease [8], fatty liver disease [9], osteoarthritis [10] and high cholesterol [11]. Therefore, the technologies for detecting fatty acids are important. High precision methods including high performance liquid chromatography and gas chromatography have been applied for detecting fatty acids in food samples [12,13]. However, these techniques require expensive instruments and often involve complicated sample preparation. Colorimetric sensors offer an alternative method, providing an easy and low-cost detection of fatty acids.

Polydiacetylenes (PDAs) are smart materials that show colorimetric response to external stimuli [[14], [15], [16]] including temperature [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], acid/base [23,24], biomaterials [[25], [26], [27], [28]], solvents [29] and UV lights [30]. PDA-based colorimetric sensors have been applied in food industry to detect milk spoilage [28], meat spoilage [31] and fruit ripening [32]. Furthermore, PDA-based sensors were utilized for sensing chemical additives in foods such as sodium benzoate [26] and organic acids [33]. The development of colorimetric sensors, that can measure the type and/or quantity of fatty acid, is important for quality control of food products. However, research works relating to this topic are quite rare [34,35]. Commercial PDA with carboxylic headgroup does not respond to fatty acid. Chen et al. modified the PDA with imidazolium (IM) headgroup, which in turn induced blue-to-violet color transition upon exposure to oleic acid [34]. The interactions between the carboxylate headgroup of fatty acid and the positively charged IM headgroup led to color transition. The insertion of fatty acid into PDA bilayer structure also played an important role. It was observed that the stearic acid and elaidic acid with linear alkyl tails did not induce any color transition. Cho et al. utilized a similar strategy by modifying PDA with amine headgroup [35]. However, the modified PDA did not provide sufficient interaction with fatty acid. To increase the sensitivity, PDA sensor had to be prepared from multiple diacetylene monomers with different ratios of amino units in the headgroup. Like the IM-PDA sensor, this system only responded to cis-fatty acids including oleic acid, linoleic acid, and palmitic acid. We point out that fatty acids in these studies were converted to sodium salts to increase interaction with the PDA headgroups and improve their solubility in aqueous media.

Our research group has been developing PDA/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites to improve their color-responsive properties for advanced sensing applications [33,[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. The nanocomposites exhibit reversible thermochromism [36], colorimetric response to acid [33], base [37], UV light [38], cationic surfactants [39], and polymers [40,41]. Furthermore, they can be synthesized in organic solvents [42,43]. In this study, we continue to develop the nanocomposites as sensors for detecting fatty acid in alcohols. Addition of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) can tune the sensitivity. This colorimetric sensor can be utilized for measuring concentrations of both cis- and saturated fatty acids. Our method is quite simple compared to the syntheses of new PDA materials in previous studies, thus, suitable for food industry applications.

Experimental

Diacetylene (DA) monomers (Sigma-Aldrich and Wako Chemical) were 10,12-pentacosadyanoic acid (DA(8,12)), 10,12 tricosadiynoic acid (DA(8,10)) and 4,6-heptadecadiynoic acid (DA(2,10)). Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles were from Nano Material Technology (Thailand). Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), oleic acid, steric acid and all solvents (AR grade) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, KemAus™ (Australia) and Labscan (Thailand).

Using solution-mixing method [37], the PDA/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites were synthesized in mixed water/ethanol solvent. The DA concentration was 1 mM and ZnO/DA ratio was 10 wt%. The blue PDA/Zn2+/ZnO suspensions from the same synthetic batch were dried at 45 °C for 24 h. Organic solvents including ethanol, 1-butanol, 1-hexanol, 1-octanol, 1,4-dioxane, toluene, hexane, and octane were added. Table S1 (supporting information) listed solvent properties. The samples were ultrasonicated for 10 min and stored for 24 h at room temperature. The dried nanocomposites were also dispersed in ethanol solution of CTAB using the same process. The concentration of CTAB varied from 0 to 100 mM. Fatty acids were consecutively added to these suspensions by using micropipette and shaken for 1 min prior to absorption spectra measurement. The nanocomposite suspensions were dropped onto filter papers (1 cm × 1 cm) and dried at room temperature providing solid-state sensors. The solid-state sensors were immersed in ethanol with oleic acid solutions for 30 min. Photographs were taken using a digital camera.

Absorption spectra were recorded by LabTech BlueStar B UV–VIS spectrophotometer. Colorimetric response (%CR) values were calculated according to the previous study [23]. Raman spectrometer (Horiba, XploRA PLUS model, 785 nm laser) was used to examine PDA conjugated backbone and alkyl side chain for conformational changes. Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Thermo Scientific, Nicolet 6700, attenuated total reflectance mode (ATR)) was employed to study local interactions in the nanocomposites.

Results and discussion

Colorimetric detection of fatty acid in organic solvents

In this section, oleic acid was used as a representative of fatty acid. The direct addition of oleic acid to PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO aqueous suspension, however, caused a phase separation (Fig. S1a, supporting information). Therefore, we changed the solvent medium to ethanol to improve the solubility of oleic acid. The nanocomposites dispersed in ethanol still exhibited a blue color, indicated by a characteristic absorption spectrum with a peak at ∼640 nm (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, the addition of oleic acid at ∼1 M induced a new peak growth at ∼540 nm, corresponding to a red phase formation. The further increase of oleic acid concentration to ∼2 M resulted in a complete blue-to-red color transition. The plot of %CR versus concentration of oleic acid (Fig. 1b) reflects the extent of blue-to-red color transition. In this system, the color-transition region at 50 %CR value is detected at ∼2 M oleic acid. Our result indicates that the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol offers a direct detection of oleic acid. We note that, in previous studies, it was necessary to convert fatty acids to sodium salts [34,35]. The chemical structures of PDAs were also modified by attaching positively charged headgroups. The preparation of colorimetric sensors in this study is much simpler, which in turn facilitates large-scale processing for industrial applications.

(a) Absorption spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol upon increasing oleic acid concentration. (b) %CR of the nanocomposites in various alcohols versus oleic acid concentration. (c) Color photographs of the nanocomposites in alcohols upon adding oleic acid.

To explore the full potential of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO, we further investigated the solvent effects on their color-transition behaviors. To minimize experimental errors, all samples were prepared from the same synthetic batch of nanocomposites. A series of linear alcohols with increased chain length was used as solvents. The increase of alkyl chain length caused systematic decrease in polarity that could affect the local interaction between oleic acid and the nanocomposites. We observed that the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO dispersed in ethanol, 1-butanol, 1-hexanol and 1-octanol as solvents exhibited colorimetric response when adding oleic acid (Fig. S2, supporting information). Interestingly, the sensitivity varied with the solvent structure. The shift of color-transition region to the lower oleic acid concentration is obvious when alcohol with the longer alkyl tail is used as a solvent (Fig. 1b). In other words, the sensitivity of the nanocomposites increases with longer chain length of linear alcohol solvent. The color photographs in Fig. 1c summarize the alcohol solvent effects on the colorimetric response of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO to oleic acid.

The polarity of solvent media was further decreased by using other organic solvents including 1,4-dioxane, toluene, hexane, and octane. However, these systems exhibited a much lower sensitivity compared to those of the alcohol solvents. In fact, only the system of 1,4-dioxane solvent showed a complete blue-to-red color transition (Fig. 2a,c). The use of other nonpolar solvents induced a partial blue-to-red color transition under the same experimental conditions (Fig. S3, supporting information). The CR values were lower than 30 % at 2.3 M oleic acid (Fig. 2b). Our results indicate that the variation of solvent polarity is not the sole factor affecting the sensitivity. We believe that the presence of ether groups (-O-) within the structure of 1,4-dioxane provides favorable interaction with Zn2+-coordinated carboxylate headgroup of PDA, weakening the local interaction within nanocomposites. The hydroxy group (-OH) of alcohols causes a similar effect. In addition, their nonpolar alkyl tails may penetrate the nonpolar region of nanocomposites. It is anticipated that 1-octanol with the longer alkyl chain causes a greater extent of local perturbation compared to that of the ethanol system. Therefore, the system of nanocomposites in 1-octanol exhibits higher sensitivity. However, additional studies are required for fully understanding these results.

(a) Absorption spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO in 1,4-dioxane upon increasing oleic acid concentration. (b) %CR of the nanocomposites in various nonpolar organic solvents versus oleic acid concentration. (c) Color photographs of the nanocomposites in 1,4-dioxane upon adding oleic acid.

Next, we investigated the effects of PDA(x,y) alkyl chain length on the sensitivity. Our previous studies have shown that the decrease of PDA alkyl tail length often results in the increase of sensitivity [44,45]. However, the shortening of alkyl segment connected to the carboxylic headgroup may lead to the opposite effects [44]. In this study, the PDA(8,10)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol exhibited the colorimetric response to oleic acid similar to that of the PDA(8,12) system (Fig. 3a,c). The plots of %CR value versus oleic acid concentration are compared in Fig. 3b. We observe that the system of PDA(8,10) with shorter alkyl tail exhibits a slight increase in sensitivity. However, the PDA(2,10)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol provided the opposite result (Fig. S4, supporting information). The plot of %CR value indicates the decrease of sensitivity compared to the PDA(8,10) system (Fig. 3b). This observation, however, is similar to the result in our previous study on the pure PDA(2,10) and PDA(8,10) systems [44]. It was observed that the shortening of alkyl segment connected to carboxylic headgroup in PDA(2,10) caused the increase of color-transition temperature. We proposed that the local confinement of carboxylic headgroup existed in the PDA(2,10) system causing the increase in the strength of interaction within the system. Therefore, it requires a greater perturbation for inducing segmental rearrangements (i.e., color transition) within the PDA(2,10)/Zn2+/ZnO.

(a) Absorption spectra of PDA(8,10)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol upon increasing the oleic acid concentration. (b) %CR of PDA(2,10)/Zn2+/ZnO, PDA(8,10)/Zn2+/ZnO,and PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol versus oleic acid concentration. (c) Color photographs of the nanocomposites in ethanol upon adding oleic acid.

We studied the mechanism of color transition by directly investigating the suspensions using Raman spectroscopy with a liquid cell. Fig. 4 shows the Raman spectra of PDA(8,10)/Zn2+/ZnO in 1-octanol recorded upon consecutive addition of oleic acid. It is known that the pristine nanocomposite in blue phase shows strong peaks at 2079 and 1450 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching modes of C ≡ C and C=C in the PDA conjugated backbone [21,[46], [47], [48], [49]]. When 1.98 M oleic acid was added to the system, the C ≡ C band shifted to 2118 cm−1. The new peaks at 1710, 1654, 1440, and 1265 cm−1 were due to the vibrations of carbonyl group (C=O), ʋcis(C=C) bond in the alkyl chain, ʋ(-CH2-), and δ(=CH)in plane, respectively, of the oleic acid in 1-octanol [[50], [51], [52]]. The shift of C ≡ C band indicated the rearrangement of some PDA conjugated backbones, inducing the blue-to-red color transition. We did not observe any notable change in Raman signals of the oleic acid during the color transition. This result suggests that a large fraction of oleic acid remains in the l-octanol solvent. Only a small amount of oleic acid may penetrate the nanocomposite structure. We note that the Raman signals of oleic acid in 1-octanol overlap with those of the alkyl segments and C=C band of the PDA. The color-transition mechanism was further investigated in the following section by using dried samples.

Raman spectra of PDA(8,10)/Zn2+/ZnO in 1-octanol upon adding oleic acid. Photographs of suspensions are included for each spectrum. Raman spectrum of oleic acid in 1-octanol is included for comparison.

Fine tuning the sensitivity

Fatty acid-responsive property can be achieved by chemical modification of PDA headgroups. The method disadvantages are high cost and time-consuming [34,35]. Furthermore, adjusting of sensitivity has never been demonstrated in any previous studies. In this section, tuning the sensitivity of PDA/Zn2+/ZnO sensors is introduced by simply incorporating the cationic surfactant, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) into the nanocomposite structure. The CTAB molecules weaken the local interactions and their headgroups with positive charge also provide additional interacting sites for carboxylate/carboxylic headgroups of fatty acid. In this study, the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB were prepared in ethanol with 0–100 mM CTAB. The presence of 100 mM CTAB in the nanocomposite suspension caused the growth of a small shoulder at ∼540 nm (Fig. 5a). This result indicated that CTAB molecules perturbed the nanocomposite structure, inducing blue-to-red color transition of some PDA chains. However, blue phase remained for most nanocomposites.

(a) Absorption spectra and (b) Raman spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO in ethanol containing 0, 10, and 100 mM CTAB. Spectrum of pure CTAB in ethanol is included for comparison. (c) FT-IR spectra of dried samples of pure CTAB, PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO, and PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB.

The conformational changes of PDA(8,12) caused by CTAB were explored by Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 5b). We observed that the addition of 100 mM CTAB induced a peak broadening at 2079 cm−1, relating to a slight rearrangement of some C ≡ C bonds in the PDA conjugated backbone. FT-IR spectroscopy showed the characteristic peaks at 1538 (ʋas(–COO-)) and 1397 cm−1(ʋs(–COO-)) (Fig. 5c). These peaks correlate to the coordination of PDA(8,12) carboxylate headgroup and Zn2+ ion from ZnO [18,24,39,40,53]. The stretching vibrations of PDA alkyl segments were detected at 2958 (ʋas(CH3)), 2916 (ʋas(CH2)), and 2848 (ʋs(CH2)) cm−1. The CTAB spectrum manifested the strong peaks corresponding to the C−H stretching vibration of methyl and methylene groups at 3018, 2917, and 2849 cm−1 [39,[54], [55], [56]]. The C–H bending vibration exhibited peaks around 1500-1400 cm−1. The peak position at 1538 cm−1 was hardly affected when adding 100 mM CTAB. Therefore, the change of local interaction near the headgroup region of PDA was quite small.

The presence of 100 mM CTAB drastically improved the sensitivity of nanocomposites. The PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100mM CTAB in ethanol exhibited the color transition at ∼0.10 M oleic acid as observed by the growth of 540 nm peak in the absorption spectra (Fig. 6a). We point out that this oleic acid concentration is about 15 times less than that of the pristine nanocomposites (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, the variation of CTAB concentration from 1 to 100 mM systematically increases the sensitivity of sensor (Fig. 6b and Fig. S5, supporting information). Higher CTAB concentrations gradually shifted the color-transition region of the system to the lower oleic acid concentrations. Fig. 6c summarizes the colorimetric response of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB to oleic acid. Our results indicate that the CTAB concentration plays an important role in controlling the colorimetric response of nanocomposites to oleic acid. The CTAB effects on the sensitivity of nanocomposites were also observed in our previous studies where polymers were used as stimuli [40,41]. It is worthwhile to note that the increase of CTAB concentration to 200, 300, 400 and 500 mM leads to further increase in sensitivity (Figs. S6 and 7, supporting information). However, a gradual blue-to-red color transition occurs in these systems prior to the addition of PMMA. Therefore, it is rather difficult to observe the colorimetric response to PMMA via naked eye.

(a) Absorption spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB in ethanol upon adding oleic acid. (b) %CR of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB versus oleic acid concentration. (c) Color photographs of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB in ethanol upon adding oleic acid.

We further investigated the PDA/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB paper-based sensors for utilizing as mobile test kits. The solid-state sensors were immersed in ethanol with oleic acid solutions for 30 min. The color transition at different oleic acid concentrations occurred as shown in color photographs (Fig. 7a). The red chromaticity level (%R) was calculated as described in the previous study [57] and used for estimating the color-transition region. The %R values were averaged from 20 points randomly selected within each sample. The plots of %R of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB paper-based sensors versus oleic acid concentration are demonstrated in Fig. 7b. The results are consistent with those observed in the ethanol suspensions. The sensitivity systematically increased when CTAB concentration increased from 0 to 100 mM. The pristine nanocomposite paper-based sensors hardly showed any color transition at 1 M oleic acid. On the other hand, the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB sensor illustrated a clear blue-to-red color transition at ∼0.20 M oleic acid. This color-transition region is comparable to that of the ethanol suspension (Fig. 6). Therefore, the effects of CTAB addition on the sensitivity are preserved in the solid-state sensors. The paper-based sensors can be used as mobile test kits for rapid detection of fatty acid in field work.

(a) Photographs showing color change of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB paper-based sensors after being immersed in ethanol solutions with different oleic acid concentrations. (b) %R versus oleic acid concentration of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB paper-based sensors.

Raman spectroscopy was utilized to investigate the effects of CTAB concentration on the sensitivity of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB sensors. The spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO with 10 and 100 mM CTAB in ethanol were examined upon the addition of oleic acid. We detected the growth of a new peak at 2118 cm−1 (C ≡ C) after adding 1.05 M oleic acid to the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB, indicating partial rearrangement of some conjugated backbones (Fig. 8a). However, the original peak at 2079 cm−1 remained dominant. The PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB spectra in Fig. 8b showed rather different results. At 1.05 M oleic acid, the vibrational band of C ≡ C completely shifted from 2079 to 2118 cm−1. This observation indicated that the backbone rearrangement occurred to a greater extent than that of the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB, corresponding to the higher sensitivity. We believe that some CTAB molecules insert the outer layer of nanocomposite structure, weakening the local interactions [39]. The CTAB headgroup with positive charge may also improve the interactions with (–COOH)/(–COO-) headgroups of oleic acid, enhancing the sensitivity.

Raman spectra of (a) PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10mM CTAB and (b) PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100mM CTAB in ethanol upon adding oleic acid. Photographs of suspensions are included for each spectrum.

We further studied the conformational changes of PDA within the solid-state sensors. With ethanol solvent removed, this system allowed the investigation of C=C signal (1450 cm−1) and alkyl signal (1150-1000 cm−1) [24,46]. The spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB showed that small peaks at 2118 cm−1 (C ≡ C) and 1510 cm−1 (C=C) was detected at 0.2 M oleic acid (Fig. 9a). For the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB under the same condition, the peaks completely shifted from 2079 to 2118 cm−1 and 1450 to 1510 cm−1 (Fig. 9b). These results were consistent with those of the liquid suspensions. However, we detected the higher extent of backbone rearrangement in the solid-state sensors. It was also observed that the sharp peaks at 1150-1000 cm−1 merged during the color transition, indicating trans-to-gauche conformational change of some alkyl segments [24,46].

Raman spectra of paper-based sensors of (a) PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB and (b) PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB upon adding oleic acid.

The FT-IR spectra of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB were measured during the color transition induced by oleic acid (Fig. S8, supporting information). The spectrum of pure oleic acid showed a strong peak at 1710 cm−1 due to the C=O stretching of carboxylic group [58]. We observed that the Zn2+-coordinated-COO- peak at 1538 cm−1 vanished when the oleic acid was added to PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-10 mM CTAB. This indicated the protonation of PDA(8,12) carboxylate headgroup by H+ ions from oleic acid and hence destroyed the coordination with Zn2+ ions. Our previous study also showed that the acidic protons corroded the ZnO surface [23]. Therefore, the weakened local interactions allowed segmental rearrangement, leading to the color transition.

Effects of fatty acid structure

It has been demonstrated that the structure of fatty acid plays an important role in the color-transition behavior of PDA-based sensors [34,35]. In fact, only the fatty acids with bent structure including oleic, linoleic, and palmitic acid induced color transition. The use of other linear fatty acids such as stearic and elaidic acid as stimuli did not cause any color transition. In other words, the PDA-based sensors in previous studies could not detect the saturated and trans-fatty acids with linear structure [34,35]. Interestingly, the PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB sensor in this study can detect stearic acid as illustrated in Fig. 10(a–c). The color transition was clearly observed upon increasing the concentration of stearic acid. In addition, the sensor can discriminate the stearic acid from oleic acid. The plot of %CR shows that the sensor is more sensitive to oleic acid, consistent with the results of previous studies [34,35]. The corresponding absorption spectra are shown in Fig. S9 (supporting information). The %CR values were ∼80 % and ∼50 % at 0.2 M of oleic acid and stearic acid, respectively (Fig. 10b). The large difference in %CR can be used to discriminate the two fatty acids. The %CR of an unknown sample can be obtained by performing a similar experiment and then compared with the standard %CR in Fig. 10b. Our result also suggests that the color-transition mechanism involves the penetration of fatty acid into PDA structure. It is expected that the bent structure of oleic acid presents a steric effect, causing a larger extent of local perturbation.

(a) Chemical structures of oleic acid and stearic acid. (b) %CR of PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-100 mM CTAB versus fatty acid concentration. (c) Color photographs of the sensors in ethanol upon adding oleic acid and stearic acid.

From the results, we summarize the color-transition mechanism in Fig. 11. The pristine PDA/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites demonstrate colorimetric response to oleic acid via the protonation at carboxylate headgroup of PDA and the corrosion of ZnO surface (Fig. 11a). The carboxylate headgroup of fatty acid may interact with some Zn2+ ions near the outer surface of sensor. When CTAB molecules are added to the nanocomposites, the sensitivity systematically increases with the CTAB concentration. In this system, the alkyl side chain of CTAB may insert the alkyl layer of PDA, weakening local interactions (Fig. 11b). We hypothesize that the CTAB headgroups interact with the carboxylate headgroup of fatty acid. Some molecules of fatty acid also penetrate the nanocomposite structure. Therefore, the sensitivity depends on both the CTAB concentration and the structure of fatty acid.

Schematic models for the color-transition mechanism of (a) pristine PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites and (b) PDA(8,12)/Zn2+/ZnO-CTAB upon exposure to fatty acid.

Conclusion

In this study, we develop the PDA/Zn2+/ZnO nanocomposites as colorimetric sensors for fatty acids using a simple and low-cost method. Using organic solvents as media increases the solubility of fatty acids, allowing direct detection. In addition, the sensitivity of nanocomposites can be tuned by varying structures and/or properties of the solvents. It is also possible to control their sensitivity by adding cationic surfactant, CTAB, into the system. The color-transition region can be systematically adjusted by varying the CTAB concentration. This property can be preserved in a solid-state sensor, fabricated by drop-casting the nanocomposite suspension onto a filter paper. The colorimetric sensors show a potential for being utilized as mobile test kits for semi-quantitative analysis of fatty acids. Additionally, the sensors can also differentiate fatty acids with bent or linear structures. Our methodologies are relatively straightforward, suitable for industrial-scale applications in the food industries.
