An individualized patient decision-making tool to improve decision making in non-small cell lung cancer: a mixed methods approach
Authors: V. Sebri, L. Provenzano, D. Monzani, F. Trovo’, D. Macocchi, M. Fernandez-Pinto, N. Dollis, E. Sarris, M. Szewczyk, I. Watermann, L. Yonah, R. Grasso, P. Dorangricchia, V. Miskovic, A.D. Dumitrascu, A. Prelaj, G. Pravettoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are asked to make decisions during their cancer journey, with a relevant impact on their quality of life, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes. Improving shared decision-making can be complex, thereby damaging patient–doctor communication.Materials and methodsWe followed three developmental phases, conducting focus groups, semistructured interviews, thinking-out-loud sessions, and a questionnaire with patients and semistructured interviews with physicians. Interviews were audio-recorded, verbatim transcribed, thematically coded, and analyzed. Phase I consisted of an evaluation of decisional needs during medical consultations; phase II tested the comprehensibility and usability of the first, static individualized patient decision aid (PDA); and phase III beta-tested the feasibility of the second, dynamic PDA.ResultsPatients with NSCLC and oncologists agreed on the need to develop a tailored PDA to promote shared decision-making. Over the three phases, findings highlight that PDA can be a helpful decision tool to (i) provide clear and appropriate information to promote patients’ knowledge and awareness, improving emotion regulation; (ii) assess patients’ preferences and values, considering also if there is information that a patient do not desire to receive (i.e. survivorship rates); (iii) have a trustworthy relationship with the physicians; and (iv) involve caregivers in the process of care efficiently.ConclusionAn online PDA for patients with NSCLC is proved to be a helpful tool for shared decision making, supporting clinical practice, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes. A clinical trial will be started with newly diagnosed patients with NSCLC and their oncologists, involving the latest, dynamic version of PDA.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer worldwide and is associated with poor prognosis and limited life expectancy in advanced stages.1 Treatment side effects can relevant affect patients’ quality of life and daily functioning, as well as that of their families.2,3 For this reason, promoting shared decision-making through effective doctor–patient communication is essential to support patients in understanding and managing their illness. Shared decision-making is a collaborative process in which patients and clinicians jointly develop appropriate care plans.4 This approach integrates clinical evidence with patients’ preferences, values, expectations, and emotional needs to ensure feasible and sustainable treatment decisions.5 However, many patients with lung cancer do not receive comprehensive information during consultations, often due to limited time and high clinical workload. This lack of information can reduce illness awareness and increase symptom burden and psychological distress.6,7 Therefore, tailored decision-making tools are recommended to enhance doctor–patient communication and support patient engagement.8 Patient decision aids (PDAs) can improve understanding of the disease and help explore patients’ information preferences and treatment options.9,10 These tools can also strengthen awareness, participation in decisions, and tolerance of treatment risks.11,12 Moreover, PDAs may facilitate emotional processing and communication with caregivers, especially when integrated with digital health and telemedicine platforms.13, 14, 15, 16

Although several decision aids have been developed, no PDAs specifically focus on non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). There is a clear gap in tools that integrate medical information with patients’ preferences and needs, highlighting the importance of personalized decision-support instruments.17 This study therefore investigates the feasibility and usability of a decision tool tailored for patients with NSCLC that incorporates both clinical information and patient preferences. The I3LUNG project aims to develop a web-based decision-making tool to better predict immunotherapy outcomes. The tool will be developed and validated on an accessible online platform within an H2020-funded project.

PDA development

Our PDA development was based on the quality criteria defined by the PDA collaboration in 2021. Accordingly, three phases have been followed (Figure 1). In phase I, relevant scientific literature on decision aids for patients with NSCLC published in peer-reviewed journals was systematically reviewed.6 Additionally, online focus groups with patients were conducted to identify patients’ needs and preferences regarding available lung cancer treatments and the potential use of artificial intelligence (AI) to build predictive models for treatment selection.3 Thirdly, semistructured interviews were conducted with oncologists experienced in NSCLC treatment to identify the content of patient-and-doctor communications and the potential utility of decision tools in this setting17 (see Table 2).Figure 1The PDA development graph regarding the three phases.PDA, patient decision aid.Table 2Questions involved in phase IPhase II—questionsFirstly, a systematic review was conducted. Based on these results, the first static version of the PDA for both stages III and IV of lung cancer was generated. It included information on illness characteristics and available treatments, which helped patients make decisions about their illness. We elucidated the characteristics of illness, oncological treatments, and their related side effects. Furthermore, the PDA detailed expected differences between treatments in terms of duration and short- and long-term side effects, and reported survival probabilities at different time points based on available literature (i.e. randomized clinical trials and large real-world studies).

In phase II (see the Consolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research checklist, Supplementary Appendix S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100730), we alpha-tested the comprehensibility and usability of this first version using a qualitative approach. Firstly, online in-depth interviews and thinking-out-loud sessions with patients with NSCLC were conducted by a researcher expert in psycho-oncology, who guided participants through the PDA and solicited feedback. Participants were invited to respond to some questions, such as ‘What is your first impression of this tool?’, ‘Do you find this tool easy to use?’, ‘Are you able to clearly understand the content presented to you by the tool?’, and ‘Do you think this tool could have been useful in supporting your treatment decision?’. Interviews and thinking-out-loud sessions were audio-recorded, verbatim transcribed, and personal information was preserved and thematically coded to identify patterns related to decisional needs and the counseling process. Therefore, patients’ privacy was maintained. These developmental steps allowed us to identify critical flaws in the first version of the PDA, enabling potential implementation, including supplementing missing information and clarifying potential ambiguities. Based on the results from phase II, we proposed a second, dynamic version of the PDA.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis led by a principal investigator and two psychologists. Focus groups explored participants’ experiences with information received during consultations, using open-ended questions to encourage spontaneous discussion. Field notes were taken anonymously, and researchers reflected on potential biases. An inductive approach19 was applied, with transcripts repeatedly reviewed to identify initial codes. Codes were grouped into subthemes through an abductive process, forming a thematic map. Themes and subthemes were then labeled to clarify emerging content. Finally, an independent researcher validated the thematic map to ensure reliability.

In phase II (see the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials style, Supplementary Appendix S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100730), we beta-tested the feasibility of the second and final version of the tool using a quantitative approach. All patients participating in this study were recruited by their treating oncologists. Then, patients were asked to anonymously complete an online study-specific questionnaire containing 32 statements on PDA usability and feasibility, inspired by the study by Petersen et al.20 In this study, participants were asked to complete a study-specific questionnaire on a five-point Likert scale regarding PDA satisfaction and comprehensibility, usability, and effectiveness. Accordingly, the present questionnaire evaluated participants’ comprehensibility, usability, and satisfaction with the PDA. Furthermore, participants were asked to rank the tool on overall satisfaction.

The working group meeting

All processes were supervised by a biweekly, multidisciplinary working group, which was structured and conducted over the first year of the I3LUNG project. The main focus of the working group was to share and discuss issues and topics to be included in the PDA tool, with particular attention to the information patients wish to receive at this early stage of the disease course. In line with the International Patient Decision Aid Standards,21 the involvement of other experts is essential throughout the development of the PDA. This approach has been demonstrated to be beneficial for developing more valuable and effective PDAs and information tools. Thus, the development of this PDA has been guided and supervised.

Results

Phase 1: systematic review, focus groups with patients with NSCLC, and semistructured interviews with physicians

In the first phase, a systematic review6 highlighted the effectiveness of heterogeneous PDAs, identifying four main outcomes: anxiety, decision-making, patients’ knowledge, and quality of life. The review emphasized the importance of doctor–patient communication and patients’ knowledge in supporting informed decisions and setting realistic expectations about treatment and life expectancy.22 Providing clear summaries of information and allowing patients time to review and discuss it with physicians is essential.23 Caregivers can also support patients by reviewing decision tools at home, increasing emotional support and understanding.24,25 Regular contact with health care professionals may further improve patients’ comprehension of medical information.26 Finally, addressing patients’ hope, priorities, and emotional support is crucial to reduce distress and enhance coping strategies.24,27

Secondly, focus groups with 22 patients with NSCLC were conducted from January 2023 to December 2024.3 Patients involved in the present study had a mean age of 66.28 years (age range = 44-72; SD = 8.67). Moreover, half of them were female (50%) and received a lung cancer diagnosis within the past 5 years (64%). There was no drop out; participants recruited participated in the focus group, providing their opinions. Focus groups lasted about 2 hours and revealed that participants obtained all necessary information about illness characteristics and treatments. However, all focus groups expressed controversial opinions about patients’ requests to be informed about current knowledge and information about the disease. Some participants reported a need to know all available information (i.e. ‘Information was very clear, I understood sufficiently what was happening on my body and how I can feel during oncological treatment’ and ‘All cancer information has been clear and appropriate. It helped me to accept the proposal of treatments, reducing anxiety’), while others said they would have preferred not to receive information about the characteristics of their illness or its long-term effects. In this regard, oncological information could have increased emotional disorders, especially anxiety and fear of cancer progression, reducing treatment adherence (i.e. ‘I don’t want to know anything, I really don’t want to know anything! I consider myself a healthy person, I haven’t had any side effects, so I’m fine’). Secondly, focus groups highlighted the relationship with physicians being emotionally sustained by the health care system of reference, emphasizing the importance of trusting their doctor (i.e. ‘I trust the doctors, and not being from the field, I can’t contradict them because I don’t have the expertise to do so’). Some patients expressed a desire to be actively involved in their cancer journey and to discuss their disease trajectory with oncologists. Others preferred not to participate in shared decision-making due to fear of making the wrong health choice, choosing instead to rely on physicians. Additionally, some participants preferred being treated by a single physician to maintain a more personal and trusting relationship (i.e. ‘My relationship with my physicians is fundamental to solving doubts. I need to discuss with my physicians what is happening because I don’t want to have doubts, which increase anxiety and distress’). The involvement of too many physicians in the cancer pathway may create uncertainty and weaken the doctor–patient relationship. Participants expressed mixed opinions about introducing additional decision-making tools during consultations. Some suggested brochures or similar materials to help explain the illness and treatment to family members. Patients also reported feeling confused during consultations due to the emotional impact of the cancer diagnosis, particularly anxiety and distress (i.e. ‘In my opinion, a booklet would have been very helpful, especially to share the information with their family members and explain to them treatments and how they work’). Therefore, some patients felt that additional physical materials would help them discuss their condition at home and avoid sharing inaccurate information. Others reported that they did not need extra decision tools because they were satisfied with the information received from physicians. They also expressed concern that additional sources might create confusion (i.e. ‘No, I think that it isn’t a good idea, I prefer to ask all the things, I don’t go looking on the Internet or other people for fear of giving me wrong information. I only ask doctors because they know how to deal with the situation’).

Thirdly, in the semistructured interviews conducted between February and April 2023,17 10 physicians reported difficulties in communicating with patients and caregivers during medical consultations. Physicians involved in these interviews had a mean age of 30.8 years (age range 27-33). Moreover, most of them were female (60%) and worked as oncologists (80%), with a smaller percentage working in palliative care (20%). As in the focus groups, the recruited oncologists actively participated in the interviews; none declined to share their experience. The interviews lasted about 45 minutes, during which physicians emphasized the importance of capturing patients’ attention by addressing their doubts and making the conversation easier and more effective. In detail, they explicitly underlined their interest in promoting patients’ knowledge about lung cancer and its available treatments. Thus, oncologists emphasized the need to enhance communication during consultations to increase patients’ awareness of the disease and, consequently, to be more proactive in their health care process (i.e. ‘I ask each patient to hear me out first and then ask questions. I may answer a patient’s question before they ask it because I systematically explain the different treatments. I always ask at the end if any clarifications need to be made’). Secondly, oncologists discussed the quality of their relationships with patients during consultations. Many physicians stated that they do not have enough time to spend with their patients, which may impact the effectiveness of the patient-and-doctor path of care. In line with this, oncologists reported their personal need to improve the management of patients’ psychological issues, impairing efficient communication. Consultations sometimes become more difficult due to patients’ anxiety and distress regarding the contents of the discussion (i.e. ‘I noticed that patients who receive certain news, without knowing you, feel distressed and anxious…it is difficult to speak about prognosis and treatments, especially with family members who sometimes often ask you to limit communication to the patient’). Lastly, physicians reported their interest in providing PDA to facilitate communication with patients and their families, thereby reducing the risk of misunderstandings during medical consultations. They highlighted their interest in appropriately involving family and caregivers, thanks to PDA, which can also help them become more aware and engage in their loved ones’ care journey.

Phase 2: alpha testing of comprehensibility and usability

In the second phase, a semistructured interview with 10 patients with NSCLC highlighted the importance of having specific and appropriate information about the tumor type and its characteristics. Patients were recruited according to the inclusion criteria, and all of them agreed to participate in the interview, which lasted about 45-60 minutes. Patients reported that the information helped guide them during the medical consultation and in decision-making regarding available treatment options. On the contrary, as mentioned above, not all patients desire additional information about their illness. Some of them reported that this may have a strong emotional impact on survivorship rates, which could generate fear and agitation. Secondly, four patients who met the inclusion criteria were involved in the thinking-out-loud sessions, and all of them agreed to participate. Each thinking-out-loud session lasted about 1 hour; patients showed interest and strong engagement with the tool. They reported their experience related to illness, explicitly referring to the relationship with physicians and their emotions. Regarding the PDA characteristics, they evidenced the helpful inclusion of details about illness and its related clinical consequences. Accordingly, they evaluated the tool as a helpful guide for patients in making health care decisions. However, not all patients expressed a desire for further information that they received during their medical consultations.

Based on these findings, we developed a second, dynamic version of PDA. In this version, we added the option to choose whether to receive information on survivorship, as well as quizzes to test patients’ knowledge and preferences. Additionally, the current version includes a box for patients to add questions and a glossary to improve health literacy (Figure 3). Lastly, the ability to send this information to the physicians was added, so they can review it before/during patients’ consultations. Specifically, physicians receive a PDA labeled with a patient’s code to maintain anonymity. Patients can provide their code to physicians to discuss the contents emerging from their PDA.Figure 3Survivorship rates, quiz, and preferences assessment into PDAs.PDA, patient decision aid.

Phase 3: beta testing of the feasibility

In the third and final phase, findings emerged from an ad hoc 32-item questionnaire administered to patients with NSCLC, showing that the second, dynamic version of PDA is overall understandable and clear. Thirty patients began completing the online questionnaire. Twenty-six patients filled out the online survey completely, collecting feedback on the PDA content and functioning through self-report measures. They were 71 years old on average. The major part of the sample consisted of males (N = 14), followed by females (N = 10) and individuals identifying as ‘others’ (N = 2). Most participants (N = 16) had received a lung cancer diagnosis more than 5 years earlier, whereas 9 had been diagnosed within the past 5 years. Regarding the first oncological treatment, the largest proportion of the sample had undergone chemotherapy (N = 12); five people received combined treatments, and six people underwent surgery as the first intervention. Only two patients underwent radiotherapy as their first treatment. Finally, after the first oncological treatments, 11 patients received combined therapies; 4 patients underwent immunotherapy, and 3 patients received radiotherapy. Two participants did not receive further oncological treatment, while only the patients underwent molecular targeted therapy. No participant received chemotherapy after the first oncological treatment. An ad hoc questionnaire was developed to assess the comprehensibility and feasibility of the PDA. Each statement was phrased positively, ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 5 (totally agree); therefore, agreement conferred a positive evaluation of the PDA. Furthermore, participants were asked to rank the tool on overall satisfaction. The response range was between 12 and 60. Considering the mean to be 2.5, we measure each specific item and its related mean. Overall, we obtain a mean of 3.95, with a standard deviation of 0.17. Moreover, regarding overall satisfaction with the second version of PDA, we obtained the following results: ‘Overall, I am satisfied with this Patient Decision Aid’ = 4.12 (see Table 3). Participants expressed overall satisfaction with the present PDA. Specifically, this tool was found to be clear and to appropriately describe illness characteristics and their related side effects. Discussion

This study describes the development of a web-based PDA for patients with NSCLC using a mixed-methods approach. Overall, participants evaluated the tool positively, highlighting its usefulness, clarity, and potential to support medical decision-making. Patients reported that the PDA clearly explains treatment options, including potential risks and benefits, helping to avoid unrealistic expectations and facilitating communication with physicians. Adequate information is essential to reduce decisional conflict, which may otherwise lead to regret or delayed decisions.28 The findings also emphasize the importance of assessing patients’ preferences, which are often overlooked during oncological consultations despite variations in physical condition and socioeconomic background. Importantly, patients do not always prefer treatments with the highest expected survival and express a desire to choose which information they receive.29

Focus group participants also noted that excessive worry may reduce treatment adherence by affecting hope and emotional regulation.30 Participants highlighted the importance of making decisions within a trustworthy context, where high-quality doctor–patient communication plays a key role in adherence and clinical outcomes. In this regard, the PDA can facilitate discussion and support shared decision-making by allowing patients to review information before consultations.31 Caregivers were also considered essential in supporting emotional regulation and decision-making, and the PDA can help facilitate communication within the patient–caregiver dyad. Overall, the results support the value of web-based PDAs in promoting informed and shared decisions, while reinforcing the central role of physicians in this collaborative process.32 These findings are consistent with research encouraging the integration of patient-centered decision tools within clinical pathways.33, 34, 35

Future research is needed to prospectively test the PDA with newly diagnosed patients with NSCLC, with a study underway comparing standard care to PDA-supported care and results expected in 2027. The current PDA is web-based and does not include AI, which could personalize interventions in the future.36,37 Study participants were mainly from Italy, with physician interviews in Italy and Greece, which may limit the external generalizability due to sociocultural factors. Selection bias is possible, as uninterested patients did not participate. Additionally, patients and doctors might have restrained negative feedback since the development team conducted all study phases. To address these limitations, multicenter trials across different countries and involving independent research teams are recommended. Future PDAs could also integrate AI to enhance personalization and patient-centered care.

Conclusions

The results of our study suggest that a web-based PDA for patients with NSCLC can be a valuable additional tool to enhance medical consultations in terms of patient–doctor communication about illness and shared decision-making. This PDA version is, in fact, capable of supporting health care decisions, serving as a helpful tool to read before treatments. The need to improve the regular medical consultation process seems evident to provide efficient decision-making. Tools for health information must be empowered to provide information to patients and to increase their involvement and collaboration with professionals.38
