Foliar Application of Calcium Phosphate Nanoparticles Improves Photosynthetic Efficiency, Nutrient Uptake, and Antioxidant Capacity in Coriandrum sativum L. under Salinity Stress
Authors: Muhammad Kaleem, Anis Ali Shah, Sheeraz Usman, Wenlong Xu, Ahmed H. Alfarhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Salt stress has a major impact on agricultural land worldwide. Poor irrigation and the unregulated use of fertilizers have resulted in the accumulation of salt in the soil. The use of nanofillers is a promising technique that can alleviate the adverse effect of abiotic stresses. In this study, the effect of calcium phosphate nanoparticles (CaP-NPs) was evaluated on coriander plants grown under salt stress. For salt stress application, 100 and 150 mM NaCl solutions were used along with a control group (water only; EC: 0.6 dS m–1). Meanwhile, a 50 mg L–1 CaP-NPs solution was applied as a foliar spray. Salt stress specifically at 150 mM greatly reduced the growth, biomass, physiological, and biochemical attributes of coriander plants. In contrast, CaP-NPs effectively ameliorated oxidative stress caused by salt stress, enhancing growth, biomass, physiological and biochemical attributes. Besides this, foliar spray of CaP-NPs at 50 mg L–1 modulated the enzymatic antioxidant defense system. Salt stress also disturbed ionic balance, as it increased the Na+ concentration in the roots and shoots of coriander plants. Salt stress reduced the K+/Na+ ratio and disturbed ion regulation. On the other hand, the CaP-NPs application increased K+ uptake and maintained the K+/Na+ ratio. Overall, the CaP-NPs application was effective in alleviating salt stress in coriander plants. The use of CaP-NPs in agriculture can do a dual job, as it can alleviate the toxic effect of salt stress and also improve the nutrient use efficiency of plants.

Introduction

Salt stress is one of the major abiotic stresses that affect the growth and productivity of coriander plants. Plants grown under salinity stress exhibited deficiency in micronutrients like zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), and manganese (Mn). High salinity levels in the soil lead to reduced water uptake and stomatal closure, negatively impacting plant development and yield at every growth stage. This poses a significant challenge to global food security, necessitating the reclamation of salt-affected lands to meet the demands of a growing population. This is because, to meet the food needs of the increasing population, salt-affected land has to be worked upon. For sustainable agriculture, several techniques have been developed to improve plant productivity in saline environments. Saline soils are characterized by an electrical conductivity (EC) of more than 4 dS m–1, with global estimates suggesting that approximately 20% of cultivable soils are affected by salinity. This figure continues to rise due to climate change and unsustainable agricultural practices. As arable land diminishes due to urbanization and desertification, agricultural expansion is shifting to marginal lands, exacerbating the challenges of food production. The primary physiological effects of salinity stress include reduced soil osmotic potential, nutrient imbalance, and ion toxicity, particularly due to excessive sodium (Na+) accumulation. ,

Salinity stress significantly reduces plant growth and yield, particularly in agricultural crops. The sensitivity of plants to salt stress is governed by intricate molecular and biochemical networks, making conventional breeding approaches for salt tolerance relatively inefficient. Salinity stress enhances Na+ uptake through plasma membrane depolarization, leading to K+ loss, which disrupts potassium ion homeostasis and lowers the K+/Na+ ratio. This ionic imbalance ultimately triggers apoptosis. Prolonged exposure to salinity further exacerbates these effects by impairing cell division and growth, causing osmotic imbalance, generating free radicals that induce oxidative stress, inhibiting key biochemical processes, and leading to DNA damage and lipid peroxidation in cell membranes. , Plant responses to salinity stress are complex, involving alterations in photosynthetic electron transport, ion compartmentation, osmotic regulation, and antioxidant defense mechanisms. The extent of these responses varies depending on plant species, genotype, and stress intensity.

Coriander (Coriandrum sativum L.), a member of the Apiaceae family, is widely cultivated as a culinary and medicinal plant, with its leaves and seeds used in food preparation and herbal remedies. C. sativum is rich in polyphenols, vitamins (C, A, and B2), and essential oils, particularly linalool, which exhibits strong antioxidant and antibacterial properties. High concentrations of essential oils in C. sativum are mainly associated with seeds. Coriander has also been shown to possess various health benefits, including lowering blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels. Multiple studies have investigated the tolerance, growth, and productivity of coriander under salinity stress. Research indicates that coriander experiences a significant reduction in growth and dry matter production at NaCl concentrations exceeding 80 mM. Furthermore, exposure to 100 and 200 mM NaCl has been shown to markedly reduce chlorophyll (Chl) content and relative water content (RWC) in coriander plants. However, the application of biostimulants such as sodium nitroprusside (SNP) can alleviate some of these adverse effects by enhancing carotenoid content and protein levels.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising approach to mitigate abiotic stress and enhance plant growth under challenging environmental conditions. Calcium phosphate nanoparticles (CaP-NPs) have garnered attention due to their role in improving nutrient uptake, photosynthetic efficiency, and stress tolerance. CaP-NPs serve as carriers of essential macronutrients such as calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P), which are vital for metabolic processes, enzyme activation, and structural integrity in plants. Studies have demonstrated that CaP-NPs can enhance plant biomass, antioxidant enzyme activity, proline accumulation, and osmotic adjustment under salinity stress. For instance, foliar application of CaP-NPs has been shown to alleviate salt-induced oxidative stress by modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging mechanisms, thereby enhancing plant resilience. In addition to improving nutrient availability, nanoparticles exhibit unique physicochemical properties, such as a high surface area-to-volume ratio and controlled nutrient release, which enhance their efficiency compared to conventional fertilizers. Recent research has reported that CaP-NPs can improve plant–water relationship, chlorophyll content, and the overall photosynthetic performance under saline conditions. Moreover, CaP-NPs have been identified as potential nanobiostimulants capable of triggering stress-responsive signaling pathways, thereby promoting plant adaptation to adverse environments.

Given the potential of CaP-NPs in enhancing plant tolerance to salinity stress, this study aims to synthesize and characterize CaP-NPs; evaluate their impact on photosynthetic efficiency, nutrient uptake, and antioxidant capacity in coriander under salinity stress; and elucidate the underlying physiological and biochemical mechanisms associated with their application. By integrating nanotechnology with sustainable agricultural practices, this study seeks to provide a novel strategy for improving crop productivity in salt-affected soils.

Results

Effect of CaP-NPs on Growth Parameters of C. sativum under Salt Stress

Salt stress had a significant influence on the growth of C. sativum plants. Conversely, CaP-NP application reversed the impact caused by salt stress on the growth of the plants. As shown in Figure A, salt stress significantly decreased the plant height by 36% and 46% under S1 (100 mM) and S2 (150 mM) salt stress, respectively, in comparison with control plants. When CaP-NPs treatment was applied, it improved the plant height by 29% in control plants. In the case of salt-stressed plants, the application of CaP-NPs significantly increased the plant height by 34% and 35% at S1 and S2 stress levels, respectively, in comparison with stress-only plants (Figure A). Salt stress exerted a toxic effect on C. sativum plants by reducing the plant fresh weight (PFW) by 36% and 52% at 100 and 150 mM NaCl stress levels, respectively, as compared to the control. However, in these stressed plants, CaP-NP treatment effectively increased the PFW by 30% and 61%, respectively, in comparison with untreated plants (Figure B). Similarly, plant dry weight (PDW) of C. sativum was also reduced by 32% and 52% under S1 and S2 salt stress, respectively, in relation to the control. In these plants, supplementation of CaP-NPs led to an increase in PDW by 23% and 60% respectively when compared to untreated plants (Figure C).

Values presented in the graphs are mean of three replicates ± standard error. Different alphabets above the bars, obtained after Duncan’s multiple range test, show that mean values are significantly different from each other at p ≤ 0.05.

Impact of CaP-NPs on Photosynthetic Pigments of C. sativum under Salt Stress

Salt stress decreased the content of chlorophyll a by 40% and 61% at S1 and S2 salt stress, respectively, when compared to the control. In contrast , supplementation with CaP-NPs augmented the level of chlorophyll a by 52% and 98%, respectively, in comparison to stress-only plants (Figure A). Similarly, a 32% and 55% reduction in chlorophyll b was observed in C. sativum plants under S1 and S2 salt stress conditions, respectively. However, in these plants, the foliar sprays of CaP-NPs enhanced the level of chlorophyll b by 25% and 57%, respectively, in relation to the corresponding untreated salt-stressed plants (Figure B). Similarly, the total chlorophyll content was also reduced by up to 59% under severe stress conditions (Figure C). In contrast, salt stress at the S1 and S2 levels increased the carotenoid levels by 23% and 32%, respectively, compared to the control. Moreover, in salt-stressed plants, the application of CaP-NPs reduced the level of carotenoid by 16% and 15%, respectively, at S1 and S2 salt stress, compared to the untreated control (Figure D).

Effect of CaP-NPs on Gas Exchange Attributes of C. sativum Plants under Salt Stress

Salt stress severely reduced the net photosynthesis rate (P n), transpiration rate (T r) and stomatal conductance (g s) at both moderate (36%, 43%, and 18%, respectively) and severe (51%, 23%, and 27%, respectively) stress levels compared to control plants. However, the intercellular CO2 (C i) level was significantly increased in salt-stressed plants at both levels (moderate: 13% and severe: 27%). Whereas, CaP-NPs foliar application reversed the negative impact of salt stress on photosynthesis-related parameters. It increased P n, T r and g s by 116%, 108%, and 100% in moderately salt-stressed plants and by 139%, 48%, and 128% in severely salt-stressed plants, respectively, compared with stress-only plants. In contrast, CaP-NP application had a negligible effect on the C i level in the leaves of C. sativum plants compared with untreated stressed plants (Figure A–D).

Effect of CaP-NPs on Water Use Efficiency (WUE) and Relative Water Content (RWC) of C. sativum Plants under Salt Stress

Salt stress also disrupted the WUE of C. sativum. At the S1 salt stress level, the instantaneous WUE measured as P n/T r was slightly improved (13%) compared to control plants, but it was significantly reduced (36%) at the S2 stress level. Similarly, the intrinsic WUE measured as P n/g s was also reduced significantly at both S1 (20%) and S2 (33%) stress levels (Figure A,B). This reduction in WUE can be attributed to the reduced RWC of C. sativum plants under stress conditions. RWC was reduced by 21% and 29%, respectively, at the S1 and S2 stress levels as compared to the control. Following the foliar application of CaP-NPs, these attributes were significantly improved under all conditions (control and stress). CaP-NPs enhanced WUE by 61% at a severe salt stress level compared to stress-only plants. Similarly, it enhanced RWC by 22% and 25% under S1 and S2 salt stress conditions (Figure C).

Effect of CaP-NPs on Stress Indicators and Total Soluble Protein (TSP) Content of C. sativum Plants under Salt Stress

C. sativum plants growing under salt stress conditions (S1 and S2) exhibit an enhancement in the level of malondialdehyde (MDA) by 151% and 305%, respectively, when compared with the control. However, when CaP-NPs were applied on these stressed plants, this trend was reversed, decreasing the MDA levels by 38% and 45%, respectively (Figure A). Conversely, supplementation of CaP-NPs at S1 and S2 salt stress decreased the H2O2 levels of C. sativum plants by 26% and 23%, respectively, as compared to the untreated plants (Figure B). As shown in Figure C, salt stress also increased the relative membrane permeability (RMP) by 39% and 70%, respectively, at the S1 and S2 salt stress levels compared to control plants. However, the application of CaP-NPs decreased the RMP in these salt-stressed plants by 10% and 23%, respectively, compared with the respective controls (Figure C).

Discussions

Soil salinization is a major constraint to global agricultural productivity, significantly reducing the yield of important horticultural crops, including coriander. Salt stress negatively affects coriander growth by inducing oxidative stress, disrupting ion homeostasis, and impairing water uptake. In the present study, foliar application of CaP-NPs mitigated these adverse effects, enhancing plant growth and physiological performance. Salinity stress resulted in a significant decline in shoot and root length, as well as fresh and dry biomass, primarily due to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damage cellular structures and inhibit cell division. , However, CaP-NP application improved these growth parameters, likely by enhancing ROS scavenging and stabilizing cellular membranes. Calcium is well-known for its role in maintaining membrane integrity, regulating ion transport, and improving stress tolerance. Additionally, phosphorus from CaP-NPs plays a crucial role in energy metabolism and root development, further supporting plant growth under salinity stress.

Salinity stress significantly reduced chlorophyll content, a widely reported phenomenon in stressed plants. Chlorophyll degradation can result from ROS-mediated oxidative damage, activation of chlorophyllase, and impairment of biosynthetic pathways. Chlorophylls and carotenoids are key photosynthetic pigments involved in light harvesting, with carotenoids playing a crucial role in protecting chlorophyll from photooxidative damage by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) specifically under stressful conditions such as salinity stress. This could be the reason for the increased level of carotenoid content in salt-stressed plants (Figure D). An increase in carotenoid content under salt stress has also been observed in maize, L. fusca, and sugarcane. In our study, CaP-NP treatment significantly restored chlorophyll levels, likely due to the protective role of calcium in stabilizing chloroplast membranes and reducing oxidative damage. However, despite increasing the level of chlorophyll, CaP-NPs reduced carotenoid levels. Carotenoids function as photoprotective pigments, scavenging ROS to prevent oxidative damage. This reduction in carotenoids despite salinity stress suggests that CaP-NPs effectively controlled ROS accumulation, thereby lowering the need for carotenoid-mediated photoprotection and allowing metabolic resources to be allocated toward chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthesis.

Photosynthetic efficiency is often compromised under salinity due to stomatal closure, impaired electron transport, and reduced levels of CO2 assimilation. Our results show that CaP-NPs significantly improved gas exchange parameters, including net photosynthesis rate (P n), stomatal conductance (g s), and transpiration rate (E). The role of calcium in regulating stomatal aperture and reducing excessive water loss under stress conditions is well established. Additionally, phosphorus is a critical component of ATP and NADPH, essential for the Calvin cycle and electron transport chain, further supporting enhanced photosynthesis. , Salt stress significantly reduced RWC and WUE, leading to water loss and impaired metabolic processes. , CaP-NP treatment alleviated these effects, improving RWC and WUE. Calcium enhances osmotic adjustment by stabilizing cell membranes and maintaining the ion balance, while phosphorus contributes to energy metabolism, enabling plants to utilize water more efficiently. Enhanced WUE indicates that CaP-NPs improve water retention, allowing plants to maintain turgor pressure and sustain physiological functions under salinity stress.

Lipid peroxidation and membrane damage, indicated by increased MDA and H2O2 levels, are hallmark symptoms of salt-induced oxidative stress. , Elevated peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids reduces membrane fluidity and increases permeability, leading to severe membrane damage, which contributes to growth retardation and decreased pigment content observed in this study under salt stress. Ahmad et al. reported a similar trend in pea leaves, where salinity-induced increases in H2O2 and MDA levels were associated with reduced growth and pigment content. Likewise, Frukh et al. documented that H2O2 accumulation led to increased MDA levels in rice under salt stress. CaP-NP treatment significantly reduced these stress indicators, suggesting a protective role in maintaining the membrane stability and reducing oxidative damage. Calcium aids in strengthening and preserving the structural integrity of the cell membrane, activates calmodulins, and enhances cytokinin synthesis, which contributes to increased cell division and growth in totality. Additionally, phosphorus enhances stress tolerance by improving ATP synthesis and energy-dependent repair mechanisms. ,

To counteract ROS accumulation, salt-stressed plants activate antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). , Consistent with these findings, Khan et al. reported enhanced activity of three key antioxidant enzymes under 120 and 150 mM NaCl in pearl millet, while Ahmad et al. demonstrated increased SOD and CAT activities in salt-stressed tomato seedlings. Our results indicate that CaP-NP treatment significantly upregulated these enzyme activities, thereby reducing oxidative stress. Supporting this perspective, Upadhyaya et al. reported that CaP-NPs reduced MDA and ROS levels at 10 and 20 mg L–1 by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, particularly CAT. Similarly, Xu et al. observed a significant increase in SOD, CAT, and POX activities, alongside a reduction in ROS production, in C. mongolicum supplemented with Ca2+ under oxidative stress. The role of Ca2+ in stimulating SOD and CAT activities has also been confirmed in tomato and rice. Flavonoids and phenolic compounds, known for their nonenzymatic antioxidant activity, effectively neutralize lipid-free radicals. , Additionally, flavonoids stabilize membrane lipids by interacting with the polar region of phospholipids, thereby preventing membrane disruption and oxidative damage. Their accumulation is a well-established indicator of salt tolerance due to their ability to mitigate ROS generation. Proline is an important osmolyte that accumulates in plants under stress, protecting cellular structures and stabilizing proteins. Our results showed that CaP-NP treatments increased proline levels, contributing to osmotic adjustment and stress tolerance. Total phenolics and flavonoids, known for their antioxidant properties, were also elevated, reinforcing the role of CaP-NPs in enhancing the plant’s defense system.

Regarding nutrient uptake, CaP-NP application significantly increased Ca, P, and K mineral content while reducing Na accumulation in shoots of C. sativum under stressful conditions. Phosphorus is known to facilitate sodium sequestration into vacuoles, preventing its toxic effects and thereby enhancing potassium uptake in the cytosol. This mechanism helps maintain ion homeostasis, improving plant growth under salinity stress. Similar to our results, Roy et al., Ahmad et al., de Andrade et al., and Mohamed et al. reported that phosphorus or Ca2+ supplementation improved crop resilience against soil salinity. The observed improvements in plant growth, photosynthesis, antioxidant defense, and nutrient uptake can be attributed to the dual action of calcium and phosphorus in CaP-NPs. Calcium plays a key role in membrane stabilization, ion homeostasis, and stress signaling, while phosphorus supports energy metabolism, ATP production, and enzymatic activity. Additionally, the nanoscale delivery of these nutrients enhances their bioavailability, ensuring efficient uptake and utilization by plants.

Conclusion

In this study, NaCl stress particularly at 150 mM significantly disrupted plant growth, biomass, and various physiological and biochemical attributes. However, foliar application of CaP-NPs effectively alleviated oxidative stress and enhanced growth, biomass, and physio-biochemical responses. Additionally, CaP-NPs modulated the enzymatic antioxidant defense system. NaCl stress also disturbed ionic balance by increasing the Na+ concentration in the roots and shoots of coriander plants, thereby reducing the K+/Na+ ratio and impairing ion homeostasis. Conversely, the CaP-NP application enhanced K+ uptake and restored ionic balance by maintaining the K+/Na+ ratio. This study demonstrated that foliar application of CaP-NPs effectively mitigated the detrimental effects of salinity stress in coriander by enhancing photosynthetic efficiency, improving nutrient uptake, and strengthening antioxidant defense. Future research should explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these responses and assess the long-term impact of CaP-NP application on soil health and crop productivity.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Synthesis of Calcium Phosphate Nanoparticles (CaP-NPs)

The first reagents were diammonium hydrogen phosphate ((NH4)2HPO4) and calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca­(NO3)4.4 H2O). Provided an aqueous 0.1 M calcium nitrate tetrahydrate solution in an ionized water solution of 0.06 M diammonium hydrogen phosphate in distilled water, a white precipitate was formed after putting a calcium nitrate tetrahydrate flask on a magnetic stirrer and allowing the sodium hydrogen phosphate solution to drop into it at a steady rate for about 3 to 4 h. The same goes for the control of the solution’s pH and the temperature in which it was incubated. The precipitate was then washed with distilled water and boiled with distilled water for 5 h, and then dried in an oven at 100 °C for an additional 5 h. Finally, the dried crystals were placed in a furnace at 600 °C (Figure ).

Characterization of CaP-NPs

In UV/vis spectrophotometer analysis, the CaP-NP sample showed a peak at 219 nm in a wavelength scan measurement in a range of 200–800 nm (Figure A). This could be due to the phosphate group, as its peak absorbance lies between 200 and 300 nm. The FTIR analysis of CaP-NPs showing a peak range between 3640 and 3530 cm–1 represented the −OH group present in the calcium phosphate structure. Similarly, the peaks within the range of 3400–3200 cm–1 showed the normal polymeric OH stretch, 1350–1260 cm–1 showed organic phosphates (PO stretch), and 554–693 cm–1 displayed the presence of the phosphate group in CaP-NPs. The presence of a peak at 1064 cm–1 showed asymmetric stretching of the phosphate group, which is a content feature in FTIR spectra of various CaP-NPs (Figure B). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of CaP-NPs showed that these nanoparticles had a rod-like shape (Figure C). Additionally, the maximum fraction of particles lies within the nanoscale range (20–100 nm), as shown in Figure D. Experimental Design and Layout

This experiment was conducted at the Botanical Garden, University of Education, Lahore, Pakistan. Seeds of Coriandrum sativum L. were procured from the Ayub Agricultural Research Institute, Faisalabad, Pakistan. A pilot experiment was conducted for the dose optimization of CaP-NPs. In this pilot experiment, three doses (25, 50, and 100 mg L–1) were selected for CaP-NPs and its precursors, i.e., (NH4)2HPO4 and Ca­(NO3)4·4H2O. Results provided in Figure S1 revealed that foliar spray of CaP-NPs outcompeted its precursors at all concentrations. Following that, a two-way, completely randomized design experiment with three replicates was conducted under open field conditions. Over the experimental duration, the average day/night temperature was 28/17 °C and relative humidity was 66% with an average rainfall of 10 mm and an 11/13-hour day/night photoperiod. Garden soil was collected, sieved, air-dried, and then used to fill the pots (5 kg/pot) having 20 cm length and 15 cm diameter. The physicochemical properties of the soil used in this experiment are listed in Table . Seeds were rinsed with 5% sodium hypochlorite and washed twice with 95% ethanol for surface sterilization. Healthy and similarly sized seeds (10–15) were sown at an equal distance. In the first week of germination, thinning was performed and 6–8 seedlings were maintained in every pot at equal distances. Two weeks after germination, the following treatments were applied: control (water only; EC: 0.6 dS m–1), salt stress; S1 (100 mM NaCl), S2 (150 mM NaCl), and foliar application of CaP-NPs (50 mg/L). These concentrations were selected partially based on the results of the pilot experiment and previous studies conducted by Amiripour et al. and by Nasarallah et al. For salt stress, a NaCl solution was prepared and used with irrigation water, while CaP-NP foliar spray was applied using a manual hand sprayer. These treatments were applied three times in total during this whole experiment at intervals of 1 week. Two weeks after the completion of treatments, growth analysis and various physio-biochemical attributes were studied as explained below.

Chlorophyll and Carotenoid Content

The assessment of carotenoid as well as chlorophyll content was conducted according to the methods described by Arnon. A 0.5 g portion of leaves was ground using a mortar and pestle to make a paste. The obtained ground sample was then preserved in 10 mL of 80% acetone and followed by homogenization. After that, Whatman No. 42 filter papers were used in filtering the produced mixture. The filtrate was then collected and kept at a refrigeration temperature of 4 °C for another 24 h. The absorbance was assessed at three distinct wavelengths: 645, 663, and 480 nm.

The following formulas were used to calculate the chlorophyll a and b parts; Chl.a=[12.7(OD663)−2.69(OD645)]×V/1000×W Chl.b=[22.9(OD645)−4.68(OD663)]×V/1000×W

Carotenoid contents were estimated using the following formula mentioned. Carotenoids=(1000×OD480)−(1.9×Chl.a−63.14×Chl.b/214)

Measuring Performance of Photosynthetic Machinery and Quantum Yield of Photosystem II

For assessing the performance of photosynthetic machinery and the efficiency of PS II, we used an OS30p+ fluorometer (Opti-Sciences, Inc., Hudson, NH 03051, USA). The highest photochemical effectiveness in photosystem II (Fv/Fm) and OJIP parameters were evaluated using the fluorometer after 15 min of dark adaptation. The fully developed third leaf of each replicate was selected to read fluorescence at an incident light of 3000 μmol m–2 s–1.

Determination of Gas Exchange Attributes

An infrared gas analyzer (IRGA) LCpro SD (ADC Bioscientific Ltd., Hoddesdon, UK) was used for the determination of the gas exchange attributes. Measurements were taken between 10:00 and 12:00 am. A fully matured and young leaf was placed in an airtight leaf chamber, and after 3 min of acclimation to the chamber climate, the values for the photosynthetic rate, transpiration rate, intercellular CO2, and stomatal conductance were measured. The reference values for the leaf chamber climate were as follows: ambient CO2 (C ref) at 420–460 μmol mol–1, photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) or Qleaf at 915 μmol m–2 s–1, leaf chamber temperature (T ch) at 24–27 °C, leaf temperature at 25–27 °C, molar gas flow rate of the leaf chamber (U) at 201 μmol s–1, ambient pressure (P) at 999 kPa, and leaf area at 6.25 cm2.

Relative Water Content

Approximately equal-sized leaves were taken from each experimental replicate; the leaves were then weighed and immediately floated on distilled water at 25–26 °C for 3 h. The leaves became turgid, and the weight of the leaves was measured again. After that, these leaves were dried in an oven for 24 h at 80 °C to get their dry weight. RWC=[((FW−DW)/(TW−DW))×100]

Determination of Malondialdehyde (MDA) Content

Only a few modifications were made to the method described by Cakmak and Horst for this study. New leaves (0.5 g, as determined by the microbalance) were provided to each experimental replication. The leaves were then placed in a Styrofoam box with the temperature kept at 4 °C, and 1 g of the sample was homogenized in 3 mL of 1.0% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After a 15 min centrifugation at 20,000 g, the homogenate was reserved in conical tubes. To this filtrate, 0.5% thiobarbituric acid solution prepared in 3 mL of a 20% trichloroacetic acid solution was added to test tubes. After that, for the identification of the presence of the virus, all samples were treated with heating in a shaking water bath at 95 °C for 50 min. The reaction was quenched rapidly by using an ice water bath in a bid to foster the formation of the precipitate. All samples were then read at wavelengths of 532 and 600 nm after another 10 min of centrifugation at 10,000 g. MDA level(nmol)=[(A532nm−A600nm)/1.56×105]×V/W×1000000

Hydrogen Peroxide Determination

Young new foliage from each replicate was very carefully collected, weighed to the nearest 0.5 g, combined, and mixed vigorously with trichloroacetic acid (TCA) which was 0.1% (5 mL) using a pestle and mortar cooled in ice. These resultant extracts were then transferred to conical tubes for further operations. All the extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min with the temperature set at 4 °C. To test tubes, 1 mL of potassium iodide, 0.5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer with a pH of 7, and 0.5 mL of the obtained supernatant solution were added. The absorbance at 390 nm was measured using a UV/vis spectrophotometer after the vertexing of this mixture, as described by Alomrani et al.

Relative Membrane Permeability

Equal-sized leaves were taken from each replicate and chopped into small pieces. These pieces were immersed in test tubes containing 20 mL of distilled water. These samples were mixed thoroughly on a vortex machine, and electrical conductivity (EC0) was calculated. EC1 was recorded after the samples were left in a refrigerator set at 4 °C for 24 h. The same samples were then heated in an autoclave at 120 °C for 20 min to measure EC2. The formula given below was used to calculate the percentage of RMP. RMP(%)=[(EC1−EC0)/(EC2−EC0)]×100

Estimation of Total Soluble Protein

From each replicate, young leaves were sampled systematically at random, weighed (0.5 g), and then ground in 10 mL of a chilled 50 mM phosphate buffer solution with a pH of 7.8. The produced extracts were then transferred to conical flasks and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 6000 g for 20 min and at 4 °C. The separated supernatant was also gently drained from the pellet, and the latter was placed into a freezing device. The total protein content in the samples was determined using the method described by Bradford. The Bradford reagent was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of Coomassie brilliant blue in 50 mL of ethanol and 100 mL of 5% phosphoric acid, and then making it up to 1 L with distilled water. For the Bradford assay, 5 mL of the Bradford reagent was dropped to 0.1 mL of the samples that were stored in a deep freezer. In this study, the OD was then measured at 595 nm using a UV/vis spectrophotometer. For this purpose, a standard curve was prepared with serial dilutions of a sample with low absorbance.

Estimation of Antioxidant Activity

Utilizing a modified version of a method from ref , we evaluated the enzymatic functions of catalase and peroxidase.

Catalase (CAT) and Peroxidase (POD) Activity

To carry out the catalase reaction, we mixed 1 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.0, 1.9 mL of 5.9 mM H2O2, and 0.1 mL of the enzyme extract, making the total reaction volume 3 mL. The rate of the catalase was determined concerning the decrease of absorbance at 240 nm at intervals of 30 s up to 120 s after starting the reaction using a UV/vis spectrograph/otometer following the standard protocol set by Chance and Maehly.

Similarly, the POD reaction solution was prepared by mixing 0.7 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer at pH 5.0, 0.6 mL of 20 mM guaiacol, 0.6 mL of 40 mM H2O2, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract, and the reaction volume was 2 mL in total. The absorbance was then measured at 470 nm at 30 second intervals for 150 s by using the above spectrophotometer. The peroxidase activity was estimated in this assay using guaiacol as the substrate coupled with H2O2 and changes in oxidation at 470 nm.

Ascorbate Peroxidase Activity (APX)

For each replicate, 3 mL of the APX reaction mixture were prepared using 2.7 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer, 0.1 mL of 7.5 mM ascorbate, 0.1 mL of 300 H2O2, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. The APX reaction mixture was then color-measured for its absorbance using a UV/vis spectrophotometer at 290 nm for a total time of 60 s with 30-s intervals between the readings.

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Activity

The reaction mixture under investigation involves methionine, nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), EDTA-Na2, riboflavin solution, and enzyme solution. Here, methionine acts as a substrate, while NBT acts as an electron carrier to form an easily quantifiable blue formazan product, which is soluble at 560 nm. EDTA-Na2 is used to capture divalent metal ions to possibly reduce the frequency of undesirable side reactions, and riboflavin acts as a photosensitizer which upon photoactivation, generates ROS such as superoxide anions. The mixture is then exposed to light with an intensity of 4000 lx for 20 min and helps in the photoactivation of riboflavin. The generated superoxide radicals reduce NBT into formazan, which in turn allows for the measurement of enzyme activitypresumably related to superoxide dismutase (SOD) or a similar proteinby measuring the absorbance at 560 nm using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer. As an emerging facet of this enzyme’s activity, the current assay aims to establish the role of the enzyme in regulating ROS in the context of the experimental system.

Determination of Proline Content

Fresh foliage was ground in sulfo-salicylic acid and maintained at ambient temperature for 24 h. The mixture was centrifuged, and ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid were added to the supernatant. The mixture was heated at 100 °C for 1 h and then cooled in an ice bath. Toluene was used for extraction, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm.

Estimation of Total Phenolics

Dried leaf specimens of 0.50 g each were accurately weighed and scooped into several centrifuge tubes. After the specimens were homogenized for 1 min in 10 mL of 80% aqueous acetone, the suspension was centrifuged at 4000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was carefully decanted, and the solid residue that remained was prepared for further analysis for Pb in the following steps. The residue was evaporated to dryness, and residual methanol was removed from the solution.

The amount of total phenolics in all the prepared specimens was determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method. From each replicate of the prepared samples, 2 mL were pipetted into tubes; then, 1 mL of the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 0.8 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate were added to each tube. Both were further mixed vigorously and incubated for 30 min to obtain the samples. Absorbance readings were obtained.

Determination of Total Flavonoid Content

To the sample extract, 0.5 mL was pipetted, and to this, 2.25 mL of distilled water were added in a lab test tube. Next, 5% NaNO2 solution (0.15 mL) was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 6 minutes. Then, 10% AlCl3·6H2O solution (0.3 mL) was added. Subsequently, 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (1.0 mL) was added after a further 5 min of incubation; the solution was mixed with a vortex. Subsequently, the absorbance was immediately determined at 510 nm, which is an optical density measurement. As described by Dogan et al., the results were reported in milligrams of rutin per hundred grams of the dried sample (mg/100 g).

Inorganic Mineral Ion Content

Dry root and shoot specimens were collected using a pestle and mortar apparatus and were made into fine powders. A volumetric measurement of 0.1 g of the shoot and root samples was digested with sulfuric acid (H2SO4). The samples were strengthened by adding 2 mL of H2SO4 to the test tubes containing the samples. After 1 day, the samples were heated on a hot plate and then gradually, and H2O2 was added until the solution became colorless. Following that, distilled water was also added to the blend. The final concentration of the solution was 50 mL. The analytical measurements for sodium (Na), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca) ions in this mixture were performed using a flame photometer after filtration. To determine phosphorus content, the digested samples were mixed with 5 mL of the ammonium molybdate reagent prepared in HNO3 and their absorbance was recorded at 470 nm using a UV/vis spectrophotometer. ,

Statistical Analysis

The statistical software R (ver. 4.4.2) was employed to conduct a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for all parameters examined throughout the research study. Duncan’s multiple range test was used for mean comparison on R software. Pearson’s correlation and principal component analysis were also performed on R software at a p ≤ 0.05 significance level.
