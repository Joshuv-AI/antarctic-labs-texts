Advancing post-stroke blood pressure management: an individualized BP strategy for function optimization
Authors: Suli Yu, Chi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke remains a major global public health concern and a leading cause of death, disability, and dementia. Despite being the most important and modifiable risk factor for stroke, Blood pressure (BP) management remain controversial and challenging in post-stroke population.ObjectiveThis study explores the critical role of blood pressure in supporting post-stroke neural function and discusses the need for individualized BP targets to optimize brain reorganization and functional rehabilitation.MethodsFor this narrative review, PubMed, EMBASE (from inception o Jan 1,2024) have been searched about studies that investigated the relationship between blood pressure control and post-stroke function outcomes. Existing strategies prioritize mortality reduction and recurrence prevention rather than functional restoration, overlooking outcomes vital to survivors’ independence.To address this gap, we propose a conceptual framework that aligns BP regulation with functional outcomes and utilizes artificial intelligence (AI) and data-driven technologies to tailor BP control strategies to each patient’s unique physiological and neurological profile.ConclusionThis individualized and function-oriented BP management represents a paradigm shift in post-stroke care. This personalized approach holds promise for improving rehabilitation outcomes and advancing the integration of precision medicine in stroke care.

Introduction

Stroke damages the cerebral vasculature and impairs neurological function. The vascular structure and neurological function are closely intertwined. Blood pressure (BP), which is the most modifiable risk factor and an important component of vascular factors, is significantly correlated with stroke prognosis.

Elevated BP [1] levels have been reported in over 80% of patients with stroke. Overload of high BP can aggravate brain cell edema, increase hemorrhage risk in infarct patients, or enlarge bleeding areas in hemorrhage patients, potentially leading to brain herniation or death. Conversely, excessively low BP may result in insufficient blood supply to the brain, enlargement of the infarct area, and exacerbation of neurological symptoms.

The criteria used to define the appropriate limits for blood pressure control post-stroke are controversial and far from definitive. A significant body of research, which has substantially influenced the development of clinical guidelines [2–6], has predominantly targeted critical outcomes, such as death, dependency, recurrent stroke, and cardiac complications (myocardial infarction, non-valvular atrial fibrillation, heart failure, etc.). However, there is a pronounced scarcity of research examining the impact of blood pressure control on restoring functional capabilities that remain impaired after the acute phase of stroke has subsided. Functional recovery is of primary concern to almost all survivors, and it is central to a patient’s quality of life and independence.

Generally, cerebral blood vessels possess the ability to self-regulate and accommodate vascular pressure within a certain range in response to fluctuations in blood pressure. However, dynamic blood flow regulation is often compromised within 96 h after stroke onset; these cerebral auto-regulation disruptions can continue for weeks or months [7,8]. Local stroke lesions may disrupt the intrinsic coordination between autonomic-central brain activity, leading to that post-stroke cerebral blood flow relies more directly on the pressure from cardiac constriction [9], which correlates with the Blood Pressure under discussion.

The rhythmic automatic nerve function that regulates the dynamic fluctuations of blood pressure is an integral component of the top-down modulation of the brain-heart axis, which represents the intricate interplay between the heart and the brain. Study [10] has illuminated that brain modulation offers promising avenues for the management of cardiac disease. The bidirectional communication between brain and heart could be instrumental in the development of more effective strategies for post-stroke management, even revolutionize blood pressure treatment strategies.

In this context, it becomes essential to investigate the impact of BP fluctuations on neruological function. We first critically analyzed the existing evidence on post-stroke blood pressure management by reviewing literature that examined the impact of BP fluctuations on functional recovery, highlight limitations in current approaches—such as reliance on generalized outcome measures and short-term BP monitoring. We then investigated the impact of vascular pressure fluctuations on neural function from both macroscopic and microscopic perspectives, and further discussed the necessity of individualized post-stroke BP regulation from a multifaceted standpoint. Finally, we proposed a conceptual framework for function-oriented BP regulation and clinical implementation. Our work aims to advance post-stroke BP management shift from a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to an individualized, intelligent and function-oriented strategy.

Literature search and analysis

To investigate the impact of blood pressure fluctuations on neurological function, we searched PubMed (from Jan 1, 1970 to Jan 1, 2024) and Embase (from Jan 1, 1947 to Jan 1, 2024) for publications with relevant text words in the title or abstract in any language that included ‘stroke’ or ‘isch(a)emic stroke’ or ‘Cerebral hemorrhage’ or ‘intracerebral hemorrhage’, and ‘blood pressure’, ‘hypertension’, ‘blood pressure management’ or ‘blood pressure lowering’. We ultimately included 21 studies that met the predefined criteria. Among them, 13 studies reported no significant association between blood pressure (BP) fluctuations and post-stroke functional outcomes, while the remaining 8 studies suggested that early BP reduction might have a detrimental effect on functional recovery. Further analysis revealed that the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was the most frequently used tool to assess functional outcomes. Three studies utilized the Barthel Index [14,18,19], and two studies adopted custom-defined outcome measures—one [11] assessing the number of days spent in hospital or under nursing care, and another [16] evaluating the need for assistance with daily activities. It is worth noting that these outcome measures—whether mRS, the Barthel Index, or investigator-defined endpoints—represent generalized assessments of disability and do not specifically quantify individual functional deficits commonly seen after stroke, such as motor, language, or cognitive impairments.

We also observed that BP measurements were predominantly conducted within 1–2 weeks after stroke onset (ranging from 24 h to 3 weeks), whereas functional outcomes were mainly assessed at two time points: 2 weeks and 90 days (ranging from 36 h to 12 months). This indicates a temporal mismatch between BP data collection and the timing of functional outcome evaluations. None of the included studies reported longitudinal BP variability data that spanned the full duration of functional follow-up, thereby limiting the ability to assess cumulative BP effects over time. Short-term BP observations may not capture delayed or aggregated effects of BP modulation on neurological recovery.

Given these limitations, we highlight the need for further investigations that extend BP monitoring throughout the post-stroke recovery timeline and incorporate more specific, domain-targeted functional assessments. Such efforts will be essential to clarify the true relationship between BP dynamics and functional prognosis and to inform individualized, function-oriented BP management strategies in stroke care.

Blood pressure and function outcomes

Under physiological conditions, cerebral autoregulation maintains stable cerebral blood flow despite systemic BP variations [32], the ideal target range for perfusion pressure lies within this range [33]. However, following a stroke, this autoregulatory mechanism is often compromised, leading to increased susceptibility of the brain to BP variations.

Emerging evidence underscores the detrimental effects of increased BP variability (BPV) on neurological outcomes poststroke. A study [34] involving 13,206 participants found that increased BPV, measured by standard deviation, was associated with a higher risk of poor functional outcomes at 1 month post-stroke.

A study [35] assessing day-by-day BPV within the first seven days post-acute ischemic stroke found that higher systolic BPV was independently associated with unfavorable outcomes at three months, as measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).

Another investigation [36] demonstrated that increased BPV independently predicted poor functional outcomes at both 1 and 12 months following acute ischemic stroke. This association was observed across various BPV metrics, including standard deviation and coefficient of variation of systolic and diastolic BP.

Furthermore, long-term BP variability, rather than absolute BP levels alone, has emerged as the strongest predictor of cerebrovascular disease [37]. Zhang [38] et al. suggest no association between diastolic blood pressure variability during the first 24 h after admission and functional outcome at 3 or 6 months of ischemic stroke. Oscillations in BP over weeks to months may contribute to ongoing cerebrovascular damage. One study [39] showed that gaining better blood pressure control over a 4-year period reduces the progression of cerebral white matter lesions and overall cognitive decline. This underscores the necessity for sustained BP control, especially since fluctuations in blood pressure during the recovery period after stroke have a more profound effect on neurological remodeling.

However, the true mechanisms underlying the relationships between BPV and the decline in brain function remain uncertain [40]. The pathophysiological consequences of BPV are thought to involve hemodynamic instability, leading to cerebral hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and subsequent inflammatory processes. These alterations have been associated with vascular remodeling, increased arterial stiffness, and enhanced β-amyloid deposition [41,42]. Moreover, systolic BPV has been linked to reductions in white matter microstructural integrity and accelerated brain atrophy [43]. Specifically, a recent study [44] from the Radboud University Nijmegen Diffusion tensor Magnetic resonance-imaging Cohort (RUNDMC) provides strong longitudinal evidence supporting that long-term (14-year) BPV contributes to progressive structural damage to white matter, a critical substrate of the brain’s functional connectivity.

Although accumulating evidence links blood pressure variability to structural changes in the brain, these alterations ultimately manifest as impaired neurological function. The critical challenge lies in elucidating the dynamic interplay between vascular pressure fluctuations and neural activity. Specifically, the pulsatile pressure environment generated by the cardiac cycle becomes particularly consequential in the post-stroke brain, where the loss of cerebrovascular autoregulation renders neural function increasingly dependent on systemic blood pressure dynamics.

Vessel pressure and neural function

Brain performance is affected by both neural and non-neural factors [45,46]. Vascular maintenance is essential for successful brain function. Stroke lesions(s) cause vascular alterations and interrupt the functionality of the cerebrovascular bed. The ill-preserved branching patterns and vessel length between the carotid arteries and capillaries after single- or multi-vessel lesions resulted in heterogeneous hemodynamic consequences.

Vessel pressure fluctuation within certain ranges of pulsatility generated by cyclic cardiac contractions is critical for maintaining the balance between the vascular-and-neural exchange of substance and energy. Dynamic blood flow regulation in response to changes in blood pressure is part of vascular maintenance, and impairment of autoregulation results in surpassing the optimal range of vascular pulsatility from arterial pressure, which may cause further damage to neural function [47]. Some researchers have claimed that extreme arterial-pressure pulsatility is the strongest pathological factor affecting neurological functions [48,49].

Vascular deregulation after vessel lesions disrupts neurovascular coupling, such as the regulation of blood flow, delivery of nutrients, and clearance of waste products in response to local neural activity [50–53]. Cai et al. [54] found that when specific brain functions are engaged, related neurons are activated, triggering a local vascular response. If the increased neuron metabolic demand does not match the vascular flow response, normal neural activity will not be sustained, leading to impaired performance. This process may be more profound during brain reestablishment post-stroke. Given that post-stroke cerebral capillary pulsatility is more dependent on overall blood pressure, non-adaptive vascular pressure transfer may not correspond well with neural activities. Excessive neural activity in post-stroke neural reorganization requires higher energy and nutrient metabolism, which translates to neuronal excitability or neurodegenerative changes [55,56] if the blood supply fails to keep up. Similarly, increased pulsatility from extravagant blood pressure may induce blood-brain barrier (BBB) breakdown [57,58] and is linked to deficits in brain structure and function [58]. Elevated pulsatility also negatively affects the efficacy of cerebral spinal fluid in clearing toxic brain metabolic products [57,58].

The detrimental effects of aberrant vascular pulsatility on neurovascular coupling and neural integrity—particularly in the context of stroke-induced vascular dysregulation—highlight a critical therapeutic challenge: how to modulate blood pressure dynamics to support functional recovery.

Post-stroke optimal blood pressure for function recovery

In a study by Marcus and colleagues [59], it was observed that blood pressure levels at the onset of ischemic stroke varied depending on the type of functional deficit. Patients with visual and neglect deficits tended to present with lower systolic blood pressure, with an average pressure of 157.7 ± 26.3 mmHg. In contrast, higher systolic blood pressures existed in those with motor deficits in the arms (160.0 ± 27.5 mmHg) or legs(159.8 ± 27.5 mmHg), as well as brainstem and cerebellar deficits (160.4/- 26.3 mmHg). These findings imply that there may be distinct blood pressure ranges associated with different neurological impairments post-stroke, raising the question of whether tailored blood pressure management strategies are required to facilitate various types of functional recovery.

In another study by He and team [60], they concentrated on post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) three months after ischemic stroke. The research revealed an optimal blood pressure range to be in the levels of 143 to 158(systolic)/93 to 102(diastolic) mmHg for the recovery phase post-stroke, which appeared to be at the lowest risk of developing PSCI. The study utilized the Montreal Cognitive Assessment to determine whether systolic blood pressures that were either too low (102–127 mmHg) or too high (171–215 mmHg) in the early stages of ischemic stroke were linked to a higher risk of PSCI after three months. Additionally, the study found that certain stroke subtypes, specifically large artery atherosclerosis and total anterior circulation infarct, were associated with an elevated risk of PSCI at the three-month mark.

He et al. showed that focusing on a single type of impairment over an extended period, such as three months, could help identify a blood pressure range that is well-suited to the recovery of that specific function. However, the approach to concentrate on cognitive impairment that does not consider the possibility of simultaneous function deficits might limit the comprehensive functional improvement that could be achieved by a more holistic rehabilitation strategy. Post-stroke deficits are known to vary, spanning motor-sensory impairment, language disorder, cognitive degeneration, visuospatial dysfunction, executive function deficit, personality, and mood change. Typically, patients with stroke exhibit one predominant type of dysfunction, often alongside other minor deficits. The diversity in post-stroke dysfunction highlights the need for modalities that cover a wide range of functions to comprehensively and effectively assess the extent of functional impairment and recovery.

Therefore, how is neurological function measured after a stroke? We analyzed the studies listed in Table 1, particularly those contributing to the establishment of blood pressure guidelines. The majority utilized the modified Rankin Scale(mRS) to evaluate functional outcomes. The mRS is a 7-point ordinal scale (ranging from 0 to 6) that evaluates the degree of disability or dependence in individuals with neurological conditions. While the mRS is a standardized, widely recognized instrument utilized as a primary or secondary outcome measure in stroke research, it has limitations in assessing specific impairments or nuances of cognitive and emotional functioning. Similar to other specific functional assessments, such as Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) or Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), the reliance on patients’ self-reported abilities and evaluators’ subjective scoring is insufficient to be an objective method to compare the efficacy of different treatments or interventions.

Therefore, this highlights the need for more detailed evaluations that can quantify the extent of brain damage and holistically evaluate the brain reorganization process. This is the first imperative step in finding a way determining the optimal range of blood pressure fluctuations to accommodate functional rehabilitation.

The assessment of dynamic brain reconstruction post-stroke

Brain performance, including motor control, speech, and cognition, is believed to be organized in large-scale network structures [61]. The brain operates economically and efficiently in a physiological state and functions as a network that maximizes information exchange at a relatively low connectivity level. This network reflects the brain’s sophisticated capacity to achieve a balance between efficient neural communication and conservation of energy and resources.

To elucidate the network properties and neural functions of the brain, Friston et al. [62] introduced the concepts of functional and effective connectivity.

Functional connectivity describes the temporal correlation of spontaneous activity among different brain regions. It provides [63] a visual description of the brain regions that signal changes in task execution on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and a snapshot of how different areas of the brain communicate, reflecting the integrative roles of brain communication during rest or in response to a specific task. Effective Connectivity delves into the causal relationships and directional influences between brain regions. This explains the degree of involvement of individual brain functional regions in cognitive processing or task execution, highlighting the synergistic cooperation within and between networks.

By applying the graph theory to the analysis of brain networks, researchers can quantify various connectivity parameters. This approach provides an objective comparative framework for assessing changes in the structural and functional organization of brain networks pre- and post-stroke, and their impact on individual behavioral functioning.

Stroke lesions disrupt the structural and functional networks in both perilesional and remote regions, resulting in pathological network patterns of connectivity reduction in interhemispheric brain regions and complementary increased connectivity in intrahemispheric regions. Deviations from the normal brain network positively correlated with the severity of functional deficits. Structural-functional connectivity coupling is decreased at large-scale brain networks in the stroke population and positively associated with the Fugl-Meyer assessment score [64]. The damaged brain network is characterized by significantly increased path length values (connectivity spending) and decreased global efficiency values (the productivity of information transfer). Zhang et al. [65] found that the pathological network after stroke showed decreased nodal efficiency and nodal degree in both the ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres, and decreased and increased nodal betweenness centrality in the contralesional hemisphere. These pathological brain network patterns correspond well with post-stroke dysfunction, offering multiple quantitative parameters to assess the functional network state and objectively evaluate the dynamic brain reorganization after stroke.

Network analysis offers a distinct advantage over traditional scale scores for assessing functional progress in stroke populations by providing a more nuanced and comprehensive perspective. Moreover, for some patients with mild neurological impairment, such as mild cognitive impairment(MCI), some studies [66,67] suggest that individuals who eventually develop mild cognitive impairment show significant changes in brain structure several years before clinical diagnosis or cognitive score decline. a meta-analysis [68] suggests that task-and region-specific function connectivity alterations can serve as a sensitive biomarker for MCI.

After identifying a viable measure for assessing functional outcomes, the next step in developing a functionally oriented precision blood pressure program involves thorough consideration of the many factors that can influence blood pressure regulation.

Factors involved in stroke-related BP management

Current BP guidelines establish stratified goals for BP management based on the cause of stroke (infarction or hemorrhage), the treatment modality adopted (reperfusion, no-reperfusion), and the stage of the disease course (acute phase, 24–72 h after stroke onset, and stable phase, usually 72 h after onset), as demonstrated in Figure 1. The guidelines clearly states that strict blood pressure control during the acute stage of cerebral hemorrhage offers more significant benefits compared to cerebral infarction in terms of hemorrhage transformation, mortality risk and neurological functional prognosis.

Stratified BP management recommended by current guidelines. SBP:systolic blood pressure; DBP:diastolic blood pressure; IVT: intravenous thrombolysis; MT:Endovascular Thrombectomy.

For reperfusion therapy, including intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and mechanical thrombectomy (MT), they introduce complex hemodynamic challenges, making BP management a critical determinant of both safety and efficacy. BP guidelines specifically mention the adoption of a more proactive blood pressure control strategy and indicates the blood pressure targets before and after reperfusion.

ENCHANTED [30] study suggested that very high pre-treatment BP (>185/110 mmHg) is associated with poor outcomes and increased risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) following IVT. Following successful recanalization, abrupt BP drops can impair cerebral blood flow, especially in patients with incomplete reperfusion. Studies like BEST-II [12] and ECHANTED/MT [31] have indicated that higher BP post-MT is associated with hemorrhagic complications, whereas lower BP may compromise penumbral tissue survival.

Given that reperfusion therapy significantly improves the functional outcome of patients, the blood pressure goal is primarily to avoid increased risk of reperfusion injury and intracranial haemorrhage.

Besides, there are many factors involved in the mechanism of post-stroke blood pressure elevation that should not be ignored in the management of blood pressure.

Different stroke subtypes may vary in their BP values. Systolic blood pressure in the acute phase following a stroke tends to resemble premorbid levels, showing only a slight increase post-event [69]. By contrast, patients with small vessel ischemic stroke tend to exhibit higher BP values compared to other stroke subtypes, often displaying a more significant SBP increase from recent premorbid levels [70]. Notably, patients with lacunar stroke are particularly prone to elevated BP when compared to other stroke causes [71–74]. A study linked larger infarctions with more prolonged elevation in BP during the initial 24 h following symptom onset [75]. Moreover, a small observational study investigating hemispheric laterality (right or left side) found that individuals with right insular lesions presented with higher systolic blood pressure(SBP) and diastolic blood pressure(DBP), increased heart rate, and elevated plasma catecholamine levels upon admission and over the subsequent 5 days [76].

Preexisting conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, kidney disease, and heart disease, often termed comorbidities, need to be considered, as they can significantly influence a patient’s condition. Early signs of subclinical organ damage, including cerebral, cardiovascular, and renal impairment, may be indicative of the patient-specific burden of hypertensive disease [77].

Additionally, age, sex, race, and ethnic differences in the distribution of stroke causes and related diseases should also be considered for a comprehensive evaluation in future studies [78].

Given the intricate relationship between blood pressure levels and various factors, a data-driven approach is needed to address the complexities of blood pressure management post-stroke. The transformative impact of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms has bolstered our confidence in achieving personalized healthcare goals.

AI framework of individual BP implementation

Leveraging the power of AI for data processing and analysis allows for the handling of complex datasets and extraction of meaningful insights that can guide clinical decision-making. Machine learning algorithms offer a unique advantage in uncovering patterns within medical datasets that may not be directly apparent.

Typically, the performance of an algorithm relies heavily on the quality of the selected features of the input data. When faced with the unknown, even professionals may not have reliable ‘experience’ a priori knowledge. In this case, deep neural networks prove valuable by propelling machine learning beyond ‘semi-automated data analysis’ into the realm of ‘fully automated data analysis’. It has the inherent capability to independently discriminate between important features, and by feeding with unknown but potentially relevant features (such as the factors discussed in the previous chapter), these neural networks (including hidden layers) can be continuously fine-tuned through feedback (activation functions and loss function), promising to uncover the nuanced interplay between blood pressure fluctuations and brain network alterations in diverse patient profiles.

Moreover, post-stroke brain reconstruction is a dynamic and evolving process may unfold over weeks to years. This temporal complexity underscores the value of reinforcement learning (RL), a subset of machine learning designed to optimize sequential decision-making. Within this framework, BP is modeled as an ‘agent’, whose policy—i.e. permissible fluctuation range—is adaptively tuned in response to patient-specific environmental states. These states include demographic variables (e.g. age, sex), clinical comorbidities (e.g. hypertension, diabetes), and stroke-specific features (e.g. lesion location, functional impairments). The ‘reward’ function is defined by improvements in functional outcomes, measured either through validated clinical scales (e.g. Fugl-Meyer Assessment) or quantitative neuroimaging parameters (e.g. functional connectivity metrics).

Crucially, the reward signal may not be immediately observable following each BP modulation but emerges cumulatively over time. This delayed feedback mirrors clinical reality, where functional gains often accrue gradually during recovery. The Deep Reinforcement Learning algorithm, through repeated interaction with patient data, learns to associate specific BP trajectories with favorable long-term outcomes, ultimately generating individualized, function-oriented BP control strategies. This data-driven feedback loop enables the continuous refinement of clinical decision pathways, bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world implementation, and laying the groundwork for precision medicine in rehabilitation.

The application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to optimize individualized blood pressure (BP) trajectories for functional recovery after stroke presents both promise and complexity. The feasibility of DRL in clinical neurorehabilitation depends on several key factors, particularly the availability of high-quality, multimodal data. The model requires longitudinal, patient-specific datasets encompassing continuous BP telemetry, neuroimaging-derived network connectivity metrics (e.g. fMRI-based graph theory parameters), and standardized functional outcome scores (such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment). These data enable the model to identify optimal BP adjustment strategies that maximize functional recovery through trial-and-error interactions with the simulated environment.

Robustness of the DRL model hinges on model generalizability and interpretability. Training the model with demographically and clinically diverse datasets is essential to reduce algorithmic bias and enhance fairness across populations. Potential sources of bias—such as underrepresentation of certain age groups, ethnicities, or comorbidity profiles—must be actively mitigated through stratified sampling, data augmentation, and fairness-aware learning strategies. Furthermore, incorporating explainability techniques (e.g. SHAP values or attention visualization) can enhance clinician trust and transparency by elucidating how specific inputs influence policy recommendations. Lastly, temporal dynamics are critical in modeling the evolving neurovascular response to BP modulation after stroke. The ability of DRL to incorporate delayed rewards aligns with the delayed and cumulative nature of neural remodeling, providing a theoretically sound foundation for modeling stroke recovery trajectories. By simulating the long-term consequences of BP strategies, DRL may offer a transformative, data-driven solution for personalized, function-oriented stroke care.

From conceptual frameworks to clinical implementation

Building upon the individualized blood pressure regulation strategy informed by the deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework, we propose a pathway toward real-world application. While the DRL model offers a powerful tool for simulating and optimizing BP trajectories tailored to functional recovery goals, its true clinical utility depends on effective translation into practical workflows. This requires not only the integration of multimodal data—such as continuous BP telemetry, functional assessments, and neuroimaging—but also adaptation to the realities of diverse clinical environments. In particular, resource-limited settings pose distinct challenges, where access to advanced neuroimaging (e.g. fMRI) and continuous BP monitoring may be restricted. Therefore, the transition from a conceptual framework to clinical implementation requires a flexible, tiered approach—beginning with pilot testing in high-resource centers and progressive adaptation for broader, decentralized use. The proposed reinforcement learning model could be integrated into electronic health records (EHRs) or mobile health applications, where minimal inputs (age, BP, stroke type, motor score) are entered by clinicians or caregivers. Conclusion

Neural and vascular networks are intertwined and interdependent in the normal functioning of the brain. Neither the neural nor vascular aspects are dispensable in brain rehabilitation after stroke. Restoring optimal functionality post-stroke is a paramount goal for survivors, and the management of blood pressure is a pivotal aspect of this endeavor.

Blood pressure, which is the most modifiable risk factor and an important component of vascular factors, correlates significantly with stroke prognosis. However, international blood pressure guidelines largely overlook functional recovery in stroke patients. We contend that a single set of BP values, ignoring specific functional deficits, may not be suitable for stroke survivors.

Moreover, advancements in precision medicine have marked a significant transition from generalized population-level treatments to more nuanced individual-level care. Medical decision-making is no longer solely based on the common characteristics of a patient group but is increasingly tailored to the specific attributes and needs of each patient. A unified blood pressure target fails to meet the diverse needs of different individuals.

Our work proposed an intelligent BP management system that transcends the traditional stratified management of various influencing factors. Instead, it aims to design a dynamic blood pressure trajectory that evolves in response to the patient’s functional progress throughout the rehabilitation cycle. We advocate for further research to contribute to this goal. The integration of advanced technologies and analytical methods suggests that this approach is not only promising, but also feasible in the foreseeable future.
