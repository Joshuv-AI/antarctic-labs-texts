Emapalumab use in malignancy-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in the United States: the REAL-HLH study
Authors: Adi Zoref-Lorenz, Carl E Allen, Edward M Behrens, Shanmuganathan Chandrakasan, May Chien, Michelle L Hermiston, Ashley P Hinson, Stefanos Intzes, Michael S Isakoff, Jennifer W Leiding, Abiola Oladapo, Arun Panigrahi, Sachit A Patel, Priti Pednekar, Anish K Ray, Jennifer A Rothman, Blachy Dávila Saldaña, Kelly J Walkovich, Joanna Weinstein, John D Yee, Michael B Jordan, REAL-HLH Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Real-world data showed that emapalumab-containing HLH-directed regimens improved or maintained normal values for most key laboratory parameters in patients with mHLH.•Despite low overall survival, the potential effectiveness of emapalumab-containing HLH-directed regimens in controlling hyperinflammation was shown.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare, aggressive, potentially life-threatening, systemic hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by excessive and uncontrolled immune activation.1, 2, 3 HLH may occur due to inherited defects in the immune system (primary HLH [pHLH]) or persistent immune stimulation by an antigenic trigger, such as infection, malignancy, autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases (eg, rheumatologic disease), metabolic disease, iatrogenic immune suppression, or other diseases (secondary HLH [sHLH]).1,2 Untreated HLH can rapidly progress to fatal multiorgan failure and death.4

Malignancy is the most frequent HLH trigger in adults (45%), but is a relatively less frequent, albeit significant condition in children (8%).5 Malignancy-associated HLH (mHLH) describes pathologic inflammation that meets HLH criteria, and arises in the setting of malignancy either during onset or relapse of disease.6 mHLH may also occur in response to cancer-directed therapy (chemotherapy), immune-activating drugs (eg, immune checkpoint blockade, chimeric antigenic receptor T-cell therapy), or bone marrow transplant.5,7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 mHLH is typically observed with hematologic malignancies, such as T/natural killer (NK)-cell lymphomas, diffuse large B-cell lymphomas, Hodgkin lymphoma, and leukemia, but can also occur in solid tumors, mostly in the context of treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors.1,5,7,16, 17, 18 Consistent with other types of HLH, mHLH may be triggered by viral, bacterial, or fungal infections.19,20 mHLH has an extremely poor prognosis, with a survival rate of <20% even after treatment, and a median overall survival of ∼2 months in adults, and 1.2 years in children and adolescents.5,9,21, 22, 23

Management of mHLH is complex, and requires an individualized, multidisciplinary approach to control the hyperinflammation, treat the underlying malignancy, and eliminate any triggers.9 To date, there are no randomized clinical trials or prospective studies determining effective therapeutic regimens. Treatment is driven by expert opinion.7 Current management of mHLH involves control of hyperinflammation using etoposide and/or corticosteroids and directed treatment of the underlying malignancy. Due to considerable overlap in the symptoms of HLH and malignancy, patient heterogeneity, and limited clinical evidence on effective therapies, no clear guidance exists on which of these treatments should be initiated first.2,3,6,24 Corticosteroids are frequently used as the initial response to mHLH, with the dual goal of preventing further organ damage and providing the ability to tolerate malignancy-specific therapy.2,3,6,8

Pathologic inflammation in HLH is driven by the persistent activation and expansion of T lymphocytes and macrophages causing excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ).6,25,26 IFN-γ, a key mediator of systemic hyperinflammation, is significantly elevated in HLH, especially in infection- or rheumatologic disease-associated HLH, but not in other febrile or rheumatologic diseases.27 High serum IFN-γ levels correlate with disease manifestation.27 In mouse models of pHLH, neutralization of tissue IFN-γ, but not of other cytokines, reduced HLH-like symptoms and prolonged survival.26 Abnormally high levels of IFN-γ and its inducible chemokine, chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), are also observed in lymphoma-associated HLH. In patients with mHLH due to antigen-activation by tumor, CXCL9 levels decreased with tumor burden in response to chemotherapy, suggesting its potential use not only as a biomarker but also as a predictor of therapeutic outcomes.28

Emapalumab is a fully human immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody that binds to IFN-γ and neutralizes its biologic activity.29 Emapalumab improved clinical and laboratory parameters of patients with pHLH, and has an established safety profile.30 It was first approved for pHLH in the United States in November 2018, and remains the only approved treatment for adult and pediatric patients with pHLH with refractory, recurrent, or progressive disease, or intolerance to conventional HLH therapy. Emapalumab also resolved HLH and improved treatment outcomes in patients with infection-associated sHLH and autoinflammatory sHLH, and macrophage activation syndrome.31, 32, 33

Unlike randomized clinical trials conducted in controlled settings and selective populations, real-world studies provide valuable evidence on drug utilization, effectiveness, and safety in routine clinical practice.34 The REAL-HLH study was conducted to assess real-world treatment patterns and outcomes among patients with HLH treated with emapalumab in the United States. In this report, results of the subset of patients with mHLH from the REAL-HLH study are presented.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective medical chart review study conducted across 33 hospitals in the United States from 20 November 2018 to 31 December 2021. Patients treated with ≥1 dose of emapalumab between 20 November 2018 and 31 October 2021 (patient identification period) in routine clinical practice were identified, and their chart data were abstracted. Patient records were analyzed from the time of emapalumab initiation (index date) to the end of the study (31 December 2021), end of data availability, or death, whichever occurred first. The protocol and data collection forms were reviewed by the Western Institutional Review-Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board (IRB) and local IRBs, where required. There was no direct patient involvement in the study; therefore, the central and local IRBs approved a waiver of informed consent. Anonymized data collection was conducted in compliance with US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act policies.

Patients

Charts of patients of any age who had received ≥1 dose of emapalumab in routine clinical practice between 20 November 2018 and 31 October 2021 were abstracted. Patients were classified as presenting with sHLH if they were treated for HLH with evidence of an underlying disorder such as malignancy, rheumatologic disease, infection etc, and did not have any genetic mutation or family history. This report focuses on the subset of all patients with HLH occurring as a complication of underlying malignancy (mHLH) and not in response to malignancy-directed treatment.

Outcomes and assessments

The primary objective of the REAL-HLH study was to describe emapalumab treatment patterns (time to initiation of emapalumab from HLH diagnosis, treatment duration, dosing patterns, and reasons for initiating emapalumab) among patients with HLH treated with emapalumab. The reasons for initiating emapalumab included to treat progressive (worsening), relapsed/recurrent (reoccurrence after improvement), or refractory (unresponsive to treatment) disease. HLH-related therapies that were administered before or concurrently with emapalumab were also analyzed.

Secondary objectives included patient demographics and clinical characteristics, and treatment outcomes. The treatment outcomes assessed were change in glucocorticoid (GC) dosing over time, the proportion of patients who achieved or maintained physician-determined normalization of laboratory parameters at any time during treatment, and time to first normalization of laboratory parameters. The proportion of patients with abnormal laboratory values was based on data availability at the time closest to or at emapalumab initiation. The proportion of patients achieving or maintaining normal laboratory parameter values was based on data availability for those patients at that specified time point, and was determined only for parameters for which data were available for ≥50% of patients. Patients with missing data at that time point were excluded from the analysis at that time point and, therefore, patient numbers may vary across time points. For ferritin, clinically acceptable reductions, defined as <2000 ng/mL or ≥80% reduction from the value at the index date, were also evaluated.30 Other outcomes included the proportion of patients considered for and who received hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT), and overall survival during the study and 12 months from emapalumab initiation. The reasons for patients not receiving transplant and causes of death were reported.

Statistical analysis

As this was not a statistical hypothesis-driven study, power calculation was not necessary for sample size estimation. The sample size was determined by chart availability for eligible patients at the participating treatment centers. Continuous variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges. Categoric variables were summarized using counts and percentages. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to evaluate time-to-event analyses, with a 2-sided 95% confidence interval for survival estimates.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 105 patients from 33 hospitals across the United States who had received ≥1 dose of emapalumab were included in the REAL-HLH chart review study. The median (range) number of patients per site was 2 (1-15). Overall, 51 of 105 (48.6%) patients were categorized as presenting with sHLH (Table 1). Based on underlying disease, these patients (n = 51) were further classified as having mHLH (n = 17) or rheumatologic disease-associated HLH (n = 15), or as “others” (n = 19; patients with miscellaneous underlying diseases and/or HLH triggers that could not be categorized as rheumatologic or malignant disease).

Most patients with mHLH (n = 17) were male (12/17; 70.6%) and non-White (10/17; 58.8%; Table 1). Patients were older than those in the other sHLH subgroups, with a median (range) age at diagnosis of 15.0 (3.0-27.0) years (Table 1). The most common malignancies were acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL; n = 6), acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and lymphoma (n = 3 each), and lymphoproliferative disorder (n = 2; Figure 1). Other malignancies included T-cell ALL plus T-cell lymphoma, myelodysplastic syndrome, and non-hematologic malignancy (n = 1 each). Overall, 6 of 14 (42.9%) patients with data available for both soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sCD25) and ferritin at HLH diagnosis had a positive Optimized HLH Inflammatory (OHI) index (sCD25 levels >3900 U/mL and ferritin levels >1000 ng/mL).24 More than half (9/17; 52.9%) of the patients presented with concomitant infections; viral infections were the most frequent (6/9; 66.7%; Table 1; supplemental Table 1). More than half (10/17, 58.8%) of the patients were critically ill and received emapalumab in an intensive care unit. Treatment patterns

Emapalumab was most frequently added to HLH-related regimens to treat refractory (unresponsive to treatment = 10/17; 58.8%) and progressive (worsening = 3/17; 17.7%) mHLH, as defined by the treating physician. Other reasons (n = 4) for initiating emapalumab were to treat suspected HLH in the setting of ALL and dysregulated immune response with severe liver toxicity, graft failure after stem cell transplant, reluctance to use conventional therapy (etoposide and prolonged use of steroids) in the context of fever and neutropenia, and as first-line therapy.

Most patients received HLH-related therapies prior to (16/17; 94.1%) and/or concurrent with (15/17; 88.2%) emapalumab (Table 2). All patients were treated with corticosteroids before or concurrent with emapalumab. Etoposide was the next most frequently used HLH-related therapy used prior (43.8%) to or concurrent (33.3%) with emapalumab.

Treatment outcomes

Change in average daily GC dose

The change in average daily GC dose over 8 weeks of treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens is shown in Figure 3 and supplemental Table 3. The median average daily GC dose was 1.75 mg/kg prednisone equivalent (n = 13) during the week preceding emapalumab initiation, which reduced by 72% to 0.49 mg/kg at week 4 (n = 10) of treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens. The median average daily dose among patients who survived and had available data at week 8 (n = 3) increased to 2.14 mg/kg.

Physician-determined normalization or maintenance of normal laboratory parameter values

The proportion of patients with abnormal laboratory values at the time closest to or at emapalumab initiation is shown in Table 1. Treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens normalized, improved, or maintained normal values for most laboratory parameters (Figure 4A; supplemental Table 4). The proportion of patients who achieved or maintained physician-determined normal values for each laboratory parameter at any time during treatment was 7 of 8 (87.5%) for CXCL9, 11 of 13 (84.6%) for fibrinogen, 6 of 10 (60%) for absolute neutrophil count, 9 of 17 (52.9%) for alanine transaminase, 3 of 9 (33.3%) for sCD25, 3 of 11 (27.3%) for absolute lymphocyte count, and 4 of 17 (23.5%) for platelets. Of 11 patients with sCD25 data at the index date, 1 patient achieved and 7 patients maintained sCD25 <3900 U/mL after 8 weeks of treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens; 2 of 6 patients with a positive OHI index achieved sCD25 <3900 U/mL. Although ferritin did not normalize in any patient, clinically acceptable reductions (<2000 ng/mL or ≥80% reduction from the value at the index date, whichever was lower) in median ferritin levels were observed during treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens (supplemental Tables 4 and 5). The median time to physician-determined first normalization of laboratory parameters ranged from 7 days (absolute lymphocyte count, alanine transaminase, and fibrinogen) to 38 days (platelets; Figure 4A; supplemental Table 4). Emapalumab-containing regimens as a bridge to malignancy-directed therapy, including HSCT

Overall, 9 of 16 (56.3%) patients who were treated with emapalumab-containing regimens were considered for HSCT, and 2 of 9 (22.2%) received transplant (Figure 4B). Seven patients were considered for transplant, but did not receive it due to death (n = 3), the clinical condition or status did not allow for transplant (n = 2), development of infection during conditioning for bone marrow transplant in a setting of uncontrolled lymphoproliferative disease (n = 1), and resolution of HLH (n = 1). Seven patients were not considered for transplant due to death (n = 3), the clinical status or condition did not allow for transplant (n = 3), and HLH that seemed to be secondary to AML and fungemia, both of which were resolved (n = 1).

Overall survival

At the end of the study, 4 of 17 patients were alive (overall survival: 23.5%). The time from initiation of emapalumab-containing regimens to end of follow-up for patients who survived was 127 days for patient 3, 356 days for patient 4, 649 days for patient 14, and 489 days for patient 16. The 1-, 3-, and 12-month survival probabilities from initiation of emapalumab-containing regimens were 52.9%, 35.3%, and 22.1% , respectively (Figure 4C), and the median (range) duration of follow-up was 43 (8-649) days. The proportion of surviving patients was lower among patients who were OHI index positive at HLH diagnosis (1/6; 16.6%; supplemental Figure 1A) or had an Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-triggered infection (0/4; 0%; supplemental Figure 1B) than in patients who were OHI index negative (3/8; 37.5%) and those without concurrent EBV infections (4/13; 30.7%) at HLH diagnosis. Causes of death

There were 13 deaths reported among patients with mHLH; 7 deaths were reported to be related to HLH, and 6 were deemed unrelated (Figure 5). The unrelated deaths occurred due to bacterial sepsis (n = 1), suspected fungemia and end-organ failure (n = 1), refractory chronic graft-versus-host disease multisystem, gastrointestinal bleeding (n = 1), AML progression, infection (n = 1), multiple medical complications related to therapy, including asparaginase-related hepatopathy (n = 1), septic shock with stenotrophomonas bacteremia, fluid overload, and severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (n = 1).

Discussion

The REAL-HLH study was a retrospective medical chart review study describing the demographics, clinical characteristics, and real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in patients with HLH treated with emapalumab in the United States. In this report, results are presented for patients with mHLH. This high-risk population was diverse (mainly non-White), and almost all patients had hematologic malignancies, mainly B-cell ALL (35.3%), AML (17.6%), and B- or T/NK-cell lymphomas (17.6%), the latter typically observed in adults. This unconventional distribution of malignancies may be due to emapalumab use in real-world pediatric centers, creating a unique cohort comprising children, adolescents, and young adults. In other retrospective studies, the most common malignancies in adults were B-cell lymphomas (29%-42%) and T/NK-cell lymphomas (22%-28%).18,24,35

In this study, mHLH was frequently observed in the presence of viral infections, especially those caused by EBV, identified in 66.7% of patients who underwent testing. EBV is associated with neoplastic diseases such as pediatric T-cell lymphoma and aggressive NK-cell leukemia that often manifest concurrently with HLH and usually have high mortality rates.7 Earlier studies have documented a substantially high prevalence of EBV infections (24%-37%) in patients with mHLH.5,35

Considerable overlap between symptoms of HLH and neoplasms (prolonged high fever, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, hyperferritinemia, etc) and a lack of specific diagnostic criteria make timely diagnosis of mHLH challenging.2,3,6,7,9 Currently, mHLH is diagnosed using the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria developed as enrollment criteria for pediatric trials focused on pHLH. Adults may differ from children in clinical presentation and presence of plausible clinical conditions; therefore, the HLH-2004 criteria may be inappropriate for adults with mHLH.4,9 According to a large multi-institutional, international cohort of adult patients with hematologic malignancies, mHLH can be diagnosed using the OHI index (sCD25 >3900 U/mL, ferritin >1000 ng/mL).24 In this study, OHI criteria were retrospectively applied to 14 patients with available sCD25 and ferritin data at HLH diagnosis. As OHI status was determined after HLH-related treatment but before emapalumab was initated, fewer patients were OHI positive (N = 6) than negative (N = 8). Furthermore, the OHI was developed based on an adult population, whereas the study cohort mainly comprised children; this may have also contributed to the discrepancy. The OHI index also identifies patients who are at high risk of mortality.24 Indeed, in our study, only 1 of 6 (16.6%) patients with a positive OHI index at diagnosis (42.9% of patients with available data) survived compared with 3 of 8 (37.5%) patients with a negative OHI index. Furthermore, 4 of 6 patients with a positive OHI index died due to HLH-related causes, which may suggest a greater requirement for effective anti-inflammatory therapy in this subpopulation.

Almost all patients received HLH-related therapies prior to or concurrent with emapalumab. However, corticosteroids and etoposide, the most frequently used HLH-related therapies, may mask an undiagnosed malignancy or trigger a fatal infection, or aggravate existing cytopenia, respectively.7,36 The most common reasons for initiating emapalumab were to treat refractory and progressive HLH. This suggests that prior HLH-directed treatment regimens inadequately controlled the disease and emapalumab was added to further control hyperinflammation. The starting dose of emapalumab varied across patients (Figure 5), reflecting the lack of standardized and effective dosing due to the lack of approved therapies and clinical trials for patients with mHLH.

Most laboratory parameters, such as fibrinogen, absolute neutrophil count, and CXCL9, improved, normalized, or remained normal (per physician’s assessment) following treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens. CXCL9 is induced by IFN-γ and may serve as a biomarker for IFN-γ activity.27 Although ferritin did not normalize, median ferritin levels decreased during treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens. Studies have shown that elevated ferritin levels are typically observed in all patients with mHLH, and reductions of ≥50% after treatment were associated with a considerably lower risk of death.5,22,37

Depending on the clinical status, <30% of patients considered for transplant to treat the underlying malignancy or HLH received transplant. HSCT is not commonly considered for treating adults with HLH, but may be used in this high-risk population to treat refractory/recurrent HLH and improve the ability to deliver malignancy-directed therapy.7 Anecdotal evidence supports the use of HSCT to potentially consolidate the remission of underlying malignancy as well as treat refractory/recurrent HLH, thereby improving overall survival.38, 39, 40

At the end of the study, 4 of 17 patients were alive (overall survival: 23.5%), and the 12-month survival probability was 22.1% from initiation of emapalumab-containing regimens. These results are consistent with the poor prognosis reported for mHLH by others. In general, patients with mHLH have a low survival, with 5-year overall survival rates of <15%.35,41 In 1 study, a median overall survival of 1.5 months was reported, and patients often had concomitant infections.22 In a retrospective study, all 10 patients with mHLH who received ≥1 dose of emapalumab progressed on high-dose steroids and etoposide and did not survive, although improvements of ≥50% from baseline were observed in ≥3 HLH-related laboratory parameters, including ferritin.42 Therefore, to improve survival, it is imperative to treat the underlying malignancy in addition to controlling the hyperinflammation. These results also underscore the critical need for timely diagnosis of the underlying malignancy, early treatment initiation, and the development of new effective therapies for treating progressive disease.37

As with all retrospective studies, data required for analyses, such as information on all prior treatments and laboratory parameters at the time closest to initiation of and during treatment with emapalumab-containing regimens for each patient, may not be uniformly available across study sites. Therefore, this study had missing or incomplete chart data, which precluded the assessment of “treatment response” and may have affected the strength of the results. Data on the time from diagnosis of the malignancy to emapalumab initiation, number of patients in remission, and whether a patient was being considered for HSCT to treat the malignancy or HLH were not collected per study protocol. Safety data were not collected, as the study did not have safety-related end points. Certain laboratory parameters lacked a standard definition of normal values, which may lead to variability in the results. As patients received HLH-related therapies concurrently with emapalumab, the results from this study cannot be attributed exclusively to emapalumab. Given the rarity of HLH, the number of patients with mHLH was small, which limited the subgroup analyses (eg, correlation between survival and normalization of laboratory parameters) that could be performed. The study may be inherently biased toward patients with a poor prognosis, as emapalumab is indicated as second-line treatment for pHLH and not currently approved for mHLH. Finally, the results of this study may not be generalizable beyond the study population.

mHLH has a worse prognosis than that of other sHLH subgroups, often due to progression of the underlying malignancy or inadequate control of HLH, with the latter further hindering malignancy-directed treatment.39 This grim outlook is exacerbated by diagnostic challenges, and a lack of approved therapies and randomized clinical trials to support effective therapeutic regimens in this population.24 These results underscore the urgent need for new HLH therapies that effectively control HLH and are well tolerated.

In conclusion, this retrospective chart review study reported real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in patients with mHLH treated with emapalumab in the United States. Emapalumab-containing regimens normalized, improved, or maintained normal values for most laboratory parameters. Although overall survival was low, the potential effectiveness of emapalumab-containing HLH-directed regimens in controlling hyperinflammation was demonstrated, especially in malignancies like myelodysplastic syndrome, AML, and B-cell ALL (33% survival rate). Systematic studies are required to evaluate and determine appropriate time of intervention, dose, and dosing frequency of emapalumab in patients with mHLH. Robust clinical trials with reliable mHLH-specific end points and real-world evidence are necessary to better assess treatment benefits of emapalumab for potentially improving patient outcomes, including survival in this difficult-to-treat, high-risk population.
