Time to intravenous diuretic administration in patients hospitalized with heart failure: An observational study
Authors: Yasuyuki Shiraishi, Yuka Kurita, Hiromasa Mori, Kazuyuki Ooishi, Miyuki Matsukawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo help establish optimized treatment strategies for congestion in patients with acute heart failure, this study aimed to provide a detailed summary of real‐world diuretic use in hospitalized patients with heart failure requiring urgent therapy in Japan.Methods and resultsThis observational study used a Japanese medical records database to extract data of patients admitted to hospital with a heart failure diagnosis and an intravenous diuretic prescription from the day before admission to 2 days after. Time from hospital visit to first dose, second dose, and maximum dose of intravenous diuretics were determined. Patients were grouped according to whether they received diuretic modification, defined as an intravenous diuretic dose increase or concomitant use of other diuretics.ResultsOverall, 1577 patients were included in the study (without diuretic modification, n = 1140 [72.3%]; with diuretic modification, n = 437 [27.7%]). The study population was 49.5% female (n = 780) and the mean age ± standard deviation was 80.1 ± 12.7 years. Intravenous diuretic treatment was received within 1 h of their hospital visit in 43.5% of patients (686/1577) and ≤2 h in 16.4% of patients (258/1577). Among 437 patients with an inadequate response following their first dose, 42.1% received an intravenous dose titration, 56.5% received combination diuretics, and 1.4% received both. Over half of the patients (59.0% [258/437]) with diuretic modification received it after the first 24 h of the hospital visit. The median time from hospital visit to first dose titration was similar to time to first combination diuretic use (18.6 h and 17.0 h, respectively). The mean ± standard deviation duration of intravenous diuretic use was significantly longer for patients with versus without diuretic modification (6.3 ± 5.2 vs. 3.7 ± 3.2 days), and a significantly greater proportion of patients (44.6% [195/437] vs. 35.0% [399/1140]) received repeated intravenous diuretic administration. Other characteristics/outcomes of intravenous diuretic use were similar with versus without diuretic modification, including in‐hospital death (15.6% [68/437] vs. 13.9% [159/1140]) and mean ± standard deviation length of hospitalization (21.9 ± 14.7 days vs. 22.1 ± 21.2 days).ConclusionsIn Japan, real‐world patterns of intravenous diuretic administration for patients with heart failure remains far from the time‐sensitive approach recommended in Japanese, European, and United States guidelines.Keywords: Acute heart failure, Guideline for heart failure, Intravenous diuretics, Real‐world study, Time‐sensitive approach

Introduction

Acute heart failure treatment requires high‐quality care that can be administered quickly. Improvement of congestive symptoms and signs, including dyspnoea, is a treatment goal for patients with heart failure per the 2017 Japanese Circulation Society/Japanese Heart Failure Society joint guidelines for acute and chronic heart failure and the 2021 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines. 1 , 2 Intravenous loop diuretics are recommended as initial therapy for congestion in acute heart failure with signs and symptoms of fluid overload. 1 , 2 Intravenous diuretics are used in 70%–80% of patients hospitalized with heart failure in Japan. 3 , 4 Despite their significance, there is a paucity of new intravenous diuretics.

Based on current guidelines or statements, intravenous diuretic use is monitored hourly among patients hospitalized with acute heart failure. 1 , 2 An international registry study reported that the most common time to diuretic administration after hospital arrival was 1 to 6 h, followed by 1 to 30 min, 30 min to 1 h, 6 to 12 h, and 12 to 24 h. In contrast, a study conducted in Japan found the median time to first furosemide administration was 1.5 h. 6 To the authors' knowledge, no studies have investigated the detailed (i.e., hourly) time to the second or subsequent diuretic administration. We expect that a detailed investigation into the real‐world intravenous diuretic use in Japan, including dose and time to first, second, or subsequent diuretic administration, will lead to both an understanding of the current status of congestive treatment and clarification of treatment issues. Such information may provide a basis for establishing optimized treatment strategies for congestion in acute heart failure.

This real‐world observational study of detailed diuretic use, including main intravenous diuretics and other combination diuretics, in patients hospitalized with heart failure was conducted to further the understanding of the current status of congestive heart failure treatment in Japan among patients starting intravenous diuretics from the day before admission to 2 days after admission.

Methods

Patients

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of heart failure during the study period and had a prescription for intravenous diuretics during hospitalization (from the day before admission to 2 days after admission). For patients hospitalized more than once during the study period, only data from the first hospitalization were included. Patients were excluded if they were aged <20 years on the date of hospital admission for heart failure, had at least one dialysis treatment before hospital admission noted in their medical record, or could not be followed to the date of hospital discharge.

This study was conducted in compliance with the protocol and the ethical principles originating in the Declaration of Helsinki and with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Research and Development Research Ethics Committee (reference number 210127). Consent was not required from patients included in the database because the patient data was anonymized. The study was registered with the University hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000046005; https://www.umin.ac.jp/).

Study design and outcome measures

This was an observational study of intravenous diuretic administration in patients hospitalized with heart failure using a medical records database. The period of hospitalization was considered the follow‐up period. The time from the hospital visit (defined as when the physician first gave medical treatment, treatment instructions, examination, and/or examination instructions to the patient upon admission to the hospital) to the following events was determined: first dose of intravenous diuretics, maximum dose, and second dose. For the second dose, the time from the first dose is presented.

To investigate differences in diuretic use, patients were grouped by whether they had diuretic modification, defined as an increase in their dose of intravenous diuretics or concomitant use of other diuretics, which included thiazide, tolvaptan, and acetazolamide (with/without diuretic modification). Data were analysed according to diuretic modification in line with the different guideline‐recommended treatment flows for patients with/without dose titration 2 , 7 and studies that have described different treatment strategies for patients who require diuretic modification. 8 , 9 The time from the hospital visit to the administration of intravenous diuretic dose titration or combined diuretic use and the intravenous diuretic dose was determined. Other information collected included duration of intravenous diuretic use, retreatment with intravenous diuretics (defined as administration of a dose at least 1 day after the consecutive dosing had ended), in‐hospital death, and length of hospital stay.

Data source

The data used in this study included medical records and receipt data from six Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) hospitals contracted by Medical Data Vision Corporation. Each hospital had at least 100 beds and a cardiologist on site. Medical record data collected between January 2018 and November 2021 were used.

Statistical methods

The sample size was determined to confirm the relationship between the number of hospitalization days and the detailed status of diuretic administration. The summary statistic for number of hospitalization days (mean ± standard deviation) for patients hospitalized with heart failure was assumed to be 25 ± 30 days. 4 The number of patients needed to estimate the width of the two‐sided 95% confidence interval of hospitalization days with an accuracy of 4 days was determined to be 865. To achieve a more precise estimate, a minimum of 865 patients and up to 1000 patients were considered necessary when accounting for patients who did not meet or violated the study eligibility criteria and patients who discontinued the study. Baseline variables and characteristics of intravenous diuretic use were reported using descriptive statistics; categorical variables were reported as counts (n) and proportions (%) of total patients with the given characteristic, and continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range, Q1 to Q3) as appropriate. No imputation was made for missing data. Comparisons of baseline variables and characteristics of diuretic use were made using a chi‐square test, two‐sample t‐test, or two‐sample Wilcoxon test. A P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analysed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute; Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

Patient disposition is shown in Figure 1 . Of the 4061 patients hospitalized who received intravenous diuretics, 1585 had a diagnosis of heart failure and received an intravenous diuretic (from hospital visit to 2 days after admission). Eight patients were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria or could not be followed up at the time of hospital discharge. In total, 1577 patients were included: 1140 (72.3%) patients without diuretic modification and 437 (27.7%) with diuretic modification.

Patient disposition.

Patient background characteristics are shown in Table 1 . The characteristics of the total population were generally similar to those of patients with and without diuretic modification. There were some significant differences in co‐morbidities, laboratory values, and concomitant medication use at baseline. The incidences of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and diabetes mellitus were significantly higher in patients with versus without diuretic modification, as was the Charlson co‐morbidity index score. Creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and brain natriuretic peptide levels were significantly higher, and the estimated glomerular filtration rate was significantly lower in patients with versus without diuretic modification. Additionally, the rates of concomitant use of beta‐blockers, angiotensin receptor blockers, aldosterone antagonists, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors, loop diuretics, thiazide diuretics, and tolvaptan at baseline were significantly higher in patients with diuretic modification compared with those without.

Outcomes of intravenous diuretic use

Most patients (70%) received their first intravenous diuretic dose ≤6 h after their visit; the dose was received within 1 h in 43.5% of patients (686/1577) and within >1 h and ≤2 h in 16.4% of patients (258/1577) (Figure 2 A and Graphical Abstract). Similarly, the time from hospital visit to the maximum dose was within 1 h in 35.0% of patients (552/1577) and within >1 to ≤2 h in 15.7% of patients (248/1577) (Figure 2 B ). Among patients without diuretic modification, almost 80% received their first and maximum dose within 12 h of their hospital visit (Figure S1 A). Among those with diuretic modification, over 50% of patients had their first dose within <1 h of their hospital visit (Figure S1 B). Around 70% of patients with diuretic modification reached their maximum intravenous diuretic dose within 24 h of their hospital visit (Figure S1 C).

Time from hospital visit to (A) the first intravenous diuretic administration (all patients) and (B) use of maximum dose of intravenous diuretics (all patients).

Among patients without diuretic modification, the second intravenous diuretic was administered within 6 h after the first administration in 12.7% of patients (126/991) and within 6–12 h in 16.8% of patients (166/991). Nearly 60% of patients with diuretic modification received a second intravenous diuretic administration ≤12 h after the first administration (Figure 3 ).

Distribution of patients without diuretic modification and with diuretic modification by time from first to second intravenous diuretic administration.

Among patients with diuretic modification, 184 (42.1%) underwent dose titration, 247 (56.5%) used combination diuretics, and six (1.4%) received both (Figure 4 A ). The median time from hospital visit to first dose titration or first combination diuretics use was 18.6 h or 17.0 h, respectively. About 30% of patients each received intravenous dose titration or combination diuretics within 6 h of their hospital visit, and about 40% and 45% within 12 h of their hospital visit, respectively (Figure 4 B and Graphical Abstract).

Distribution and time of intravenous diuretic modification. (A) Number of patients with intravenous diuretics dose titration, combination diuretics, or both and (B) time from hospital visit to either intravenous diuretics dose titration or combination diuretics.

The initial dose of intravenous diuretics was ≤20 mg furosemide equivalent dose (azosemide, 30 mg; torsemide, 4 mg) for most patients, regardless of whether they had a diuretic modification; only 15% of patients who had a diuretic modification had a dose increase at their second dose of intravenous diuretic (Figures S2 and S3 ). Among patients with intravenous diuretics dose titration, 40.7% received >20 mg furosemide equivalent at the second administration. Among patients who received combination diuretics, there were few increases in the dose of intravenous diuretics from the initial to second dose administration (Figure S3 ). Table S1 shows the initial and maximum daily intravenous doses and the number of doses received within 24 h of the hospital visit.

The duration of intravenous diuretic use was significantly longer for patients with versus without diuretic modification (6.3 ± 5.2 vs. 3.7 ± 3.2 days), and a significantly greater proportion of patients (44.6% vs. 35.0%) received repeated intravenous diuretic administration (Table 2 ). There was little difference between the groups for in‐hospital death rates and length of hospitalization.

Discussion

This medical chart review reports real‐world treatment patterns of intravenous diuretic use, including time of first dose administration relative to the hospital visit and dose per single administration in patients hospitalized with heart failure. Although the guidelines recommend immediate treatment of congestion in patients with acute heart failure (i.e., a time‐sensitive approach), these real‐world data describing time to treatment of congestion are considered very rare. This study found that 43.5% of the patients were treated with intravenous diuretics very early during their hospital visit (i.e., ≤1 h after their visit), and most received a first dose of ≤20 mg furosemide equivalent. Among patients who had an inadequate response following their first dose of intravenous diuretics, 42.1% received an intravenous dose titration, 56.5% received combination diuretics, and 1.4% received both. Only half of diuretic modifications (i.e., intravenous dose titration or administration of combination diuretics) occurred in the first 24 h of the hospital visit. Other characteristics of intravenous diuretic use and outcomes were similar regardless of whether a diuretic modification was prescribed.

Previous studies have demonstrated that diuretic response affects outcome. 10 , 11 Consequently, it is assumed that patients without diuretic modification had a favourable response to intravenous diuretics. In clinical practice, the recommended treatment flow diverges according to whether patients require diuretic modification, 2 , 7 and real‐world patient data have been evaluated using these groupings. 9 The poor renal function, high brain natriuretic peptide, and a higher proportion of patients taking heart failure medications at baseline collectively suggest that patients who required diuretic modification tended to have more severe heart failure than those who did not.

Our group previously conducted a real‐world database study on intravenous diuretic use for treating congestion in patients hospitalized due to heart failure using data from DPC entries. 12 The data used in that study, which were representative of approximately 26% of Japanese acute care hospitals that have adopted the DPC, were collected daily rather than hourly. As such, there was limited information on the time to diuretic administration, and the dose of a single administration was unavailable. The patient background characteristics of the present study and the real‐world database study were generally similar. However, the mean Charlson co‐morbidity index reported in the present study was somewhat lower than the mean value reported for the real‐world database study. 12 This may be because of insufficient preadmission data availability. Given the similarity of background characteristics, the authors consider that although the data presented herein are from only six DPC hospitals, they can be extrapolated to sufficiently represent DPC data in general. This study provides clarification on the initial single dose of intravenous diuretics and found that 88.5% of the patients received ≤20 mg. Although this was not investigated in the previously published DPC data study, 12 in comparison, the baseline doses of oral loop diuretics in that study were as follows: 51.5% for doses ≤20 mg, 28.8% for >20 mg but ≤40 mg, 14.7% for >40 mg but ≤80 mg, and 5.1% for >80 mg. Based on these results and the loop diuretic dosage recommended by the ESC guidelines, it seems that the initial single dose of IV diuretics administered in these situations may not be sufficient.

Patients with heart failure who receive intravenous diuretics ≥2 h versus <2 h after hospital admission are 1.290 times more likely to be readmitted within 30 days. 13 Those treated with intravenous diuretics <90 min versus ≥90 min after admission have a shorter hospitalization and are less likely to die while hospitalized. 14 A study from Japan reported that patients who received intravenous diuretic treatment <60 min after hospital arrival had significantly lower in‐hospital mortality compared with those who received treatment later after arrival. 6 Most patients in the present study were given intravenous diuretics within 2 h of their hospital visit. This may indicate a spreading of the concept of ‘door to furosemide’ in the importance of initial heart failure treatment as emphasized in treatment guidelines from different countries. In clinical practice, the importance of relieving congestion and administering adequate doses of intravenous diuretics as soon as possible is emphasized. We observed that only around half of the patients with diuretic modification received it within 24 h of admission. The European Society of Cardiology Guidelines recommend patient reassessment and adjusting the diuretic administration within 2–6 h of the first administration. 2 Furthermore, our study found that only 70% of those with diuretic modification received the maximum dose within 24 h. Thirty per cent of patients requiring diuretic modification (i.e., intravenous dose titration or administration of combination diuretics) did not receive an adequate dose of diuretics until 24 h after their hospital visit. These findings suggest that treatment guidelines have yet to be fully adopted in Japan. One possible reason for this may have been that these patients were orally treated with combination diuretics. This observation highlights that despite some patients receiving an early second dose in their hospital visit per guideline recommendations, the full adoption of clinical practice guidelines for the second dose remains to be achieved.

Characteristics of diuretic use and outcomes did not notably differ, regardless of whether patients received diuretic modification. Looking at the time of the first administration as a starting point, 14% of patients received the second dose within 6 h and 19% from 6 to 12 h. These may represent cases in which a strategy utilizing additional dosing was employed, with the same dose of intravenous diuretics administered within a short period due to inadequate response, versus a diuretic modification approach. This may have impacted the lack of difference in outcomes. Furthermore, nearly 20% of patients without diuretic modification received three or more doses of intravenous diuretics within 24 h, further suggesting that some patients received the same dose more than once. This suggestion is supported by the daily dose data presented in Table S1 . As previously noted, patients who did not require diuretic modification were assumed to have a good initial response and, consequently, better outcomes than patients with a poor initial response. 10 Our findings demonstrate that some patients with an inadequate response were treated by repeating diuretic administrations rather than a diuretic modification. This likely impacted our ability to clearly stratify patients with a poor initial response based on diuretic modification alone and contributed to the lack of difference in outcomes between groups.

This study had several limitations to consider. First, a limited number of hospitals (N = 6) were included. Second, because follow‐up with patients who were transferred to another hospital was not possible, co‐morbidities and drug treatment before hospitalization may not have been accurately collected. Third, data regarding diuretic response, such as urine volume and sodium concentration, were not collected. In clinical practice, diuretics and other drugs are adjusted based on the diuretic response, so we focused on whether diuretic modification was used. Furthermore, data were not available for grade of heart failure, left ventricular ejection fraction, blood pressure/hemodynamic stability, symptoms, and initial heart failure diagnosis. Data analysis was not performed for New York Heart Association classification due to missing data for many of the patients. Fourth, most patients received a relatively low dose of furosemide (or equivalent), which generally reflects the clinical situation in Japan. We speculate that the dose of furosemide (or equivalent) may be influenced by racial and body type differences between the Japanese population and other populations. Fifth, our research on the initial diagnosis of heart failure was based on previous Japanese observational studies of patients hospitalized for HF 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 and found a lower induction rate of heart failure medications on admission. This suggests that our study included a large number of patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, as evidenced by their advanced age, and a significant proportion of patients with newly diagnosed heart failure. It is important to note that our study was conducted using data from patients in Japan, so the results may not be generalizable to the global population.

The findings of this study can be considered generally representative of real‐world DPC data. The patient backgrounds in the present study were similar to those in our previously reported real‐world database study, which included data representing approximately 26% of hospitals in Japan that have adopted the DPC. 12 Most patients hospitalized with heart failure initiated intravenous diuretics within 1 h of their hospital visit and were treated with ≤20 mg intravenous diuretic drug. Among patients with an inadequate response after the first dose, slightly more received combined diuretics than had intravenous dose titration, and very few had both. In clinical practice, the initial diuretic administration occurs early during the hospital visit as recommended in the guidelines, but utilization of the time‐sensitive approach to additional diuretic administration is not well established. This is particularly true regarding the administration of second and subsequent diuretic treatments when the initial dose is inadequate.
