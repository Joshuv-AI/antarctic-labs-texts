Association between lactate-to-albumin ratio and mortality in patients with sepsis-induced coagulopathy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yichao Zhu, Zhiyu Li, Tao Zhou, Hongyang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the association between the lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC). Clinical data from 1960 patients admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) with SIC were extracted from the MIMIC-IV (3.1) database. The study population was divided into four groups according to quartiles of LAR. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was ICU mortality. Kaplan–Meier curves, Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic spline analyses, and subgroup analyses were used to investigate the association between LAR and adverse outcomes. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive ability of LAR for adverse outcomes in patients with SIC. Finally, a nomogram model was constructed using the Boruta algorithm to integrate key predictors. This study included a total of 1960 patients with SIC. The LAR was independently associated with both 28-day all-cause mortality and ICU mortality (both p < 0.01). Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated that patients with higher LAR levels had significantly lower 28-day and ICU survival rates (Log-rank test, p < 0.001).Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis indicated a linear relationship between LAR and mortality risk (overall p < 0.001; nonlinearity p = 0.068). ROC analysis showed that LAR had moderate predictive accuracy. When combined with the SOFA score, its predictive performance improved further. Building upon this, the final nomogram achieved the highest predictive accuracy for 28-day mortality. Compared with traditional scoring systems, the newly developed model demonstrated significant improvements in both reclassification and discrimination, as reflected by the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI).Subgroup analyses revealed that this association remained consistent across all subgroups. Using Boruta to select predictive factors, a nomogram incorporating LAR was constructed, demonstrating good predictive performance for 28-day all-cause mortality (AUC 0.779, 95% CI 0.756–0.802).An online web server was built (https://shaojian.shinyapps.io/sic-mortality-predictor/) to facilitate the use of the nomogram. Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by infection-induced dysregulation of the immune response, leading to multiple organ dysfunction1,2. According to statistics, the incidence of sepsis in ICU patients is approximately 58 cases per 100,000 population per year, with a mortality rate as high as 41.9%3. This situation poses a major challenge to public health systems worldwide. Mortality further increases when sepsis is complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Studies have shown that 22%–66% of patients with sepsis may develop SIC, a condition that is typically associated with adverse clinical outcomes and higher mortality4,5. In 2017, the diagnostic criteria and scoring system for SIC were proposed, and in 2019, they were adopted by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH)6. The SIC score is based on platelet count, INR, and the total SOFA score. Platelet count (< 100 or 100–149 × 10⁹/L), INR (> 1.4 or 1.2–1.4), and SOFA score (≥ 2 or 1) are assigned 2 or 1 points accordingly. SIC is diagnosed when the total score is ≥ 4 with a combined coagulation and SOFA score > 2. This scoring system is widely used for screening and diagnosing sepsis-induced coagulopathy7.

Lactate serves as a biomarker of tissue hypoperfusion and metabolic stress. In patients with sepsis, both accelerated aerobic glycolysis and impaired hepatic lactate clearance contribute to the development of hyperlactatemia. Lactate has been well established as being closely associated with disease severity and prognosis in sepsis and is recognized as an effective biomarker for risk stratification. Furthermore, analysis of lactate trajectories can help identify distinct subphenotypes of sepsis8,9.Albumin, primarily synthesized by the liver, is the major determinant of plasma oncotic pressure and one of the most abundant proteins in circulation. Serum albumin levels are influenced by systemic inflammation, and lower albumin concentrations are generally associated with poorer clinical outcomes10. In addition, nutritional status and hepatic function can affect albumin levels, while certain medications and other factors may influence lactate concentrations11–13.

Therefore, relying solely on either lactate or albumin as an individual indicator to assess disease severity may be limited. The LAR, derived from two easily obtainable clinical laboratory parameters, has attracted increasing attention from researchers. LAR has been demonstrated to possess considerable prognostic value in patients with sepsis and septic shock14,15. Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to investigate the association between LAR and clinical outcomes in patients with SIC admitted to the ICU, using data from the MIMIC-IV database.

Materials and methods

Data

This study utilized data from the Multiparameter Intelligent Monitoring in Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV) database (version 3.1), which includes ICU patient data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2022. The creation anduse of the MIMIC database was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, affiliated with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Patient consent was not required as all protected personal information was de-identified and deleted. Clinical trial number: not applicable. This retrospective observational study involved the extraction of clinical data from SIC patients using database management software and language tools. Data were then exported, processed, and analyzed using data analysis software to ensure that patient treatment was not compromised and remained safe. The author, Zhu. YC, was certified and licensed by the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) to use the MIMIC-IV database in accordance with relevant regulations(Record ID: 65,961,851).

Population selection criteria

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection (Sepsis 3.0), accompanied by confirmed or suspected infection and a rapid increase in the SOFA score by 2 points or more16. This study included patients diagnosed with SIC within 24 h of ICU admission. The scoring system identifies SIC using PT-INR, platelet count, and SOFA score. Detailed diagnostic criteria for SIC are provided in (Table S1). Only patients admitted to the ICU for the first time were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Patients lacking critical diagnostic data; 2. Minors (< 18 years old); 3. Pregnant women; 4. ICU stay of less than 24 h; 5. Patients who received heparin or warfarin during their ICU stay; 6. Patients with missing lactate or albumin records.

Data collection and variable definitions

Patient demographic information included age, sex, and ethnicity. Physiological parameters such as heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), oxygen saturation, and body temperature, along with various laboratory test results, including hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), anion gap (AG), calcium, glucose, potassium, sodium, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), lactate, and pH, were all collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Medical history data, including hypertension, acute kidney injury (AKI), cerebrovascular accident (CVA), chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus, heart failure, ischemic heart disease (IHD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as the use of vasoactive drugs, antibiotics, mechanical ventilation (MV), and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), were also recorded. Additionally, three clinical severity scores were collected: SOFA score, the Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) score.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was 28-day all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome measure was ICU mortality. The LAR was defined as an index calculated using the following formula: LAR = Lactate (mmol/L) / Albumin (g/dL).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using DecisionLinnc software version 1.1.7.417. This platform integrates multiple programming language environments and streamlines data handling, analysis, and machine learning processes through a graphical interface. Continuous variables following a normal distribution are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD), while continuous variables not following a normal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables are expressed as counts or percentages (%). The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for continuous variables. Variables with missing data rates exceeding 20% were excluded, while variables with missing data rates below 20% were handled using multiple imputation methods. Patients were divided into four groups based on LAR quartiles. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to compare 28-day survival rates among the four groups, and the Log-rank test was employed for comparison. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among variables, and variables with a VIF greater than 5 were excluded as covariates. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to evaluate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for event occurrence. Based on previous literature, covariates were incorporated into the model18,19. Model I was adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, SIRS score, and vital signs (including respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and body temperature). Model II further adjusted for comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, AKI, heart failure, ischemic heart disease) in addition to Model I. Model III incorporated laboratory parameters (PLT, RDW, WBC, ALT, AST, glucose, calcium, potassium, sodium, creatinine, and BUN) in addition to Model II. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to assess the association between the LAR index and mortality risk in SIC patients. The optimal number of knots was determined according to the minimum Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).ROC curve analysis was performed to determine the area under the curve (AUC) for predicting survival. Calibration curves were constructed to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. In addition, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess the incremental predictive value of the proposed model, while Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate its clinical utility for predicting early mortality in patients with SIC.To evaluate the stability of the relationship was explored across ten subgroups. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Patient characteristics

The patient inclusion and exclusion process is illustrated in (Fig. 1). Data from 10,980 patients diagnosed with sepsis-induced coagulopathy were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. Based on the exclusion criteria, 1,960 patients met the eligibility criteria for analysis. The baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in (Table 1). The median age of the patients was 64 years (IQR: 54–76), with 1,144 (58.37%) being male. Among the patients, 698 (35.61%) had hypertension, 1,153 (58.83%) developed acute kidney injury, 335 (17.09%) had a history of chronic kidney disease, 536 (27.35%) had diabetes mellitus, 118 (6.02%) had a history of stroke, 1,482 (75.61%) had heart failure, 501 (25.56%) had coronary heart disease, and 279 (14.23%) had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Patients were divided into four groups based on LAR quartiles: the first quartile group (0.16 ≤ LAR < 0.56), the second quartile group (0.56 ≤ LAR < 0.83), the third quartile group (0.84 ≤ LAR < 1.27), and the fourth quartile group (1.27 ≤ LAR ≤ 5.56). Significant statistical differences were found between groups in various laboratory parameters (p < 0.05), including Hb, RDW, WBC, ALT, AST, creatinine, INR, PT, AG, calcium, potassium, sodium, pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂. Patients in the fourth quartile group had higher SOFA scores, OASIS, and SIRS scores, as well as a higher incidence of mechanical ventilation, CRRT, vasoactive drug use, and antibiotic administration, with all differences being statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Clinical outcomes

In terms of clinical outcomes, the 28-day mortality and ICU mortality rates were highest in the fourth quartile group (p < 0.001), with rates of 48.98% and 41.41%, respectively (Fig. 2) and (Table 2). The variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis results are shown in (Table S3), which were used to identify variables with multicollinearity. When the LAR index was treated as a continuous variable, Cox proportional hazards regression analysis revealed that an increase in the LAR index was significantly associated with poor prognosis in patients with SIC in the unadjusted model (HR, 1.65; 95% CI 1.52–1.79; p < 0.001), and this association remained significant in the fully adjusted model (HR, 1.27; 95% CI 1.15–1.39; p < 0.001). For secondary outcomes, similar results were observed in the unadjusted model (HR, 1.43; 95% CI 1.31–1.56; p < 0.001), and in the fully adjusted model (HR, 1.24; 95% CI 1.12–1.38; p < 0.001)(Table 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves and ROC curves

The Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients in the fourth quartile had the lowest 28-day survival rate, while those in the first quartile had the highest survival rate (Log-rank test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3), which was consistent with the results of the Cox regression analysis.Subsequently, ROC curve analysis was performed to compare the predictive performance of lactate, albumin, LAR, SOFA score, and the combined SOFA + LAR model. The results showed that the AUC values of LAR for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality and ICU mortality were 0.661 (95% CI 0.633–0.690) and 0.635 (95% CI 0.595–0.674), respectively. LAR demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared with lactate alone (AUC 0.647 and 0.628) and albumin alone (AUC 0.560 and 0.535). LAR exhibited moderate discriminative ability in predicting both 28-day all-cause mortality and ICU mortality, indicating its superior performance as an independent predictor. Furthermore, when LAR was combined with the SOFA score, the predictive performance improved, with AUC values increasing to 0.717 for 28-day all-cause mortality and 0.724 for ICU mortality. Selection of predictive factors and construction of the nomogram

We employed the Boruta machine learning algorithm to identify six key predictive factors, which were subsequently used to construct a practical clinical prediction model. The selected variables included the LAR, SOFA score, OASIS score, CRRT use, RDW, and vasoactive drug use (Supplementary Fig. 1). These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to further evaluate their independent associations with 28-day mortality (Table S4).A nomogram was then developed to predict 28-day survival probabilities in patients (Fig. 7). By summing the points assigned to each predictor, the total score can be calculated to estimate the probability of 28-day all-cause mortality. For example, a patient with an LAR of 0.82 corresponds to 8.2 points, a SOFA score of 8 corresponds to 13.8 points, an OASIS score of 36 corresponds to 42 points, and an RDW of 16% corresponds to 31.4 points. No use of CRRT or vasopressors contributes 0 points for each variable. The total score is 95.4, corresponding to an estimated 28-day all-cause mortality risk of 13.5% according to the nomogram.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.779 (95% CI 0.756–0.802), indicating good predictive performance of the nomogram (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To further assess the internal stability and discriminative capability of the model, 1000 iterations of non-parametric bootstrap resampling were performed. After bias correction, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) for predicting 28-day mortality was 0.744, suggesting reliable predictive performance.

The calibration of the nomogram was evaluated using calibration plots (Supplementary Fig. 3). The plots showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, further confirming the model’s calibration accuracy and reliability.

The incremental prognostic value of the final model compared to the SOFA score was further supported, as evidenced by significant improvements in reclassification and discrimination indices (NRI = 0.62, 95% CI 0.52–0.71; IDI = 0.11, 95% CI 0.09–0.12). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, across a broad range of threshold probabilities, the newly developed prognostic model consistently demonstrated a higher net benefit than the SOFA score, suggesting superior clinical utility in clinical decision-making.

Discussion

In this retrospective study involving 1,960 patients diagnosed with SIC, we found that higher LAR was associated with poorer prognosis in SIC patients. We also identified a linear relationship between LAR and both 28-day all-cause mortality and ICU mortality in SIC patients. Specifically, compared to patients in the first quartile, those in the fourth quartile had a 70% higher 28-day all-cause mortality and a 68% higher ICU mortality. This association remained robust even after adjusting for confounding variables in subgroup analyses. Additionally, in ROC curve analysis, the predictive performance of LAR, as a derived index, was superior to that of lactate or albumin as independent predictors.

Lactate, a metabolic product of glycolysis, is generated from pyruvate through the catalytic action of lactate dehydrogenase20. Under normal physiological conditions, blood lactate levels in the human body are typically maintained below 2 mmol/L. However, in situations such as tissue hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, or ischemia, lactate levels may become elevated. In 1964, lactate was first introduced as a prognostic indicator21. With the publication of the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3), a serum lactate level exceeding 2 mmol/L was established as one of the diagnostic criteria for sepsis and septic shock16, underscoring the pivotal role of lactate in pathophysiological processes. Elevated lactate levels have been found to be closely associated with poor prognosis in various diseases, particularly in patients with heart failure, ARDS, trauma, burns, cancer, and diabetes22–26.

Albumin is the primary plasma protein synthesized by the liver, accounting for more than 60% of total serum protein27. It plays essential roles in maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure, transporting lipophilic substances, regulating acid–base balance, and exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Under normal physiological conditions, serum albumin levels typically range between 35 and 50 g/L28,29. However, when hepatic function is impaired, or in cases of malnutrition, kidney disease, or acute and chronic inflammation, albumin levels often decrease30. Changes in serum albumin concentration have been widely used in prognostic assessment across various diseases. In particular, hypoalbuminemia is frequently associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients with liver disease, kidney disease, heart failure, and sepsis29,31,32.

The lactate-to-albumin ratio has attracted increasing attention from researchers and has been found to be associated with the prognosis of various diseases. LAR has been identified as an independent risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with acute pancreatitis upon admission33. In patients with heart failure and chronic kidney disease, those in the high-LAR group exhibited significantly poorer survival outcomes compared with those in the low-LAR group, and LAR was significantly associated with both in-hospital and 1-year mortality34. Zhao et al. reported that when LAR exceeds 0.53, it is positively correlated with 30-day and 90-day mortality risks in patients with cerebral infarction35. In patients with sepsis, a LAR ≥ 0.95 was significantly associated with increased risks of in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality among those with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury36. Similarly, another study demonstrated that for every 1-unit increase in LAR, the 28-day mortality risk in patients with sepsis-associated liver injury (SALI) increased by 21%37. In SIC, coagulation activation and microvascular thrombosis exacerbate microcirculatory dysfunction, leading to insufficient oxygen supply and further increasing lactate levels2. Meanwhile, endothelial damage and liver dysfunction aggravate albumin leakage and reduce synthesis, creating a vicious cycle that further worsens the condition10. Lactate, through acidosis, inhibits thrombin activity and promotes fibrinolysis, thereby worsening coagulopathy. Low albumin reflects systemic inflammation, where inflammatory factors activate the coagulation system and suppress anticoagulant mechanisms, while reduced albumin may lead to microvascular leakage, accelerating the consumption of coagulation factors and further exacerbating coagulopathy36. Therefore, LAR, as a composite index, not only evaluates the severity of coagulopathy but also provides more precise prognostic information than using lactate or albumin alone.In this study, we further refined the sepsis patient population and explored the relationship between the LAR and the prognosis of SIC patients. Using the Boruta algorithm, we developed a nomogram model and an online prediction tool to help clinicians more accurately assess patient prognosis. These tools translate LAR from theoretical research into clinical practice, providing strong support for clinical decision-making and thus promoting personalized, precision treatment and improved patient management.

In our study, we found that although the predictive performance of the derived index LAR was slightly inferior to that of the SOFA score, it outperformed lactate and albumin as individual predictors of prognosis in patients with sepsis-induced coagulopathy, consistent with previous research findings35,38–40. Since lactate and albumin often change in opposite directions, with lactate levels tending to rise while albumin levels decrease, and vice versa, LAR may provide a more comprehensive reflection of the patient’s clinical condition32. We observed a significant interaction effect in the diabetes subgroup (P for interaction = 0.04), suggesting that the prognostic value of LAR may differ between patients with and without diabetes. Diabetes is often accompanied by chronic microvascular dysfunction, endothelial injury, and impaired tissue perfusion, which may aggravate tissue hypoxia during sepsis and thereby promote lactate production. In addition, diabetic patients frequently exhibit metabolic dysregulation and enhanced systemic inflammation, which may lead to reduced lactate clearance and lower albumin levels. Therefore, the association between elevated LAR and adverse outcomes may be more pronounced in SIC patients with diabetes. SOFA is widely used to evaluate organ dysfunction but may not fully capture key SIC-related pathophysiological processes such as microcirculatory impairment, metabolic derangement, and systemic inflammation. In contrast, LAR integrates information from lactate and albumin: elevated lactate reflects tissue hypoperfusion and metabolic stress, whereas decreased albumin indicates inflammatory activation, increased vascular permeability, and impaired nutritional reserve. Therefore, LAR may better represent the complex pathophysiology of SIC. In our study, combining LAR with SOFA improved predictive performance, suggesting complementary prognostic value beyond traditional severity scoring. The final nomogram further integrates multidimensional predictors and provides individualized mortality probability, and its web-based calculator may facilitate early risk stratification and clinical decision support. In clinical practice, the web-based model can be embedded into hospital electronic health record (EHR) systems or clinical decision support platforms to enable automatic calculation of risk probabilities once relevant laboratory and clinical data are entered. This facilitates real-time, individualized risk assessment without increasing clinicians’ workload.

The estimated risk probabilities may assist clinicians in early risk stratification, guiding closer monitoring, timely correction of coagulation abnormalities, optimization of hemodynamic management, and more proactive implementation of organ support when appropriate.

Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational study based on electronic medical records, it is inevitably subject to selection bias. Second, as a single-center retrospective study based on electronic medical records, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite multivariable adjustment and subgroup analyses. Some unmeasured confounders, such as infection source, pathogen information, antimicrobial treatment, fluid resuscitation strategies, and nutritional status, may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, this study only analyzed LAR values measured within the first 24 h after ICU admission. This single measurement may be influenced by early resuscitation and therapeutic interventions and therefore may not fully reflect the dynamic changes in metabolic status and disease progression. Future prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether dynamic changes in LAR can provide additional prognostic information and improve risk stratification. Additionally, the findings need to be validated through multicenter and prospective studies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the LAR index exhibits a linear relationship with both 28-day all-cause mortality and ICU mortality in patients with SIC, indicating its potential as a prognostic biomarker for these patients. Further randomized controlled trials are warranted to explore the impact of LAR-guided therapeutic strategies on improving outcomes in SIC patients. In addition, a web-based prediction tool was developed to enhance the clinical applicability of the model and facilitate individualized risk stratification.
