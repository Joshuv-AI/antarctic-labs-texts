Combining Renal Autotransplantation and Endovascular Aortic Repair for Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm in an LVAD Patient
Authors: Kaan Kardes, Leonard Pitts, Brigitta Globke, Robert Öllinger, Gaik Nersesian, Julius Kaemmel, Jasper Iske, Leonhard Wert, Robert Hammerschmidt, Semih Buz

ABSTRACT

Case Description

Open surgical repair remains a valid alternative for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) in patients unsuitable for endovascular aortic repair (EVAR). Kidney autotransplantation has been described in conjunction with both open and endovascular approaches. Patients with a left ventricular assist device (LVAD) require lifelong anticoagulation, increasing the risk of hemorrhagic complications and necessitating careful interdisciplinary planning.Take-Home Message•Renal autotransplantation may serve as a viable bailout strategy enabling endovascular aortic repair in highly selected patients requiring organ preservation.

We report a 72-year-old man with an LVAD and progressive AAA unsuitable for standard EVAR who underwent a staged approach: laparoscopic left nephrectomy, EVAR, and autotransplantation of the left kidney into the right iliac fossa.

History of Presentation

The patient underwent surveillance computed tomography (CT) for a known AAA.

On admission, the patient was asymptomatic and hemodynamically stable. Auscultation revealed vesicular breath sounds bilaterally, and pulmonary function was uncompromised. Diuresis was adequate. The abdomen was soft, with normoactive bowel sounds present in all 4 quadrants. International normalized ratio was 2.2, and activated clotting time was 163 seconds.

Past Medical History

His history included type A aortic dissection (DeBakey I) treated with ascending aortic replacement (2011), followed by myocardial infarction requiring thrombus aspiration. In 2012, a third-degree atrioventricular block required pacemaker implantation, later upgraded to cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (2018). In 2017, total arch replacement with frozen elephant trunk was performed, followed by thoracic endovascular aortic repair for a stent graft–induced new entry tear. In 2019, a continuous-flow LVAD (HeartWare) was implanted for end-stage ischemic cardiomyopathy. Additional history included hypertension, hyperlipoproteinemia, urothelial bladder carcinoma (2024), and chronic kidney disease stage 3a.

Differential Diagnosis

The diagnosis was unequivocally established on preoperative imaging.

Investigations

Preoperative CT (Figure 1) demonstrated progression of the AAA with persistent dissection and aneurysm enlargement to 6.6 cm infrarenally and 7.7 cm prebifurcation. The entry tear was located in the infrarenal aorta. The right renal artery originated from the true lumen, whereas the left arose from the false lumen with delayed perfusion. The inferior mesenteric and left iliac arteries also originated from the false lumen. Given the high rupture risk, EVAR was recommended to seal the entry tear and promote favorable remodeling. However, this posed a high risk of occluding the left renal artery.

Radioisotope renography showed preserved renal function (left 53%, right 47%).

After interdisciplinary discussion, staged renal autotransplantation was planned.

Management

Target coagulation parameters were defined interdisciplinary as an activated clotting time of 150 to 160 seconds, an international normalized ratio of <1.5, and an activated partial thromboplastin time of 55 seconds under unfractionated heparin. A prothrombin complex concentrate of 1,000 IU was administered.

After the induction of general anesthesia, laparoscopic left nephrectomy was performed via a hand-assisted approach through a midline supraumbilical incision. Extensive adhesions were dissected. The ureter, gonadal vein, and adrenal vein were divided. Three renal arteries were identified, 2 of which were preserved and unified into a single ostium for reimplantation. The kidney was explanted, flushed with a histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate solution, and stored on ice (Figure 2). The abdominal wall was closed in layers using absorbable sutures, while the skin was closed with intracutaneous sutures.Figure 2Kidney Explantation and Organ Preparation(A) Endoscopic view during kidney explantation. EVAR was subsequently performed via bilateral femoral access using closure devices (ProGlide, Abbott Cardiovascular).

At the landing zone, the true lumen diameter measured 9 × 29 mm, corresponding to an average diameter of 19 mm. Thus, we decided to use a main body stent with a proximal diameter of 23 mm, as well as iliac stents with a diameter of 18 mm.

A GORE Excluder main body measuring 23 × 12 × 12 cm (GORE) was deployed below the right renal artery, followed by bilateral iliac extensions.

Balloon modeling ensured adequate expansion of the true lumen. A proximal endoleak was identified and successfully treated with an aortic extender (GORE). Final angiography confirmed exclusion of the aneurysm. An angiographic summary of the EVAR procedure is shown in Video 1.

For autotransplantation, a hockey-stick incision was made in the right iliac fossa to expose the external iliac vessels. Venous and arterial anastomoses were performed end-to-side with 5-0 and 6-0 Prolene, respectively. Cold ischemia time was 5 hours, and warm ischemia time 20 minutes. After reperfusion and saline flushing, graft perfusion normalized, and diuresis was commenced immediately. A Redon drain was placed posterior to the graft. Ureterocystostomy was performed using the Lich–Gregoir technique with double-J stent placement and antireflux sutures. Intraoperative ultrasound confirmed adequate perfusion, and the wound was closed in layers with skin staples.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit in stable condition. Postoperatively, anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin was maintained with an activated partial thromboplastin time target of 50 seconds, while antiplatelet therapy with 100 mg of aspirin was continued. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1.

Daily sonography showed no perirenal hematoma or segmental malperfusion. Renal function remained stable, with mean diuresis >100 mL/h and serum creatinine of 1.5 mg/dL on the day of surgery and 1.4 mg/dL at discharge. The urinary catheter and Redon drain were removed on postoperative day 7.

CT on postoperative day 13 (Figure 3, Video 2) demonstrated exclusion of the entry tear and blocked perfusion of the false lumen. At 16-month follow-up, CT showed complete resolution of the previously noted false lumen perfusion. However, a newly detected stent graft–induced entry tear will require secondary EVAR.

Discussion

The absence of physiologic pulsatility in LVAD patients has been associated with adverse vascular remodeling, including endothelial dysfunction, altered extracellular matrix composition, and reduced aortic wall integrity, potentially accelerating aneurysm formation and progression, particularly in the setting of dissection and chronic anticoagulation.1

Management in this case was constrained by the intersection of LVAD physiology, bleeding risk, and complex aortic and renal anatomy.2 Open repair was not feasible due to the combined risk of severe perioperative bleeding under therapeutic anticoagulation and LVAD-associated coagulopathy, as well as the necessity of a thoracoabdominal approach, which was precluded by the course of the driveline.

Standard EVAR was also unsuitable, as it would have compromised perfusion of the left kidney. More complex endovascular strategies, including branched or fenestrated repair, were technically prohibitive. The narrow true lumen would have required extensive septostomy, introducing significant procedural risk. Furthermore, catheterization of the renal artery through thrombus-bearing segments posed a substantial risk of embolization and potential technical failure.

Branched EVAR within a lumen of approximately 9 mm would have severely limited maneuverability, complicating target-vessel cannulation. In addition, custom-made branched devices entail manufacturing delays of several weeks, during which continued aneurysm expansion carries a non-negligible rupture risk.

The use of branched or fenestrated devices would also have required extensive visceral and renal artery stenting, exposing the patient to cumulative risks of branch thrombosis and reintervention, which have been reported in up to 10% and 34% of cases, respectively.3, 4, 5

Given the patient's prior thoracic endovascular aortic repair, complete exclusion of the aneurysm would have necessitated continuous aortic coverage from the thoracic segment to the iliac arteries, substantially increasing the risk of spinal cord ischemia.5

Fenestrated repair was further limited by the patient's anatomy. The left renal artery originated from the false lumen within a markedly dilated aneurysmal segment, resulting in an excessive gap of 48 mm between the target vessel and the graft, a configuration known to compromise stability and technical success.6

Alternative strategies, such as iliac–renal bypass, were considered but deemed suboptimal due to limited evidence on long-term patency and the risk of prolonged renal warm ischemia during complex endovascular reconstruction.7,8

Renal autotransplantation was therefore selected to preserve renal function while enabling definitive aneurysm exclusion. Although traditionally reserved for complex renal pathology, it has emerged as a viable adjunct in selected cases of complex aortic disease. Notably, vascular complication rates in renal transplantation are low, with excellent long-term outcomes reported.9,10

Given the lack of standardized anticoagulation protocols for high-risk LVAD patients undergoing major noncardiac procedures, a tailored perioperative strategy was employed. Anticoagulation targets were adapted dynamically based on LVAD-specific recommendations and clinical course, allowing safe transition back to oral therapy without thrombotic or bleeding complications. Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing the combination of renal autotransplantation and EVAR in an LVAD patient.

Through an interdisciplinary approach decided upon by the heart team in correspondence with the vascular and transplant surgical departments, a sound strategy going beyond conventional treatment algorithms could be established, addressing the complex pathology and enabling exclusion of the aneurysm while avoiding the prohibitive risks associated with open repair, as well as preserving renal perfusion.

Renal autotransplantation combined with EVAR may represent a valuable bailout option in carefully selected high-risk patients unsuitable for standard approaches. Ultimately, the feasibility of this approach is limited to high-volume centers with extensive experience in advanced endovascular aortic interventions and kidney transplantation.
