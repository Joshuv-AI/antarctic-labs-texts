Molecular and phenotypic characterization of carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii from bovine mastitis milk with development of a novel optimized spectrophotometric UniCarb assay
Authors: Farouk Hassan, Süheyla Turkyilmaz, Taghrid S El-Mahdy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria are increasingly reported in veterinary medicine, posing a serious threat to both animal and public health. Phenotypic assays, including the Modified Hodge Test (MHT), modified and optimized carbapenem inactivation methods (mCIM/oCIM), Carba NP, and CarbAcineto NP, were evaluated. Background

The global emergence of carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria poses a serious threat to antimicrobial efficacy within the One Health continuum, impacting both human and veterinary health sectors [1, 2]. Among these, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii are designated by the WHO as critical-priority pathogens due to their multidrug resistance, zoonotic potential, and limited therapeutic options [3, 4]. Detection of these organisms in subclinical mastitis milk underscores a concerning interface for interspecies transmission and foodborne dissemination of antimicrobial resistance [5, 6].

High-density dairy systems are particularly vulnerable, as bovine mastitis fosters persistent antimicrobial exposure, environmental contamination, and opportunistic colonization by resistant pathogens [7, 8]. While multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative organisms in milk are well documented, the isolation of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) and pandrug-resistant (PDR) K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii remains rare and alarming [9, 10].

The clinical utility of carbapenems, once considered last-resort agents against extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing strains, is now undermined by the global dissemination of carbapenemase genes such as blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA-48 in K. pneumoniae and blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-51 in A. baumannii [11–15]. Furthermore, blaIMP and blaVIM metallo-lactamases contribute to resistance in A. baumannii, complicating both detection and treatment [16]. Although primarily associated with Enterobacteriaceae, blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA-48 have also been sporadically reported in A. baumannii, justifying their inclusion in molecular screening to detect rare or emerging resistance events [11, 12]. These genes are often carried on mobile genetic elements, enabling rapid horizontal transmission across human, animal, and environmental reservoirs [17, 18].

Timely detection of carbapenemases is therefore essential for containment and effective antimicrobial stewardship [19]. While multiplex PCR provides excellent sensitivity and specificity [20], many veterinary and resource-limited laboratories still rely on phenotypic methods such as the legacy Modified Hodge Test (MHT). Despite its removal from current CLSI recommendations, MHT is sometimes retained for comparative purposes [21, 22]. Newer alternatives such as the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM), optimized carbapenem inactivation method (oCIM), and synergy-based inhibitor tests offer improved accuracy but often vary in reliability depending on bacterial species and enzyme class [23–25].

The Carba NP test introduced phenol red–based colorimetric detection of carbapenem hydrolysis [26–28], but its accuracy is limited by interspecies variability, buffer dependency, and subjective interpretation, especially in non-fermenters like A. baumannii [28, 29]. The CarbAcineto NP adaptation, using high-salt lysis buffers for Acinetobacter spp., improved detection but remains constrained by species-dependent performance, operator subjectivity, and limited cross-species applicability [26, 29].

Given these limitations, there is a critical need for a standardized, rapid, and objective assay applicable across species. Accordingly, this study aimed to develop a single, species-independent method capable of enhancing carbapenemase enzyme release and enabling spectrophotometric detection in both K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii, thereby advancing One Health antimicrobial resistance surveillance.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Between September 2024 and March 2025, a total of 335 quarter milk samples were collected from California Mastitis Test (CMT)-positive dairy cows on 30 commercial farms in Aydın Aydin Province, Türkiye, following established veterinary mastitis sampling protocols [30, 31]. Samples (5–10 mL) were aseptically obtained from the most affected udder quarter of cows aged 3–11 years that had not received antibiotic treatment for at least three weeks. All farms employed automated milking systems. Collected samples were kept refrigerated (4 °C) during transport and processed microbiologically within 24 h.

Bacterial isolation

Each milk sample was pre-enriched in Buffered Peptone Water (Oxoid, UK) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Aliquots were then streaked onto 5% sheep blood agar, MacConkey agar, and CHROMagar™ Orientation (CHROMagar, France). After 24 h of aerobic incubation at 37 °C, distinct colonies were selected and subcultured for purification. Preliminary identification was performed based on colony morphology, Gram staining, and standard biochemical tests according to veterinary diagnostic protocols [32].

Molecular identification by 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from purified colonies using the GeneMark DNA-EX-96 kit (GMbiotech). DNA purity was assessed spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop, OD260/280 = 1.8–2.0) [33]. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified with universal primers as described by Weisburg et al. [34], and PCR products were confirmed on 1.5% agarose gels. Amplicons were purified using the HighPrep™ PCR Cleanup System (MAGBIO) and sequenced with BigDye Terminator v3.1 chemistry on an ABI 3730xl capillary sequencer. Reads were assembled and curated in BioEdit [35], and species identification was performed using BLASTn [36] with thresholds of ≥ 98.6% identity and ≥ 97% coverage. Verified 16S rRNA sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PV710479–PV710512.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method according to CLSI M100, 34th edition (2024) [21]. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland and inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK). Twelve antibiotic disks (Bioanalyse, Türkiye) were applied, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Inhibition zones were measured and interpreted according to CLSI breakpoints. Isolates were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), or pandrug-resistant (PDR) based on international criteria.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem were determined by broth microdilution according to CLSI M100, 34th edition (2024) [21]. Two-fold serial dilutions were prepared in cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (Oxoid, UK) and inoculated with ~ 5 × 105 CFU/mL bacterial suspensions (0.5 McFarland). Plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 18–20 h. MICs were assessed both visually and spectrophotometrically at OD₆₀₀. CLSI resistance thresholds were ≥ 4 µg/mL for imipenem and meropenem, and ≥ 2 µg/mL for ertapenem [37]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was included as a quality control strain [38].

Multiplex PCR for carbapenemase gene detection

Multiplex PCR was performed to detect blaNDM (184 bp), blaKPC (308 bp), and blaOXA-48 (502 bp) in Klebsiella pneumoniae, and blaNDM (184 bp), blaKPC (308 bp), blaOXA-48 (502 bp), blaOXA-23 (403 bp), blaOXA-51 (261 bp), blaVIM (184 bp), and blaIMP (270 bp) in Acinetobacter baumannii, following established protocols [19, 34, 39, 40]. Each 25 µL reaction contained FIREPol® Master Mix (Solis BioDyne), 0.5 µM of each primer, and 2 µL of DNA template. Thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 57–59 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Positive control strains included ATCC BAA-2146 (blaNDM), NCTC 13442 (blaOXA-48), BAA-1705 (blaKPC), BAA-1790 (A. baumannii carrying blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-51), NCTC 13438 (blaVIM-2), and NCTC 13437 (blaIMP-1). A no-template control was also included. The same negative controls used in the phenotypic assays (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, K. pneumoniae ATCC 13883, and two PCR-negative clinical isolates) served as PCR-negative standards. Amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels stained with SYBR™ Safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized under UV illumination (Table 1).

Amplicon sequencing and confirmation

To validate primer specificity, one representative amplicon per carbapenemase gene was selected as a proxy for the other PCR-positive products of the same gene family. Multiplex PCR products for blaNDM (184 bp), blaKPC (308 bp), blaOXA-48-like (502 bp), blaOXA-23 (403 bp), and blaOXA-51-like (261 bp) were purified using a spin-column kit and Sanger-sequenced bidirectionally with the same primer pairs (Applied Biosystems 3500/3730xl; BigDye v3.1 chemistry). Chromatograms were inspected for mixed peaks; primer sequences were trimmed, and high-quality consensus reads were assembled (minimum Phred Q ≥ 30). Primer-trimmed consensus sequences were queried by BLASTx (nr) and BLASTn with an AMR-focused filter. Gene confirmation required 95–100% identity and ≥ 95% query coverage on BLASTn or a concordant top BLASTx hit annotated as the intended β-lactamase family with E ≤ 1 × 10⁻2⁰.

Allele names (e.g., OXA-23, OXA-66, OXA-48, KPC-2/−3, NDM-1/−5) were assigned only when the amplicon encompassed the allele-defining polymorphisms and matched 100% at those positions; otherwise, results were reported at the family level (e.g., blaNDM-like, blaKPC-like, blaOXA-48-like, blaOXA-51-like). Sequencing results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Phenotypic carbapenemase detection

Modified Hodge Test (MHT)

The Modified Hodge Test (MHT) was performed according to legacy CLSI M100 guidelines [21]. A lawn of Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (0.5 McFarland, 1:10 dilution) was spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar, and a 10 µg meropenem disc was placed at the center. Test isolates were streaked radially from the disc, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Cloverleaf-like indentation around the streaks was interpreted as positive for carbapenemase production [41]. K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1705 and E. coli ATCC 25922 served as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Modified Carbapenem Inactivation Method (mCIM)

The modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) was performed according to CLSI M100 (2024) guidelines [21]. A 10 µL loopful of the test isolate was suspended in 2 mL of tryptic soy broth containing a 10 µg meropenem disc and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The disc was then transferred to Mueller–Hinton agar seeded with Escherichia coli ATCC 25922. After incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, inhibition zone diameters were interpreted as follows: ≤ 15 mm, positive; 16–18 mm, indeterminate; and ≥ 19 mm, negative [22].

Optimized Carbapenem Inactivation Method (oCIM)

The optimized carbapenem inactivation method (oCIM), adapted for Acinetobacter baumannii, was performed as previously described [20–22]. Bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 8.0, centrifuged, and resuspended in 200 µL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) containing a 10 µg meropenem disc. After incubation at 37 °C for 6 h, the disc was transferred to Mueller–Hinton agar seeded with Escherichia coli ATCC 25922. Inhibition zones were interpreted according to mCIM breakpoints.

Commercial disk-based carbapenemase detection (99007 Panel)

The 99007 panel (Liofilchem, Italy) was used to differentiate carbapenemase types by testing meropenem alone and in combination with EDTA (for metallo-β-lactamases, MBLs), phenylboronic acid (for KPC), temocillin (for OXA-48-like enzymes), and cloxacillin (for AmpC). Results were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, based on zone diameter shifts and synergy patterns [42].

Spectrophotometric carbapenemase detection assays

Carba NP test

The Carba NP assay was performed as described by Nordmann et al. [23], using phenol red (0.05%) with or without imipenem (6 mg/mL) in Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.4). Bacterial suspensions were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, centrifuged, and the supernatant absorbance was measured at 560 nm. Carbapenemase activity was indicated by a color change and reduced absorbance, with pH values estimated using a phenol red calibration curve.

CarbAcineto NP test

The CarbAcineto NP assay, adapted for Acinetobacter spp., replaced Tris–HCl with 5 M NaCl to enhance bacterial lysis, as described by Dortet et al. [26]. Colonies were incubated with phenol red (0.5 mg/mL) with or without imipenem (6 mg/mL), centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 560 nm. Carbapenemase activity was indicated by changes in color and absorbance.

UniCarb test

A cross-species lysis buffer (Tris–HCl, NaCl, MgCl₂, ZnSO₄, glycerol, Tween-20, and lysozyme) was formulated to enhance enzyme extraction from K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii. Colonies were suspended in the buffer, sonicated, and centrifuged. The resulting extract was mixed with phenol red and imipenem, incubated at 42 °C, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm. This in-house UniCarb assay integrates optimized lysis, spectrophotometric detection, and polynomial calibration, overcoming the species-specific limitations of the Carba NP test [23].

Statistical analysis

A phenol red calibration curve (pH 0–10) was generated by measuring absorbance at 560 nm. Polynomial regression models (second- to fourth-order) were fitted using the least-squares method. The fourth-order model (R2 = 0.9344) was selected as the best compromise between accuracy and model complexity, avoiding overfitting [37, 38].

pH estimation from absorbance

Assay pH values were estimated from absorbance readings using the fourth-order polynomial model. Root-solving was performed with the fsolve function in SciPy to ensure objective and reproducible calculations [39].

Correlation and descriptive statistics

Pearson and Spearman correlation tests confirmed a strong association between absorbance and pH values (p < 0.0001). Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), standard error (SE), and 95% confidence intervals (CI), were calculated for both positive and negative groups.

Comparative tests

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on distribution, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied for within-group comparisons, Friedman tests for repeated measures, and either independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for interspecies analyses [40].

Diagnostic performance

Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-score, Youden’s index, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), Cohen’s kappa, and McNemar’s test were used to assess assay performance against PCR-confirmed results [40–42]. Confusion matrices were constructed for both visual and spectrophotometric outcomes.

Computational tools

All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 using NumPy [43], SciPy [39], pandas [44], matplotlib [45], and scikit-learn [46].

Results

Culture results and distribution of mastitis-associated bacteria

Of the 335 quarter milk samples obtained from 84 CMT-positive cows, 49 (14.6%) yielded bacterial growth: 34 Gram-negative (10.1% of all samples; 69.4% of positive cultures) and 15 Gram-positive (4.5% of all samples; 30.6% of positive cultures) isolates. The remaining 286 samples (85.4%) were culture-negative, which may reflect low bacterial loads, non-bacterial etiologies, or organisms not recoverable under aerobic conditions. As this study focused on Gram-negative pathogens, only these isolates were subjected to subsequent phenotypic and molecular analyses.

16S rRNA gene sequencing identification of study isolates

All 34 Gram-negative isolates were identified by partial 16S rRNA sequencing as A. baumannii (n = 13, 99.15–99.72% identity; 97–100% coverage) and K. pneumoniae (n = 21, 98.33–99.71% identity; 99–100% coverage), values consistent with species-level assignment. The verified sequences have been deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PV710479–PV710512. No other Gram-negative species were recovered in this dataset, which is why the present study is limited to K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii.

Antibiotic susceptibility tests

Kirby bauer antibiogram

Antibiotic resistance profiles of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii

A total of 21 K. pneumoniae and 13 A. baumannii isolates were tested against 12 antibiotics using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. Only culture-confirmed K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii isolates were included, while other mastitis pathogens were excluded from analysis. K. pneumoniae showed complete resistance (100%) to all tested β-lactams (AMC, CTX, CRO, CAZ, FOX, IMP) and a high resistance rate to ATM (90%). Universal resistance was also observed to CIP and SXT, whereas resistance to CN, C, and LEV was 67%, 52%, and 90%, respectively, consistent with an MDR phenotype. In contrast, A. baumannii exhibited complete resistance (100%) to all tested agents, including β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and carbapenems, qualifying as PDR. These results highlight a critical resistance burden in mastitis-associated pathogens and emphasize the need for integrated AMR surveillance. A binary heatmap of isolate-level resistance is shown in Fig. 1, with full antibiogram data provided in Supplementary Table S2.

MDR/XDR/PDR classification of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii isolates

All K. pneumoniae (n = 21) and A. baumannii (n = 13) isolates were classified as either XDR or PDR, with no MDR profiles detected. Within K. pneumoniae, 38% (8/21) were XDR, whereas 62% (13/21) exhibited a PDR phenotype (Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, A. baumannii demonstrated complete uniformity, with all isolates (100%, 13/13) classified as PDR. These findings highlight the severe therapeutic challenge posed by mastitis-associated carbapenemase producers.

Broth microdilution MIC profiling

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing by broth microdilution demonstrated extensive carbapenem resistance in all K. pneumoniae (n = 21) and A. baumannii (n = 13) isolates. In K. pneumoniae, ertapenem showed the broadest MIC range (32–2048 µg/mL; median > 512 µg/mL), followed by meropenem (32–512 µg/mL) and imipenem (8–1024 µg/mL). Although inter-isolate variability was observed, all values exceeded CLSI resistance thresholds (≥ 2 µg/mL for ertapenem, ≥ 4 µg/mL for meropenem, ≥ 8 µg/mL for imipenem), indicating high-level resistance. In A. baumannii, imipenem MICs were narrowly clustered at 256 µg/mL, while meropenem ranged from 64–256 µg/mL (median = 128 µg/mL). Ertapenem exhibited uniformly elevated values (1024–2048 µg/mL), confirming exceptional resistance. No isolate tested below CLSI breakpoints, establishing universal phenotypic resistance. Genotypic detection of carbapenemase genes

Multiplex PCR demonstrated extensive carbapenemase gene carriage among K. pneumoniae (n = 21) and A. baumannii (n = 13) isolates. In K. pneumoniae, blaNDM was most prevalent (90.5%, 19/21), followed by blaOXA-48-like (61.9%, 13/21) and blaKPC (33.3%, 7/21). Co-carriage was common: isolates K1, K3, and K8 harbored both blaNDM and blaOXA-48-like, and K1 was additionally positive for blaKPC. Representative gel bands are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, and aggregate gene profiles are summarized in Fig. 3.

Red indicates gene presence; blue indicates absence

All A. baumannii isolates (100%, 13/13) harbored intrinsic blaOXA-51-like together with blaOXA-23; blaVIM, blaIMP (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3), blaNDM, blaOXA-48-like, and blaKPC were not detected. Primer specificity was verified using positive controls (Supplementary Fig. S4). To confirm PCR specificity, one representative amplicon per carbapenemase gene target (blaNDM, blaKPC, blaOXA-48-like, blaOXA-23, blaOXA-51-like) was bidirectionally sequenced. All validated amplicons matched their corresponding β-lactamase families with high identity values, confirming assay specificity (Supplementary Table S1).

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemases

Disk tests (99007 panel)

The 99007 disk panel was applied to phenotypically detect carbapenemase production in K. pneumoniae, using multiplex PCR as the reference standard. Among the tested combinations, MR + ED (for blaNDM) demonstrated the best performance, with 76% positivity, 84% sensitivity, 100% specificity, an F1-score of 0.91, a Youden index of 0.84, and moderate agreement (Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ) = 0.50), corresponding to 16 true positives and 3 false negatives. MR + BO (for blaKPC) showed 71% positivity and 86% sensitivity but poor specificity (36%), resulting in an F1-score of 0.55, a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 3.33, slight agreement (κ = 0.17), and significant discordance with PCR (McNemar’s p = 0.0215). TMO (for blaOXA-48) provided limited diagnostic value, with 29% positivity, 36% sensitivity, 86% specificity, an F1-score of 0.50, and κ = 0.17. MR + Cl remained consistently negative across all isolates, indicating the absence of AmpC activity. Full performance metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Overall, MR + ED emerged as the most reliable phenotypic marker, although molecular confirmation remains essential for accurate detection in multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae.

Modified hodge test

Carbapenemase activity in 21 K. pneumoniae isolates and four controls was assessed using the Modified Hodge Test (MHT), with multiplex PCR as the reference standard. Twelve isolates tested positive and nine negative, with representative cloverleaf patterns observed. Analysis showed 6 true positives, 6 false positives, 8 true negatives, and 1 false negative, corresponding to 86% sensitivity, 57% specificity, 50% positive predictive value (PPV), 89% negative predictive value (NPV), 67% overall accuracy, an F1-score of 0.63, a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 8.0, and fair agreement (κ = 0.36). McNemar’s test indicated no significant discordance with PCR (p = 0.125). Full performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Although MHT demonstrated acceptable sensitivity, its poor specificity limits its utility as a standalone diagnostic tool.

Modified Carbapenem Inactivation Method (mCIM)

The mCIM assay was applied to 25 isolates (21 PCR-confirmed K. pneumoniae and 4 negative controls). Analysis identified 20 true positives, 3 true negatives, 1 false positive, and 1 false negative, corresponding to 95% sensitivity, 75% specificity, 95% positive predictive value (PPV), 75% negative predictive value (NPV), and 92% overall accuracy. The F1-score was 0.95, with a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 60.0, and Cohen’s kappa indicated substantial agreement (κ = 0.70). McNemar’s test showed no significant discordance with PCR (p = 1.0), confirming the high diagnostic reliability of mCIM for detecting carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae. Full performance metrics are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Optimized Carbapenem Inactivation Method (oCIM)

The oCIM assay was evaluated on 21 K. pneumoniae and 13 A. baumannii isolates, each tested alongside four PCR-negative controls. In K. pneumoniae, oCIM correctly identified all 21 PCR-confirmed positives, with one false positive among the controls. Analysis yielded 100% sensitivity, 75% specificity, 95% positive predictive value (PPV), 100% negative predictive value (NPV), 96% overall accuracy, an F1-score of 0.98, substantial agreement (κ = 0.83), and no significant discordance with PCR by McNemar’s test (p = 1.0) (Supplementary Fig. S23). Similarly, in A. baumannii, oCIM detected all 13 PCR-confirmed positives with one false positive among controls. Diagnostic indices included 100% sensitivity, 75% specificity, 93% PPV, 100% NPV, 94% overall accuracy, an F1-score of 0.96, substantial agreement (κ = 0.82), and no significant discordance (p = 1.0). Full performance metrics are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Spectrophotometric and pH-based evaluation of carbapenemase detection assays

The Carba NP assay (Fig. 4A) demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in K. pneumoniae. PCR-positive isolates showed a mean absorbance of 0.719 ± 0.165 and a mean pH of 6.994 ± 0.244. Strong inverse correlations between absorbance and pH (Pearson’s r = 0.9965, Spearman’s ρ = 0.9917; p < 0.0001) supported robust polynomial modeling. Using an optimal cutoff of 1.09, spectrophotometric interpretation achieved perfect classification (Area Under the Curve (AUC) = 1.0, Youden’s index = 1.0). Visual interpretation yielded 90% sensitivity and 100% specificity, whereas spectrophotometry correctly classified all isolates (F1-score = 1.00, κ = 1.00; McNemar’s p = 1.0) (Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S5).

In A. baumannii, PCR-positive isolates had a mean absorbance of 0.922 ± 0.111 and a mean pH of 7.271 ± 0.161, with near-perfect correlations between absorbance and pH (r = 0.9994, ρ = 0.9986; p < 0.0001). CarbAcineto NP test

The CarbAcineto NP assay (Fig. 4B) was applied to K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii using both visual and spectrophotometric interpretation. In K. pneumoniae, PCR-positive isolates showed a mean absorbance of 0.128 ± 0.174 and a mean pH of 5.969 ± 0.409, with strong correlations between absorbance and pH (Pearson’s r = 0.935; Spearman’s ρ = 0.849; p < 0.0001). An optimal cutoff of 0.70 enabled perfect discrimination (AUC = 1.0). Visual interpretation achieved 95% sensitivity and 100% specificity, whereas spectrophotometry reached 100% across all diagnostic parameters (Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S5).

In A. baumannii, PCR-positive isolates exhibited a mean absorbance of 0.732 ± 0.060 and a mean pH of 7.072 ± 0.118, with similarly strong correlations (r = 0.945; ρ = 0.847; p < 0.0001). A cutoff of 0.831 allowed complete classification by spectrophotometry (100% sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy), while visual interpretation yielded 92.3% sensitivity and 94.1% accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S5).

These findings highlight the superior diagnostic accuracy and objectivity of spectrophotometric CarbAcineto NP compared with traditional visual interpretation.

UniCarb test

The UniCarb assay (Fig. 4C) demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance in both K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii. In K. pneumoniae, PCR-positive isolates exhibited a mean absorbance of 0.0391 ± 0.0033 and a mean pH of 5.719 ± 0.0096, with near-perfect correlations between absorbance and pH (Pearson’s r = 0.995; Spearman’s ρ = 0.993; p < 0.0001). An optimal cutoff of 0.044 enabled perfect classification (AUC = 1.0). Both visual and spectrophotometric interpretation achieved 100% sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, NPV, F1-score, κ = 1.00, and no discordance with PCR (McNemar’s p = 1.0) (Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S5).

In A. baumannii, PCR-positive isolates showed a mean absorbance of 0.0429 ± 0.0022 and a mean pH of 5.729 ± 0.0062, with extremely strong correlations (r = 0.9994; ρ = 1.000; p < 0.0001). A cutoff of 0.047 again enabled perfect diagnostic classification, with both visual and spectrophotometric interpretation achieving 100% across all evaluated parameters (Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S5).

These findings establish UniCarb as a rapid, cross-species assay with complete diagnostic reliability, offering superior robustness compared to conventional phenotypic tests.

Comparative analysis of carbapenemase detection assays

A comparative evaluation of the Carba NP, CarbAcineto NP, and UniCarb assays was conducted to assess diagnostic resolution and interspecies neutrality in detecting carbapenemase activity in K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii. Each assay was applied using both visual and spectrophotometric (OD₅₆₀) interpretation, with pH values modeled by fourth-order polynomial regression. Normality testing confirmed Gaussian distribution for Carba NP and UniCarb (p > 0.05), whereas CarbAcineto NP violated normality assumptions (p < 0.00001), necessitating non-parametric analysis. Friedman and Wilcoxon tests revealed significant overall differences among the assays (p < 0.00001) (Supplementary Table S6).

Cross-species comparisons showed that Carba NP and CarbAcineto NP exhibited significant interspecies variation in absorbance and pH, with A. baumannii producing higher pH values in Carba NP (~ 7.3) and CarbAcineto NP (~ 7.1), thereby reducing test contrast (p < 0.00001). In contrast, UniCarb demonstrated stable performance, with no significant absorbance variation (p = 0.442) and only negligible pH differences (ΔpH ≈ 0.01; p = 0.0004), confirming its species-independent diagnostic profile. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, B, UniCarb maintained minimal interspecies deviation. Diagnostic performance metrics further reinforced these findings: spectrophotometric interpretation improved accuracy for both Carba NP and CarbAcineto NP (κ = 1.00), while UniCarb achieved perfect accuracy and reproducibility under both visual and spectrophotometric interpretation, underscoring its superiority as a cross-species diagnostic assay.

Discussion

Carbapenem resistance has been extensively investigated in human medicine, but its occurrence in veterinary pathogens remains less well studied. The global emergence of carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria in livestock highlights a growing concern within the One Health framework of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [1–4]. In this study, K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii isolated from bovine mastitic milk were confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing (≥ 98.3%) [36]. Among the tested isolates, all A. baumannii strains were classified as PDR, while K. pneumoniae showed 62% PDR and 38% XDR. These levels are higher than those reported in earlier mastitis studies [6]. MIC testing confirmed substantial carbapenem resistance, with ertapenem MICs frequently exceeding 1024 µg/mL in A. baumannii, well above CLSI breakpoints [21].

Genotypic analysis revealed multiple carbapenemase genes in K. pneumoniae: blaNDM (90.5%), blaOXA-48 (61.9%), and blaKPC (33.3%), indicating polygenic resistance mechanisms [11, 12, 19]. In contrast, A. baumannii consistently carried intrinsic blaOXA-51 and acquired blaOXA-23, but lacked blaNDM, blaOXA-48, blaKPC, blaVIM, and blaIMP. This pattern highlights a distinct resistance ecology compared to clinical strains, with resistance primarily driven by blaOXA-23, since blaOXA-51 alone confers only weak hydrolytic activity [12, 15].

Phenotypic detection methods demonstrated variable performance. The Modified Hodge Test (MHT) showed poor specificity (57%) and low diagnostic agreement (κ = 0.36), reaffirming its diminished clinical relevance [21, 41]. mCIM and oCIM performed better (κ = 0.70 and 0.83), with oCIM emerging as a reliable cross-species tool [22, 26]. Visual Carba NP and CarbAcineto NP assays were enzyme- and species-dependent: Carba NP achieved 90% sensitivity in K. pneumoniae but only 46% in A. baumannii, whereas CarbAcineto NP improved detection in both species (95% and 92%) [23–26]. Nevertheless, both assays remained observer-dependent and inconsistent across species.

Although immunochromatographic assays such as NG-Test® CARBA-5 are increasingly used in human medicine, their diagnostic scope is limited. According to the manufacturer (NG Biotech, France), CARBA-5 detects only NDM, KPC, VIM, IMP, and OXA-48-like carbapenemases, and is validated primarily for Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, excluding Acinetobacter spp. [47]. Its variant-specific design requires frequent updates, and the high per-test cost restricts feasibility for veterinary or environmental surveillance. These limitations prevent its adoption as a universal AMR monitoring tool outside well-resourced clinical laboratories.

To address these challenges, we developed the UniCarb assay, incorporating a spectrophotometric module for objective OD₅₆₀ measurement and polynomial pH modeling. This approach enhanced interpretability, particularly in borderline cases, and achieved perfect diagnostic performance (AUC = 1.00) in both species, correcting errors from visual interpretation. UniCarb integrated optimized lysis, imipenem hydrolysis, and statistical modeling to deliver consistent, species-independent performance, eliminating variability in absorbance and pH dynamics (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Tables S4–S6). Absorbance differences between species were non-significant (p = 0.442), while minimal pH variation (ΔpH ≈ 0.01; p = 0.0004) was biologically negligible an achievement unmatched by conventional or modified assays.

The modified Carba NP described by Bakour et al. [48] incorporated CTAB-mediated lysis and visual pH interpretation but lacked quantitative validation and remained prone to observer bias and interspecies inconsistency. In contrast, UniCarb leverages absorbance-based quantification, polynomial regression modeling, and ROC-optimized cutoffs, enabling rapid (< 45 min), reproducible, and species-independent detection across enzyme classes, including slow hydrolyzers like OXA-23. McNemar’s test confirmed UniCarb’s superiority (p < 0.01), and diagnostic odds ratios (DOR > 500) far exceeded those of Carba NP and CarbAcineto NP (DOR = 10–30).

Importantly, the UniCarb test represents more than an incremental refinement; it is a conceptual advance in carbapenemase detection. Unlike existing assays, UniCarb is the first to combine a cross-species optimized extraction buffer with quantitative spectrophotometric readouts and polynomial pH modeling. This innovation eliminates operator bias, ensures reproducibility, and enables application across different Gram-negative pathogens with identical diagnostic accuracy. By validating UniCarb in bovine mastitis isolates an underexplored veterinary reservoir this study expands carbapenemase surveillance beyond clinical medicine into the One Health continuum, linking human, animal, and environmental health. To our knowledge, this is the first universal, species-independent carbapenemase assay validated in veterinary pathogens, and the only one to demonstrate perfect diagnostic indices across multiple enzyme classes, including challenging OXA-type enzymes.

From a regional perspective, the emergence of carbapenemase-producing mastitis pathogens in Türkiye is concerning, reflecting the infiltration of resistance beyond hospitals into veterinary reservoirs. Reports of OXA-48-like K. pneumoniae in companion animals [49] and carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae from both veterinary and human sources [50] highlight cross-species dissemination at the national level. Globally, a systematic review found carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae prevalence exceeding 40% across livestock, humans, and the environment [51], while recent One Health reports document carbapenemase genes in food animals, wildlife, and environmental niches [52].

In this context, UniCarb provides a practical and globally relevant diagnostic solution, offering rapid, quantitative, and cross-species detection of carbapenemase activity. By eliminating observer bias and interspecies variability, it strengthens AMR surveillance not only in Türkiye, where access to advanced molecular diagnostics remains limited, but also in low- and middle-income regions that urgently require affordable alternatives to PCR. Its compatibility with both veterinary and clinical workflows positions UniCarb as a robust, novel tool for precision AMR monitoring and early carbapenemase outbreak detection at the human–animal interface.

Conclusion

This study confirms the presence of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii in bovine mastitic milk, underscoring a critical One Health concern. Molecular analysis revealed blaNDM, blaKPC, and blaOXA-48 in K. pneumoniae, while A. baumannii carried blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-51 but lacked blaNDM, blaKPC, blaOXA-48, blaVIM, and blaIMP. Conventional phenotypic methods demonstrated variable accuracy, whereas our novel UniCarb assay achieved perfect diagnostic performance (100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, κ = 1.0). Its rapid, quantitative, cost-effectiveness and cross-species reliability highlight UniCarb as a robust tool for AMR surveillance, with particular value for veterinary and clinical applications in resource-limited settings.

Future perspectives

Although UniCarb demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii, broader validation across diverse Enterobacteriaceae and non-fermenting Gram-negative species is essential to establish its universal applicability. Coupling UniCarb with real-time PCR to quantify carbapenemase gene expression may provide functional correlations between genotype and absorbance-based hydrolysis, facilitating differentiation among enzyme classes (A, B, and D). Such advancements could further enhance diagnostic precision and extend UniCarb’s value as a standardized tool for One Health antimicrobial resistance surveillance.
