Multilayer GNN for predictive maintenance and clustering in power grids
Authors: Muhammad Kazim, Harun Pirim, Chau Le, Trung Le, Om Prakash Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnplanned power outages create major economic costs. To better predict and manage these events, we present a multilayer graph neural network (GNN) framework that captures spatial, short-term co-occurrence, and statistically enriched co-failure patterns using 7 years of Oklahoma Gas & Electric data from 347 substations. These relations are encoded with weighted graph convolutions and fused by attention into a unified representation. For predictive maintenance, the model flags substations requiring near-term intervention across 30-, 60-, and 180-day horizons, achieving a peak 30-day F1 score of 0.8935. The same representation supports resilience planning by clustering substations into eight risk groups, each with distinct incident rates and recovery times. The highest-risk group has over six times higher incident rates than the low-risk groups. Combining prediction and clustering in a single framework provides utilities with an integrated basis for scheduling maintenance, prioritizing inspections, and targeting grid-hardening investments to minimize outage impacts and enhance reliability.

Introduction

Energy infrastructure forms the backbone of economic productivity and societal functioning. In the United States alone, unplanned power outages cost over $150 billion annually.1 To mitigate such disruptions, proactive grid management, encompassing predictive maintenance (PdM) and resilience-based strategies, is paramount. PdM enables the early detection of equipment degradation, reducing unplanned downtime by up to 70% and lowering maintenance costs by 30%.2 Complementary to PdM, resilience-based clustering supports operational resilience through zoning strategies and prioritized maintenance planning. These integrated approaches offer substantial economic benefits and enhanced reliability. While predictive analytics in energy infrastructure has demonstrated high return-on-investment ratios, existing frameworks often fall short in fully leveraging the complex interdependencies within power systems.

A fundamental challenge in current PdM and grid resilience methodologies is their inability to model the intricate relationships that govern power system failures comprehensively. Traditional models, including rule-based, statistical, and even many machine learning approaches, often treat infrastructure assets as isolated entities. This simplification fails to capture the cascading nature of failures that propagate through interconnected grid components, overlooking crucial spatial proximity, temporal degradation trends, and causal dependencies among assets.3,4,5,6 Such limitations lead to suboptimal forecasts and fragmented resilience strategies.7 Even existing graph-based methods, while acknowledging topology, typically rely on single-layer representations that inadequately capture the heterogeneous and dynamic relationships inherent in modern power grids.6

To overcome these limitations, advanced modeling techniques are required that can capture the multifaceted nature of power grid interactions. Previous studies have explored various graph neural network (GNN) architectures to model complex relational data. For instance, spatial-temporal GNNs have been widely applied to dynamic graph problems such as traffic forecasting and social network analysis.8,9 These models effectively capture spatial dependencies between nodes and temporal dynamics over time, making them suitable for systems with evolving interactions. However, in domains such as power grids, where relationships between substations may change due to operational changes or failures, spatial-temporal GNNs often assume a fixed graph structure, limiting their adaptability. This highlights a critical need for models that can dynamically integrate multiple types of dependencies, spatial, temporal, and statistical co-failure relationships, to better model the resilience of power grid substations.

Addressing these structural complexities necessitates a shift toward multilayer network theory, which provides a formal framework for modeling interdependent infrastructures by preserving both intra-layer and inter-layer dynamics.10,11 In the context of power systems, nodes such as substations, transformers, and transmission lines often participate in diverse relational layers, ranging from physical proximity to temporal fault sequences and causal pathways. Multilayer modeling captures these relationships explicitly, overcoming the loss of information typical in aggregated graphs.12,13,14 Applications of multilayer analysis in the energy sector have demonstrated its value in identifying critical nodes whose failures induce cascading disruptions15 and in characterizing the resilience of coupled gas-electricity systems.16 These findings establish multilayer modeling as a powerful tool for understanding and mitigating systemic vulnerabilities in energy infrastructures, providing a strong theoretical basis for our proposed approach.

Building on the foundation of graph-based modeling, GNNs have emerged as a scalable and data-driven approach for learning from graph-structured data in PdM and resilience tasks. In power grids, GNNs have been used for overload prediction,17 equipment health indexing,18 and cyber-physical interaction modeling.19 However, a significant limitation of most existing GNN-based PdM models in energy systems is their predominant focus on spatial features, often neglecting the crucial temporal and causal aspects essential for accurately forecasting real-world failures. Moreover, these models generally employ single-layer graphs, which overlook the nuanced, layered interdependencies among system components.20 Consequently, they fail to capture how failures evolve over time, how causal chains propagate through the system, and how risks concentrate across different regions and subsystems.

Multilayer GNNs offer a promising solution by integrating spatial, temporal, and failure propagation patterns into a unified predictive framework. Early multilayer GNN approaches, such as supra-graph modeling,21 simplify the representation but overlook the semantics of individual layers. While multilayer GNNs such as MGCN22 and attention-based multi-relational GNNs23 have advanced layer-wise information fusion, they are primarily designed for entity-type heterogeneity rather than the spatiotemporal-causal dynamics essential for comprehensive power grid modeling. For instance, MGCN focuses on integrating multiple relation types but does not explicitly account for temporal evolution or causal propagation of failures. Similarly, Li et al.24 proposed a multi-channel GNN for event detection in power systems but overlooked explicit multilayer graph structures and relied on static type labels, limiting its adaptability to dynamic grid conditions. Zhang et al.25 demonstrated that multilayer GNNs can outperform single-layer variants by 10%–15% in wind power forecasting. Despite these advancements, a cohesive, end-to-end framework for multilayer PdM in power systems, one that effectively combines topology, time-series behavior, and failure causality, remains conspicuously absent.

Clustering in multilayer power grids plays a critical role in resilience planning, particularly for segmenting infrastructure into operational zones based on vulnerability or failure patterns. Traditional clustering methods such as k-means, spectral clustering, or even single-layer Louvain methods, fail to incorporate inter-layer dependencies and often assume equal importance across all features. This fragmentation limits the ability to systematically prioritize risk and segment the grid in a way that aligns with both PdM outcomes and real-time operational constraints. While generalized multilayer clustering techniques26 address some of these issues by optimizing inter-layer modularity, they seldom integrate predictive layers (e.g., causal fault propagation) with operational relevance. In power systems, multilayer clustering has been applied for controlled islanding, partitioning the grid into self-sustaining zones during disruptions,27 and for maintenance planning.28 Still, these approaches often treat layers heuristically or independently. Moreover, traditional methods like k-means or spectral clustering are often inadequate for power grid applications. k-means relies on Euclidean distances, which fail to account for the non-linear, relational dependencies between substations, such as those driven by electrical flows or temporal failure patterns. Similarly, spectral clustering, which leverages graph Laplacians, assumes a static graph structure and may overlook dynamic or multi-relational interactions, such as co-failures over time.27 These gaps necessitate a more sophisticated approach to resilience grouping that captures the complex interplay of spatial, temporal, and statistical dependencies in power grids, directly linking to our proposed clustering component.

Despite a growing body of work applying multilayer GNNs to individual energy tasks,17,24,29 no existing framework unifies multilayer PdM and resilience-based clustering within a scalable architecture tailored for power grids. This represents a significant research gap that our study aims to address.

To bridge this critical gap, our study makes three key contributions.(1)We propose a multilayer GNN-based PdM framework that explicitly captures spatial, temporal, and causal interdependencies using layer-specific message passing. This enables the model to learn tailored representations for each edge modality based on its distinct relational semantics, leading to superior failure prediction accuracy.(2)We introduce a risk-aware clustering approach that leverages the rich graph embeddings generated by our framework, incorporating exogenous failure drivers (e.g., vegetation, lightning, and weather) for infrastructure segmentation and prioritization. This provides operationally meaningful resilience insights that are directly linked to predictive outcomes.(3)We validate our comprehensive approach on real-world power grid data, demonstrating its effectiveness in identifying high-risk substations, accurately forecasting failures, and supporting operationally meaningful resilience clustering (i.e., groupings that reflect actual outage patterns, recovery times, and maintenance priorities).

Crucially, the architecture is designed for scalability and modularity: it supports large-scale, heterogeneous grid data and enables multi-task learning through modular layer-wise encoders, shared representations across tasks, and efficient graph sampling. By unifying multilayer PdM and risk-based clustering into a single framework, this work advances a proactive, interpretable, and scalable paradigm for energy system management.

Results

This section presents the experimental outcomes of our multilayer GNN framework applied to the Oklahoma Gas & Electric (OGE) dataset, as introduced in introduction section and elaborated in STAR Methods section. We evaluate the model’s performance on two core tasks: PdM and resilience clustering. For PdM, we assess the model’s ability to predict substation maintenance needs across 30-, 60-, and 180-day windows, leveraging the spatial, temporal, and causal layers defined in multilayer network construction section. For resilience clustering, we analyze the quality and operational utility of substation clusters derived from the fused GNN embeddings (resilience clustering section).

PdM performance

The PdM task is formulated as a binary classification problem, predicting whether a substation requires proactive replacement or major maintenance (yi = 1) or not (yi = 0), as outlined in PdM target section. Given the class imbalance (19.4% positive labels), we report accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score alongside balanced accuracy and receiver operating characteristic – area under the curve (ROC-AUC) to provide a comprehensive evaluation, with focal loss employed during training to address this imbalance.

Quantitative results for different time windows

We assessed PdM performance across three maintenance planning horizons (30, 60, and 180 days) using the multilayer GNN with stratified 3-fold cross-validation. For each fold, the model was trained on a subset of substations and tested on the remainder, ensuring no temporal leakage. The dataset spans 2015–2021, with up to 100 training epochs per fold. 180-Day window

For the 180-day window, the multilayer GNN achieved an accuracy of 0.8444 ± 0.0241, precision of 0.8225 ± 0.0456, recall of 0.8763 ± 0.0261, and F1 score of 0.8472 ± 0.0179. The balanced accuracy was 0.8476 ± 0.0388, and the ROC-AUC was 0.9010 ± 0.0150, indicating that the classifier maintains strong discrimination between classes despite the skewed label distribution. These results (Table 1) indicate robust identification of substations needing maintenance over a longer horizon, balancing false positives and missed detections effectively.

60-Day window

For the 60-day window, performance improved, with an accuracy of 0.8588 ± 0.0148, precision of 0.8817 ± 0.0152, recall of 0.8586 ± 0.0128, and F1 score of 0.8700 ± 0.0136. The balanced accuracy rose to 0.8684 ± 0.0241, and the ROC-AUC reached 0.9145 ± 0.0201, reflecting the model’s ability to correctly identify both positive and negative cases across the mid-term horizon. The higher precision suggests fewer false positives, advantageous for resource-constrained maintenance planning over mid-term horizons (Table 2).

30-Day window

The 30-day window yielded the highest performance: accuracy of 0.8846 ± 0.0253, precision of 0.9336 ± 0.0131, recall of 0.8576 ± 0.0412, and F1 score of 0.8935 ± 0.0258. Complementing these metrics, the balanced accuracy was 0.8805 ± 0.0142, and the ROC-AUC was 0.9360 ± 0.0232, underscoring the model’s strong discriminative power even in the most imbalanced short-term setting. The elevated precision reflects the model’s strength in pinpointing urgent maintenance needs with minimal false positives, ideal for short-term operational decisions (Table 3).

Across all time windows, the multilayer GNN (Multi_GNN) outperformed baselines, particularly in F1 score. For the 180-day window, it achieved an F1 score of 0.8472, surpassing SPATIAL_ONLY (0.7354) by 15% and Random Forest (0.8450) slightly. Similar trends held for 60- and 30-day windows, with the largest gains over single-layer models (10%–15%), echoing improvements noted in wind power forecasting (introduction section). Moreover, ROC-AUC scores consistently exceeded 0.90 across windows for the full model, while remaining below 0.90 for the single-layer configurations, reinforcing the benefit of modeling heterogeneous interdependencies jointly. Although the Random Forest baseline performs competitively on some metrics, the GNN consistently yields higher F1 scores and ROC-AUC values. Importantly, the Random Forest operates on flattened features and cannot leverage network structure. In contrast, the GNN propagates information along spatial, temporal, and causal edges, enabling both improved predictive accuracy and the extraction of fused embeddings for downstream resilience clustering. This underscores the value of modeling heterogeneous interdependencies jointly and illustrates why a graph-based approach is preferable despite the modest incremental gains in raw classification metrics.

Resilience-oriented substation clustering

For the resilience clustering analysis, we used the same fused node embeddings hi(fused) generated by our multilayer weighted graph convolutional network (WGCN) (multi-layer GNN architecture section) for the PdM task. Each relational modality, spatial, temporal, and causal, is encoded by a dedicated WGCN encoder, and the resulting representations are combined via attention-weighted fusion. This shared embedding captures the relative importance of each modality and is reused for clustering without additional training.

The clustering pipeline involved three stages: (1) representation extraction from the fused WGCN embeddings, (2) nonlinear manifold reduction using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with cosine similarity, and (3) density-based clustering using Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) (min_cluster_size = 15, epsilon = 0.25), which is well suited for identifying variable-density structures in high-dimensional spaces.30

This process yielded eight distinct operational clusters, with a Silhouette score of 0.626 and a Davies-Bouldin index of 0.527, indicating well-separated and internally coherent groupings. Risk profile differentiation

To assess whether the clusters reflect meaningful differences in risk, we evaluated the distribution of four normalized risk indicators: vegetation, lightning, weather, and equipment. where p = 0.0001 indicates extremely strong statistical significance, confirming that the observed differences in risk exposure across clusters are unlikely to have occurred by chance. Clusters 5 and 4 consistently score highest on risk exposure, whereas clusters 0, 1, and 2 form the low-risk tier. This validates that the model’s embeddings preserve and enhance risk information in the clustering space. To enhance interpretability, we conducted a post hoc analysis of the dominant features driving membership in the high-risk clusters. Cluster 5, for example, exhibited higher-than-average values for historical incident frequency, vegetation-related outages, and lightning density. These patterns were not only consistent across multiple clustering runs but also spatially aligned with urban-industrial corridors exposed to severe weather and vegetation overgrowth. Substations in this cluster also had elevated causal propagation scores, indicating their potential to both initiate and receive cascading failures. These factors provide a domain-consistent explanation for the high-risk label and demonstrate that the GNN embedding preserves interpretable and operationally relevant signals.

Discussion

This research addressed the pressing challenge of modeling complex interdependencies in energy infrastructure by developing a multilayer GNN framework tailored for PdM and resilience clustering. Applied to the OGE dataset spanning 2015–2021, our approach integrated spatial, temporal, and causal layers to capture heterogeneous relationships among 347 substations, surpassing the limitations of traditional single-layer models. For PdM, the multilayer GNN demonstrated superior performance across 30-, 60-, and 180-day planning horizons, achieving a peak F1 score of 0.8935 ± 0.0258 in the 30-day window. Ablation studies revealed the causal layer’s critical role, with its absence reducing the F1 score to 0.7354 ± 0.0418 when only the spatial layer was used, affirming the value of joint modeling of fault causality and temporal dynamics.

In resilience clustering, the proposed framework identified eight operationally meaningful substation clusters, each exhibiting distinct reliability and risk characteristics. Cluster 5 emerged as the most critical group, with the highest incident rate (388.4 incidents/year), prolonged recovery durations (602.6 min), and elevated environmental risk scores. In contrast, clusters 0, 1, and 2 demonstrated low incident frequencies (mean ≈ 62–64), minimal customer impact, and faster restoration times, indicating greater resilience. The clustering quality was quantitatively validated with a Silhouette score of 0.626 and a Davies-Bouldin index of 0.527, while one-way ANOVA confirmed statistically significant differentiation across all four risk factors (p<0.0001). These findings underscore the framework’s effectiveness in modeling multi-dimensional risk and its utility in supporting targeted maintenance, investment prioritization, and resilience planning across the electric grid.

Future work

Future research will focus on extending the current PdM and clustering framework beyond binary classification. Specifically, we plan to repurpose the learned multilayer substation embeddings to support multiclass classification such as predicting specific categories of failure causes and regression tasks, including estimates of outage duration, time-to-failure, or cost impact. In parallel, future work may explore how well the proposed architecture generalizes across different utility regions or infrastructure types, enabling broader applicability of the model beyond the OGE dataset.

Beyond these planned extensions, two directions merit exploration by the broader research community: (1) developing online learning variants that update embeddings as new events stream in and (2) integrating multilayer GNNs into enterprise asset management platforms to enable automated, grid-wide maintenance scheduling.

In summary, this study advances energy infrastructure management by unifying PdM and resilience clustering within a multilayer GNN framework. By leveraging heterogeneous interdependencies, it provides a robust foundation for proactive grid management and sets the stage for deeper predictive analytics in future energy systems.

Limitations of the study

The labeling strategy for PdM is based on historical incident sequences, specifically the computed time until the next observed major failure. While appropriate for offline training, this approach assumes full temporal visibility within the dataset and may require adaptation for real-time or streaming applications where future incidents are not yet observable at the time of prediction. For resilience clustering, the absence of ground truth cluster labels necessitates manual tuning of HDBSCAN parameters, which limits the plug-and-play generalizability of the clustering framework across different utility networks. Furthermore, the survival analysis assumes full observability of substation failure histories, an assumption that may not hold in real-time settings where event data can be censored or incomplete.

The dataset provides seven years of operational data, averaging approximately 4.2 incidents per day. Preprocessing pipeline

Before model construction, we implement a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline to ensure data integrity. In brief, we (i) remove or impute records with invalid timestamps and unresolved substation identifiers, (ii) standardize substation names and reconcile them with reference data via fuzzy matching, (iii) impute missing voltages using a combination of string extraction, feeder-based voting, and regional defaults, and (iv) harmonize device-type categories through a mapping and imputation process. These steps produce clean, aligned node and edge attributes for the heterogeneous graph. Full details, including code snippets and validation statistics, are provided in Supplemental Information (Data S1).

Multilayer Network Construction

We construct a weighted multilayer heterogeneous graph31 G to represent inter-substation dependencies across spatial, temporal, and causal dimensions.32 The graph is defined as:(Equation 1)G=(V,Espatial,Etemporal,Ecausal),where each node v∈V corresponds to a substation, and each weighted edge layer encodes a distinct class of relational structure.

Node Features (V):

Each of the |V|=347 substations is represented by an 8-dimensional numeric vector comprising: Geospatial (Latitude, Longitude), Topological (Number of connections, Avg. line voltage, Line length (km)), Incident Statistics (Historical incident count, Mean SAIFI), Electrical (Nominal voltage), and Risk Indicators (Vegetation, Lightning, Weather, and Equipment failure counts). Categorical metadata (e.g., substation type via PLANTCLASS) is separately one-hot encoded and appended. All numeric features are standardized using StandardScaler, except geospatial coordinates, which are preserved in raw form to maintain spatial fidelity.

Edge Layers (E):

All three edge layers are weighted, with edge-specific attributes incorporated into the downstream GNN. Specifically.(1)Espatial: Encodes physical transmission lines and proximity-based substation relationships. This includes either a unit weight for physical connections or a distance-decay weight for proximity-based links.(2)Etemporal: Captures time-decayed weights based on inter-incident time gaps and co-occurrence of outage events across substations.(3)Ecausal: Represents statistically enriched correlations such as standardized co-occurrence scores and frequency ratios in outage causes (e.g., lightning, vegetation) between substations.

Graph assembly

We integrate node features and edge layers into a unified heterogeneous graph using the PyTorch Geometric HeteroData class.31,33 Each substation is indexed via a unique identifier, and edge types are explicitly annotated as triplets of the form: (substation, edge_type, substation), where edge_type ∈ {spatial, temporal, causal}. Edge attributes are type-specific: spatial and temporal edges include physical and proximity metadata (e.g., line length, shared feeders), while causal edges incorporate co-occurrence statistics and one-hot encoded cause labels. The final heterogeneous graph object is stored in PyTorch’s binary.pt format using torch.save() for efficient loading in downstream graph learning workflows.34,35

The temporal layer encodes short-term causal or co-occurrence relationships between substations based on incident timestamps. We define an adaptive yet globally fixed time window θ for constructing temporal edges by calculating the 80th percentile of inter-incident arrival times across the entire dataset. Specifically, incident timestamps are sorted, and the difference between consecutive outage events is computed to form a distribution of inter-arrival intervals. The 80th percentile of this distribution yields a data-driven temporal window θ that captures typical co-occurrence dynamics while avoiding excessive noise. This window is uniformly applied across all substations and outage types, making it globally adaptive but not localized per node. The chosen percentile offers a balance between lower thresholds and risk of omitting valid correlations, while higher values can lead to overly dense edge sets. While we do not vary the percentile in this study, the chosen 80th percentile was informed by empirical inspection of outage dynamics and was found to produce a well-balanced temporal edge network. Multi-layer GNN architecture

To generate predictive embeddings for each substation and classify the needs_replacement label (yi), we design a specialized Graph Neural Network (GNN) that processes each relational layer independently and then fuses the resulting embeddings. The architecture comprises two main components:

Layer-specific message passing

We employ separate GNN modules for each edge type spatial, temporal, and causal to enable targeted message passing tailored to the unique semantics and statistical properties of each relational layer. This separation allows the model to learn modality-specific representations, as spatial edges reflect physical proximity, temporal edges encode time-sensitive co-occurrence patterns, and causal edges capture enriched statistical dependencies. Each node’s features are normalized with respect to the edge type, and edge attributes (e.g., distance, temporal decay, causal strength) are incorporated into the respective message-passing functions.(1)Spatial Layer: We implement a weighted Graph Convolutional Network (WGCN) inspired by Kipf & Welling’s GCN.39 The WGCN dynamically computes learnable edge weights from physical features (voltage level, geographic distance, shared feeders) so that influential spatial connections receive higher weights. This design captures physical and geographic interactions while maintaining compatibility with our code base.(2)Temporal Layer: We likewise employ a WGCN for the temporal layer. Exponential time-decay weights are incorporated into the edge weights to modulate the influence of recent events. This formulation allows the model to capture short-term temporal patterns in failure propagation through the same convolutional mechanism, ensuring a consistent treatment of spatial and temporal dependencies.(3)Causal Layer: The causal layer is also instantiated as a WGCN, where edge weights are derived from statistically enriched co-occurrence metrics (e.g., standardized co-failure scores and frequency ratios). Although Graph Isomorphism Networks (GIN)40 are known for their expressive power, our empirical tuning found that a unified WGCN with learnable edge weights performed comparably while simplifying the architecture and facilitating joint optimization across modalities.

Attention-based fusion and prediction

To combine the layer-specific embeddings, we apply an attention-based fusion mechanism that learns to weight each embedding via trainable attention scores:(Equation 10)hi(fused)=Φ(αs·hi(spatial)⊕αt·hi(temporal)⊕αc·hi(causal))where ⊕ denotes vector concatenation, and Φ(·) represents a feed-forward neural network (typically composed of one or two multilayer perceptron layers). The attention weights αs, αt, and αc are normalized coefficients learned via multi-head self-attention, enabling the model to adaptively weigh the contribution of each relational modality during fusion. The resulting fused embedding hi(fused) is passed through a final linear layer followed by a log-softmax activation to produce a two-class probability distribution.

Training and evaluation

The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer41 with a learning rate of 10-3 and weight decay of 10-5. Gradient clipping is applied with an l2-norm threshold of ‖∇‖2 ≤ 1.0 to stabilize training. We employ early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overfitting.

To address the class imbalance in the target labels, we use the focal loss function38 with parameters α=0.75 and γ=2. This formulation emphasizes hard-to-classify positive samples by downweighting easy negatives. The focal loss is defined as:(Equation 11)Lfocal=−α(1−pt)γlog(pt)where pt is the predicted probability of the true class, and α and γ control the degree of focus on misclassified examples. The optimizer is coupled with a learning rate scheduler that reduces the learning rate on validation loss plateaus, further enhancing convergence stability.

Each WGCN encoder consists of two layers with a hidden dimension of 256 and a dropout rate of 0.5. These hyperparameters were chosen via a grid search over {2, 3} layers, hidden dimensions {128, 256}, and dropout rates {0.3, 0.5}, with validation F1-score as the selection criterion. Training was limited to 100 epochs with a patience of 20 epochs for early stopping. Across these experiments, the chosen configuration offered the best balance of model capacity and generalization, and we apply the same settings to all three relational modalities for consistency and simplicity.

The dataset is partitioned using a temporal split to mimic real-world deployment settings: data from 2015–2018 is used for training, 2019 for validation, and 2020–2021 for testing. For robustness, we perform three-fold cross-validation on each time window (30, 60, and 180 days), and report averaged metrics across folds.

Resilience clustering

To support long-term resilience planning, we cluster substations in the same embedding space used for predictive maintenance. Specifically, we operate directly on the fused node embeddings hi(fused) generated by the WGCN-based GNN. This ensures that both the predictive and clustering tasks leverage a common multi-modal representation of spatial, temporal, and causal dependencies, avoiding the need for a separate hierarchical encoder.

Embedding generation

To maintain coherence with the predictive-maintenance architecture, we have used the fused embedding hi(fused) from Equation 10 as the sole input to the resilience pipeline. This embedding is produced by our two-layer WGCN encoders for each relational layer (spatial, temporal, causal), followed by an attention-based fusion. Because the same embedding captures all relevant dependencies, it can be reused for both predictive maintenance and resilience analysis without additional training.

Clustering in latent space

We apply UMAP42 for dimensionality reduction (to R3) followed by HDBSCAN30 clustering on the embedding space. HDBSCAN infers the number of clusters and marks outliers as noise. We set: min_cluster_size = 15, min_samples = 5, and cluster_selection_epsilon = 0.5. These settings yield robust, interpretable clusters that group substations with similar multi-risk exposure patterns.

Cluster interpretation and evaluation

The learned clusters are interpreted through a structured post-processing pipeline. Once embeddings are generated from the multilayer-GNN, we apply dimensionality reduction (e.g., UMAP) to map high-dimensional embeddings to a lower-dimensional space while preserving local structure. Cluster assignments are obtained using HDBSCAN,30 which automatically infers the number of clusters and identifies noise points. Quantification and statistical analysis
