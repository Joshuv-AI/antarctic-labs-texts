Degree of milling-oriented framework for aroma regulation in Daohuaxiang rice
Authors: Rui Lai, Jie Liu, Xiaoji Fei, Jikai Zhao, Qing Huang, Hongzhou An, Hanxiao Qin, Wenxuan Pan, Qing Li, Yong Chen, Liu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice aroma, a critical determinant of consumer acceptance, is influenced by the degree of milling (DOM). In this work, the volatile fingerprints of 11 Daohuaxiang rice samples across a DOM gradient were characterized using gas chromatography-olfactometry-mass spectrometry, chemometrics, and quantitative aroma structural metrics. Of 50 identified volatiles, 24 differentiated DOM levels, and 12 were key odor-active compounds. As DOM increased, 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline supplanted nonanal and 2-pentylfuran as the dominant aroma contributor. The dominant odor index (DOI) increased from 0.26 to 0.39, whereas Shannon diversity (H′) and Pielou's evenness (J′) decreased from 1.718 and 0.883 to 1.522 and 0.782, indicating a simplified aroma profile. The 3.40% bran degree sample achieved the highest sensory score, characterized by the dominance (DOI = 0.39) of 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline and moderate diversity (H′ = 1.537) and evenness (J′ = 0.790) of key odor-active compounds. A DOM-oriented aroma-quality framework was established, linking DOM-induced changes in proximate composition to key odor-active compound profiles and aroma quality.Keywords: Degree of milling, GC-O-MS, Odor-active compound, Aroma structure, Aroma regulation framework

Introduction

Rice is a crucial staple food, especially in Asia, serving as a fundamental component of daily diets and a vital nutrient source (Wang et al., 2011). Nutrient distribution in rice kernel was non-uniform, as proteins, lipids, fiber, and minerals were concentrated in the embryo, bran, and outer endosperm (Lamberts et al., 2007). Therefore, although milling improved texture and eating quality, it also removed valuable nutrients. Conversely, retaining more bran could substantially enhance nutritional quality and increase yield (Billiris et al., 2012; Lanning & Siebenmorgen, 2011). Driven by consumer health consciousness, a lower degree of milling has become an emerging trend (Qu et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2008). However, this adjustment impacted not only yield and nutrition but also notably altered rice aroma (Choi et al., 2018).

The odor was a crucial quality parameter of rice, with even minor variations significantly influencing the consumer preference and purchase decisions (Champagne, 2008; Yin, Maradza, et al., 2022). The degree of milling (DOM) significantly impacted the intensity of corn, grain-starchy, and metallic flavor attributes in cooked rice (Champagne et al., 1997). Compared with well-milled rice, brown rice showed higher intensities of cooked grain, feedy, wet cardboard, nutty, burlap, and woody notes, whereas its sulfury note was less intense (Billiris et al., 2012; Rodríguez-Arzuaga et al., 2016). The sweet taste of cooked rice increased with higher DOM, while the puffed-corn flavor decreased (Park et al., 2001).

The aroma of cooked rice was derived from two primary sources: the inherent volatile compounds within the grain, and new volatile compounds generated from precursors, such as lipids and proteins, through chemical reactions during the cooking process (Hu et al., 2020). The major conversion reactions include lipid oxidation and the thermal decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides, through which unsaturated fatty acids can be converted into aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, and furans, such as hexanal, heptanal, nonanal, octanal, 1-octen-3-ol, and 2-pentylfuran (Dias et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2020; Lai et al., 2025). In addition, Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation between reducing sugars or carbonyl compounds and amino acids/protein-derived peptides can generate aldehydes, heterocyclic compounds, and nitrogen-containing aroma compounds (Hu et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2022). For fragrant rice, 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline is mainly associated with genetic and precursor-controlled pathways in the grain, while Maillard-type reactions during heating may further contribute to its formation (Wei et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2022). The final aromatic quality was ultimately determined by the complex combination of all these volatile compounds (Bergman et al., 2000). These natural volatile compounds and the precursors were predominantly concentrated in the rice bran layer (Li et al., 2023; Mahmud et al., 2017). Their overall content decreased significantly as the milling degree increased, especially the contents of acids, esters, and alcohols (Bergman et al., 2000; Li et al., 2023). The content of 3-hexanone, benzene, 2-pentylfuran, and pentanal detected in a fragrant rice variety, Cheonjihyang-1-se, exhibited a progressive decrease as the DOM increased from 1.33% to 11.0% (Mahmud et al., 2017). Compared to the total volatile compounds present in the brown rice, the industrial milling process decreased the overall volatile compounds in the well-milled Yichuan fragrant rice by 30% (Liu & Yang, 2011). Similarly, unmilled black rice had a significantly higher content of total volatiles at 158 ng/g compared to milled black rice with 67.7 ng/g (Choi et al., 2019). The presence of unique aromatic compounds (e.g., guaiacol and vanillin) and off-flavor compounds (e.g., lipid oxidation products) in uncooked and cooked black rice decreased as the DOM increased (Hu et al., 2020). The impact of altering the DOM on the aroma of milled rice, particularly significant for fragrant rice varieties with unique aromas, should not be overlooked despite enhancements in head rice yield and nutritional value.

High-quality rice has become the primary focus of the rice industry. Daohuaxiang rice, a fragrant variety cultivated in Wuchang City, Heilongjiang Province, is highly prized for its distinctive aroma and excellent taste, occupying a prominent position in the premium rice market. In this study, Daohuaxiang rice samples with different DOM were prepared to establish volatile compound fingerprints and identify key odor-active compounds, thereby elucidating the regulatory mechanism by which DOM influences the dynamic changes in the aroma of cooked rice. Ultimately, a DOM-oriented aroma regulation framework was proposed, providing a theoretical basis and practical guidance for the standardized production and flavor quality improvement of medium-milled rice.

Materials and methods

Materials

The paddy rice used in this study was Daohuaxiang No. 2, a specific rice cultivar. The samples were harvested in 2020 from Wuchang City, Heilongjiang Province, China, supplied by Wilmar International, and used for experiments in 2021. The initial moisture content of the rice was 14.19 ± 0.06%. The basic kernel characteristics before milling were as follows: length, 6.75 ± 0.17 mm; width, 2.54 ± 0.40 mm; thickness, 2.03 ± 0.08 mm; and length-to-width ratio, 2.65 ± 0.06. The grains were classified as medium grain. The n-alkanes solution (C5–C30) was purchased from Agilent Technology Co., Ltd. (Palo Alto, CA, USA). The standard odor compounds employed in this study, including 2-methyl-3-heptanone as the internal standard, were procured from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade.

Rice milling

Daohuaxiang paddy rice was dehulled using a rice huller (THU35C, Satake, Japan) to obtain brown rice. The brown rice was pre-milled using a rice mill (TM05C, Satake, Japan) equipped with a 40# abrasive roll. For each batch, 200.00 g of brown rice was milled at 1060 r/min for 23 s to obtain lightly milled rice. The pre-milled rice obtained from multiple batches was then combined, thoroughly mixed, and further milled using a rice mill (CBS 550AS (2), Satake, Japan). For each milling operation, 1000.00 g of sample was used, and the milling time was set to 5 min. Rice samples with different DOM were prepared by adjusting the whiteness control dial. The resulting DOM were 2.16 ± 0.15%, 4.28 ± 0.10%, 5.87 ± 0.20%, 8.15 ± 0.08%, 9.07 ± 0.06%, 10.10 ± 0.16%, 11.13 ± 0.04%, 11.98 ± 0.16%, 13.19 ± 0.20% and 14.11 ± 0.14%. After milling, the rice samples were passed through an 18-mesh sieve to remove bran powder, packed in sealed bags, and stored at 4 °C until further analysis. Milling degree analysis

The bran degree of Daohuaxiang rice was determined following the Chinese national standard method GB/T 5502–2018. Briefly, 12 g of rice was immersed in water (1 min), stained with 15 mL of Eosin Y-Methylene Blue (2 min), and washed with 80% ethanol, with the wash step repeated three times. The samples were then dried, and bran degree, scanned images, and color-enhanced pictures were obtained using a JMJT-12 rice appearance quality tester (Dong Fu Jiu Heng, Beijing, China).

Grain surface morphology observation

Grain surface morphology across DOM levels was documented using a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) under reflected light. Representative kernels were imaged (15×).

Extraction of volatile compounds

Headspace solid-phase microextraction was employed to isolate volatile compounds from cooked rice. Briefly, 2 g of raw rice, 2.6 mL of distilled water, and 8 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone (8.0784 ppm, internal standard) were placed in a 20 mL headspace vial and steamed in a rice cooker for 40 min. A 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS solid-phase microextraction fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was preconditioned at 250 °C for 10 min and then exposed to the sample headspace at 60 °C for 30 min. The fiber was subsequently inserted into the GC injection port at 250 °C and thermally desorbed for 5 min in splitless mode.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

Volatile compounds were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B GC coupled with an Agilent 5977B MSD (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and a fused silica capillary column (HP-5, 60 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm). High-purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature program was: 40 °C (1 min), ramped to 100 °C at 3 °C/min (2 min hold), to 160 °C at 3 °C/min (2 min hold), to 200 °C at 3 °C/min (3 min hold), and to 250 °C at 5 °C/min (3 min hold). Mass spectra were acquired in EI mode (70 eV), scanning m/z 35–450, with transfer line, quadrupole, and ion source temperatures at 280 °C, 150 °C, and 230 °C, respectively. Retention indices (RIs) were determined using C5–C30 n-alkanes. The compounds were identified by matching spectra with the NIST 11.L library (score ≥ 80%), confirmed by RI comparison with authentic standards or literature (Table S1). Relative quantification was performed with an internal standard (IS) calibration method. The method was validated (linearity, LOD/LOQ, recovery, and precision), and results complied with the predefined criteria.

Sensory evaluation

Cooked rice was prepared following the Chinese national standard GB/T 15682-2008. For each sample, 10 g of rice was rinsed twice in 300 mL ultrapure water within 8 min, soaked at a rice-to-water ratio of 1:1.6 (w/v) at 25 °C for 30 min, and then steamed for 40 min, followed by 20 min equilibration. After cooling to approximately 25 °C, the cooked rice samples were transferred into covered dishes for odor sensory evaluation.

The odor sensory evaluation was conducted by 12 trained panelists with normal olfactory ability and experience in rice sensory evaluation. Panelists refrained from smoking, eating, or using scented products before evaluation. The tests were carried out in a clean and odor-free sensory room. Samples were coded with random three-digit numbers and presented in a randomized order, with a 3 min interval between samples to minimize sensory fatigue. The additional sample with a bran degree of 6.0% was prepared and evaluated under the same conditions.

As this study focused on aroma quality, only the odor attribute specified in GB/T 15682–2008 was evaluated. The odor score was assessed using a 20-point scale based on typical cooked-rice aroma, aroma intensity, and the presence or absence of off-odors. To make the scoring process more operational and reproducible, the detailed scoring criteria were organized as Table S2 in the Supplementary Material based on GB/T 15682–2008. The sensory score of each sample was expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of the 12 panelists. The sensory evaluation procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan University of Technology (Approval No. HAUTETHI-2023-07030154).

Gas chromatography-olfactometry (GC-O) analysis

GC-O analysis was performed using the same GC–MS system described in Section 2.6, equipped with an olfactory detector port (ODP 3, Gerstel Inc., Demirheim, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) to identify odor-active compounds. Volatile compounds were extracted under the conditions described in Section 2.5. The capillary column output was directed in a 1:1 proportion to both the mass spectrometer and the sniffing outlet. GC-O analysis was performed using a modified detection-frequency method. The GC-O panel consisted of eight trained assessors (five females and three males, aged 23–35 years), all of whom had a background in food science and previous experience in cereal or rice aroma evaluation. Before GC-O analysis, the assessors were familiarized with representative reference odorants associated with cooked rice aroma, such as 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, hexanal, nonanal, decanal, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-pentylfuran, 2-ethylfuran, pentanal, and other representative odorants. After training, all assessors were able to correctly recognize these reference odorants and provide consistent odor descriptors and retention-time records in preliminary GC-O runs. During GC-O analysis, each assessor independently recorded the odor descriptor and corresponding retention time. An odor-active compound was considered valid only when it was detected by at least six of the eight assessors at a consistent retention time with a comparable odor description. The sensory evaluation procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan University of Technology (Approval No. HAUTETHI-2023-07030154).

Calculation of odor activity values (OAVs)

For each compound, the OAV was calculated as its concentration divided by the corresponding odor threshold in water (due to the water content of cooked rice exceeding 50%) (Chen et al., 2023; Lai et al., 2026). Odor thresholds in water were determined by referencing existing literature (Table S1) (Yin, Shi, et al., 2022).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate. The significance of differences among sample means was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a 95% confidence level using IBM SPSS Statistics 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For differential volatile screening, one-way ANOVA was performed for each of the 50 volatile compounds across the 11 DOM levels using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method using the p.adjust function in the R stats package. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were carried out using the Metabo (version 5.0). Spearman's rank correlation was employed for the correlation analyses because of the small sample size (n = 11 across the DOM gradient), potential non-normality and outliers, and predominantly monotonic rather than strictly linear relationships. The Spearman correlation analysis was performed using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Bran degree characterization

The 11 samples were arranged along a residual bran degree gradient from BD96.75 to BD0.10 and, according to GB/T 1354-2018, were classified as brown, undermilled (>7% bran degree), medium-milled (2–7% bran degree), and well-milled (<2% bran degree) (Table 1). Fig. 1 illustrated the gradual removal of the bran layer from rice grains during milling: BD96.75 retained an almost intact bran layer; BD35.85 exhibited ruptures with localized endosperm exposure; BD20.75, BD13.10, and BD7.05 showed patchy residual bran with decreasing coverage and increasing endosperm exposure; BD3.40, BD2.45, BD2.10, BD1.95, and BD0.45 displayed only scattered remnants; and BD0.10 showed virtually no bran residues. Surface morphology observations showed that with increasing DOM, bran layer coverage decreased progressively while endosperm exposure increased accordingly. During this process, the content of lipids, proteins, ash, and dietary fiber in rice showed a gradual decline (Table S3).

Volatile compounds and variation trends with bran degree

In the 11 cooked Daohuaxiang rice samples with different DOM, a total of 50 volatile compounds belonging to 9 chemical categories were detected, including 4 alcohols, 10 aldehydes, 10 alkanes, 7 heterocyclic compounds, 8 aromatic hydrocarbons, 4 ketones, 3 terpenes, 1 sulfide, and 3 others (Table 2). With the decrease of bran degree, the number of volatile compounds decreased from 43 to 22, and the total content of volatile compounds showed an increasing then decreasing trend, reaching its maximum value at BD35.85 (Fig. 2A). Although the bran degree decreased by 60.90 percentage points from BD96.75 to BD35.85 and the number of volatile compounds was reduced by two (Table 2), the total volatile content increased by 6.57% (120.45 μg/kg) (Fig. 2A). This increase might be attributed to the increase in the content of 15 volatile compounds when the bran degree decreased from 96.75% (BD96.75) to 35.85% (BD35.85), including 1-octen-3-ol, benzaldehyde, hexanal, pentanal, 2,2,4,6,6-pentamethylheptane, tetradecane, 2-butylfuran, 2-ethylfuran, 2-pentylfuran, 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, 2-nonanone, geranylacetone, d-limonene, dimethyl sulfide, and pyrrolo[3,2-d]pyrimidin-2,4(1H,3H)-dione (Table 2). Among them, the largest increase in content was 2,2,4,6,6-pentamethylheptane, followed by 2-butylfuran. This might be due to the breakage of the bran layer leading to contact between its components and sufficient exposure to oxygen, as well as the milling process promoting the activation of endogenous enzymes on the rice grain surface, thereby accelerating the rate of biochemical reactions on the rice grain surface, including the degradation and oxidation of fatty acids, the decomposition of proteins, and the Maillard reaction (Tong et al., 2019). Aldehydes were the predominant volatile compounds in all samples, except for BD35.85 (Fig. 2B). In BD35.85, the concentration of 2-pentylfuran rose sharply, by 47.43% relative to BD96.75, elevating the heterocyclic-compound class above aldehydes to become the most abundant volatile category. Additionally, in BD96.75, BD35.85, BD20.75, BD13.10, BD1.95, BD0.45, and BD0.10, heterocyclic compounds had the second-highest content, whereas in medium-milled rice and BD7.05, aromatic hydrocarbons ranked second and heterocyclic compounds dropped to third (Fig. 2B).

Comprehensive characterization of milling-induced volatile profiles

Volatile fingerprint establishment

A volatile fingerprint based on the relative contents of volatile compounds was established to better understand the effect of DOM on cooked Daohuaxiang rice. Significance analysis of microarrays confirmed that all 50 compounds differed significantly across the 11 DOM levels (Fig. S1). A heatmap coupled with hierarchical clustering was then generated to visualize similarities and differences in volatile compounds among samples and to establish the volatile fingerprint across DOM levels (Fig. 3A). Cluster analysis revealed significant differences in the volatile compounds of cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM, which could be classified into four categories: brown rice, undermilled rice, medium-milled rice, and well-milled rice, indicating distinct volatile profiles for each category (Fig. 3A). BD7.05, classified as undermilled rice, was grouped with medium-milled rice, presumably because its bran degree was closer to that of medium-milled rice (2–7%), and therefore its volatile profile was also more similar. It is evident that the majority of volatile compounds were present in the lowest amount or undetected in milled rice (medium-milled rice and well-milled rice), while most volatile compounds were found in the highest amount in brown rice and undermilled rice (Fig. 3A). Some volatile compounds were not detected in brown rice, including (E)-2-heptenal, 2,2,4,4-tetramethyloctane, hexadecane, 3-methyltridecane, 2-propylfuran, o-xylene, and o-cymene (Table 2), indicating that they might have been produced during and/or after milling.

A) Volatile fingerprints of cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM. B) The PCA score plot and C) the PCA loading plot were constructed using the content of volatile compounds in the cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM. The variable codes of volatile compounds refer to Table 2.

PCA-based characterization of volatile profiles

Unsupervised PCA was performed on volatile compound profiles to explore the overall structure of the dataset. Fig. 3B presented the score plot of the first two principal components, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 44.8% and 20.6% of the total variance, respectively. Cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM showed a clear separation, indicating that the milling degree significantly impacts the types and contents of volatile compounds in cooked rice. This result was consistent with the results of HCA in Section 3.3.1. PC1 explained the variation between brown rice, undermilled rice and medium-milled rice, well-milled rice. 2-methylfuran, styrene, 1-hexanol, ethylbenzene, tridecane, 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, and p-xylene had significant contributions to PC1 (Fig. 3C). Thus, these volatile compounds help to differentiate between brown rice, undermilled rice, and medium-milled rice, well-milled rice, and their content was higher in brown rice and/or undermilled rice. The PCA loading plot further showed that most volatile compounds were associated with brown and under-milled rice, whereas only hexadecane, 2-butanone, 2,2,4,4-tetramethyloctane, o-xylene, and 3-methylbutanal were more closely related to medium-milled rice (Fig. 3C).

Identification of differential volatiles

Supervised PLS-DA was performed to identify the volatile compounds driving the separation among cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM (Fig. 4A). Five-fold cross-validation showed high fit (accuracy = 0.87778, R2 = 0.99884, and Q2 = 0.99703; Fig. S2 A), and 1000-permutation tests confirmed significance (Fig. S2 B). Variable importance in projection (VIP) values were then used to rank the relative contribution and explanatory power of individual volatile compounds to overall differences in sample volatile profiles (Zhao, Xi, Xu, Jin, et al., 2022). Variables with VIP values greater than 1 indicate variables that were determined to be important in the projection scores (Fig. 4B) (Lai et al., 2025; Zhao, Xi, Xu, Yin, et al., 2022). To further ensure the statistical significance of the selected variables, one-way ANOVA was performed for all 50 volatile compounds to evaluate differences among the 11 DOM levels, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing. The results showed that all 50 volatile compounds differed significantly among DOM levels, with FDR-adjusted q values <0.05 (Table S4). Therefore, differential volatiles were defined using the combined criteria of VIP > 1.0 and FDR-adjusted q < 0.05. Based on these criteria, 24 volatile compounds were finally selected as differential volatiles, including 1 alcohol (1-hexanol), 4 aldehydes (heptanal, nonanal, octanal, and pentanal), 3 alkanes (decane, tridecane, and vinyl acetate), 5 heterocyclic compounds (2-butylfuran, 2-ethylfuran, 2-methylfuran, 2-pentylfuran, and 3-methylfuran), 5 aromatic hydrocarbons (m-xylene, 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, ethylbenzene, p-xylene, and styrene), 3 ketones (2-heptanone, 2-nonanone, and 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one), 2 terpenes (geranylacetone and d-limonene), and 1 other (ethenyl hexanoate). These volatile compounds were defined as differential volatiles. For completeness, unsupervised K-means clustering of differential volatiles across milling degrees corroborated the reported trends (Fig. S3 and Table S1).

Identification of key odor-active compounds

The relative content of volatile compounds alone does not provide a comprehensive measure of their contribution to the overall aroma of rice (Lam & Proctor, 2003). Hence, the odor-active compounds present in the samples were determined using GC-O, revealing a total of 14 odor-active compounds (Table S1). When the content of volatile compounds in the sample exceeded their odor threshold (i.e., OAV > 1.0), they have a significant impact on the overall odor of the sample (Chen et al., 2023). Subsequently, the OAVs of the 14 odor-active compounds were calculated. Among them, 12 volatile compounds were classified as key odor-active compounds, as their OAVs exceeded 1.0 in at least one sample (Fig. 5A).

C) DOI, H′, and J′ across cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM (mean ± SE; different letters indicate significant differences). D) Correlation analysis among bran degree, DOI, H′, J′, KOCTop3, KOCTop5, and KOCTop5-KOCTop3. E) Odor sensory evaluation results of cooked medium- and well-milled Daohuaxiang rice. The odor sensory score in Fig. 5E was evaluated using the 20-point odor scale specified in GB/T 15682-2008, and the detailed scoring criteria are described in Table S2. KOC: key odor-active compounds contribution; DOI: dominant odorant index; H′: Shannon diversity index; J′: Pielou's evenness index.

The 12 key odor-active compounds identified in this study were mainly associated with four sources or formation pathways: 1) Lipid oxidation-related pathway: This pathway involved 1-octen-3-ol, decanal, heptanal, hexanal, nonanal, octanal, pentanal, and 2-pentylfuran. These compounds were mainly derived from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, hydroperoxide cleavage, and secondary oxidation reactions, with linoleic acid and oleic acid considered important upstream precursors (Dias et al., 2021; Lai et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021); 2) Amino compound-mediated transformation of lipid oxidation products: This pathway involved 2-ethylfuran and 2-butylfuran, both of which belong to lipid-derived 2-alkylfurans. Their direct precursors were generally considered to be lipid oxidation-derived α,β-unsaturated aldehydes. Specifically, 2-hexenal and 2-octenal could be further converted into 2-ethylfuran and 2-butylfuran, respectively. Nitrogen-containing constituents, such as amino acids, peptides, and proteins, might also have promoted the formation of these compounds (Adams et al., 2011); 3) Amino acid metabolism–Maillard-type reaction-related pathway: This pathway corresponded to 2-AP, which was mainly associated with endogenous formation during the development of aromatic rice grains. Maillard-type reactions during cooking might also have contributed to its formation to some extent (Wakte et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2017); 4) Thermal reaction pathway related to aromatic amino acids: This pathway corresponded to ethylbenzene, which might have been associated with thermal reactions involving aromatic amino acids (Baltes & Mevissen, 1988, Yang et al., 2024). The results of correlation analysis indicated that lipid and protein content in raw rice were significantly and positively associated with the concentrations of key odor-active compounds (Fig. S4). These bran degree dependent changes in key odor-active compounds were plausibly mediated by precursor availability and microstructural exposure, which modulated lipid oxidation and nitrogen-involving pathways (Lai, Liu, Huang, Fei, Yin, et al., 2025; Wood et al., 2012; Yang, Lee, et al., 2008).

Decanal (flowery, green, citrus odor) and ethylbenzene (sweet, aromatic, pungent oil-like odor) had OAV values greater than 1 only in BD96.75. Thus, they likely contributed stronger floral, citrus, and pungent characteristics to cooked brown rice compared with cooked milled rice (Dias et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). The OAV of pentanal and 2-ethylfuran was only greater than 1.0 in cooked brown rice and cooked undermilled rice. Thus, the pentanal with a nutty and sweet odor could bring a more pleasant odor to cooked brown rice and cooked undermilled rice, while 2-ethylfuran with a beany odor could bring them an unpleasant odor (Daygon et al., 2016; Mathure et al., 2014). 2-butylfuran was mainly present in cooked brown rice and cooked undermilled rice, and was considered to be one of the main contributors to the nutty and roasted aroma generated during the cooking of certain types of rice (Concepcion et al., 2018). Moreover, seven other key odor-active compounds had OAV values greater than 1.0 in all samples, and their content was generally higher in cooked brown and undermilled rice than in cooked medium- and well-milled rice. Among them, 1-octen-3-ol and octanal were believed to be associated with off-odors in rice (Gao et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2020; Mathure et al., 2014; Yang, Shewfelt, et al., 2008). Hexanal and 2-pentylfuran contribute off-odors at high concentrations but impart pleasant notes at lower content (Hu et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2017). Heptanal has fresh, fruity-like, and floral odors; nonanal has citrus, green, fresh, floral, and fatty odors; and 2-AP has popcorn and sweet odors. These three compounds all contribute pleasant aromas to rice (Ch et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2020). Overall, as DOM increased, both the number and levels of key odor-active compounds declined, which inevitably led to changes in the aroma quality of cooked rice.

Coupling analysis of aroma structure and sensory evaluation

Aroma structure characterization based on quantitative analysis of key odor-active compounds

To characterize the evolution of the aroma structure of cooked Daohuaxiang rice with different DOM (BD96.75-BD0.10), the KOCTop-3, KOCTop-5, DOI, H′, and J′ were calculated based on the OAVs of key odor-active compounds (Fig. 5 B and C). These metrics were employed to quantify odorant dominance as well as the diversity and evenness of key odor-active compounds. The KOCTop-3, KOCTop-5, KOCTop-5-KOCTop-3, and DOI of brown rice and undermilled rice (BD96.75-BD7.05) were significantly lower than those of medium- and well-milled rice (BD3.40-BD0.10) except BD1.95 (Fig. 5B). As bran degree decreased from 96.75% (BD96.75) to 0.10% (BD0.10), the dominant key odor-active compounds shifted sequentially from nonanal (BD96.75) to 2-pentylfuran (BD35.85), and finally to 2-AP (BD20.75-BD2.45 and BD1.95-BD0.10), with hexanal briefly dominant at BD2.10 (Fig. 5A). The enhanced dominance of 2-AP should not be simply interpreted as an increase in its absolute content, but rather as a result of the redistribution of OAV contributions among key odor-active compounds. As DOM increased, the lipid-rich bran layer was progressively removed, leading to a decrease in lipid content in rice kernels. This reduction might have decreased the supply of lipid oxidation substrates and was therefore unfavorable for the formation and release of lipid-derived odor-active compounds, such as nonanal, hexanal, heptanal, octanal, pentanal, 1-octen-3-ol, and 2-pentylfuran. In contrast, 2-AP was mainly formed during the grain-filling stage and was distributed in both the bran layer and endosperm of rice kernels (Bounphanousay et al., 2008; Wei et al., 2017). In addition, Maillard-type reactions occurring during rice cooking might also have contributed to the formation of 2-AP through amino acid–aldehyde intermediates (Wei et al., 2017). Therefore, compared with lipid-derived key odor-active compounds, 2-AP was less directly dependent on bran lipids, and its relative OAV contribution became more prominent as the contribution of lipid-derived odorants decreased. Correlation analysis showed that bran degree was significantly positively correlated with H′ and J′, but significantly negatively correlated with KOCTop-3, KOCTop-5, KOCTop-5-KOCTop-3, and DOI (Fig. 5D). In summary, decreasing bran degree progressively shifted the aroma structure of cooked Daohuaxiang rice from a complex type to a simpler one dominated by 2-AP. Within the bran degree range of medium- and well-milled rice (BD3.40-BD0.10), the overall trend still followed a pattern of increasing dominance, decreasing diversity and evenness. However, at BD1.95, a local trough in KOCTop-3 and DOI was accompanied by a peak in H′ and J′ (Fig. 5 B and C). This phenomenon was regarded as a stage-specific reorganization instead of a reversal of the overall trend in the relative contributions of key odor-active compounds.

Sensory evaluation

The odor sensory evaluation results showed that BD3.40 had the highest sensory score (Fig. 5E). All cooked rice samples emitted the characteristic aroma of rice, with BD3.40 showing a strong aroma intensity, while the other six samples exhibited a mild rice aroma. In order to understand whether the odor of cooked medium-milled rice with a bran degree higher than 3.40% was superior to that of BD3.40, this study prepared additional samples with a bran degree of 6.0% and conducted an odor sensory evaluation of the cooked rice. The results showed that its odor sensory evaluation score was lower than BD3.40 (Fig. 5E). Thus, keeping the bran degree at 3.40% enables the cooked Daohuaxiang rice to attain optimal aroma quality under the present experimental conditions.

Considering the aroma structure, although BD3.40 and BD2.10 showed no significant differences in DOI, H′, or J′ (p ≥ 0.05, Fig.5C), BD3.40 was dominated by 2-AP, a typical aroma compound, whereas BD2.10 was dominated by hexanal (Fig.5A). Specifically, BD3.40 had a much higher OAV of 2-AP than BD2.10 (99.33 vs. 54.98), whereas BD2.10 showed a higher OAV of hexanal (84.68 vs. 70.51). Therefore, BD3.40 might have presented a more typical 2-AP-centered popcorn-like and sweet aroma, while BD2.10 might have been more influenced by green and aldehydic notes from hexanal. This compound-level difference might have contributed to the divergence in sensory evaluation scores. Meanwhile, BD0.10 (commercial DOM sample) and BD3.40 showed no significant difference in DOI (Fig. 5C), and both were dominated by 2-AP with identical Top-3 compositions of key odor-active compound (Fig.5A). However, compared with BD3.40, BD0.10 exhibited a higher KOCTop-3 and significantly lower (p < 0.05) H′ and J′ values (Fig. 5C). These features indicated that BD0.10 was more inclined toward a few key odor-active compounds in its aroma profile, which undermined diversity and evenness and might have resulted in weaker perceived complexity than BD3.40. This was probably the main factor underlying its lower sensory score compared with BD3.40. These findings indicate that, in addition to dominance by typical aroma compounds, a moderate level of complexity (H′ and J′) was also essential for achieving superior sensory quality. Given that BD3.40 had the highest DOI with 2-AP as the dominant key odor-active compound and that H′ and J′ had not yet declined to the lowest levels among all samples, its aroma accentuated the popcorn-like note of 2-AP while retaining moderate complexity. This combination might be the decisive factor for BD3.40 achieving the highest sensory aroma score. Thus, optimizing rice aroma was not achieved by maximizing a single metric, but rather by balancing odorant dominance with aroma complexity.

Compound-level drivers of aroma structural differences between the optimal DOM sample and commercial DOM sample

An OPLS-DA model was constructed to identify candidate key odor-active compounds associated with the aroma-structure differences between the sample with the highest sensory score (BD3.40; Section 3.5.2) and the commercial DOM sample (BD0.10). The model showed strong predictive performance, and the VIP values were obtained (Fig. 6 A and B). The permutation test based on 1000 permutations confirmed the validity of the OPLS-DA model, with R2Y = 0.979 and Q2 = 0.960 (both p < 0.001; Fig. S5). The potential differential compounds identified by OPLS-DA were subsequently validated using a combination of two-sample t-tests corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for false discovery rate (FDR) and fold-change (FC) evaluation. Compounds with VIP > 1.0, FC > 1.2 or < 0.8, and FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 were regarded as significantly different. Based on these thresholds, heptanal and octanal were identified as the differential key odor-active compounds distinguishing BD3.40 from BD0.10 (Fig. 6C). Thus, the higher diversity and evenness observed in BD3.40 might have been attributable to its higher content of heptanal and octanal compared with BD0.10. Heptanal was commonly described as having fresh, fruity, and floral notes, whereas octanal was associated with green, fatty, and citrus-like odors (Hu et al., 2020; Lai et al., 2026). Compared with BD0.10, BD3.40 retained higher content of these two aldehydes, which might have contributed additional fresh, fruity, floral, and citrus-like odor to its aroma profile. Because both heptanal and octanal were lipid-derived aldehydes, their lower content in BD0.10 might have been associated with the further reduction in lipid content during extensive milling.

DOM-oriented aroma regulation framework

DOM changed the proximate composition of rice grains, thereby potentially affecting the material basis related to volatile formation during cooking and contributing to the reorganization of key odor-active compound profiles and changes in aroma quality. Based on these findings, a DOM-oriented aroma-quality framework was proposed, summarized as “DOM → proximate composition → key odor-active compound profile → aroma quality.” The framework encompassed three interrelated levels:

Level 1: DOM alters the proximate compositionMilling first altered the physical structure of rice kernels through mechanical abrasion, during which the bran layers were progressively removed (Fig. 1). As DOM increased from 0% to 14.11%, the bran degree decreased from 96.75% to 0.10% (Table 1), indicating a progressive transition from brown rice to well-milled rice. Because lipids, proteins, ash, and dietary fiber were mainly distributed in the bran layer and outer endosperm, the decrease in bran degree was generally accompanied by reductions in these proximate constituents. Specifically, from BD96.75 to BD0.10, lipid content decreased from 2.04% to 0.23%, protein content from 7.00% to 6.25%, ash content from 1.23% to 0.34%, and dietary fiber content from 3.87% to 0.94% (Table S3). These results indicated that increasing DOM not only reduced the bran layer but also decreased bran-associated proximate constituents.

Level 1: DOM alters the proximate composition

Milling first altered the physical structure of rice kernels through mechanical abrasion, during which the bran layers were progressively removed (Fig. 1). As DOM increased from 0% to 14.11%, the bran degree decreased from 96.75% to 0.10% (Table 1), indicating a progressive transition from brown rice to well-milled rice. Because lipids, proteins, ash, and dietary fiber were mainly distributed in the bran layer and outer endosperm, the decrease in bran degree was generally accompanied by reductions in these proximate constituents. Specifically, from BD96.75 to BD0.10, lipid content decreased from 2.04% to 0.23%, protein content from 7.00% to 6.25%, ash content from 1.23% to 0.34%, and dietary fiber content from 3.87% to 0.94% (Table S3). These results indicated that increasing DOM not only reduced the bran layer but also decreased bran-associated proximate constituents.

Level 2: Proximate composition reshaped the key odor-active compound profile.The formation of volatile compounds in cooked rice was closely associated with lipid oxidation, the transformation of lipid oxidation products, and nitrogen-containing reactions (Guan & Zhang, 2022, Lai et al., 2025). Therefore, changes in the retention of proximate constituents, particularly lipids and proteins, could have further contributed to reshaping the profile of key odor-active compounds in cooked rice. The 12 key odor-active compounds identified in this study were mainly associated with four sources or formation pathways: (1) lipid oxidation-related pathways, including 1-octen-3-ol, decanal, heptanal, hexanal, nonanal, octanal, pentanal, and 2-pentylfuran; (2) amino compound-mediated transformation of lipid oxidation products, including 2-ethylfuran and 2-butylfuran; (3) amino acid metabolism- and Maillard-type reaction-related pathways, corresponding to 2-AP; and (4) thermal reactions related to aromatic amino acids, corresponding to ethylbenzene (Section 3.4). Level 3: Restructuring of the key odor-active compound profile was reflected in aroma quality differences.The aroma quality of cooked rice largely depended on the composition of key odor-active compounds, the identity of the dominant key odor-active compounds, and their relative contributions. Therefore, restructuring of the key odor-active compound profile was ultimately reflected in differences in the aroma sensory quality of cooked Daohuaxiang rice. Aroma sensory evaluation showed that BD3.40 received the highest score (Fig. 5E). As shown in Section 3.5, the sensory advantage of BD3.40 was not simply attributable to the dominance of 2-AP, but rather to the balance between the expression of 2-AP-associated characteristic aroma and a moderate level of aroma complexity. In contrast, although BD0.10 was also dominated by 2-AP, it showed lower aroma complexity and might have lacked some fresh, fruity, floral, and citrus-like background notes, resulting in a less layered aroma profile. In BD2.10, the dominant compound shifted from 2-AP to hexanal, which made the characteristic Daohuaxiang aroma less pronounced than that in BD3.40. Therefore, high aroma quality in cooked Daohuaxiang rice was mainly achieved when two conditions were met: (i) typical aroma compounds (e.g., 2-AP) had to serve as the dominant key odor-active compound, and (ii) this dominance had to be balanced with diversity and evenness of the key odor-active compounds.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated the regulatory role of the DOM in the aroma formation of cooked Daohuaxiang rice and proposed a DOM-oriented, three-level framework for aroma regulation. In this framework, changes in proximate composition induced by DOM reshaped the profile of key odor-active compounds, thereby altering the overall aroma quality. As the empirical basis for this regulatory framework, the present study first demonstrated the decisive effect of DOM on the volatile compounds profile. Subsequently, 24 differential volatiles that significantly discriminated samples with different DOM were identified. As DOM increased from brown rice (BD96.75) to BD0.10, the DOI (from 0.26 to 0.39), KOCTop-3 (from 0.64 to 0.81), and KOCTop-5 (from 0.94 to 0.97) increased, whereas KOCTop-5–KOCTop-3 (from 0.30 to 0.16), H′ (from 1.718 to 1.522), and J′ (from 0.883 to 0.782) decreased. These changes indicated that with progressive milling, the aroma of cooked Daohuaxiang rice became increasingly dominated by a few key odor-active compounds, accompanied by a significant reduction in both diversity and evenness of key odor-active compounds, leading to a gradual simplification of the aroma profile. Among all samples, BD3.40 achieved the highest odor sensory evaluation score, which may be mainly attributed to the dominant contribution of 2-AP and the highest DOI value (0.39), together with the moderate diversity (H′ = 1.537) and evenness (J′ = 0.790) of key odor-active compounds, thereby giving the cooked rice a more layered aroma. Although the commercial DOM sample (BD0.10) also exhibited 2-AP dominance and the highest DOI (0.39), its lower content of heptanal and octanal might have resulted in reduced diversity and evenness of key odor-active compounds, producing a more monotonous aroma. Therefore, achieving optimal aroma quality during the milling depends on maintaining the dominance of typical aroma compounds such as 2-AP, while simultaneously maintaining moderate diversity and evenness of key odor-active compounds. For Daohuaxiang rice, processors aiming to highlight its characteristic aroma should avoid excessive milling and maintain a target bran degree of 3.40%.
