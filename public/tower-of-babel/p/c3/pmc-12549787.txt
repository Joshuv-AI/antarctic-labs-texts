A novel EGFR-targeted photosensitizer for the theranostics of skin cancer
Authors: Huijuan Li, Huijun Li, Shuang Qi, Xiaohui Tang, Shiqi Zhao, Xin Bian, Baoqing Tian, Hua Zhang, Yuchun Wei, Dianlong Jia, Xinyue Han, Qing Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a critical therapeutic target implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of skin cancer, holding significant promise for enhancing precision diagnosis and treatment efficacy. In this study, we developed ICG-ZEGFR, a novel EGFR-targeting agent engineered through conjugation of the photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG) to an EGFR-targeted dimeric affibody (ZEGFR) for the theranostics of EGFR-positive skin cancer. In vitro, ICG-ZEGFR-mediated photothermal therapy (PTT) induced significant cell death in EGFR-positive A431 cells, while exhibiting minimal effects on EGFR-negative MLE-12 cells. Compared to free ICG, ICG-ZEGFR enhanced tumor retention, and significantly improved tumor-targeting capability, making it advantageous for identifying EGFR-positive tumor tissues. Furthermore, ICG-ZEGFR also exhibited excellent photothermal conversion performance in vivo, and effectively suppressed the growth of A431 tumors through thermal ablation. Importantly, ICG-ZEGFR demonstrated favorable short-term safety profiles during the in vivo treatment assay. In conclusion, the successfully developed ICG-ZEGFR in this study, as a novel tumor-targeted photosensitizer, innovatively breaks through the limitations of traditional single-modal treatment. This study would open up new perspectives and pathways for integrated clinical diagnosis and treatment of EGFR-positive skin cancer.

Introduction

Skin cancer is a highly prevalent malignancy worldwide, primarily classified into melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSCs) that originate from keratinocytes (Mallardo et al., 2025). According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, NMSC highly accounts for 78.8 % of skin cancer diagnoses globally, ranking fifth among all cancer types, with approximately 1.23 million new cases and 69,000 deaths reported worldwide (Wang et al., 2025). The incidence of NMSC continues to rise at an annual rate of 3–8 % (Almaani et al., 2023). Major etiological factors include chronic ultraviolet exposure, genetic background related to skin pigmentation, and environmental carcinogens such as arsenic and tar (Perez et al., 2022; Sol et al., 2024). Recent advances in elucidating the pathogenesis of skin cancer have revealed a close association with multiple cellular signaling pathways, cell cycle regulators, apoptosis-related proteins, and immune checkpoints (Bailey et al., 2023; Reschke et al., 2025). Among these, the EGFR signaling pathway plays a central role in the development and progression of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) and some basal cell carcinomas (Hasan et al., 2023). EGFR is commonly overexpressed in epithelial-derived tumors, including skin cancers, and its aberrant activation initiates downstream signaling cascades, such as the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, promoting tumor cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and angiogenesis (Tito et al., 2025). EGFR overexpression correlates with increased tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis (He et al., 2024). Monitoring EGFR expression levels aids in the timely diagnosis and therapeutic evaluation of skin cancer (Wu et al., 2020). Thus, EGFR is considered as an important target for skin cancer diagnosis and therapy.

Current therapeutic modalities for skin cancer include surgical excision, cryotherapy, radiotherapy, local chemotherapy, and stealth drug delivery (Li et al., 2024; Sadman et al., 2025; Schmults et al., 2023). For advanced or metastatic skin cancer, substantial progress has been achieved with targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (Janssen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025), though challenges remain, including drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and high treatment costs. Consequently, effective early diagnosis is crucial throughout the therapeutic continuum, and developing novel, safe, and effective EGFR-targeted diagnostic and therapeutic agents is of great scientific significance.

Near-infrared (NIR) laser-based photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) represent non-invasive, innovative cancer treatment strategies, offering the advantages of precise control via external laser sources, high tumor specificity, and minimal damage to surrounding healthy tissues (Newaj et al., 2024). PDT employs photosensitizers that, upon activation by specific wavelengths of laser, transfer energy to molecular oxygen within tissues, generating cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) that irreversibly damage tumor cells, leading to necrosis and apoptosis (Sarı et al., 2023; Wu Klingler et al., 2022). Recently, EGFR-targeted PDT has been investigated for skin cancer, providing new therapeutic avenues for EGFR-positive skin cancers (Bitsch et al., 2023). However, PDT efficacy is largely limited by the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, as oxygen is the main driver of its cytotoxic activity (Sun et al., 2021). In contrast, PTT utilizes photothermal transduction agents (PTAs) that convert NIR laser (780–1100 nm) into heat, ablate tumors with localized hyperthermia (Sun et al., 2022; Yue and Zhao, 2021). PTT efficacy is not constrained by tumor hypoxia, and the acidic, lactate-rich microenvironment of hypoxic tumors further sensitizes cells to heat-induced death, thereby enhancing PTT's antitumor effect (Li et al., 2020; Nikfarjam et al., 2005). Furthermore, PTT can also induce ROS generation, providing synergistic anti-cancer effects (Prasad et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019a). Thus, PTT offers a more competitive therapeutic approach for skin cancer(Sharker et al., 2017).

The therapeutic outcome of PTT is largely dependent on PTAs, which should exhibit high photothermal conversion efficiency, superior photothermal stability, good biocompatibility, and safety (Liang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2016). Indocyanine green (ICG) is a clinically approved organic fluorescent dye with excellent photothermal properties, widely used for intraoperative tumor fluorescence imaging, tumor margin assessment, and sentinel lymph node mapping (Digital et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2021). Therefore, ICG enables both tumor fluorescence imaging and PTT applications. Nonetheless, ICG exhibits rapid plasma clearance (half-life 3–4 min), insufficient tumor targeting, and is readily eliminated, limiting its direct use in the PTT (Hu et al., 2022). Enhanced tumor accumulation of ICG can be achieved through targeted delivery strategies, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy (Liu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019b). Affibody molecules are non-immunoglobulin scaffold proteins characterized by a simple, compact structure, robust tissue penetration, relatively short half-life, and strong tumor-associated antigen specificity and affinity (Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b). Besides, affibody exhibiting stable physicochemical properties, is amenable to modifications, and serves as an ideal tumor diagnosis probe and targeted therapy molecule. Previous studies have shown that the ZEGFR:2377 affibody specifically binds to EGFR and exhibits favorable tumor targeting in vivo, making it a promising diagnostic and therapeutic agent for EGFR-positive skin cancers (Jia et al., 2020).

In this study, the amino acid sequence of ZEGFR:2377 was engineered to include a C-terminal cysteine, enabling construction of a dimeric affibody (ZEGFR) via disulfide bonds for enhanced EGFR-binding affinity. Subsequently, ICG was conjugated to ZEGFR via the N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester, yielding a novel photothermal agent, ICG-ZEGFR, for EGFR-positive skin cancer fluorescence imaging and PTT (Scheme 1). Our results demonstrated that ICG-ZEGFR exhibited excellent tumor-targeting in vivo, enabled high-contrast fluorescence tumor imaging. Moreover, ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT specifically killed EGFR-positive A431 skin cancer cells in vitro and thermally ablated tumor tissues in vivo with robust anti-tumor efficacy. Materials and methods

Materials

β-D-thiogalactoside (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), Ni-NTA resin (GenScript, Nanjing, China), β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 6-carboxy fluorescein succinimidyl ester (Ruixi Biological Technology, Xi'an, China), Dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), Superdex G75 Increase 10/30 column (GE HealthCare, IL, USA), ICG (Ruixi Biological Technology, Xi'an, China), ICG-NHS ester (Ruixi Biological Technology, Xi'an, China), Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (Gibco, CA, USA), Fetal bovine serum (Gibco, CA, USA), CCK-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan), Calcein-AM (MedChemexpress, NJ, USA), Propidium iodide (MedChemexpress, NJ, USA), Reactive oxygen species assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Rabbit anti-human EGFR antibody Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam, MA, USA), Rabbit anti-human Ki67 antibody (Abcam, MA, USA), Donkey anti-rabbit IgG DyLight 550 (Abcam, MA, USA), Tunel kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). All remaining reagents employed in this research were of analytical grade.

Protein expression and purification

The dimeric ZEGFR affibody was prepared as previously described (Fan et al., 2019). Briefly, the ZEGFR:2377 gene with an added C-terminal cysteine was synthesized and cloned into the pQE30 plasmid to construct expression plasmid of pQE30-ZEGFR. Then the expression plasmid was transformed into E. coli M15, and recombinant protein expression was induced at 37 °C with 0.05 nM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside for 6 h. Cells were lysed on ice via ultrasonication, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, following ZEGFR purification process using high-affinity Ni-NTA resin. The successful preparation and oligomeric status of ZEGFR were verified by SDS-PAGE and exclusion chromatography (SEC). For SEC analysis, ZEGFR was loaded onto a prepacked gel filtration column (Superdex™ 75 Increase 10/300GL) under non-reducing native condition or reduced with β-mercaptoethanol (2-ME), and the elution profiles at 280 nm were recorded. The secondary structure characterization was conducted by circular dichroism spectroscopy.

Cell culture

A431 human epidermoid carcinoma cell and MLE-12 murine lung epithelial cell were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, 2 mM l-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO₂.

Protein labeling

For in vitro cellular binding assay, ZEGFR was labeled with 6-carboxy fluorescein (6-FAM). Briefly, the 6-carboxy fluorescein succinimidyl ester dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to ZEGFR (pH 8.0) at a 3:1 (6-FAM: protein) molar ratio and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The mixture was then dialyzed overnight against PBS at 4 °C. ICG-ZEGFR was synthesized by mixing ICG-N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (ICG-NHS) dissolved in DMSO and ZEGFR (1 mg/mL, pH 9.0 in PBS with 30 % DMSO) at a molar ratio of 3:1 (ICG-NHS: ZEGFR), reacted for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. The product was dialyzed overnight at 4 °C against PBS containing 0.4 M arginine to remove unreacted ICG-NHS, yielding ICG-ZEGFR. To validate the formation of ICG-ZEGFR, the protein was undergone SDS-PAGE, followed by gel scanning with a fluorescence imaging system (Alliance 4.7, UVITEC, Cambridge, UK). In addition, the absorption spectra of ZEGFR, ICG, and ICG-ZEGFR analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (TU-1950, PERSEE, Beijing, China) was also performed to confirm the conjugation of ICG.

In vitro protein–cell binding assay

Flow cytometry was used to assess EGFR expression in A431 and MLE-12 cells. Cells (4 × 105/tube) were stained with EGFR antibody in DMEM, incubated in the dark for 1 h, and analyzed by flow cytometry. To evaluate the protein–cell binding, equal numbers of A431 and MLE-12 cells were resuspended in DMEM containing 6-FAM-ZEGFR (200 nM), incubated for 1 h in the dark, washed three times with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. For receptor-blocking assay, cells were preincubated with free ZEGFR (4 μM) for 0.5 h, followed by incubation with 6-FAM-ZEGFR (200 nM) for 1 h in the dark, then washed and analyzed by flow cytometry. In addition, fluorescence microscopy was further used to verify the cellular binding. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates overnight, incubated with 6-FAM-ZEGFR (200 nM) for 1 h (with or without receptor-blocking), washed, and observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

In vitro photothermal therapy

To assess the cellular phototoxicity of ZEGFR, A431 or MLE-12 cells (2 × 104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates, exposed to various concentrations of ZEGFR for 24 h, and cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8). Cells treated with PBS were served as 100 % viability controls. To validate the photothermal performance of ICG-ZEGFR in vitro, the ICG-ZEGFR solution (10 μM, 100 μL) was dispensed to a 96-well plate and irradiated with different output power densities of 808 nm NIR laser (MW-GX-808/5000mw, Changchun Feimiao Technology Co., Ltd., Changchun, China) for 90 s or the selected power (1.5 W/cm2) of 808 nm NIR laser for different durations. During the irradiation process, temperature elevations were monitored, and thermal infrared images were obtained using a thermal imaging camera (VarioCAM HD head 680, Infotec GmbH, HH, Germany). To evaluate the phototoxicity of ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT, cells incubated with ICG-ZEGFR for 1 h were washed, then irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min). After 24 h, cell viability was determined using CCK-8. Besides, live/dead cell staining assay was conducted to further assess the phototoxicity. A431 cells, after appropriate treatments (PBS, ICG-ZEGFR, NIR, or ICG-ZEGFR/NIR), were stained with calcein-AM/PI dual-staining working solution for 30 min, and imaged by inverted fluorescence microscopy. Finally, intracellular ROS generation by ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT was also assessed using the reactive oxygen species assay kit. Briefly, A431 cells incubated with ICG-ZEGFR for 1 h and DCFH-DA (20 μM) for 20 min were washed with PBS, then irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. ROS fluorescence was visualized by the fluorescence microscopy.

Tumor optical imaging

To evaluate in vivo tumor targeting and imaging ability of ICG-ZEGFR, female BALB/c nude mice (n = 3, 14–16 g) were subcutaneously inoculated with 2 × 106 A431 cells to establish a xenograft skin cancer model. Tumor length and width were measured daily, and tumor volume was calculated as: V = length × width2/2. When the tumor volumes reached about 200 mm3, ICG-ZEGFR (10 nmol) or equimolar free ICG was injected via the tail vein. Subsequently, at various time points post-injection, dynamic in vivo fluorescence imaging of the tumor-bearing mice was performed using an optical imaging system (IVIS Spectrum CT, PerkinElmer, MA, USA, excitation: 745 nm, emission: 840 nm).

In vivo photothermal performance

The photothermal conversion performance of ICG-ZEGFR in vivo was analyzed by infrared thermal imaging experiments. BALB/c nude mice subcutaneously bearing A431 tumor xenografts (∼200 mm3) were intravenously injected with ICG-ZEGFR (10 nmol) or equimolar free ICG, and after 4 h, tumors were irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2, 5 min). The infrared thermal imaging was captured, and tumor temperature was recorded every minute using the thermal imaging camera.

In vivo PTT

BALB/c nude mice subcutaneously bearing A431 tumor xenografts (∼150 mm3) were randomly divided into PBS, ICG-ZEGFR, NIR, ICG/NIR and ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment groups (n = 5 per group). Mice intravenously received ICG-ZEGFR or free ICG (10 nmol), and after 4 h, tumors were irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2, 10 min). The above treatment process was performed twice, and tumor volume and body weight were recorded daily. After the treatment, tumors were excised, weighed, and stained for Ki67, TUNEL, and HE to evaluate proliferation, apoptosis, and histology. Blood samples were analyzed for liver, renal, and cardiac function indexes: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), urea (UREA), uric acid (UA), and creatine kinase (CK). Major organs were also histologically examined by HE staining for the biosafety evaluation of ICG-ZEGFR.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 13.0). All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. The significance levels are indicated as follows: (*) for P < 0.05, (**) for P < 0.01, (***) for P < 0.001, and (ns) for not significant.

For ICG-ZEGFR construction, an NHS-ester crosslinking reaction was used to conjugate ICG to ZEGFR. SDS-PAGE analysis under reducing and non-reducing conditions showed that the molecular weight of protein increased slightly after conjugation with ICG (Fig. 1C, top, lane 2 and 4), which is likely mainly attributable to ICG's molecular mass. In addition, conjugation with ICG did not alter the aggregation state of ZEGFR. Gel fluorescence imaging and IVIS scanning (Fig. 1C, middle and bottom, lane 2 and 4) showed clear fluorescence signals of ICG-ZEGFR. As shown in Fig. 1D, results of SEC analysis indicated that the elution volume of ZEGFR under non-reducing native condition was 11.3 mL. UV–Vis spectra (Fig. 1E) indicated a specific absorption peak at ∼780 nm for ICG-ZEGFR, consistent with ICG's characteristic absorption, which was absent for ZEGFR. Collectively, these results confirmed the successful construction of ICG-ZEGFR.

Specific binding of ZEGFR to EGFR-positive skin cancer cells

Firstly, flow cytometry was used to assess the expression of EGFR in MLE-12 and A431 cells, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2A, flow cytometry revealed that A431 cells strongly expressed EGFR, while MLE-12 cells did not. Further cellular binding assay showed that ZEGFR bound to A431 cells but not to MLE-12 cells (Fig. 2B), which was consistent with the EGFR expression status. Besides, fluorescence imaging revealed a strong fluorescence signal when 6-FAM–ZEGFR bound to A431 cells (Fig. 2C). Pre-incubation with free ZEGFR competitively blocked EGFR, resulting in markedly decreased positive binding rates (Fig. 2B) and fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2C). ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT specifically killed EGFR-positive skin cancer cells in vitro

The in vitro cytotoxicity of ZEGFR was first evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. S1, ZEGFR did not exhibit significant cytotoxicity in either EGFR-positive A431 or EGFR-negative MLE-12 cells. Then the photothermal performance of ICG-ZEGFR in vitro was further validated. As shown in Fig. S2A, the temperature increases of ICG and ICG-ZEGFR rose with increasing irradiation power. After NIR laser irradiation at 1.5 W/cm2 for 90 s, the temperatures of ICG and ICG-ZEGFR increased by 15.13 ± 0.21 °C and 15.93 ± 0.91 °C, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. S2B). However, the PBS showed no obvious temperature increase, indicating that ICG-ZEGFR exhibits good photothermal performance in vitro. Upon incubation with ICG-ZEGFR and subsequent 808 nm NIR laser irradiation (1 W/cm2, 5 min), EGFR-positive A431 cell exhibited dose-dependent cell death, while the viability of MLE-12 cell was unaffected (Fig. 3A). ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment reduced the viability of A431 cell to just 23 %, and separate ICG-ZEGFR or NIR treatments were non-toxic. The results of calcein-AM/PI staining assay (Fig. 3B) revealed extensive PI-positive (red, dead) A431 cells following ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT in vitro. In contrast, no significant PI fluorescence signal was detected in other treatment groups, and only calcein-AM-positive (green, live) cells were observed. These results showed that ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT specifically killed EGFR-positive A431 skin cancer cells in vitro. In addition to photothermal effects, ICG could also induce the generation of ROS under NIR laser irradiation, synergistically enhancing anti-tumor activity. As shown in Fig. 3C and S3, significantly elevated intracellular ROS (green fluorescence) was detected following ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment, but not with ICG-ZEGFR or NIR laser alone. Ex vivo tissues fluorescence imaging indicated that the tumor fluorescence signal intensity in ICG-ZEGFR group was 3.87 times higher than muscle, significantly greater than free ICG (1.81 times, Fig. 4C and D). ICG-ZEGFR exhibited excellent photothermal performance in vivo

The in vivo photothermal performance of ICG-ZEGFR was evaluated by photothermal imaging in mice subcutaneously bearing A431 tumor xenografts. Mice were injected via the tail vein with equal molar amounts of free ICG or ICG-ZEGFR (10 nmol). Four hours after injection, the tumor sites were irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2, 5 min), and in vivo photothermal imaging was performed using the thermal imaging camera every minute during irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, ICG-ZEGFR produced pronounced photothermal conversion. After 5 min of laser irradiation (1.5 W/cm2), the average tumor temperature in ICG-ZEGFR group rose by 21.4 °C, versus only 8.9 °C increase in free ICG group. ICG-ZEGFR-mediated in vivo PTT significantly inhibited the growth of skin cancer

A subcutaneous A431 tumor xenografts mice model was established, and the tumor site was irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1.5 W/cm2, 10 min) 4 h after intravenous injection of ICG-ZEGFR to perform in vivo PTT (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, the ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment group exhibited strongly suppressed tumor growth, and the tumor volume was only 209.1 ± 68.8 mm3. While tumor volumes did not differ between PBS, ICG-ZEGFR, and NIR laser treatment groups (tumor volumes: 699.1 ± 111.9, 638.3 ± 155.7, and 606.8 ± 42.4 mm3, respectively). In comparison, there was also no significant difference in tumor volume between the ICG/NIR treatment group and PBS control group (Fig. S4A, B and C, 619.7 ± 191.1 mm3 vs 607.2 ± 79.8 mm3). The tumor weights in PBS and ICG/NIR treatment groups were respectively 0.71 ± 0.19 g and 0.68 ± 0.15 g (Fig. S4D and E, ns, not significant), suggesting that ICG-mediated PTT exerted no antitumor effect. As shown in Fig. S5, the macroscopic photographs of tumor region indicated that ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT induced apparent subcutaneous hemorrhage and tissue necrosis in the tumor grafts. In contrast, no obvious signs of acute injury, such as significant redness, swelling, ulceration, or necrosis, were observed in the healthy tissues surrounding the treated area of tumors after ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT. This outcome could be primarily attributed to the high tumor-targeting capability of ICG-ZEGFR, resulting in minimal distribution in the surrounding normal tissues. Consequently, upon NIR laser irradiation, photothermal energy conversion and heat generation are highly concentrated within the tumor region, thereby minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissues. Therefore, the results demonstrated favorable local safety profiles of ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT. After treatment, the tumor tissues were excised and weighed (Fig. 6C). The tumor weight was only 0.17 ± 0.06 g in the ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment group, significantly lower than other groups (PBS: 0.74 ± 0.14 g; ICG-ZEGFR: 0.72 ± 0.11 g; NIR: 0.64 ± 0.18 g, Fig. 6D). As shown in Fig. 6E, the results of TUNEL showed ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT induced extensive apoptosis of tumor cells, resulting in large areas of tumor tissue necrosis. In addition, in the ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment group, the expression of Ki67, a tumor proliferation-specific marker, was also significantly suppressed (Fig. 6E). In contrast, no obvious tumor cell apoptosis or inhibition of Ki67 expression was observed in the other treatment groups. As shown in Fig. 6E, ICG-ZEGFR-mediated PTT effectively induces hemorrhagic necrosis in tumor tissues through high temperatures, thereby disrupting the structural integrity of tumors. As known, the disruption of tumor integrity and potential risk of cell migration is an issue that requires attention in the field of local ablation therapies, including PTT. Concerning the potential risk of tumor cell migration, any local destructive treatment, including surgery, radiotherapy, radiofrequency ablation, or PTT may carry a risk of distant metastasis when complete eradication of tumor cells is not achieved in one session (Deng et al., 2021; Stefanovic et al., 2022; Vilalta et al., 2016). This is not a challenge unique to PTT, but rather a common clinical issue faced by multiple local therapies (Ou-Yang et al., 2023). To mitigate this risk, ICG-ZEGFR is not only a novel photothermal agent but also a fluorescent diagnostic probe. Real-time fluorescence imaging mediated by ICG-ZEGFR during treatment enables monitoring of intratumoral distribution and ensures that laser irradiation covers the entire tumor, aiming for complete ablation. Post-treatment, any residual or abnormal fluorescence signals, which may suggest remaining or migrating cells, can be detected promptly, enabling timely re-intervention or adjustment of the treatment protocol, thereby achieving integrated diagnosis and therapy. Short-term biosafety evaluation of ICG-ZEGFR

Throughout the entire treatment process in vivo, ICG-ZEGFR has been confirmed to have favorable local safety profiles, and the systemic biosafety evaluation is also key for clinical translation. As shown in Fig.7A, all treatment groups maintained comparable weight to the PBS control group during the in vivo PTT experiment. Except for the tumors, EGFR is also widely expressed in organs and tissues such as placenta, lungs, liver, kidneys, intestines, skin, brain, mammary glands, and submandibular glands, with expression levels varying across tissues (Tito et al., 2025). For example, EGFR is highly expressed in metabolic organs like the liver and kidneys, and the in vivo biodistribution results (Fig. 4C) have indicated that ICG-ZEGFR is mainly metabolized via the hepatic and renal pathways. As a result, off-target effects are therefore unavoidable, and evaluating the impact of ICG-ZEGFR on hepatic and renal function is particularly crucial. Therefore, the hepatic and renal function related serum biochemistry was evaluated after the treatment. As shown in Fig. 7B, serum ALT, AST, UREA, CREA and UA levels reflecting hepatic and renal function in the ICG-ZEGFR and ICG-ZEGFR/NIR treatment groups were both within normal limits versus PBS. Moreover, the levels of cardiac function marker of CK also remained within the normal range in all treatment groups. These results indicated that ICG-ZEGFR did not induce acute hepatic, renal, and cardiac toxicity. Conclusions

In this study, we successfully prepared an EGFR-targeted dimeric affibody of ZEGFR, and then conjugated ZEGFR with ICG to construct a novel photothermal agent of ICG-ZEGFR for the diagnosis and photothermal therapy of EGFR-positive skin cancer. Due to the tumor-targeting ability of ZEGFR, ICG-ZEGFR specifically accumulated in EGFR-positive tumor tissues, leveraging the fluorescent properties of ICG for molecular imaging and offering a new approach for early diagnosis and localization of skin cancer. Furthermore, ICG-ZEGFR exhibited outstanding photothermal performance under NIR laser irradiation, efficiently converting laser to heat, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis via thermal ablation, showing potent anti-tumor effects and good biosafety in vivo. In summary, ICG-ZEGFR is an ideal theranostic agent for EGFR-positive skin cancer, demonstrating excellent potential for clinical translation and application.
