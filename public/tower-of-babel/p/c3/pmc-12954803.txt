Genetic and immunologic determinants of BCG disease: from mechanism to prevention
Authors: Feng Chen, Yifan Shen, Qianhui Yan, Daishan Zheng, Xiaoli Chen, Longyu Fang, Mengchen Sun, Yuansi Zhang, Maoping Chu, Enhui Yang, Xiu-Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) infection, a disseminated infection triggered in immunocompromised hosts by the administration of the attenuated BCG vaccine, is characterized by a pathogenic mechanism that involves the synergistic interaction between host immune deficiencies and strain-specific genetic mutations.MethodsThis review synthesizes published multi-omics and proteomics findings from the past decade, along with reported results from in vitro drug susceptibility assays and molecular diagnostics, to explore key pathogenic genes in BCG strains. We compare differences in pathogen characteristics, host immune responses, and clinical manifestations between BCG disease and tuberculosis (TB) disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). Additionally, we summarize evidence on BCG drug susceptibility and the roles of resistance-related genes in drug resistance mechanisms.ResultsThe study revealed the following findings: 1) The occurrence of BCG disease follows the “dual-hit model,” where defects in the host interferon-gamma/interleukin-12 (IFN-γ/IL-12) signaling pathway (in 70% of cases) synergize with genetic mutations in BCG strains (such as pks12 and mma3), leading to immune evasion and disseminated infection; 2) Compared to Mtb, BCG strains exhibit reduced virulence due to the loss of the RD1 region, but strain heterogeneity leads to differences in cell wall lipid metabolism, which induces atypical systemic symptoms in immunocompromised hosts; 3) Some BCG strains display low-level resistance to isoniazid and rifampicin, with resistance linked to mma3 mutations (resulting in increased MIC for isoniazid) and inactivation of pncA (resulting in resistance to pyrazinamide); 4) Genetic modifications of strains (such as BCGΔBCG1419c vaccine), delayed vaccination, and individualized immune monitoring can effectively reduce the infection risk in immunocompromised hosts. Its prevention and control require an integrated approach combining molecular mechanisms and clinical practice improvements.Keywords: BCG disease, immune deficiency, drug resistance, vaccination strategies, genetic mutations

Introduction

The Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine is the most widely used and historically the oldest tuberculosis vaccine, with proven protective efficacy against severe childhood tuberculosis, such as tuberculous meningitis and disseminated tuberculosis [1]. Despite undergoing multiple generations of passage and attenuation (including the deletion of critical pathogenic regions such as RD1), BCG strains typically cause only mild local reactions in immunocompetent hosts. However, in immunocompromised individuals, the attenuated strain may undergo abnormal proliferation, resulting in either localized BCGitis or disseminated BCGosis, depending on the extent of immune dysfunction and bacterial spread. Localized BCGitis often presents as injection-site abscesses or regional lymphadenitis, while disseminated BCGosis can involve multiple organs and poses life-threatening risks, particularly in patients with underlying immunodeficiencies [2]. Additionally, the long-term artificial culture and variations in production processes have resulted in significant genetic diversity among BCG strains, leading to differences in cell wall metabolism, antigen expression, and drug sensitivity. Therefore, the clinical manifestations of BCG disease can be regarded as a prototypical example of an ‘attenuated vaccine breaking through immune surveillance.’ In hosts with defects in the interferon-gamma/interleukin-12 (IFN-γ/IL-12) pathway, even the attenuated BCG strain may bypass immune defenses and cause disseminated infection. This phenomenon not only reveals the dynamic balance mechanism of host-pathogen interaction but also provides a unique perspective for vaccine safety assessment [3]. This review focuses specifically on BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts. We aim to systematically analyze the molecular and genetic mechanisms of pathogenesis, clinical phenotypes, and associated drug resistance challenges. The ultimate objective is to inform individualized treatment strategies and improve risk-based vaccination policies, especially in populations with known or suspected immune deficits [1,4].

Methods

Literature search strategy

This review was conducted using a systematic approach to identify relevant studies published between 2012 and 2024. We performed structured literature searches across the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search strategy employed combinations of the following terms: ‘BCG disease,’ ‘BCG strain variation,’ ‘multi-omics,’ ‘drug resistance,’ ‘IFN-γ/IL-12 pathway,’ and ‘immunodeficiency.’ These terms were chosen to encompass studies addressing the genetic basis, clinical manifestations, immune mechanisms, and drug resistance challenges of BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts.

We screened titles and abstracts to identify studies that reported genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and experimental infection findings. Studies were included if they directly addressed the pathogenesis of BCG disease or the impact of immune deficiencies on BCG’s clinical presentation and therapeutic resistance.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that: Focused on BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts, including primary immunodeficiencies such as SCID, MSMD, and CGD. Described genomic, proteomic, or experimental analyses of BCG strains or host responses. Reported clinical data related to BCG infection, such as case reports, cohort studies, and clinical trials. We excluded studies that: Focused on BCG as a vaccine for TB prevention without discussing BCG disease or its complications. Were not peer-reviewed or did not present original data (e.g. reviews without primary data or studies not related to BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts).

Data extraction and synthesis

For each study, we extracted key data related to: Pathogen-related factors, including BCG strain mutations, drug resistance profiles, and mechanisms of immune evasion. Host-related factors, such as defects in immune pathways (e.g. IFN-γ/IL-12 axis) and their effects on BCG disease development. Clinical features, including manifestations of disseminated vs. localized BCG disease and diagnostic approaches.

We synthesized these findings narratively, comparing and contrasting results across different studies, and identified gaps in the current literature. No meta-analysis or secondary re-analysis of primary datasets was performed.

Pathogenic mechanisms of BCG disease: the dual role of immunodeficiency and key gene mutations

BCG disease is an infection triggered in immunocompromised hosts following the administration of the BCG vaccine. Its pathogenic mechanism involves the interplay between the host’s immune deficiencies and genetic mutations in BCG strains. The immune-deficient state compromises the host’s capacity to recognize and eliminate BCG, while the organism itself evades immune surveillance through genetic mutations and metabolic reprogramming. Together, these factors promote persistent infection and, in some cases, disseminated disease. Recent studies indicate that the development of this disease is driven not by a single factor but by the synergistic imbalance between host and pathogen factors.

Classification and molecular basis of host immunodeficiency

Among primary immunodeficiencies, defects in the IFN-γ/IL-12 axis are the most frequently reported in association with BCG disease, which may manifest as localized/regional BCGitis or disseminated BCGosis. Mutations in genes such as IFNGR1/2, IL12RB1, STAT1, and IRF8 impair macrophage activation and mycobacterial killing, leading to disseminated infection after BCG vaccination. Epidemiological studies indicate that defects in this pathway are responsible for up to 70% of disseminated BCG disease cases [5–7]. Another important primary immunodeficiency is chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) [8]. CGD results from defects in the NADPH oxidase complex—most commonly due to mutations in CYBB, but also in NCF1, NCF2, NCF4, or CYBA—leading to failure of the respiratory burst in neutrophils and macrophages. The absence of adequate reactive oxygen species (ROS) severely compromises the killing of catalase-positive pathogens, including Mycobacterium bovis BCG. Clinically, CGD patients may develop persistent local BCGitis or disseminated BCGosis after vaccination, reflecting the inability to clear even attenuated mycobacteria. Histologically, BCG-induced granulomas in CGD may resemble the intrinsic inflammatory lesions of the disease itself, thereby complicating diagnosis. A recent study confirmed that impaired oxidative burst in CGD significantly increases the risk and severity of BCG disease, underscoring the need for pre-vaccination immune screening in suspected cases [9–12].

BCG disease can also occur in the context of secondary immunosuppression. The most prominent example is HIV infection [13], in which progressive depletion of CD4+ T cells disrupts the cellular immune response essential for controlling mycobacterial infections. Infants born with perinatally acquired HIV are particularly susceptible to disseminated BCG disease following routine vaccination. Similarly, iatrogenic immunosuppression—resulting from prolonged corticosteroid therapy, chemotherapy, or biologics targeting TNF-α—may impair granuloma formation and macrophage activation, thereby predisposing patients to severe BCG-related complications [14–16]. Organ transplant recipients on maintenance immunosuppressive regimens and individuals with severe malnutrition or other chronic debilitating conditions also represent immunocompromised hosts.

In both primary and acquired forms of immunodeficiency, defective cellular immunity—whether due to impaired cytokine signaling, phagocyte function, or T-cell depletion—creates a permissive environment for BCG organisms to replicate unchecked, leading to prolonged local disease or disseminated infection. This highlights the critical importance of immune status assessment before BCG vaccination in populations at risk [10,17,18].

Key gene mutations in BCG strains and disruption of signaling pathways

Genetic mutations in BCG strains play a pivotal role in their pathogenicity (Table 1). Mutations in genes such as pks12, mma3, and sigK markedly alter cell wall structure and function, thereby influencing both drug permeability and recognition by the host immune system [2]. Mutation of pks12 impairs lipid synthesis, leading to cell wall remodeling; mutation of sigK reduces the expression of critical antigens, weakening host immune responses and promoting infection dissemination. Moreover, transcriptional regulation is of particular importance: studies have shown that the cytokine expression profile (e.g. TNF-α and IL-10) in host cells after BCG infection differs markedly from that after M. tuberculosis infection, indicating that BCG strains utilize distinct transcriptional mechanisms to modulate host immunity, which may lead to immune dysregulation and disseminated disease in immunocompromised hosts [4,19].

The dual-hit model: synergistic interaction between host deficiencies and BCG mutations

Recent studies have proposed the “dual-hit model” to explain the pathogenesis of BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts [20–22]. According to this model, host immune deficiencies and genetic mutations in BCG strains do not act in isolation but rather interact synergistically to increase the risk of persistent or disseminated infection [4,23–25]. Under normal physiological conditions, the Th1-type immune response—characterized by effective IFN-γ/IL-12 signaling—ensures rapid activation of macrophages and containment of BCG following vaccination. However, in patients with defects in this axis (e.g. SCID, MSMD, CGD), the host fails to clear even low-virulence strains, providing a permissive environment for BCG survival and replication [25–28]. At the same time, BCG strains, which have undergone extensive in vitro passage, often accumulate mutations in genes such as mma3, pks12, and sigK. These mutations modify lipid metabolism, diminish antigen expression, and reduce drug permeability—features that collectively enhance the bacterium’s capacity to evade host immunity and resist standard antimicrobial therapy [2,29]. This creates a bidirectional loop: immunodeficiency allows for bacterial persistence, while bacterial mutations restore aspects of virulence. For instance, mutations in mma3 disrupt methoxymycolic acid biosynthesis, increasing the MIC for isoniazid and modifying cell wall architecture, thus masking recognition by immune cells [2]. Similarly, cycA mutations impair amino acid uptake and have been linked to cycloserine resistance [29]. These molecular adaptations, while subtle in vitro, may have significant implications in vivo, especially in immunocompromised hosts where selective pressure allows these traits to manifest as clinical virulence [2,30].

Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that BCG strains may undergo site-specific genetic evolution within the host. In certain SCID or IFN-γ receptor-deficient patients, BCG strains isolated from different anatomical sites exhibit divergent SNP profiles, indicating adaptive mutational trajectories under localized immune pressure [4,29]. This intra-host evolution exacerbates disease progression and may contribute to the development of multidrug resistance and therapeutic failure. In essence, the dual-hit model reveals a cooperative mechanism: while immune deficiencies lower the threshold for infection, BCG genetic alterations tip the balance toward pathogenicity. These interactions are particularly dangerous in neonates, HIV-infected individuals, and patients receiving TNF-α inhibitors or chemotherapy, all of whom lack robust immune surveillance [25–27] (Table 2).

Differences between BCG disease and tuberculosis (TB) disease

Although BCG strains, as an attenuated live vaccine, typically induce localized reactions in immunocompetent hosts, in immunocompromised hosts, BCG can still cause severe disseminated infections. In contrast, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), a more virulent strain, utilizes specific pathogenic mechanisms and immune evasion strategies to cause pulmonary tuberculosis and other forms of systemic tuberculosis. Studies indicate that the primary differences between BCG and Mtb are evident in pathogen characteristics, host immune responses, clinical phenotypes, and treatment strategies, with these differences being even more pronounced in immunocompromised hosts [31–36] (Table 3).

Pathogen characteristics

Mtb possesses a complete genome and a highly specialized pathogenic system, including the ESX-1 secretion apparatus, which enables lysosomal escape, intracellular replication, and progression to severe pulmonary and disseminated tuberculosis [37,38]. In contrast, BCG strains, due to the loss of the RD1 region, lack the ESX-1 secretion system, significantly reducing their pathogenicity. However, due to the genetic diversity within BCG strains, different sub-strains (genetically distinct variants or subtypes of BCG strains) exhibit variations in cell wall composition, lipid metabolism, and drug resistance. Therefore, in immunocompromised hosts, some BCG strains may still present phenotypes resembling disseminated infections [2].

Host immune status and immune response

TB disease typically induces a robust Th1 immune response, characterized by IFN-γ and TNF-α–mediated macrophage activation and granuloma formation that restricts bacterial dissemination. In contrast, BCG, being an attenuated strain, usually only triggers a localized inflammatory response in most immunocompetent individuals [39–41]. Studies have demonstrated that following BCG infection, the generation of CD8+ memory T cells, regulation of apoptosis pathways, and cytokine expression profiles differ substantially from those observed in TB disease [42]. These differences may impair the ability of immunocompromised hosts to effectively clear the bacteria, increasing the risk of BCG disease [19,43].

Clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, imaging features, and differential diagnosis

The clinical spectrum of BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts ranges from benign localized inflammation to severe disseminated infection. In immunocompetent individuals, BCG vaccination typically results in mild self-limiting inflammation at the injection site and transient axillary lymphadenopathy. However, in patients with primary immunodeficiencies, the attenuated BCG strain may cause significant pathology [44–46].

BCGitis is a heterogeneous term that has been variably used in the literature to describe both localized BCG infection and systemic immune-mediated complications following BCG exposure. In its localized infectious form, BCG disease commonly manifests as persistent ulceration at the inoculation site [47], accompanied by ipsilateral axillary lymphadenitis. Regional lymph nodes may enlarge and occasionally suppurate. Histopathology typically shows granulomatous inflammation with Langhans giant cells and occasional caseation. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) may be detected in aspirates or biopsy specimens [48]. Ultrasound or CT scans usually reveal hypoechoic lymphadenopathy, with peripheral enhancement or calcification in chronic cases [44,49]. Beyond these typical findings, urological manifestations—including granulomatous cystitis, prostatitis, and epididymitis—may occur, particularly after intravesical BCG therapy for bladder cancer. These may present with symptoms like dysuria, hematuria, or scrotal swelling. Renal involvement, such as granulomatous nephritis, may also be seen and can mimic renal tumors on imaging [50–53]. Importantly, BCGitis has also been used to denote systemic immune-mediated manifestations, such as sterile hepatitis or pneumonitis [54,55], in which organ injury is driven primarily by dysregulated inflammatory responses rather than direct bacterial dissemination. These immune-mediated complications should be distinguished from disseminated BCG infection.

Disseminated BCG disease (BCGosis) involves multiple organ systems beyond the inoculation site. Patients often present with persistent fever, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, generalized lymphadenopathy, and organ-specific signs such as osteomyelitis, miliary pneumonitis, or CNS involvement [29,46,56–59]. Chest CT may reveal diffuse miliary nodules or ground-glass opacities. Skeletal radiographs may show osteolytic lesions in long bones, while abdominal imaging often detects low-density hepatic or splenic nodules [60,61]. CNS imaging may demonstrate granulomatous lesions or calcifications, particularly in SCID patients [62]. Laboratory investigations in disseminated cases often reveal pancytopenia, elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), and positive AFB or PCR for Mycobacterium bovis BCG [50,63]. Molecular assays such as PCR or CB-NAAT are essential for strain confirmation. Immunological profiling may detect low CD4+ T-cell counts, abnormal immunoglobulin levels, or mutations in the IL-12/IFN-γ axis (e.g. RAG1, STAT1) [44,45]. In rare cases, allergic hepatitis and vascular infections such as infectious aneurysms may also be observed, particularly in elderly or immunocompromised patients [64–66].

Differentiating BCG disease from TB disease is essential, as both may present with granulomatous inflammation. BCG disease typically presents earlier, often within weeks after vaccination or bladder instillation, whereas Mtb usually appears later. BCG disease tends to involve lymph nodes near the injection site or presents with atypical miliary spread, while Mtb more commonly affects the pulmonary apex and mediastinal nodes [49,67,68]. Importantly, BCG strains are intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide, which aids in distinguishing them from wild-type Mtb in both molecular and phenotypic testing [38,46,61]. Clinical case reports underscore the importance of early recognition. Infants with SCID may initially present with localized lymphadenitis that progresses to systemic disease. Imaging showing miliary lung nodules or bone lesions, in combination with molecular confirmation of BCG strain and immunologic findings, is essential for timely diagnosis [29,50,61]. In summary, BCG disease exhibits a diverse array of clinical and radiological features depending on the host’s immune status. Localized disease is typically self-limiting but may herald underlying immunodeficiency. Disseminated BCG disease carries high mortality if not recognized early, and its differentiation from Mtb requires careful integration of clinical, laboratory, and imaging data.

Differences in treatment strategies

Due to the well-characterized pathogenic mechanisms of Mtb, standard four-drug anti-tuberculosis regimens are typically employed for long-term treatment. In contrast, for BCG disease patients, treatment strategies require pathogen-specific consideration. BCG strains are intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide, and low-level resistance to isoniazid has been reported in certain strains, as detailed in Section 5.1. Thus, treatment requires individualized regimens guided by drug susceptibility testing and may also necessitate immunomodulatory therapies to facilitate clearance of the pathogen [69,70]. In summary, TB disease and BCG disease differ fundamentally in pathogen integrity, host immune activation, and clinical manifestations, providing theoretical support for developing treatment strategies tailored to different pathogens in clinical practice [37,38].

Sensitivity to anti-tuberculosis drugs and mechanisms of drug resistance

Drug sensitivity differences and resistance mechanisms in BCG strains

In vitro drug susceptibility testing has revealed significant variation among BCG strains in their responses to anti-tuberculosis drugs such as isoniazid and rifampicin. Earlier strains (used in the initial phases of vaccine development or derived from older lineages) generally exhibit lower minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs), reflecting higher sensitivity. By contrast, later strains that have accumulated mutations over time—such as those affecting the mma3 gene—show elevated MICs, consistent with low-level resistance [2,40]. The innate resistance of BCG is also closely tied to its molecular characteristics. For example, inactivation of the pncA gene leads to loss of pyrazinamidase activity and intrinsic resistance to pyrazinamide, while mutations of the mma3 gene impair methoxymycolate synthesis, reducing drug-target exposure and weakening the efficacy of isoniazid [2,70].

Genomic and transcriptomic studies have provided additional insights into the resistance mechanisms of BCG. Point mutations of the mma3 gene alter lipid metabolism, thereby contributing to isoniazid resistance; mutations in cycA are associated with aminoglycoside resistance; and abnormal expression of regulatory genes such as whiB3 may affect drug uptake by modifying cell wall permeability [2,70]. Canonical resistance mechanisms known in Mycobacterium tuberculosis also help explain resistance in BCG strains—for instance, rpoB mutations altering RNA polymerase confer rifampicin resistance, while katG mutations eliminate catalase-peroxidase activity, abolishing isoniazid activation [71]. In addition, non-genetic mechanisms, including phenotypic tolerance and biofilm formation, contribute to resistance emergence. These mechanisms reflect bacterial adaptability, as gene and protein expression changes may produce resistance phenotypes not fully explained by known single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [72,73]. These molecular insights have important clinical implications. Variations in drug susceptibility directly influence therapeutic effectiveness and drug choice, reinforcing the need for rigorous drug susceptibility testing of clinical isolates to tailor treatment regimens [74].

Risk factors for acquired resistance and preventive measures

In immunocompromised hosts, long-term colonization leads to prolonged sub-inhibitory drug exposure, which provides favorable conditions for the selection of resistant strains, particularly those capable of inducing mutations in the whiB3 gene, which are closely linked to the regulation of oxidative stress responses[75]. Another potential mechanism for resistance arises from co-infection with environmental non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), which may transfer katG gene mutations through plasmid exchange (although rare, this is possible). Therefore, routine drug susceptibility monitoring is recommended in clinical practice, particularly for immunocompromised patients, to strengthen the formulation of personalized treatment plans and potentially include immune modulators to enhance treatment efficacy and reduce the spread of resistant strains [69,70]. Molecular diagnostic technologies such as Xpert Mtb/XDR have been employed for rapid detection of rifampicin and second-line drug resistance, but resistance testing for fluoroquinolones and ethambutol still requires further optimization [76].

Current status of BCG resistant strains

Studies have reported differences in the resistance rates of BCG strains used in different production processes and regions, which may be linked to genetic diversity and long-term host stress conditions [2]. Isoniazid resistance has been observed in later BCG strains, with strains such as Pasteur, Tice, and Connaught exhibiting higher MIC values for INH (ranging from 0.1 μg/mL to 1 μg/mL or more), indicating reduced sensitivity. In contrast, earlier strains like Tokyo 172 and Moscow typically show low MICs (∼0.05 μg/mL) for INH, reflecting good sensitivity [2,77]. For rifampicin, while resistance has been less frequently reported, subtle variations in RIF resistance can exist across BCG strains. Given that all BCG strains share a common bovine origin (derived from M. bovis), the RIF resistance profile remains generally homogeneous across different strains. However, minor differences in susceptibility may occur due to variations in cell wall composition and genetic regulation during strain propagation [78].

Preventive measures and clinical management

To address the emergence of resistant strains, it is recommended that regular drug susceptibility testing be conducted in clinical settings and that personalized treatment regimens be promoted. Drug combinations, such as the use of fluoroquinolones and bedaquiline, have proven effective in improving treatment outcomes, although attention must be given to the potential emergence of resistance mutations in bedaquiline (e.g. atpE I66M, atpE A63P) [79]. Additionally, certain adjuvant therapies (e.g. metformin, verapamil) have shown potential in reversing drug resistance in some studies [80]. The integrated use of novel immune-modulatory drugs and targeted new therapies has become a crucial strategy for enhancing treatment efficacy and reducing the risk of resistant strain spread [69,70]. These measures help ensure effective control of resistant strains during treatment.

Recommendations for improving BCG vaccination strategies

To reduce the risk of BCG disease in immunocompromised hosts while ensuring the achievement of tuberculosis control goals, optimizing the BCG vaccination strategy is essential. First, immunodeficiency screening, such as T-cell receptor excision circles (TREC)/K-cell receptor excision circles (KREC) testing and genetic screening, can effectively identify high-risk individuals, thereby preventing the inappropriate administration of live vaccines [81]. Secondly, adjusting the timing and regimen of vaccination, exploring low-dose, delayed vaccination, or alternative vaccination routes can help reduce the risk of systemic dissemination and enhance immune efficacy. Finally, individualized preventive strategies, including immune modulation, prophylactic antimicrobial treatment, and gene therapy, can provide more precise control measures for immunocompromised patients. By integrating these optimized strategies, it is possible to effectively reduce the occurrence of BCG disease while ensuring immune protection and providing safer and more effective vaccination options for immunocompromised populations.

Strain genetic modification and development of novel vaccines

In recent years, genetic engineering techniques have provided new avenues to enhance the safety and immunogenicity of BCG vaccines. For example, the BCGΔBCG1419c vaccine, through the deletion of genes related to c-di-GMP metabolism, significantly enhances the immune response of CD8+ memory T cells and reduces pulmonary inflammation, thereby effectively lowering the risk of BCG disease in immunocompromised populations [43,82]. These genetically modified vaccines can reduce the risk of systemic dissemination while maintaining immune protection. Additionally, while recombinant subunit vaccines such as MtbVac avoid using live bacteria and reduce potential risks, their immunogenicity remains insufficient, requiring further research and optimization to address the balance between immune protection and safety [43,82].

Optimization of vaccination routes and timing

For immunocompromised hosts, such as HIV-infected individuals or children with congenital immune deficiencies, traditional intradermal vaccination may pose considerable risks. Therefore, it is recommended to adjust the vaccination regimen, such as adopting low-dose vaccination, delaying vaccination, or using alternative routes such as percutaneous or mucosal administration, which would enhance immunogenicity while reducing the risk of systemic dissemination [83,84]. Further research suggests that individualized immune assessments and molecular genetic testing (e.g. IL12RB1, IFNGR1 gene testing) to screen high-risk individuals can provide scientific evidence for optimizing vaccination timing and achieving precision vaccination [3,40,85]. The core of this strategy is to ensure that the vaccine is administered safely and effectively in immunocompromised populations. Additionally, for immunocompromised hosts, it is advised to delay BCG vaccination until immune deficiencies are clearly excluded, and to explore the development of inactivated or recombinant subunit vaccines as alternatives to reduce the risks associated with BCG vaccination in immunocompromised hosts [37,38].

Combined immune modulation strategies

The combined use of novel adjuvants or immune modulators can activate protective immune responses while inhibiting excessive immune inflammation, thus reducing the risk of BCG disease triggered by the vaccine [86,87]. Experimental results show that such combined immune strategies can enhance Th1/Th17 immune responses, promote the formation of CD8+ memory T cells, and mitigate the risk of cytokine storms, particularly in immunocompromised patients, providing robust support for vaccine administration [43]. This approach not only helps enhance immune protection but also offers a new possibility for the safe vaccination of immunocompromised populations.

Translational barriers in novel vaccine development

Despite providing new options for BCG vaccination, novel BCG vaccines and recombinant subunit vaccines still face certain translational barriers. For instance, the genetic modification of the BCGΔmma3 strain enhances vaccine safety but requires further balancing between immunogenicity and protective efficacy [88,89]. Moreover, although recombinant subunit vaccines like MtbVac avoid the use of live bacteria and reduce risks during vaccination, their immune protection remains significantly limited [90,91]. Future vaccine development must overcome these barriers and optimize aspects such as immune protection, stability, and long-term efficacy to improve their feasibility for widespread application.

Individualized immune assessment, risk screening, and preventive strategies for immunocompromised patients

High-throughput molecular diagnostic technologies allow for dynamic monitoring of individual immune status, which is crucial for the early identification of immune deficiencies prior to vaccination. By combining genetic testing with immune function assessments, personalized vaccination regimens can be designed, and scientific evidence can be provided for subsequent dose adjustments and immune enhancement therapies, reducing the risk of BCG disease [3,85,92]. For patients with known conditions such as MSMD or other primary immunodeficiencies, a comprehensive preventive and therapeutic strategy should be adopted. Prophylactic antimicrobial treatments and immune modulation measures (e.g. IFN-γ therapy) are recommended, alongside potential interventions like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or gene therapy in severe cases. Additionally, exploring new vaccine delivery methods, particularly receptor-mediated targeted delivery, can improve vaccine efficacy and reduce the risk of systemic dissemination [1,2,4,37].

Discussion and future perspectives

The pathogenesis of BCG disease highlights the critical interplay between host immune status and pathogen genomic evolution. The dual-hit model provides a mechanistic scaffold for BCG disease pathogenesis, extending to other live-vaccine complications. Moreover, the intrinsic and acquired resistance profiles of BCG strains add to therapeutic complexity, highlighting the urgent need for resistance-guided personalized regimens rather than empiric therapy. To address these challenges, vaccination strategies require systematic optimization. Precision risk stratification based on immune screening is essential for immunocompromised hosts; novel vaccine platforms, including genetically attenuated strains and subunit alternatives, show promise in reducing dissemination risk but require validation in immunocompromised populations; and vaccination regimens (dose, timing, and route) should be context-specific—for example, delayed vaccination or mucosal delivery routes for HIV-exposed infants.

Nevertheless, key knowledge gaps remain. First, it is unclear how BCG genomic plasticity (e.g. locus-specific SNP divergence) regulates immune evasion under defined immune-deficient states, though integrative multi-omics analyses may help resolve these networks. Second, the contribution of non-genetic mechanisms to treatment failure requires quantitative assessment. Third, translational challenges for next-generation vaccines—including balancing immunogenicity with efficacy and ensuring scalability—demand coordinated efforts. Future breakthroughs will rely on integrating real-world data streams. Machine learning applied to host genomic, immunologic, and BCG resistance datasets may empower risk prediction models; point-of-care rapid diagnostic tools should be prioritized for BCG strain identification and resistance testing; and clinical trials must evaluate immunomodulatory adjuvants (e.g. IFN-γ, immune checkpoint inhibitors) as adjunctive strategies in high-risk cases. Ultimately, by aligning biomarker discovery, vaccine engineering, and personalized clinical pathways, molecular insights can be transformed into actionable interventions, allowing BCG disease to shift from a diagnostic challenge to a preventable complication in global tuberculosis control.

Conclusion

BCG disease epitomizes the peril of live-vaccine-derived infections in immunocompromised hosts, where host-pathogen co-evolution fuels therapeutic failure. Our synthesis establishes the Dual-Hit Model as a unifying paradigm for understanding such breakthrough events beyond BCG. To secure global TB control, future efforts must prioritize predictive risk frameworks and immune-adapted vaccine platforms—transforming vaccine safety from reactive surveillance to pre-emptive interception.
