Family-based interventions on self-management behaviours and blood pressure control in individuals with hypertension: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Getu Mosisa Kebebew, Dereje Chala Diriba, Tieghan Killackey, Tasneem Owadally, Monica Parry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, the number of adults with hypertension doubled within the last three decades, and more than one billion adults have hypertension. Primary study results suggest family-based interventions (i.e., interventions in which at least one family member is involved) for individuals with hypertension have a positive effect on blood pressure (BP) and self-management behaviours (SMB). However, the evidence has not been systematically reviewed and synthesized. Thus, this systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes the available evidence to explore the effects of family-based interventions on BP and SMB.MethodsA comprehensive search of six databases: MEDLINE [Ovid], Cochrane Library, Scopus, EMBASE [Ovid], PsycINFO [Ovid], and CINAHL [EBSCO], and the grey literature ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global was completed on March 14, 2025. The Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions was used to guide the review, and the PRISMA-2020 guideline was used to report the review. Search outputs were imported into EndNote and exported to Covidence for de-duplication and screening. Data extraction included study-level and intervention-level characteristics reported using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR). Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane’s Risk of Bias (ROB) tool. Statistical meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 (RevMan) software.ResultsA total of 8,548 studies were identified in the search. After removing 3,826 duplicates, 4,722 studies remained for title and abstract screening. In total, 56 studies were included for a full-text review, and 19 studies were included for data extraction; 15 were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and four were quasi-experimental studies. The duration of the interventions varied across studies and ranged from one month to 24 months. The overall pooled data indicated that family-based interventions reduced systolic blood pressure (MD = -7.74, 95% CI: [-10.24, -5.24], p < 0.001; I2 = 95%) and diastolic blood pressure (MD = -3.74, 95% CI: [-5.07, -2.42], p < 0.001; I2= 91%) compared to usual care. Family-based interventions also contributed to improvements in adherence to SMB in individuals with hypertension.ConclusionFamily-based interventions reduced both SBP and DBP, with better reduction for interventions delivered and evaluated within 6 months, and improved SMBs in individuals with hypertension. Furthermore, no studies from low-income countries were included in this review. Future research should examine the feasibility, accessibility, and effectiveness of family-based interventions in these settings. It is also recommended that future studies incorporate multiple components of an intervention, including practical sessions that are culturally tailored and theory-based, with ongoing reminders to improve the maintenance of hypertension behaviour change beyond six months.Trial registrationThe protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251010902).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27203-9.Keywords: Blood pressure, Family-based intervention, Individuals with hypertension, Self-management behaviours, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, and hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP)/diastolic blood pressure (DBP) 140/90 mmHg or higher, accounts for most of the deaths [1, 2]. Worldwide, between 1990 and 2019, the number of adults living with hypertension doubled from 331 million to 626 million in women and from 317 million to 652 million in men because of population growth and aging [3]. Hypertension is the leading risk factor for stroke, ischemic heart disease, renal failure, and death if not managed at an early stage [4]; contributing to 10.04 million deaths and 218 million Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) globally [5].

Uncontrolled hypertension remains a major public health concern worldwide despite the availability and accessibility of antihypertensive therapy, health education, and counseling in the healthcare setting [6]. Modifiable risk factors such as weight, diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking contribute to uncontrolled hypertension [7–9]. Antihypertensive therapy and lifestyle interventions are recommended for the effective management of hypertension, and the latter further contributes to the management of most cardiovascular risk factors [10, 11]. An effective combination of antihypertensive therapy and lifestyle interventions lowers the risk of mortality among adults with hypertension [12].

The term self-management, which is commonly defined as the individual’s ability to manage lifestyle change, with attention to symptoms, therapy, and the physical and psychosocial consequences, is widely used for the management of chronic diseases [13, 14]. The Individual and Family Self-Management Theory (IFSMT) describes self-management along three dimensions: context (condition-specific risk and protective factors, components of the physical and social environment, and unique characteristics of individuals and family members); process (knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills and abilities, social facilitation); and outcomes (self-management behaviours [SMBs], which include adherence to anti-hypertensive therapies, engagement in lifestyle interventions such as diet, weight control, exercise, and health status [i.e., systolic and diastolic blood pressure control]) [15] (Supplementary Figure S1).

Current evidence suggests that family-based interventions (i.e., involvement of at least one family member in the interventions designed for individuals with hypertension) play a crucial role in improving blood pressure, medication adherence, psychological well-being, and lifestyle change outcomes [16–19]. The number of studies examining the effectiveness of family involvement in the management of individuals with hypertension is increasing; however, the evidence is not synthesized. A recent scoping review conducted by Isnaini et al. [20] reported positive effects of family-centered education on blood pressure, behaviour change, quality of life, self-esteem, self-efficacy, and family empowerment among older adults. However, the review was limited to older adults and did not comprehensively synthesize the effectiveness of family-based interventions among all individuals with hypertension. Findings of this current systematic review and meta-analysis will help policymakers and clinicians strengthen the role of family members in managing hypertension in the community and healthcare institutions. Thus, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively synthesize the evidence of the effectiveness of family-based interventions on lowering BP and improving SMBs of individuals with hypertension. Specific objectives are to determine: (1) if family-based interventions are effective in lowering blood pressure in individuals who have hypertension, (2) if family-based interventions are effective in improving individual SMBs, and (3) the materials, procedures, providers, modes of delivery, location, timing, intensity/dose, tailoring, and fidelity of existing family-based interventions.

Methods

The protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on March 18, 2025 (registration number: CRD420251010902). We conducted this review according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [21], and it is reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA-2020) guidelines [22].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected according to the Participant/population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study type (PICOS) framework.

Inclusion criteria

Participants included adult individuals with hypertension, defined as a SBP and DBP of 140/90 mmHg or higher [2], aged 18 years and above. Any intervention designed for individuals with hypertension and involved family members was included. Family-based interventions involved the engagement of at least one family member (e.g., spouse, child, parent, or others) in the designed intervention aimed to improve the SMBs of individuals with hypertension [16], conducted either in the community or the health care institutions. Participants who received usual care or a non-family-based intervention were considered as a comparator. The search was not limited by outcomes, but the outcomes of interest included SBP and DBP and/or SMBs (antihypertensive therapy or lifestyle behaviours). Original or primary studies that included a randomized controlled trial (RCT) or a quasi-experimental design with a control group published in English were included.

Exclusion criteria

Previous reviews, including systematic, rapid, scoping, and literature reviews and study protocols and articles published in languages other than English, were excluded.

Search strategy

An initial search was conducted using the MEDLINE database, and then all searches were translated to other databases, including Ovid Embase, EBSCO CINAHL, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, and Cochrane Library databases (Supplementary Table S1). All databases were searched from database inception to March 14, 2025. The search was conducted using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and the key terms including “hypertension” and “family-based intervention”. These keywords were combined by the Boolean operators ‘OR’ and ‘AND’. A search for grey literature was completed using ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global. Furthermore, the reference lists of relevant articles were also screened for related articles. The search was not limited by the year of publication or study setting.

Study selection process

The search results of all databases were imported into EndNote 21 to manage the records and then exported to Covidence [23] for screening and removal of duplicates. Title and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (GMK and TO) for relevance to the eligibility criteria. Articles that did not meet eligibility criteria were excluded. Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were moved to full-text screening, which again was independently completed by two reviewers (GMK and TO). The reasons for the exclusion of articles in full-text screening are reported in Fig. 1. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus or the involvement of a third reviewer (MP).

PRISMA flow chart of included studies

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently completed by two reviewers (GMK and TO). The data extraction format included study-level and intervention-level characteristics. Study-level characteristics included author, year, country of publication, study design, sample size, which included the number of individuals with hypertension and their family members, family member-individual with hypertension relationship, sex, and marital status. Intervention-level characteristics were described using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) [24]. This included the “why” (rationale, theoretical framework, or goal), “what” (materials and procedures used), “who” (intervention provider), “how” (mode of delivery), “where” (the location where intervention occurred), “when” and “how much” (duration and frequency of intervention), “tailoring” (the goal setting adapted/individualized goal setting), “modifications” (any changes to intervention), and the “how well” (planned/actual). The IFSMT also informed the extraction of data variables. Any disagreements were again resolved by consensus or the involvement of a third reviewer (MP). In addition, in case the studies had missing or incomplete data, the corresponding author was contacted through email to request further details.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the retrieved articles was critically and independently assessed by two authors (GMK and MP) using Cochrane’s Risk of Bias (ROB) tools. Cochrane’s Risk of Bias (ROB 2) was used to assess RCT studies. This tool included five domains of bias: risk of bias arising from the randomization process, risk of bias due to deviation from intended intervention, risk of bias due to missing outcome data, risk of bias in the measurement of outcome, and risk of bias in the selection of reported results. The overall risk-of-bias judgement was then rated as low risk of bias (low risk in all domains), some concerns (some concerns in at least one domain but not high risk in any domain), or high risk bias (high risk in at least one domain or some concerns in multiple domains) [25]. The ROBINS-I tool was used to assess the risk of bias in non-randomized studies. This tool included seven domains of bias: risk of bias due to confounding, risk of bias in selection of participants into the study, risk of bias in the classification of interventions, risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, risk of bias due to missing data, risk of bias in measurement of outcomes, and risk of bias in the selection of the reported results.

The overall risk-of-bias judgement using the ROBINS-I tool is then rated as low risk of bias (risk of bias for all domains), moderate risk of bias (low or moderate risk of bias for all domains), serious risk of bias (serious risk of bias in at least one domain, but not at critical risk of bias in any domain), critical risk of bias (critical risk of bias in at least one domain), or no information (there is no clear indication that the study is at serious or critical risk of bias, and there is a lack of information in one or more key domains of bias) [26]. Any disagreements in risk of bias assessments between the reviewers (GMK and MP) were resolved by consensus. The summary of the risk of bias of ROB 2 and ROBINS-I was organized using the Risk-Of-Bias VISualization (robvis) tool [27].

Data synthesis and analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 software. The meta-analyses for the continuous outcomes (i.e., SBP and DBP) were performed using the Inverse Variance statistical method to calculate the mean difference (MD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity across the studies was assessed using I2 statistics, with thresholds of 0%-40%, 30%-60%, 50%-90% and greater than 75% indicating might not be important, moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively [21]. In line with Cochrane handbook [21], the mean and standard deviation (SD) of post-intervention values and changes from baselines were extracted. When studies reported 95% confidence intervals (CIs) rather than standard deviations (SDs), SDs were calculated using the 95% CI. A random effects model was used as significant heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the duration of the interventions (i.e., ≤ 3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months). When statistical pooling was not possible due to outcome heterogeneity, the results were presented narratively and in tabular form. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Publication bias was assessed using the funnel plot and Egger’s regression test.

Assessing certainty in the findings

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [28]. The overall quality of evidence for SBP and DBP outcomes was assessed using the GRADE domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The GRADE analysis was performed using the online GRADEpro GDT software [29]. Two reviewers (GMK and MP) independently assessed the quality of evidence, resolving any disagreements through discussion to reach consensus.

Results

Study selection

A total of 8,548 studies were identified as follows: Scopus (n = 2,996), Embase (n = 2,707), MEDLINE (n = 1,391), CINAHL (n = 654), Cochrane Library (n = 513), PsycINFO (n = 213), and ProQuest Dissertation and Thesis Global (n = 74), and then imported into Covidence. There were 3,826 duplicates removed manually and automatically using Covidence, leaving 4,722 studies for title and abstract screening and 56 studies included for a full-text review. In total, 19 studies were included in this review. A full list of the 37 studies excluded during full-text review is available in Supplementary Table S2. No studies were identified in the references of included studies (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The studies included in this review were published between 1982 and 2024. Of the 19 studies included, five (n = 5, 26.32%) were conducted in Iran [30–34], three each (n = 3, 15.79%) in the United States (US) [35–37] and Thailand [38–40], two each (n = 2, 10.53%) in China [16, 41] and Indonesia [42, 43], one each (n = 1, 5.26%) in Spain [44], Pakistan [45], Taiwan [46] and Colombia and Malaysia [47]. Two studies (parent and companion papers) included an efficacy pilot trial [17] and an effectiveness RCT [41] and were consolidated as a single study in this review; data were primarily extracted from the effectiveness RCT. Fifteen (n = 15, 78.95%) of the included studies were RCTs, and the remaining four (n = 4, 21.05%) were quasi-experimental studies. Risk of bias of studies

Of 15 RCTs assessed by ROB 2, the overall risk of bias was low in three (n = 3, 20%) studies [16, 37, 47], some concerns were reported in 10 (n = 10, 66.67%) studies [30, 31, 33, 34, 36, 41–45], and a high risk of bias reported in two (n = 2, 13.33%) studies [32, 35]. Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions was judged to be low for 8/15 included studies, as participant adherence was high, deviations from the protocol were minimal and balanced across groups, and outcome assessors were blinded. A summary of the risk of bias of RCT studies is presented in Fig. 2.

Risk of bias of RCT studies summary traffic light plot

Of the four studies assessed by ROBINS-I, the overall risk of bias was moderate. Two studies (n = 2, 50%) were rated as having moderate risk in domain I, primarily because they did not indicate how they managed confounding [39, 40]. All studies (n = 4, 100%) were rated as moderate in domain VI, as they did not indicate blinding of the outcome assessment. A summary of the risk of bias for quasi-experimental studies is presented in Fig. 3.

Characteristics of participants

Study participants – Individuals with or at high risk for hypertension

Sample sizes ranged from 30 to 1,371. Ten studies (n = 10, 52.63%) included participants with a mean age in the 60s [16, 31, 33, 34, 38, 39, 41, 43, 44, 47], seven studies (n = 7, 36.84%) with a mean age in the 50s [30, 32, 36, 37, 42, 45, 46], one study (n = 1, 5.26%) with a mean age in the 40s [35], and a mean age of 60 to 75 years in one study (n = 1, 5.26%) [40]. Of the included studies, 18 (94.74%) recruited individuals with hypertension, and one study (n = 1, 5.26%) included individuals at high risk for hypertension [38]. Except in three studies (n = 3, 15.79%), the majority of the participants in the included studies were female [33, 41, 46]. Eleven studies (n = 11, 57.89%) reported the marital status of the participants, and the majority of the participants were reported to be married [16, 30–32, 34, 35, 38–41, 46].

Study participants - family members

In all studies (n = 19, 100%), at least one family member was recruited to participate, but in two (n = 2, 10.53%) studies, all family/household members were recruited to participate in the study [45, 46]. In one study, individuals with hypertension did not directly participate in the training sessions; instead, mothers or persons responsible for the family’s diet participated in the training [34]. In the included studies, family members, including spouses, children, grandchildren, partners, and others such as friends, participated in the intervention. Family members participated in training sessions, supporting individuals with hypertension in adhering to medication and health behaviours, blood pressure monitoring, and diet preparation. The characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Characteristics of the intervention

Theory

Of the 19 included studies, six (n = 6, 31.58%) utilized a theory or conceptual framework. The theories included the social support theory [38], shared care model [41], self-efficacy and social support theory [40], Johnson and family empowerment model [31], the Health Belief Model (HBM) [34] and support theory/family empowerment model, and geragogy (i.e., process of supporting learning in older adults) [43].

Contents and materials of the intervention

The contents of the intervention delivered for the participants included information on hypertension and risk factors, lifestyle modification (i.e., diet, exercise, weight management, stress management, and smoking cessation), self-management (i.e., medication adherence, blood pressure monitoring at home), and the role of family in the management of hypertension. A calendar/reminder pill box to mark daily medication was used in three (n = 3, 15.79%) studies [16, 32, 44]. A booklet/training manual was provided to the study participants in five (n = 5, 26.32%) studies [30–32, 36, 40]. A website which was developed by the research team to include all materials of a health education programme focused on hypertension was used in one study (n = 1, 5.26%) [46], and Short Message Service (SMS)/text messaging was used in two studies (n = 2, 10.53%) [16, 37]. Three (n = 3, 15.79%) studies incorporated an educational video as a component of their intervention [34, 38, 40]. Cooking demonstrations were included in the intervention in three (n = 3, 15.79%) studies [38, 42, 43], and a physical exercise demonstration class was included in three (n = 3,15.79%) studies [38, 40, 42]. In all studies (n = 19, 100%), usual care was provided to the control group.

Intervention providers

Interventions were delivered or facilitated by nurses (n = 4, 21.05%) [31, 33, 41, 46], physicians (n = 2, 10.53%) [16, 44], non-physician health workers under physician supervision (n = 1, 5.26%) [47], public health nurses or pharmacists (n = 1, 5.26%) [35], multidisciplinary team members (i.e., researchers, public health nurses, care managers) (n = 1, 5.26%) [38], clinical nurses, pharmacists with public health training, and community health volunteers (n = 1, 5.26%) [39], research team members, clinicians and nurses (n = 1, 5.26%) [37], health care team members (n = 1, 5.26%) [32], community health professions/community health workers (n = 3, 15.79%) [34, 42, 45], researchers, and primary health care educators (n = 1, 5.26%) [36].

Mode of delivery, location, and duration of the intervention

In most studies (n = 18, 94.74%), the intervention was delivered primarily in person [16, 30–36, 38–47], and in one (n = 1,5.26%) study, the intervention was delivered by telephone [37]. The interventions were delivered in groups (n = 8, 42.1%) (i.e., involved ≥ two individuals with hypertension, family members or family dyads), individually (n = 7, 36.84%) (i.e., involved one individual with hypertension and a family member), and both in group and individual sessions (i.e., home visit) in six studies (n = 6, 31.58%). The interventions were delivered in the community and or health care settings, including hospitals and health clinics. The duration of the interventions varied across studies and ranged from one month to 24 months. The details of the characteristics of the intervention are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Outcomes

All studies measured systolic and diastolic blood pressure at baseline and at the end of the intervention; three studies conducted interim analyses [16, 42, 47], and these were included in the meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the duration of the interventions at ≤ 3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months.

Effects of family-based intervention on systolic blood pressure

Of the total studies, 17 studies (n = 17, 89.47%) reported changes in SBP [16, 30–34, 37–47]. However, one study [43] did not report the mean and SD and was excluded from the meta-analysis. The overall pooled data indicated that family-based interventions reduced SBP by 7.74 mmHg (MD = -7.74, 95% CI: [-10.24, -5.24], p < 0.001; I2 = 95%). The pooled data, at ≤ 3 months, family-based interventions showed a significant reduction by 9.82 mmHg in SBP compared to usual care (MD = -9.82, 95% CI: [-14.79, -4.85], p < 0.001; I2 = 93%). A significant reduction in SBP by 7.12 mmHg was observed in the pooled data between 3 and 6 months duration (MD = -7.12, 95% CI: [-10.77, -3.48], p < 0.001; I2 =95%). However, at 6 months or greater, family-based interventions showed a reduction in SBP of 4.09 mmHg, but the difference was not significant when compared to usual care (MD = -4.09, 95% CI: [-10.66, 2.48], p = 0.22; I2 =93%) (Fig. 4).

Effects of family-based intervention on diastolic blood pressure

Seventeen studies (n = 17, 89.47%) reported changes in DBP [16, 30–34, 37–47]. The overall results demonstrated that family-based interventions reduced DBP by 3.74 mmHg (MD = -3.74, 95% CI: [-5.07, -2.42], p < 0.001; I2 =91%). At 3 months and less, family-based interventions reduced DBP by 4.56 mmHg (MD = -4.56, 95% CI: [-7.49, -1.64], p = 0.002; I2 =89%). A significant reduction in DBP was observed between 3 and 6 months duration (MD = -3.70, 95% CI: [-5.90, -1.49], p = 0.001; I2 =93%). Similarly, at 6 months and greater duration, family-based interventions showed a reduction in DBP (MD = -1.91, 95% CI: [-3.68, -0.15], p = 0.03; I2 =63%) compared to usual care (Fig. 5).

Effects of family-based intervention on blood pressure control

Six studies (n = 6, 31.58%) reported the effectiveness of family-based interventions on blood pressure control [16, 35, 36, 41, 44, 47]. Blood pressure control was defined differently across these studies. In a study conducted by Morisky et al. [36], the average of SBP and DBP over 6 months was taken to measure blood pressure control. In Zeng et al. [41], the proportion of individuals with well-controlled or normal BP was measured using individuals with normal BP at each measurement to the number of individuals with hypertension enrolled in the group. In Earp et al. [35], uncontrolled DBP was defined as a DBP ≥ 95 mm Hg. In Schalm et al. [47], controlled SBP/DBP was defined as < 140/90 mmHg. In three studies [36, 41, 47], significant changes in blood pressure control were reported. In one study, a significant control in blood pressure was observed in SBP but not in DBP [44]. In the study conducted by Shen et al. [16], a significant improvement was observed in blood pressure control at 6 months but not at 12 months. In the study conducted by Earp et al. [35], three arms (Group 1: visits from nurses/pharmacists plus family members involved, Group II: home visits from nurses/pharmacists, and Group III: routine care) were used. After one year, in all three groups, the proportion of individuals with uncontrolled DBP significantly declined. However, in year two, individuals in group I and II demonstrated greater improvement compared to individuals in the control group (i.e., proportion of individuals with controlled diastolic blood pressure: group I: 17% and group II: 21%). In the study conducted by Mehta et al. [37], no significant changes were observed in blood pressure control.

Effects of family based interventions on self-management behaviours

Medication adherence

Of the 19 studies, nine (n = 9, 47.37%) reported medication adherence [16, 30, 32, 36, 37, 39, 42, 44, 47]. In three out of nine studies, medication adherence was assessed using Morisky Medication Adherence Scale [32, 42, 47], in one study, assesed by Hill-Bone Compliance to High Blood Pressure Therapy Scale [30], in three studies medication adherence was assessed by self-report [16, 36, 37], one study used pill count [39], and one study used an electronic Medication Event Monitoring System (MEMS) device [44]. Eight out of nine studies reported significant changes in medication adherence [16, 30, 32, 36, 39, 42, 44, 47]. However, in Shen et al., a significant difference in medication adherence was observed at 6 months but not at 12 months [16]. Three arms (i.e., remote monitoring [RM], social support [SS], and usual care [UC]) were included in the study conducted by Mehta et al. [37] and all groups self reported adherence to medications at both baseline and at the end of the visit. However, differences were observed in the proportion of participants who reported difficulty remembering to take their medications at 4 months follow-up visits compared to baseline, among the following groups: RM arm: 27.4%, SS arm: 19.3% UC arm: 43.2%.

Lifestyle behaviours

Nine studies reported lifestyle behaviour outcomes [16, 30, 34, 36, 38, 41–43, 47]. The lifestyle behaviours reported included diet (i.e., salt, fruit, and vegetable consumption), exercise, weight, and tobacco smoking. There was variability in salt consumption measures used across the studies, including self-report [30], Score Sodium Questionnaire (SSQ) scale in one study [42], 24-hour sodium concentration in the urine [34], Dietary Sodium Restriction Questionnaire scale was used to measure dietary salt restriction, compact salt meter was used to measure the percentage of salt content in the food, and a KME-03 salinity checker was used to measure the concentration of salt in the urine and [43], and measures were not reported in two studies [16, 47]. In the six studies that assessed salt consumption, a significant reduction in salt consumption was reported [16, 30, 34, 42, 43, 47].

The effectiveness of family support on weight control was assessed in two studies [36, 47], and a significant change was reported only in one study [36]. Significant improvement in physical activity and consumption of vegetables was also reported in the study conducted by Schwalm et al. [47]. Two studies reported the significant change in SMB scores [38, 41]. In the study conducted by Shen et al., a significant reduction in tobacco smoking was observed at 6 months but not at 12 months [16]. Details of outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using the funnel plot and Egger’s test. The funnel plot for SBP was symmetrical and showed no publication bias (Fig. 6). The Egger’s regression test was calculated for each duration of the interventions: ≤ 3 months (z = -1.53, p = 0.126), 3–6 months (z = -0.336, p = 0.737), and > 6 months (z = -1.638, p = 0.10). The Egger’s regression test for asymmetry did not reveal significant evidence of publication bias. The funnel plot for DBP was also symmetrical and demonstrated no publication bias (Fig. 7). The Egger’s regression test for DBP at each duration of the interventions was calculated. At ≤ 3months (z = -0.522, p = 0.602) and 3–6 months (z = -0.415, p = 0.678), the test showed no significant evidence of publication bias. However, at > 6 months, the Egger’s regression test indicated significant asymmetry (z = -2.29, p = 0.02), suggesting potential publication bias. This suggested smaller studies tended to report larger effects.

Quality of evidence

The quality of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach with GRADE pro GDT, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The overall certainty of evidence for outcomes SBP and DBP was rated as moderate due to serious risk of bias identified in some of the included studies (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively synthesize the evidence on the effectiveness of family-based interventions in lowering BP and improving SMBs in individuals with hypertension. To our knowledge, this is the first review to address a summary of the effectiveness of family involvement for individuals with hypertension on lowering BP and improving SMBs. Most of the studies included in this review had a low to moderate risk of bias; in the included RCTs, risk was primarily related to the randomization process and the measurement of outcomes. In quasi-experimental studies, all studies had some concerns in the measurement of outcomes, with concerns also noted in confounding and deviation from the intended intervention. These biases could potentially affect the observed effects. However, despite the results of the bias assessments, all studies were included in the narrative synthesis, and all studies reporting mean and SD were included in the meta-analysis. The evidence generated in this systematic review and meta-analysis is from studies conducted in middle-and high-income countries; no studies were conducted in low-income countries. This limits the ability to generalize the findings to low-income countries and highlights the need for primary research assessing the effectiveness of family-based interventions in the management of hypertension among individuals with hypertension in these settings.

The ultimate goal of hypertension treatment is to reduce cardiovascular-related complications among individuals with hypertension, and lowering the BP plays a significant role in reducing the risk of major cardiovascular disease events [48, 49]. Family-based approaches play a significant role in the health promotion and prevention of diseases [50]. Family members’ psychological support, practical guidance, and encouragement are pivotal in promoting adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviours (i.e., diet and physical activity), improving medication adherence, and enhancing compliance to medical appointments, thereby improving health outcomes, including lowering blood pressure and reducing potential cardiovascular-related complications [30, 51, 52]. These findings underscore the value of integrating family member engagement into hypertension management strategies.

The IFSMT served as a guiding framework, helping to clarify the context, process, and outcomes considered in the review (i.e., proximal outcomes: lifestyle behaviours and medication adherence, and distal outcome: change in blood pressure [SBP and DBP]). The pooled findings of this review indicated that interventions that involve family members were effective in reducing the SBP and DBP of individuals with hypertension. This is consistent with a recent scoping review that suggested family-centered education for older adults with hypertension reduced blood pressure [20]. This implies that the involvement of family members in the management of individuals with hypertension is more effective than individualized interventions (i.e., those targeted only to the individual with hypertension).

In this review, the duration of the intervention, contents, and format of the sessions varied across the included studies. The duration of the interventions ranged from one month to 24 months. The statistical analysis showed that short-duration interventions (≤ 3 months) indicated a greater reduction in SBP and DBP than longer ones. This may be due to shorter interventions being more intensive and promoting participants’ engagement and adherence to the intervention than longer durations. Thus, it is recommended that future studies incorporate multiple components of an intervention, including practical sessions that are culturally tailored and theory-based, with ongoing reminders to improve the maintenance of hypertension behaviour change beyond six months.

This review also evaluated the effect of family member involvement on medication adherence in reducing blood pressure and improving blood pressure control. Among the studies reporting medication adherence, the majority showed that individuals with hypertension who received family-based educational interventions were more likely to adhere to anti-hypertensive medication compared to non-family-based interventions [16, 30, 32, 36, 39, 42, 44, 47]. These findings are consistent with a review by Win et al. [53], who demonstrated that family member support was one of the factors that improved medication adherence for individuals with hypertension. Similarly, a separate systematic review demonstrated that support from family members promoted medication adherence among individuals with hypertension [54]. Moreover, supportive materials including a calendar and pill box reminders, could encourage individuals with hypertension to adhere to their medication schedules.

The effect of family-based interventions on lifestyle behaviours was also assessed in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Results suggest family involvement in hypertension management improved lifestyle behaviours for diet, weight management, and physical activities. Six studies reported a significant reduction in salt consumption [16, 30, 34, 42, 43, 47]. Among the studies reporting on salt consumption, some incorporated cooking demonstrations and recruited family members who were responsible for the family’s diet to participate in the intervention [38, 42, 43]. Thus, education on a low-salt diet might improve knowledge and practices related to salt consumption in the daily diet, and this, in turn, significantly improves blood pressure among individuals with hypertension [55]. A significant improvement in weight control and physical activities was also reported by others [36, 47]. This suggests family involvement plays a crucial role in improving lifestyle behaviours of individuals with hypertension, which is the cornerstone in the prevention and management of hypertension and other cardiovascular diseases.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis present several strengths. First, a comprehensive search strategy was employed using multiple databases in consultation with a librarian. Second, the TIDieR checklist was used to extract the characteristics of the intervention used in the included studies. Despite these strengths, this review has some limitations. First, the search was limited to the English language, so studies conducted in other languages could have been missed. Second, all studies included in this review were conducted in middle and high-income countries; there are no studies conducted in low-income countries. Third, most of the studies included in this review had a low to moderate risk of bias, with a few rated as high risk, which may reduce the certainty of the evidence. Furthermore, high heterogeneity was observed in the included studies, with potential sources heterogeneity including the types of interventions, diverse cultural settings, and the differences in study designs. This heterogeneity may negatively impact the certainty of evidence presented in this review. Finally, using a combination of change-from-baseline and post-intervention scores when performing the meta-analysis of mean differences, in line with Cochrane handbook guidance [21], enabled the inclusion of more studies. However, combining these scores could increase the heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

In this review, while family-based interventions reduced both SBP and DBP, with better reduction for interventions delivered and evaluated within 6 months, the long-term efficacy remains inconclusive or unsupported by the current meta-analysis. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies incorporate multiple components of an intervention, including practical sessions that are culturally tailored and theory-based, with ongoing reminders to improve the maintenance of hypertension behaviour change beyond six months. Furthermore, no studies from low-income countries were included in this review. Future research should examine the feasibility, accessibility, and effectiveness of family-based interventions in these settings.
