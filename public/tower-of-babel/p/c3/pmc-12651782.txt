An engineered VSV-vectored rabies vaccine with an RABV-G-L71S mutation
Authors: Mingxi Yue, Yuhe Zhu, Hongchao Wu, Siping Yan, Jinlei Zhang, Mengqi Chang, Liqin Liu, Hui Huang, Jie Lu, Wenrong Wu, Ruitong Chen, Shuyu Wu, Yibing Gao, Yujiao Cao, Hangtian Ding, Qingbing Zheng, Yingbin Wang, Quan Yuan, Hualong Xiong, Kegong Tian, Tianying Zhang, Ningshao Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA replicating recombinant rabies vaccine, rVSV-RABV, was constructed using vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) expressing the rabies virus glycoprotein (RABV-G, SAD-B19 strain). A leucine-to-serine mutation at position 71 (L71S) in the RABV-G extracellular domain significantly enhanced the viral titer and G protein expression. The derived strain, rVSV-RABV-L71S, demonstrated production and immunogenic advantages. As an inactivated vaccine formulated with GEL-02 adjuvant, it induced stronger neutralizing antibody responses, along with innate and Th1-biased T cell immunity in mice, compared to commercially inactivated vaccines. As a live vaccine, it conferred robust protection against the CVS-24 challenge in mice (NIH titer ∼4.8-fold higher than reference) and elicited superior neutralizing antibodies in dogs. Both inactivated and live rVSV-RABV-L71S formulations exhibited favorable safety profiles. rVSV-RABV-L71S represents a promising, low-cost, high-efficacy rabies vaccine candidate suitable for widespread use in developing countries.

Introduction

Rabies accounts for roughly 59,000 human fatalities each year and leads to 3.7 million disability-adjusted life years.1 Not only does it inflict substantial agony on patients and their families, but it also places a considerable socio-economic burden. Human pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis represent the most efficacious strategies for rabies containment. Administering vaccines and immunoglobulins to those bitten by rabid animals can markedly avert the occurrence of the disease. Given that dogs serve as the principal reservoirs of the rabies virus, vaccinating them efficiently halts the spread of the virus. During the latter part of the twentieth century, several nations in Western Europe, the Americas, and Japan eradicated canine rabies via extensive vaccination initiatives, underscoring the crucial significance of vaccinating both humans and dogs in the battle against this deadly disease.2,3,4 In addition, the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Organization for Animal Health (OIE), and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) put forward a plan in 2015 aiming to achieve the global elimination of dog-transmitted rabies by 2030. However, in economically underdeveloped areas, especially in certain parts of Africa and Asia, the overall vaccination rate of the rabies vaccine is relatively low.1 For a rabies vaccine to be widely accessible in developing countries, it must not only be safe and efficacious but also, and more crucially, possess a relatively low manufacturing cost.

The pioneering rabies vaccine was devised by Louis Pasteur. He inactivated the spinal cords of infected rabbits via sun exposure and then inoculated them into humans. Despite its initial success, this approach suffered from an erratic inactivation procedure, which sometimes triggered rabies in the vaccinated individuals, and there were substantial variations among different vaccine batches.5 As cell culture technology progressed, the in vitro cultivation of viruses became more refined. Whole-virus rabies vaccines, which are inactivated using agents such as β-propiolactone or formaldehyde, are currently widely employed. Presently, the majority of approved vaccines are of the inactivated whole-virus variety. However, they are constrained by feeble immunogenicity, necessitating multiple doses, along with notable production risks and high costs.6 In middle- and low-income nations, the exorbitant costs of post-exposure prophylaxis and the intricate immunization regimens frequently result in postponed treatments and suboptimal compliance.7

Live attenuated rabies vaccines possess remarkable immunogenicity that can trigger both humoral and cellular immune reactions. Nevertheless, their residual virulence and the risk of reversion mutations raise safety issues. At present, there is a huge need for novel rabies vaccines that exhibit potent immunogenicity, require fewer doses, have a rapid onset of action, and are cost-effective. The recent progress in molecular biology has given rise to new vaccine platforms, bringing optimism for the creation of innovative rabies vaccines.8 Among these emerging platforms, viral vector vaccine technology presents multiple benefits. It has the capacity to express heterologous antigens, which is essential for the development of vaccines against highly pathogenic agents such as the rabies virus.6 Moreover, viral vector vaccines are inherently strongly immunogenic, and the molecular patterns they express can elicit innate immune responses without the need for adjuvants.9 Additionally, as a complete virus particle, it can prompt the body to generate specific humoral and cellular immune responses. These characteristics endow viral vectors with tremendous potential for vaccine development.

The idea of viral vector vaccines originated from the insertion of the hepatitis B surface antigen gene into a modified vaccinia virus.10 During the COVID-19 pandemic, numerous adenovirus-based COVID-19 vaccines have exhibited outstanding immunogenicity and safety in real-world applications.11 In fact, prior to the pandemic of COVID-19, the European Medicines Agency and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved the VSV-based Ebola vaccine rVSV-ZEBOV in 2019, thus making VSV the first viral vector that received approval as a prophylactic vaccine for use in humans.12 According to WHO data, during the Ebola outbreak in Congo, nearly 100,000 people were administered the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine, which showed an efficacy rate of 97.5% and a favorable safety record.13 These results suggest that VSV vector vaccines hold substantial application value and development potential in the field of vaccinology.

The G protein of the rabies virus (RABV) serves as the primary target for inducing the production of neutralizing antibodies.14 Furthermore, RABV represents a particularly compelling target for rVSV-based vaccine development. As both RABV and VSV belong to the Rhabdoviridae family, there is an expectation that the heterologous RABV glycoprotein will be structurally compatible and expressed in a cognate fashion within the rVSV platform. In our study, we developed a recombinant replication-competent vaccine, VSV-RABV, by replacing the native VSV-G gene with the RABV-G gene. A high-titer mutant strain of rVSV-RABV, containing an L71S mutation in the extracellular domain of RABV-G, was obtained through adaptive passage in BHK-21 cells. This mutation markedly enhanced the expression of RABV-G and the viral titer. Subsequently, via reverse genetic modification, the L71S mutation was directly incorporated into rVSV-RABV, named rVSV-RABV-L71S, and its functionality was further validated, aligning with the results of the adaptive passage. Inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S exhibited favorable immunogenicity in both mice and dogs. Specifically, inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S induced stronger neutralizing antibodies in mice compared to commercial rabies vaccines. Similarly, live rVSV-RABV-L71S also demonstrated potent immunogenicity in dogs, as a single immunization could induce high-titer neutralizing antibodies against CVS-11 live virus in dogs, which were significantly stronger than those induced by commercial rabies vaccines. In addition, mice inoculated with live rVSV-RABV-L71S successfully resisted the challenge of the CVS-24 strain, and its NIH vaccine potency was approximately five times higher than that of the reference vaccine. Finally, both inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and live rVSV-RABV-L71S possess good safety profiles.

Results

The construction and characterization of a rabies vaccine based on the VSV vector

For the construction of a recombinant VSV vaccine harboring the RABV-G protein, the RABV-G gene from the SAD-B19 strain (GenBank: M31046.1) was subjected to codon optimization to enhance expression and then cloned into the plasmid pVSV-FL+(2), replacing the native VSV-G gene, as illustrated in Figure 1A. Through the process of virus packaging and rescue, we acquired the recombinant vaccine strain rVSV-RABV. The purified rVSV-RABV exhibited the characteristic morphology of a classic rhabdovirus, similar to rVSV-WT, as shown in Figure 1B. Moreover, the results of electron microscopy with gold-conjugated antibody labeling of VSV particles indicated that rVSV-RABV correctly displayed the RABV-G protein on the particle surface, as seen in Figure 1C. Subsequently, we further examined the virological characteristics of rVSV-RABV. SDS-PAGE and western blot analyses revealed the characteristic protein bands of the virus, namely VSV-L, VSV-N, VSV-P, VSV-M, and either RABV-G (67 kDa) or VSV-G. Notably, rVSV-RABV exhibited the specific band of RABV-G, as shown in Figures 1F and 1G. Additionally, to assess the growth kinetics of rVSV-RABV, the virus present in the cell supernatant was quantified using a viral plaque assay at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-infection of BHK-21 cells. The titer peaked at 48 h post-infection for rVSV-RABV, as depicted in Figure 1I. Meanwhile, the plaques formed by rVSV-RABV were smaller than those formed by rVSV-WT, as shown in Figure 1D. We also tested the RABV-G protein using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and the results suggested that rVSV-RABV correctly expressed the RABV-G protein, as shown in Figure 1H. Overall, we have successfully constructed a recombinant VSV carrying the RABV-G protein.

The leucine-to-serine mutation at position 71 of RABV-G in rVSV-RABV significantly increased viral titer and RABV-G protein expression

In the development of viral vaccines, the directed evolution of vaccine strains represents a prevalent approach, with one such method being the continuous adaptive passage of viruses within specific cell lines. Numerous studies have indicated that the virus titer tends to increase progressively with the increase in passage numbers within host cells.15,16,17 Furthermore, the rabies vaccine produced in BHK-21 cells has been in use for a long period of time and has been demonstrated to be effective and safe.18 Accordingly, we undertook the adaptive passage of rVSV-RABV in BHK-21 cells, as depicted in Figure 2A. It was observed that the cytopathic effect induced by virus infection was markedly intensified when rVSV-RABV underwent adaptive passage up to 12 passages. Subsequently, rVSV-RABV was continuously passaged for a total of 24 passages, and rVSV-RABV-P0, P12, and P24 were selected for further virological characteristic analysis. Initially, the viral plaque titer was evaluated. It is noteworthy that compared with BHK-21 cells, Vero cells were selected for plaque titration assays due to their suitable cell morphology, growth rate, and adherent monolayer stability. It was found that the plaque titers of rVSV-RABV-P12 and P24 exceeded those of P0. Although no statistically significant difference was detected in the plaque titers between P0 and P12, the titer of P12 was nonetheless approximately 3.5-fold higher than that of P0 (p = 0.0543). Moreover, the titer of P24 was significantly greater than that of P0 by over 7-fold (p = 0.0152), as illustrated in Figure 2C. Concurrently, the plaques formed by P12 and P24 were visibly larger than those produced by P0, implying an enhanced ability of the virus to replicate in Vero-E6 cells, as shown in Figure 2B. Additionally, the cell supernatants of BHK-21 cells infected with rVSV-RABV-P0, P12, or P24 at an MOI of 0.1 for 48 h were analyzed via ELISA. As presented in Figure 2D, the expression of the RABV-G protein in P12 and P24 was substantially higher than that in P0. To investigate the root cause underlying the alterations in the biological characteristics of the virus following adaptive passage, we used next-generation sequencing technology to identify the adaptive virus. By comparing the sequencing outcomes, it was revealed that the leucine at position 71 within the extracellular domain of the RABV-G protein was mutated to serine (L71S) on rVSV-RABV-P12, yet this mutation was not present on rVSV-RABV-P0. Moreover, the L71S mutation could still be detected in rVSV-RABV-P24, suggesting that L71S constitutes a stable genetic mutation (Figure 2E).

Subsequently, in order to verify the function of the L71S mutation on RABV-G, we proceeded to construct a novel recombinant rVSV-RABV carrying the L71S mutation on RABV-G (denoted as rVSV-RABV-L71S) (Figure 2G). Through cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) technology, the RABV-G protein displayed on the surface of rVSV-RABV-L71S could be clearly visualized (Figure 2K). Furthermore, rVSV-RABV-L71S exhibited a stronger capacity for plaque formation compared to rVSV-RABV (Figure 2H). In a similar vein, we examined the growth curves of rVSV-RABV-L71S and rVSV-RABV. Analogously, the virus titer of rVSV-RABV-L71S was noticeably higher than that of rVSV-RABV at each time point of 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Additionally, the expression level of the RABV-G protein was also higher for rVSV-RABV-L71S at 24, 48, and 72 h in comparison with rVSV-RABV, as depicted in Figures 2I and 2J.

The function of the L71S mutation was further elaborated by means of packaging a VSV-based pseudovirus. 293T cells were transfected with either the RABV-G-L71S or RABV-G plasmids and were subsequently infected with VSV-eGFP-ΔG. Twenty-four hours later, the cell suspension was collected and lysed. Through Western blot analysis, it was found that the expression of the RABV-G protein was significantly enhanced in the cells transfected with the RABV-G-L71S plasmid when contrasted with those transfected with the RABV-G plasmid (Figure 2F). Collectively, these results obtained from reverse-genetic modification were in line with those from the adaptive passage of the virus, indicating that we had indeed identified a functional L71S mutation that could markedly increase viral titers as well as the expression level of the RABV-G protein of rVSV-RABV.

Subsequently, with the aim of elucidating the rationale behind the virological property alterations induced by the L71S mutation in RABV-G, we carried out a preliminary structural examination of the 71st amino acid within the extracellular domain of the CVS-11 strain19 and the Pasteur strain,20 leveraging published data. Excluding the signal peptide, the 71st amino acid in the extracellular region corresponds to the 52nd position. Structural analysis reveals that residue 52 is situated within the G protein conformation. The substitution of leucine with serine at this site potentially compromises the stability of the pre-G protein, consequently influencing the infectivity of RABV-52S (Figure S1). We surmised that this phenomenon would exert a comparable effect on the SAD-B19 strain employed in our study, which might constitute one of the factors contributing to the elevation in virus titer and G protein expression following the L71S mutation. The cryo-ET density map has been deposited in the Electron Microscopy DataBank under the accession codes EMD-66217 and EMD-66218.

The inactivated recombinational VSV vaccine carrying RABV-G protein is highly immunogenic in mice

The RABV-G-specific neutralizing antibody serves as a crucial parameter for evaluating the effectiveness of rabies vaccines. The VSV-based virus neutralization system holds significant application potential.21,22 In a prior investigation, we developed a neutralization assay based on the SARS-CoV-2 S-protein-bearing VSV pseudovirus and ACE2-overexpressing BHK-21 cells. The serum neutralization titers of patients with COVID-19 convalescent determined by this assay exhibited a strong correlation with those obtained from the wild-type SARS-CoV-2 neutralization assay.22 Leveraging our previous experience, we endeavored to establish a neutralization system predicated on the RABV-G protein (SAD-B19)-bearing VSV pseudovirus and BHK-21 cells (Figure 3A). Subsequently, we examined the correlation between pseudovirus neutralization and wild-type rabies virus CVS-11 neutralization. Mouse serum from Balb/c mice immunized with the inactivated rVSV-RABV vaccine and human serum from volunteers inoculated with the inactivated rabies vaccine were employed for correlation analyses. The inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with GEL-02 adjuvant elicited potent humoral, cellular, and innate immune responses in mice

We hope to further explore the preliminary mechanism of the inactivated vaccine inducing an immune response. The germinal center (GC) response is critical for the generation of affinity-matured plasma cells and memory B cells capable of mediating long-term protective immunity.23 Therefore, we first evaluated the response of GC stimulated by inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S. Inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S or inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with GEL-02 adjuvant immunized C57BL/6J mice with 1.0 × 10ˆ7 PFU/100 μL, and then the inguinal lymph nodes at 3 and 7 days after immunization and the spleen at 28 days after immunization were taken for flow cytometry analysis, Figure 4A. Inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with GEL-02 adjuvant significantly induced a stronger GC response at 7 days (p = 0.0168) and partially enhanced plasma cell proliferation, Figure 4B, in addition, a higher proportion of PC were also detected at 28 days of immunization (Figure S2B). These results are consistent with specific neutralizing antibodies induced by rVSV-RABV-L71S, Figures 3E and 3F. Next, specific T cell responses induced by the inactivated vaccine in mice were evaluated. The spleens of mice that had received inactivated vaccine were collected 7 days after a single-dose immunization and also 7 days after a two-dose immunization for IFN-γ ELISPOT analysis, as illustrated in Figure 4A. It was observed that inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with GEL-02 adjuvant elicited a stronger specific T cell response compared to the MOCK group, regardless of whether it was 7 days after a single dose or 7 days after two doses, as depicted in Figures 4E and 4F. Next, in order to explore the type of specific T cell response induced by inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with GEL-02 adjuvant, we analyzed the expression of T cell cytokines in the spleen using flow cytometry. We measured the specific levels of IL-4 and IFN-γ in both CD4 and CD8 T cells at 7 days and 28 days post-immunization. The results demonstrated that in the early stages of immunity, the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant seemed to induce stronger CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells (approximately twice the proportion of the MOCK group). Moreover, compared to the MOCK group, the proportions of CD4+IL-4+, CD8+IL-4+, and CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells were 1.61, 1.78, and 1.59 times higher, respectively, as shown in Figure S4B. After enhanced immunity, the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant significantly induced stronger CD4+IFN-γ+ and CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells (p = 0.0156 and 0.0066, respectively), while there was no significant difference in the IL-4+ T cells, as presented in Figure 4G. These results suggest that the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant is more likely to induce a Th1-type-biased immune response.

Further, we examined some innate immune cell subsets. The inguinal lymph nodes at 3 and 7 days after immunization and the spleen at 28 days after immunization were taken for flow cytometry analysis (Figure 4A). We discovered that both inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S alone and the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S with GEL-02 adjuvant were able to trigger the activation of macrophage co-stimulatory signals, namely CD80 and CD86, at 3 days after immunization, as shown in Figures 3C and S3B. Simultaneously, following enhanced immunity, both inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and its combination with GEL-02 adjuvant induced the proliferation of macrophages and neutrophils (p = 0.0156 and 0.0125, respectively), as presented in Figures 4D and S3C. These experimental findings imply that the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant have the capacity to activate innate immunity. In particular, it can activate and promote the proliferation of innate immune cells. This activation is beneficial for the rapid dissemination of vaccine antigens within the body and enables these antigens to quickly engage in the adaptive immune response.

In summary, we conducted a preliminary exploration of the immune mechanism induced by inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and its combination with GEL-02 adjuvant. With regard to adaptive immunity, the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant is capable of inducing a GC response. Moreover, it can also trigger a specific T cell response that exhibits a Th1-type bias. In terms of innate immunity, the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant initiates the activation of macrophage co-stimulatory signals such as CD80 and CD86. Additionally, it promotes the proliferation of macrophages and neutrophils. These actions within the innate immune system are essential, as they help to set the stage for the subsequent activation of the adaptive immune response.

Immunogenicity of live rVSV-RABV-L71S in dogs and mice

Inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S demonstrated favorable immunogenicity in mice, which led us to further investigate the immunogenicity of live rVSV-RABV-L71S in these animals and also explore its protective effect against CVS-24 challenge in mice. For the experiment in dogs, live rVSV-RABV-L71S or inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S+GEL-02 adjuvant was administered to dogs via subcutaneous injection at a dose of 1.0 × 10ˆ8 PFU/1 mL. As a control, the commercial inactivated rabies vaccine was given to dogs at a volume of 1 mL, as depicted in Figure 5A. We observed that immunizing dogs with a single dose of live rVSV-RABV-L71S was capable of inducing the production of high-titer neutralizing antibodies against wild-type CVS-11. Merely 7 days after immunization, the induced neutralizing antibody titer exceeded the WHO-recommended neutralizing antibody titer of 0.5 IU/mL by approximately 17 times. Moreover, the peak value of the neutralizing antibody titer reached an average of around 40 IU/mL. Even 60 days after immunization, antibody levels remained relatively high, with an average of 30 IU/mL. Additionally, during the entire observation period, the neutralizing antibody titers in the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group were significantly higher than those in the commercial inactivated rabies vaccine group; the geometric mean titer increased by 2.7- to 21.9-fold. rVSV-RABV-L71S exhibits a stronger replication capacity and a higher level of G protein expression compared to the wild-type strain, as shown in Figures 2I and 2J. This characteristic endows it with a significant advantage in the production process, enabling us to enhance the immunogenicity of the vaccine by increasing the immune dose. Consequently, we referred to the NIH inactivated rabies vaccine titer detection method to assess the vaccine titer of live rVSV-RABV-L71S at high immune doses. Live rVSV-RABV-L71S was diluted to a concentration of 4.0 × 10ˆ8 PFU/mL and then subjected to a 5-fold gradient dilution, resulting in a total of four gradients, as illustrated in Figure 5D. Similarly, the inactivated rabies vaccines, which served as the reference vaccine with known vaccine titers (11.4 IU/mL, purchased from the China Institute of Food and Drug Control), were diluted in a similar way, as depicted in Figure 5F. KM mice were intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mL of the vaccine from each gradient for 0 days and 21 days. Subsequently, on 28 days, the mice were intracranially inoculated with the CVS-24 strain at a dose of 50 LD50/0.3 mL, as shown in Figure 5C. Meanwhile, CVS-24 (50 LD50/0.03 mL) was serially diluted 10-fold with PBS, and four dilutions (100, 10−1, 10−2, 10−3) were used to inoculate 10 mice in each dilution group (with 0.03 mL per mouse), serving as the virus backdrop control, as presented in Figures S6A and S6B.

Firstly, the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group achieved complete protection against CVS-24 challenge at 5−1 and 5−3 dilutions, with a 100% survival rate among the mice. Secondly, at the same dilutions (5−2, 5−3, and 5−4), the survival rates in the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group were higher than those in the reference vaccine group (95% and 85%, 100% and 50%, and 75% and 45%, respectively), as demonstrated in Figures 5D and 5F. By fitting the NIH vaccine titers, it was found that the vaccine titers of live rVSV-RABV-L71S exceeded those of the reference vaccine by approximately 4.8 times, as shown in Figure 5E.

In fact, when KM mice were immunized with a dose of 2.0 × 10ˆ8 PFU per injection via intraperitoneal injection, this rapidly induced the production of specific neutralizing antibodies, and the antibody titers continued to increase over the course of 28 days, as depicted in Figures S5A and S5B.

We further investigated the histopathological conditions as well as the distribution of the RABV-G protein in the dead mice from the virus backdrop group and the surviving mice in the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group 14 days after the challenge. In the dead mice of the virus backdrop group, when examined by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, moderate to severe neuronal cell necrosis was observed in the brain tissue. Additionally, there was infiltration of a variable number of glial cells, ranging from a few to a large quantity, into the interstitium. The optic neuron phenomenon, or vascular mantle, was also noticed. Moreover, through immunohistochemistry (IHC), the presence of RABV was detected, as shown in Figure 5G(c, d). On the other hand, in the surviving mice of the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group, no significant pathological changes were observed in the brain tissue in most cases. However, in some instances, mild neuronal cell necrosis was detected. A small amount of glial cell infiltration was observed in the interstitium. Through IHC, the result for RABV was negative, as depicted in Figure 5G(a, b). These data collectively indicate that live rVSV-RABV-L71S possesses good immunogenicity in mice and is capable of protecting mice from the CVS-24 challenge.

The inactivated or live rVSV-RABV-L71S is highly safe in mice

The aforementioned experiments have demonstrated that rVSV-RABV-L71S exhibits good immunogenicity in both mice and dogs. Ultimately, we explored the safety aspects of both the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S vaccine and the live rVSV-RABV-L71S vaccine. It is known that the pathogenicity of VSV is largely due to VSV-G. Utilizing heterologous glycoproteins to substitute VSV-G not only enables the expression of exogenous glycoproteins on the viral surface but also serves to attenuate VSV. For instance, the recombinant VSV vector Ebola virus or Marburg virus attenuated live vaccine constructed in the study by Jones, Steven M., et al. demonstrated a favorable safety profile when administered intramuscularly in non-human primates.24 Furthermore, the VSV vector-based Ebola vaccine, rVSV-EBOV, was found to be safe and well-tolerated following intramuscular vaccination in clinical trials (NCT02283099, NCT02287480, and NCT02296983). These data collectively suggest that vaccines based on the VSV vector are generally safe.

In our study, we evaluated the safety of both the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and the live rVSV-RABV-L71S. Balb/c mice were administered a single injection of 1.0 × 10ˆ7 PFU/100 μL via intramuscular injection. In the live rVSV-RABV-L71S group, the body weight of the mice decreased briefly after immunization. However, it then increased rapidly and almost regained the initial body weight 15 days after immunization. In the case of the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S group, the body weight of the mice completely recovered to the initial level 9 days after immunization. Notably, the survival rate of the mice was 100% throughout the observation period, regardless of whether they received the live vaccine or the inactivated vaccine, as depicted in Figures 6A and 6C. The contents of serum ALT and AST in both the inactivated and live vaccine groups were similar to those in the MOCK group on days 1, 3, and 7 after immunization. Collectively, the above data suggest that rVSV-RABV-L71S demonstrates good safety characteristics in both its inactivated and live vaccine formulations.

Discussion

VSV indeed presents multiple advantages when serving as a vaccine vector. Firstly, its genome is characterized by high stability and flexibility. This feature provides substantial potential for modifications, making genetic engineering operations relatively straightforward. Scientists can easily introduce or alter specific genes within the VSV genome to achieve desired functions or characteristics for vaccine development, which is a crucial aspect in tailoring the vector to carry various antigens effectively. Secondly, the transcription and translation processes of VSV genes take place in the cytoplasm. This cytoplasmic localization eliminates the concern of the viral genetic material integrating into the host genome. For instance, compared to some other viral vectors where integration risks could potentially lead to the mutagenesis or activation of oncogenes in the host cells, VSV’s cytoplasmic activity significantly enhances its safety profile, giving it an edge in vaccine vector selection.25 Moreover, VSV has a remarkable replication speed. Just 2–3 h after infection, viral protein expression and the production of progeny virus can already be observed. And within a short span of 24 h, high-titer progeny virus can be detected. This rapid replication ability not only enables the quick production of the vaccine vector itself but also ensures a sufficient quantity of the vector carrying the antigen for immunization purposes. In addition, VSV has the capacity to infect and replicate in a diverse range of mammalian cells. This broad tropism simplifies the production process of high-titer virus, as it can grow well in different cell lines commonly used in biotechnological manufacturing. Crucially, in contrast to adenoviruses that have a high prevalence of pre-existing immunity in human populations, VSV has a relatively low seropositivity rate. Typically, it mainly infects individuals who are associated with laboratory work26 or have frequent contact with animals.27,28,29 This low pre-existing immunity in the general population means that when used as a vaccine vector, it is less likely to be neutralized by pre-existing antibodies in the body before it can elicit an immune response against the target antigen, thereby improving the effectiveness of the vaccine. These favorable attributes collectively make VSV a highly viable candidate as a vaccine vector. Besides the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine, VSV-based vaccines targeting other pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2, Zika virus, and Lassa virus have also shown promising immunogenicity and safety profiles in preclinical studies.30,31,32 In the current study, we replaced the VSV-G gene with the RABV membrane protein G gene from strain SAD-B19 to construct the recombinant rabies vaccine strain rVSV-RABV (Figure 1A). BHK-21 is an acceptable cell line for vaccine production, so it was chosen as the production host.33 Through the adaptive passage in BHK-21 cells and reverse the genetic modification of rVSV-RABV, we discovered and verified that after the leucine at position 71 in the extracellular region of RABV-G was mutated into serine, the titer of rVSV-RABV and the expression level of the RABV-G protein were significantly increased.

We conducted retrieval of the G protein gene sequences of the existing rabies vaccine strains that are used either for human or veterinary purposes. Through this process, we were able to identify two distinct types of strains, differentiated by having either leucine or serine at amino acid position 71 within the extracellular region of the G protein. The ERA strain, the SRV9 strain, and the SAD-B19 strain that were utilized in our present study all share a common origin, which is the SAD strain. The SAD strain has been continuously passaged through animals or cells, resulting in the formation of these strains. Interestingly, throughout this process, the amino acid at position 71 of the G protein has consistently remained leucine. This finding implies that the L71S mutation we came across might potentially represent a unique amino acid mutation site, at least when considering the strains that are derived from the SAD strain. Additionally, the amino acid at position 71 of the G protein in strains such as BD06, CTN-1, Flury-LEP, and PV is serine, indicating the polymorphism of this site. Subsequently, we delved into some of the currently published data. For instance, the Bali strain34 was serially passaged in BHK-21 cells, while the CTN-1 strain35 underwent serial passaging in chicken embryo cells. In both cases, the final infectious titers obtained were lower than those of rVSV-RABV-L71S. However, it remains uncertain whether the culture advantage exhibited by rVSV-RABV-L71S is more attributable to the VSV vector itself or to the L71S mutation. This aspect calls for further in-depth investigation. Furthermore, the specific functionality of the L71S mutation within the rabies virus also warrants additional exploration. Regrettably, due to the constraints imposed by our experimental conditions, we were unable to carry out this part of the work. This inability constitutes one of the limitations of our current study.

Furthermore, an exploration into the immunogenicity of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S was conducted in mice. The results indicated that there was no significant difference in immunogenicity among rVSV-RABV-P0, rVSV-RABV-P24, and rVSV-RABV-L71S. This suggests that the L71S mutation did not affect the immunogenicity of the vaccine strain, Figures 3D and 3F. Compared with the commercially inactivated rabies vaccine, the combination of rVSV-RABV-L71S and the GE-02 adjuvant exhibits stronger immunogenicity, whether in the early or late stages of immunization (Figures 3E and 3F). Furthermore, a single-dose immunization of dogs with the combination of rVSV-RABV-L71S and the GEL-02 adjuvant also induced high-titer neutralizing antibodies that persisted for 60 days (Figures 5A and 5B). These results demonstrate that the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant possesses favorable immunogenicity in both mice and dogs.

The promising immunogenicity of the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S spurred us to explore the mechanisms underlying its immune response. The combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant can effectively induce the formation of germinal centers. This may contribute to its efficient induction of neutralizing antibodies (Figures 4A, 4B, and S2B).

Furthermore, the pathogen-associated molecular patterns presented by the viral vector possess the capacity to effectively trigger innate immune responses.36 We found the activation of macrophage co-stimulatory signals CD80 and CD86, respectively (at 3 days after single-dose immunization), as well as the proliferation of macrophages and neutrophils (at 7 days after two-dose immunization). These results indicate that the combination of rVSV-RABV-L71S and GEL-02 adjuvant can effectively activate innate immunity.

T cell responses play a crucial role in eliminating the rabies virus from the central nervous system, with the Th1-biased response being of particular importance. The ELISPOT assay in mice showed that the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S combined with the GEL-02 adjuvant induced stronger IFN-γ+ T cell responses both 7 days after a single immunization and 7 days after a two-dose immunization (Figures 4E and 4F). Through detailed flow cytometry analysis, we observed a significant enhancement in CD4+IFN-γ+ T cell responses and CD8+IFN-γ+ T cell responses. These findings suggest that the combination of inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S and the GEL-02 adjuvant tends to promote a Th1-type immune response.

The attenuated live rabies vaccine shows robust immunogenicity; nevertheless, it brings about substantial safety issues.37 Viral vector vaccines can effectively maintain the potent immunogenicity of live vaccines while resolving the safety concerns related to live rabies vaccines. Therefore, we further explored the immunogenicity of the live rVSV-RABV-L71S. In both mice and dogs, the live rVSV-RABV-L71S demonstrated strong immunogenicity, promptly inducing high titers of wild-type CVS-11 neutralizing antibodies within 7 days after a single dose and reaching a peak around 28 days post-immunization.

One of the problems with the current inactivated rabies vaccine is that it requires multiple doses of immunization to generate sufficient neutralizing antibodies to safeguard the body. A single dose of live rVSV-RABV-L71S can produce a rapid and potent neutralizing antibody response, which can substantially reduce the number of immunizations, simplify the immunization process, and promote the vaccine’s application. Moreover, in further mouse challenge experiments, the live rVSV-RABV-L71S provided effective protection against viral challenge, with the NIH titer approximately five times higher than that of the reference vaccine.

In fact, many scholars have used viral vectors to construct live vaccines against rabies. Zhang, Ya-Nan et al. developed an attenuated live rabies vaccine based on the Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, designated VEEV-RABV-G. This vaccine is highly attenuated and provides substantial protective immunity against the lethal RABV challenge.38 Previously, rabies vaccines based on the VSV vector have been documented in the literature. In the study conducted by Liang, Minglong et al., a replicating recombinant vaccine, namely VSV-RABVG, was developed. Through intranasal immunization, this vaccine induced high levels of neutralizing antibody titers in mice and effectively safeguarded them against lethal infections caused by the Mexican dog rabies virus (DRV). However, it was noted that significant weight loss occurred in mice when they were administered intranasal doses of 1.0 × 10ˆ7 plaque-forming units (PFUs) and 1.0 × 10ˆ6 PFU.39 Moreover, the replication-deficient rabies vaccine developed by Park, Jung-Eun et al., which utilized the VSV vector, was able to induce specific antibodies against RABV in mice. Nevertheless, its immunogenicity was found to be inferior to that of the inactivated rabies vaccine, and the study did not present further results regarding its protective challenge efficacy.40 In our research, the live rVSV-RABV-L71S demonstrated favorable safety when administered via intramuscular injection at a dose of 1.0 × 10ˆ7 PFU. This finding implies that the meticulous consideration of the immunization route for live vaccines is indeed necessary. Additionally, when it comes to viral vector-based rabies vaccines, the choice between constructing a replication-deficient recombinant virus or a replicating recombinant virus might be a crucial factor that exerts an impact on immunogenicity.

The experimental data presented above demonstrate that rVSV-RABV-L71S exhibits a high level of immunogenicity, irrespective of whether it is administered in an inactivated or live vaccine form. Subsequently, we evaluated the safety profiles of both vaccine modalities. Upon the immunization of Balb/c mice, no mortality was detected within a 15-day time frame, and the body weights of the mice were fully recovered. Hematological analysis disclosed that the live vaccine elicited transient aberrations during the early post-immunization phase, which returned to normal by the 7 day. The hematological parameters for the inactivated vaccine were similar to those of the MOCK group. Measurements of ALT and AST levels verified that neither the live nor the inactivated vaccine induced liver damage in the mice.

In contrast to studies such as Liang et al.39 where intranasal immunization with a VSV-vectored rabies vaccine induced significant weight loss in mice—suggesting potential neurotropism-related risks associated with mucosal delivery of live vectors—mice receiving intramuscular inoculation of our vaccine exhibited only transient and self-limiting weight reduction without manifestations of neurological impairment (e.g., locomotor deficits, seizures, or aberrant behaviors). This safety profile aligns with clinical evaluations of the VSV-vectored Ebola vaccine (Ervebo),41 wherein no significant neurotoxicity signals were documented via the IM route. Nevertheless, comprehensive neurotoxicity assessment for live VSV-vectored vaccines—including intracranial challenge models and histopathological characterization of neural tissues—remains critical. The absence of such dedicated analyses constitutes a limitation of the present study. Besides, the evaluation of viremia in dogs vaccinated with live attenuated vaccines to assess the potential for neural invasion remains highly relevant. While we recognize the scientific merit of such experiments, our current ability to conduct this assessment is constrained by limitations in animal resource availability, precluding us from performing these analyses at this stage.

Overall, by utilizing the VSV vector, we constructed a recombinant rabies vaccine carrying a functional point mutation, L71S. The L71S mutation leads to the enhancement of the virus replication ability and the expression of RABV-G protein in the recombinant vaccine strain. Compared with the original strain before mutation, we can produce a recombinant rabies vaccine with good immunogenicity at a lower cost, which will contribute to the popularization of low-cost rabies vaccines in developing countries in the future.

Limitations of the study

It remains unclear whether the observed culture advantage of rVSV-RABV-L71S is primarily attributed to the VSV vector itself or to the L71S mutation, a distinction that requires further investigation. Furthermore, the specific functional role of the L71S mutation within the rabies virus glycoprotein remains to be elucidated. Last, comprehensive neurotoxicity profiling of live VSV-vectored vaccines is imperative. Collectively, these aspects constitute limitations of the current research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

BHK-21 cells (CCL-10) and Vero-E6 cells (CRL-1586) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), while 293T cells were kindly provided by Dr. Jiahuai Han. All these cell lines were cultivated at 37°C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (of Australian origin, catalog number 10099141), HEPES (catalog number 15630-080), and sodium pyruvate (catalog number S410JV). BHK-21 cells were utilized for virus production and the VSV-pseudovirus neutralization assay. Vero-E6 cells served in plaque titration studies and Immunofluorescence analysis. 293T cells were employed for viral packaging. No mycoplasma contamination was detected in any of the cells. For long-term storage, all cell lines were cryopreserved at -80°C and in liquid nitrogen utilizing a cell freezing medium composed of 90% serum and 10% DMSO, following a gradual freezing protocol.

Method details

Plasmids

Professor Scripps Juan Karos generously provided the plasmids pBS-VSV-N, pBS-VSV-P, pBS-VSV-L, and pBS-VSV-G. Our laboratory constructed the pVSV-FL-RABVG (SAD-B19) plasmid. The recombinant plasmids encoding G protein of rabies virus SAD-B19, SAD-B19-L71S and G protein of VSV were cloned into eukaryotic expression plasmid pCAG after codon optimization (Addgene).

The construction and adaptive passage of replication-competent rVSV-RABV

293T cells were utilized for rVSV-RABV packaging. In summary, 0.001% poly-L-lysine was added to the 6-well plate. Next, 293T cells were infected with rVv-T7 poxvirus (MOI=5, generously provided by Professor Scripps Juan Karos). A mixture of OPTI-MEM and plasmids containing pBS-VSV-N, pBS-VSV-P, pBS-VSV-G, pBS-VSV-L, pBS-VSV-M, and pVSV-FL-RABVG (SAD-B19) was then introduced into the cells. Following this, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS. When the cytopathic effects exceeding 90%, the supernatant was collected and filtered using a 0·22 μm filter and stored at -80°C.

For viral rescue, BHK-21 cells were pre-seeded, and the plasmid pCAG-VSV-G was transfected into these cells. Afterward, the virus, post-filtration, was added to the BHK-21 cells. Six hours post-infection, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 5% FBS. The supernatant, showing cytopathic effects exceeding 90% and typically within 48-72 hours, was collected, stored at -80°C, and marked as P0.

Viral adaptive passage involved seeding BHK-21 cells in a six-well plate and incubating them under a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C for 16-20 hours. Then the virus was diluted with serum-free DMEM at a ratio of 1:200, followed by 5-fold gradient dilution, taking 0·5 mL of virus under each gradient to infect cells for 1·5 hours. After adding DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS to support cell growth, the supernatant was collected once cytopathic effects exceeded 90%. The virus obtained was labeled as P1 and used for subsequent infections.

The construction of VSV based pseudovirus

VSV-based pseudoviruses expressing the G proteins of SAD-B19 (GenBank: M31046.1), were created as detailed previously.22 Briefly, these plasmids were transfected into 293T cells, and following 24 hours, the VSV-eGFP-ΔG (Addgene, 31842) was inoculated into cells. Anti-VSV-G rat serum diluted in 3% FBS DMEM was employed to inhibit the infectivity of residual VSV-eGFP-ΔG post infection 1·5 hours. The cell supernatant containing VSV-based pseudoviruses was collected approximately 15 hours later.

The production, purification and inactivation of rVSV

BHK-21 cells were utilized for rVSV production, seeded at a density between 2·0-3·0 x 10ˆ5 cells/ mL in a cell factory and incubated under a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C for 16-20 hours. Following incubation, the medium was discarded and the cells were washed at least twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, rVSV was inoculated into the cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0·1 in serum-free DMEM for a 1·5-hour infection period, after which DMEM supplemented with HEPES and 5% FBS was added.

The supernatant was collected and harvested for purification when more than 90% of cytopathic effect were observed, generally within 48-72 hours post-infection, and centrifuged at 2,300 g for 30 minutes at 4°C to eliminate cell debris. Following centrifugation, the virus-containing supernatant underwent dialysis in PBS with 5% sorbitol overnight, buffer exchanged thrice. After dialysis, the harvest was passed through a 0·22 μm filter to clarify the virus, subsequently purified using ligand-activated core chromatography with Capto Core 700 (Cytiva & GE, 17548102). The equilibration and elution buffers comprised PBS with 5% sorbitol and a blend of 1M NaOH with 30% isopropanol, respectively. The virus was then concentrated via tangential flow filtration (VIVAFLOW 200 100,000 MWCO HYDROSART, VF20H4) and subjected to aseptic filtration before application.

For rVSV inactivation, beta-propiolactone was introduced to the virus at a 1:4500 ratio and incubated at 4°C for 48 hours with gentle agitation to ensure thorough inactivation. The process concluded with a 2-hour beta-propiolactone hydrolysis in an electric water bath at 37°C. The inactivated virus was stored at 4°C.

The plaque titration

For plaque titration, Vero-E6 cells were seeded at a density of 2·0-3·0 x 10ˆ5 cells/mL in a six-well plate and incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C for 16-20 hours. We diluted the virus in 2% FBS DMEM at a 1:200 ratio, followed by a five-fold serial dilution, and infected the Vero-E6 cells with 500 μL of the virus per well for 1·5 hours. After ensuring thorough infection using a shaker, we added 3 mL of 5% FBS DMEM containing 1·5% agarose to the cells. Once the agarose had solidified, we inverted the six-well plate and incubated it for a 48-hour cultivation period. To immobilize the plaques, we added 3 mL of 4% formaldehyde to the wells for at least 3 hours before gently removing the formaldehyde and especially the agarose. We then stained the cells with a 1% crystal violet solution for 20-40 minutes and washed the plate with water. The titer was determined by multiplying the dilution factor by the number of plaque spots and doubling the result to express it in Plaque Forming Units per milliliter (PFU/mL).

Immunofluorescence assay

Vero-E6 cells were used for the immunofluorescence assay. Briefly, previously seeded Vero-E6 cells in a 96-well plate were infected with the virus at a suitable dilution. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0·1% Triton-X100. The 4% BSA solution (in PBS) was used to block the plate to reduce non-specific binding. We then added the anti-RABV-G monoclonal antibody 22C6 and the Anti-mouse IgG (H+L), F(ab')2 Fragment (Alexa Fluor® 647 Conjugate, Cat.No.4410S) to the plate sequentially. DAPI was used to stain the cell nuclei. Phenix or Operetta CLS equipment and the Columbus system (PerkinElmer) were utilized to acquire and analyze the fluorescence images.

Next Generation Sequencing

Next Generation Sequencing involved using a viral DNA/RNA extraction kit (magnetic beads method, NA007-3) to isolate viral RNA, which was then converted into cDNA through reverse transcription using AMV reverse transcriptase (M5108L). The cDNA was amplified by PCR with PrimeSTAR® GXL DNA Polymerase (R050A), and the resulting PCR products were prepared for Next Generation Sequencing. The DNA fragments were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis.

SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting

For SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting, the lysis of samples, facilitated by reducing agents at 100°C for 10 minutes, released viral proteins. The GenScript SurePAGE (M00655) precast mini polyacrylamide gels were used for electrophoresis, followed by Coomassie Blue staining or Western blotting for protein analysis.

Negative-staining electron microscopy

The rVSV was purified using zonal centrifugation with a potassium tartrate density gradient and further analysis by negative-staining electron microscopy.42 The virus was concentrated using a SW28 rotor in a Beckman OPTIMA L-90K ultracentrifuge, operating at 30,000 g for 2 hours at 4°C. Subsequent to the removal of the supernatant, the sediment was resuspended in 4-6 mL of PBS. This suspension was then layered onto a density gradient comprising 0-30% potassium tartrate and 30-0% glycerol. After centrifugation at 175,000 g for 2 hours, the band containing rVSV was meticulously extracted with a syringe and subsequently diluted in 10 mL of PBS. This dilution was then pelleted at 30,000 g for 2 hours. Following this, the supernatant was discarded, and the sample was stored on wet ice overnight before being resuspended in approximately 200 μL of PBS. A 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid was used for virus adsorption and 2% phosphotungstic acid for staining. Specimens were imaged using the FEI Tecnai T12 TEM at a magnification of 150,000x.

Electron microscopy of VSV particles labeled with gold-conjugated antibodies

The virus solution was mixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in a 1:1 ratio, resulting in a total volume of 20 μL. This mixture was then applied to the surface of carbon grids for 20 minutes. The carbon grids were washed with 100 μL of PBS twice, each for 3 minutes. Subsequently, the grids were incubated on 100 μL droplets of 50 mM glycine three times, for 3 minutes each. To minimize non-specific reactions, 100 μL of 5% BSA was added to the carbon grids for 10 minutes. Following this, the grids were exposed to a 20 μL droplet of mouse mAb 22C6 (an anti-RABV-G mAb at 50 μg/mL) for 30 minutes. The carbon grids underwent cleaning with 0·1% BSA six times, each for 3 minutes. the carbon grids were exposed to a 20 μL droplet of the 10 nm colloidal gold-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (GA1004), diluted 20-fold with 0·5% BSA, for 30 minutes. Afterward, the grids were washed with 0·5% BSA and PBS six times each, for 3 minutes per wash. Finally, the carbon grids were placed into a droplet of 1% glutaraldehyde for 2 minutes and thereafter washed with deionized water six times. The carbon grids were negatively stained with uranyl acetate for 90 seconds. The sample was observed by electron microscopy after drying.

Cryo-ET sample preparation

A 3 μl purified rVSV-RABV-L71S is applied to a glow-discharged grid ((Quantifoil R2/2 Cu 200), allowed to adsorb for 10 s, blotted for 5 s to remove excess liquid, and then rapidly immersed in liquid ethane for freezing. Plunge freezing was carried out using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with the freezing conditions of 100% humidity and 4°C.

Cryo-ET data collection and processing

Cryo-ET data were collected on the 300 kV Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was equipped with a Biocontinuum HD imaging filter and a K3 direct detection camera (Gatan). The tilt series was obtained using TEM Tomography 5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a dose-symmetric acquisition scheme. The tilt range extended from -60° to 60° in 3° increments, with a target defocus ranging of -2 to -5 μm. Data acquisition was performed in super-resolution mode, with a nominal magnification of 64,000x and a calibrated pixel size of 1·38 Å. Micrographs at each tilt angle were divided into 10 frames with an exposure time of 0·26 seconds, resulting in a total accumulated electron dose of ∼123 e−/Å2 for 41 tilt series.

Warp43 was employed to preprocess the raw data, which included motion correction and defocus estimation, resulting in drift-corrected stack files. Subsequently, marker-free alignment was conducted in AreTomo44 to reconstruct the complete tomograms. Finally, 3dmod was utilized for visualization and plotting. The three-dimensional reconstruction is stored in the electron microscopy database. For the relevant data, please visit: Electron Microscopy Data Bank: EMD-66217 and Electron Microscopy Data Bank: EMD-66218.

Pseudovirus neutralization assay

The methodology for the pseudovirus-based neutralization assay is detailed in our previous article.22 In brief, BHK-21 cells were pre-seeded in a 96-well plate with 2·0×10ˆ5 cells / 100 μL. The serum and pseudovirus mixture were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for one hour before being transferred to the 96-well plate for a 16-20 hour infection period. The Phenix or Operetta CLS instruments and the Columbus system (PerkinElmer) were employed to capture and analyze fluorescence images, respectively. The count of GFP-positive cells in each well was determined and used to assess the infection level. The rate of neutralization inhibition of serum against the pseudovirus was calculated as follows:(number of GFP positive cells in MOCK Wells−number of GFP positive cells in experimental Wells)/number of GFP positive cells in MOCK Wells×100%. Furthermore, the serum's ID50, defined as the dilution required to inhibit 50% of GFP-positive fluorescent spots, was determined through non-linear regression, specifically log(inhibitor) versus response (with four parameters).

Wild type CVS-11-based neutralization assay

Incubate the test serum at 56°C for 30 minutes. Add 100 μL of serum-free MEM medium to each well of a 96-well plate, then introduce 50 μL of the test serum into the first column. Mix thoroughly, and subsequently transfer 50 μL to the next well to achieve a 3-fold serial dilution. Conduct a similar 3-fold serial dilution using the diluted standard positive serum. Dilute the rabies virus strain CVS-11 to 2000 TCID50/mL with serum-free MEM medium and add 50 μL to each well. For virus control wells, introduce 100 μL of serum-free MEM medium into each well of a 96-well plate, adding 50 μL of CVS-11 to the first column. After thorough mixing, transfer 50 μL to the succeeding well for a continued 3-fold serial dilution. Incubate the 96-well plate at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 60 minutes.

Digest BHK-21 cells using 0·125% trypsin and inoculate them with 8%FBS MEM medium at a density of 2·0×10ˆ4 cells/50 μL into a 96-well plate. Add the serum-virus mixture to the BHK-21 cell suspension at 50 μL per well, treating the virus control wells similarly. Establish normal cell controls and add an additional 50 μL of serum-free MEM culture medium per well. Incubate the entire 96-well plate at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 48 hours, after which perform an immunofluorescence assay. The actual virus titer in the virus control wells ranges between 50 and 200 TCID50/50 μL, with no specific fluorescence detected in the cell control wells, thus validating the experiment. Calculate the 50% protective dose (PD50) using the Reed-Muench method and determine the RABV neutralizing antibody titer of the serum.

Flow cytometry

Groin lymph nodes and spleen tissues from mice euthanized by cervical dislocation were processed using an 80-mesh cell strainer, PBS or 1640 medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum (2% FACS) as the buffer. For lymph node cells, the cell density was adjusted for staining. Splenic cells were treated with 10 mL erythrocyte lysis buffer at 4°C for 10 minutes, followed 10-20 mL 2% FACS. Two million cells are taken for staining. For the staining of Germinal center B cells and plasma cells, cells were combined with TCRβ-PerCP-Cy5·5, B220-APC-Cy7, CD95-PE, GL7-APC, CD138-BV421, IgD-FITC, and aqua-BV510, incubated for 30 minutes, and washed twice with 2% FACS buffer. Cells were re-suspended in 200 μL of 2% FACS buffer and filtered through an 80-mesh cell strainer for analysis. For APC staining, CD16/CD32 was used for 30 minutes, followed by MHCII-APC, CD11C-BV421, CD11b-PE, CD80-FITC, CD86-PE-Cy7, Ly6C-APC-Cy7, Ly6G-BV605 and aqua-BV510 used for an additional 30 minutes. Cells were washed twice with the 2% FACS buffer and re-suspended in 200 μL of the buffer, followed by filtration through an 80-mesh cell strainer.

For flow cytometric staining of cytokines, the pretreatment of spleen cells was consistent with the foregoing. The cells were stimulated with pooled RABV-G peptide (2 μg/mL) for 16 hours at 37°C and 5% CO2, followed by the addition of a Golgi inhibitor (1:1000) for 4 hours. The cells were stained after cleaning with 2% FACS. Cells were incubated with CD4-FITC, CD8-PE-Cy7, and aqua-BV510 for 30 minutes. Subsequently, they were treated with 100 μL of Cytoperm solution for approximately 30 minutes to facilitate fixation and permeabilization. Then, cells were washed twice with 200 μL of 1 x Perm solution. IL-4-BV421 and IFN-γ-APC were diluted with 1 x Perm solution and applied to stain the samples for an additional 30 minutes. Finally, the samples were washed twice more with 1 x Perm solution, resuspended in a 200 μL 1 x Perm solution, and filtered through an 80-mesh cell filter. The data was analyzed using flowjo.

IFN-γ ELISPOT assay

The pretreatment of splenic cells was conducted similarly to that used for flow cytometry. The cell suspensions were seeded at a density of 5·0 x 10ˆ5 cells/well and stimulated with pooled RABV-G-peptides (2 μg/mL; GenScript) for 12 hours at 37°C,5% CO2. Subsequently, IFN-γ ELISPOT assays were performed using an IFN-γ ELISPOT kit, following the manufacturer's instructions (Dakewe Biotech Co., Ltd.). Spots were developed and counted using the ImmunoSpot® S5 UV analyzer (Cellular Technology Co., Ltd.). The number of IFN-γ positive cells was calculated by subtracting the count in non-stimulated wells from that in pooled peptide-stimulated wells.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentration of RABV-G protein in rVSV samples was quantified using an ELISA. Plates of 96 wells were coated with 200 ng per well of the monoclonal capture antibody 11A7-2, specific to RABV-G. Next, the plate is blocked with sealing fluid 1 (Xiamen Wantai Biological Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) at 37°C for 2 hours. The plates incubated at 37°C for 1 hour with 100 μL of 3-fold serially diluted viral samples. After 5 rounds of washing, the plates were further incubated with the monoclonal antibody 1A2, targeting a distinct epitope from 11A7-2 and conjugated with horseradish peroxidase in a 1:1500 (V/V) ratio, for 0·5 hours at 37°C. Tetramethyl benzidine substrate solution (Xiamen Wantai Biological Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) was then added with 100 μL per well, and the mixture was incubated for 10 minutes at 37°C. The reaction was halted by the addition of 50 μL of 2 M sulfuric acid per well, and optical density was measured at 450 nm, using 630 nm as the reference wavelength. The recombinant RABV-G protein, expressed by Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells, was used as the standard substance for calculating the concentration of RABV-G in the virus. For more details, refer to the literature by Xia et al.45

All mice were kept in specific pathogen free laboratory. To evaluate antibody responses, BALB/c mice were immunized intramuscularly with inactivated rVSV-RABV-P0, rVSV-RABV-P24, rVSV-RABV-L71S or rVSV-RABVGEL-02, rVSV-RABV-L71S+GEL-02 (the dose of both groups was 1·0 × 10ˆ7 PFU per dose of 100 μL, GEL-02 adjuvant was added to the inactivated rVSV-RABV-L71S according to the instructions). Studies of protection for CVS-24 challenge

The protective efficacy of live rVSV-RABV-L71S vaccine was evaluated according to the efficacy test method of inactivated rabies vaccine in Chinese Veterinary Medicine Dictionary. The live rVSV-RABV-L71S vaccine (tested vaccine, TV) was diluted to 4·0 x 10ˆ8 PFU/mL using PBS. Then, the TV was serially diluted 5-fold with PBS in four dilutions of 1:5, 1:25, 1:125, and 1:625, and then diluted and stored in an ice bath until use. The Inactivated rabies whole virus vaccine (reference vaccine, batch number: 250009-201909, RV) with a titer of 11·4 IU/mL, was purchased from China Institute for Food and Drug Control. Similarly, the RV was serially diluted 5-fold with PBS in four dilutions of 1:25, 1:125, 1:625, and 1:3125, and then diluted and stored in an ice bath until use. Four dilutions of the TV and the RV were injected intraperitoneally (0·5 mL) into KUNMING mice, and the booster vaccination was performed 7 days later. Fourteen days after the first immunization, 0·03 mL CVS-24 strain virus (50 LD50/ 0·03 mL) was injected into the brain of each mouse. At the same time, CVS-24 virus (50 LD50/ 0·03 mL) was serially diluted 10 times with in PBS, and four dilutions (100, 10-1, 10-2, 10-3) were used to inoculate 10 mice in each dilution (0·03 mL per mouse) as the virus backdrop control. Observation continued for 14 days after challenge. Mice that died within 4 days after inoculation (including day 4) were excluded as nonspecific deaths. The daily number of deaths from 5 to 14 days in each group was recorded. When more than 80% of the mice injected in the 100 groups of virus backdrop experiments die, and the content of the challenge virus is 5-100 LD50/0.03 ml, the experiment is considered valid. The ED50 of the TV and the RV were calculated. Subsequently, by comparing the two, the NIH potency (International Units) of the TV was determined.
