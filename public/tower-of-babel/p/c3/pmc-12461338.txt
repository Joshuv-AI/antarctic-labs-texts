Geobacter sulfurreducens Immobilized Boron-Doped Diamond Electrodes for Uranium(VI) in Water Electrochemical Bioremediation
Authors: Alexis J. Acevedo-González, Elliott G. Rosario-Cruz, Angelica A. Chacon, Gary A. Toranzos, Carlos R. Cabrera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The proliferation of nuclear science and technology has resulted in an increase in nuclear waste containing uranium, posing significant risks to both human and environmental health. This study proposes the use of Geobacter sulfurreducens (G. sulfurreducens) modified boron-doped diamond electrodes to facilitate the reduction and removal of uranium­(VI) from aqueous media. The bioremediation process involves electrochemically immobilizing the bacteria on a boron-doped diamond electrode (BDD). The immobilization process requires applying reduction potentials ranging from −0.40 to −0.70 V (vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl)), with −0.60 V identified as the optimal potential for effective bacterial modification. The uranium source is provided by a 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution in G. sulfurreducens growth medium. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals a highly uniform layer of uranium on the electrode surface. Energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (EDS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) studies confirm the presence of uranium in the system. Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) successfully elucidate the reduction process of U­(VI) to predominantly U­(IV) using a bacteria-electrode coupled system. Additionally, a comparison is made with the electrochemical removal of uranyl ions using the electrodeposition method on unmodified BDD. Results demonstrate the presence of three uranium oxide species (UO2, UO3, and U3O8) on the BDD electrode after experimentation, in contrast to the G. sulfurreducens/BDD assembly, which achieves the predominant reduction of UO2 2+ to UO2 with a small quantity of UO3 as the final species. This study highlights the efficient electrochemical removal of uranyl ions from aqueous media at the G. sulfurreducens/BDD interface through chronoamperometry, presenting a promising approach for remediating sites contaminated with radioactive materials. The findings contribute to the exploration of sustainable alternatives for managing nuclear waste, emphasizing the potential of this electrochemical bioremediation strategy.

Introduction

The use of radionuclides in research, fuel production, and weapon manufacturing has led to significant contamination issues, manifesting in the pollution of trillions of gallons of groundwater and millions of cubic meters of soil and debris. − The staggering scale of this contamination has prompted estimates of bioremediation costs exceeding a trillion dollars. In 2023, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) reported the disposal of approximately 3.30 million cubic feet and 172,000 Curies of low-level radioactive waste in the USA, underscoring the critical need for a cost-effective removal and remediation strategy. Among the radionuclides studied, uranium (U) emerges as a significant contributor to environmental contamination, driving the need for effective and innovative removal techniques.

Uranium is a natural element widely dispersed in the Earth’s crust, rocks, and soil. Remarkably, uranium is more abundant than gold, silver, or mercury. Natural uranium exists in three isotopic forms, 234U (0.0055%), 235U (0.7200%), and 238U (99.2745%), and exhibit half-lives of 2.45 × 105, 7.04 × 108, and 4.47 × 109 years, respectively, indicating remarkably long lifespans. Uranium can adopt +3, +4, +5, and +6 oxidation states with the +4 and +6 oxidation states being the only stable species in the environment; the +3 and +5 ions are relatively unstable. This radioactive element has been valuable for the development of civil, industrial, and military applications, facilitating its exposure to the environment.

The primary method for obtaining uranium is mining, which involves milling and leaching processes. − Unfortunately, these methods have resulted in extensive environmental damage, primarily due to the improper handling of radioactive materials. This has allowed uranium to contaminate soil and groundwater, subsequently entering terrestrial and aquatic food chains. − Another critical aspect of uranium contamination is the distinction between depleted uranium (D.U.). D.U. is produced by removing 235U from natural uranium, resulting in a substance that is 40% less radiotoxic than natural uranium. Depleted uranium is utilized in military ammunition, guidance devices, compasses, radiation shielding materials, and X-ray targets, extending its application across various fields and contributing to its exposure in the environment. Also, the improper disposal of D.U. at radioactive waste sites can lead to environmental contamination.

A pivotal consideration in the context of uranium contamination is the prevalence of the stable and toxic uranium­(VI) ion. Typically, it exists in water bodies as a uranyl ion (UO2 2+), and its abundance in the environment can be attributed to its high solubility in water, which allows it to form soluble complexes with hydroxides, carbonates, sulfates, and phosphates within a pH range of 4.0 to 8.5. ,− These properties collectively result in adverse environmental effects, primarily impacting surface water, groundwater, and soils and often infiltrating drinking water sources. The health risks associated with various forms of uranium include kidney disease, lung disease, respiratory disorders, bone disease, liver disease, DNA damage, endocrine disruption, cancers, reproductive toxicity, and neurological defects. ,−

In response to this environmental challenge, we employed electrochemical techniques coupled with the metabolic processes of microorganisms to remove uranium in its hexavalent state (U­(VI)) from aqueous media via electrochemical bioremediation, first reported by Lovley and Gregory using carbon electrodes. , The process involves creating a coupled system between the microorganism and an electrode, using the microorganism’s metabolic processes to reduce U­(VI) to U­(IV) electrochemically. Uranium­(IV) is highly insoluble in water, allowing us to precipitate it and remove it from the aqueous media. Our approach leverages the unique attributes of a boron-doped diamond (BDD) electrode as an electron donor for microorganisms. The BDD electrode offers distinctive advantages in bioelectrochemical processes, including chemical and dimensional stability, prolonged durability, inertness, high conductivity, a wide potential window, and excellent biocompatibility. − Uranium electrodeposition has previously been demonstrated in aqueous systems using various electrode materials, such as boron-doped diamond, stainless steel, graphene foam, and polyaniline/graphite sheet electrodes. −

Bioremediation of uranium contamination through enzymatic uranium reduction may play a key role in nuclear wastewater remediation. − This is a process in which wastes are biologically degraded or transformed under controlled conditions to levels below the concentration limits established by regulatory authorities. In this study, the bioremediation process is integrated with an electrochemical technique to develop a more cost-effective, efficient, and environmentally friendly approach. , It employs various eco-friendly microbial processes to address environmental pollution issues. Many microbes are able to adapt to toxic wastes and naturally convert a wide variety of hazardous chemicals into less or nontoxic forms. Due to this capability of bioremediation, mainly using bacteria, the technology has been considered and studied for removing radionuclides and heavy metal ions from nature. Even though early studies on microbial electrochemistry date back more than a hundred years, only in the past two decades has the research area been the subject of systematic study. ,, One of the initial works by Lovley and Bond demonstrating the effective interaction between microorganisms and electrodes was conducted in the early 2000s. In this research, Lovley et al. immobilized Geobacter sulfurreducens on a graphite electrode, using it as an electron acceptor, by applying an oxidative potential of 0.20 V vs Ag/AgCl. Subsequent data showed the immobilization process of microorganisms on various electrodes and their use in bioremediation processes, including gold electrodes, stainless steel electrodes, graphite paper electrodes, glassy carbon electrodes, and more recently, a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) electrode modified with Fe2O3.

As a central component of our bioremediation strategy, Geobacter sulfurreducens (G. sulfurreducens) ,− has emerged as an electroactive bacterium with exceptional characteristics. These include direct electron transfer associated with the interaction of C-type cytochromes, high current density production, conductive pili linked to the electron transfer process, and electron recovery rates of up to 95%. Additionally, the biofilm produced by G. sulfurreducens is thought to participate in the U­(VI) reduction process. ,,− While previous studies have explored the interactions between microorganisms and electrodes, our work focuses on the synergistic utilization of BDD in tandem with G. sulfurreducens for the removal and reduction of U­(VI) from aqueous media. ,,

The electrochemical immobilization of G. sulfurreducens on the BDD was achieved using the chronoamperometry (CA) technique. , In this process, a reduction potential was applied, allowing the BDD electrode to function as an electron donor for the bacteria, replacing any organic compounds. Similarly, the electrochemical removal of U­(VI) from the solution is performed using G. sulfurreducens immobilized on the electrode, with the uranyl ion serving as the electron acceptor. Although the electrochemical bioremediation of U­(VI) was demonstrated in a previous study, the resulting species of uranium was assumed to be U­(IV). In this study, we employed Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses to corroborate the uranium species obtained after the reduction process of U­(VI) through the electrochemical interactions of G. sulfurreducens attached to the BDD.

Furthermore, this work investigated uranium­(VI) remediation using the electrodeposition method with the BDD electrode in an unmodified state. The CA technique was employed again to remove and reduce uranium cations from the aqueous media. This study aimed to compare the processes and results obtained using the BDD electrode with and without the bacteria. Raman spectroscopy and XPS analyses were conducted to identify the species formed after the experiments. Based on previous studies, we anticipated the presence of three uranium oxides with this method: UO2, UO3, and U3O8.

First, a cyclic voltammetry procedure was applied to characterize the constructed system, with uranium oxidation and reduction peaks observed to confirm the presence of the ions. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to compare the morphology and topography of the BDD electrode after U­(VI) remediation processes. We captured the initial formation of a highly uniform layer over the BDD electrode housing immobilized bacteria, providing the first visual insights into the U/G. sulfurreducens/BDD film following the bioelectrochemical procedure. Complementary energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (EDS) analysis confirmed the presence of uranium on the electrode surface. Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were used to identify the uranium species on the BDD electrode surface after remediation, with both the unmodified electrode and the one modified with the bacterium.

The results obtained in this study demonstrate the efficacy of the electrochemical bioremediation process for U­(VI) using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system compared to the use of the bare electrode. The developed procedure emerges as a promising alternative in the pursuit of effective removal and recovery solutions for this radioactive material utilizing the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system within an electrochemical bioremediation framework.

Experimental/Methods

Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

The experiments were conducted using a culture of the G. sulfurreducens PCA strain (ATCC# 51573). Cells were cultivated under anaerobic conditions (20% CO2/80% N2) in a Coy Laboratories Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber Type A. The G. sulfurreducens growth medium consisted of (per liter): 1.5 g of NH4Cl, 0.60 g of NaH2PO4, 0.10 g of KCl, 2.5 g of NaHCO3, 10 mL of vitamin mix (Wolfe’s Vitamin Solution from ATCC), and 10 mL of trace mineral mix (Modified Wolfe’s Minerals from ATCC). ,, The medium was supplemented with 10 mM sodium acetate, serving as an electron donor for G. sulfurreducens. After the medium was autoclaved under standard conditions, it was further supplemented in the anaerobic chamber with filtered sterilized 5% coenzyme M and 50 mM sodium fumarate, which acted as an electron acceptor for the bacteria. The solution was aseptically adjusted to a pH of 6.80. A stock of G. sulfurreducens was then inoculated into 5 mL of the medium and incubated at 30 °C for 6 days. Following cell growth, the cultures were cryopreserved using a 1:1 ratio of cells to sterile glycerol and stored in the vapor phase of liquid nitrogen.

Bacterial Electrochemical Immobilization and Uranium Bioremediation Process

A frozen cell vial was thawed under aseptic and anaerobic conditions within a Coy Laboratories chamber using a gas mixture of 20% CO2 and 80% N2. After centrifugation at 6000 rpm, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in a sterile medium devoid of sodium acetate. Simultaneously, an electrochemical cell was prepared using the same growth medium without sodium acetate. Electrochemical studies were conducted using a CH Instruments Electrochemical Analyzer potentiostat with a three-electrode system: the boron-doped diamond (BDD) electrode served as the working electrode (cathode), an Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) reference electrode was used, and a platinum wire (Pt) acted as the counter electrode.

Using the chronoamperometry (CA) technique, the bacteria were electrochemically immobilized on the BDD electrode at potentials ranging from −0.40 to −0.70 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl), utilizing the growth medium without sodium acetate. The potential was maintained during the inoculation of the resuspended pellets into the electrochemical cell. All electrochemical procedures were conducted at 30 °C, with each study lasting from 5 to 10 days. Based on the observed results, the optimal potential for bacterial immobilization was determined to be −0.60 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl).

After successfully immobilizing G. sulfurreducens on the BDD electrode surface at the optimized potential, the growth medium was replaced with a 2.00 mM uranyl acetate dihydrate (UO2(C2H3O2)2·2H2O) solution dissolved in a growth media (pH: 6.80) that lacked sodium acetate or sodium fumarate, while maintaining the optimal potential of −0.60 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl). This change in medium facilitated the reduction and removal processes of uranium, with G. sulfurreducens utilizing U­(VI) present in the solution as an electron acceptor. The samples were then left to dry inside the anaerobic chamber under aseptic conditions.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted to characterize the system electrochemically, following electrochemical bioremediation. The experiments were performed within a potential window of −0.80 to 0.80 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) (−0.20 to 1.50 V vs RHE) at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. The BDD electrode served as the working electrode, and a platinum wire was utilized as the counter electrode. Additionally, a blank experiment was carried out using the growth medium solution to provide a basis for comparison with the results obtained after the electrochemical bioremediation process.

Uranium Remediation with the Unmodified BDD Electrode

Electrodeposition experiments were conducted on the BDD electrode without the bacterium to compare the uranium remediation processes with and without G. sulfurreducens. A 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution in 0.10 M KClO4 was utilized for the uranium remediation process. The electrochemical setup consisted of a three-electrode system with the BDD as the working electrode, a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) as the reference, and a platinum wire as the counter electrode. The electrochemical cell was purged for 15 min with ultrahigh-purity (UHP) nitrogen gas prior to the experiment. During the uranium electrodeposition process, a potential of −0.80 V vs RHE was applied for 40 min. Afterward, the samples were rinsed with sterile deionized water, dried under argon, and stored in sealed vials without specific temperature or atmosphere control.

Following the uranium electrodeposition procedure, a cyclic voltammetry (CV) study was conducted to characterize the process. The applied potential window during the experiments ranged from −0.90 to 1.50 V vs RHE, and the scans were performed at a rate of 10 mV/s. Concurrently, a blank experiment was conducted with the bare BDD electrode in a 0.10 M KClO4 solution to facilitate comparison of the results obtained after the electrodeposition process.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)/X-ray Fluorescence Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging and X-ray fluorescence energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analyses were performed by using a JEOL JSM-IT500HR SEM/EDS system. SEM images were captured at an accelerated voltage of 20.0 kV and a working distance (WD) of 10.1 mm, with magnification ranging from 325× to 18,000×. EDS analyses were conducted at 20.0 kV with a magnification of 50×, and all SEM/EDS studies were performed under high vacuum conditions. Samples containing G. sulfurreducens were coated with gold using a Pelco SC-7 Auto Sputter Coater for 10 s to achieve a thickness of 10 nm.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy characterization analyses were carried out on samples using a Thermo Scientific DXR Raman Microscope with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The laser power was set at 5.0 mW, and wavelengths between 50 and 3000 cm–1 were measured. The standard samples of UO2, UO3, and U3O8, from International Bio-Analytical Laboratories, Inc., were used to compare the obtained phase patterns from the uranium deposits in BDD and G. sulfurreducens/BDD electrodes.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the predominant uranium composition and material functionalities on the BDD electrode surface. The analysis was performed using a high-energy resolution XPS surface analysis system by Thermo Fisher with an Al Kα monochromatic source and a pass energy of 50.0 eV. The XPS instrument was equipped with software to identify surface species binding energies, referenced to C 1s at 285.0 eV.

Results and Discussion

Electrochemical Immobilization of Geobacter sulfurreducens

The immobilization of G. sulfurreducens on the BDD electrode was conducted using the chronoamperometry (CA) technique, with the BDD serving as the working electrode or cathode. Various reduction potentials ranging from −0.40 to −0.70 V vs Ag/AgCl were applied over 5 days to evaluate the effectiveness of the immobilization process. The results, presented in Figure , highlight key aspects of bacterial behavior under these applied potentials.

(A) Chronoamperometry of G. sulfurreducens immobilization and growth process applying potentials between −0.40 and −0.70 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) and (B) scanning electron microscopy after G. sulfurreducens immobilization on the boron-doped diamond process at an applied potential of −0.60 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) in G. sulfurreducens growth media for 10 days.

In the four experiments conducted, a distinct bacterial growth layer was observed on the first and second days. Following these initial observations, a gradual increase in current was noted at −0.40 and −0.70 V, indicating the possible effective immobilization of G. sulfurreducens on the BDD electrode, attributed to its conductive nature. ,,,, Conversely, a decrease in current at −0.50 and −0.60 V toward the end of the experiments suggested a less effective BDD modification process with the bacteria.

However, SEM images (Figure S1) revealed higher concentrations of microorganisms at −0.50 and −0.60 V compared to −0.40 and −0.70 V. This disparity implies that while G. sulfurreducens growth may be more pronounced at −0.50 and −0.60 V, and on the other hand, the bacteria could exhibit greater metabolic activity at −0.40 and −0.70 V. However, further research is necessary to thoroughly understand the microbiological behavior of Geobacter sulfurreducens at these potentials comprehensively. Subsequent experiments identified −0.60 V as the optimal potential for bacterial immobilization on the BDD surface for 10 days (Figure B).

After establishing the optimal potential for electrochemical immobilization, additional studies were conducted at −0.60 V vs Ag/AgCl to further validate the immobilization process. These studies, performed over 10 days under the same anaerobic conditions, yielded similar results to those depicted in Figure S2, where G. sulfurreducens growth was observed on the first and second days, followed by a growth plateau. Additionally, SEM analysis indicated a high density of bacteria immobilized on the active area of the BDD electrode (Figure B).

After corroborating the bacteria’s effective immobilization procedure, the next step proceeded with the electrochemical bioremediation of U­(VI) using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system.

Electrochemical Remediation and Bioremediation of U­(VI)

In this study, two processes were conducted to remediate U­(VI) from aqueous media: one using an unmodified BDD electrode and the other employing a G. sulfurreducens/BDD assembly. These procedures allow for a comparative analysis of the results, enabling us to assess the effectiveness of both methods in achieving the removal and reduction of U­(VI) to U­(IV).

Figure A presents the results obtained from the uranyl ion removal process using a bare BDD electrode. The chronoamperometry (CA) study reveals notable observations following this remediation process. First, the graph indicates a very consistent behavior throughout the remediation procedures. The consistent behavior observed from the initial minutes of the process, coupled with the high current density illustrated in Figure A, suggests that the electrodeposition of uranium on the boron-doped diamond (BDD) electrode occurs within a short period.

Chronoamperometry obtained after U­(VI) remediation processes using (A) the bare BDD and (B) G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system under 20% CO2/80% N2 atmosphere in 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution in the G. sulfurreducens growth media (0–3 days: G. sulfurreducens modification; 3–10 days: uranium bioremediation).

Continuing the discussion on uranium removal processes, Figure B illustrates the results obtained after electrochemical bioremediation using bacteria coupled with the BDD electrode. G. sulfurreducens was immobilized on the electrode at −0.60 V vs Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) for 3 days, following the achievement of a growth plateau. On the first day, G. sulfurreducens exhibited a plateau-like growth, indicating successful bacterial immobilization. The current remained stable for the next 2 days, signifying that the bacteria had reached their maximum growth on the electrode’s active area.

Subsequently, the growth medium was replaced with a uranyl acetate/media solution while maintaining the potential. Upon introduction of the 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution, the current density gradually increased from −0.28 to −0.40 μA cm– 2 starting on the sixth day. The observed variations and fluctuations in the plot are attributed to the electrochemical interactions occurring within the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system in response to the presence of U­(VI) in the solution, linked to its reduction and removal processes.

The amount of U removed was determined to be 5.13 × 10– 3 mg for the process using the unmodified BDD electrode, and 0.119 mg using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD assembly system (see the Supporting Information for details). It is important to note that while a significant quantity of material was removed, the BDD-only experiments lasted for only 40 min, whereas the bacteria/BDD system was run for 7 days. Beyond the amount of U removed, a primary objective of this research is the reduction of U­(VI) to U­(IV). Moreover, it is crucial to recognize the comparison between two distinct ecosystems: biological and material systems. Consequently, the requirements for conducting both experiments differ. Various characterization studies will be analyzed in the following sections to corroborate this reduction.

Conversely, a study was conducted to assess the daily removal of uranium (U) from the solution. Figure A presents the results of this investigation, demonstrating a consistent increase in the quantity of U removed over time without reaching a maximum plateau. Despite the relatively small active area of the BDD electrode (0.283 cm2) compared to the volume of the 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution (5.00 mL), these findings suggest that the electrochemical remediation process of U­(VI) using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD assembly system could continue for an extended duration before approaching its maximum U removal capacity.

(A) Analysis of the quantity of U removed each day conducing the electrochemical bioremediation process using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system under 20% CO2/80% N2 atmosphere in 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution in the G. sulfurreducens growth media. (B) SEM images of the after uranium electrochemical bioremediation using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system (scale: 5 μm).

Another intriguing behavior emerges between the fourth and sixth days of observation. Initially, on the fifth day, the observed decrease in uranium (U) removal might suggest that the process is reaching a plateau in terms of maximum U removal from the solution. This conclusion is based on the diminishing difference in mass removed between days 4 and 5. However, between days 5 and 6, there is a notable increase in the difference in U removed, challenging the notion of a plateau reached between days 4 and 5. This fluctuation in the amount of U material removed from the solution each day following the electrochemical bioremediation process is visualized in Figure S3. This observed behavior could imply the occurrence of a second growth event involving G. sulfurreducens. This subsequent growth may account for the increased removal of U from the solution and the resulting change in the difference observed after the fifth day.

These results are highly promising, indicating that even with the limited active area of the BDD electrode used for immobilizing G. sulfurreducens and conducting the electrochemical bioremediation process, the population of immobilized bacteria remained active for an extended period without requiring interruptions or modifications to the electrode. This suggests that employing a larger BDD electrode area during the immobilization process could facilitate the attachment of a greater quantity of G. sulfurreducens, thereby enhancing the cost-effectiveness and prolonging the U removal performance. Further studies are planned to explore and analyze these possibilities in greater detail.

To validate the immobilization, electrodeposition, and removal processes, an SEM/EDS study was conducted. The differences in morphology following the remediation processes are evident upon comparison of Figure B with Figure S4B. Additionally, Figure S4A displays the BDD electrode prior to any remediation methods, providing a baseline for understanding how these processes affect its topography. In experiments where U­(VI) remediation was achieved through the electrodeposition process using unmodified BDD, the formation of mounds or islands on the electrode surface is noted (Figure S4B–D). This formation of islands or 3D structures can be attributed to the Volmer–Weber deposition process, which suggests that weak interactions between U and the atoms of the BDD electrode surface lead to such growth patterns.

Figure B illustrates the modification of the BDD electrode following U­(VI) electrochemical bioremediation with G. sulfurreducens. The images reveal a highly uniform layer formed on the electrode surface with no signs of crystal growth by the end of the experiment. Notably, bacteria are observed beneath this layer (see also Figure S5), indicating that two concurrent processes likely occurred on the BDD surface during the application of potential. The first process appears to involve U reduction facilitated by the metabolic activities of G. sulfurreducens, while the second involves the electroreduction of U­(VI) due to the applied potential. The thickness of the layer formed of the BDD electrode surface after the electrochemical remediation process was determined with a result of 0.137 μm (see the Supporting Information for details).

The EDS analysis highlights the differences between the U­(VI) electrodeposition remediation and the uranyl ion electrochemical bioremediation processes (Figure ). Both methods confirm the presence of uranium on the BDD electrode postremediation, with uranium detected in the spectrum between 2.70 and 3.70 keV. Notably, the mass weight percentage of uranium was significantly higher when the bacterium was utilized in the U­(VI) remediation process, measuring 41.54%, compared to only 5.24% when using the BDD electrode alone (see Tables S1 and S2).

X-ray fluorescence energy-dispersive spectroscopy studies after uranyl ion remediation utilizing (A) as the unmodified BDD electrode and (B) G. sulfurreducens/BDD electrode assembly.

The other elements identified in both EDS spectra originated from the BDD electrode, the prepared growth media, and the uranyl acetate solutions as well as from the Au coating process used for SEM studies. The mass percentage of uranium (U) relative to other elements was the highest recorded in the electrochemical bioremediation study (Table S2), further confirming the effectiveness of the electrochemical process in removing U­(VI) from aqueous media.

Moreover, the EDS mapping study (Figure S6A,B) indicated that more U­(VI) was removed from the solution when the electrochemical remediation process was conducted with G. sulfurreducens immobilized on the BDD electrode surface. These results highlight the effectiveness of this bioelectrochemical system in removing this hazardous contaminant from aqueous media in an eco-friendly manner.

However, it is essential to identify the uranium species obtained after the removal of U­(VI) from the solutions with the expectation of detecting U­(IV) on the BDD surface in both interfaces: the U/BDD film and the U/G. sulfurreducens/BDD film. To accomplish this, cyclic voltammetry, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS analyses were performed to identify the present uranium species.

Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) Characterization

Following the uranium­(VI) remediation processes, we conducted electrochemical characterization by using cyclic voltammetry (CV). Figure displays the voltammograms obtained from both the unmodified BDD and G. sulfurreducens/BDD systems. In both cases, the study was compared with a blank, revealing an increase in current after the remediation studies. Notably, the current shows a more pronounced increase when only the electrode was used compared to the system with the immobilized bacteria. It is important to recognize that while G. sulfurreducens is an electroactive microorganism, several factors influence its conductivity. The organic composition of the bacterium means that its conductivity cannot be directly compared to that of a metallic conductor.

Cyclic voltammogram results after the U­(VI) remediation using (A) as the BDD electrode and (B) as the G. sulfurreducens/BDD electrode system. (C) Magnification of the U reduction peak after the remediation process using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD system under a 20% CO2/80% N2 atmosphere in 2.0 mM uranyl acetate solution in the Geobacter sulfurreducens growth media and at a scan rate of 10 mV/s.

Additionally, the bacterium forms a biofilm on the BDD electrode surface, which may increase resistance in the electron transfer process, as indicated by the voltammogram structure in Figure B. The electrochemical characterization also revealed the presence of uranium on the electrode surface, as oxidation signals from U­(IV) to U­(VI) were consistently detected at approximately 0.80 V vs RHE in both remediation and bioremediation processes (Figure A,B). The cyclic voltammetry results were normalized to the RHE electrode for better clarity in this discussion.

In contrast, the reduction signal for the remediation study using the unmodified BDD electrode (Figure A) was determined to be around 0.03 V vs RHE. However, in Figure B, the reduction peak from U­(VI) to U­(IV) is nearly indistinguishable, while the oxidation peak is notably pronounced. Upon magnification in Figure C, the reduction peak for U­(VI) becomes observable. This outcome suggests that during the cyclic voltammetry (CV) characterization, the uranium on the G. sulfurreducens/BDD electrode could predominantly be in the U­(IV) state.

The difference in reduction signals could be attributed to variations in the pH and temperature between the experiments. Additionally, the direct interaction of U­(VI) with the electrode in one case versus the predominant interaction with the bacteria in the other may contribute to the observed disparity in the reduction peaks. Having completed the electrochemical characterization through cyclic voltammetry, the subsequent sections will explore the morphological changes following U­(VI) remediation and identify the uranium species that were achieved.

Signals at 122 and 764 cm– 1 are present in this spectrum, indicating the possible presence of UO2 and UO3, respectively, at the G. sulfurreducens/BDD interface. Although UO2 and UO3 could overlap at 764 cm–1, this peak could indicate a major probability of the presence of UO3 in the system. This finding is consistent with literature and Raman studies on various uranium oxide standards, such as UO2, UO3, and U3O8 (refer to Figure S8 in the Supporting Information). These results confirm the successful reduction of the uranyl ion (U­(VI)) to predominantly UO2 (uranium­(IV)) within the system, along with a lesser presence of UO3. ,, Furthermore, the sustained presence of the predominant U­(IV) oxidation state after the electrochemical bioremediation process highlights the significance of G. sulfurreducens in achieving this outcome.

Based on the results obtained in this study, the reduction of U­(VI) presented in eq is proposed as the primary mechanism for removing this ion from aqueous solution. The findings suggest that this reduction reaction is primarily facilitated by the various metabolic activities of G. sulfurreducens in conjunction with the BDD electrode. These metabolic processes include mechanisms occurring on the surface of the bacterium, particularly those involving C-type cytochrome proteins, which are key components of the terminal reductase complex in the cell. Additionally, electron transfer occurs through conductive pili, and uranium­(VI) reduction is facilitated by the biofilm produced by G. sulfurreducens. ,,− Furthermore, it should be noted that part of the uranyl ion reduction and removal process may also be associated with an electrodeposition mechanism, attributed to the applied reduction potential of −0.60 V during electrochemical bioremediation.

In both processes, the removal of U­(VI) from the solution is demonstrated but the differences between the Raman spectra are evident. While there has been discussion regarding the distinctive signals of various uranium species in the Raman spectroscopy analyses, Figure A clearly shows a blend of uranium oxide species. Notably, although all species signals overlap, the broad band between 300 and 500 cm– 1 is characteristicserving as a fingerprintof U3O8. This band consistently appears in the Raman spectra, where U3O8 has been reported in the literature. Therefore, the absence of this band in Figure B further justifies the lack of U3O8 in that spectrum.

Similarly, the Raman spectrum of UO3 shows an absence of peaks in the 120 cm– 1 region. Additionally, some characteristic peaks of this compound in the 330 and 480 cm– 1 regions are not present in the Raman spectrum shown in Figure B. Although these characteristics are absent in the spectra, the peak observed at 764 cm– 1 suggests the potential presence of the UO3 species following the electrochemical bioremediation process. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis is discussed in the next section to corroborate the results obtained from the Raman spectroscopy study.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis is a crucial component in validating the results obtained from Raman spectroscopy following U­(VI) electrodeposition and electrochemical bioremediation using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD system. Figure S9 presents the XPS survey spectra after the uranium remediation and bioremediation processes. In both cases, the presence of uranium is confirmed (Figure S9A,B, Supporting Information).

The C 1s and O 1s core-level regions, as well as the valence band (VB) region, were analyzed, revealing binding energy peaks around 285.0 eV for C 1s and 531.0 eV for O 1s. The VB XPS binding energy region for the U/BDD and U/G. sulfurreducens/BDD films were explored within the 0 to 35 eV range, encompassing both the outer valence molecular orbitals (0 to 15 eV) and the inner valence molecular orbitals (15 to 35 eV). Additionally, Figure S9B shows the presence of Na and P, which are not detected in Figure S9A, likely due to the use of growth media in the electrochemical bioremediation study.

The 4f binding energy region was utilized to identify the uranium oxide species on the electrode surface after the remediation processes. The stoichiometry of the uranium oxides on the electrodes was determined by analyzing the binding energy difference between the spin–orbit split doublet in the U 4f region, specifically the 4f5/2 and 4f7/2 peaks, as well as the energy difference between the shakeup satellites and the 4f5/2 signal. ,− Figure S10 presents the U 4f spectrum for both cases.

In Figure S10A, the spectrum obtained using the unmodified BDD shows the 4f5 / 2 and 4f7 / 2 peaks at 392.9 and 382.0 eV, respectively, with an ΔE of 10.9 eV. Similarly, Figure S10B, which depicts the spectrum from the G. sulfurreducens/BDD film used for U­(VI) remediation, shows the U 4f5 / 2 and U 4f7 / 2 signals at 392.5 and 381.6 eV, respectively, also with an ΔE of 10.9 eV. These values are characteristic of the U 4f doublet peaks.

A significant difference between the two uranium electrochemical remediation processes was noted when identifying the deposited species using the energy difference between the 4f5/2 peak and the shakeup satellites (Figure S10A and Table 3S, Supporting Information). For the unmodified BDD electrode, an ΔE of 6.9 eV was observed, indicating the presence of U4 + attributed to UO2 stoichiometry. An ΔE of 9.4 eV was associated with U5 +, corresponding to U3O8, while an ΔE value of 4.1 eV was related to U6 +, corresponding to UO3 stoichiometry. These results corroborate the findings from Raman spectroscopy.

After identifying the uranium oxide species postremoval of U­(VI) using the unmodified BDD electrode, a curve fitting and deconvolution study of the U 4f7/2 peak was conducted. This analysis identified UO2, U3O8, and UO3 signals at 380.7, 382.0, and 383.0 eV, respectively (Figure A). The deconvolution analysis also provided the uranium speciation composition percentages: U4 + (23.62%), U5 + (57.76%), and U6 + (18.62%) (see Table S4, Supporting Information). The same analysis was performed following the electrochemical bioremediation of U­(VI) using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD system. Figure S10B shows the ΔE between the 4f5/2 peak and the shakeup satellites. In this study, ΔEs of 6.9 4.1 and 10.8 eV were found, corresponding to the stoichiometry of U4+ (UO2) and U6+ (UO3), respectively (see Table S3, Supporting Information). The curve fitting and deconvolution analysis of the peak 4f7/2 (Figure B) identified UO2 and UO3 signals at 381.5 and 383.0 eV, respectively, with corresponding percentages of 75.15% for UO2 and 24.85% for UO3 (see Table S4, Supporting Information).

The results obtained from the XPS studies align with those from the Raman spectroscopy analyses, revealing a predominant presence of UO2 and a small quantity of UO3, and indicating that the Raman peak at 120 cm–1 corresponds to UO2 and the peak at 764 cm–1 corresponds to UO3. It is important to emphasize that in both studies, the samples were exposed to atmospheric conditions after the electrochemical bioremediation process, which could have caused the oxidation of U­(IV) to U­(VI), resulting in the observed presence of UO3 in the small percentage found. However, the atmospheric exposure was shorter in the Raman spectroscopy studies compared to the XPS analyses, where the samples were analyzed after a considerable delay, because the samples were sent to another laboratory for the XPS studies. This delay likely led to further oxidation of UO2 to UO3. This may explain the higher percentage of UO2 observed in the Raman spectroscopy and XPS results after electrochemical bioremediation of U­(VI) using G. sulfurreducens compared with the unmodified BDD electrode, even though both samples were exposed to air. It is likely that the uranium oxide species on the BDD electrode with bacteria could initially be UO2 before atmospheric exposure.

Thus, the results from Raman spectroscopy and the XPS study corroborate each other, highlighting the significance of G. sulfurreducens in maintaining U­(IV) as the predominant uranium species after the remediation process. Moreover, the XPS results confirmed the presence of the three uranium oxide species identified by Raman spectroscopy after the electrochemical remediation process using the unmodified BDD. Finally, the XPS data reinforced the proposed reactions and mechanisms for both electrochemical remediation processes studied in this work. Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated the efficacy of the electrochemical immobilization process of G. sulfurreducens on a BDD electrode surface. By leveraging electrochemical procedures and harnessing the metabolic processes of the bacteria, effective removal of U­(VI) from the aqueous medium and its predominant reduction to U­(IV) was achieved. However, a small percentage of UO3 was observed after the electrochemical bioremediation process. This presence could be attributed to atmospheric exposure of the samples during their transfer to Raman spectroscopy and XPS studies. In contrast, using the unmodified BDD electrode resulted in some removal of U­(VI), but Raman spectroscopy indicated a mixture of uranium oxides, including UO2, UO3, and U3O8, after the remediation process. The results from Raman spectroscopy were corroborated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) studies, which confirmed the presence of the three uranium oxide species following electrochemical remediation with the unmodified BDD electrode.

Notably, while XPS indicated the presence of UO3 after electrochemical bioremediation using the G. sulfurreducens/BDD system, the high percentage of UO2 (75.15%) compared to UO3 (24.85%) suggests that UO2 was the predominant species on the BDD surface. This UO2 likely oxidized to UO3 upon atmospheric exposure. Furthermore, the comparison of UO2 percentages between unmodified BDD and the G. sulfurreducens/BDD film, along with the cyclic voltammetry results, supports this hypothesis. These findings underscore the significance of G. sulfurreducens in the electrochemical system created with the BDD electrode, not only for removing the uranyl ion but, crucially, for obtaining and maintaining most of the U­(VI) reduced in the form of the U­(IV) oxidation state on the electrode following electrochemical bioremediation processes.

Furthermore, SEM studies revealed distinct morphologies on the BDD surface after both remediation processes. The G. sulfurreducens/BDD system exhibited the formation of a highly uniform layer, suggesting its potential use in future applications, such as in the development of biosensors. In contrast, the use of the BDD alone resulted in the formation of 3D structures or islands. To deepen our understanding of the interactions between U­(VI), G. sulfurreducens, and the boron-doped diamond electrode, future studies will incorporate techniques such as X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES), X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD), and atomic force microscopy (AFM). Thus, this approach emerges as a favorable option for addressing removal and recovery challenges related to radioactive materials by utilizing a G. sulfurreducens/BDD coupled system within the framework of electrochemical bioremediation. The observed synergy at the U/G. sulfurreducens/BDD interface positions this system as a promising electrochemical alternative for uranium waste remediation, significantly contributing to advancements in bionuclear technologies and the pursuit of effective bioremediation processes.
