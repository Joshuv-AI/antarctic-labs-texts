Visualization of oxophilic regulation for alkaline hydrogen evolution on Ni-based electrocatalyst via multimodal operando approaches
Authors: Jiongchong Fang, Zuochao Chen, Junqiang Li, Qing Zhang, Lin Zeng, Yaoxian Yang, Xueqiang Zhang, Hui Zhang, Haifeng Gao, Xinxin Lu, Jugang Ma, Zhirun Xie, Ailong Li, Fuyuan Yang, Yun Hau Ng, Guosong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alkaline hydrogen evolution reaction holds immense promise for sustainable energy conversion due to its inherent compatibility with earth-abundant electrocatalysts and potential for large-scale, high-purity hydrogen production. However, sluggish kinetics remain a formidable bottleneck for its industrial deployment. Although incorporating oxophilic components is widely used to promote water activation, the catalytic role of oxygen-affinity-related sites is often interpreted statically, without considering dynamic evolution of local coordination under operating conditions. Here, we synthesize a crystalline-amorphous Ni/TiO2 heterojunction via a grain-boundary segregation strategy and combine multi-modal operando techniques to directly visualize reversible, bias-induced reconstruction of the oxophilic component. These observations provide direct evidence that, at the Ni/TiO2 interface, oxygen affinity is a tunable parameter encoded by nanoscale coordination and interfacial chemistry, and elucidate how its modulation governs water dissociation and hydroxyl handling. We demonstrate that the resulting four-coordinated Ti sites serve as highly efficient centers for water dissociation and hydroxyl adsorption, thereby synergistically optimizing the hydrogen adsorption/desorption energetics. This work offers a mechanistic and methodological basis for rational nano-heterointerfaces engineering in advanced electrocatalysts.

Introduction

Electrolytic water splitting, capable of large-scale production of high-purity hydrogen, represents a premier pathway for renewable energy conversion and achieving zero-carbon emissions1,2. While noble-metal-based catalysts exhibit superior activity and stability, their scarcity and prohibitive costs impede widespread industrial deployment. In this context, alkaline water electrolysis provides a viable platform for the utilization of earth-abundant, non-noble metal catalysts3,4. Among these, nickel (Ni) has emerged as a prime candidate due to its near-platinum hydrogen evolution activity, tunable electronic structure, and robust corrosion resistance5,6. Nevertheless, Ni-based catalysts are hampered by a dual limitation: a deficiency of efficient sites for water adsorption and dissociation that renders the Volmer step (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{H}}}_{2}O+{e}^{-}\to {H}^{*}+{{OH}}^{-}$$\end{document}H2O+e−→H*+OH−) the rate-determining step7, and an excessively strong hydrogen binding energy (HBE) that obstructs hydrogen desorption8, both of which collectively dictate the sluggish reaction kinetics in alkaline media.

To address these challenges, strategies such as alloying9, heteroatom doping10, defect engineering11, and heterostructure construction7 have been explored. Specifically, introducing oxophilic components (e.g., oxides or hydroxides) to form Ni/MOx (M represents oxophilic atom) heterojunctions has proven to be one of the most effective solutions1,12,13. The introduction of oxophilic species not only provides specialized sites for water dissociation and hydroxyl (OH*) adsorption/desorption2,14,15, but also facilitates the formation of asymmetric Ni–O–M bridge oxygen bonds. Substantial evidence suggests that the asymmetry of these interfacial bridge bonds, driven by electronegativity differences, induces local charge redistribution and accelerates electron transfer kinetics14,16. Furthermore, asymmetric orbital hybridization enables precise tuning of the d-band center, effectively breaking the linear scaling relations of intermediate adsorption energies17. This symmetry breaking also significantly enhances metal–oxygen orbital overlap, bolstering both intrinsic conductivity and structural robustness18. However, the role of oxophilic components is still often interpreted in static terms, with insufficient attention paid to how local coordination and interfacial electronic structure evolve under operating conditions and thereby reshape their catalytic function. Among transition metals, titanium (Ti) stands out as its high Ti–O bond energy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rm\triangle H=662\,{kJ}\,{{mol}}^{-1}$$\end{document}△H=662kJmol−1) and pronounced oxophilicity (or Lewis acidity)19–22, making it an ideal candidate for constructing asymmetric bridge oxygen bonds with Ni to synergistically optimize water dissociation and hydrogen energetics. Nonetheless, those favorable properties were mostly evident using simulated measurement parameters in a near-ideal condition14,15, lacking direct evidence during the operating mode. Real-time changes of such asymmetric interfaces have not yet been documented. Fundamentally, their dynamic evolution remains the key to understand their catalytic origin. Operando characterization—deciphering the transient coordination reconstruction and valence state variation—could offer a promising avenue. Thus, capturing the atomic-scale dynamic behavior of these metal-oxide interfaces is paramount for the rational development of high-performance alkaline HER catalysts.

Herein, we report a Ni/TiO2 heterojunction catalyst for alkaline HER via magnetron sputtering. Leveraging a grain boundary segregation mechanism with precisely controlled Ti solute concentrations (2.7 at%), we achieve confined oxide decoration of Ni nanoparticles. This approach enables the in situ formation of an ultra-thin, continuous, and defect-rich amorphous TiO2 encapsulation layer at the nanocrystalline grain boundaries, which effectively suppresses the sintering of Ni nanoparticles while maximizing the density of uniform Ni/TiO2 heterointerfaces. The resulting catalyst exhibits a low overpotential (η10 = 32 mV) outperforming commercial Pt/C, along with good stability. Importantly, this unique crystalline–amorphous architecture of Ni/TiO2 heterojunction acts as a pre-catalyst, necessitating a comprehensive suite of multi-modal operando characterization techniques. Ultimately, we captured the multi-dimensional dynamic reconstruction of oxophilic Ti species during the reaction, revealing the formation of four-coordinated Ti3+ as an effective active site. Notably, this working-state transformation tunes the effective oxophilicity from a nominally fixed material trait into an adjustable interfacial property, which regulates intermediate adsorption and optimizes the entire HER process. These findings provide a complete mapping of the alkaline HER pathway and offer atomic-scale insights and methodological guidance for the advancement of nickel-based catalytic systems.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of Ni/TiO2 heterojunction catalyst

The Ni/TiO2 heterojunction catalysts were synthesized via dual-target magnetron co-sputtering, predicated on a grain boundary segregation mechanism controlled by the Ni/Ti atomic ratio (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Driven by the atomic size mismatch, Ti solute atoms preferentially segregate to the grain boundaries (GBs) of nanocrystalline Ni instead of remaining in the Ni lattice. This segregation is thermodynamically favored in the defect-rich GB regions, which possess excess free volume and undercoordinated sites that can more readily accommodate Ti than the crystalline grain regions, thereby alleviating local lattice strain and lowering the grain-boundary energy23–27. Under the present co-sputtering conditions, rapid film growth together with limited surface diffusion kinetically preserves the Ti-enriched grain-boundary configuration, thereby suppressing long-range elemental redistribution and equilibrium phase precipitation28,29. As a result, precise stoichiometric control through magnetron sputtering enables the selective deposition of Ti at the grain boundaries of Ni nanocrystallites (~10 nm). Subsequent spontaneous oxidation yields a uniform and high-density Ni/TiO2 heterojunction, in which amorphous TiO2 fills and bridges grain boundaries approximately 3 nm in width (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 1–4).Fig. 1Catalyst preparation principle and characterization of Pure Ni and NiTi catalysts.a Schematic diagram of catalysts preparation and grain boundary segregation principle. b HAADF-STEM image and corresponding EDS mapping of Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalyst. c XRD patterns of Pure Ni and different Ti concentrations of NiTi catalysts. It should be noted that this grain-boundary segregation strategy is highly sensitive to the concentration of the introduced secondary element, exhibiting a limited solubility threshold. When the Ti content exceeds this threshold (2.7 at%), excess Ti flushes into the Ni lattice as solute atoms, disrupting the crystalline order and collapsing the characteristic island-like morphology (Supplementary Fig. 5). This over-incorporation induces amorphization and lattice strain, manifested as the broadening and shifting of the Ni (111) diffraction peak (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 6). STEM-EELS/EDS mapping further confirms that excessive Ti leads to the burial of Ni active sites under an inert TiO2 overlayer, underscoring the criticality of interfacial precision (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Accordingly, an optimal Ti concentration of 2.7 at% was identified. At this loading, a crystalline–amorphous composite structure is obtained, corresponding to highly crystalline (d1) and amorphous (d2) regions (Fig. 1b, d, e). The d1 region exhibits a lattice spacing of 2.03 Å, corresponding to the Ni (111) plane (PDF#04-0850), with no observable strain or defects (Supplementary Fig. 9). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of Ni 2p and Ti 2p core levels reveals electronic interaction between Ni and TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 10). The introduction of Ti significantly increases the proportion of metallic Ni, accompanied by a shift in binding energy from 852.74 eV to 852.62 eV (a decrease of 0.12 eV)30, indicating facilitated metallic Ni formation (Supplementary Fig. 11). Concurrently, the Ti 2p spectra exclusively show Ti species, lacking the sub-valent states (Ti3+, Ti2+) typically found in pure TiO2 films. These observations suggest a unidirectional Ti-to-Ni charge transfer pathway31,32, which effectively stabilizes high-density metallic Ni sites against oxidative deactivation.

Electrocatalytic performance of catalysts

Subsequently, electrochemical testing was conducted to validate whether this carefully engineered structure indeed exhibits the best catalytic activity and stability. Electrochemical evaluations in 1 M KOH reveal that HER activity is highly sensitive to the Ti content. Among catalysts with varying Ti loadings, Ni97.3Ti2.7 (2.7 at% Ti) exhibits the highest activity (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13 and Supplementary Table 3), delivering an overpotential (η10) of only 32 mV (Fig. 2a), which surpasses Pure Ni and commercial Pt/C. Control experiments performed in KOH electrolytes of different purities further confirm that this activity advantage is intrinsic, rather than arising from electrolyte-purity effects (Supplementary Fig. 14). The Tafel slope undergoes a dramatic reduction from 189.87 mV dec for Pure Ni to 43.42 mV dec (Fig. 2b), underscoring that the optimal construction of the Ni/TiO2 heterojunction fundamentally alters the HER pathway by accelerating the water dissociation step and shifting the rate-determining step (RDS) from the Volmer to the Heyrovsky reaction. Crucially, while the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) remains comparable across samples (Supplementary Fig. 15), the turnover frequency (TOF) of Ni97.3Ti2.7 at 150 mV is 7.45 times higher than that of pristine Ni (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 16), providing unequivocal evidence of enhanced intrinsic activity. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) shows a significant decrease in charge transfer resistance and no obvious low-frequency Warburg impedance in Nyquist plots, indicating that, within the current-density range mainly examined in this work, the enhanced HER performance is governed predominantly by optimized interfacial reaction kinetics rather than by mass-transfer effects (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 4). These results emphasize the profound impact of integrating oxophilic components on accelerating alkaline HER kinetics, while simultaneously highlighting the necessity for precise stoichiometric modulation. To probe the origin of the intrinsic activity, we examined the underpotential deposition of hydrogen (Hupd) on Pure Ni and Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalysts using cyclic voltammetry (CV). For Ni97.3Ti2.7, the H adsorption and desorption peaks shift cathodically by 0.02 V and 0.085 V, respectively, compared to Pure Ni (Fig. 2e), signifying a weakening of the excessively strong Ni–H binding energy, thereby facilitating the hydrogen desorption process7,33,34. Operando EIS was further employed to resolve the multi-step interfacial kinetics (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 20). Combined analysis of the Nyquist and Bode plots identifies two distinguishable interfacial processes for both catalysts. The low-frequency response, manifested as the low-frequency semicircle in the Nyquist plots and the corresponding low-frequency phase feature in the Bode plots, is associated with the Volmer step (water dissociation)35. Meanwhile, the mid-frequency response, reflected by the mid-frequency semicircle and the corresponding phase feature, is related to the Heyrovsky step (hydrogen desorption)36. With increasing cathodic bias, Pure Ni shows only a slow reduction in the low-frequency response and little change in the mid-frequency response, indicating sluggish water dissociation and less favorable hydrogen-transfer kinetics. In contrast, Ni97.3Ti2.7 exhibits a much faster attenuation of the low-frequency phase angle and a marked reduction in the mid-frequency phase angle with increasing bias, further confirming the optimized hydrogen energetics afforded by the asymmetric interfacial environment36,37.

Operando dynamic reconstruction of oxophilic component TiO2

While electrochemical assessments reflect the macroscopic performance and stability of the catalyst, standard ex-situ post-mortem characterization only identifies the emergence of metallic Ni resulting from electrochemical reduction and surface-bound Ni(OH)2 species (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22). These observations fail to provide definitive evidence regarding the origin of the enhanced activity. Motivated by the presence of a robust metal-oxide heterointerface within our catalyst, we employed operando Raman spectroscopy to monitor the interfacial evolution of TiO2 component in real-time. In 1 M KOH electrolyte, the Pure Ni electrode spontaneously forms NiO/Ni(OH)2 oxophilic species which, despite facilitating hydroxyl adsorption, exhibit limited stability during sustained HER (Fig. 3a)38. In contrast, the operando Raman spectra of Ni97.3Ti2.7 reveal a characteristic mode at 688 cm−1, indexed to Eg vibration mode of anatase TiO2 (Fig. 3b)39. Upon increasing the cathodic overpotential, this peak undergoes a pronounced blue shift, signifying substantial reorganization of the local Ti–O bonding environment and supporting the formation of oxygen vacancies together with the evolution of TiOx toward a highly distorted, lower-coordination state40. This structural reconfiguration is highly reversible, as the peak returns to its initial position upon removing the bias. Notably, Argon-annealed control samples—both of which lack the amorphous TiO2 phase—exhibits no such blue shift (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24), highlighting the critical role of the amorphous interface in inducing a dynamic active state. Although pre-catalysts often undergo morphological or valence changes during operation, operando electrochemical atomic force microscopy (EC-AFM) did not reveal clear morphological evolution—likely attributable to the inherent lateral resolution limits of the technique, which preclude the real-time visualization of structural nuances in sub−10 nm nanocrystallites. To validate the Raman findings and quantify the coordination environment, we performed operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). The Ni and Ti K-edge XANES spectra (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 26) indicate a simultaneous decrease in the oxidation states of both Ni and Ti during HER. Specifically, Ti retains a relatively high-valent character, whereas the Ni K-edge shifts toward the Ni foil reference, evidencing enhanced metallic-Ni character together with partial reduction of TiO2 under HER conditions. Compared with the anatase TiO2 reference, the Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalyst exhibits significantly suppressed white line intensities and broadened post-edge features (w1, w2, and w3), reflecting substantial perturbation of the anatase-like Ti coordination environment and supporting the formation of an oxygen-vacancy-rich TiOx phase41,42. The w1-w3 features arise mainly from dipole-allowed Ti 1 s → 4p/np-like final states, which are shaped by Ti 4p/O 2p hybridization and Ti–O–Ti multiple scattering43,44. Their attenuation is further consistent with the ultrasmall TiOx clusters of 2–3 nm observed by STEM (Fig. 1b), where the finite cluster size and high fraction of surface Ti sites shorten the effective scattering pathways and reduce the scattering coherence required for anatase-like post-edge resonances, especially w2 and w3. Meanwhile, the disordered and undercoordinated TiOx environment perturbs the local Ti–O ligand field and Ti 4p-O 2p hybridized states, leading to the broadening and intensity loss of w145,46. Pre-edge fine structure analysis further reveals that while the reference TiO2 shows the typical triple peak characteristic (p1, p2, and p3) of octahedral inversion symmetry41,47, Ni97.3Ti2.7 displays a significantly enhanced p2 single peak, suggesting that initial oxygen vacancies have already disrupted the local inversion symmetry. Under HER potentials, the pre-edge peak further shifts toward lower energy, pointing to a progressive reduction in Ti–O coordination (Fig. 3e)42,46. More importantly, wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28) shows that the second-shell Ti–O–Ti scattering features shift toward higher k-space under reaction conditions, signifying the formation of Ti–O–Ni interfacial linkages and robust electronic interaction. Quantitative EXAFS fitting (Supplementary Figs. 29–31 and Supplementary Table 6) confirms that at −40 mV vs. RHE, the Ti–O coordination number drops from 5.1 to 3.6, indicating a bias-induced evolution of the TiOx species from a predominantly octahedral [TiO6]-like environment toward a strongly undercoordinated state approaching [TiO4] (Fig. 3h). This structural evolution is consistent with the Raman-observed blue shift of the Ti–O vibrational feature. Simultaneously, an unexpected increase in the Ni–O coordination number provides direct evidence for the dynamic formation of the heterometallic Ti–O–Ni bridge (Supplementary Table 7). These coordinatively unsaturated Ti4c sites likely exhibit enhanced Lewis acidity and serve as effective centers for H2O dissociation and OH− adsorption kinetics48,49, thereby boosting alkaline HER activity and stability.

Direct correlation between OH* adsorption and Ti oxidation state

To establish a direct link between the evolution of oxophilic species and interfacial adsorption dynamics, we utilized ambient-pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (AP-XPS) to track Ti oxidation state and surface adsorbates during reaction. The O 1 s spectra (Fig. 4a–e) show the dynamic evolution of Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalyst under 0.3 mbar of water vapor. A pronounced hydroxyl peak is observed prior to the reaction, primarily originating from the Ni(OH)2. At 40 mV vs. RHE, the intensification of gaseous and liquid water signals indicates the presence of undissociated interfacial water. As the potential shifts to −20 mV vs. RHE, the water adsorption signal weakens significantly while the OH* adsorption signal strengthens, demonstrating optimized water dissociation and hydroxyl binding at this potential. Integrated Ni 2p and Ti 2p analysis (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33) further reveals the dynamic reconstruction of the active sites. At 40 mV vs. RHE, metallic Ni emerges as Ni(OH)2 is reduced, accompanied by an increase in Ti4+ content. At this stage, where TiO2 (especially Ti4+) remains the dominant oxophilic species, water dissociation is limited by the excessively strong hydroxyl binding on Ti. As the overpotential increases, Ti4+ is progressively reduced to Ti3+, concomitant with an elongation of the Ti–O bond from 1.92 Å to 1.95 Å that indicates weakened Ti–O bonding50 (Supplementary Table 6), and the surface composition eventually becoming exclusively Ti3+ at −20 mV vs. RHE. Crucially, the Ti3+ concentration correlates positively with the interfacial OH* adsorption ratio, while the Ni(OH)2 fraction continues to decline (Fig. 4f). These correlations effectively decouple the role of Ti3+ from other oxophilic species, establishing it as the predominant engine for optimizing OH* adsorption kinetics and intermediate stabilization. These results suggest a mechanistic transition: the pre-catalyst surface is initially dominated by Ni(OH)2; upon bias application, Ni(OH)2 is reduced to Ni (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35), and TiO2 (initially as Ti4+) assumes the role of the oxophilic component. At higher overpotentials, the reversible reconstruction into Ti3+ sites provides the optimal energetics for water dissociation and hydroxyl transport, fundamentally accelerating the alkaline HER process. Mechanism investigation

To elucidate the atomic-scale mechanism by which coordinatively unsaturated Ti4c sites (representative of Ti3+) accelerate interfacial kinetics, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on an idealized heterointerface model, while noting that this approach provides qualitative mechanistic insight rather than an exact description of the catalyst under operando conditions. Two structural paradigms were established: a pristine Ni(111) surface and a Ni(111) substrate modified by TiO2 clusters deposited into tri-atomic vacancies, featuring both four-coordinated (Ti4c) and five-coordinated (Ti5c) environments (Supplementary Fig. 36). The TiO2 cluster model was adopted from a literature-reported stable configuration to represent a physically realistic local oxide motif at the Ni/TiO2 heterointerface51–53. The calculated reaction energetics (Fig. 5a) reveal that Ti4c possesses the most pronounced water adsorption affinity among all models, a phenomenon attributed to its unsaturated coordination and the localized electronic modulation exerted by the persistent Ti–O–Ni linkages. Crucially, the water dissociation barrier at Ti4c is a mere 0.75 eV, substantially lower than that of Ti5c. Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis (Fig. 5b) further demonstrates that the d-band center of Ti4c (−0.530 eV) is positioned closer to the Fermi level than that of Ti5c (−0.608 eV), corroborating more favorable kinetics for water activation. These theoretical findings are in good agreement with our operando AP-XPS observations, confirming that the dynamic structural reconfiguration of the amorphous TiO2 pre-catalyst generates highly active Ti4c centers that effectively overcome the Volmer-step bottleneck. The optimized configurations of the water adsorption, transition state, and co-adsorption states (H* and OH*) are illustrated in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38. Upon dissociation, the water molecule preferentially splits at the Ti4c site to form a transient Ti–O(H) –Ni bridging hydroxyl species. This finding provides a critical theoretical rationale for our operando EXAFS data: while the reduction of Ni species to a metallic state would typically decrease the Ni–O coordination number, the experimental increase in Ni–O coordination is precisely explained by the emergence of these bridging hydroxyls. This bridging configuration significantly optimizes the OH* adsorption free energy compared to the terminal Ti–OH species on Ti5c sites (Fig. 5d). Charge density difference (CDD) analysis (Fig. 5e) identifies Ti as an electron-depletion zone, with significant charge accumulation occurring at the flanking oxygen sites. This unidirectional charge transfer—facilitated by the Ti–O–Ni and Ti–O(H) –Ni linkages—not only stabilizes the metallic Ni active sites but also prevents the over-reduction of Ti species under cathodic bias, thereby ensuring the structural integrity of the heterointerface. Regarding hydrogen energetics, pure Ni(111) exhibits an excessively strong hydrogen binding energy (HBE = −0.27 eV), consistent with its suboptimal position on the HER volcano plot. In contrast, for the Ti4c model, hydrogen preferentially adsorbs on the electron-rich oxygen sites with an HBE approaching the ideal value of 0 eV, signifying near-optimal hydrogen turnover kinetics. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the formation of asymmetric Ti–O–Ni and Ti–O(H) –Ni linkages creates a unique local electronic environment that facilitates the rapid adsorption/desorption of reaction intermediates. This provides profound atomic-scale insights into how oxophilic components, when precisely engineered at a heterointerface, fundamentally catalyze the alkaline HER process.

Discussion

Leveraging a grain boundary segregation strategy, we synthesize a crystalline–amorphous Ni/TiO2 heterojunction electrocatalyst via magnetron sputtering. Characterized by uniform and high-density interfacial sites, the catalyst achieves an overpotential of only 32 mV at 10 mA cm−2, alongside robust long-term stability. Recognizing its pre-catalyst nature, we combine a multi-dimensional operando characterization techniques suite to visualize the complete catalytic cycle in real-time. Importantly, our operando evidence demonstrates that, at the Ni/TiO2 heterointerface, potential-driven reconstruction reshapes the local coordination and valence environment of TiOx, driving it toward an oxygen-vacancy-rich and lower-coordination state that regulates water dissociation and hydroxyl handling. These findings establish a direct link between interfacial coordination dynamics and alkaline HER kinetics in a well-defined Ni/TiO2 system, and provide mechanistic guidance for the future design of oxide-modified nano-heterointerfaces in advanced HER electrocatalysts.

Methods

Materials synthesis

NiTi catalysts were deposited via magnetron sputtering onto ultrasonically cleaned carbon paper (CP) purchased from Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd and polished silicon wafers purchased from Kaihua Lijing Electronics Co., Ltd. High-purity nickel (99.99%) and titanium (99.99%) targets purchased from OuLeRui (Baoding) New Materials Technology Co., Ltd were used for pulsed DC and RF sputtering, respectively, with a fixed 80% pulse duty cycle. Prior to deposition, both targets were pre-sputtered for 3 min to remove surface impurities. During the process, the substrate stage rotated at 2 rpm. The resulting samples—denoted as Pure Ni, Ni99.3Ti0.7, Ni97.3Ti2.7, Ni92.6Ti7.4, and Ni77.5Ti22.5—were prepared under specific conditions as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The actual atomic ratios of all samples were confirmed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), with the quantitative data provided in Supplementary Table 2. Finally, the catalysts were naturally oxidized under 20 sccm O2 flow for 30 min.

The 20% Pt/C electrode was synthesized by spraying coating. Pt/C ink was prepared by dispersing 10 mg 20% Pt/C powder (Adamas) in a mixture of 7 mL Isopropanol (Aladdin), 3 mL deionized water, and 22 μL Nafion (5 wt%, Dupont) under sonication. 4 mL ink was coated on the CP (2 × 2 cm2). After drying in air at 80 °C, the mass loading of Pt/C was ~1 mg cm−2.

Characterization

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken on the Apreo2 S Lovac high-performance field emission gun scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) of the Thermo Fisher Scientific Brno s.r.o. Atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM imaging and corresponding EDS/EELS images were conducted using a Titan Cubed Themis G2 microscope, operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. X-ray Diffraction was conducted on a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer under Bragg-Brentano (BB) geometry with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Raman spectra were measured on Horiba XploRA PLUS Confocal In Situ Raman Spectroscopy with laser excitation of 532 nm. X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were recorded on ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer. Agilent 7700X inductively coupled plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) detected the concentration of metal ions. The AFM tests were performed using a DIMENSION Icon atomic force microscope (Bruker Nano Inc.) in the air with calibrated TESPA-V2 tips (Bruker Nano Inc.).

Electrochemical measurement

All electrochemical measurements were conducted in 1 M freshly prepared potassium hydroxide (KOH, pH = 13.92 ± 0.08) electrolyte using a CHI 760 electrochemistry workstation. Unless otherwise specified, KOH (99.99%, Aladdin) was used throughout this work. Measurements were carried out in a single-compartment three-electrode cell, in which the catalyst-coated carbon paper served as the working electrode, a graphite rod was used as the counter electrode, and a standard Hg/HgO electrode was used as the reference electrode. The Hg/HgO reference electrode was calibrated in 1 M KOH against a standard Hg/HgO reference electrode after the open-circuit potential had stabilized. The catalyst was synthesized on a carbon paper (CP, 1 × 1 cm2) through magnetron sputtering. To measure the performance of the HER, all the experiments were performed at 25 °C.

All potentials were calibrated relatively to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) with 90% iR compensation through the calculation formula of ERHE = EHg/HgO + 0.098 + 0.0592 × pH. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were recorded at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out at −1 V (vs. Hg/HgO) from 0.1 to 105 Hz with an AC perturbation amplitude of 5 mV. Operando ambient pressure XPS measurements and data analysis

Operando ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (AP-XPS) was carried out at BL02B at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The beamline 02B is a bending magnet beamline providing photons with an energy range from 40 to 2000 eV, with a photon flux of 1011 photons/s and an energy resolving power of up to 13,000. The analysis chamber was separated from the differentially pumped electrostatic lens system and the hemispherical electron analyzer (Phoibos 150, SPECS Surface Nano Analysis GmbH) by a 0.3 mm aperture. A 100 nm-thick SiNx window was used to isolate the beamline from the analysis chamber. The C 1 s, O 1 s, Ni 2p, and Ti 2p XPS spectra were all collected at the photon energy of 1240 eV with a step size of 0.1 eV using the Hipp-3 electron energy analyzer (Scienta Omicron). For the operando measurements, a custom-built, three-electrode in situ reaction cell was used in the AP-XPS experiment. The batch cell was filled with degassed 1 M KOH electrolyte, containing the Ag/AgCl reference electrode and Pt counter electrode (Supplementary Fig. 40). Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalysts for operando experiments were deposited on Fumasep FAA-3-PK-130 anion-exchange membrane by magnetron sputtering, forming a uniform and continuous catalyst layer directly exposed to the ambient gaseous environment in the analysis chamber. A stable background pressure of ~0.3 mbar was built up in the analysis chamber because of the leakage of H2O from the electrolyte. Beam damage was minimized by examining the spectral integrity of Ti 2p3/2 from the catalyst layer and adjusting the sampling location at each probed experimental condition.

The photon energy was calibrated by a gold foil on the sample holder, and the binding energy was calibrated by the C 1 s peak on Ni97.3Ti2.7 at 284.8 eV. All spectral data were processed and analyzed in Advantage software using a Smart background, a Shirley-based background model, which was subtracted from the plotted spectra. Symmetric components were fitted with Gaussian–Lorentzian mixed line shapes, whereas the metallic Ni component was fitted with an asymmetric line shape. The binding energy of each fitted peak corresponding to a particular species or chemical bond was allowed a maximum variation of ±0.1 – 0.2 eV. An exception was made for gas-phase and liquid-phase water-related features, whose apparent binding energies may shift with applied bias in operando AP-XPS measurements56,57. The absolute BE values of the fitted peaks may vary from those reported in earlier XPS studies on Ni-based materials; however, the chemical shifts in binding energy were consistent with the values measured previously for those materials.

Operando XAS measurement and data analysis

Ni97.3Ti2.7 Catalysts for operando experiments were deposited on carbon paper by magnetron sputtering mentioned above. Operando XAS measurements were carried out in a flow cell containing 1 M KOH electrolyte under a three-electrode configuration (Supplementary Fig. 41), with the catalyst-coated carbon paper serving as the working electrode, a graphite rod as the counter electrode, and a standard Hg/HgO electrode as the reference electrode. The X-ray absorption spectra (XAS), including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) of the samples were collected at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS) center, where a pair of channel-cut Si (111) crystals was used in the monochromator. Ni and Ti K-edge XANES data were recorded in transmission and fluorescence modes, respectively, using the corresponding metal foils as references. The storage ring was working at an energy of 700 MeV with an average electron current of below 200 mA.

XAS data reduction, processing, and EXAFS fitting were carried out using the Athena and Artemis interfaces in the Demeter data analysis packages58, with theoretical scattering paths calculated by the FEFF6 program59. The energy calibration was performed using Ni and Ti foil standards, which were measured simultaneously with the corresponding samples. The pre-edge background was removed by linear fitting, and the edge jump was normalized using Athena software. The χ(k) data were isolated by subtracting a smooth, third-order polynomial approximating the absorption background of an isolated atom. The k3-weighted χ(k) spectra were Fourier transformed using a Hanning window function (Δk = 1.0). EXAFS fitting was conducted in R-space using Artemis by nonlinear least-squares refinement of the EXAFS equation. The coordination number (CN), interatomic distance (R), Debye–Waller factor (σ2), and energy shift (ΔE0) were refined as fitting parameters. The amplitude reduction factor, S02, was determined by fitting the Ni foil and Ti foil references and was obtained as 0.770 and 0.953, respectively. These values were set in the EXAFS analysis of samples to determine the CNs.

Operando electrochemical atomic force microscopy measurement

Pure Ni and Ni97.3Ti2.7 catalysts for operando experiments were deposited on carbon paper or silicon substrate by magnetron sputtering mentioned above. The operando EC-AFM tests were performed using a DIMENSION Icon atomic force microscopy (Bruker Nano Inc.) with calibrated SCANASYST-FLUID+ tips (Bruker Nano Inc.). All operando experiments were conducted in a custom-designed electrochemical cell using a three-electrode configuration in an aqueous electrolyte of 0.01 M KOH (pH ≈12). The catalysts served as the working electrode, with platinum wires functioning as both the counter and reference electrodes. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed prior to the operando measurements to identify the HER potential window and confirm that the observed electrochemical response originated exclusively from the hydrogen evolution reaction.

DFT calculation details

All DFT calculations were carried out using the projector augmented plane-wave method implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package60. The exchange-correlation potential was treated within the generalized gradient approximation using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional61, and the long-range van der Waals interactions were described by the DFT-D3 approach62. The plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff was set to 520 eV, and the electronic self-consistent-field convergence threshold was set to 10−6 eV. A 15 Å vacuum layer was introduced perpendicular to the sheet to avoid artificial interaction between periodic images, and the Brillouin zone integration was sampled with a 2 × 3 × 1 k-mesh. All the structures were relaxed until the residual forces on each atom were below 0.05 eV Å−1. Transition states (TS) for water dissociation were identified using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) and dimer methods and were verified by vibrational frequency analysis, with each optimized transition state showing a single imaginary frequency along the reaction coordinate.
