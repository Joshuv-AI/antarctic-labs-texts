Magrolimab Plus Azacitidine Versus Placebo Plus Azacitidine in Patients With Untreated Higher-Risk Myelodysplastic Syndromes: The Phase III ENHANCE Study
Authors: David A Sallman, Guillermo Garcia-Manero, Naval Daver, Camille N Abboud, Eytan Stein, Karilyn Larkin, Anna B Halpern, Shannon R McCurdy, Monzr M Al Malki, Guru Subramanian Guru Murthy, Lewis R Silverman, Richard A Larson, Peter Greenberg, Ivana Gojo, Tomasz Wrobel, Uwe Platzbecker, Cecily Forsyth, Pankit Vachhani, Mei Dong, Jiang Shao, Lisa DS Johnson, Anderson Tan, Calvin Lee, Parul Doshi, Paresh Vyas, Andrew H Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSETo evaluate the efficacy and safety of the cluster of differentiation 47–targeted antibody magrolimab plus azacitidine (Magro/Aza) versus azacitidine alone in treatment-naïve patients with higher-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) in the phase III ENHANCE study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04313881).METHODSBased on the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System, patients with intermediate- to very-high-risk MDS were randomly assigned to receive Magro (1 mg/kg on days [D]1 and 4; 15 mg/kg on D8; 30 mg/kg on D11 and D15, and then once per week for five doses, followed by 30 mg/kg maintenance doses once every 2 weeks)/Aza (75 mg/m2 daily on D1-7 or on D1-5 and 8-9 in 28-day cycles) or matched placebo plus azacitidine (Placebo/Aza). Dual primary end points were complete remission (CR) rate (per 2006 International Working Group criteria) and overall survival (OS).RESULTSAt final analysis, 539 patients were randomly assigned to Magro/Aza (n = 268) or Placebo/Aza (n = 271) arms. Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced between treatment arms. In the Magro/Aza versus Placebo/Aza arms, the CR rate was 21.3% versus 23.6% (odds ratio, 0.876 [95% CI, 0.585 to 1.312]; P = .5218), and median OS was 15.9 versus 18.6 months (hazard ratio, 1.203 [95% CI, 0.947 to 1.528]; P = .1299). Magro/Aza had a higher incidence of grade ≥3 adverse events (AEs; 92.8% v 79.2%), AE-associated study drug discontinuations (24.0% v 12.1%), serious AEs (71.9% v 51.5%), and fatal AEs (15.2% v 9.8%) versus Placebo/Aza.CONCLUSIONENHANCE did not meet the primary end points of CR rate and OS, and showed more frequent severe AEs in patients treated in the Magro/Aza arm.

INTRODUCTION

Without treatment, patients with higher-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (HR-MDS) have a median overall survival (OS) of <2 years and a high risk of progression to AML.1,2 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (SCT), with/without prior chemotherapy, is the only potentially curative MDS therapy.3,4 MDS typically occurs at an advanced age, with the highest incidence in those age 65 years and older.5 Most patients are SCT-ineligible due to high morbidity/mortality risks.3

Only 7%-32% of patients achieve complete remission (CR) with standard-of-care hypomethylating agents (HMAs), such as azacitidine,6,7 which has limited durability and short survival (median duration of response, 10 months; median OS, 18-26 months).6,8 Azacitidine monotherapy is less effective for TP53-mutated MDS (median OS, ≤12 months).9 New treatments are needed to improve therapeutic efficacy, extend response duration, and maintain tolerability.10-15

The antiphagocytic signal cluster-of-differentiation (CD)-47 is overexpressed in cancers, including MDS, enabling immune evasion.16,17 Magrolimab (monoclonal antibody) selectively targets CD47, induces tumor cell phagocytosis, and further enhances phagocytosis with azacitidine in preclinical MDS/AML models.16-19 In a phase Ib study, magrolimab plus azacitidine (Magro/Aza) was well tolerated with promising efficacy in untreated HR-MDS (CR rate, 33%; median OS, not reached [NR]), and excellent long-term survival in patients who underwent SCT (36% of cohort).20 The randomized, double-blind, multicenter phase III ENHANCE study evaluated Magro/Aza versus placebo plus azacitidine (Placebo/Aza) in treatment-naïve HR-MDS.

METHODS

Patients

Eligible adults had treatment-naïve, intermediate-, high-, or very-high-risk MDS, per WHO classification with Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-R)1 risk criteria; an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance score 0-2; and were not immediately eligible for allogeneic SCT at enrollment (subsequent SCT was allowed). Eligibility criteria are detailed in the protocol.

Trial Design and Interventions

Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive Magro/Aza or Placebo/Aza until disease progression (including treatment failure or relapse after CR/partial remission), loss of clinical benefit, unacceptable toxicities, SCT, investigator discretion, or consent withdrawal. Crossover between arms was prohibited. Randomization stratification included geography (US v ex-US sites), cytogenetic risk (very good/good/intermediate v poor/very poor v unknown per IPSS-R), and bone marrow (BM) blasts (≥10% v <10%). Sample size estimation is described in the Data Supplement (online only).

Magrolimab priming (1 mg/kg) was administered intravenously on days (D) 1 and D4 before escalating to 15 mg/kg on D8, then 30 mg/kg on D11 and D15, and then once per week for five doses; 30 mg/kg maintenance doses were subsequently administered once every 2 weeks beginning 1 week after the fifth once-per-week dose. All patients received subcutaneous/intravenous azacitidine 75 mg/m2 on D1-D7 or on D1-D5 and D8 and D9 every 28-day cycle for a planned minimum of six cycles. Dose modification criteria, repriming indication, and infusion-related reaction mitigation are summarized in the Data Supplement. Approximately 2 years after study initiation (April 2022), anemia management guidance was implemented to better mitigate on-target anemia associated with initial magrolimab exposure. Hemoglobin levels ≥9 g/dL before the first two doses of magrolimab/placebo were required. For hemoglobin <9 g/dL, RBC transfusion to ≥9 g/dL was required. Additionally, postinfusion hemoglobin monitoring was required to measure levels, assess anemia symptoms after the first two magrolimab infusions (3-6 hours after infusion initiation), and consider blood transfusions as clinically appropriate.

Outcomes and Assessments

Dual primary end points were CR rate (investigator-assessed) and OS. Secondary end points included objective response rate (ORR), transfusion independence (TI) rate, event-free survival (EFS), progression-free survival (PFS), CR rate in TP53-mutated MDS (assessed locally), minimal residual disease (MRD)–negative (assessed centrally) response rate, and time-to-transformation to AML (Data Supplement). TP53 mutation status in the biomarker-evaluable population (BEP) was assessed centrally (Data Supplement). Follow-up assessment, including Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy (FACT)-Anemia response, is detailed in the Data Supplement.

Treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) severity was graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs v5.0 (Data Supplement). Broad criteria, including anemia-associated preferred terms, were used to characterize hemolysis (Data Supplement).

Trial Oversight

ENHANCE (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04313881) was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines, Declaration of Helsinki, and local authority requirements. Institutional review boards/independent ethics committees approved the study at each site. All patients provided written informed consent before entry. An independent data monitoring committee reviewed unblinded data generated during the study to monitor and ensure patient safety. Gilead Sciences, the sponsor, funded the trial and participated in its design, conduct, analysis, and data interpretation. All authors had full access to the data and vouched for the completeness and accuracy of the data and analyses and adherence to the protocol.

Statistical Analysis

The efficacy analysis set included all randomly assigned patients in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. The safety population included all patients who received ≥1 dose of a study treatment. If the null hypothesis for CR was rejected at a two-sided significance level of 0.03, OS testing was planned at a two-sided significance level of 0.05; otherwise, it would be tested at a two-sided significance level of 0.02.

CR rate was evaluated at the first interim efficacy analysis (with ≥8 months follow-up on the first 348 randomly assigned patients). If the null hypothesis for CR was not rejected at this time, a nonbinding futility analysis with a hazard ratio (HR) futility boundary of 0.95 was planned for the second interim efficacy analysis, after approximately 257 (71% of 364 planned) deaths (Data Supplement).

CR comparison between arms used the stratified Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test. OS was estimated by using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the stratified log-rank test. HRs and 95% CIs between arms were estimated using the stratified Cox proportional hazards regression model.

Secondary end point and relevant subgroup analyses were conducted similar to that of primary end points; subgroup analysis P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Ad hoc, exploratory landmark and time-dependent covariate analyses are detailed in the Data Supplement.

RESULTS

Patients

From September 9, 2020, through October 14, 2022, 539 patients from 116 sites across 18 countries were randomly assigned and included in the ITT population (Magro/Aza, 268 patients v Placebo/Aza, 271 patients; Fig 1). Overall, characteristics were well balanced between arms (Table 1). Median age was 70 (range, 19-88) years and 26.3% had an intermediate IPSS-R. Numerically, more Magro/Aza-treated patients were age 65 years and older, ineligible for SCT, TP53-mutation–positive, and RBC transfusion–dependent at baseline versus Placebo/Aza-treated patients (Table 1). On study, 55 (20.5%) versus 95 (35.1%) patients proceeded to allogeneic SCT; median time to SCT was 6.05 versus 5.85 months (Data Supplement, Table S1).

Efficacy

Primary End Points

At the first interim analysis (cutoff date, August 12, 2022), CR rate was 20.5% with Magro/Aza versus 25.0% with Placebo/Aza (odds ratio [OR], 0.779 [95% CI, 0.471 to 1.288]; P = .3307; Data Supplement, Table S2). As CR rate was not significant, the second interim analysis (cutoff date, May 19, 2023) was performed (Data Supplement), wherein OS met the prespecified futility boundary (HR, 1.254 [95% CI, 0.980 to 1.605]; P = .0722), and the study was terminated.

After discontinuation of all patients from study treatment, a final analysis was performed (cutoff date, November 29, 2023). At final analysis, median duration of OS follow-up was 11.4 months with Magro/Aza versus 12.5 months with Placebo/Aza. In total, there were 145 (54.1%) versus 132 (48.7%) deaths. Median OS (15.9 v 18.6 months; HR, 1.203 [95% CI, 0.947 to 1.528]; P = .1299; Fig 2A) and CR rate (21.3% v 23.6%; OR, 0.876 [95% CI, 0.585 to 1.312]; P = .5218; Table 2) were similar to those at respective futility and interim analyses. After the anemia management change, there was no OS difference between treatments (median, 14.1 v not estimable [NE] months; HR, 0.850 [95% CI, 0.484 to 1.495]; P = .5785; Data Supplement, Supplementary Results and Fig S1). Landmark analysis of OS by CR status is shown in the Data Supplement (Fig S2). After adjusting for CR status as a time-dependent covariate, the OS HR (95% CI) for Magro/Aza versus Placebo/Aza was 1.11 (0.872 to 1.417); the HR (95% CI) for CR versus non-CR was 0.39 (0.273 to 0.570; Data Supplement, Table S3).

Key Secondary End Points

ORR was 53.7% with Magro/Aza versus 58.7% with Placebo/Aza (OR, 0.821 [95% CI, 0.584 to 1.155]; P = .2563; Table 2). Eight-week RBC TI rates were 27.9% versus 35.2% (OR, 0.720 [95% CI, 0.427 to 1.212]; P = .2191). Median (95% CI) EFS was 13.0 (10.2 to 15.9) versus 12.9 (10.8 to 14.6) months (HR, 0.979 [95% CI, 0.746 to 1.285]; P = .8788; Fig 2B). Median (95% CI) PFS was 9.0 (8.3 to 10.9) versus 9.4 (8.6 to 11.4) months (HR, 1.020 [95% CI, 0.802 to 1.297]; P = .8720; Fig 2C). FACT-Anemia response rates were 37.7% versus 49.8% (Data Supplement, Table S4).

In preplanned CR and OS subgroup analyses, most results were similar to those in the ITT population (Figs 3A and 3B). In patients with locally assessed TP53 status, CR rates (95% CI) were 17.7% (10.0 to 27.9) versus 32.8% (21.6 to 45.7) in the TP53-mutated group (OR, 0.441 [95% CI, 0.203 to 0.960]; P = .0375). CR rates (95% CI) were 21.6% (13.5 to 31.6) versus 18.4% (11.8 to 26.8) in the TP53–wild-type group (OR, 1.219 [95% CI, 0.609 to 2.442]; P = .5760). In patients with locally assessed TP53 status, median OS (95% CI) was 10.1 (7.4 to 13.3) versus 10.4 (8.3 to 13.7) months in the TP53-mutated group (HR, 1.119 [95% CI, 0.763 to 1.641]; P = .5630). Median OS (95% CI) in the TP53–wild-type group was 23.7 (15.1 to NE) versus 26.2 (20.2 to NE) months (HR, 1.103 [95% CI, 0.716 to 1.697]; P = .6568).

MRD-negative response rates (95% CI) were 21.6% (16.9 to 27.1) versus 22.5% (17.7 to 28.0; OR, 0.947 [95% CI, 0.629 to 1.426]; P = .7950; Table 2). Transformation to AML occurred in 34 (12.7%) versus 43 (15.9%) patients (Table 2). Median (95% CI) time-to-transformation to AML was NR (21.2 to NE) versus 25.5 (25.5 to NE) months (HR, 0.837 [95% CI, 0.522 to 1.343]; P = .4610).

Median OS (95% CI) after the 12-month landmark for patients who underwent SCT was NR (10.8 to NE) versus NR (19.2 to NE) months and 17.6 (8.8 to NE) versus 14.2 (6.9 to NE) months in those who did not (Data Supplement, Fig S4); OS rates 100 days after SCT were 90.8% versus 93.7%. After adjusting for SCT status as a time-dependent covariate, the OS HR (95% CI) for Magro/Aza versus Placebo/Aza was 1.13 (0.886 to 1.434); the HR (95% CI) for SCT versus non-SCT was 0.50 (0.350 to 0.710; Data Supplement, Table S3).

Central Assessment of TP53 Mutation Status

Central assessment of targeted sequencing data from 237 versus 235 patients (BEP) showed 37.9% had a TP53 mutation (41.8% v 34.0%). OS in the BEP appeared shorter in TP53-mutated versus TP53–wild-type groups (Fig 3C). Median OS (95% CI) was 10.2 (7.8 to 12.4) versus 11.6 (9.4 to 15.2) months in the TP53-mutated group, and 29.6 (22.4 to NE) versus 31.2 (21.3 to NE) months in the TP53–wild-type group. OS in those who received SCT was shorter in the TP53-mutant group than in the TP53–wild-type group (Data Supplement, Fig S5). TP53 status did not affect the CR rate in the BEP (Data Supplement, Table S5).

Safety

All safety analysis results are presented as Magro/Aza versus Placebo/Aza.

The safety population included 527 patients. Median (range) duration of magrolimab versus placebo exposure was 19.1 (0.1 to 134.7) versus 19.3 (0.1 to 130.9) weeks. Median (range) duration of azacitidine exposure was 18.0 (0.1 to 135.9) versus 18.8 (0.1 to 131.7) weeks (median five cycles per arm).

DISCUSSION

Magro/Aza treatment of HR-MDS did not improve CR rate, and futility was met at the prespecified interim OS analysis. At final analysis, CR rate and OS were consistent with those at prior analyses. Secondary efficacy end points were consistent with the observed OS and CR rate, with Magro/Aza showing no improvement in outcomes. Magrolimab did not improve azacitidine efficacy in any subgroup. In TP53–wild-type patients, OS was similar between arms, regardless of SCT status. Conversely, there was a trend toward inferior OS in TP53-mutant HR-MDS with Magro/Aza, particularly in patients who proceeded to allogeneic SCT. The lower CR rate with Magro/Aza versus Placebo/Aza in the TP53-mutated subgroup could explain the inferior outcomes in Magro/Aza-treated patients who proceeded to SCT.

Safety/tolerability of Magro/Aza were worse than Placebo/Aza, with more TEAEs that were grade ≥3, serious, or led to study drug discontinuation or death. Magrolimab is known to cause anemia, neutropenia, and infusion-related reaction. In ENHANCE, higher incidences of grade ≥3 and serious hematologic TEAEs and infections with Magro/Aza appeared to impact overall tolerability and consequent treatment administration, resulting in higher drug discontinuation rates, including magrolimab discontinuation due to TEAEs within the first two cycles (14.9% v 5.9%). After enrollment of 388 patients, anemia management guidance was implemented to include hemoglobin requirements before and hemoglobin monitoring after the first two magrolimab/placebo doses. This reduced TEAE-related treatment discontinuations in both arms. Although enhanced anemia management improved the tolerability of Magro/Aza, safety profile differences between arms persisted. Notably, the numerical OS difference between arms diminished after the change.

Patients with HR-MDS (typically immunocompromised and have prolonged BM dysfunction) need tolerable therapy that induces rapid BM function improvements. In ENHANCE, Magro/Aza was less tolerable than Placebo/Aza, which likely affected efficacy as continued treatment is typically required to achieve response. SCT rate differences and related outcomes may have contributed to the OS difference in the ITT population and need to be controlled in future pivotal studies. In ENHANCE, landmark and time-dependent analyses indicated an association between SCT and improved OS. OS was comparable between treatment arms regardless of SCT status; however, notably fewer patients in the Magro/Aza arm underwent SCT than in the Placebo/Aza arm. Poorer tolerability likely contributed to the substantially lower SCT rate with Magro/Aza, which occurred regardless of SCT eligibility at enrollment. The SCT rate was also substantially higher in a previous phase Ib study of Magro/Aza in HR-MDS20 compared with that in ENHANCE (36% v 21%).

A potential risk factor that might have affected SCT rates and other ENHANCE outcomes is the high incidence of TP53-mutated disease (not a stratification factor). Although patients with TP53-mutant MDS/AML have similar ORRs to patients with TP53–wild-type MDS/AML, OS outcomes are significantly worse and independent of SCT status.21,22 Magro/Aza did not benefit patients with TP53 mutations versus Placebo/Aza, underscoring the continuing need for effective therapies in this challenging population. Exploration into molecular/genetic mutation profiles is underway to gain further insights into the ENHANCE results.

Little is known about the impact of achieving MRD-negative response in patients with HR-MDS. In AML, MRD-negative response, particularly with a CR, has been shown to predict clinical benefit.23-25 In ENHANCE, landmark and time-dependent analyses identified a treatment-independent association between achievement of CR or MRD-negative response and improved OS. To our knowledge, these data represent the first randomized phase III trial indicating the potential benefit of achieving MRD-negative response in HR-MDS and the importance of incorporating MRD analysis in future studies.

HR-MDS remains a disease with high unmet need. Other randomized studies have not shown superiority to HMAs. Recently, the phase III VERONA trial (N = 509) did not show a significant improvement in OS or CR rates for venetoclax/Aza versus Placebo/Aza.26 Important advancements/refinements in MDS biology and molecular and/or clinical classification may inform future trial designs.1,27-29 Tolerability and impact on the ability to receive treatment and/or SCT are important considerations for future HR-MDS studies evaluating survival.30,31 Although phase Ib Magro/Aza data showed promising safety and efficacy,20 these did not fully extrapolate to ENHANCE possibly due to smaller patient numbers and lack of a control arm in the phase Ib study. ENHANCE data also suggest a potential need for clinical studies to consider early stopping rules for toxicity/mortality. Confounding factors, such as imbalance in SCT eligibility and TP53 mutation status, and safety management changes during enrollment limited ENHANCE data interpretation.

In conclusion, magrolimab did not improve azacitidine efficacy in patients with HR-MDS and trended toward worse OS in TP53-mutant disease. ENHANCE showed a higher incidence of serious TEAEs and TEAEs leading to discontinuation or death. On-target toxicities, a major challenge, could potentially be mitigated with other anti-CD47 strategies under assessment.32 These findings underscore the need for new treatments and individualized studies in TP53-mutant and TP53–wild-type MDS.
