Severe Rheumatic Carditis in a High Resource Setting
Authors: Jake Lesser, Heena Mansuri, Shanna Kowalsky-Herbst, Julie Kantor, Jose U. Mendoza, Ryan Halickman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 6-year-old boy developed acute rheumatic fever after group A streptococcal pharyngitis. Despite prompt diagnosis and adequate treatment, he developed progressive rheumatic heart disease. Given the rarity of acute rheumatic fever in the U.S. population, timely diagnosis with echocardiographic screening and vigilant follow-up are crucial to mitigate rheumatic heart disease complications.

History of Presentation

A 6-year-old boy presented to the emergency department (ED) with 1 week of right knee pain and left foot pain associated with sore throat, fevers, and abdominal pain in the setting of group A streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis diagnosed by rapid point-of-care testing 5 days prior. He had been taking properly dosed amoxicillin-clavulanate, but his symptoms worsened. The patient had not been exposed to sick contacts but did travel to North Carolina several months earlier where he stayed in a cabin. Immunizations were up to date. On arrival at the ED, the patient appeared ill but was cooperative. Vitals signs were within normal limits for age. His posterior oropharynx was erythematous, and he had bilaterally enlarged palatine tonsils without exudates. A systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border was appreciated. There was mild diffuse abdominal tenderness and skin was warm to touch but without any rash. An approximately 1 × 1-cm nontender lymph node was palpated on the right side of the neck. The right knee had no visible swelling or erythema, but pain was elicited on flexion. Mild swelling was noted on the anterior aspect of the left foot. The patient was unable to bear weight on examination. There was no evidence of choreiform movements, subcutaneous nodules, or rash.

Past Medical History

The patient had a past medical history of eczema.

Differential Diagnosis

The constellation of positive GAS testing associated with prolonged fevers and polyarthralgias raised concern for acute rheumatic fever (ARF). Other differential diagnoses included scarlet fever, Lyme disease, postviral arthritis, and poststreptococcal reactive arthritis.

Investigations

Initial laboratory testing was remarkable for elevated inflammatory markers including erythrocyte sedimentation rate >145 mm/h, procalcitonin 0.28 ng/mL, and C-reactive protein 13.3 mg/dL. The respiratory pathogen panel was negative. Antecedent GAS infection was confirmed with an elevated antistreptolysin O titer (>900 IU/L) and anti-DNase B titer (2,920 U/mL). Lyme antibody testing was negative. Complete blood cell count, urinalysis, and complete metabolic panel were unremarkable.

An electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with probable left ventricular hypertrophy and no evidence of prolonged PR interval. Doppler ultrasound of the neck showed multilevel cervical lymphadenopathy. An ultrasound of the right knee showed no evidence of joint effusion. Similarly, a radiograph of the left foot revealed no acute findings.

An echocardiogram revealed a structurally normal appearing aortic valve with mild regurgitation and no stenosis. The aortic regurgitation was seen in multiple views, had a jet length of 1.2 cm, had a peak velocity of 3.5 m/s, and had a pan diastolic jet in at least 1 envelope. There was mild tricuspid regurgitation, trivial mitral regurgitation, and trivial pulmonary regurgitation. Biventricular systolic function was qualitatively within normal limits, and there was no significant pericardial effusion visualized. These findings met criteria for ARF carditis with aortic valve involvement.1

Management

On admission, the patient was started on amoxicillin due to a national shortage of benzathine penicillin G (BPG). After completion of a 10-day course of amoxicillin, the patient was started on secondary penicillin prophylaxis. The patient was discharged on oral penicillin VK twice daily until BPG becomes available for monthly injection. Laboratory work just prior to discharge was notable for downtrending C-reactive protein and procalcitonin but no change in erythrocyte sedimentation rate.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postdischarge, the patient had persistent migrating joint pain often associated with fevers and was evaluated by pediatric rheumatology. His symptoms continued despite regularly administered ibuprofen and he was transitioned to indomethacin with eventual resolution. He continued to take oral penicillin as prescribed because intramuscular injections were not available based on his weight. Subsequent follow-up evaluation with cardiology demonstrated stable valvular disease; however, 6 weeks after his initial presentation, a new I/IV early diastolic murmur was heard during a scheduled follow-up appointment. Echocardiogram performed at that time (Figure 1, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3) demonstrated stable mild to moderate aortic insufficiency with interval progression to moderate mitral insufficiency in the setting of a new prolapse of the anterior leaflet. The left atrium was also dilated, and there was a new moderate left ventricular dilation with preserved function. He was started on lisinopril for afterload reduction and a course of steroids to minimize effects of inflammation. Two weeks later, his echocardiogram demonstrated stable mitral valve and aortic insufficiency and in the setting of persistent heart disease he was diagnosed with rheumatic heart disease (RHD). Discussion

ARF is an aberrant immune response to GAS infection. This immune response typically manifests about 2 to 4 weeks after GAS infection and can lead to the development of various manifestations including Sydenham chorea, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, and migratory polyarthritis. A particularly unfortunate sequela of this acute illness is RHD which carries an increased risk of morbidity and mortality. Chronic cardiac valve damage can predispose affected individuals to heart failure, stroke, arrythmia, and early death.3,4 The presentation of these manifestations can vary significantly between individuals, making the diagnosis of ARF challenging. Therefore, the revised Jones criteria was developed with the aim of facilitating the recognition of ARF and in turn helping in the prevention of adverse complications.5

Per the Centers of Disease Control and Prevention, annual ARF incidence in the United States is unknown because it is no longer a nationally notifiable disease. In the 1920s, ARF was a leading cause of death among children; however, the incidence has declined over time in high-income countries. By the end of the 20th century, ARF had nearly disappeared from high-income countries but continues to be prevalent among low-income nations.6 This is thought to be the result of improved living conditions and the use of antibiotics for the treatment of GAS infections.

Based on current Jones criteria guidelines for ARF in low-risk populations with evidence of preceding GAS infection, this patient met the major criteria of carditis, based on both initial aortic valve disease and subsequent worsening echocardiogram findings, and the minor criteria of polyarthralgia, elevated inflammatory markers, and fever.7 He was started on appropriate oral penicillin prophylaxis; however, ideally, he will be switched to intramuscular treatment when national shortages of BPG resolve. Intramuscular prophylaxis is especially essential in the pediatric population because reliably administering oral medication, particularly to school-aged children at high risk of recurrent viral illnesses that often cause gastrointestinal symptoms, is incredibly difficult. At the time of discharge, the patient was to be on penicillin prophylaxis until 21 years of age, per current guidelines.2

Conclusions

ARF is a condition characterized by an aberrant immune response to GAS infection leading to the development of various manifestations including carditis. An unfortunate sequela is RHD, which is much more common in low- and middle-income countries.7 That said, although still rare in resource-rich countries, clinicians need to be mindful of the importance of close pediatric cardiologist follow-up for continued diagnostic evaluations and management, even in patients whose symptoms resolve and are on proper prophylaxis.
