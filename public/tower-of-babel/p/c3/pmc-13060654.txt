Advancing dementia identification using machine learning and real‐world sequential health data
Authors: Cristian Gonzalez‐Prieto, Mukish Yelanchezian, Gillian Dobbie, Daniel Wilson, Claudia Rivera‐Rodriguez, Bede Oulaghan, Susan Yates, Sarah Cullum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTimely identification of dementia remains a major clinical challenge globally, with many cases being unrecognized. This study evaluated whether using machine learning models with routinely collected health data can support dementia case finding.MethodDe‐identified datasets were used to create a nested case–control of 8195 individuals with dementia and 8195 matched controls. Four models incorporating both cross‐sectional and longitudinal features were developed and tested.ResultsThe best‐performing model achieved an area under the curve of 0.86 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.84–0.87), with sensitivity of 73.3% (95% CI: 72.3–74.2) and specificity of 87.5% (95% CI: 86.7–88.2). Key predictors included time‐stamped International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision diagnostic codes, health‐care use, referral to aged residential care, and hospital delirium assessments.DiscussionA unique feature was the inclusion of “timestamp data” that allowed us to assess the longitudinal changes which may have improved performance of the model. These findings demonstrate the potential for using machine learning with routine health data to enhance early dementia detection.Highlights Machine learning models using routine health data in a real‐world setting in New Zealand had good accuracy for identification of dementia (area under the curve 0.86, sensitivity 73.3%, and specificity 87.5%).Longitudinal sequential “timestamp” data is a unique feature that improved the performance of machine learning models for dementia case finding.Key predictors included International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes, health‐care use, referrals to aged residential care, and positive delirium scores.

INTRODUCTION

The global burden of dementia is increasing rapidly, with the number of people affected projected to triple to 150 million by 2050. 1 Despite this trend, diagnostic capacity remains limited, particularly in early stages of the disease, due in part to a shortage of specialist clinicians and the limitations of current diagnostic tools. Conventional dementia screening tests, such as pen‐and‐paper cognitive tests, are time consuming and resource intensive, and are therefore often overlooked in routine health checks. Many cognitive tests are also culturally and educationally biased as they were mostly developed for English‐speaking Western populations, making their use problematic in diverse populations, in which such tools may lead to inequitable diagnostic outcomes. 2 , 3

In Aotearoa, New Zealand (NZ), dementia poses a growing public health concern. 4 , 5 Māori and Pacific peoples are disproportionately affected, due to a higher prevalence of modifiable risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity. 6 These groups also bear a greater proportion of the financial and caregiving burden associated with the condition due partially to a lack of culturally appropriate services. 7 , 8 Despite the urgency of the problem, global rates of undiagnosed dementia remain high, 9 with approximately half of all dementia cases in NZ going unrecognized. 5

There is a pressing need for scalable, equitable, and efficient approaches to support earlier identification of dementia, particularly among underdiagnosed populations. Routinely collected health data—such as hospital records, diagnostic codes, and prescription histories—offer an underused but promising resource in this context. These datasets are increasingly being leveraged in health research for risk prediction and case finding using machine learning methods. 10 Machine learning models have already demonstrated success in predicting a range of chronic conditions using routine health data, including heart failure 11 and chronic kidney disease. 12 In NZ there are national tools available to predict risk of cardiovascular disease 13 and diabetes. 14 Despite cardiovascular disease and diabetes being major risk factors for dementia, there are currently no risk prediction models available to identify individuals with dementia in NZ.

This study addresses a critical gap by developing machine learning models to identify individuals with dementia using routinely collected health data. We aimed to evaluate a range of models capable of leveraging both cross‐sectional and longitudinal inputs to classify dementia. Particular attention was given to the temporal dynamics of prediction, as a possible measure of dementia prediction prior to disease onset. To enhance model transparency and clinical relevance, we also aimed to examine feature importance to identify key predictors for dementia.

METHODS

Sample

Routinely collected health data were obtained for all patients aged ≥ 65 years from Counties Manukau District, Health New Zealand, Te Whatu Ora, NZ. The data were extracted and de‐identified by the Health Informatics Department, ensuring patient confidentiality. This department has access to several linked national datasets and hospital‐specific sources that are integrated into the hospital's data infrastructure.

The longitudinal variables included laboratory test results, length of hospital stay, International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes corresponding to each hospitalization event, and Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) scores for delirium, 15 all accompanied by their respective timestamps to preserve the temporal dimension of the data. Here, timestamps use sequential longitudinal health data and quantify when each observation occurred by measuring the number of days elapsed since birth to the event, enabling the models to learn temporal patterns in health trajectories rather than treating observations as unordered events. The cross‐sectional variables included sociodemographic characteristics, referrals to aged residential care (ARC) and length of stay in ARC facility, number of visits to outpatient clinics, and number of medications prescribed. Dementia case ascertainment

Case ascertainment of dementia was based on the presence of one or more of the following: ICD‐10 codes related to dementia in the Public and Private Hospital Discharges, records of antidementia drug prescriptions in the Pharmaceutical Data, and/or dementia diagnoses in the interRAI dataset. Patients were classified as having a dementia diagnosis if they had at least one record in one or more of these three datasets indicative of dementia. This method only captures cases of dementia that have been diagnosed and coded. 4 Capture–recapture modeling analysis has demonstrated that, as in most high‐income countries, up to half of dementia is not currently identified in NZ, and therefore not coded in national datasets, 5 but this is the most accurate method of dementia case ascertainment available to date and was therefore used for the current analysis.

Study design

A nested one‐to‐one case–control design was implemented, retaining pre‐diagnostic information for individuals up to the date of dementia diagnosis (index date). The age‐matched control health data were only collected up to the date that the case was diagnosed. This design was selected to address class imbalance and ensure consistency in the length of time that data was collected (“data length”) for both cases and controls. Each case was randomly matched with a control based on age and “data length,” defined as the number of years since the start of data collection for each patient across all datasets.

Machine learning models

We implemented four different machine learning models capable of processing both longitudinal and cross‐sectional data, and able to account for irregular measurement intervals between individuals within a dataset, to compare their performance. Model selection was guided by their capacity to handle temporal dynamics relevant to dementia identification, as key features may evolve over time leading up to diagnosis. Additionally, the approaches varied in how temporal information was incorporated into the models, reflecting an exploratory approach given the absence of a standard methodology for determining the most effective model in this context. The first model (RNN) consisted of a recurrent neural network/gated recurrent unit–based architecture for sequential data processing, also paired with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network for cross‐sectional data. The hyper‐parameters were fine‐tuned using the validation dataset. The second model (Att2) incorporated attention mechanisms, using standard positional encoding and included timestamps as additional features. This attention‐based architecture included three modules: (1) attention on longitudinal features, (2) attention on temporal aspects, and (3) attention on cross‐sectional information. The final two models were standard transformer models: the third model (Transf1) uses standard positional encodings with timestamps as features, while the fourth model (Transf2) uses timestamps as inputs for the positional encoding.

The dataset was randomly split into three mutually exclusive subsets: 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. Model parameters were learned using the training data, while the validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning and monitoring convergence. Once training was finalized, performance was assessed on the independent test set, which was not used during model development (Figure 1).

The performance of the models was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the F1 score. The F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity, offering a single measure that balances both; a value closer to 1 indicates better overall performance.

Feature importance

Permutation importance identifies the features most critical to the dementia identification task. 16 This method requires the trained model f^, features vector x labels y, and a performance measure L(y,f^). The procedure involves permuting the values of each feature j individually, thereby disrupting its association with the true outcome y. To address the method's dependency on permutations, we repeated this process k=50 times for each model. The permutation importance PI^ for the j‐th feature is defined as: PI^j=1k∑r=1kPIrj^ where PIrj^ represents the permutation importance for the j‐th feature in the r‐th iteration (r=1,…,k).

To identify the features most relevant to dementia classification, permutation feature importance analysis was conducted on the models trained using the Significance‐Filtered dataset. The feature integration strategy excluded longitudinal variables with limited sample size and short temporal coverage, as well as cross‐sectional features that did not show statistically significant differences between dementia cases and controls. This reduced the total number of features and, in turn, lowered the computational cost of the analysis. Each feature underwent 100 permutations to assess its contribution to model performance.

Data preprocessing was conducted using R, 17 with model implementation in Python.

Important predictors of dementia across models and datasets

Features that were important for dementia detection in all models were number of ICD‐10 diagnoses, attending geriatric or psychogeriatric outpatients, presence of delirium, and referral for placement in a care home. All of these except for attending geriatric and psychogeriatric outpatients were accompanied with their unique timestamp data. Blood test data including hematological, renal, liver, and thyroid tests were also accompanied by timestamp data and all were found to be important predictors. Pharmacy‐related data were found to be minor features of importance, specifically vitamin supplements in the Transf1 model and psychotropic medications in the Transf2 model; these were not accompanied by timestamp data.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the feasibility of applying machine learning methods to routinely collected health data for identifying individuals with undiagnosed dementia. Our findings suggest that machine learning offers a feasible, scalable, and potentially equitable strategy for enhancing dementia detection in real‐world settings.

In this study, four distinct machine learning architectures were trained and evaluated using routinely collected health data to identify dementia. The models demonstrated strong overall performance, with AUC values comparable to or exceeding those reported in previous studies. The transformer‐based model Transf1 achieved the highest AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.87), with sensitivity of 73.3% (95% CI: 72.3–74.2) and specificity of 87.5% (95% CI: 86.7–88.2). Interestingly, traditional recurrent neural networks performed similarly to more complex transformer‐based models, suggesting that simpler architectures may be better suited for clinical implementation and are viable for real‐world deployment. We found that time‐stamped ICD‐10 diagnostic codes, health‐care use, ARC referrals, and records of delirium all contributed meaningfully to model predictions. While exact rankings varied by model, these features were consistently among the highest in permutation importance scores, indicating a stable signal across datasets and architectures.

Strengths

A unique feature of our study was the inclusion of timestamp data for several important variables including length of hospital stay, ICD‐10 codes at hospital discharge, delirium scores, and laboratory test results. Timestamp data allows us to assess the longitudinal changes that may reflect the progressive nature of cognitive impairment and dementia in which we often see increased comorbidities, use of health services, and admission to care homes, before the diagnosis is even made. 18

The inclusion of delirium‐related features is a novel aspect of the study. Delirium screening is seldom available in routine datasets, yet delirium is strongly associated with dementia. 19 , 20 , 21 Although coverage in the present dataset was incomplete, the ability to incorporate these measures represents an important methodological advantage and directly enhances the clinical relevance of the models. Our study also incorporated features related to ARC, which have rarely been included in previous machine‐learning research on dementia, despite dementia prevalence being known to be > 50% in care home settings. 22 ARC referral and residence were identified as predictive for dementia; this is an intuitively obvious finding, but one that hasn't previously been demonstrated.

Another key strength lies in the multimodal approach to dementia ascertainment. Previous studies have typically defined dementia using diagnosis codes alone, or in combination with antidementia medications. In contrast, our study leveraged three complementary sources of case identification: diagnosis codes, antidementia drug prescriptions, and interRAI assessments. The integration of interRAI data provides an additional and valuable clinical perspective, improving the likelihood of identifying true dementia cases in a context where under‐ascertainment is well documented. 5 , 9 This multimodal definition therefore strengthens the validity of the outcome labels used for model training and evaluation.

Finally, the incorporation of ethnicity in the model is a key strength as there is a disproportionate burden of dementia among the Maori and Pacific peoples living in NZ in whom earlier diagnosis may offer the opportunity to address modifiable risk factors. 4 Notably, ethnicity wasn't ranked in the feature importance, which supports the notion that it is not ethnicity per se that is the predictor of dementia; instead, the differences are likely mediated through underlying health disparities such as comorbidity, which were directly captured in the model.

Limitations

The nested case–control study design used matching by “data length” and age. While beneficial for minimizing confounding, the design restricted the inclusion of age as a predictive variable. Age is one of the strongest known risk factors for dementia and excluding it may have limited the models’ ability to capture age‐related risk heterogeneity. Conversely, incorporating age could have dominated the model and obscured the contribution of other clinically relevant features. Future research could explore alternative modeling strategies, such as approaches leveraging genetic proxies (e.g., Mendelian randomization) or techniques capable of handling partial age information, to balance predictive accuracy with the need for nuanced risk estimation.

Diagnostic information was incorporated as ordered sequences over time (“time stamped”). While this approach enhances predictive capacity, it limits the identification of individual high‐impact diagnostic codes or explicit “chains” of events (e.g., diabetes → falls → dementia). This may reduce transparency compared to models based on predefined clinical groupings and could raise interpretability concerns. Future research might combine temporal modeling with clinically guided feature selection to improve interpretability.

A further limitation relates to model explainability. While deep learning methods offer substantial flexibility and strong performance in complex, high‐dimensional health data, they remain inherently less interpretable than traditional statistical models. Existing explainability techniques, such as permutation feature importance or attention mechanisms, provide valuable insights but remain computationally intensive and offer limited clarity regarding the relative influence of individual features. This restricts the ability to fully understand model behavior across different clinical or demographic subgroups, including ethnicity, and limits the immediate clinical interpretability of the findings.

From a clinical perspective, this study is subject to potential health care interaction bias. High health‐care use may preferentially identify individuals with greater engagement with hospital‐based services. Consequently, sensitivity may be reduced for “invisible” patients—those experiencing cognitive decline but with limited contact with secondary care, including minority ethnic groups facing barriers to health‐care access.

Related to this is the possibility that the model may be identifying patterns of health‐care use that are associated with mortality rather than dementia. As dementia and mortality are closely associated it is not possible in this analysis to disentangle the effects; however, this may not matter in the real‐world application of the model as the aim is to identify undiagnosed dementia to improve health care, and whether there is a higher risk of future mortality.

Finally, as the analysis was restricted to late‐onset dementia (≥ 65 years), the study offers limited insight into early‐onset dementia, a group known to experience more complex diagnostic pathways and longer delays to diagnosis. This could be an area for further investigation in future research.

Comparison to existing research

Seven international studies were identified in the literature that shared similar objectives and methods, using a nested case–control study design: one in the UK, 23 one in Korea, 24 one in Hong Kong 25 and four in the United States. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 These studies focused on detecting dementia using data up to the date of diagnosis. Figure 4 presents the performance value of our best model for dementia identification (Transf1) compared to these seven studies. The reported AUCs were between 0.69 and 0.94 and our model ranked third for AUC behind Li et al. 28 and Ford et al., 23 but performed better than the remaining three models that reported AUCs. 25 , 26 , 27

Comparison of performance metrics across studies using similar study designs. AUC, area under the curve.

Improved model performance may be attributed to very large sample sizes enabling models to better learn feature interactions and improve classification accuracy and the inclusion of a greater number of features. 23 , 28 However, some studies with larger samples had lower AUCs, so there are likely to be other reasons for difference in performance. 25 , 26 Studies that defined dementia using a combination of diagnostic codes and antidementia medications will have reduced misclassification thereby improving case ascertainment and the performance of the models. 28 , 30 One study also incorporated natural language processing (NLP) with clinical notes for the identification of dementia, which may have further improved performance. 23 Those studies that relied solely on ICD‐10 dementia codes recorded in electronic health records to ascertain cases performed less well and this may have been due to higher risks of misclassification. 25 , 27 Nori et al. 26 ascertained cases using a multimodal approach but the model did not perform well. This may have been because it included individuals aged ≥ 45 years and classified mild cognitive impairment (MCI) as a dementia disease state, which may have introduced potential heterogeneity because young‐onset dementia has different etiologies and clinical patterns compared to late‐onset dementia, and not all MCI converts to dementia.

Only four studies including ours reported specificity and, of these, all were > 0.85 with the highest being 0.98. 23 High specificity is important as it reduces the number of false positives if this method is used to case‐find dementia, thus reducing unnecessary diagnostic assessments that may cause avoidable distress to the person and their family.

Key features for dementia identification

The permutation feature importance analysis provided insights into the most influential predictors in dementia identification in our study. Age is the greatest risk factor for dementia but did not present as an important feature in our study because we used an age‐matched case control design, as did many of the comparable studies. However the study design used by Li et al. allowed incorporation of age as a feature, and demonstrated its importance as a predictor of dementia. 28

Other than age, the most important predictors of dementia in our models were time‐stamped ICD‐10 codes at hospital discharge, health‐care use, ARC referrals, and records of delirium. We compared our findings to four comparable studies that used electronic health records only in their models. 24 , 25 , 27 , 28 All four of these studies reported comorbidity (ICD‐10 codes) and two reported health‐care use 24 , 25 to be important predictors, which is understandable as both are associated with increased risk of dementia. 18 Studies that measured specific comorbidities as predictors of dementia identified obesity, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and depression as strong predictors, which aligns with those described in the Lancet Commission report (2024). 31 We did not measure the effect of individual hospital discharge ICD‐10 codes but believe the use of timestamp data with hospital discharge ICD‐10 codes acted as a surrogate for increasingly complex multimorbidity.

No other studies included referral to or residence in a care home in their models, but we found these to be important features for identification of dementia in our study. There is also the possibility that referral to ARC is a strong predictor due to diagnostic opportunity bias, as referral to ARC may follow clinical assessment processes that contribute to dementia recognition. Although all input data were restricted to the pre‐diagnosis period to minimize information leakage, ARC‐related variables may still partially capture patterns of health‐care engagement associated with diagnostic ascertainment rather than disease biology alone. To assess this, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding ARC referral and residence variables. Model performance remained stable (see Table 3), suggesting that while ARC features are informative, they did not materially drive classification or introduce substantial bias. We retained ARC data in the final model as we aimed to maximize the potential for health‐care system–mediated signals influencing dementia prediction.

Implications and future directions

The most important predictors of dementia in our models were longitudinal ICD‐10 hospital discharge codes, health‐care use, ARC referrals, and records of delirium, all of which are likely to be a proxy measure of the patients’ comorbidities and frailty, which are related to risk of dementia. Combined with timestamp data they create a longitudinal picture of the person's health journey (and associated dementia risk) that individual clinicians may not see on a day‐to‐day basis. This provides a potentially powerful model that could be used as a clinical decision support tool in clinical settings, for example as a tool for dementia case finding, which would have particular relevance when access to specialist diagnostic services is limited. Although the nested case–control design is useful as it represents the underlying target population and helps to balance the sample, the disease prevalence is much higher than in a real‐world setting. Future work should evaluate model performance under real‐world prevalence conditions and assess calibration in population‐representative cohorts prior to clinical implementation.

The results of this study align with previous research indicating that machine learning models have the potential to assist health‐care professionals in identifying undiagnosed dementia cases and flagging high‐risk individuals. Such tools have the potential to enable timely interventions to support the person with dementia but there is also need for further research into the ethical implications of implementing machine learning models in clinical practice. 23 Any screening program must be ethically justified by the availability of an accepted and available treatment for identified patients, including appropriate diagnostic procedures and structured post‐diagnostic support.

The models should be understood as context‐specific implementations rather than universally deployable tools. We note that, while the specific features and data streams (e.g., interRAI data) used in this study may not be available elsewhere, the underlying modeling framework remains transferable. In particular, machine learning approaches such as transfer learning or domain adaptation could allow models trained in the NZ setting to be adapted to alternative health‐care systems by fine‐tuning on locally available data sources. Future work should explicitly evaluate cross‐system generalizability and identify minimal feature sets that preserve predictive performance across different health‐care contexts.

CONCLUSION

Our findings reinforce the value of routinely collected health data as a scalable and underused resource for developing machine learning–based dementia case‐finding tools. By supporting equitable identification of at‐risk individuals, such models have the potential to improve access to timely interventions, optimize health‐care planning, and reduce the burden of undiagnosed dementia. Moving forward, careful attention must be paid to the ethical dimensions of model implementation, including transparency, fairness, and cultural appropriateness, to ensure public trust and clinical utility.
