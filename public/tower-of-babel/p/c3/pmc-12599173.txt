Can therapists and senior advisors predict improvement of PTSD severity, based on clinical intuition and liking of their patients?
Authors: Frank Enning, Kathlen Priebe, Regina Steil, Nora Görg, Meike Müller-Engelmann, Christian Schmahl, Nikolaus Kleindienst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective: Predicting symptom improvement in psychotherapy is a complex challenge. This study aims to extend traditional prediction methods by examining the potential roles of therapists’ and senior advisors’ clinical judgments and of their levels of liking of patients in predicting symptom improvement.Methods: Eighty women with PTSD related to childhood abuse treated with either cognitive processing therapy (CPT) or dialectical behaviour therapy for PTSD (DBT-PTSD) were included in the study. Four general linear models were used for predicting pre-to-post changes in the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (ΔCAPS) from (i–ii) the therapists’ and senior advisors’ intuitive predictions of symptom change, and (iii–iv) the therapists’ and senior advisors’ liking of their patient. These four ratings were assessed after the fourth therapy session.Results: Therapists’, but not clinical senior advisors’ intuitive predictions emerged as a strong and significant predictor of ΔCAPS (F1,73 = 11.37, p = .0012, partial η2 = 0.1348, and F3,69 = 1.04, p = .3799). Liking ratings of both the therapist and the senior advisor were largely unrelated to treatment success (p-values > .15; partial ηs2 < 0.0270). None of the four predictor*group interactions were significant.Conclusions: Firstly, therapists may have a good sense of whether the symptoms of their patient will improve after only four therapy sessions. Further research should clarify whether early clinical assessment by therapists is a substantive source of information that can be expanded upon and integrated into individualized treatment, or whether it acts as a self-fulfilling prophecy. Secondly, the lack of substantial association between therapist’s low liking of their patients is encouraging, given that ratings ranged from as low as 4/10 to 10/10, yet outcomes remained stable within these structured and supervised PTSD protocols. Further research should clarify whether this advantage also applies to treatments with a low degree of manualization and supervision.Trial registration: German Clinical Trials Register identifier: DRKS00005578..KEYWORDS: Childhood abuse, dialectical behaviour therapy, cognitive processing therapy, dissociation, (complex) posttraumatic stress disorder, psychotherapy

Background

Identifying reliable predictors of therapy outcomes – in particular symptom improvement, – is critical for refining treatment selection and advancing personalized therapeutic approaches (Connor & Callahan, 2015).

Various predictors of PTSD treatment outcomes have been investigated, including demographic characteristics, symptom severity, biomarkers, and process variables (Karatzias et al., 2019; McDonagh et al., 2005). However, findings on many predictors remain inconsistent and often lack replication (Keyan et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2020; Semmlinger et al., 2024). For example, dissociation has been identified as a significant predictor of treatment success in some studies but was unrelated to outcomes in others. Where effects were observed, higher dissociation was usually associated with poorer treatment response. In a meta-analysis by Hoeboer et al. (2020) dissociation was not a significant predictor of psychotherapy effectiveness in PTSD. Similarly, co-occurring psychiatric disorders have been linked to treatment response, but with only modest and inconsistent effects (Stuke et al., 2021). For example, meta-analytic findings indicate that comorbid major depression and substance-use disorders are associated with poorer outcomes, although effects are generally small (Lewis et al., 2020). While younger age has been associated with poorer treatment outcomes in some studies (Cornelisse et al., 2023; Semmlinger et al., 2024), demographic factors generally show weak or inconsistent associations with PTSD treatment results (Kratzer et al., 2018; Steuwe et al., 2023). One factor consistently associated with better treatment outcomes is a strong therapeutic alliance (Howard et al., 2022; Sharf et al., 2010; Steil et al., 2023).

The therapeutic alliance, as defined by Howard et al. (2022) and Steil et al. (2023), consists of three key components: agreement between therapist and patient on treatment goals, consensus on specific strategies and interventions, and an emotional bond characterized by trust and rapport. Therapeutic alliance is particularly relevant in PTSD treatment, where emotional distress and interpersonal mistrust can hinder therapy effectiveness (Sharf et al., 2010; Steil et al., 2023; Swift & Greenberg, 2012). Recent research has emphasized the importance of therapist- patient interactions in shaping treatment outcomes. For example, Bowker et al. (2025) demonstrated that first impressions during clinical assessments significantly predict treatment engagement and retention. Given that in clinical practice therapists and senior advisors typically rely on their clinical judgment rather than formal assessment tools to predict treatment success, further exploration of clinical predictors is warranted (Norcross & Lambert, 2018).

Beyond formal assessment tools, therapists and senior advisors often rely on clinical intuition, whether a patient will benefit from treatment. In this trial, ‘senior advisors’ were licensed psychotherapists with extensive clinical experience who were not the treating therapist. They briefly met each patient after session four and attended weekly case conferences, providing input in a supervisory capacity. Their ratings therefore represent a comparatively independent perspective. Rather than being a vague ‘gut feeling’, clinical intuition can be defined as a rapid, non-conscious form of reasoning that draws on tacit knowledge and accumulated clinical experience (Dane & Pratt, 2007; Kahneman & Klein, 2009). It involves the implicit processing of complex information, enabling clinicians to detect subtle patterns that may not be accessible through deliberate reasoning. While therapists typically form such impressions through ongoing therapeutic contact, senior advisors have mostly limited contact with patients, allowing for a more independent perspective. Clinical intuition has gained attention as a potentially valuable factor in clinical decision-making. For example, Bowker et al. (2025) found that initial impressions formed during the first interactions with patients suffering from depression or anxiety were significantly related to whether the patients commenced treatment (treatment initiation) and their subsequent dropout rates. In their study, clinical intuition was measured using brief clinician ratings of first impressions following the initial assessment. This emphasizes the importance of early therapist-patient interactions. However, their study had a relatively small sample size and did not assess the predictive validity of these intuitive impressions with regard to long-term treatment outcomes.

Another factor that may influence treatment outcomes is the therapist's spontaneous sense of liking for the patient. We use the term ‘liking’ descriptively, reflecting the exact wording of our item. It denotes an early affective appraisal, which may arise from mechanisms such as reciprocity (Sprecher et al., 2013), and should be distinguished from the therapeutic alliance, which involves collaboration on goals, tasks, and bond (Howard et al., 2022; Steil et al., 2023). While extensive research highlights the benefits of positive regard, including expressing warmth and conveying appreciation (Farber et al., 2018), the impact of spontaneous liking, or its absence, remains largely unexplored. It remains unclear to what extent such responses relate to treatment outcome. In a correlative analysis, Traynor et al. (2011) suggest that patients who were perceived more favourably by their therapists are more likely to remain engaged in therapy and show greater improvement. It remains largely unknown to which extent the degree of liking may influence treatment engagement and outcomes. At any rate, these findings are based on limited sample sizes and require replication. When studying psychotherapy of BPD or PTSD related to childhood abuse, between therapist variance of the degree of liking might be particularly high. Research by Hepp et al. indicates that individuals with BPD may be perceived negatively by others, thus potentially affecting therapeutic alliance and outcomes (Hepp et al., 2019; Hepp et al., 2021). Given the high prevalence of co-occurring borderline personality disorder (BPD) among individuals with PTSD (Grant et al., 2008; Scheiderer et al., 2015), therapist’s liking may be particularly relevant in this group of patients. Similar considerations regarding clinical judgment and liking of the patient apply to the senior advisors.

Given these considerations about clinical judgment, it is worth examining the broader evidence base. Research regarding the accuracy of clinical predictions dates back to the 1950s. Meehl (1954) suggested that standardized assessment methods tend to outperform clinical intuition, and during the following decades, prediction of outcome focused on rigorous methods based on statistics applied to questionnaires, interviews, and biological markers (Semmlinger et al., 2024).

In most studies, questionnaires were used both at the start of therapy, e.g. for assessing therapist and patient variables and to systematically record the therapeutic process (Eubanks et al., 2018; Flückiger et al., 2012; Norcross & Lambert, 2018; Stiles, 2009). However, recent meta-analyses yielded somewhat disappointing evidence for most predictors with inconsistent results and small to medium effect sizes at best (Keyan et al., 2024; Semmlinger et al., 2024).

At present, we are still a long way from being able to predict the success of psychotherapy with any real degree of reliability. Accordingly, it seems plausible to study and to potentially use all available sources for outcome prediction, including intuitive clinical judgments.

In addition to their complementary role in outcome prediction, clinical assessments have the practical advantage of being available without further precautions. Especially in routine care settings, where access to structured assessment tools is limited, such as in outpatient practice, clinical intuition offers a low threshold and broadly available option. While standardized methods may be more accurate on average, both approaches have their place in clinical decision making, and their thoughtful integration may offer a useful step forward.

With the attempt to make progress in these areas, our study aims at systematically evaluating whether the therapists’ and senior advisors’ clinical intuition predict treatment outcome. In particular, we examined whether early assessments and the degree of liking of the patient, as documented by the therapist and senior advisor after the fourth treatment session, are associated with improvement in PTSD symptomatology in patients treated with CPT or DBT-PTSD.

Methods

Study design and procedure

The data is part of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) (Bohus et al., 2019; Bohus et al., 2020) comparing two psychological treatments (CPT and DBT-PTSD) for treating PTSD related to childhood abuse (CA). In the original RCT, 193 women were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive 45 therapeutic sessions during 15 months of outpatient treatment with either CPT or DBT-PTSD. A detailed description of general procedures including randomization and recruitment can be found in publications (Bohus et al., 2019; Bohus et al., 2020; Kleindienst et al., 2021). In short, we included women aged 18–65 years into the study if they met the criteria of a CA-related PTSD and met at least three diagnostic criteria for BPD, including the criterion on affective instability (criterion 6). Exclusion criteria were a diagnosis of schizophrenia, a lifetime diagnosis of bipolar I disorder, substance dependence (current), medical conditions contradicting exposure therapy (in particular pregnancy), psychopathology requiring immediate treatment in a different setting, unstable living situation, treatment with CPT or DBT-PTSD in the previous year, mental retardation, and a life-threatening suicide attempt in the previous 2 months. As described previously (Bohus et al., 2020), non-suicidal self-injury, high-risk behaviours, or suicide attempts with a low risk of death were not among the exclusion criteria. Individuals who had provided their written informed consent were randomized to receive either CPT or DBT-PTSD. Further detail is provided in Bohus et al. (2019) and in the German Clinical Trials Register (registration number DRKS00005578). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the pertinent ethical review boards. Generative AI (ChatGPT4o) was used to assist with formulation and language refinement in the introduction section. All AI-generated content was critically reviewed and edited by the authors.

The present study is a hypothesis-driven post-hoc analysis predicting change in PTSD-severity from clinical prediction and the degree of liking of the patient by both the therapist and the senior advisor. Their clinical prediction was based on a structured meeting with the patient and therapist after session four. Systematic assessments regarding clinical prediction and liking were planned for all 193 participants in the original RCT. However, only data from 80 participants could be included in the present analysis due to incomplete or missing prediction forms. In the other cases, the rating by the therapist and the senior advisor were missing or the participants had stopped the therapy. These ratings were assessed in the fourth week of treatment, after four therapeutic sessions and an introductory meeting between therapist, patient and senior advisor. Both the therapist and the senior advisor were asked to ‘please rate on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (maximum)’: ‘How much will the patient benefit from the therapy in terms of PTSD?’ [clinical prediction] and ‘How much do you like the patient (be honest!)?’ [liking of the patient]. Among those 80 participants, both ratings (i.e. clinical prediction and liking) of the treating therapist were available for 77 individuals. A total of 73 senior advisors gave their ratings – 72 provided both ratings, and one senior advisor provided the rating for clinical prediction only. The therapist and the senior advisor were explicitly blind to each other’s assessment. In particular, they carried out their ratings at different locations and sealed them in an envelope.

Measures

Diagnoses of PTSD were established from the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS-5), (Dittmann et al., 2017; Weathers et al., 2018). Co-occurring axis-I disorders and BPD were assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID-I, First et al., 1997) and the International Personality Disorder Examination (IPDE, Loranger et al., 1994), respectively. All diagnoses were based on interviews carried out by specifically trained psychologists who were blind with respect to the treatment group. The Borderline Symptom List short form (BSL-23) assesses borderline symptom severity across 23 items rated 0–4, summarized as a mean score; higher scores indicate greater symptom burden. The Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) is a single clinician rating from 0 to 100; higher scores reflect better overall functioning. In line with the pre-registration of the RCT (reg. number DRKS00005578 at the German Clinical Trials Register) the total score of the CAPS-5 was used as the pertinent variable for assessing potential benefit from the therapy in terms of PTSD. The CAPS-5 items were related to the index trauma, i.e. the currently most distressing event or cluster of events (Priebe et al., 2018). The CAPS-5 was assessed repeatedly including a baseline assessment, and scheduled assessments after 3 months, 6, 12 and post therapy after 15 months (Bohus et al., 2020; Kleindienst et al., 2021). Missing values in the dependent variable (i.e. the CAPS-5 total score) were imputed by methods aiming at minimizing both overfitting and bias. To this end, a two-step procedure was applied: if no more than 2 CAPS-5 items (≤10%) were missing, stochastic regression imputation based on the other CAPS-5 items was applied at the item level. If the entire CAPS-5 assessment or more than 3 items were missing, multiple imputation based on the scores preceding and following the score to be imputed, dropout status, and treatment group were applied at the scale level. The imputation has been described in detail elsewhere (Bohus et al., 2020). The independent variables (i.e. clinical prediction and liking) were not imputed.

Participants

The sample studied here included the 80 cis-women aged 18–62 for whom the therapist and/or the senior advisor provided a rating for the intuitive predictions of symptom change and/or the liking of their patient. All patients had a diagnosis of PTSD assessed from the CAPS-5. Besides PTSD the patients currently met an average of 2.5 ± 1.52 axis-I disorders (range: 0–6). Forty-eight women (60%) have been randomized to CPT, 32 women (40%) have been randomized to DBT-PTSD. Further characteristics of the patients included in the study are provided in Table 1. No significant differences between the two groups regarding baseline characteristics were detected. A total of 26.3% (21/80) of the participants dropped out.

Treatments

CPT is a psychological treatment focused on identifying, challenging, and restructuring dysfunctional cognitions and emotions related to trauma (Resick et al., 2008). In our study, sessions 1–4 established emergency procedures, patient history, and dysfunctional behaviours before starting the original CPT protocol at session 5 (Bohus et al., 2019; Kleindienst et al., 2021). Then, patients write an ‘impact statement’ about the worst event, its effects, and their beliefs on trust, safety, control, power, self-esteem, and intimacy. These beliefs (‘stuck points’) are identified and challenged (Resick & Schnicke, 1992).

DBT-PTSD is a modular treatment tailored for complex CA-associated PTSD, incorporating DBT skills training, cognitive and exposure-based techniques, and compassion-focused interventions (Bohus et al., 2019). It was specifically tailored for women with complex PTSD and combined PTSD and BPD diagnoses (Bohus et al., 2013; Bohus et al., 2020; Kleindienst et al., 2021). Further details on DBT-PTSD are available in prior publications (Bohus et al., 2019; Bohus et al., 2020). Both treatments were parallelized in terms of session frequency and dose within the study.

To ensure integrity of the treatments, prior to the study, participating therapists were trained in either DBT-PTSD or CPT in 4-day workshops led by the respective treatment developers. They were assigned exclusively to either CPT or DBT-PTSD. All therapists had regular weekly team consultations. The arms were balanced with respect to therapists’ experience, age, and structural characteristics, such as the number of patients (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Detailed therapist demographics (e.g. gender, mean age, level of training, academic degree) were not systematically recorded in this study and are therefore not reported. All therapists had a background in cognitive–behavioral therapy.

Statistical analysis

Separate general linear models (GLMs) were used for testing the main hypotheses, i.e. whether (i) clinical prediction of improvement and (ii) the degree of liking of the patient predicted change in PTSD symptom severity. The change in PTSD symptom severity was operationalized as change in the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (ΔCAPS) from baseline (T1) to post-treatment (T6) (Δ16CAPS). In the sensitivity analyses we used change from the 3-month assessment (T2) to post-treatment (Δ26CAPS). Sequential sum of squares (i.e. type-I sum of squares) were used. To account for the potentially confounding effect of group (CPT vs DBT-PTSD), group was included as a covariate (x1). The predictor (e.g. clinical prediction by the therapist) was next (x2), followed by the interaction of group by predictor. Bonferroni–Holm correction was applied, thereby correcting for four analyses. To avoid spurious results, we checked for significant deviations from normality and for influential outliers (criterion: Cook's D > 4/sqrt(n)) which is the default threshold in the software used (SAS™ 9.4). Because the assessments of the therapists and senior advisors was not exactly a baseline assessment but rather fell into the interval between the first two regular assessments of the CAPS at baseline and after three months, each of the four models was supplemented with a sensitivity analysis. In this sensitivity analysis the criterion ΔCAPS defined as CAPSpre-CAPSpost was replaced using ΔCAPS from the first assessment after these assessments (i.e. 3 months after the start of treatment) to post-treatment instead. Furthermore, we reported the usual first order correlations between the variables of interest. During the manuscript non-adjusted 2-tailed p-values are reported. However, all p-values reported as statistically significant remained significant after Bonferroni–Holm correction. Analyses were performed with SAS™ (v.9.4).

Results

Clinical prediction of treatment success

The mean therapist rating for clinical prediction of PTSD improvement was 7.38 ± 1.46 (range: 3–10). The corresponding mean senior advisor rating was 7.36 ± 1.58 (range: 3–10). This means that both therapists and senior advisors were mostly optimistic regarding the improvement of their patient. The ratings of the therapists and of the senior advisors were strongly correlated (r = 0.5680, p < .0001).

When clinical predictions are based on the therapists’ ratings, the general linear model (GLM) including group, clinical prediction and the interaction of group by clinical prediction significantly predicted pre–post improvements in the severity of PTSD (Δ16CAPS) (F3,73 = 4.94, p = .0035). The effect of group was not significant (F1,73 = 3.25, p = .0755, partial η2 = 0.0426). The effect of clinical prediction was significant, even after accounting for the effect of group (F1,73 = 11.37, p = .0012, partial η2 = 0.1348). The interaction of group by clinical prediction did not significantly explain additional variance within this model (F1,73 = 0.18, p = .6691, partial η2 = 0.0025). These results are summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Figure 1.

Degree of liking of the patient

The therapists’ mean rating of liking of the patient was 7.83 ± 1.54 (range: 4–10). The corresponding mean score for senior advisors was 7.86 ± 1.43 (range: 3–10). This result means that both therapists and senior advisors generally felt a high level of liking of the patient. The ratings of the therapist and of the senior advisor regarding the liking of the patients were significantly correlated (r = 0.4033, p < .0001). Furthermore, the therapists’ liking of the patient was significantly correlated with the the therapists’ clinical prediction of PTSD improvement (r = 0.3791, p = .0007). Similarly, the senior advisors’ liking of the patient was positively correlated with the senior advisors’ clinical prediction of PTSD improvement (r = 0.5981, p < .0001).

Based on the therapists’ liking of the patient, the general linear model (GLM) including group, liking and the interaction of group by liking did not significantly predict pre–post improvements in the severity of PTSD (Δ16CAPS) (F3,73 = 1.81, p = .1531). Within this model, none of the effects were significant (group: F1,73 = 2.90, p = .0926, partial η2 = 0.0383; liking: F1,73 = 2.02, p = .1598, partial η2 = 0.0269; group*liking: F1,73 = 0.51, p = .4794, partial η2 = 0.0069). Sensitivity analysis

The results of the four models predicting changes in PTSD severity pre (T1) to post (T6) (Δ16CAPS) based on therapist and senior advisor clinical prediction and spontaneous liking of the patient were fully confirmed by analyses of improvements in PTSD severity after the T2 assessment (Δ26CAPS), i.e. the first assessment of CAPS after clinical prediction and spontaneous liking of the patient were assessed. Again, the effect of clinical prediction by the therapist was significant (F1,73 = 11.71, p = .0010, partial η2 = 0.1382), even when accounting for the effect of group. Again, this result remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Again, none of the other three predictors (i.e. the senior advisor’s clinical prediction and the therapist's or senior advisor’s spontaneous liking of the patient) were significant or approached statistical significance (all uncorrected p-values > .1).

The robustness of the main findings was further supported by uncorrected correlation analyses. Again, the therapist's clinical prediction was positively correlated with pre–post improvements in PTSD severity (r = 0.3917, p = .0004). In contrast, no significant correlations were found for the other predictors (senior advisor’s clinical prediction: r = 0.1567, p = .1737; therapist's spontaneous liking of the patient: r = 0.1288, p = .2774; senior advisor’s spontaneous liking of the patient: r = 0.1239, p = .2999).

Discussion

The present study addressed two questions. First, we examined whether therapists’ and senior advisors’ clinical predictions of disorder-specific symptomatic improvement actually predicted their patients’ improvement in PTSD-related symptom severity. Second, we examined whether therapists’ and senior advisors’ levels of liking of the patient were related to change in PTSD-related symptom severity.

Therapists’ (but not senior advisors’) clinical prediction emerged as a clinically and statistically significant predictor of symptom change. With respect to the degree of liking, it is true that some patients elicited much more liking from the therapists and senior advisors than others. However, we found no evidence that the degree of the liking towards the patient at an early stage of treatment is related to change in PTSD-related symptom severity. In contrast, most patients improved – irrespective of the degree of liking that they have elicited from the therapists and senior advisors. In sum, our findings indicate that therapists’ clinical intuition after the fourth session is statistically significant predictor of PTSD symptom improvement, while the degree of liking for the patient were not significantly associated with treatment outcomes.

Our findings align with previous research emphasizing the significance of clinical intuition in therapeutic settings. Westen and Weinberger (2004) highlighted that experienced clinicians often rely on their intuitive judgments to make accurate predictions about treatment outcomes. Our study extends this finding specifically to PTSD treatment, providing empirical evidence that therapists’ clinical predictions are indicative of PTSD-specific improvement. Additionally, research has demonstrated that therapist- patient interactions, including the therapeutic alliance, are predictive of PTSD symptom reduction (Sharf et al., 2010; Steil et al., 2023; Swift & Greenberg, 2012).

The finding that clinical predictions were significantly associated with symptom improvement supports the growing interest in intuitive clinical judgment as a predictor of treatment success. This aligns with Bowker et al. (2025), who found that first impressions during clinical assessments predicted treatment engagement and retention. Our study builds on these findings by demonstrating that such clinical intuition is also predictive for symptomatic improvement in PTSD. However, while therapists’ predictions were significant, senior advisors’ clinical judgments did not emerge as predictors. This might be related to the specific roles of therapists and senior advisors. Therapists interact with patients more frequently and may develop a deeper understanding of their progress and responsiveness to treatment. Furthermore, therapists’ predictions were not independent assessments but may have interacted with the treatment process, potentially contributing to a self-fulfilling prophecy. These predictions should therefore not be interpreted as objective measurements but as clinical impressions that may interact with the therapeutic process itself. It remains unclear to what extent such early expectations reflect accurate assessment versus exerting influence on the treatment trajectory, for example by shaping therapeutic behaviour or alliance-related dynamics. In this context, affective factors such as the therapist's liking of the patient might also play a role.

In contrast, our study did not find evidence that therapists’ or senior advisors’ level of liking towards the patient was related to PTSD symptom change. This finding is notable, as prior research (Traynor et al., 2011) suggested that patients with psychotic symptoms perceived more favourably by their therapists tend to remain engaged and show greater improvement. Our results suggest that while spontaneous liking may influence engagement, it does not necessarily translate into improved symptom outcomes. This distinction may be particularly relevant in PTSD treatment, where emotional distress and a lack of interpersonal trust can challenge therapeutic relationships (Sharf et al., 2010).

Furthermore, the absence of an association between therapist liking and symptom improvement aligns with the broader literature on positive regard. While Farber et al. (2018) highlighted the benefits of positive regard in psychotherapy, our findings indicate that spontaneous liking, or liking as an intuitive response, may not have the same impact on symptom reduction. This supports the notion that professional therapeutic relationships can remain effective regardless of personal affinity towards the patient. A plausible reading is that early liking shapes how the relationship feels, whereas structured goal and task focused work, as captured by the alliance literature, more reliably relates to symptom change in these manualized treatments (Swift & Greenberg, 2012). Spontaneous liking itself is likely influenced by common interpersonal factors, with social psychological studies highlighting the role of similarity and reciprocity in early impressions (Sprecher et al., 2013). In addition, therapist characteristics may shape both clinical predictions and liking. Factors such as professional experience, interpersonal style, or personal values could influence how therapists form early impressions, although these variables were not examined in the present study.

Finally, our study also explored the potential role of senior advisor intuition in predicting treatment outcomes. Senior advisors play a key role in guiding treatment strategies and case discussions (Callahan et al., 2009), and their outside perspective on the therapist- patient relationship could provide unique insights. However, our findings suggest that senior advisors’ clinical judgments at an early stage of treatment are not strong predictors of PTSD symptom change. This may be due to their more limited direct interactions with patients compared to therapists. This finding aligns with earlier theoretical work Meehl (1954), which emphasized that clinical judgement is often less reliable than statistical methods. Recent meta-analyses have similarly highlighted that even standardized predictors of treatment outcome yield inconsistent or weak results (Keyan et al., 2024; Semmlinger et al., 2024). In addition, a number of studies have explored the role of clinical intuition in predicting treatment engagement or outcomes, but results remain mixed (Bowker et al., 2025; Westen & Weinberger, 2004).

Limitations

Our study has several strengths, including a well-defined sample of cis-women with PTSD related to childhood abuse and the use of established and psychometrically tested instruments (e.g. the CAPS-5) for assessing PTSD symptom severity. Additionally, the perspective of both therapists and senior advisors provides a dual view of clinical predictions and sympathies. The use of rigorous diagnostic procedures and manualized state-of-the-art therapies assessed for adherence during the study further strengthens the validity of our findings.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. While our findings suggest that therapists’ clinical predictions are significant predictors of PTSD symptom improvement, our study design does not allow us to determine the mechanisms underlying these predictions. Future research should explore whether these predictions reflect an implicit understanding of patient characteristics, responsiveness to treatment, or other unmeasured factors or whether clinical predictions might (partially) act as self-fulfilling prophecies. Furthermore, the possibility that therapists’ early predictions could become self-fulfilling cannot be ruled out. Future studies could address this issue by comparing the predictions of clinicians who are treating patients with those of clinicians who are not. Our study focused on therapist and senior advisor perspectives but did not incorporate patient perceptions of the therapeutic process. Given that therapeutic alliance is a known predictor of treatment success (Steil et al., 2023), future studies should investigate whether alignment between therapist and patient expectations enhances or moderates treatment outcomes. Our reliance on early-session predictions means that we did not examine how clinical intuition evolves over the course of therapy. It is possible that therapist perceptions change as they gain more experience with a patient, which could further refine their predictive accuracy. Longitudinal studies assessing predictions at multiple time points could provide additional insights into how intuitive judgments develop and influence outcomes. Furthermore, it should be acknowledged that our study exclusively included cis-women treated for PTSD related to interpersonal childhood trauma, thus limiting the generalizability of the results to other genders and to other forms of PTSD and to other mental disorders. The focus on two specific therapeutic approaches (CPT and DBT-PTSD) means that the findings might not be directly transferable to other PTSD treatments. Specifically, these therapies are based on operational manuals, and all therapists have been extensively trained to adhere to these manuals. Furthermore, the therapists were not left on their own, but were supervised and participated in consultation teams. All of that might have mitigated the impact of therapists’ levels of liking for the patient. Additionally, the subjective nature of the clinical prediction and liking ratings could introduce personal biases, despite efforts to standardize these assessments. Future studies should aim to replicate these findings in larger, more diverse samples and across different therapeutic modalities to enhance the external validity of the results. Finally, the sample size of 80 participants makes it improbable to detect small effects. While the original study sample was larger, this analysis includes only those cases for which complete prediction data were available.

Conclusions

The findings of our study have significant implications for both clinical practice and future research. The finding that therapists’ liking of their patients was essentially unrelated to outcomes underscores the robust effectiveness of structured, manualized PTSD therapies.

The observed association between therapists’ early predictions and symptom change suggests that clinical intuition at an early stage of treatment may offer relevant information. Further research is needed to elucidate whether these therapist perceptions can be made explicit and used to the patient's benefit for the further course of therapy.
