The effect of cortical and subcortical parcel volumes on postictal generalized EEG suppression
Authors: Vladimir Bascarevic, Zorica Jokovic, Sabrina Hadziosmanovic, Aleksa Pejovic, Matthias Koepp, Fenglai Xiao, Lawrence Binding, Zoran Bukumiric, Nikola Vojvodic, Dragoslav Sokic, Aleksandar J Ristic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlterations to brain structures and networks involved in central autonomic and respiratory control are possible biomarkers of SUDEP, but were mainly demonstrated in people at high risk. Postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES) has been recorded in every case of near or confirmed SUDEP. The structural alterations underpinning PGES are not known.MethodsThis retrospective study included the brain MRIs of 100 patients with focal and generalized epilepsy who had experienced at least one generalized tonic‐clonic seizure (GTCS) documented through long‐term video‐EEG monitoring, with PGES defined as postictal EEG activity below 10 microvolts lasting at least 20 s. Brain MRIs were parcellated into cortical and subcortical volumes, with further parcellation of thalamic subfields, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were applied to identify brain regions associated with PGES.ResultsPGES was observed in 52 of 100 patients. Patients with PGES were older and had a slower heart rate before and after seizures compared to those without PGES. The tonic phase of GTCS was longer in patients with PGES. Multivariate analysis revealed that the right thalamus and both precuneus were independent predictors of PGES. These findings may help doctors understand which patients are more at risk after seizures and how to better protect them.Keywords: cortical/subcortical parcellation, epilepsy, generalized tonic‐clonic seizures (GTCS), postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES), thalamus

INTRODUCTION

Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is the leading cause of lost years of death in epilepsy (1), with a 15‐fold increase in people with frequent (>3 per year) focal‐to‐bilateral (generalized) tonic‐clonic seizures (FBTCS). 1 Postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES), characterized by an immediate postictal generalized absence of EEG activity greater than ten microvolts in amplitude for durations ranging from a few seconds to several minutes, 2 was recorded in every case of near or confirmed SUDEP 3 and has been explored as a potential indicator for identifying patients at higher risk of SUDEP. The physiological mechanisms surrounding seizure termination leading to SUDEP during and after seizures in humans are not yet fully understood.

Objective and relatively subtle structural and functional MRI alterations are present in patients eventually dying from SUDEP, possibly reflecting an underlying intrinsic vulnerability. MRI, routinely acquired in both SUDEP cases and high‐risk epileptic patients, identified bilateral thinning of posterior thalami (pulvinar) and increased gray matter volumes in the right hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and amygdala, 4 , 5 possibly reflecting an underlying chronic autonomic dysregulation with the inability to recover breathing from hypoxic challenge (susceptibility biomarker).

Only a few neuroimaging studies detected microstructural brain abnormalities linked to PGES with reduced cortical thickness in the right paracentral lobule, inferior parietal lobule, supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and middle temporal lobe. 6 Using 18fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) in patients with focal to bilateral tonic‐clonic seizures showed hypometabolism in the right temporal pole in PGES patients compared to those without PGES. 7

Most of the structural MRI findings associated with SUDEP have been observed in patients at high risk, often defined by frequent FBTCS or nocturnal seizures. Consequently, it remains unclear whether these alterations reflect true susceptibility biomarkers of SUDEP, are secondary to seizure burden, or potentially even reflect compensatory mechanisms. In this study, we focus on postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES), a phenomenon observed in all documented SUDEP cases and proposed as a clinical biomarker of elevated SUDEP risk. By examining structural brain alterations in patients with and without PGES, we aim to identify regions potentially involved in the mechanisms leading to PGES. While our study does not establish causality, it offers a complementary approach to existing SUDEP research by investigating how PGES—an intermediate marker—may be structurally mediated, thus informing hypotheses for future longitudinal or mechanistic studies.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

The study was conducted at the Epilepsy Center of the Neurology Clinic, University Clinical Center of Serbia, enrolling epilepsy patients admitted between 2012 and 2022. These patients were diagnosed based on the 2017 International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria after experiencing at least one FBTCS. Confirmation was obtained through long‐term video‐EEG (vEEG) monitoring. Two experienced clinical neurophysiologists, SH and AJR, reviewed the vEEG recordings and classified the patients into two groups: those with (PGES+) and those without (PGES‐) PGES, following established PGES criteria. 2 PGES was defined as postictal EEG activity below ten microvolts lasting 20 s or more. The duration of the PGES was noted, and in the case of multiple FBTCS, the median value was used for further analysis.

The study excluded individuals either older than 70 or younger than 14 years (to minimize confounding effects of structural brain changes related to early neurodevelopment or advanced aging), and those with MRIs of poor quality. The study excluded individuals with large structural lesions visible on conventional MRI, including focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), long‐term epilepsy‐associated tumors (LEAT), extensive gliosis, remote strokes, or other mass lesions. Patients with subtle or equivocal MRI abnormalities, including small or uncertain FCDs, were not included. The only structural pathology allowed was isolated hippocampal sclerosis, without additional temporal or extratemporal abnormalities. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study received institutional approval from the Ethics Committee, adhering to internationally accepted ethical standards including the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, as revised in 1975, 1983, and 1989.

MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING

The T1‐weighted (T1w) MRI sequence is used for anatomical characterization. High‐resolution structural scans were acquired using the sagittal plane at 1.5 T (Philips Achieva, Amsterdam, Netherlands) and 1.5 T (Siemens, Avanto/Aera, Erlangen, Germany). The imaging parameters were for 1.5 T Philips: TR = 25 ms, flip angle = 8°, acquisition matrix of 256 × 232 × 180; for 1.5 T Siemens: TR = 1700 ms, flip angle = 4° and acquisition matrix of 240 × 256 × 180, yielding voxel size for all MRI machines were of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3.

FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) was used for full processing of MR imaging data, which involved skull‐stripping, bias field correction, registration, and anatomical segmentation. We segmented the cortex into 66 regions (33 in each hemisphere) and the thalamus and basal ganglia into 14 regions (7 on each side). For cortical regions, this method uses intensity and continuity information from the entire 3D MR volume in segmentation and deformation procedures to produce cortical thickness. Cortical thickness is calculated as the closest distance from the gray/white boundary to the gray/CSF boundary at each vertex on the surface. 8

Based on initial assessment, further detailed analysis focused on the thalamic subfields. We used THOMAS (Thalamus‐Optimized Multi‐Atlas Segmentation) v 2.1, which represents a code for segmentation of the thalamus into 11 nuclei and MTT (mammillothalamic tract). It used multi‐atlas segmentation and PICSL's joint label fusion (Joint label fusion is for combining candidate segmentations produced by registering and warping multiple atlases for a target image, and it was applied in MICCAI 2012 Grand Challenge on Multi‐Atlas Labeling). For segmentation, we used 3D T1‐weighted images acquired with Siemens 1.5 T and 3 T scanners (MPRAGE sequences) and Philips 1.5 T scanners (TFE sequences), as commonly used and validated in studies employing the THOMAS segmentation pipeline. This segmentation program creates left and right directories for individual labels, and output that is full‐sized fused labels. All output files are in NII format.

All clinical and demographic data were statistically compared using either Student's t‐test, Mann–Whitney U‐test, or chi‐squared test according to different variable types using the SPSS software package (Version 26.0 for Mac, http://www.spss.com/). Statistical significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05. Initial analyses on 80 brain parcels were performed using univariate logistic regression models to identify which specific brain parcellation volumes were associated with PGES. Each model included age, gender, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV), and presence of hippocampal sclerosis as covariates to control for potential confounding effects. Following univariate logistic regression, regions identified as having significant associations with PGES presence underwent further evaluation using multivariate logistic regression to assess their independent contributions while adjusting for the same covariates. The Omnibus Tests of Model Coefficients were used to evaluate the model and assess whether the inclusion of predictors significantly improved the prediction of the outcome. The goodness of fit for the final model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test. The test yielded a p‐value of 0.263, which is greater than the conventional significance level of 0.05, indicating that the model provides an adequate fit to the data. This suggests that the predicted probabilities do not significantly differ from the observed outcomes, supporting the appropriateness of the binary logistic regression model. Based on initial assessment, further detailed analysis focused on the thalamic subfields identified as significant predictors in the multivariate logistic regression were performed again while adjusting for the same covariates.

RESULTS

Out of 224 patients with focal and generalized epilepsy who were hospitalized over a 10‐year period (2012–2022) and who had at least one generalized convulsive seizure documented via long‐term video‐EEG, we included 100 patients (mean age 32.9 ± 11.6 years, range 14–70) with non‐lesional epilepsy, or with pure hippocampal sclerosis (n = 17). Mean age of epilepsy onset was 15.1 ± 11.6 years, and average duration of epilepsy was 17.6 ± 13.3 years. The presence of PGES was noted in 52 patients, with a median duration of 60 s (range 26–240 s). Patients with PGES were older than those without PGES (35.4 ± 12.4 vs. 30.1 ± 10.2 years, p = 0.021). Additionally, patients with PGES had a slower heart rate both before and after the seizure compared to those without PGES (before seizure: 70.2 ± 11.5 bpm vs. 75.9 ± 12.1 bpm, p = 0.018; after seizure: 120.0 ± 26.6 bpm vs. 131.6 ± 26.2 bpm, p = 0.031). There was no difference between frequency or duration of PGES in relation to seizure lateralization among those 68 (33 left) patients with clearly identified unilateral focus on video‐EEG monitoring (left seizure lateralization: PGES+ n = 18/33; right seizure lateralization PGES duration 45.8 ± 45.6 s n = 35/69).

Out of 80 potential brain regions analyzed individually, six were identified in the univariate models as having significant associations with the presence of PGES (p < 0.05): left and right lateral occipital, left and right precuneus, and left and right thalamus. In the multivariate logistic model, variables that were statistically significant at the 0.05 level in the univariate models were included. Due to strong correlations and multicollinearity among the predictors, left and right precuneus, as well as left and right lateral occipital regions, were included in the multivariate model through interaction terms.

The Omnibus Tests of Model Coefficients indicated that the logistic regression model, with the included predictors, significantly improved the prediction of PGES compared to a model without predictors (chi‐square = 37.303, df = 8, p < .001). The model explained 42% of the variance in the dependent variable. The multivariable logistic regression model identified the right thalamus and precuneus as significant predictors for the presence of PGES (Figure 1 and S1).

DISCUSSION

Searching for an imaging biomarker of SUDEP, we observed alterations in the right thalamus and precuneus in patients who showed PGES, a phenomenon that has been linked to SUDEP.

The generalization of seizures relies significantly on the role of thalamic structures. Among all thalamic regions, the ipsilateral medial‐dorsal thalamic nuclei exhibit the strongest thalamocortical connectivity in individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) and FBTCS. 9 Furthermore, thalamic epileptogenicity has been associated with the extent of epileptogenic networks, emphasizing the thalamus's central involvement in seizure propagation and dynamics. 10 Structural imaging studies have consistently reported cumulative thalamic damage, 11 and recent ultra‐high‐field (7 T) MRI research has identified widespread hippocampal and thalamic atrophy in epilepsy. 12 These findings support the growing evidence that thalamotemporal connections play a fundamental role in the pathological network underlying TLE. 13

We observed a significant involvement of the right thalamus, which was greater in the PGES+ group. Among the thalamic nuclei, we identified the ventral anterior nucleus of the thalamus as the only thalamic subregion associated with PGES. Again, its volume was increased in the PGES+ group. A reduction in gray matter volume within the left posterior thalamus has been identified in SUDEP cases and individuals at high risk, compared to both healthy controls and those at lower risk. 4 However, thalamic volume loss is a well‐documented finding in patients with FBTCS, who are at the highest risk for SUDEP. 14 This raises the possibility that structural alterations in SUDEP may primarily affect the larger right thalamus. Additionally, the first MRI study focusing specifically on patients who later succumbed to SUDEP reported volume increases, particularly in the right amygdala. 5

Recent imaging studies have identified enlargements in additional anterior limbic structures, including the bilateral amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, and entorhinal cortex, in SUDEP cases and high‐risk individuals. 4 Notably, the subcallosal cortex, a region associated with blood pressure regulation, 15 exhibited enlargement exclusively in those who later succumbed to SUDEP. 4 Increased bilateral mesial temporal structure volumes, particularly in the amygdala, have also been observed in a subset of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy patients who experienced poor post‐surgical outcomes. 16 These volume increases may indicate gliosis or inflammation, potentially resulting from recurrent hypoxic damage following seizures. 17 , 18

The right thalamus plays a more critical role in networks related to consciousness and awareness. Furthermore, differences in thalamic connectivity with cortical regions involved in awareness processing and sensory integration may contribute to this discrepancy, 19 linking this asymmetry more closely to the impact of GTCS. However, we did not observe an effect of focus lateralization on either the presence or duration of PGES, or on the size of the right thalamus, which was increased both ipsi‐ and contra‐laterally, suggesting that our findings are specific to the right thalamus and independent of seizure localization.

The involvement of the right ventral anterior nucleus provides further insight into the specific neural pathways involved in postictal suppression considering the known outputs of the direct and indirect pathways, which include projections from the globus pallidus interna (GPi) that exert a tonic inhibitory influence on the ventral anterior and ventral lateral thalamus. These pathways function as regulatory mechanisms, suppressing excessive or involuntary motor activity by modulating cortical input. 20 Our results suggest that this inhibitory control might be altered in patients with PGES, potentially leading to increased thalamocortical synchronization that could contribute to the suppression of cortical activity following a seizure. While traditionally recognized for its role in motor regulation, the involvement of the ventral anterior nucleus in PGES may hint at a broader function in seizure dynamics and recovery. Although speculative, it is possible that dysfunction in these circuits may play a part both in the termination of seizures as well as the occurrence of PGES.

This idea is further supported by a case report of global EEG voltage suppression associated with a unilateral thalamic infarction in a neonate. The clinical presentation suggested a unilateral neonatal stroke involving the middle cerebral artery, and what was particularly striking was the length of time that the EEG remained generally suppressed—lasting 4–5 days. 21 The extended EEG suppression following a thalamic infarction highlights how crucial the thalamus is for maintaining regular brain activity. It suggests that significant disruptions in post‐seizure brain function can occur when thalamic pathways are damaged or not functioning correctly. This further reinforces the link between thalamic activity and prolonged EEG suppression in certain neurological conditions.

Reduced responsiveness to oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, particularly during sleep, results from impaired automatic control of breathing in a condition known as congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS). This disorder provides valuable insight into how the brain regulates breathing and cardiovascular function. The precuneus, a key part of the default mode network (DMN), is deeply involved in consciousness, reinforcing that disruptions in these brain networks can affect awareness. Studies have shown strong structural and functional links between the precuneus and the thalamus, playing a central role in the DMN's activity. 22 This is consistent with findings that loss of consciousness during temporal lobe seizures is associated with increased synchronization between brain regions crucial for awareness, particularly the thalamus and parietal cortices. 23 They may still communicate through broader brain networks even though a clearly defined direct connection between the ventral anterior nucleus of the thalamus and the precuneus has not been established. This connection could be especially relevant in tasks requiring movement coordination and higher‐level cognitive processes like self‐awareness and spatial reasoning.

Seizure dynamics also appeared to play a role in the occurrence of PGES. Although the overall frequency of FBTCS did not differ substantially between groups, patients with PGES experienced longer tonic phases during seizures, which is in line with previous findings. 24 This may suggest that seizures with more prolonged tonic activity involve greater cortical and subcortical engagement, potentially disrupting the thalamocortical circuits responsible for maintaining normal postictal brain function. Furthermore, the clinical features of patients with PGES, including hippocampal sclerosis and prolonged tonic phase during seizures, suggest that specific structural vulnerabilities may contribute to this postictal phenomenon. These characteristics may indicate an underlying mechanism that heightens the risk of dysfunction, especially in situations where autonomic and respiratory systems are under stress, such as during and following GTCS. 3 Understanding these characteristics could help identify patients at greater risk for SUDEP.

While the exclusion of patients with poor‐quality MRIs or significant structural lesions strengthened the internal validity of our analysis, it may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader epilepsy population, particularly those with lesional or more complex forms of epilepsy. Additionally, the cross‐sectional nature of this study precludes the ability to determine causality, necessitating further longitudinal research to explore whether these structural abnormalities directly contribute to the development of PGES or are a consequence of repeated seizure activity.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that structural differences in the right thalamus and precuneus are associated with PGES, potentially reflecting network vulnerabilities involved in PGES.
