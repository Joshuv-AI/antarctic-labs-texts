Outcomes of Synthetic Corneal Endothelial Implant in Patients with Corneal Oedema with or without Prior Endothelial Keratoplasty: A Case Series
Authors: Issac Levy, Ritika Mukhija, Mayank A Nanavaty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the safety and efficacy of artificial endothelial layer (EndoArt®) implantation in patients with chronic corneal edema with or without prior endothelial keratoplasty at a tertiary referral center.MethodsThis retrospective, non-comparative study reviewed medical records of consecutive patients who underwent EndoArt® (EyeYon Medical, Israel) implantation between December 2024 and November 2025. Patients with chronic corneal oedema and a minimum 12-week follow-up were included; eyes with prior penetrating keratoplasty were excluded. Primary outcome was central corneal thickness (CCT) measured by anterior segment optical coherence tomography. Secondary outcomes included best-corrected distance visual acuity (BCDVA), intraocular pressure, resuturing/rebubbling rates, and complications.ResultsNine eyes of nine patients were included (mean age: 72±17 years; 55% female). Four patients (44%) had previous failed endothelial keratoplasty. Mean follow-up was 27±14 weeks. Mean baseline CCT decreased significantly from 780±106μm [95% CI: 710.0, 861.3μm] to 574±107μm [95% CI 502.8, 643.1μm], postoperatively (adjusted for implant thickness), representing a 35% reduction (265±89 μm; 95% CI: −338.8 to −190.8μm; p<0.01). Mean BCDVA improved from 2.28±0.54 LogMAR [95% CI 1.87, 2.71 LogMAR] to 1.16±0.79 LogMAR [95% CI 0.55, 1.78 LogMAR]. Three patients (33%) achieved postoperative BCDVA of ≤0.5 LogMAR. Two patients (22%) required re-suturing; no rebubbling was performed. No endophthalmitis, inflammatory reactions, or chronic intraocular pressure elevation occurred.ConclusionArtificial corneal endothelial implant was associated with significant reduction in corneal thickness (35%) and improvements in visual acuity in eyes with chronic endothelial dysfunction, with or without prior endothelial keratoplasty. The procedure was feasible in complex eyes with prior failed endothelial keratoplasty and glaucoma drainage devices. EndoArt® represents a viable donor-tissue-independent alternative for high-risk patients in whom traditional keratoplasty carries a guarded prognosis.Keywords: artificial endothelial layer, EndoArt®, corneal oedema, endothelial keratoplasty, synthetic corneal implant, DMEK/DSAEK failure, bullous keratopathy

Introduction

Corneal transplantation remains the most commonly performed and clinically successful human tissue transplant, restoring vision in a wide range of corneal pathologies, including endothelial failure, keratoconus, trauma, and infection.1 Despite this success, a significant global shortage of donor corneal tissue persists, with only one cornea available for every seventy individuals requiring transplantation.2 A comprehensive global survey estimated that approximately 12.7 million people worldwide are awaiting corneal grafts, underscoring the substantial imbalance between growing demand and limited donor availability.2 This shortage is particularly pronounced in developing regions, where access to corneal transplantation remains severely restricted.3

Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is the leading indication for endothelial keratoplasty worldwide, affecting approximately 7.33% of adults aged over 30 years, according to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis.4 The disease accounts for approximately 39% of all corneal transplantations performed globally.2 Pseudophakic Bullous keratopathy (PBK), characterized by endothelial cell loss leading to stromal edema and epithelial blistering after cataract surgery, is another major reason for endothelial keratoplasty. This unmet need has accelerated interest in donor-independent solutions, including bioengineered corneas and synthetic endothelial substitutes. Endothelial keratoplasty techniques have evolved significantly over the past two decades, progressing from Descemet’s stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK) to Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK), with the latter providing superior visual outcomes and lower rejection rates.5,6 However, these procedures remain contingent on donor tissue availability and entail inherent risks of immunological rejection, particularly in repeat transplantation, where rejection rates increase substantially with each subsequent graft.7,8 The five-year graft failure rate for repeat penetrating keratoplasty ranges from 34% to 70%, underscoring the clinical need for alternative therapeutic strategies.9

EndoArt® (EyeYon Medical, Ness Ziona, Israel) represents a novel synthetic “endothelial implant” designed as a 6.0–6.5 mm dome-shaped, 50μm-thick hydrophilic acrylic posterior corneal layer that replaces the barrier function of the native endothelium without requiring donor tissue.10,11 The device functions via a passive barrier mechanism rather than replacing active endothelial cell function. By blocking the central posterior cornea, Artificial corneal endothelial implant reduces aqueous humor influx into the corneal stroma, thereby establishing a new fluid equilibrium that facilitates corneal deturgescence.12 The exposed peripheral cornea maintains sufficient nutrient transport and fluid exchange to preserve corneal health.12 The device offers several theoretical benefits, including extended shelf life without refrigeration requirements, a simpler surgical learning curve compared to traditional keratoplasty, elimination of immunological rejection risk, and the ability to reposition or remove the implant without endothelial cell loss or graft damage.13 Previous studies have reported significant central corneal thickness (CCT) reduction with restoration of central transparency and visual acuity improvement following Artificial corneal endothelial implant procedure.11,14,15 The cumulative worldwide implantation experience now exceeds ~800 cases with follow-up extending to 6.5 years, demonstrating an approximately 68% rate of visual acuity gain at one year and mean CCT reductions exceeding 200 μm.16

However, the majority of published EndoArt® studies to date have been conducted in highly selected, high-risk cohorts — predominantly eyes with multiple prior graft failures or complex anterior segment pathology — and do not reflect the broader spectrum of patients encountered in real-world tertiary practice.10,15,17 Critically, there is a paucity of prospective or retrospective real-world data on EndoArt® outcomes in mixed patient populations that include both eyes with and without prior endothelial keratoplasty (EK). This distinction is clinically important: eyes naïve to prior EK retain native Descemet membrane anatomy and potentially different adhesion dynamics compared to eyes with prior failed DSAEK or DMEK, yet no single real-world series has directly compared or jointly reported outcomes across both subgroups. We therefore conducted this retrospective case series with the hypothesis that EndoArt® implantation is safe and effective across a clinically mixed population of patients with chronic corneal oedema, regardless of prior endothelial keratoplasty status. The purpose of this study was to report real-world outcomes from a UK tertiary referral centre, evaluating central corneal thickness reduction, visual acuity, and safety profile in patients both with and without prior failed endothelial keratoplasty — a population not adequately represented in the existing literature.

Methods

This retrospective, non-comparative case series was conducted at Sussex Eye Hospital in Brighton, United Kingdom, a tertiary-care ophthalmology center. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital’s audit department (University Hospitals Sussex NHS Foundation Trust audit number 2854). Patient consent to review medical records was waived by the University Hospitals Sussex NHS Foundation Trust as this was a retrospective observational study using anonymised data. All data were handled in accordance with applicable data protection regulations and patient confidentiality was maintained at all times. Medical records of all consecutive patients who underwent artificial corneal endothelial implant (EyeYon Medical, Ness Ziona, Israel) procedure between December 2024 and November 2025 were systematically reviewed.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients with chronic corneal edema secondary to endothelial dysfunction who underwent Artificial corneal endothelial implant with a minimum follow-up period of 12 weeks. Exclusion criteria included eyes with prior penetrating keratoplasty, as the irregular posterior corneal surface in these cases may affect implant adhesion and outcomes.18

The surgical technique was analogous to that described for DSAEK.19 In brief, a superior main incision was created using a 2.8 mm keratome (MSL28, Mani, Japan), along with two side paracenteses of approximately 1.2–1.5 mm using a corneal knife (MST15, Mani, Japan). The anterior chamber was filled with ophthalmic viscoelastic device (OVD) (Healon®, Johnson & Johnson, USA). In cases with prior failed DMEK or DSAEK grafts, the failed graft tissue was removed before implantation. For patients without prior endothelial keratoplasty, a 7 mm Descemetorhexis was performed with a reverse Sinskey hook, followed by removal of the Descemet membrane with a Melles scraper. The OVD was thoroughly cleared from the anterior chamber to facilitate implant adhesion. The Artificial corneal endothelial implant was inserted into the anterior chamber using an iris repositor, centered on the posterior corneal surface, and secured using 50% sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) gas mixture (GOT Multi SF6, Alchimia, Italy) to maintain tamponade. Between one and four transfixing sutures of 10–0 Ethilon (Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, USA) were placed, entering the anterior chamber from the limbus and exiting through the Artificial corneal endothelial implant at approximately 50% towards the center of the implant.20 All corneal incisions were sutured with 10–0 Ethilon, and the anterior chamber was fully inflated with SF6 50% mixture. Dilation drops (tropicamide 1%, phenylephrine 10%, cyclopentolate 1%; Bausch and Lomb, USA) were instilled to prevent pupillary block. A 5’o clock paracentesis was created for use at slit lamp postoperatively.21 One hour postoperatively, the gas bubble was reduced to 90% fill. The following morning, patients were reviewed, and the gas bubble was decreased to 75–80% if necessary to prevent pupillary block.22 In cases where implant positioning was suboptimal or partial detachment occurred postoperatively, the implant was either re-sutured or sutures were adjusted based on anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) findings. Postoperative management included topical tobramycin 0.3%/dexamethasone 0.1% (Tobradex; Novartis, Switzerland) four times daily, tapered over one month. Subsequently, loteprednol etabonate 0.5% (Lotemax; Bausch and Lomb, USA) was initiated and tapered over three months.22 Unlike traditional endothelial keratoplasty, long-term immunosuppressive therapy is not required following Artificial corneal endothelial implant procedure, as the synthetic material eliminates immunological rejection risk.10

The primary outcome measure was CCT, measured using AS-OCT (Solix; Haag-Streit, Switzerland) at baseline and at each postoperative visit. For analysis, 50 μm (the thickness of the Artificial corneal endothelial implant) was subtracted from postoperative measurements to enable direct comparison with preoperative values and accurately assess stromal thickness reduction. Secondary outcome measures included best-corrected distance visual acuity (BCDVA) using Snellen charts converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) notation, intraocular pressure (IOP) measured by iCare tonometry (Mainline, UK) or Goldmann applanation tonometry, resuturing or rebubbling rate, and postoperative complications.

Visual acuity was recorded in logMAR notation. For eyes with qualitative acuity categorized as “counting fingers” or “hand motion”, logMAR values of 2.0 and 2.6 were assigned, respectively, in accordance with previously established standards for statistical analysis.23–25 Data were collected using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365, Redmond, WA, USA) and statistical analysis was done using StatPlus:mac Pro (AnalystSoft Inc). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed to compare preoperative and postoperative CCT and visual acuity measurements. A p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Nine eyes of nine patients underwent procedure with artificial corneal endothelial implant during the study period. The cohort comprised five female patients (55%) and four male patients (45%), with a mean age of 72±17 years (range: 33–91 years). Eight eyes (89%) were right eyes. The mean follow-up period was 27±14 weeks (range: 12–48 weeks). Four patients (44%) had undergone at least one previous endothelial keratoplasty that had failed, including three with prior DMEK and one with prior DSAEK. Three patients (33%) had previous glaucoma drainage device surgery, and two patients (22%) had undergone prior pars plana vitrectomy. Discussion

This study contributes to the growing body of literature on Artificial corneal endothelial implant procedure in patients with decompensated corneal endothelium, chronic oedema, and limited options for repeat donor keratoplasty. Our results demonstrate that Artificial corneal endothelial implant can be successfully implanted with or without prior endothelial keratoplasty, achieving significant reductions in corneal thickness and visual improvement, comparable to previous reports in high-risk populations.

We found a substantial mean CCT reduction of 265 μm (35%) following Artificial corneal endothelial implant procedure, consistent with previous studies reporting mean CCT reductions of 28–37% across various patient populations.11,15,17 A recent systematic review of Artificial corneal endothelial implant outcomes reported an average CCT reduction of 29% and a rebubbling rate of 47.8%.17 Our series demonstrated a lower reintervention rate, with only 22% of patients requiring re-suturing and none requiring rebubbling. This may be attributable to our surgical technique of placing multiple transfixing sutures in high-risk cases, particularly those with compromised gas tamponade capacity. The significant reduction in corneal thickness observed in our cohort suggests that the Artificial corneal endothelial implant effectively replaces the barrier function of the dysfunctional corneal endothelium.12 Unlike conventional endothelial keratoplasty, which relies on active endothelial cell pump function, Artificial corneal endothelial implant functions through a passive barrier mechanism that reduces aqueous influx into the corneal stroma.10,12

Visual acuity significantly improved, with mean postoperative BCDVA improving to 1.16 LogMAR from a preoperative acuity of 2.28 LogMAR. However, these results are modest compared with conventional endothelial keratoplasty procedures, in which DMEK typically achieves a mean BCDVA of approximately 0.08 LogMAR at long-term follow-up.27,28 Several factors may explain this difference. First, most patients offered Artificial corneal endothelial implant in our series had limited visual potential due to coexisting ocular comorbidities, including advanced glaucoma (33%, patients 2,5,6), previous vitrectomy (33%, patients 1,2,7), and chronic macular pathology (22%, patients 6,7) (Table 1). These conditions independently limit achievable visual outcomes regardless of corneal clarity.29 Second, the optical properties of synthetic implant materials may differ from native endothelium or donor tissue, potentially affecting final visual outcomes. Corneal higher-order aberrations and backscattered light are elevated in eyes with endothelial dysfunction and remain higher even after endothelial keratoplasty.30 Third, the interface between synthetic materials and host tissue may introduce additional optical irregularities contributing to visual limitations. Nevertheless, one-third of patients without other significant causes of visual impairment achieved functional vision of 0.3–0.5 LogMAR, suggesting that Artificial corneal endothelial implant may provide acceptable visual rehabilitation in appropriately selected patients. The primary purpose of this synthetic implant is not to replace human tissue entirely but to serve as an alternative therapeutic option in refractory cases where human tissue is unavailable, unsuitable, or associated with a high failure or rejection risk.11,12,17

Four patients (44%) in our series had undergone at least one previous corneal graft that had failed (Table 1). Repeat corneal transplantation is associated with significantly higher rates of complications and graft failure compared to primary procedures.7,8 The five-year graft failure rate for repeat penetrating keratoplasty ranges from 34% to 70%, with increased risk of immunological rejection following each successive graft.9 DMEK after failed endothelial keratoplasty also carries an elevated risk, with studies reporting secondary graft failure rates of 35–56% in eyes with previous glaucoma drainage devices.31 The Artificial corneal endothelial implant offers theoretical advantages in such high-risk eyes, as it does not depend on viable endothelial cells and cannot undergo immunological rejection. This eliminates concerns about allograft rejection that complicate repeat allogenic transplantation.10 Unlike prior studies focusing on multiple failed grafts, we included two DMEK-eligible patients who declined surgery due to prolonged waiting times for donor tissue. Both patients experienced significant improvements in corneal clarity and visual acuity, suggesting Artificial corneal endothelial implant may be considered as a bridging therapy or alternative in urgent cases where access to human tissue is limited.

We observed that patients with a previous DMEK graft and an original descemetorhexis of 8 mm or less, with visible Descemet membrane at the corneal periphery, recovered faster than those with larger descemetorhexis or absent peripheral endothelium. We propose that the combination of the peripheral endothelial cell rim and the Artificial corneal endothelial implant passive barrier mechanism establishes a dual equilibrium that facilitates central corneal clearance while maintaining peripheral corneal health without edema. This observation warrants further investigation in larger prospective studies. In eyes with regular corneas and adequate gas tamponade, implant attachment was achieved with gradual improvement. However, attachment is more challenging in vitrectomized eyes without an intact lens capsule, eyes with glaucoma drainage devices, and those with irregular posterior corneal surfaces after penetrating keratoplasty.18,32 Based on our experience, when gas tamponade is limited, approximately four transfixing sutures are generally required to secure the implant effectively. These sutures may cause some graft distortion, potentially delaying complete adhesion and necessitating further adjustment based on AS-OCT findings. In two patients, the Artificial corneal endothelial implant remained only partially attached, limiting the reduction in corneal thickness and clarity. Conservative management has been adopted, as spontaneous reattachment of partially detached Artificial corneal endothelial implants has been reported.26 The ability to reposition the Artificial corneal endothelial implant without risking endothelial cell loss represents a distinct advantage over biological grafts, enabling surgeons to optimize outcomes through secondary interventions without the constraints of donor tissue preservation.10

The stable IOP measurements observed postoperatively suggest that the implant does not significantly interfere with aqueous humor dynamics, an important consideration given that one-third of our patients had pre-existing glaucoma. Glaucoma represents the most common late complication after full-thickness artificial corneal implantation procedures.33 The presence of glaucoma drainage devices in the anterior chamber constitutes a known risk factor for endothelial decompensation and subsequent graft failure after human keratoplasty.31,34,35 For these patients, Artificial corneal endothelial implant may be a viable option, offering improved visual function with a reduced risk of requiring repeated corneal transplants. We did not encounter endophthalmitis, inflammatory reactions, or chronic macular edema within our cohort, consistent with the biocompatible properties of the hydrophilic acrylic material, which is the same material commonly used in intraocular lens manufacturing.12

Our study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including a small sample size (n=9) and a relatively short follow-up duration. Postoperative outcome measures were analysed using the most recent available follow-up visit for each patient, resulting in variable follow-up durations across the cohort. In addition, the retrospective tertiary-centre design may introduce selection and referral bias, as patients included were often complex cases with limited alternatives to conventional keratoplasty, and with varying degrees of previous ocular surgery and comorbidities, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The absence of a control group precludes direct comparison with conventional endothelial keratoplasty outcomes in similar patient populations. Long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated sustained corneal thickness reduction in Artificial corneal endothelial implant patients up to 72 months postoperatively, with the formation of fibrotic tissue between the device periphery and the host cornea, potentially explaining the strong adhesion over time.14,15 Continued follow-up of our cohort will be necessary to assess long-term durability and visual outcomes. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating its efficacy compared to standard endothelial keratoplasty, and larger multicenter studies will be essential to establish definitive guidelines for patient selection and optimal surgical technique. The development of pharmacological adjuncts, such as Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitors, may further enhance outcomes by promoting peripheral endothelial cell migration and function.36,37 Additionally, optimizing surgical techniques for challenging anatomical configurations, including vitrectomized eyes and those with glaucoma drainage devices, remains an active area of investigation.

In clinical practice, the decision to proceed with an artificial corneal endothelial implant should be individualised and guided by both anatomical factors and visual potential. Ideal candidates are patients with symptomatic corneal oedema and limited suitability for conventional endothelial keratoplasty, particularly those with a high risk of graft failure, such as eyes with prior failed grafts, glaucoma drainage devices, or altered anterior segment anatomy. Adequate posterior corneal regularity and the ability to achieve sufficient postoperative tamponade are important considerations for successful implant attachment. Conversely, patients with good visual potential and favourable ocular anatomy remain better candidates for DMEK or DSAEK, given their superior optical outcomes. In addition, patient preference and access to donor tissue may influence decision-making.

Artificial corneal endothelial implantation resulted in significant corneal thickness reduction and visual acuity improvement in eyes with chronic endothelial dysfunction and regular corneal anatomy. The procedure was feasible in complex eyes with previous failed endothelial keratoplasty and glaucoma drainage devices, with an acceptable safety profile. The 35% mean reduction in central corneal thickness and improvement in visual acuity from hand motion/counting fingers to ambulatory vision in most patients support the clinical utility of this synthetic implant in appropriately selected cases.
