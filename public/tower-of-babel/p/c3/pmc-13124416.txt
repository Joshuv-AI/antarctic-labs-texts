Incidence of anxiety and depression in epilepsy patients and its correlation with disease control: A retrospective study
Authors: Ling Tong, Jie Fan, Xiaoqi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This single-center retrospective study explores the incidence of anxiety and depression in epilepsy patients and analyze their correlation with seizure control. A total of 201 epilepsy patients treated at our hospital between February 1, 2022, and February 1, 2024 were included. Based on the Hamilton anxiety rating scale (HAM-A) and self-rating depression scale (SDS) results, patients were divided into an AD group (73 cases) and a NAD group (128 cases). Among the AD group, 45 patients (61.6%) underwent anxiety/depression screening for the first time, highlighting the lack of routine psychological assessment in epilepsy management. Demographic characteristics, epilepsy history, seizure characteristics, medication usage, adherence, seizure control, auxiliary examinations, and psychological interventions were collected. Univariate analysis was performed to screen potential factors, and multivariate logistic regression was subsequently used to identify independent risk factors for anxiety/depression. Patients in the AD group had a younger average age (34.5 ± 12.6 years vs 39.1 ± 11.7 years, P = .047), shorter disease duration (P = .043), higher seizure frequency (P = .034), and poorer seizure control (seizure-free rate 24.7% vs 43.8%, P = .007). Additionally, a higher proportion of patients in this group used 3 or more antiepileptic drugs (17.8% vs 11.7%), and had lower medication adherence (19.2% vs 8.6%, P = .052). Anxiety/depression patients were more likely to receive cognitive behavioral therapy (47.9% vs 32.8%, P = .027), individual psychological counseling (39.7% vs 19.5%, P = .011), and emotional support (43.8% vs 36.7%, P = .039). Multivariate logistic regression analysis suggested that shorter disease duration (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.07–3.11, P = .029), higher seizure frequency (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.12–4.23, P = .021), and low adherence (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.01–6.30, P = .048) were independent risk factors for anxiety/depression. Anxiety and depression are relatively common in epilepsy patients, affecting about 36.3% of them. Emotional disorders are closely related to poor seizure control, lower medication adherence, and complex medication regimens. There is a need to strengthen the assessment and intervention of emotional states in epilepsy patients, especially in those with shorter disease duration and frequent seizures, to promote early identification and management of mental health issues. This can help improve comprehensive treatment outcomes and quality of life.

1. Introduction

Epilepsy is a common chronic neurological disorder that affects approximately 50 million people worldwide. The incidence rate in high-income countries is about 49 cases per 100,000 people annually, whereas in low- and middle-income countries, it is even higher, reaching up to 139 cases per 100,000 people per year.[1–3] The unpredictability, chronic progression, and social stigma associated with seizures place a significant burden on patients both physically and psychologically.[4,5]

Beyond the physical symptoms, accumulating evidence indicates that the prevalence of emotional disorders such as anxiety and depression is markedly higher in epilepsy patients compared with the general population.[6] These psychiatric comorbidities not only impair quality of life but also interfere with seizure control and treatment adherence.[7] Previous studies report that 30% to 50% of epilepsy patients exhibit depressive symptoms,[8] while 20% to 40% experience anxiety.[9] The development of such disorders is multifactorial, involving seizure frequency, disease duration, social support, adverse effects of antiepileptic drugs, and illness perception.[10] Furthermore, emotional dysregulation can reduce adherence to treatment and aggravate seizure frequency, creating a vicious cycle that worsens overall prognosis.[11,12]

Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain the close association between epilepsy and psychiatric comorbidities. Neurobiological factors include dysfunction of limbic circuits, neurotransmitter imbalance (e.g., serotonergic and GABAergic systems), and neuroinflammatory responses such as microglial activation, which may contribute to comorbid depression and anxiety.[5] In addition, psychosocial stressors such as stigma, social isolation, and maladaptive coping strategies further exacerbate the psychological burden.[6,10] Certain antiepileptic drugs may also have adverse psychiatric effects, thereby aggravating mood disorders in susceptible patients.[3,12] These findings support the concept of a bidirectional relationship between epilepsy and emotional disorders, where each condition can worsen the other.[12]

Despite these insights, systematic screening and intervention for epilepsy-related anxiety and depression remain insufficient in clinical practice, especially in primary healthcare settings, where standardized assessment tools and psychological support resources are limited. Most existing studies have focused on either depression or anxiety alone, rather than their comorbidity, and many are cross-sectional in design, lacking longitudinal clinical evidence. Consequently, the prevalence characteristics, influencing factors, and their relationship with seizure control and adherence are not yet fully understood.

Therefore, this retrospective study based on nearly 2 years of clinical data from a single-center hospital systematically evaluated the emotional states of 201 epilepsy patients. By integrating disease characteristics, seizure frequency, medication use, treatment adherence, and psychological interventions, the study aimed to clarify the occurrence and influencing factors of anxiety and depression and to analyze their correlation with seizure control. These findings are expected to provide evidence-based insights for comprehensive epilepsy management and support the integration of routine psychological evaluation into standard clinical care.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan First Hospital. This study is a retrospective analysis of patients treated for epilepsy between February 1, 2022, and February 1, 2024, at the hospital, with complete medical records. A total of 201 eligible patients were selected for the study. Based on the existing medical records and emotional assessment results, patients were divided into 2 groups: Anxiety or depression group (AD group): Patients who were diagnosed with anxiety and/or depression during the study period based on emotional assessment scales (e.g., Hamilton anxiety rating scale (HAM-A), self-rating depression scale (SDS)). The criteria for inclusion in this group were an anxiety score ≥ 14 or a depression score ≥ 50, as assessed by the scales. No anxiety or depression group (NAD group): Patients who were not diagnosed with anxiety or depression during the study period. The criteria for inclusion in this group were an anxiety score < 14 or a depression score < 50. The AD Group included 73 patients, and the NAD group included 128 patients. Among the AD group, 45 patients (61.6%) underwent anxiety/depression screening for the first time, emphasizing the need for routine psychological assessment in epilepsy management.

Inclusion criteria: Patients who had been diagnosed with epilepsy prior to enrollment and met the criteria of the International League Against Epilepsy or the ICD-10 standards with a clear history of seizures. The patient’s medical record must be complete, including detailed medical history, treatment regimen (such as medication therapy, seizure control status), emotional assessment records (e.g., results from HAM-A and SDS for assessing anxiety and depression), and follow-up data. Only adult patients aged ≥ 18 years were included, and they must have undergone at least one standardized emotional assessment during the study period, with the assessment results available in the medical records.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with severe systemic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, etc., that could potentially affect epilepsy treatment or emotional management. Patients with severe mental disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or severe cognitive impairment, that require special treatment and could not follow the study’s intervention design. Patients with incomplete medical records, missing treatment records, emotional assessment data, or follow-up records. Patients currently taking antidepressants, anxiolytics, or other medications that might affect emotional assessments, and who had unstable medication usage or failed to report their medication usage. Patients who were unable to maintain continuous follow-up during the study period or who discontinued treatment or follow-up prematurely.

This study retrospectively collected clinical data from epilepsy patients between February 2022 and February 2024. Data collection was carried out using standardized electronic medical record extraction forms, independently completed by 2 trained researchers and cross-checked for accuracy. The collected data included:

Demographic characteristics: Basic information such as the patient’s age, sex, marital status, education level, and occupation.

3.2. Completion criteria for interventions

3.2.1. Psychological support

3.2.1.1. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)[13]

The patient must have participated in the prescribed number of CBT sessions (e.g., once a week for 8–12 weeks) and completed the major activities during the therapy (e.g., mood tracking, assignments).

If the patient completes at least 80% of the scheduled CBT sessions or participates and continuously engages in self-monitoring activities, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.1.2. Support groups[14]

The patient must have attended a prescribed number of group sessions (e.g., once a month for more than 6 months).

If the patient attends 50% or more of the group sessions, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.1.3. Relaxation techniques (yoga, meditation)[15]

The patient participated in relaxation technique training during the designated treatment period (e.g., once a week for 4–6 weeks) and practiced regularly.

If the patient’s participation rate exceeds 60% and they complete at least half of the exercises (including practicing meditation or yoga at home), they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.2. Nursing interventions

3.2.2.1. Individual counseling[16]

The patient completed the prescribed number of individual sessions with a counselor (e.g., once a week for 6–12 weeks).

If the patient attends at least 80% of the individual counseling sessions and achieves the treatment goals or shows improvement in key symptoms (such as anxiety or depression), they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.2.2. Group counseling[17]

The patient participated in more than 50% of the group counseling sessions, typically one session per month for 3 to 6 months.

If the patient attends at least 4 group sessions and actively engages in the discussions, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.2.3. Emotional support & companionship[18]

The patient received ongoing support from social workers, volunteers, or family members (e.g., weekly companionship for 1–2 hours).

If the patient receives at least 3 months of companionship or emotional support, with interactions at least once a week, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.2.4. Health education[19]

The patient participated in educational activities related to health management and medication use, attending at least 70% of each course.

If the patient attends at least 5 health education sessions and completes the post-course assignments, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.3. Physical therapy

3.2.3.1. Exercise therapy[20]

The patient attended at least one planned exercise session per week (e.g., endurance or physical training), and continued for at least 8 to 12 weeks.

If the patient participates in more than 80% of the exercise sessions and achieves improvements in physical fitness or exercise capacity during the program, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

3.2.3.2. Occupational therapy[21]

The patient completed the activities prescribed by the occupational therapist (e.g., enhancing self-care skills, improving quality of life activities), attending at least one session per week.

If the patient participates in at least 60% of the occupational therapy activities and is able to independently complete the corresponding daily tasks, they are considered to have “completed” the intervention.

4. Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS 26.0 and R 4.1.0 software for statistical analysis. All continuous variables were first subjected to the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality. For data that were not normally distributed, logarithmic transformation was applied to meet the requirements for analysis. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Group comparisons were made using independent sample t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Mann–Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and group comparisons were performed using chi-square tests or Fisher exact test (when the expected frequency < 5). To investigate the independent influencing factors of anxiety/depression symptoms, univariate analysis was first conducted to screen potential factors (P < .1). Subsequently, a multivariate logistic regression model (Enter method) was constructed to calculate the regression coefficients (β), standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and P-values for each variable. For subgroup analysis of the effects of nursing interventions, stratification was based on epilepsy duration (≤5 years vs >5 years), seizure frequency (>1 time/week vs ≤1 time/week), and seizure duration (≤1 minute vs >1 minute). The effects of each intervention were evaluated separately within these subgroups. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation under the assumption of missing at random. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

5. Results

5.1. Comparison of clinical characteristics between the two groups

This study compared the clinical characteristics of 73 epilepsy patients with anxiety and depression (AD group) and 128 epilepsy patients without anxiety and depression (NAD group). The results showed that the AD group was younger (34.5 ± 12.6 years vs 39.1 ± 11.7 years, P = .047), but there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of gender distribution and the etiology of epilepsy. Regarding disease duration, the AD group had a shorter epilepsy duration (P = .043). The proportion of patients with a disease duration of <1 year was significantly higher in the AD group compared to the control group (20.5% vs 9.4%). In terms of seizure characteristics, the AD group had a higher seizure frequency (24.7% of patients experienced seizures > 1 time/week vs 11.7%, P = .034), and there was a difference in seizure duration distribution (P = .041). However, there were no statistical differences between the 2 groups in seizure type. There were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of electroencephalogram (EEG) and imaging examination results (Table 1).

5.2. Comparison of Medication characteristics and seizure control

In terms of antiepileptic drug use, the AD group had a significantly lower proportion of patients who were not on medication compared to the control group (13.7% vs 22.6%, P = .045). However, the proportion of patients using 3 or more antiepileptic drugs was higher in the AD group (17.8% vs 11.7%) (Table 2). Regarding medication adherence, the AD group showed poorer adherence, with a significantly higher proportion of patients exhibiting low adherence compared to the control group (19.2% vs 8.6%, P = .052). More importantly, seizure control was significantly worse in the AD group (P = .007). The proportion of patients who were seizure-free was significantly lower in the AD group compared to the control group (24.7% vs 43.8%), while the proportion of patients with uncontrolled seizures was higher (27.4% vs 19.5%). However, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of EEG results (P = .293).

5.3. Comparison of psychological support and nursing interventions

This study compared the psychological support, nursing interventions, and physical therapy received by 73 epilepsy patients with anxiety/depression and 128 epilepsy patients without anxiety/depression (Table 3). The results showed that, in terms of psychological support services, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the AD group received cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) compared to the control group (47.9% vs 32.8%, P = .027). However, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of participation in support groups (24.7% vs 27.3%) and use of relaxation techniques (32.9% vs 31.3%). In terms of nursing interventions, the AD group had a significantly higher proportion of patients receiving individual psychological counseling (39.7% vs 19.5%, P = .011) and emotional support services (43.8% vs 36.7%, P = .039), while there were no differences between the groups in terms of participation in group counseling and health education. It is worth noting that both groups showed no statistically significant differences in participation rates in physical therapy programs such as exercise therapy (20.5% vs 21.1%) and occupational therapy (13.7% vs 14.1%) (P = .887).

5.4. Multivariate regression analysis

Multivariate regression analysis identified key predictors of anxiety/depression symptoms in epilepsy patients (Table 4). The results showed that seizure frequency was the most significant factor affecting psychiatric symptoms (β = 0.53, P < .001), followed by seizure duration (β = 0.37, P = .004) and epilepsy duration (β = 0.2, P = .014). Notably, the complexity of antiepileptic drug regimens (β = 0.42, P = .021) was positively correlated with the severity of symptoms, while good seizure control (β = −0.78, P < .001) showed the strongest protective effect. In terms of treatment interventions, the study confirmed that cognitive behavioral therapy (β = −0.65, P = .009) and emotional support services (β = −0.48, P = .027) had a significant impact on alleviating anxiety/depression symptoms. The age factor, after adjusting for other variables, was not statistically significant (P = .174). These findings provide important clinical insights: for patients with poor seizure control, longer disease duration, or those requiring multiple antiepileptic drugs, psychological interventions should be prioritized to improve their mental health.

5.5. Subgroup analysis of nursing intervention effects

The subgroup analysis revealed that epilepsy patients with different clinical characteristics responded differently to psychological nursing interventions (Table 5). For patients with an epilepsy duration of ≤5 years, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) showed significant effectiveness (β = −0.72, P = .004). In contrast, in the low-frequency seizure group, only CBT remained significant (β = −0.6, P = .014). 6. Discussion

This study reveals that 36.3% of epilepsy patients experienced anxiety and/or depression, confirming the high burden of emotional disorders in this population. Patients in the AD group were generally younger, had shorter disease durations, and reported more frequent seizures, suggesting that newly diagnosed or younger patients face greater psychological challenges after the onset of epilepsy. Multivariate regression analysis further demonstrated that seizure frequency, seizure duration, and complex antiepileptic drug regimens were major risk factors, while good seizure control was the strongest protective factor. In addition, cognitive behavioral therapy and emotional support showed significant benefits in alleviating psychiatric symptoms.

These findings are in line with previous epidemiological studies reporting prevalence rates of 30 to 40% for emotional disorders in epilepsy patients, indicating the consistency of our results with existing evidence.[22] Similar to Mula et al,[23] our study confirmed that seizure frequency is the most important predictor of anxiety and depression, whereas the protective effect of seizure control is consistent with Khatooni’s prospective findings.[24] However, the observation that patients with shorter disease duration had higher risks of emotional disorders contrasts with the traditional belief that longer disease duration leads to more psychological burden, highlighting the early psychological impact following diagnosis. Moreover, the limited effects of group counseling and health education differ from Reuber et al’s results,[25] suggesting that the effectiveness of interventions may vary depending on the clinical setting and population.

The possible mechanisms underlying these associations may involve bidirectional interactions between epilepsy and emotional disorders. Frequent seizures may disrupt limbic system function, impair social roles, and aggravate the unpredictability of daily life, thereby increasing psychological distress.[22] Neuroinflammation and microglial activation have also been identified as important contributors to epilepsy-related depression.[5] In addition, neurotransmitter dysregulation – such as reduced serotonergic and GABAergic activity – has been linked to both seizures and emotional instability, providing a biological basis for their comorbidity.[6] The use of multiple antiepileptic drugs may further contribute through drug–drug interactions or cumulative side effects, especially for medications known to affect mood such as topiramate.[3,23] Conversely, effective seizure control may mitigate this cycle by reducing neurobiological stress and improving patients’ confidence in disease management.

Clinically, these results underscore the need to strengthen psychological assessments and interventions in epilepsy management. Particular attention should be given to newly diagnosed patients and those with high seizure frequency, poor medication adherence, or multiple-drug regimens. Early implementation of interventions, especially a combined approach of cognitive behavioral therapy and individualized emotional support, may improve both mental health and seizure outcomes. The stratified effects of interventions also suggest that tailoring psychological support to clinical characteristics could maximize therapeutic benefits.

In addition, the causal relationship among seizure frequency, medication side effects, and emotional disorders warrants further attention. On the one hand, frequent seizures may aggravate psychological distress and reduce patients’ confidence in treatment. On the other hand, adverse effects of antiepileptic drugs, such as cognitive or mood disturbances, may directly exacerbate anxiety and depression, thereby reducing adherence. Negative emotions toward medication and fear of unpredictable seizures could further reinforce this vicious cycle. Although our retrospective design did not allow us to clarify these causal pathways, this issue highlights an important direction for future prospective studies.

This study has several limitations. As a single-center retrospective analysis, selection bias cannot be completely avoided, and excluding patients who were already on psychiatric medications may have underestimated the prevalence of emotional disorders. This exclusion also limited our ability to evaluate the potential impact of psychiatric drug treatment on patients’ emotional status and seizure control. Emotional assessments were based on rating scales rather than clinical diagnoses, which could limit accuracy. Non-randomized allocation of psychological interventions may also bias the evaluation of their effects. Furthermore, the influence of specific antiepileptic drug types on mood was not systematically analyzed, and long-term outcomes of interventions were not tracked.

Future research should therefore adopt multi-center, prospective designs with standardized diagnostic tools to validate these findings. Comparative studies of different psychological interventions and detailed assessments of the psychiatric effects of antiepileptic drugs are also needed. In addition, exploring synergistic strategies that combine seizure control with stratified psychological interventions may provide more precise and effective management of comorbid emotional disorders in epilepsy. Long-term follow-up studies are particularly necessary to determine the sustained effects of interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy, emotional support, and counseling, and to clarify whether early psychological assessment at the time of diagnosis can alter the long-term trajectory of mental health in epilepsy patients.
