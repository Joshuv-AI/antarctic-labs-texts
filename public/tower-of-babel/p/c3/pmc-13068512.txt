In silico docking and in vivo assessment of Mondia whitei fruit extract in attenuating CdCl2-Induced neurodegeneration in rat
Authors: Scholastica O. Anadozie, Olusola B. Adewale, Oluwole B. Akawa, Asueiza U. Aduma, Ikechukwu T. Ebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium is a toxic heavy metal found in the atmosphere. Its frequent exposure can lead to neurodegeneration. Medicinal plants are scavengers of free radicals that could result from exposure to heavy metals. In this study, the neuroprotective effect of the aqueous extract of Mondia whitei fruit (AEMW) against cadmium chloride (CdCl2)-induced neurotoxicity was investigated using an in silico and in vivo approach. The biological constituents in the AEMW were identified using high-performance liquid chromatography, and the compounds were docked against a target protein related to neurodegeneration. Rats were exposed to CdCl2 at 5 mg/kg for 5 days and co-treated with AEMW (250 or 500 mg/kg) for 7 days via oral administration. The levels of biogenic amines and activities of cholinergic and oxidative stress biomarkers were also evaluated. The molecular docking and ligand interactions of this study identified rutin, quercetin and kaempferol as the hit compounds with binding affinities of −15.197, −12.181 and −11.327 kcal/mol for acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and 10.325, −7.168 and −6.558 kcal/mol for human serotonin transporter (SERT), respectively. Cadmium chloride caused a significant (p < 0.05) elevation in the activities of AChE and BChE, and the levels of dopamine, serotonin and malondialdehyde with a significant (p < 0.05) decrease in the antioxidant activities. Co-administration of CdCl2 with AEMW, especially at the dose of 500 mg/kg, attenuated the effect of CdCl2 in the rats. Similar effect was also observed in the rat's brain histology. Therefore, AEMW could be a potential cholinesterase inhibitor to manage neuronal damage caused by heavy metal.

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is an environmental pollutant that harms humans and animals. It is formed as a by-product of zinc, lead and copper, during the processing of their ores. It is used in the production of batteries, plastic stabilizers and car pigments [1]. Cadmium deposits from these processes can contaminate soil and water bodies and negatively affect agricultural products, aquatic organisms, and drinking water [2]. It is one of the heavy metals to which humans are frequently exposed due to its industrial use. Other sources of Cd exposure include fossil fuel combustion, tobacco smoke, dumpsites and incineration [3]. Cadmium deposits are exceedingly high in humans due to its non-biodegradable nature, thereby, prolonging its toxic effect on the food supply chain [4]. Its toxicity in living organisms is dependent on the concentration and route of exposure. Continuous exposure to Cd can lead to its accumulation in vital organs, including the brain [5].

Cadmium has been reported to cause peripheral neuropathy, cerebral hemorrhage, learning and cognitive impairment, encephalopathy, and olfactory dysfunction in humans and experimental animals. These alterations are common to most conditions involving cholinergic neurotoxicity [5,6]. The olfactory epithelium takes Cd directly through inhalation to the nervous system, which crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [6]. Studies have reported that Cd harms the hippocampus, a part of the brain responsible for learning and memory [7,8]. Another route by which Cd enters the neuronal cells is via zinc transporters such as the zinc transporter 6 (ZIP6) and zinc transporter 3 (ZnT3). Studies have shown that dysfunction of these two transporters can lead to Cd accumulation in the neurons, which subsequently leads to neurodegeneration [6,9]. Cadmium neurotoxicity has been associated with oxidative stress (OS). Studies have reported elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and beta amyloid (β-amyloid) in rats exposed to Cd, which could contribute to the development of AD [10]. Cadmium increases the process of lipid peroxidation by generating free radicals and ROS in the brain, thereby inhibiting the antioxidant defense system and causing alterations in the functions of several biomolecules [11]. In addition, Cd has been reported to affect neuronal cells by altering the cholinergic system and neurotransmitters in the brain of rats [12]. The inhibitory effect of Cd on acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activities and its blockage of the synaptic membrane response to acetylcholine (ACh) stimuli have previously been reported in neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) [6]. Cadmium is considered a neurotoxicant in this study because the clinical implications of chronic Cd poisoning mimic those of neurodegenerative patients [6,13].

Neurodegeneration is a neuronal condition characterized by the steady, irreversible, and progressive degeneration of nerve cells, and a key feature of AD [14,15]. Although the pathogenesis of AD is not fully understood, it is believed that beta-amyloid plaques, hyperphosphorylated tau protein, ageing, OS, and environmental factors play a vital role in the progression of the disease [16,17]. Several clinical and pathological studies have reported the link between heavy metals and cognitive functions, thereby resulting in various kinds of neurodegeneration [18,19]. Conventional drugs for managing related conditions have numerous drawbacks, including low bioavailability, non-tissue specificity and poor blood-brain barrier penetration. Therefore, researchers are exploring medicinal plants with antioxidant capacities as an alternative therapy. Medicinal plants with antioxidant properties have been used to treat and prevent several human diseases [20], and studies are exploring the effects of several natural products as preventive therapeutic interventions for neurodegeneration-related disorders.

Herbs or medicinal plants have been used to manage several ailments since the prehistoric era. Plants are relatively safe, cost-friendly and effective. Also, they are rich sources of antioxidants, phytochemicals, vitamins, and minerals [21].

Mondia whitei is a running climber plant mostly common in moist tropical and subtropical regions of East, South and West Africa [22]. In Nigeria, the fruit is consumed as a snack by the Ishan-speaking tribe of Edo State, and in South Africa, the root is used as a meat tenderizer [23]. Traditionally, the fruit and root of the plant are used in managing erectile dysfunction. Previous studies have reported the aphrodisiac, anti-sickling and anticancer properties of the plant [24,25]. Several isolated compounds, such as para-pentyl phenyl benzoate and 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde, with known pharmacological properties, have been identified in the fruit of the plant [26].

Since Cd causes neurodegeneration via OS, the antioxidant activities of the M. whitei plant could scavenge the ROS and, thus, mitigate the effect of CdCl2 on the neuronal functions of rats. Even with the belief that fruits of M. whitei have great pharmacological properties, scientific evidence on the possible protective effect against neurotoxicity is still lacking. To investigate the neuroprotective activities of the AEMW, the bioactive compounds in the plant extract were first identified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and their neuroprotective properties were assessed against neurodegeneration-related protein targets using molecular docking studies. Further assessments were performed in vivo to examine the neuroprotective activities of the AEMW in CdCl2-induced neuronal damage by evaluating the cholinergic activities, levels of biogenic amines, and inflammatory and OS biomarkers in rats as biomarkers of neurotoxicity.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cadmium chloride (CdCl2) and glutathione were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemicals Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). ELISA kits for serotonin and dopamine were products of Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA. All other chemicals were used in their purchase form without further purification.

Collection of plant material and preparation of extract

Mondia whitei fruits were sourced from King's market in Ekpoma, Edo State, Nigeria. Identification and authentication of the plant was done by Mr. Felix Omotayo, a botanist at the Ekiti State University (EKSU), Ado Ekiti, Nigeria. A dehydrated plant sample was kept at the University herbarium under the number UHAE2022051 for future reference. The fruits were washed in clean water, diced into tiny pieces, air-dried, and pulverized. One hundred (100) g of the powdered plant was soaked in 0.5 L of distilled water for 24 h, then filtered with muslin cloth and Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The resulting filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C, and then lyophilized to powder form. The powdered sample was then stored in −20 °C freezer till needed.

High-performance liquid chromatography-diode array detector (HPLC-DAD) analysis

The chromatographic fingerprinting of the phytochemicals in AEMW fruit was identified using a C18 reversed‐phase column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) under a gradient elution condition. The mobile phase at a flow rate of 1 mL/min consisted of 20 μL solvent mixture known as solvent A (water-formic acid (1%, (v/v)) and solvent B (methanol, HPLC grade). Before the chromatographic runs, the M. whitei extract (5 mg mL−1) and mobile phase were filtered through a membrane filter (0.45 μm) and then degassed ultrasonically. The chromatographic peaks of the identified phytocompounds (phenolic and flavonoids) were compared with the retention time (Rt) and diode array detector (DAD) spectra of those of the reference standards at 240 - 380 nm. The chromatographic procedures were run in replicate at ambient temperature, and the concentration of the identified compounds was calculated from the peak areas of the chromatogram.

Bioactive compounds library and molecular docking preparation

Two-dimensional (2D) crystal structures in SDF format of HPLC identified phytochemicals present in the M. whitei plant, as well as some standard drugs modulating the identified and retrieved molecular targets, were retrieved from PubChem (https://PubChem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), a web-based chemistry database. The ligands were force-field treated using OPLS3 on the LigPrep interface in Schrodinger [27], and prepared for molecular docking. Before docking, however, co-crystallized ligand molecules were excluded from the grid generation. The co-crystallized ligands were extracted and docked to the active site of the protein using GLIDE tool on Maestro 11.5 employing the Extra Precision (XP) algorithm for proper validation, whereas docking scores and poses with shape complementarity illustrating best-ranked protein-ligand conformations as well as lowest binding free energies were subsequently incorporated and selected for evaluation and visualization.

Receptor retrieval and protein preparation

Ten (10) druggable macromolecular (receptors, enzymes, and transporters) targets implicated in the biosynthesis, uptake/reuptake, degradation and autoreceptor regulation of neurotransmitters with possible connections to neurodegeneration, including acetylcholine, dopamine, and serotonin, were retrieved from the protein data bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org). These macromolecular targets include acetylcholinesterase (AChE) (PDB ID: 7E3H) [28]; butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) (PDB ID: 4TPK) [29]; monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A (PDB ID: 2Z5X)) [30]; monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B (PDB ID: 6FVZ)) [31]; serotonin transporter (SERT (PDB ID: 5I6X)) [32]; tryptophan hydroxylase (TrpOH (PDB ID: 1MLW)) [33]; dopamine transporter, DAT (PDB ID: 4XP4) [34]; catechol-O-methyltransferase, COMT (PDB ID: 6I3C) [35]; tyrosine hydroxylase (TyrOH (PDB ID: 2TOH)) [36] and dopamine D2 autoreceptor (D2R (PDB ID: 6CM4)) [37]. Experimental design

Animal maintenance

Twenty-five male Wistar rats, 5 weeks old and weighing 90-130 g, were obtained from the animal breeding house of the Afe Babalola University, Ado Ekiti (ABUAD), Nigeria. The rats were maintained and housed in a well-ventilated polypropylene cage at room temperature, relative humidity of 45 - 60% and a 12-h light/dark cycle. During the animal study (a 7-day acclimatization and 7-day treatment period), rats were allowed access to drinking water and pelletized rat chow.

Animal grouping and treatment

The rats were arbitrarily assigned into four groups (n = 5); Group I served as control rats (without treatment). Group II was orally administered with 5 mg/kg b.w. CdCl2. Group III was orally administered with 5 mg/kg b.w. CdCl2 and co-treated with 250 mg/kg b.w AEMW. Group IV was orally administered with 5 mg/kg b.w. CdCl2 and co-treated with 500 mg/kg b.w. AEMW. The rats were exposed to CdCl2 for 5 days and co-treated with AEMW for 7 days. The doses of AEMW with no sign of toxicity chosen from the pilot study carried out in our laboratory (data not displayed) were used for this study.

Sample preparation

Twenty-four (24) h after the last treatment, rats were sacrificed by quick inhalation (anesthesia) of diethyl ether. The rats’ brains were rapidly dissected, weighed and rinsed in cold 0.9% sodium chloride (NaCl). A small portion of tissue was cut out from three animals in each group for histopathology. The striatum, amygdala and prefrontal cortex were excised for neurobiochemical assessments, while the remaining part of the brain was homogenized in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, and centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min to obtain supernatants for antioxidant assessments, which were stored at −20 °C until use.

Determination of neurological and oxidative stress markers

The serotonin and dopamine levels were estimated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits; serotonin/5-HT (Cat# E-EL-0033) and dopamine/DA (Cat# E-EL-0046) (ElabScience, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The neural cholinergic and antioxidant activities were assessed using the following methods: Ellman et al. [39] for AChE) and BChE activities, Misra and Fridovich [40], Sinha [41] and Habig et al. [42] for superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione-S-transferase (GST) activities, respectively, and Beutler [43] for reduced glutathione (GSH) level. Levels of OS (malondialdehyde (MDA)) and inflammatory (nitric oxide (NO)) biomarkers were determined using the methods by Varshney and Kale [44] and Green et al. [45], respectively.

Histological examination

The rat's brain was sectioned and processed for histology following the method described by Drury and Wallington [46]. The sectioned tissue on a glass slide was counterstained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and viewed under a light microscope at 800x magnification after drying.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on GraphPad Prism version 5, and values were expressed as mean ± SD A multiple comparison test was done using Tukey's post hoc test.

Result

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of AEMW

The HPLC analysis of the AEMW showed the presence of seven known bioactive compounds: quinic acid, rutin, vanillin, ferulic acid, quercetin, coumarin and kaempferol (Fig. 1). Binding affinity

Table 1 summarises the docking scores of the bioactive compounds in AEMW against targeted proteins and receptors. The hit compounds, rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, exhibited strong binding energies of −15.197, −12.181, and −11.327 kcal/mol for 7E3H, and −14.286, −9.081, and 8.662 kcal/mol against 4TPK. The binding affinity of these compounds to 4TPK was higher than that of the co-crystallized ligand (−6.689 kcal/mol); however, a lower binding energy was observed with the 7E3H when compared with the co-crystallized ligand (donepezil). Complex with 2Z5X and 6FVZ, rutin did not exhibit binding affinity; however, the binding energies for quercetin and kaempferol were −11.309 and −9.587 kcal/mol, respectively, and −10.548 and −7.295 kcal/mol for 2Z5X and 6FVZ, respectively, as compared with their co-crystallized ligands −8.526 and −10.776 kcal/mol. Against the SERT (5I6X), the three hit compounds demonstrated a binding energy of −10.325, −7.168 and −6.558 kcal/mol when compared to the co-crystallized ligand (−9.737 kcal/mol) and the standard drug (−10.171 kcal/mol). 1MLW also exhibited stronger affinity with the hit compounds −10.834, −10.185 and −9.163 kcal/mol when compared with the co-crystallized ligand (−5.138 kcal/mol) and standard drug (−5.138 kcal/mol). In addition, the 4XP1 exhibited binding energies of −11.325, −10.763, and −9.688 kcal/mol with rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol, respectively, compared to the co-crystallized ligand (−7.312 kcal/mol) and the standard drug (−5.841 kcal/mol). The same binding affinity occurred with 6I3C and 2TOH. Considering the dopamine regulation, 6CM4 exhibited a stronger affinity with rutin (−11.306 kcal/mol) but a lower affinity with quercetin (−9.170 kcal/mol) and kaempferol (−9.055 kcal/mol) when compared with the co-crystallized ligand (−11.054 kcal/mol).

In vivo study

AEMW inhibits the activities of acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase in CdCl2-induced neurodegeneration in rats

Fig. 6 shows the effect of AEMW on the AChE (Fig. 6a) and BChE (Fig. 6b) activities in the brain of the rats exposed to CdCl2. The activities of these cholinergic enzymes were significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CdCl2-only group and the CdCl2 and AEMW (250 mg/kg) co-administered group when compared to the control. Remarkably, compared with the CdCl2 group, co-administration of CdCl2 and AEMW (250- and 500 mg/kg) significantly (p < 0.05) decreased the activities of AChE and BChE in the brain of rats.

AEMW regulates serotonin and dopamine levels in CdCl2-induced neurodegeneration in rats

Fig. 7 depicts the effect of AEMW on serotonin and dopamine levels in the brain of CdCl2-induced neurotoxic rats. Cadmium exposure resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) decrease in levels of serotonin (Fig. 7a) and dopamine (Fig. 7b) in comparison to the control. Interestingly, AEMW significantly (p < 0.05) increased the levels of these biogenic amines in CdCl2 exposed rats co-treated with 500 mg/kg AEMW when compared to the CdCl2-only group.

AEMW modulates the level of NO in CdCl2-induced neurodegeneration in rats

Fig. 8 displays the effect of AEMW on NO levels in the brain of rats exposed to CdCl2. Compared with the control, Cd exposure significantly (p < 0.05) increased NO levels in the CdCl2 group as well as the CdCl2 rats co-treated with AEMW (250 mg/kg). Interestingly, AEMW significantly (p < 0.05) reduced the NO level when compared to the CdCl2-only group.

AEMW reverses lipid peroxidation in the brain of CdCl2-induced neurodegeneration in rats

The MDA levels were significantly (p < 0.05) elevated in the brain of rats intoxicated with CdCl2, and in the CdCl2 and 250 mg/kg AEMW co-administered groups, when compared with the control (Fig. 9). Compared to the CdCl2 group, co-administration of rats with CdCl2 and AEMW (250- and 500 mg/kg) significantly (p < 0.05) decreased the MDA level in the brain of rats.

AEMW restores antioxidant status in the brain of CdCl2-induced neurodegeneration in rats

Cadmium significantly (p < 0.05) reduced the activities of CAT (Fig. 10a), and SOD (Fig. 10b) and level of GSH (Fig. 10c) in rats, as well as in rats treated with CdCl2 and co-administered with 250 mg/kg AEMW, when compared with the control group. Treatment with AEMW at tested doses mitigated the effect of CdCl2 on these parameters in experimental rats.

Effect of CdCl2 and AEMW on the histology of the brain

Fig. 11 displays the microscopic observations of the effect of AEMW and CdCl2 on brain histology. The control showed normal histoarchitecture of the prefrontal cortex in the brain of the rat. Rats in the control group showed a well-structured, normal and evenly distributed viable neuronal cells (Fig. 11a). The CdCl2-intoxicated group showed severe damage in the prefrontal cortex region of the brain, characterized by disintegrated and pyknotic neurons (Fig. 11b). Treatment of CdCl2-intoxicated rats with 250 mg/kg AEMW resulted in slight morphological changes and a reduction in the neuronal cells (atrophy) (Fig. 11c). However, the 500 mg/kg AEMW attenuated the neural injury caused by CdCl2 in the rats by exhibiting normal and evenly distributed neuronal cells (Fig. 11d). The number of degenerating neurons were significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CdCl2 group when compared with the control (Fig. 12). However, there was significant (p < 0.05) reduction in neuronal degeneration in CdCl2 intoxicated rats co-treated with AEMW (250- and 500 mg/kg) when compared with the untreated CdCl2-intoxicated group.

Discussion

Cadmium is a toxic metal that causes harmful effects on various cellular and metabolic processes in humans and animals. It accumulates in different parts of the brain, leading to neuronal damage [11]. Cadmium induces neural damage and causes cognitive impairment through the generation of ROS and its interaction with macromolecules in the brain [47]. Cadmium crosses the BBB and neuronal olfactory, altering the mitochondrial functions and endogenous antioxidant enzymes, which could help prevent neurodegeneration [6]. Cadmium causes cell death in the cholinergic and non-cholinergic neurons of the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, thereby leading to a reduction in neural synaptic activity and alteration of the cholinergic system. This study showed the link between Cd toxicity, OS and neurodegeneration. To ascertain this, the biological activity of the AEMW was determined by the HPLC analysis and in silico studies, and to further confirm the protective effect of AEMW against CdCl2-induced neurotoxicity, biochemical and biogenic amine functions of the brain of rats were assessed.

High-performance liquid chromatography is an analytical method used to identify and separate analytes from a mixture of compounds. In this study, of the seven compounds identified, vanillin was the most abundant (Fig. 1) and has been reported to have several pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, antidiarrheal, anticancer and neuroprotective properties [48]. Gul et al. [49] in their study reported that vanillin acted as a cholinergic inhibitor by blocking the activities of AChE and BChE. This can halt the formation of amyloid aggregates and tau protein in the brain. This supports the outcome of the present study. Other identified compounds in the AEMW extract possessed antioxidant and neuroprotective properties [[50], [51], [52]], thus suggesting that the AEMW extract could be a potential novel therapy for managing neurotoxicity.

Molecular docking accelerated virtual screening has been employed in drug discovery initiatives, with notable advances in computer-aided drug discovery providing valuable predictive insights into protein–ligand interaction, which are crucial for drug development [28,53]. The use of bioactive plant compounds has emerged as promising alternative in the management of various disease conditions, which may be because of their safety profile [54]. In the present study, molecular docking was used to explore the structural compatibility of compounds identified by HPLC analysis of the AEMW with selected receptors, enzymes, and transporter targets implicated in the neurotransmitter biosynthesis, uptake/reuptake, degradation and autoreceptor regulation, particularly those related to dopamine and serotonin pathway (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3).

The neuroprotective effects of quercetin, rutin and kaempferol have been extensively reported in experimental models [55,56]], [ [57,58]]. Rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, coumarin, vanillin, ferulic acid, and quinic acid, identified by the HPLC analysis of this study were assessed for their neuroprotective effect using molecular docking. Based on predicted binding affinities and ligand residue interactions, rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol were identified as hit compounds (Table 1), suggesting favourable structural complementarity with several neurodegeneration-related targets. These compounds have previously been reported to possess neuroprotective properties [59]. The docking analyses of the present study revealed that these compounds have binding poses compatible with the binding sites of AChE and BChE, as well as selected neurotransmitter-related proteins, such as TrpOH, DAT, COM, TyrOH and D2R. Thus, suggesting that these compounds could target cholinergic enzymes and proteins involved in dopamine and serotonin signaling, which are implicated in most neuronal conditions.

Empirically, at the residue level, rutin, quercetin and kaempferol showed predicted binding interactions with amino acid residues such as TRP286 and PHE295 within the AChE binding pocket, including hydrogen bonding with PHE295, pi-pi stacking with TYR341 and TRP286 (Fig. 2), which have been previously related to AChE ligand recognition [59]. In addition, the docking of these compounds to BChE revealed interactions with the basic amino residues, such as TRP82, GLH197, LEU286, and HIS438 along with hydrogen bonding and pi-pi stacking patterns reported for BChE binding sites [29].Fig. 2Molecular docking of the 2D interactions of the three hit compounds complexed with cholinergic enzymes, 7E3H and 4TPK.Fig. 2

Acetylcholinesterase and BChE are cholinergic markers used in assessing neuronal damage. While the former is an enzyme that catalyze the breakdown of acetylcholine (ACh) to choline and acetate, the latter is a non-specific cholinesterase that hydrolyzes other choline esters such as butyrylcholine and propionylcholine [68]. It is mainly synthesized in the liver and is primarily found in the blood plasma. Although this enzyme has cholinergic functions, it is not as abundant as AChE in the brain. Changes in the activities of these enzymes can result in cholinergic deficiency, a hallmark of most neurological disorders, including AD [69]. Acetylcholine (Ach) plays a vital role in cognitive and memory functions. The activities of AChE and BChE are crucial, as they serve as significant biomarkers for detecting neurotoxic effects of certain xenobiotics, including heavy metals. Consequently, their activities are typically affected in NDs [70]. An increase in the brain's AChE activity leads to a decline in Ach levels. Cadmium exposure has been reported to increase AChE activity [71]. The elevated AChE and BChE activities observed in the Cd group of this study (Fig. 6) may be linked to its increased permeability through the BBB, resulting from the damage caused by CdCl2 in the cholinergic brain synapses and neuromuscular junctions. Cadmium can accumulate in specific brain regions, thereby, disrupting neurons and inducing OS, leading to neurotoxicity. Several studies have reported an increase in the activities of AChE and BChE in rats exposed to Cd [5,72]. In the central nervous system, dopamine is involved in motor function and coordination processes, while serotonin regulates cognitive processes such as anxiety, learning and memory function [73]. The brain is particularly at risk of OS because of its abundant lipid sources, leading to lipid peroxidation [75]. The utilization of oxygen and the compromised antioxidant system can promote the neurotoxicity. Rats exposed to CdCl2 have been reported to have an increased OS level and cognitive dysfunctions, which are involved in the several NDs [76]. Therefore, targeting OS level could be a promising therapeutic strategy to manage Cd-induced neurotoxicity. Antioxidants are metal chelators that allow interaction between a ligand and a central atom, thereby reducing reactivity [77]. Cadmium increases the production of ROS by disrupting the antioxidant system. This is evident in this study where CdCl2 elevated the levels of NO and MDA and reduced the antioxidant parameters (SOD, CAT and GSH) in the brain of rats (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10). The dysfunctional antioxidant system indicates neuronal damage associated with Cd toxicity. These damages were, however, reversed by the AEMW, thus suggesting the neuroprotection of AEMW against CdCl2-induced neurotoxicity in the rats. It is noteworthy that the AEMW restored the redox balance in the rat's brain in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that enhancing antioxidant status in a diseased brain can reduce OS generation and, consequently, offer a treatment option for neurotoxicity. The present study also has limitations, although HPLC profiling identified major phytoconstituents, the extract remains a complex mixture, and the specific compounds responsible for the neuroprotective effects were not isolated or independently validated in vivo. The present study did not evaluate the AEMW-only group to establish the safety and baseline effects of the extract as this have been previously done in our pilot study (data not shown) so as to reduce the number of experimental rats used in this study. In additional the molecular evaluation of the downstream signaling pathways via gene/protein expression was not explored in this study.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that rutin, quercetin and kaempferol, among the other phytochemicals in the AEMW identified by HPLC, possessed structural compatibility and potential binding affinities to cholinergic and monoaminergic targeted protein related to neurodegeneration. This, thus, highlights their potential as therapeutic candidates for mitigating diseases associated with neurotoxicity. The results of the in vivo studies showed that CdCl2 caused neurochemical and neuropathological changes in the rat's brain by increasing the activities of the cholinergic systems, as well as increasing the OS markers and lowering the endogenous antioxidant activity. Co-treatment with AEMW was able to protect the rats from the neurotoxic effect of CdCl2. The neuroprotective properties of the plant could be related to the inherent phytochemicals in the plant, which demonstrated potent antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and neuroprotective properties. The findings of this study showed the potential of AEMW as a cholinesterase inhibitor and can be used as a preventive and therapeutic agents for Cd-induced neurotoxicity. Author's contributions.
