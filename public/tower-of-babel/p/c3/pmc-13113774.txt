Recurrent cerebral venous thrombosis associated with IgG4-related hypertrophic pachymeningitis: Case report and literature review
Authors: Yanshu Wang, Yang Zhao, ChangYu Lu, Lihua Wang, Sheng-Guang Li, Xianzeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) is a systemic inflammatory condition marked by tissue infiltration of IgG4-positive plasma cells, often resulting in fibrosis. While IgG4-RD commonly affects organs such as the pancreas and salivary glands, central nervous system involvement, particularly hypertrophic pachymeningitis (HP), is rare and can present unique clinical challenges. This case report describes a 67-year-old male with IgG4-related hypertrophic pachymeningitis complicated by recurrent cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT), a rarely reported association. The patient initially responded to high-dose corticosteroids, but experienced symptom relapse upon tapering, necessitating treatment with rituximab for sustained disease control.In addition to presenting this case, we provide a comprehensive review of the literature on IgG4-RHP with CVT, analyzing clinical presentation, anatomical involvement, IgG4 serum levels, histopathological findings, treatment strategies, and outcomes across reported cases. Our analysis highlights the challenges of managing recurrent CVT in IgG4-RHP and underscores the importance of immunosuppressive therapy, particularly B-cell depletion, in achieving long-term disease stability. This case and literature review offer insights into the pathophysiology, diagnostic approach, and therapeutic considerations for this complex and rare manifestation of IgG4-RD.

Introduction

IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) is a chronic, multi-organ, immune-mediated inflammatory condition characterized by IgG4 + plasma cell infiltration, often resulting in tissue fibrosis and organ dysfunction [1]. While IgG4-RD is most commonly associated with autoimmune pancreatitis, it can also impact the central nervous system (CNS), primarily presenting as hypertrophic pachymeningitis (HP) and, in rare cases, affecting the cerebral parenchyma or intracranial vasculature [2, 3]. Recent case reports have increasingly recognized IgG4-related hypertrophic pachymeningitis (IgG4-RHP), with a limited but growing number of documented cases associated with cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT). This suggests a complex interplay between chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and venous obstruction, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms [4].

The clinical manifestations of HP are diverse, typically including headaches, cranial neuropathies, motor deficits, or cerebellar ataxia, but these symptoms can often lead to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis [5]. In CNS presentations of IgG4-RD, inflammation-associated venous occlusion is rare, and recurrent venous thrombosis is even less common. CVT as a complication of IgG4-RHP is hypothesized to involve local immune responses that lead to vascular wall inflammation and fibrosis, exacerbating venous outflow obstruction [6]. In several reported cases, the combination of IgG4-RHP with CVT has necessitated a multimodal therapeutic approach, often including corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents such as rituximab to manage both inflammatory and thrombotic components of the disease [7, 8].

We present the case of a 67-year-old male with IgG4-RHP complicated by recurrent CVT. Although initially responsive to high-dose glucocorticoids, the patient experienced multiple relapses during steroid tapering, eventually achieving disease stability with rituximab therapy. In light of this case and the analysis of similar cases in the literature, we aim to explore the potential mechanisms underlying CVT in IgG4-RHP and analyze the limitations of existing treatment approaches, providing insights into the optimal management of this complex clinical entity.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male presented with a one-year history of persistent headaches, which had intensified over the past three months, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and left-sided hearing loss. The patient’s medical history included left myringotomy tube placement for mastoiditis and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. He denied oral/genital ulcers, uveitis, or skin erythema nodosum. Physical examination was unremarkable except for left-sided hearing loss, with no other neurological deficits observed.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a thickened dura mater with abnormal enhancement in the left tentorium cerebelli and temporal lobe (Fig. 1a-c). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and magnetic resonance venogram (MRV) identified cerebral venous occlusion (CVO) involving the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses (Fig. 2a, b). An attempt at mechanical endovascular recanalization was unsuccessful. A [18 F]-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) scan showed increased uptake in the intracranial meningeal region (left cerebellar tentorium, left temporal lobe), bilateral posterior ethmoid sinus wall, and abdominal aorta, with no suspicious malignant lesions detected elsewhere.

Laboratory evaluations were mostly within normal limits, except for an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 50 mm/h and an IgG4 level of 202 mg/dL. Rheumatologic markers, including P-ANCA, C-ANCA, ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, anti-RNP, anticardiolipin antibodies, β2-glycoprotein, C3, C4, RF, anti-CCP, anti-SSA/Ro, and anti-SSB/La, were normal or within normal limits. Tests for acquired and genetic thrombophilia (e.g. protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency, and homocysteine levels) yielded negative results. Tumor markers, including CA724, CA199, CA125, AFP, and CEA, were also negative.

A lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 180 mm H2O. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed a protein level of 0.59 g/L and a cell count of 23 cells/µL, predominantly mononuclear. Cytological examination of the CSF showed no malignant cells. A meningeal biopsy demonstrated dense plasma cell infiltration, fibrosis, and an increased number of IgG4-positive plasma cells (more than 80 IgG4 + plasma cells per high-powered field, constituting approximately 40% of IgG+ plasma cells) (Fig. 3a, b), with no evidence of obliterative phlebitis, vasculitis, or granulomas.

The patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone (0.5 g/day, halved every three days), which led to a rapid alleviation of headaches, with the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score decreasing from 9/10 to 1/10. One week after the first methylprednisolone pulse, repeat MRV showed partial recanalization of the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses (Fig. 2c). However, upon tapering steroids to oral prednisone (60 mg daily), the headache recurred with a VAS score of 8/10, and MRV once again indicated occlusion of the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses (Fig. 2d). Given the patient’s responsiveness to high-dose steroids, intravenous pulse methylprednisolone (1 g/day, halved for three days) was re-administered, leading to dramatic headache relief (VAS 1/10). One week after the second methylprednisolone pulse, Follow-up MRI and MRV revealed a significant reduction in dural thickening (Fig. 1d-f) and complete recanalization of the affected sinuses (Fig. 2e).

Before switching to oral prednisone at 60 mg, weekly intravenous cyclophosphamide (400 mg) was added. Nevertheless, the patient’s headaches recurred with a VAS score of 6/10, necessitating the maintenance of oral prednisone at 60 mg. Due to steroid-associated adverse effects, including a 3 kg weight gain and femoral head necrosis, prednisone was quickly tapered to 10 mg and maintained alongside weekly intravenous cyclophosphamide. Despite treatment, moderate pain (VAS 5/10) persisted one year later, and MRV showed recurrent occlusion of the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses before rituximab (Fig. 2f).

After consulting with a specialist in rheumatology and immunology, rituximab (0.5 g weekly for four weeks) was initiated. Following the rituximab treatment, the patient experienced marked improvement in headaches (VAS 0/10), with significant reduction in dural thickening (Fig. 1g-i); however, the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses remained occluded at 2 year follow up after rituximab (Fig. 2f). After admission, the patient was given enoxaparin for anticoagulation. One and a half months later, a re-examination showed that the cerebral veins had recanalized, and the treatment was switched to long-term oral rivaroxaban.

The cases presented in Table 1 reveal a broad age range (26–67 years) and include both male and female patients. This reflects that IgG4-RHP with CVT can occur across a wide demographic, though the condition appears to have a slight female predominance. Disease duration varies significantly, from acute presentations to chronic cases extending over several years. This range underscores the heterogeneity of IgG4-RHP’s clinical course, which may progress insidiously or present with acute symptoms, often complicating diagnosis and treatment planning.

Anatomical diagnoses in these cases typically involved hypertrophic pachymeningitis with additional findings, such as optic neuritis and orbital mass [3, 8]. Thrombosis commonly affected major sinuses, including the superior sagittal, straight, transverse, and sigmoid sinuses. The elevated IgG4 levels observed in all cases(>135 mg/dL) support the diagnosis of IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD). Elevated serum IgG4 serves as a key indicator for IgG4-RD; however, normal values do not rule out the diagnosis, particularly in localized disease. According to the 2019 ACR/EULAR criteria for IgG4-RD, this case met the inclusion/exclusion criteria, with a total score of 28 points, fulfilling the classification for IgG4-RD.

Histopathological examination consistently shows dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with IgG4-positive plasma cells and storiform fibrosis. This pattern is a hallmark of IgG4-RHP, observed in the majority of cases. In addition to these findings, some cases exhibited obliterative phlebitis or rare phlebitis, highlighting the potential role of inflammatory processes in venous occlusion [6]. Pathological examination of the meningeal biopsy in our patient revealed no obliterative phlebitis, but the patient did present with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis. Notably, recanalization of the venous sinus was achieved during high-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy, but re-occlusion occurred once the glucocorticoid dosage was reduced to 60 mg orally. This treatment outcome suggests that inflammatory also play a very important role in cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.

Treatment strategies for IgG4-RHP with CVT generally include high-dose corticosteroids as the first-line approach. In several cases, immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine and rituximab were used as steroid-sparing therapies to maintain remission and prevent relapse. For patients with recurrent or steroid-refractory, rituximab was associated with a favorable clinical outcomes [6, 9]. Anticoagulants were frequently administered alongside immunosuppressive therapy to manage venous thrombosis. In our case, initial treatment with high-dose steroids led to symptomatic improvement; the disease relapsed upon steroid tapering, rituximab was associated with sustained clinical remission in this patient. However we explicitly acknowledge the limitations of inferring treatment superiority from one case plus limited literature.

In conclusion, IgG4-RHP with CVT represents a rare but challenging clinical entity. The cases reviewed here suggest that, while corticosteroids are effective in the initial management, additional immunosuppressive agents like rituximab may be necessary for long-term stabilization. After thoroughly excluding key differential diagnoses (such as tuberculosis, ANCA associated vasculitis, Behcet’s disease, paraneoplastic syndromes, etc.), the combination of characteristic clinical-radiological features and a favorable response to initial immunotherapy can provide strong supportive evidence for the diagnosis of IgG4-RD. Considering the non-negligible risks associated with dural biopsy, histopathological confirmation via biopsy should be regarded as a definitive diagnostic measure reserved for cases where diagnosis remains ambiguous, treatment response is insufficient, or the exclusion of other specific diseases is required. Further research is essential to optimize treatment protocols and improve outcomes for patients with this complex manifestation of IgG4-RD.
