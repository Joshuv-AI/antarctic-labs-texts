Mortality risk and vulnerability to hot nights in mainland China
Authors: Jingjing Wu, Tanchun Yu, Shiqi Liu, Jie Wang, Peng Yin, Jiangmei Liu, Yalan Li, Rujia Miao, Hong Yuan, Yao Lu, Maigeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlthough high temperatures pose major public health risks, limited research has focused on nighttime heat, especially in developing countries. Evidence on the health impacts of hot nights for vulnerable groups remains scarce. We conducted a nationwide time-series study using data from China’s Disease Surveillance Point system between 2015 and 2019 (May–September). Hot nights, defined as daily minimum temperatures above the 95th percentile, were assessed using hot night excess (HNE) and hot night duration (HND). We evaluated all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks associated with hot night exposure and identified vulnerable populations through stratified analyses. Hot nights significantly increased both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across mainland China, with peaking immediately and lasting up to five days. Older adults and females were particularly susceptible. These findings highlight the urgent need to address the health impacts of nighttime heat, especially among vulnerable populations, and support the development of targeted strategies to reduce heat-related mortality.Subject areas: public health, Atmospheric science, Climatology, environmental health

Introduction

Higher temperatures are occurring more often and at a greater intensity, contributing to 11.7 million disability-adjusted life years globally.1 Recent studies have focused on the effects of extreme heat, such as that during heatwaves,2,3,4 on mortality, but the association between nighttime heat and mortality has been understudied. Projections indicate that under low-emission scenarios, the frequency and intensity of hot nights are expected to increase, and the attributable mortality due to hot nights could increase nearly 6-fold by 2090.5 The limited existing studies conducted in high-income nations, highlighting the mortality burden from hot nights,5,6,7,8,9 while no evidence exploring the actual national burden caused by it such as China, which faces ongoing challenges from extreme heatwaves and may have an even more significant burden. Especially, China, as a populous developing country, faces the more serious challenge of heat and hot nights.5,10 Furthermore, no investigations have attempted to identify vulnerable subgroups and unfavorable city-level characteristics for hot nights, thereby presenting a challenge in the measures of shielding the population from hot nights.

Nighttime warming has been proven to disrupt the circadian clock, an important regulator of physiology and disease, and contributes to death and a wide range of diseases.11,12 Many cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as acute myocardial infarction, stroke, and arrhythmias, are regulated by the cardiovascular circadian clock.12,13,14,15 The disruption of the cardiovascular circadian clock can exacerbate the incidence and severity of CVD events.16 Notably, the cardiovascular system, which serves as the bridge between organs and metabolism, is more likely to be influenced by the circadian clock through multiple pathways and mechanisms.13 However, whether exposure to a hot night is an exogenous risk factor that increases the risk of CVD-related mortality remains unknown, especially for individuals living in mainland China.

Therefore, in this study, we used two established nationwide datasets from 491 major counties in China to explore the relationships of exposure to hot nights, characterized as hot night duration (HND) and hot night excess (HNE), with daily all-cause and cardiovascular mortality at the national and regional levels and to identify populations with greater vulnerability to the effects of hot nights. Elucidating the impact of hot nights on mortality and identifying specific vulnerable populations in China may be essential for policy establishment.

A total of 491 countries in Mainland China with available data on temperature and mortality were included in the present study during the warm season from 2015 to 2019. Lag structure of hot nights on mortality

The lag-response associations of HND and HNE with mortality from lag day 0 to lag day 7 are shown in Figure 1. At the national level, we observed the increased risk of all-cause, CVD, CHD, and stroke mortality related to HND and HNE during lag day 0–5 (Figure 1). Specifically, we found that the all-cause mortality associated with HND and HNE appeared on lag day 0 immediately and gradually decreased until lag day 5, with a mortality replacement (RR less than 1) occurring on subsequent days. For CVD mortality, the associated risks manifesting immediately on lag day 0, gradually increased to a peak on lag day 1, and then steadily decreased to lag day 4–5 following a hot night. For subtypes of CVD mortality, CHD mortality risk showed no association with HND, while stroke mortality risk showed similar lag patterns caused by hot nights, with the effects of hot nights continuing to lag day 5.

Exposure-response association between hot nights and mortality

By studying the lag effect as described above, we discovered that the maximum lag duration for the adverse effects of hot night indices on death reached up to 5 days. We then explored the cumulative exposure-response association of HND and HNE with death during the lag period of 0–5 days. J-shaped exposure-response curves for the association between HND with all outcomes were observed, as well as for HNE (Figure 2). Notably, when the duration and intensity of hot nights exceeded 80% and 20°C–30°C, respectively, the effect of all the outcomes was significantly and persistently increased (Figure 2). The effect values at the 99th percentile of HNE and HND are presented in Figure S2. Both HND and HNE were associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (RR = 1.115, 95%CI, 1.093–1.138 for HND; RR = 1.224, 95%CI, 1.186–1.264 for HNE), CVD morality (RR = 1.179, 95%CI, 1.145–1.215 for HND; RR = 1.338, 95%CI, 1.279–1.400 for HNE), and stroke mortality (RR = 1.159, 95%CI, 1.116–1.204 for HND; RR = 1.306, 95%CI, 1.233–1.383 for HNE). In terms of CHD mortality, there was a significant association with HNE (RR = 1.346, 95%CI, 1.269–1.427), while HND had no effect on CHD mortality (RR = 1.115, 95%CI, 0.899–1.384; Figure S2).

Age, sex, economic development levels, and region-specific differences in hot-night-related mortality

When we repeated our analysis within different sub-group populations, the exposure-response curves for association all showed a similar “J-shape” and the risks of mortality increased with higher HND and HNE (Figure 3). However, the effect modifications were found in the association between hot night exposure and mortality.

When stratified by socio-demographic characteristics, we observed that the effect of hot nights on mortality is age-specific and sex-specific, with older (≥65 years) and females experiencing a more pronounced effect compared to younger and male (Figures 3 and 4). Specially, the RRs of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality associated with HND were 1.094 (95%CI, 1.069, 1.120) and 1.123 (95%CI, 1.088, 1.158) in the older adults and 1.159 (95%CI, 1.127–1.192) and 1.229 (95%CI, 1.181–1.278) in females (Figure 4). For HNE, the RR of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality were 1.218 (95%CI, 1.173–1.263) and 1.262 (95%CI, 1.201–1.325) in older adults and were 1.260 (95%CI, 1.204–1.318) and 1.366 (95%CI, 1.287–1.450) in females. These results suggest that older adults and females are expected to be more susceptible to hot-night-related mortality (Figure 4).

The susceptibility difference to hot nights across different regions is shown in Figures 3 and 4. Differential magnitudes of different economic development levels and region-specific estimates were observed on all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. After stratifying by different economic levels, individuals living in higher GDP levels had a higher hot-night-related CVD mortality risk (Figure 4). For all-cause mortality, individuals living in middle GDP levels had a higher RR for hot-night vulnerability. In the region-stratified analysis, differences effects of hot nights on mortality in region-specific populations were also found. We found that the exposure-response curves for the association of HND with mortality were steeper in the northern region, while the association of HNE with mortality was steeper in the southern region (Figure 3). Similar results were observed in the national cumulative risk distribution maps. Although HND and HNE levels were generally higher in the southern regions (Figure S1), the cumulative risk maps revealed that the associated cumulative risks were higher in the northern regions (Figure S3). The hot night-related mortality burden varied economically and geographically.

Sensitivity analysis

The associations of HND and HNE with mortality were robust, as shown in the results of sensitivity analysis (Figure 5; Figures S4–S7). After changing the lag period from 7days to 14 days, the results of the overall lag structure showed consistency with our main analysis (Figure S4). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in the exposure-response curves and cumulative RRs for hot night definitions based on NH90th and NH95th (used in the main analysis) (Figures 5 and S5). After changing the df of the time variable used in the first stage, the results were still similar (Figures 5 and S6). The pooled cumulative RRs and exposure-response curves of all-cause and CVD mortality associated with HND and HNE were not appreciably changed after further adjusting for daily mean PM2.5 and O3 concentrations, respectively (Figures 5 and S7). When using daily maximum temperature instead of daily mean temperature as the control variable, the results were similar to those of the main analysis. However, the thresholds for high-temperature days (HND) and high-temperature hours (HNE) shifted significantly earlier (Figures 5 and S8). Meanwhile, neither further adjustment for the 5-year average gross domestic product (GDP) nor redefining the threshold as the moving 95th percentile (NH95th) altered the exposure–response curves or the cumulative relative risk (RR) estimates (Figures 5 and S8). In addition, the lag patterns observed with the moving threshold were consistent with those from the main analysis using a fixed threshold. (Figure S9)

Discussion

In this national time-series study, we investigated the association between exposure to hot nights (quantified by HND and HNE) and the risk of mortality in mainland China. Using data from national surveys, we depicted the national distribution of the association between exposure to hot nights and mortality. The risk of mortality among populations exposed to relatively high HND and HNE appeared immediately on lag day 0 and lasted for 3–5 days, with the largest lag effect of HND and HNE on mortality occurring on lag day 0 or day 1. The cumulative risk of mortality increased sharply when the HND was greater than 80% or the HNE was greater than 20–30°C. Additionally, this risk was particularly evident among older adults and female populations, suggesting that they are priority target populations in the effort to decrease the negative impact of exposure to hot nights on mortality.

Previous studies have explored the associations of hot nights with mortality mainly in developed countries or regions.5,6,7,8,9 A modeling study projected that hot night-related mortality substantially increases over time in East Asia, including Japan, Korea, and China.5 However, this study only included 28 cities in these three countries, and thus, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Our study used the data from 491 Chinese counties and can provide more convincing nationwide evidence for China that hot nights increase the risk of mortality. Furthermore, Kim et al. reported that hot nights were immediately associated with mortality in Japan, and the lag effect of hot nights on mortality lasted for approximately 14 days8 However, a time-series study involving 11 cities from Southern Europe revealed that the lag effect between hot night indices and mortality was immediate and lasted for 3 or 4 days in at least half of the cities,6 which is consistent with our finding about the lag structure of hot night effect. Our results also demonstrated that the risk of mortality in China associated with HND and HNE appeared immediately during the same time window, with nonlinear associations with mortality. These differences in the lag effect may be due to differences in geographical characteristics between areas.

Exposure to daily higher temperatures could lead to an increased CVD and related mortality burden.17,18,19,20,21 In particular, hot nights further affect circadian rhythms by influencing sleep-wake cycles, which may further contribute to the negative effects of high temperatures.11 A previous study based on hourly temperature data of London from 1993 to 2015 has found that nighttime exposures result in an additional contribution to heat-related mortality, especially for CVD, which may be highest for stroke.7 A population-based study conducted in England and Wales reported that warmer summer nights are positively associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality.22 Besides, the latest epidemiological study of 15 Chinese cities also reported that nighttime heat is associated with a greater risk of CVD events than daytime heat.18 In our study, exposure to both a higher frequency and intensity of hot nights can increase the risk of cardiovascular mortality. This association is biologically plausible and can be explained. Hot nights could prevent the decrease in core body temperature, which could disrupt sleep, and this can further induce circadian rhythm disruption at the national level. The circadian clock is present in virtually all cells in the body23 and coordinates behavior, physiology, and metabolism with Earth’s diurnal cycle.24 Its disruption significantly impacts multiple physiological functions, such as body temperature regulation25 and the sleep-wake rhythm,24,26 leading to a spectrum of disorders. Indeed, clinical studies have correlated circadian disruption with CVD events and have shown that the disruption of the cardiovascular circadian clock can exacerbate the incidence and severity of CVD events, especially for nighttime stroke.16,27,28,29 This evidence is significant for the establishment and improvement of public health policy to prevent CVD-related adverse outcomes caused by heat.

According to our subgroup analysis, we observed that the effect of hot nights on mortality, especially cardiovascular mortality, was greater in older adults and females, which is consistent with previous heat-related studies.30,31,32 Older adults are more susceptible to heat-related mortality as age-related declines in physiological heat adaptation, including a reduced ability to regulate body temperature,33,34,35 an altered sweating response during heat stress,34,35 and impaired increases in the skin blood flow response to heat,34,35 together with a higher incidence of preexisting chronic diseases and medication use.36,37 In addition to the recognized factors, older adults tend to experience circadian rhythm disruptions and poor sleep,35 which can be exacerbated by higher nighttime temperatures.11 Regarding the sex difference, a potential explanation for it is that sex-related physiological and thermoregulatory differences, such as a larger surface area-to-mass ratio and lower sweat rate, result in reduced heat tolerance in women.38 Our findings suggest that older people and females should be more cautious about the adverse effects of hot nights, and highlight the need to prioritize the protection of older and vulnerable populations when developing public health response strategies for hot nights.

In addition, our results also showed that the effect of hot nights on mortality can be modified by socioeconomic and geographical factors. The effect of HNE on cardiovascular mortality was greater in populations in regions with higher economic development levels and in the southern regions of China. For socioeconomic factors, the urban heat island effect may be a primary reason for this situation. In highly urbanized areas, the urban heat island effect is exacerbated by the release of waste heat from industrial and residential sources such as traffic, lighting, and air conditioning, resulting in higher nighttime temperatures.39 In addition, night shift workers tend to frequently occur in regions with higher economic levels, and usually have severe circadian rhythm disruption. Nighttime warming may exacerbate the effects of circadian rhythm disruption, which can increase the risk of CVD.40

Although China has introduced heat-related policies such as work restrictions41 and heat warnings,42 they rely solely on daytime temperatures and absolute thresholds, with no measures targeting nighttime heat. This gap warrants greater policy attention. First, enhancing nighttime heat warning systems—particularly in northern regions with lower heat adaptation—is crucial, as hot nights are expected to increase even under strict carbon control scenarios.5 Second, while indoor cooling such as air conditioning43 can reduce health risks, policies must balance its benefits against potential air pollution,44 as recent studies show heat and pollution may jointly affect health.45 Lastly, structural adaptations of buildings such as cool and evaporative roofs and urban greening have proven effective in reducing heat exposure43 and protecting against nighttime warming.5 Some Chinese cities have adopted green roofs, and broader implementation is recommended. Lastly, we should also pay attention to the differences in the effects of hot nights experienced by different regions for better policy promotion.

There were several major strengths of this study. First, in contrast to previous studies confined to a single city or several cities of a developed country, the present study was the first study conducted in mainland China and included 491 counties, which is representative of the general adult population in China and can be used to derive novel, comprehensive, and robust evidence on the relationship between hot nights and mortality. Therefore, the findings can be interpreted as the nationally actual mortality burden of China caused by hot nights. Second, this study extended previous findings; we evaluated elevated nighttime temperatures from both temporal and intensity perspectives using two indicators simultaneously. Third, we found that compared to other populations, older females in China seem to have greater sensitivity to hot nights and provide evidence for key management populations exposed to hot nights. These findings have implications for public health specialists to mitigate the excess burden of health contributed by hot nights.

In conclusion, this study provided comprehensive evidence highlighting the severe and pressing impact of exposure to hot nights on mortality. Notably, older people and females appear to be particularly vulnerable to the effects of hot nights, warranting further investigation. Moreover, regional variations in the effects of hot nights underscore the need for region-specific tailored strategies to address those effects. These nationwide findings can inform public health initiatives by demonstrating the need for increased awareness and policy interventions to alleviate the mortality burden associated with hot nights.

Limitations of the study

However, there are several limitations in this study. Firstly, similar to most epidemiology studies investigating environmental health effects, we used temperature data at the county level rather than individual direct measurements to define the hot night exposure, which could introduce measurement errors of exposure. However, the time series analysis used in our study could decrease the effects, as the analysis can clarify the association between exposure and mortality.46 Secondly, as an ecological study, the influence of multimorbidity and other confounders at the individual level can not be considered. Thirdly, we focused on the effects of hot nights in this study and did not assess the effects of daytime heat. However, we have adjusted the daily mean temperature in our main model. Finally, we need to acknowledge that it is inevitable for mortality code errors in such a large-scale nationwide study, which is probably. Therefore, the cause-specific estimated mortality risks due to hot nights should be interpreted cautiously, while the estimates for all-cause mortality are more reliable. Due to data constraints, we were unable to validate the hourly temperature data with precise station-level observations. However, the data were obtained from the ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset, which has been validated and widely applied in temperature studies across mainland China. Future research is needed to address this limitation and enable validation.

Method details

Data collection

The Disease Surveillance Point System (DSPS), which is administered by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, provides representative national and provincial estimates of mortality in China and is aimed at investigating regional and national disease burdens. This system comprises 605 counties from 322 cities and covers a population of 323.8 million individuals, accounting for approximately 24.3% of the total population of mainland China in 2013.47 The development of DSPS has been previously described in detail.17,47,48 Since we focused on the association between mortality risk and exposure to hot nights, we restricted the study period to the warmest 5 months (May to September) of the year for each year during 2015–2019. For this analysis, daily mortality data from 2015 to 2019 were extracted from the DSPS, and counties with complete temperature data and more than 3 deaths per day were included in the analysis. In addition to collecting information on the cause of death, the address where the death occurred, the death code, and the date of death, we also collected demographic information, including sex and age, for each person who died.

Exposure assessment

The daily mean temperature, dewpoint temperature, and pressure from 2015 to 2019 were calculated using Python version 3.8.3 and were based on hourly data collected from the ERA5-Land dataset (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu), and the spatial resolution of the dataset was 10 × 10 km. ERA5-Land is a reanalysis dataset providing a consistent view of the evolution of land variables over several decades at an enhanced resolution compared to that of ERA5. ERA5-Land was produced by replaying the land component of the ECMWF ERA5 climate reanalysis. The daily mean humidity was estimated by combining the temperature, dewpoint temperature, and pressure. The units of daily temperature and daily humidity were °C and %, respectively.

The total number of night hours was defined as the number of hours between sunset (day t) and sunrise (day t+1) (each sunset and the sunrise of the following day) according to the methods of a previous study.6 The local times of sunset and sunrise for our study period (May to September for each year during 2015–2019) were obtained from the Convenience Consultation Network (http://www.BMCX.com). We obtained the daily sunset and sunrise times for each county based on the longitude and latitude of the address using Python version 3.8.3.

Hot nights were defined as nights for which the daily minimum temperature was higher than the threshold. According to the methods of previous studies,8 the threshold was defined as the 95th percentile of the daily minimum temperature (HN95th) during the study period for each county in this study. HND and HNE are two indicators used to evaluate the duration and intensity of hot nights. HND is calculated as the sum of the number of hours at night that exceeded HN95th divided by the total number of night hours, to present the duration of the heat effect at night.6 HNE was calculated as the sum of the excess heat during the period with temperatures ≥ HN95th, to quantify the intensity of a hot night.5 The daily HND and daily HNE results are presented as % and °C, respectively. Detailed information about HND and HNE are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Outcome definition

The assessment of mortality was coded according to the 10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). The daily cause-specific mortality data extracted for this study included all-cause mortality (codes A00–Z99), and CVD mortality (codes I00-I99). To allow for identifying effects on CVD subtypes mortality, we also assessed mortality associated with coronary heart disease (CHD; codes I20-I25) and stroke (codes I60-I69) mortality.

Environmental data

Air pollutants were linked to the data of mortality based on the date and address are the indexes. Daily concentrations of air pollutants, including particulate matter with Fine Particular Matter (PM2.5) and ozone (O3), for each county during 2015–2019 were extracted from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html), with a spatial resolution of 1 × 1 km2. We obtained these daily PM2.5 and O3 concentrations for each city based on the longitude and latitude of the address through the Baidu Web service API “Geocoder” with Python version 3.8.3.49 Details on the assessment of these air pollutants is provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The associations of HND and HNE with mortality in mainland China were estimated by using a two-stage analytical approach

In the first stage, generalized additive models with quasi-Poisson regression combined with distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNMs) were used to estimate the associations between HND and HNE (compared with nonhot nights) and mortality in each county.50,51 Cross-basis functions for hot nights at each location established by the DLNM included two natural B-splines, one for simulating exposure-response curves with 3 internal knots at equally spaced percentiles of the location-specific index distributions and another for lag-response curves with intercepts with 3 internal knots placed at equal intervals on the log scale.51 We a priori-selected a maximum lag period of up to 7 days according to previous studies on hot nights.6,18 Then, the maximum lag duration for the adverse effects of hot night indices on mortality that was revealed in the lag-response relationship was used to assess the exposure-response association. Seasonal and long-term trends in mortality were controlled using a natural cubic spline smoothing function of calendar day with 4 degrees of freedom (df) per year and an indicator of a day of the week. To control the potentially nonlinear effects of weather conditions, we adjusted the daily mean temperature (natural cubic spline; 6 df) and humidity (natural cubic spline; 3 df).

In the second stage, random-effects meta-regression models were used to pool the county-specific exposure-response associations and the lag-response associations obtained from the first stage.52 The overall relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of mortality associated with hot nights were calculated compared to those associated with non-hot nights (HND = 0°C and HNE = 0%) at the 99th percentiles of regional HNE and HND. Heterogeneity was assessed by the Cochran Q test and multivariate extension of the I2 statistic.53,54

Stratified and sensitivity analyses

Additionally, to explore the role of potential effect modifiers on the association between hot nights and mortality, we conducted stratified analyses by sex (male or female), age (<65 or ≥65 years), and region (southern or northern area). To further identify the potential effect of socioeconomic factors, we collected data on the annual average per capital gross domestic product (GDP) during 2015–2019 from statistical yearbooks in China at the city level, to represent its economic development levels. This stratified analysis was limited to the regions with GDP information. Then, we categorized all counties in mainland China into three groups based on tertiles of 5-year average GDP levels (unit of renminbi, RMB): low (≤38,122), medium (38,122-62,644), and high (≥62,644). In addition, we further generated cumulative risk maps to illustrate the spatial distribution of cumulative risks.

Furthermore, to test the reliability of the main results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we extended the lag days of hot nights from 7 days to 14 days, to validate that the lag days used in our study were sufficient to capture the effect of hot nights. Second, we further changed the thresholds of hot nights from the 95th (HN95th) to the 90th (HN90th) percentile of the daily minimum temperature during the study period. Third, we changed df of the time (4–6 df). To control the effect of other environmental factors, we further included PM2.5 and O3 in the main model, which was restricted to only counties with complete meteorological and air pollution data during the same period. Then, to remove the diurnal effect more accurately, we repeat the main analysis and control for the daily maximum temperature instead of the daily average. To determine the explanation of economic heterogeneity, we further control for the 5-year average GDP for each county. Lastly, we use a moving 95th percentile, which adapts locally and throughout the year to the climate conditions for each county to repeat the main analysis.

All the statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software (version 16.0) and R software (version 4.1.3). The R packages “dlnm” and “mvmeta” were used to construct a distributed lag nonlinear model and conduct the meta-analysis. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
