Transorbital intraoperative electrocorticography-guided surgical resection for medically refractory developmental epileptic encephalopathy with spike-wave activation in sleep
Authors: Michael E Baumgartner, Sudha Kessler, Alier J Franco, Samuel B Tomlinson, Benjamin C Kennedy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•EEG abnormalities in sleep and developmental delay/regression are typical of DEE-SWAS.•Surgical interventions remain uncommon for DEE-SWAS.•An 11-year-old boy with a history of epilepsy presented with refractory DEE-SWAS.•iECoG was employed to guide extent of resection of a focus adjacent to Broca’s area.•Surgery resulted in improved cognitive function and resolution of EEG abnormalities.Keywords: Epilepsy, Intraoperative electrocorticography, DEE-SWAS, ECoG, Resection, ESES, CSWS

Introduction

DEE-SWAS (also known as electrical status epilepticus of sleep, ESES, or the syndrome of continuous spike wave in sleep, CSWS) is a rare, poorly understood epileptic syndrome characterized by spike-wave discharges that are activated during sleep associated with neurodevelopmental decline or plateauing [1]. DEE-SWAS is associated with heterogeneous etiologies, including genetic and structural abnormalities [1], [2]. While treatment recommendations have been based primarily on retrospective studies, a randomized controlled trial was recently published, RESCUE-ESES, comparing treatment with either corticosteroids or clobazam, which found that corticosteroid treatment was associated with improved IQ outcomes relative to clobazam [3]. While DEE-SWAS results from heterogeneous etiologies, a recent review article reported that roughly 45 % of cases are due to a structural abnormality [2], and retrospective studies of small numbers of patients who have undergone resective surgery for DEE-SWAS show generally favorable outcomes with regard to seizure control, resolution of EEG abnormalities, and improvements in cognitive and language function [4], [5]. In one retrospective case series, 51 % of patients with DEE-SWAS demonstrated focal electrographic activity and 49 % were associated with discernable structural abnormalities on neuroimaging. The location of focal discharges is linked to the type of cognitive impairment that ensues, and DEE-SWAS patients with generalized discharges are more likely to experience global developmental issues [6].

Intraoperative electrocorticography (iECoG) has several well-characterized limitations which have led to controversy regarding its utility. iECoG relies on interictal recordings during surgery, thus constraining measurements to a short time window wherein patients are sedated with medications that suppress epileptiform activity. This substantially alters neuronal function and reduces the likelihood of capturing electrographic patterns associated with epilepsy. Furthermore, the act of performing surgery is thought to irritate the cortex, potentially altering local EEG patterns and increasing noise [7]. Furthermore, extraoperative recordings by grid or depth electrodes can provide a much larger sample size of interictal spiking in both time and space in the absence of anesthetics that suppress neural activity. However, a recent meta-analysis found that iECoG was associated with longer seizure freedom in patients with focal cortical dysplasia, which has interictal EEG patterns that are thought to represent a biomarker suitable for iECoG [7]. The distinctive EEG properties of DEE-SWAS may make them similarly suitable candidates for iECoG: spiking is often nearly continuous in sleep.

The present study is the case of an 11-year-old boy with medically refractory focal DEE-SWAS who was treated with iECoG-guided surgical resection through the orbit. This case is intended to illustrate the clinical, imaging, and electrographic presentation of focal DEE-SWAS amenable to surgical management, along with illustration of the potential utility of iECoG in such circumstances.

Case report

An 11-year-old left-handed boy with a history of ADHD and focal epilepsy presented with cognitive decline.

He had no significant medical history other than a prior diagnosis of ADHD until 6 years of age, when he experienced two focal seizures characterized by arousal from sleep, staring blankly, jaw clenching, hypermotor limb movements, and emesis. Each seizure was short and self-resolved. He had no known risk factors for epilepsy (no perinatal difficulties, no history of head trauma or CNS infection, and no first degree family history of epilepsy). At baseline, he attended school but was noted by his teacher and parents to have issues with organization, difficulty with multi-step instructions, slow progress, and difficulty answering questions. An initial routine EEG in wakefulness showed left frontal spike wave discharges and slowing. Initial treatment for a diagnosis of focal epilepsy was with oxcarbazepine (OXC). When he began showing signs of worsening inattention and experiencing a decline in school performance, ambulatory EEG monitoring while still on OXC revealed a spike wave index (SWI) of greater than 80 % in sleep, leading to a diagnosis of DEE-SWAS (Fig. 1).

Left frontal discharges have a field to the right, indicated by lower amplitude synchronous sharp waves on the right; no independent right hemispheric discharges were apparent.

Though no further clinical seizures occurred on a 40 mg/kg/day dose of OXC, it was discontinued when the diagnosis of DEE-SWAS was made, as there have been case reports of OXC inducing DEE-SWAS [8], [9]. Resolution of interictal discharges and cognitive improvement occurred briefly with initiation of valproate, but neither was sustained. Subsequent trials of many other antiseizure medications alone and in combination – including clobazam, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, and corticosteroids – did not lead to sustained improvement of his EEG findings or his cognition. Symptomatic treatment of attentional impairments with methylphenidate and classroom accommodations modestly ameliorated academic dysfunction but did not return him to his prior performance status.

Between ages 7 and 10 years, formal neuropsychological testing showed a decline in multiple domains of cognition, but particularly in verbal comprehension abilities (such as vocabulary and abstract verbal reasoning abilities) and fluid reasoning which dropped from the low average range to the extremely low range. On the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V), his verbal comprehension index dropped from 86 to 68.

Structural MR imaging revealed subtle abnormal folding patterns in the anterior left frontal lobe raising concern for a malformation of cortical development. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) identified abundant epileptiform abnormalities in the left frontopolar region (Fig. 2A-B). PET/MRI detected no areas of hypometabolism or hypermetabolism.

A multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conference discussion concluded that while no clinical seizures were occurring, the patient’s DEE-SWAS was causing substantial functional impairment with a clear decline in abilities despite multiple medication trials, with cognitive symptomatology concordant with the hypothesized epileptogenic zone based on EEG, MEG, and MRI findings. Stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) was performed without complication. He had a single population of spike discharges that were localized to the left frontal convexity maximally negative in the pars orbitalis and middle frontal gyrus (mid and anterior portion), with fields extending to the anterior and posterior orbitofrontal gyri and anterior insula. The location of these discharges was concordant with MRI and MEG findings (Fig. 2C-D). Language mapping with sEEG was attempted but unsuccessful in producing speech arrest or language errors.

Given the proximity of this lesion to canonical Broca’s area, a functional MRI (fMRI) study was performed which confirmed left-sided language function in the left inferior frontal gyrus, adjacent to the SWAS focus. At epilepsy conference, continued medical management was deemed unlikely to achieve adequate symptom control, given that his EEG abnormalities had been highly refractory to medication. Given the highly concordant imaging and electrographic findings indicative of a SWAS focus, surgical resection was considered the most likely treatment to improve his neurologic decline, though the proximity to Broca’s area risked creating post-operative language deficits. Responsive neurostimulator (RNS) placement was also considered and discussed with the family, but the benefit of RNS in SWAS remains largely speculative, despite the lower risk to language function. The patient’s family was engaged in a shared decision-making process, and a plan was made for resection to reduce or eliminate the SWAS.

In SWAS, there are no discrete seizures and therefore there is no “onset zone.” Furthermore, the interictal discharges did not clearly delineate a small brain volume with a high degree of spatial specificity despite the high density of intracranial electrodes that had been placed, but instead most of the left frontal lobe. As a result, based on the observation of the SWAS by sEEG, there was not a clear boundary within which a modest resection would be likely to reduce the SWAS. Rather, resection of all brain in which spiking could be identified would certainly result in a significant language deficit given the fMRI-confirmed left frontal language dominance. In this specific situation, it was posited that iECoG might offer some physiologic differentiation between adequate and inadequate extent of resection. Given his distinctive focal, highly regular discharges, iECoG seemed feasible to assist in decision-making. Given the proximity to fMRI-confirmed Broca’s area, a larger-than-necessary resection could have significant consequences for speech and quality of life. However, a minimal resection of the area focusing on the highest amplitude spiking, without eradicating SWAS, would likely result in continued cognitive regression. For this reason, iECoG was employed to balance these considerations. Given the location of the seizure focus at the anterior skull base just over the left orbit and adjacent language function confirmed on fMRI, an eyebrow incision with a small superior orbitotomy would allow for optimal cosmesis, minimize pain by avoiding temporalis dissection, and avoid injury to, and even exposure of, language cortex.

Under total intravenous anesthesia with propofol, remifentanil, and decadron, the patient was secured with a Mayfield clamp and registered to the navigation system. An incision was made on the superior margin of the left eyebrow using a 15 blade, and dissection to the superior orbital rim was performed with a Colorado tip Bovie, avoiding the supraorbital nerve. Small fishhooks were used for scalp retraction with small cottonoid patties under them to protect the skin from the pressure of the hooks. Periorbita was dissected from the orbital roof and superolateral orbit with a Penfield 1. A small amount of temporalis was lifted off the skull, and a keyhole burr hole was made down to the periorbita and frontal dura. The bone overlying the left inferior frontal gyrus – the region responsible for the highest amplitude spiking – was elevated as a bone flap. To ensure minimal bone disruption and optimal cosmesis, the bone flap was pre-plated and a Misonix Bone Scalpel was used to make a lateral orbitotomy and a medial superior orbitotomy, as well as connect those two orbitotomies through the orbital roof. The dura was opened in a C-shaped fashion and flapped inferiorly toward the globe. A four-contact ECoG strip was laid along the anterior cranial fossa, dorsomedially (Fig. 3), and dorsolaterally, and spiking was observed. All iECoG data were acquired via a NIM-Eclipse 32 channel system (Medtronic) using standard methods. Then, two gyri determined by preoperative studies to be involved in the highest amplitude of the SWAS were resected en bloc and sent for pathology. These had also contained most of the MEG positivity. iECoG was checked again ventrally, dorsolaterally, and dorsomedially, and continued to demonstrate spiking. Further resection proceeded judiciously into the adjacent gyri in which the SWAS had been of lower amplitude on sEEG. iECoG was checked multiple times during this resection. Eventually, when the gyrus just anterior to the fMRI-identified language task-activated BOLD signal was resected, the iECoG record was noted to clear completely with no further spiking. At this point, hemostasis was achieved and the dura was closed primarily. The bone was reaffixed with titanium plates and screws, and the layers of the soft tissue were closed with dissolvable suture, including a subcuticular 4–0 monocryl and small amount of skin glue, taking care to keep the glue out of the hair of the eyebrow. He awoke without neurologic deficit or any word-finding difficulty and was discharged home in less than two days. Modest periorbital swelling had already begun to resolve by the time of discharge, and a total of two doses of 1.6 mg of morphine were administered for pain control. His pain was noted to be adequately controlled for discharge by postoperative day 2. Pathology was consistent with focal cortical dysplasia type 1A. Post-operative MRI demonstrated resection of a volume slightly beyond the spike abnormalities identified on MEG (Fig. 4A).

At one month follow up, the patient’s mother reported no new post-operative language deficits and subjective improvement in his pre-operative word-finding difficulty. At two and a half months follow up, he exhibited no focal neurologic deficits, and his mental status and language function were noted to be age-appropriate with parents reporting that he was expressing himself more clearly than preoperatively. He underwent repeat ambulatory EEG at 2 months and 8 months after surgery. He was on lamotrigine and levetiracetam until 6 months following surgery, at which point his levetiracetam was weaned. Both studies revealed complete resolution of SWAS with no remaining epileptiform discharges while awake nor in all stages of sleep (Fig. 4B). His surgical site healed with good cosmesis (Fig. 5). At formal neuropsychological evaluation 9 months following surgery, the patient was noted to be doing as well or better than in the year prior to his surgery across almost all domains. His IQ was stable relative to preoperatively, and he was noted to have probable improvements in attention, impulse control, and processing speed. He was also noted to be stable across other domains including cognitive flexibility, working memory, verbal retrieval, fine motor speed and dexterity, verbal and visual learning and memory, and word reading. The patient’s mother furthermore reports improvement in his day-to-day executive functions.

Discussion

DEE-SWAS remains a poorly understood phenomenon, and there is no consensus on optimal pharmacologic or surgical treatment strategies. A Cochrane review published in 2020 concluded that there was no evidence from trials to support or refute the use of pharmacologic treatment for DEE-SWAS [10]. Until recently, therefore, treatment recommendations were derived from case reports and retrospective series. Earlier this year, however, the first randomized controlled trial for pharmacologic management of DEE-SWAS was published [3], which found that corticosteroids resulted in a 25 % IQ responder rate (defined as improvement of at least 11.25 IF points) vs 0 % for clobazam. While this makes a compelling argument for corticosteroids over clobazam, a 75 % non-responder rate indicates that there remains much room for improvement in current DEE-SWAS management strategies. As DEE-SWAS is often a self-limited process, surgical interventions remain controversial, with surgery typically being pursued in the setting of cognitive decline in spite of medical management [5]. Although surgical intervention is uncommon for DEE-SWAS, favorable outcomes have been reported in the literature [2], [4]. Furthermore, in patients presenting with both DEE-SWAS and clinical seizures, resective surgeries can achieve both resolution of DEE-SWAS and seizures [5]. While available data are limited, case series argue that patients whose DEE-SWAS resolved following surgery exhibit a halt in cognitive decline and frequently demonstrate meaningful gains in cognitive development [5], [11]. A meta-analysis by van den Munckhof et al. found that surgery for DEE-SWAS was associated with a clinical improvement in 90 % of cases and cognitive improvement in 80 % of cases. Furthermore, these improvements were durable in 93 % of cases for which follow-up data were available. These rates of improvement were superior to those reported for patients treated with anti-seizure medications, benzodiazepines, and steroids [12]. Surgery, like in many other forms of epilepsy, likely reflects an effective intervention for patients with medically refractory focal DEE-SWAS.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of focal DEE-SWAS treated with iECoG-guided surgical resection, use of iECoG through an eyebrow orbitotomy, and use of eyebrow orbitotomy for DEE-SWAS. The patient exhibited an insidious onset of cognitive decline years after his initial clinical seizures had resolved. Importantly, preoperative studies including EEG, MRI, MEG, fMRI, neuropsychological testing, and sEEG were all highly concordant, pointing toward a focal source of this patient’s DEE-SWAS adjacent to eloquent cortex. These specific conditions created a clinical scenario amenable to iECoG-guided resection, wherein there was a clear need for maximal safe resection with an electrographic biomarker which could be readily observed intraoperatively. This report is consistent with other reports in the literature arguing that surgery can achieve complete resolution of EEG abnormalities, arrest, and potentially improve cognitive decline. Thus, surgery is a potentially curative intervention in select cases of DEE-SWAS with a focal onset.

During this case, iECoG was checked frequently during resection. Following initial resection of the pre-operatively determined highest amplitude focus, persistent spike-wave activity was noted on iECoG. These findings prompted further resection, which may have contributed to the observed resolution of his EEG abnormalities. While a single case does not provide clear evidence of this, this study represents proof-of-concept for the feasibility of iECoG in the management of DEE-SWAS, as well as its use in minimally invasive skull base approaches.

Conclusions

While DEE-SWAS is not typically managed surgically, surgical intervention can achieve complete resolution of EEG abnormalities in select cases with a focal onset. While rigorous studies investigating the effect on cognitive decline are lacking, case reports such as this argue that surgical intervention can achieve favorable outcomes and should be considered in selected cases. iECoG may be a useful tool guiding extent of resection in DEE-SWAS.
