Quantitative assessment of inspiratory loading on postprandial glycemia and metabolic response in healthy adults
Authors: Dongwook Yeo, Jesse C. Schwartz, Jinkyung Cho, Bruce D. Johnson, Chul-Ho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postprandial blood glucose (BG) regulation is a central determinant of metabolic health and cardiometabolic risk. While conventional exercise effectively lowers postprandial BG, alternative approaches that impose minimal musculoskeletal load remain underexplored. Inspiratory loading (IL) —repeated inspirations against graded resistance —selectively activates oxidative respiratory muscles. However, its real-time metabolic and mechanical demands and their influence on systemic glucose regulation have not been quantified. In this study, we quantitatively characterized the concurrent metabolic and mechanical responses to graded IL and examined their acute effects on postprandial BG in healthy adults. Six participants completed three randomized IL sessions—SHAM (no resistance), MOD (~ 40% maximal inspiratory pressure, MIP), and HIGH (~ 60% MIP)—separated by ≥ 72-h washouts. After fasting measurements, participants ingested 45 g of D-glucose followed by a 30-min IL protocol (6 × 4-min bouts with 1-min rest). Oxygen consumption (VO2), heart rate (HR), inspiratory pressure (IP), and estimated work of breathing (eWOB) were continuously recorded, and BG was measured before and after IL. Glucose ingestion elevated BG across all conditions. IL elicited load-dependent increases in VO2, HR, IP, and eWOB, with MOD and HIGH both significantly elevated relative to SHAM. Post-IL, BG decreased in HIGH and MOD but remained elevated in SHAM (p < 0.05). The change in BG correlated inversely with both VO2 and inspiratory effort indices (IP, eWOB; r = − 0.54 to − 0.59). These findings provide quantitative evidence that respiratory muscle activation alone can elevate whole-body metabolic demand and acutely attenuate postprandial BG elevation. Inspiratory loading, therefore, represents a unique, accessible, and time-efficient stimulus capable of engaging both metabolic and mechanical pathways relevant to glycemic regulation across diverse populations.

Introduction

Postprandial blood glucose (BG) significantly impacts hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and appears to be a better predictor of cardiovascular disease than fasting BG. As such, postprandial glycemic control is a forefront strategy for preventing type 2 diabetes and its complications1–3. Conventional whole-body exercise has been strongly suggested for glycemic control, specifically for postprandial BG control, as it can acutely increase glucose uptake by skeletal muscles via both insulin-dependent and independent ways4. However, adherence to structured exercise remains a persistent challenge in real-world settings, especially among older adults, due to a lack of social or clinical support, limited access to facilities, time constraints, poor physical health (e.g., severe obesity or functional limitations), and conditions like quadriplegia or paraplegia, where adjunct therapies are especially important5–7. Therefore, identifying alternative, low-impact modalities capable of transiently stimulating metabolism and improving glycemic regulation holds relevance for both understanding physiological mechanisms and addressing clinical needs.

Inspiratory loading (IL), repeated breathing against inspiratory resistance, engages the diaphragm, intercostals, and ~ 18 accessory respiratory muscles that possess a high oxidative capacity and substantial energy requirements8,9. This loading paradigm is typically employed as the fundamental method of respiratory muscle training (RMT), where individuals perform repeated inspirations against pressure-threshold resistance to improve respiratory muscle strength and endurance over weeks of practice8.

RMT is easy-to-perform, portable, and low-impact, and has therefore been proposed as a feasible adjunct or alternative for individuals who are unable to participate in conventional exercise programs. However, the acute systemic effects of IL remain poorly understood. Although prior studies have suggested that acute IL may influence glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes10,11, none have characterized the concurrent metabolic and mechanical demands of the IL itself — including whole-body oxygen consumption (VO2) and energy expenditure — leaving unanswered the fundamental question of how much work the respiratory muscles actually performed and whether that effort is sufficient to alter BG levels.

Real-time assessment of metabolic and mechanical parameters during IL provides a unique opportunity to examine the physiological coupling between respiratory effort and systemic metabolism. In this study, we employed a custom-built IL system integrated with real-time mass spectrometry breath-by-breath gas-exchange analysis and inspiratory pressure (IP) monitoring to quantify VO2, heart rate (HR), and estimated work of breathing (eWOB) across graded inspiratory loads performed following glucose ingestion.

We hypothesized that IL would elicit measurable, intensity-dependent increases in metabolic activity and attenuate the postprandial rise in BG in healthy adults. By quantifying these acute responses, this study aims to establish a reproducible experimental framework for characterizing how respiratory effort modulates whole-body energy turnover and glycemic regulation.

Results

D-glucose intake significantly increased BG levels across all conditions compared to baseline (baseline vs. pre-IL/post-GLU; p < 0.05 for all). No between-condition differences were observed at pre-IL. Following IL, BG exhibited condition-dependent responses (Table 1; Fig. 1). During IL, VO2 increased relative to baseline and differed significantly among conditions with both MOD and HIGH higher than SHAM (7.68 ± 0.55 and 8.31 ± 0.93 vs. 5.60 ± 0.61 mL/kg/min; MOD vs. SHAM: p = 0.0003, g = 3.94; HIGH vs. SHAM: p = 0.0006, g = 3.34), but not significantly different from each other. HR also rose in a load-dependent manner (82.3 ± 15.7 bpm in MOD, 81.5 ± 13.1 bpm in HIGH, and 65.8 ± 11.7 bpm in SHAM; MOD vs. SHAM: p = 0.009, g = 1.87; HIGH vs. SHAM: p = 0.001, g = 3.25) and returned to baseline levels following loading.

IP and eWOB increased substantially during loaded conditions. During IL, IP reached 39.9 ± 17.3 cmH2O in MOD and 61.4 ± 17.5 cmH2O in HIGH, both significantly greater than SHAM (3.2 ± 3.5 cmH2O; MOD vs. SHAM: p = 0.02, g = 1.54; HIGH vs. SHAM: p = 0.003, g = 2.39), with HIGH higher than MOD (p = 0.0003, g = 3.88). Similarly, eWOB was greatest in HIGH (146.6 ± 55.3 J/min), followed by MOD (130.2 ± 62.0) and SHAM (3.2 ± 1.6; MOD vs. SHAM: p = 0.013, g = 1.70; HIGH vs. SHAM: p = 0.005, g = 2.15). Both IP and eWOB returned toward baseline following the cessation of loading (Fig. 2).

Across all conditions, the change in BG (ΔBG; post-IL – pre-IL) was inversely correlated with both metabolic and mechanical indices measured during IL.

ΔBG correlated negatively with ΔVO2 (r = −0.54, p = 0.022), and more strongly with ΔeWOB (r = −0.59, p = 0.011) and ΔIP (r = −0.59, p = 0.01).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that a 30-minute single session of IL attenuated postprandial increases in BG in healthy adults, with the most pronounced effects observed at higher loading intensity (~ 60% of MIP). Both VO2 and eWOB increased during IL, reflecting substantial metabolic and mechanical demands that coincided with a suppression of postprandial BG elevation. Correlation analyses indicated that changes in BG were inversely related to both VO2 and indices of inspiratory effort (IP and eWOB), suggesting that glycemic responses may be influenced by the combined metabolic and mechanical loading of the respiratory muscles.

Although the respiratory muscles represent a relatively small proportion of total skeletal muscle mass, their activation can induce measurable systemic metabolic effects. Prior studies have characterized the systemic consequences of respiratory muscle work: Aaron et al.12 quantified the oxygen cost of exercise hyperpnea under high-flow, low-resistance conditions, while Jones et al.13 demonstrated that ΔVO2 scales linearly with integrated inspiratory mouth pressure during resistive loading — a relationship our findings corroborate. Sheel et al.14,15 further established that fatiguing inspiratory muscle contractions elicit sympathetically mediated cardiovascular responses, consistent with the HR increase observed in the present study. Collectively, these prior studies support the principle that respiratory muscles behave as metabolically active skeletal muscle; the present study extends this framework by simultaneously linking quantified mechanical demand (IP, eWOB) and metabolic activation to a postprandial glycemic outcome. Sustained inspiratory efforts increase whole-body oxygen consumption and energy turnover, similar to moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, with postprandial BG reductions of similar magnitude to those reported following postprandial walking in healthy adults16,17. The magnitude of IP and eWOB observed in this study also exhibits resistance-type characteristics, supporting the concept that IL integrates both endurance-like and strength-like components. This hybrid nature may account for the concurrent elevation of VO2 and inspiratory work and underlies its potential to engage multiple physiological pathways that influence glucose utilization.

These findings extend prior work on the cardiorespiratory and autonomic effects of inspiratory loading. While previous studies have reported reductions in blood glucose following inspiratory muscle exercise10,11, these observations have been largely interpreted through autonomic regulation. Our results move beyond this framework by providing quantitative evidence of a direct systemic metabolic and glycemic effect. Demonstrating measurable BG modulation in metabolically healthy adults implies that such effect could be more pronounced in individuals with impaired glucose regulation. Considering that IL can be performed with minimal musculoskeletal stress, without requiring large muscle group activation, it may offer a practical and accessible adjunct to conventional limb-based exercise. Future investigations should examine the reproducibility of these acute effects in clinical populations (e.g., insulin resistance, obesity, or limited mobility) and assess the safety, adherence, and chronic adaptations associated with regular IL exercise. Incorporating eucapnic control will further help isolate the mechanisms underlying the observed glycemic Responses.

In conclusion, this study quantitatively characterized, for the first time, the simultaneous metabolic and mechanical responses to graded inspiratory loading using real-time measures of VO2, IP, and WOB. IL elicited distinct load-dependent increases in both metabolic demand and inspiratory effort, which were associated with reductions in postprandial BG levels. These results provide a physiological framework for understanding how respiratory muscle activation contributes to systemic metabolic regulation and support further investigation of IL as a feasible adjunct for improving glycemic control in at-risk populations.

Methods

Ethical approval and participants

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (protocol #23-010410; approved January 16th, 2024). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and all study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Six healthy adults (3 males and 3 females; age: 46.00 ± 14.83 years; body mass index [BMI]: 22.78 ± 2.46 kg/m2) were enrolled. None of the participants reported cardiovascular, pulmonary, metabolic, or neuromuscular disorders or were taking medications known to affect glucose metabolism or respiratory function.

Participants arrived at the laboratory after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and abstained from caffeine, alcohol, and vigorous physical activity for 24 h before each visit. Each participant completed all trials at the same time of day to control circadian variation.

Inspiratory loading (IL) system

The IL system consisted of validated, commercially available components integrated to deliver controlled inspiratory resistance during the IL sessions. This system incorporated a three-way valve (Hans Rudolph Inc., Shawnee, KS, USA) connected to the pneumotach (MGC Diagnostics, St. Paul, MN, USA) modified to embed both a pressure sensor and a gas sampling line within the flow head. This configuration allowed simultaneous, real-time measurement of inspiratory pressure (IP), airflow, and gas exchange through a single integrated unit (Fig. 3).

On the inspiratory limb of the valve, a precision-fabricated resistor plate was mounted to impose IL. Each plate contained a single circular aperture corresponding to 1% or 2% of the total surface area, thereby restricting airflow exclusively through the aperture. By interchanging resistor plates of different aperture ratios, inspiratory loads equivalent to approximately 40% and 60% of each participant’s maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) were achieved.

Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) measurement

MIP was measured using the same pneumotach used for IL, equipped with the embedded pressure sensor to ensure consistent calibration across experimental sessions. A disposable mouthpiece was attached to the pneumotach inlet, while the expiratory port on the opposite side was completely occluded to prevent airflow during the maneuver. Participants were instructed to exhale fully to residual volume and then perform a maximal inspiratory effort against the occluded airway for approximately 2–3 s. The peak negative pressure generated during the maneuver was recorded as MIP. Each participant performed three trials with at least 30 s of rest between attempts, and the mean of the three measurements was used for analysis.

Experimental design and procedures

Each participant completed three randomized crossover IL sessions: sham control (SHAM, no resistance), moderate (MOD, ~ 40% of MIP), and high (HIGH, ~ 60% of MIP). A minimum washout period of 72 h was provided between sessions to avoid carry-over effects (Fig. 4).

Upon arrival, participants were seated comfortably and fitted with a mouthpiece connected to the IL apparatus. Participants rested quietly for 10 min before baseline recordings. Baseline parameters included VO2, tidal volume (Vt), respiratory rate (RR), heart rate (HR), IP, estimated work of breathing (eWOB), and fasting BG by finger prick.

Following baseline measurements, participants consumed 45 g of D-glucose gel (Life Nutrition LLC, Palm Bay, FL, USA) followed by 250 mL of water. Fifteen minutes after ingestion, BG was measured to assess the postprandial response. Participants then performed a 30-min IL protocol consisting of six consecutive 4-min loading bouts separated by 1-min rest intervals.

During IL session, VO2, Vt, IP, RR, and HR were continuously recorded in real time at 1000 Hz via the PowerLab 16/30 data acquisition system (ML880, ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand) using breath-by-breath analysis. RR was guided by an auditory metronome and maintained at 30 breath/min. At 5 min following the final loading bout, BG was measured again (post-IL) to evaluate the acute postprandial response.

Calculation of estimated work of breathing (eWOB)

IP tracings were exported to quantify eWOB. where AUCIP represents the average IP AUC, Vt is the average tidal volume, and RR is the average respiratory rate.

Statistical analysis

A single value representing each IL session was derived for each participant and condition by averaging all breaths recorded across the six active 4-minute loading bouts. Baseline and post-IL values represent the mean of the final 5 min of the respective rest and recovery periods.

A two-way repeated-measured ANOVA (Condition × Time) was used to evaluate the effects of IL intensity (SHAM, MOD, HIGH) and timepoint on VO2, HR, IP, eWOB (baseline and IL) and BG (baseline, pre-IL, also defined as post-Glucose [GLU], post-IL). When significant main or interaction effects were detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed.

Within-condition comparisons across timepoints (baseline, pre-IL, post-IL) were analyzed for BG only using paired t-tests. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate association between changes in BG and changes in VO2 or eWOB. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to all parametric analyses. Given the small sample size, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was considered appropriate as it is robust to minor violations of normality in within-subject designs. Effect sizes were calculated as Hedges’ g based on paired differences. A statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed in SPSS Statistics, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).
