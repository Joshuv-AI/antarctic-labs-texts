Pharmacists may facilitate opioid tapering before total hip or knee arthroplasty
Authors: Jason W. Busse, Loren Regier, Aaron Gazendam
Published: 2026

Opioids are commonly prescribed for chronic noncancer pain, with an estimated 27% of patients receiving these medications (1). However, increasing awareness of their limited benefits (which may further attenuate over time) (2), and of associated harms (3), has resulted in efforts to reduce their use. These efforts have extended to patients undergoing hip or knee arthroplasty, as observational studies have found preoperative use of opioids is associated with worse postoperative pain and function, increased risk of surgical site infection, and higher readmission rates (4).

Efforts to taper opioids, however, require careful consideration. In 2016, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention released a Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain (5). They noted an association between higher doses of opioids and increased risk of nonfatal and fatal overdose and made a strong recommendation to avoid increasing the dosage of opioids to 90 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) or more per day for patients with chronic noncancer pain. However, they did not specify if this recommendation applied to new or legacy patients (6). Several policymakers and regulatory colleges enforced this recommendation, leading to involuntary tapering among patients prescribed higher-dose opioids for chronic pain (6-9). Restriction of prescription opioids may result in unmanaged pain or withdrawal symptoms, which some patients may mitigate by seeking illicit opioids (10). Although intended to reduce harms, aggressive tapering has been associated with increased overdose, death, and mental health crises (11).

Conversely, randomized trials of voluntary opioid-tapering have reported significant dose reduction without increased pain (12), although patients with chronic pain may remain reluctant to pursue tapering. For example, Sandhu et al. were required to screen 21,353 adults prescribed strong opioids for chronic noncancer pain to identify 608 who were willing to enter a randomized trial of education and support for opioid tapering vs. usual care (13). While the trial demonstrated effectiveness, with 29% (65 of 225) of intervention patients discontinuing opioids versus 7% (15 of 208) of patients receiving usual care (absolute difference, 21.7%, 95% CI: 14.8% to 28.6%), the authors acknowledged: “individuals in this trial volunteered to participate and, therefore, were likely more committed to reduce use of opioid medications than people who did not participate”. A critical question is how best to engage patients in a trial of opioid tapering.

The qualitative study by Young and colleagues (14) is an important contribution to this evidence gap. Embedded as part of their pilot trial (15) exploring pharmacist-partnered opioid tapering vs. usual care prior to total hip or knee arthroplasty, they invited 35 patients allocated to tapering to share their experiences. Twenty patients participated and analysis of their consultation transcripts revealed four major themes. First, motivations to taper; knowing their surgery date, a desire for improved quality of life, and interest in avoiding side effects were motivating factors to engage in a trial of opioid tapering. Given that the primary goal of joint replacement surgery is pain relief, it is reasonable to explore if patients may be motivated to reduce—or even discontinue—opioid use in the period leading up to surgery.

The second theme was knowledge and beliefs about opioids and tapering. Providing patients with education on the benefits and harms of opioids for chronic pain may prove motivating for tapering. Patients often overestimate the benefits of opioids, and underestimate harms (16). Providing information on opioid tapering, including a tailored tapering plan set jointly with patients and endorsed by their prescriber, is important. The pilot trial engaged patients in voluntary tapering only, set a schedule for dose reduction with patients, discussed non-opioid analgesia, and were clear that patients could discontinue tapering at any time or return to a previous dose if a small reduction in opioids was not tolerated. This approach is in keeping with recommended best practices (17).

Regarding what information may be helpful to patients: current evidence on benefits and harms of opioid therapy for chronic pain, as well as the potential benefits of dose reduction or discontinuation. For example, long-term opioid therapy (versus placebo) will not prove helpful for most patients living with chronic pain. Opioids provide important benefits in pain relief to 1 in 8 patients, important improvement in physical functioning to 1 in 12, and improved sleep quality to 1 in 17; there is no benefit to social, role, or emotional functioning (2). Further, effects on pain relief probably attenuate over time as tolerance develops (18). Harms include vomiting in 1 in 14 patients (2). Fatal overdose occurs in 1 in 1,000 patients, and non-fatal overdose in 1 in 500 patients, with risk increasing at higher doses (3). Reducing opioid dose may reduce the risk of fatal and non-fatal overdose, but may also result in decreased function, increased pain, and symptoms of withdrawal (19)—which should be minimized with a gradual tapering schedule. Patient decision aids that graphically provide absolute risks and harms of opioid therapy for chronic pain are likely to be helpful for patients and can be found at https://app.magicapp.org/#/guideline/8nyb0E.

The third theme was psychosocial context, highlighting that patients come from diverse settings and experiences which influence their views towards opioid tapering. Eliciting these issues from patients (e.g., spouses with a history of opioid use disorder, family members who are supportive of non-opioid analgesia) can help identify barriers and facilitators to opioid tapering. Healthy People 2030 defines social determinants of health as including five domains: (I) economic stability, (II) education access and quality, (III) healthcare access and quality, (IV) neighborhood and built environment, and (V) social and community context (20). These factors can affect the ability of individuals to engage in opioid tapering and a pharmacist-led tapering program has the potential to explore and address these potential barriers. For example, in the pilot trial by Liu and colleagues (15), access issues were mitigated by providing the pharmacist-partnered opioid tapering intervention remotely through telehealth.

The fourth theme was attitudes towards tapering, which were categorized as optimistic, hesitant, and resistant. While more receptive attitudes were associated with greater success with tapering, several hesitant and resistant patients were also able to reduce or even discontinue their opioids before surgery. Further, not all optimistic patients were able to reduce their use of opioids. Understanding the basis of patients’ attitudes towards opioid tapering can facilitate more individualized care. Some hesitant patients feared exacerbations in their pain, and resistant patients were prescribed such low doses of opioids (average of 10 MME/day) that some questioned the benefits of tapering.

The pilot trial demonstrated that a patient-centred, pharmacist-partnered approach to opioid tapering before total joint surgery is feasible and may show large effects. Twenty-seven of 30 patients (90%) allocated to pharmacist-partnered tapering versus 5 of 30 (17%) allocated to usual care successfully reduced their opioid use by ≥50% prior to surgery. Further, only 1% of screened patients (5 of 498) were excluded from the pilot trial because they did not wish to taper opioids, suggesting high receptivity among this population. A definitive trial is required to confirm these findings, and success in this study may not be generalizable to patients receiving higher opioid doses or with more complex substance use or psychiatric history.

The associated qualitative study provides important insights regarding how best to successfully engage total joint arthroplasty patients in an opioid tapering plan. Specifically, understanding their individual circumstances and establishing a date for surgery, providing clear evidence on the benefits and harms of opioid therapy, setting a tapering schedule in partnership with patients including the option to discontinue or return to a previous dose, and providing support including information on non-opioid analgesia. A limitation of the qualitative study was its restriction to analysing audio-recorded pharmacist counselling sessions. Future qualitative studies may benefit from directly interviewing patients who chose to taper/not taper and the pharmacists or prescribers they worked with.

Both the pilot trial and qualitative study reinforce the important role that pharmacists can play in working with patients to taper opioids prior to joint replacement surgery (15). Pharmacists were able to provide empathetic, personalized, and supportive care that helped build trust and engagement in the tapering process. The approach featured 5 basic components (21).

The intervention was delivered via video telehealth or telephone instead of in-person, which facilitates scalability and access to patients in remote or under-serviced areas. Remotely delivered counselling has been shown to be as effective as in-person delivery (22). The initial session took place 3 months before surgery and resulted in an individualized pain management and opioid tapering plan. The rate of tapering was based on duration of opioid use. For example, the opioid dose was adjusted weekly for patients prescribed opioids for < 3 months, and monthly for those with longer durations of use. The magnitude of each opioid dose reduction was 10–25% of the patient’s most recent daily opioid dose, and all plans were reviewed and approved by a pain medicine specialist.

The five components of the tapering process were administered through a motivational interviewing approach, which applies three communication skills—listening, asking, and informing (23). The process allows knowledge to take a temporary back seat to exploring the patient’s circumstances, attitudes, and concerns. This allows opioid tapering plans to be tailored to the individual, respects patient autonomy, and promotes shared decision-making, ensuring patients feel empowered and well supported in their pain management journey. The literature supports that patients value the pharmacist’s role in managing common challenges with opioid use, opioid tapering, and pain management (23). Pharmacists in such roles are often more easily accessible than physicians and can help patients navigate difficult discussions with their prescriber. Pharmacists are also able, by virtue of such contact, to provide emotional and troubleshooting support during the tapering process. When such service occurs within a multidisciplinary team setting, there is potential for an increase in trust and reassurance that patients are participants in, rather than recipients of, their care plan. Furthermore, clinicians typically value the potential for greater patient safety, alertness for signs of a substance use disorder, and reduced clinical burden (24,25).

Although not all patients may wish to taper opioids prior to surgery, or be able to do so, there is evidence that opportunities exist. A survey of 200 total joint arthroplasty patients found that 50% were receptive to reduced opioid use postoperatively (26). Receptivity was strongly associated with the belief that opioids cause more harms than alternatives, and that non-opioid analgesia is as effective as, or more effective than opioids. A recent randomized trial provides these assurances. Two hundred orthopaedic surgery patients were randomized to usual care (oxycodone, codeine, or hydromorphone) vs. an opioid-sparing protocol (naproxen, acetaminophen, and pantoprazole, with hydromorphone as rescue medication). Patients allocated to opioid-sparing consumed an average of 64 MME less at 6 weeks post-surgery. Further, there was no difference in pain scores or patient satisfaction between groups, and patients allocated to opioid-sparing were 35% less likely to report adverse effects (27).

Reduced use of opioids is a surrogate for outcomes of direct importance to patients, and Young et al. call for research to establish if pharmacist-partnered opioid tapering before surgery leads to improved patient-important outcomes (14). This is critical, as risk factors for post-surgical outcomes may be interrelated. For example, patients prescribed opioids before surgery are more likely to meet criteria for anxiety and depression (28)—both of which are associated with worse post-surgical outcomes (29). Retrospective observational studies have found that reducing opioid use before surgery is associated with lower risk of infection, shorter length of hospital stay, fewer readmissions, and less revision surgery for total hip and knee arthroplasty (30-32). However, rigorously conducted randomized trials are needed to confirm these findings.
