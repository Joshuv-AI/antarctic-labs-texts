Geodynamics of a global plate reorganization from topological data analysis
Authors: Alexandre Janin, Nicolas Coltice, Nicolas Chamot-Rooke, Julien Tierny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Earth’s lithosphere is fragmented into tectonic plates that move relative to each other and the mantle, following a dynamic equilibrium that evolves over time. Sporadically, abrupt kinematic changes occur that typically lead to regional-scale plate reconfigurations, and hypothetically to global-scale events, affecting the motion of all major plates. Identifying and understanding such global plate reorganizations has proven challenging because of the geometric nature of plate tectonic theory and the scarcity of preserved kinematic changes. Here we combine Earth-like global mantle convection with topological data analysis to show that global plate reorganization events can emerge from mantle–lithosphere interactions. Unlike regional changes, during these rare global events, the entire plate network shifts to a new dynamic equilibrium by the formation and destruction of plate boundaries at global scale, affecting the dynamics of the entire mantle. Such events represent transitions between two stable states in the coupled mantle–plates system. Our findings suggest that Earth may have experienced a similar global tectonic reorganization during the mid-Eocene around 47 million years ago. Our new approach provides a quantitative framework to unveil mantle–lithosphere interactions from global geodynamic models.

Main

Plate tectonics successfully describes the instantaneous motion of lithospheric plates, as demonstrated by global kinematic models1–3. However, plate motion is not constant, and sporadically, tectonic plates experience abrupt changes in the norm and/or direction of their motion4,5. These abrupt kinematic changes, or plate reorganization events, are an important feature of Earth’s plate motion6,7, well recorded in the oceanic lithosphere8–11. Although generally limited to one plate and its immediate surrounding, the observation of strong temporal concordances of regional kinematic changes involving together a large fraction of the plate network, one in the mid-Cretaceous12–14 and the other in the mid-Eocene15–19, has led to the hypothesis that plate reorganizations occasionally occur on a global scale, affecting the motion of major lithospheric plates.

How can global reorganizations be identified unequivocally? What is the chronology of events through a reorganization? How long do they take? What forces drive them? Answering such key questions has so far been limited by our approach and the observations available. Indeed, the study of plate reorganizations is impeded by the non-dynamical but geometrical nature of plate tectonic theory, the rarity of kinematic changes preserved in the geological record and the difficulty of identifying them. To overcome these hurdles, we combine a new methodology coupling topological data analysis20–22 and geodynamics with a fully dynamical state-of-the-art mantle convection model computed by ref. 23 that self-consistently generates Earth major tectonic features. This approach allows us to detect automatically and time-track plates emerging at the model surface. It offers an alternative to plate reconstruction models in the study of plate kinematic changes, avoiding the limitations linked to the sparsity of observations. It further allows us to explore the dynamic links between the lithosphere and the mantle during reorganizations.

The model analysed here is the only 3D spherical mantle convection calculation published yet that self-consistently generates the major tectonic features of Earth at Earth-like convective vigour23. It represents a unique high-resolution numerical solution providing 1,500 Myr of coupled evolution between tectonics and mantle dynamics with an average surface horizontal resolution of 23 km and less than 4,000 years per time step. It successfully reproduces major features and complexity of the present-day Earth tectonics such as supercontinent cycles (including extro and introversion), one-sided subductions, slab roll-back and advance, slab stagnation around the 660-km boundary, back-arc extension, concurrent rifting and collision of continents, interactions of plumes with mid-ocean ridges and slabs, diffuse and sharp plate boundaries23 (Fig. 1, Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2 and Methods). Performing a plate tectonic analysis automatically on such a model is a topological and computing challenge. We develop here a new methodology (Methods) to automatically generate a plate tessellation every 2 Myr using the surface velocity field and a topological and kinematic analysis. Each plate is then a rigid polygon surrounded by a high-velocity gradient and is tracked over time assuming independent time steps. This new methodology enables us to document the kinematic history of all detected tectonic plates over 474 Myr and follow their fragmentation and fusion using a global evolution graph comprising 30,000 vertices (approximately 126 plates detected per time step). Kinematic change on a global scale

Plates detected at the surface of the model show first-order similarities with Earth tectonics in size, shape and motion (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2). Figure 1 tracks the motion of larger plates during two selected time windows using synthetic hotspot tracks, that is, tracks of Lagrangian particles generated every 2 Myr at the same location in the mantle and advected with plate motion. Similar to Earth, the motion of model’s plates is quasi-stationary over periods that can last several tens of millions of years (Fig. 1), consistent with our understanding of long-lived plate forces24,25, separated by abrupt transitions that purely convective mechanisms cannot explain alone and during which the norm and/or the direction of the plate motion changes8–11. A typical example of an abrupt event is the sudden fragmentation at time 252 Myr of the plate bearing synthetic hotspot tracks B and C responsible for a local kinematic change that affects both the azimuth and the norm of the regional velocity field (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 3). The reorganization is brief, less than 2 Myr, and the motion of the other plates remains unaffected. This type of regional plate reorganization event (RPRE) is analogous to documented Earth’s kinematic changes8–11 and appears to occur commonly at the surface of the model and the surface of Earth (Fig. 1).

In contrast to these regional events, one major global event occurs at 136 Myr (Fig. 1b) involving changes in the motion of all the major plates between 124 and 150 Myr. The detection of such a global plate reorganization event (GPRE) shows that global kinematic changes can arise self-consistently from the dynamics of the coupled mantle–lithosphere system, suggesting that GPREs are likely to occur on Earth, as hypothesized from plate reconstruction models15,16,18. On the basis of our plate motion analysis, we identified only one GPRE over the 474 Myr period, suggesting that GPREs, unlike RPREs, are rare. This is consistent with the existence of only two potential candidates for GPREs since the breakup of Pangaea12,15,18. The time series of surface motions show that unlike short-lived RPREs, the total duration of the GPRE, defined as the instability period in the velocity of plates, lasts ~ 20−25 Myr (Fig. 1b). Nevertheless, the motion of the major plates responds synchronously (within 2 Myr) to a peak event at 136 Myr (Fig. 1b), suggesting that the GPRE represents a transition phase between two tectonic stable states culminating in a sharp reorganization event. Thus, the apparent temporal extent proposed for the mid-Cretaceous (~111−100 million years ago (Ma) (refs. 12,13)) and mid-Eocene (~53−40 Ma (refs. 17,18), with a potential main event at 47 Ma (ref. 26)) GPREs might relate to the processes leading to a GPRE rather than being uncertainties in the timing of its surface expression.

Reorganizations of the plate network

In the convection model, the GPRE identified from plate kinematics corresponds to a local maximum in the number of plates (Fig. 2a). Its onset is marked by a rapid increase in the length of boundaries, especially converging ones (Fig. 2b), whereas its end coincides with a decrease in the length of the boundaries (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 4a). The abrupt decrease in the surface of deformed areas (Fig. 2a) highlights a regularization of the localization of the deformation into narrower and more stable plate boundaries. No such change in the number of plates nor the length of the boundaries was observed for the 252 Myr RPRE (Fig. 2a,b). This observation suggests that GPREs are linked to a reorganization of the plate network structure by the formation (plate fragmentation) and the destruction (plate coalescence) of plate boundaries. However, global parameters such as plate number or total length of the boundaries may be biased by intense local readjustments, questioning their interpretation at a global scale. To investigate more deeply the evolution of the plate network through the formation and destruction of plate boundaries in space and time, we computed a new type of graph representing the lineage of the tectonic plates in the convection model (Fig. 3, Methods and Extended Data Figs. 5–9). This representation shows that the plate network can be described between times t and t + τ as a set of plate groups (Methods), each group being surrounded by stable continuous plate boundaries. On the temporal evolution graph of the plate network (Fig. 3), these groups take the form of spindles in which plates exchange fragments only with other plates within the group through fragmentation and coalescence. For example, between 220−278 Myr, the two plates that originally carried the synthetic hotspot tracks F and G (Fig. 3a) are part of the same group and ultimately merge to form a larger plate. On the temporal plate graph, the RPRE at 252 Myr shows relative stability of the global plate network, the main event being the destabilization of the plate group containing the fragmenting plate (Fig. 3a). On the opposite, the GPRE is characterized by interactions between most plate groups, forming a knot in the graph (Fig. 3b) that coincides with the reorganization of the plate velocities (Fig. 1b). The network of plates becomes highly interconnected during this event with plates exchanging fragments through the creation and the destruction of plate boundaries, confirming here that the entire plate network reorganizes globally. Furthermore, our results show that the organization of the plate network during a GPRE is a transient state that leads to a new stable state in response to a change in the force balance at a global scale.

Mantle flow reorganizations

Quantifying the dynamic interactions between Earth’s mantle and lithosphere is challenging due to our poor knowledge of the history of Earth’s mantle27,28. Our convection model, with its self-organization of mantle flow and plate tectonics, provides a unique window on mantle–lithosphere interactions.

During the detected GPRE, the mean velocity norm at various depths mirrors the first-order evolution of plate velocities (Fig. 2e). Globally, the onset of the GPRE is marked by a velocity increase, peaking at 136 Myr, followed by a sharp decline (~20% at the surface; Fig. 2e). The average second invariant of the strain rate evolves similarly (Extended Data Fig. 4b), indicating mantle deformation and flow reconfiguration. This mantle dynamic reconfiguration during the GPRE coincides with a 40% reduction in net lithosphere rotation after its peak during the main GPRE event (Fig. 2d). Thus, the global reorganization of the plate network during the GPRE coincides with a major reorganization of mantle dynamics from the surface to its base. Conversely, the RPRE at 252 Myr does not correlate with global velocity change, highlighting that the surface extent of a GPRE reflects its depth extent.

The GPRE is followed by a 50% increase in the number of plumes at 150 Myr (Fig. 2c). We interpret this plume pulse as the result of an increase in vigour of the return flow triggered by a slab superflux during the GPRE, visible in the increase of the length of convergent plate boundaries (Fig. 2b), as proposed by ref. 29 for the mid-Cretaceous plume pulse 130 Ma. The delay of ~14 Myr between the main GPRE event and the onset of the plume pulse is consistent with the ascent of hot buoyant material from the base of the mantle at an average velocity of ~21 cm yr−1, similar to Earth estimates between 23 and 54 cm yr−1 (refs. 30,31).

The synchronous response over the whole mantle through a GPRE provides new arguments to re-evaluate the nature of the mid-Eocene event around 47 Ma. The change in the motion of the Pacific plumes relative to each other32–35 and to the Indo–Atlantic plumes36–38 has been interpreted as a main argument against a global plate motion change during the mid-Eocene. Indeed, the observed kinks in the Pacific hotspot tracks do not reflect only the motion of the Pacific plate relative to a fixed mantle but also the motion of plumes themselves relative to the mantle32–35. Furthermore, absolute plate motion (APM) models based solely on the Indo–Atlantic plumes do not predict any notable change in plate motion around 47 Ma when reprojected on the Pacific37,39. However, these Indo–Atlantic APM models accurately predict the Pacific hotspot tracks within the uncertainty ellipses from the present to approximately 47 Ma, but begin to deviate substantially from the actual Pacific hotspot tracks around 47 Ma. Besides, our analysis of the GPRE in the convection model shows that the mantle responds synchronously and globally to a GPRE. Thus, in contrast to previous studies37, during a GPRE we expect a radical change in the predictions of hotspot tracks from APM models derived from distinct hotspot populations (the Indo–Atlantic and Pacific hotspots being above two different mantle domains40,41). We propose that the reorganization of plate motion during the mid-Eocene was accompanied by a reorganization of mantle flow, explaining the loss of coherence between Pacific and Indo–Atlantic APM models around chron 21 and the synchronous motion change for individual plumes such as Hawaii–Emperor.

Origins of global kinematic changes

The timescales for both regional and global events are too short to correspond to a convective overturn42. However, lithospheric-scale events capable of triggering strain localization processes such as slab detachment, subduction initiation or cessation or plate fragmentation, are considered as potential mechanisms for Earth RPREs6,10,43–46, similar, for instance, to the model’s RPRE at 252 Myr (Fig. 1), following a plate fragmentation (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Fig. 3). On the other hand, the mechanisms capable of triggering a global plate motion change remain unclear. A drastic change in plate boundary forces has been a favoured scenario16,47 but remains speculative in the absence of observations or modelling results on a global scale18,42,47,48.

The time series (Fig. 2) and the temporal plate graph (Fig. 3) show that the GPRE in the model is linked to the creation and destruction of plate boundaries, especially convergent plate boundaries. Snapshots of the model over the GPRE (Fig. 4) show that it coincides with the development of a hemispheric-scale convergent plate boundary separating the main continental fragments (between 50 and 120 °E; Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 3). This plate boundary evolves in just ~25 Myr from a series of individual and isolated local subduction zones to a long and semi-continuous convergence zone. This reconfiguration leads to a change in the forces acting on adjacent plates, as illustrated by the rapid rotation of the velocity vectors, accompanied by local subduction initiations/propagations and cessations (Fig. 4). Similarly, an increase in subduction zone initiation is suspected on Earth around the mid-Eocene event45,46, particularly in the Pacific Ocean, indicating changes in the balance of plate forces, with subduction initiations possibly both driving and resulting from the plate reorganization. We do not observe a spatial propagation of the reorganization during the GPRE identified in the model. We propose here that rapid changes in plate boundary forces at this long convergent boundary are then transmitted to the entire plate network through both the rigid lithosphere and the underlying mantle. The observation of synchronous reorganization of mantle flow and surface motions indicates that the change in mantle dynamics is not purely passive but has accompanied the change in plate motion and plays a role in up-scaling the kinematic change from local to global. This role of the mantle in the development of the GPRE coupled with forces transmitted through the rigid lithosphere, provides a possible mechanical explanation for the link between the India–Eurasia collision and the Tonga–Kermadec and Izu–Bonin–Mariana subduction initiations in the Western Pacific Ocean just before 47 Ma (ref. 26), suggesting with the arguments we presented on the APM models that the mid-Eocene event is likely to be a GPRE.

Our results show that the temporal evolution of plate motion follows a dynamic equilibrium punctuated by phases of rapid transitions. Plates respond to a set of forces acting on them. In most cases, far-field forces are not able to destabilize plates or their boundaries, and plate motion remains constant for long periods. Sporadically, plates reorganize regionally due to local changes in plate boundary forces. Only during rare events, the GPREs, the entire Earth’s plate network reorganizes globally together with mantle flow. These events represent a transient phase of the plate motion and plate network evolution between two more stable states. A drastic change in boundary forces at hemispheric- or global-scale convergent plate boundary is likely to trigger a GPRE with forces instantaneously (<2 Myr) transmitted through both the lithosphere and the mantle to the entire mantle–lithosphere system.

Methods

Mantle convection modelling

In this study, we analyse the three-dimensional, spherical mantle convection model developed by ref. 23 to investigate the dynamic interactions between Earth’s tectonic plates and the underlying mantle during plate reorganizations. This model is uniquely designed to push the limits of self-consistent plate generation by incorporating Earth-like convective vigour, a pseudo-plastic rheology and deformable continental rafts. It further accounts for extreme viscosity gradients—necessitating high-resolution simulations (average surface horizontal resolution of 23 km and less than 4,000 years per time step) and a weak, lubricating upper oceanic crust, both features being central in generating one-sided subduction (Extended Data Fig. 1). The deep structure of the modelled mantle includes a viscosity jump at 660 km by a factor of 30, as suggested by geoid50 and post-glacial rebound studies51. The combined effect of this feature and the strong temperature dependence generate self-consistently a low viscosity asthenosphere. In the model, both deep dense material at the core–mantle boundary and a decrease of the thermal expansivity structures the plume network and dynamics49.

Plate rigidity criterion

Plate tessellation requires an objective and quantitative diagnostic to assess the rigidity of a piece of the sphere and determine whether it can be interpreted as a tectonic plate.

Plate tectonics provides a framework for the study of plate dynamics and theorizes that the motion of each plate consists of a single rigid-body rotation. We solve a linear inverse problem to find the optimal Euler pole of all the plates of a given tessellation and the associated misfit. Tessellation

Whether for the Earth or a 3D mantle convection model, the plate tessellation method we propose in this study follows the same procedure in two main steps: (1) the automatic detection of polygon (potentially plate) boundaries based on the segmentation of the norm of the surface velocity gradient ∥∇vs∥ using topological data analysis methods and (2) the adaptive selection of polygon boundaries to maximize the rigidity of the detected polygons and the uniqueness of their solid body rotation.

The automatic detection of polygon boundaries (step 1) is based on the computation of the Morse complex57,58 of ∥∇vs∥ after its topological simplification59,60, according to a minimum persistence threshold pmin on its pairs of critical points. In this framework, the polygon boundaries are the integral lines linking local maxima with saddle points of ∥∇vs∥ (ascending Morse complexes), that is, the crest lines of ∥∇vs∥ (Extended Data Fig. 6). The parameter pmin conditions the detection limit of the diffuse plate boundaries and acts as a noise-reduction parameter. The higher pmin, the higher the persistence of critical point pairs must be to be considered as belonging to a polygon boundary. As a result, the higher pmin is, the fewer polygons there are (Extended Data Fig. 7).

The aim of the step 1 of our plate tessellation process is to compute the segmentation of a given surface velocity field for N different values of pmin (from pmin,i=0 to pmin,i=N, the minimum and the maximum values considered, respectively) whose effect on the surface segmentation ranges from over- to under-tessellation (Extended Data Fig. 7). In this study, we choose N = 24 to fine tesselate the intermediate states (Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8b).

Step 2 consists in combining segmentations with different values of topological persistence to obtain a tessellation where pmin varies laterally. The resulting tessellation map (Extended Data Fig. 8a) corresponds to a set of polygons whose geometry is defined at the highest value of pmin for which they can be defined as rigid. This step ensures that the smallest number of rigid polygons bounded by velocity gradients is obtained, thus constraining the uniqueness of their solid body rotation. The values of pmin,i=0 and pmin,i=N are chosen to minimize the area of polygons defined at these values on the final tessellation maps (located in the distribution queue; Extended Data Fig. 8b).

Distinction between plates and deformable blocks

The result of the tessellation is a set of polygons mapping the surface of the sphere. We make a clear distinction here between plates (rigid entities) and blocks (deformable) by analogy with Earth, based on a threshold of strain rate within polygons. A polygon is considered deformable (orange in Fig. 1 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 5) if more than 90% of its surface has a strain rate greater than 5 × 10−9 yr−1, a threshold corresponding to a low strain rate limit for Earth diffuse plate boundaries in GSRM v.2.153. We use the same threshold to compute the deformed surface area (Fig. 2a) from the second invariant of the strain rate at the surface of the model.

Time tracking

The time tracking of polygons is a separate step from tessellation. The time tracking is computed once all the tessellation maps have been calculated for all the time steps of interest. We track polygons over time by tracking their centroids defined as their centres of figure embedded in their surface. The Extended Data Fig. 9 illustrates our workflow. At each time step, we derive a persistence diagram pairing for each polygon its centroid (maxima) with the point belonging to its edge that is furthest from the polygon’s centroid. The persistence of the pair is defined as the haversine distance between the two points of the pair. Centroids are tracked using a Lifted Wasserstein Matcher64 (filter ‘TTKTrackingFromPersistenceDiagrams’ in TTK).

Plate groups

We define here the set of plate groups between the times t and t + τ as the set of induced subgraphs formed from a larger plate graph G(V, E) keeping only the vertices (that is, plates) between the time t and t + τ. A plate group is a set of vertices of G(V, E) that are connected by edges between the time t and t + τ.
