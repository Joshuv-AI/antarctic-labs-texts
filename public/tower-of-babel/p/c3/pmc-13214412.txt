The clinical application of laparoscopic-assisted suture hook technique in the repair of Morgagni hernia
Authors: Jishen Zhang, Defeng Jin, Xianqi Cai, Zhaoqing Cui, Yutao Wei
Published: 2026

Introduction

Morgagni hernia, also known as parasternal hernia, was first described by the Italian anatomist and pathologist Giovanni Morgagni in 1769 during his autopsy observations [1]. The incidence of Morgagni hernia is approximately 0.02%–0.05%, accounting for about 3%–4% of all congenital diaphragmatic hernias [2]. The herniated contents are typically the omentum and the transverse colon, while the stomach and small intestine are less commonly involved. Common symptoms of Morgagni hernia in adults include shortness of breath following activity, upper abdominal distension or pain, and nausea or vomiting. In children, symptoms may present as respiratory distress and recurrent chest infections; however, some patients may be asymptomatic. Currently, clinical diagnosis typically utilizes chest X-rays, CT scans, and MRI, which have significant advantages in determining the location and size of the diaphragmatic defect and the characteristics of the herniated contents [3, 4]. Once a diagnosis is confirmed, surgical treatment should always be recommended. Therefore, this paper aims to report our surgical experiences in the management of Morgagni hernia.

Surgical methods

After successful general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position on the operating table, and the surgical field was routinely disinfected with sterile drapes applied. The primary surgeon stood on the left side of the patient, while the assistant stood on the right. A 1.5 cm incision was made above or below the umbilicus to establish pneumoperitoneum, and a laparoscope was inserted for exploration (Fig. 3). Subsequently, a 1 cm incision was made at the junction of the left mid-clavicular line and the umbilicus for the main operating port, and another 1 cm incision was made at the junction of the right mid-clavicular line and the umbilicus for the auxiliary operating port. Using an ultrasonic scalpel, electric hook and grasping forceps, the adhesions surrounding the hernia sac were dissected. The hernia contents were reduced back into the abdominal cavity with grasping forceps while preserving the hernia sac (Fig. 4). During reduction, careful muscle relaxation and gentle manipulation were emphasized to avoid excessive traction. Under laparoscopic vision, a 2 − 0 pledgeted non-absorbable suture was used to perform intermittent intra-corporeal suturing of the Morgagni hernia defect with a needle holder (Fig. 5). It is important to ensure that the suturing is performed at the robust margins of the hernial ring and that pledgets are used to prevent suture cutting. The primary surgeon manipulated the hook-needle with the right hand (Fig. 6). Through a single subxiphoid incision, the hook-needle was inserted in two divergent directions. Under laparoscopic visualization, the internal grasping forceps of the hook-needle was gradually extended to sequentially retrieve both ends of the prosthetic suture through the abdominal wall (Fig. 7). The distance between the two insertion points was kept sufficiently wide to prevent suture cutting through the tissue after knotting. The prosthetic suture was then secured with an extracorporeal hand-knot and anchored to the subcutaneous tissue of the abdominal wall, achieving in-situ closure of the hernia defect (Fig. 8). Finally, thorough hemostasis was achieved, and the number of gauze and instruments was verified before closing the abdomen. Results

Basic characteristics

This study analyzed the clinical data of 5 adult patients who underwent surgical repair of Morgagni hernia at our hospital (Table 1). The majority of the patients were male (60%), and all cases occurred on the right side (100%). The vast majority of patients were overweight or obese (80%), with a mean BMI of 26.03. All patients presented with symptoms (100%), the most common of which were chest pain and upper abdominal pain. The mean age of the study population was 59.4 years.

Surgical and intraoperative variables

All patients underwent laparoscopic-assisted repair of Morgagni hernia using the hook method. No patients required conversion to open surgery during the procedure. Laparoscopic exploration revealed that the omentum was the most common hernia content (100%), followed by the transverse colon (60%). The mean operative time was 57.6 min. The average intraoperative blood loss was 8 ml. No drainage tubes were placed in any patients during the procedure (100%). All patients retained the hernia sac during surgery (100%).

Postoperative variables and follow-up

All patients recovered uneventfully, with a mean postoperative hospital stay of 2.8 days. All patients achieved symptom relief during a mean follow-up period of 10 months after discharge. Regular follow-up CT imaging demonstrated favorable surgical repair outcomes of Morgagni hernia, with no evidence of recurrence reported in any CT findings (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Morgagni hernia is a relatively rare type of congenital diaphragmatic hernia, resulting from a potential defect at the junction of two muscle groups that form the diaphragm, located at the sternal xiphoid and the inner aspects of the 7th to 12th ribs, known as the Morgagni foramen. This condition is a congenital disease caused by embryonic developmental defects. Most patients with Morgagni hernia are diagnosed during childhood or adolescence, although a small number of cases are diagnosed in adulthood [5]. It has been reported that 70% of patients with Morgagni hernia are female, and 92% of the hernias have a hernia sac. This condition typically occurs on the right side of the diaphragm, accounting for approximately 90% of cases, while 5% occur on the left side, as the defect on the left side is protected by the pericardium. The remaining 5% are bilateral cases [6–8]. Among the 5 patients included in this study, only 2 patients were female, and all Morgagni hernias occurred on the right side.

Currently, the treatment options for Morgagni hernia are quite limited, with surgical intervention being the only effective approach. Various surgical techniques have been reported both domestically and internationally for the treatment of Morgagni hernia. These include traditional open surgical methods as well as minimally invasive approaches such as laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery. For traditional open surgery, incisions can be made subcostally, through the midline, or para-median. In cases requiring thoracotomy, an anterolateral incision in the 4th or 5th intercostal space may be utilized, and if necessary, a right thoracotomy combined with an abdominal incision can be performed. The advantages of open surgery include easier anatomical dissection of adhesions, facilitating the reduction of hernia contents, and providing direct visualization of the functional status and vascular supply of the hernia contents [9–12]. However, with the continuous advancement and development of medical technology, minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques have gradually replaced traditional surgeries. These techniques offer several advantages, including minimal surgical trauma, rapid postoperative recovery, shorter hospital stays, and a lower incidence of postoperative complications [13]. Previous studies by Young et al. showed that among their 43 patients who underwent Morgagni hernia surgery, those who underwent laparoscopic surgery had the shortest hospital stay and a low recurrence rate [14]. The study by Katsaros I et al. also demonstrated that laparoscopic surgery can shorten the hospital stay compared to open surgery [15]. Our study of the 5 patients also reported similar results, with an average intraoperative blood loss of 8 ml. The recovery was rapid, with an average postoperative hospital stay of 2.8 days, and no complications or deaths were observed following the surgery.

With the increasing application of robotic surgery in clinical practice both domestically and internationally, some surgeons have begun employing this method to repair Morgagni hernias. The advantages of robotic surgery include the provision of a three-dimensional stereoscopic view and enhanced maneuverability due to the jointed instruments. Additionally, robotic surgery has shown similar outcomes in terms of postoperative hospital stay and recovery compared to laparoscopic procedures. Previously, Wei et al. reported that 4 patients with Morgagni hernia treated with robotic surgery had a significantly lower estimated average blood loss, an average operative time of 129.8 min, and an average hospital stay of 2 days [16]. Pulle MV et al. reported that the median operative time for the 7 patients was 140 min, with a median hospital stay of 3 days, and no complications or deaths were observed [17]. While robotic surgery can address some of the limitations of laparoscopic surgery, it is associated with longer operative times, higher procedure costs, and significant equipment maintenance expenses, which can impose a financial burden on both patients and hospitals. Therefore, minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques remain the most commonly selected surgical approach both domestically and internationally.

Currently, there is no clear consensus on the repair methods for Morgagni hernia both domestically and internationally. Previously, Horton JD et al. reported that among the 298 cases they collected from transabdominal surgeries, 88% underwent primary repair, while 6% opted for mesh reinforcement. In laparoscopic surgeries, the proportions of primary repair and mesh repair were 29% and 64%, respectively. No recurrence of Morgagni hernia was reported in either surgical approach during postoperative follow-up [12]. In a report by Kao AM et al. that among the 15 patients, 11 patients (73.3%) underwent mesh repair, while the remaining 4 patients (26.7%) received primary sutured repair, no recurrence of Morgagni hernia was observed in any of the patients during postoperative follow-up [18]. Therefore, mesh repair and primary repair are currently the two most commonly utilized methods by clinicians for the repair of Morgagni hernias [19, 20]. Since mesh implantation may increase the risk of immune rejection and postoperative complications, all 5 patients in our report underwent primary repair without mesh placement, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up. However, there is currently no definitive surgical approach for the primary repair of Morgagni hernias. Previously, Wei et al. reported on the treatment of 4 patients with Morgagni hernia, where they first performed primary repair followed by the placement of a mesh for reinforcement [16]. In a study by Zaharie F et al.that 5 patients with Morgagni hernia who underwent primary repair were treated using a Reverdin needle for interrupted extracorporeal subcutaneous suturing with extracorporeal manual knot tying. The median operative time for the 5 patients was 110 min, and the average blood loss was 30 milliliters [13]. We employed an intra-abdominal approach using a patch valve suture technique for the primary closure of the hernia, followed by an extracorporeal manual knot tying using a hook technique to secure the closure, there are currently no related studies reporting the use of laparoscopic hook technique for the treatment of Morgagni hernia. The average operative time for our 5 patients was 57.6 min, with an average intraoperative blood loss of 8 mL, and an average postoperative hospital stay of 2.8 days. No recurrences were observed during postoperative follow-up. Compared to other primary repair methods, we found that this technique resulted in a shorter operative time, less tissue trauma, was simple to learn, facilitated quicker recovery, and had a lower recurrence rate.

In addition to the lack of a clear consensus on the repair technique, there is significant debate regarding whether to excise the hernial sac in Morgagni hernias. Some experts believe that the removal of the hernial sac can effectively eliminate pathological tissue, reduce the risk of recurrence, and decrease the likelihood of postoperative fluid accumulation within the sac [21, 22]. Some experts also suggest that retaining the hernial sac may help avoid damage to the mediastinal structures and pericardium, thus reducing the risk of pneumothorax [23–25]. However, a recent study by Zaharie F et al. found that the decision to excise the hernial sac during the procedure had no significant impact on the occurrence of postoperative complications [13]. In our study, we retained the hernial sac in all 5 patients, and no recurrences were observed during postoperative follow-up. We believe that preserving the hernial sac can significantly shorten the operative time and reduce surgical trauma. This approach is especially preferable for elderly and high-risk patients, as we lean towards retaining the hernial sac in these cases.

Regarding the positioning of the surgeon and the assistant during surgery, we recommend that the primary surgeon stand on the patient’s left side and the assistant on the right. Since most clinicians are right-handed, standing on the patient’s left provides the surgeon with a wider and more comfortable operating space for thoracic or upper abdominal procedures, while the assistant on the right can more conveniently pass instruments and perform supporting tasks. If the primary surgeon is left-handed, the positions should be reversed. Given that Morgagni hernias are located posterior to the sternum and often adjacent to critical structures such as the heart and lungs, we do not recommend the surgeon stand between the patient’s legs for lateral repair, as this increases surgical difficulty and the risk of intraoperative bleeding.

Conclusion

In summary, laparoscopic-assisted hook suture repair of Morgagni hernia is a safe and feasible surgical approach. It offers advantages such as shorter operative time, less trauma, faster recovery, and a low recurrence rate. Additionally, the technique is straightforward and easy to learn, with minimal requirements for laparoscopic suturing and knot-tying skills. However, since Morgagni hernia is a relatively rare type of diaphragmatic hernia, the current sample size is limited and there is a lack of long-term follow-up data. Therefore, we recommend conducting multi-center, large-sample clinical studies with long-term follow-up to obtain more reliable and accurate conclusions.
