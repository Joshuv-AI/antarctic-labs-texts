Tricuspid Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair in Orthotopic Heart Transplantation Recipients
Authors: Chloe Kharsa, Isadora Sande Mathias, Mangesh Kritya, Gal Sella, Karl Abou Zeid, Joe Aoun, Arvin Bhimaraj, Ashrith Guha, Janardhana R. Gorthi, Nadeen N. Faza, Stephen H. Little, Marvin D. Atkins, Michael J. Reardon, Neal S. Kleiman, Sachin S. Goel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tricuspid regurgitation in orthotopic heart transplantation recipients is a common complication that has been associated with poor outcomes. Surgical intervention carries a high risk in this population given prior sternotomy, immunosuppression, and comorbidities. We report a case series of 4 orthotopic heart transplantation recipients with severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation who were treated successfully with tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. All patients demonstrated clinical and echocardiographic improvement postprocedure, with no procedural complications or rehospitalizations at short-term follow-up.

Functional tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is frequently encountered in patients after orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT), with an estimated incidence ranging from 19% to 84%.1,2 The underlying mechanisms typically involve annular dilation and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, which may be further aggravated by repeated endomyocardial biopsies.3 Although medical management remains the first-line treatment, it often fails to provide adequate symptom relief in cases of severe TR. Surgical intervention carries high risk in this population and is typically reserved for carefully selected patients. In this context, tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) is a less invasive alternative treatment option.4 In this case series, we present 4 OHT recipients who underwent successful T-TEER for severe symptomatic TR.

Case 1

A 71-year-old woman with a history of OHT 2 years earlier for nonischemic cardiomyopathy presented with progressive dyspnea (NYHA functional class III) and orthopnea despite optimized diuretic therapy. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe functional TR with annular dilation, mild mitral regurgitation, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (55%-59%), and normal RV systolic function. Given her prior sternotomy, chronic immunosuppression, and underlying chronic kidney disease, she was deemed at high risk for surgery. She underwent successful T-TEER with deployment of a single TriClip XTW device (Abbott) at the anteroseptal commissure. Postprocedural TTE demonstrated a reduction in TR severity from severe to mild. At the 2-month follow-up, she reported symptomatic improvement (NYHA functional class II), with no adverse events.

Case 2

A 66-year-old man with a history of heart transplantation 9 years earlier, prior surgical tricuspid valve repair with an annuloplasty band and neochords, end-stage renal disease on dialysis, and prior lung volume reduction surgery presented with progressive dyspnea and volume overload despite regular dialysis. Echocardiography showed moderate to severe TR, right atrial enlargement, and mildly to moderately reduced RV systolic function. Given his comorbidities, he was not a surgical candidate (Society of Thoracic Surgeons operative mortality risk: 4.81%). He underwent T-TEER with 2 TriClip XTW devices deployed in the anteroseptal position. Postprocedural imaging demonstrated a significant reduction in TR, with a 3-mm transvalvular gradient. He was discharged on day 4 and remained symptom free until re-presenting 10 days later with right groin pain, without evidence of vascular injury or hematoma.

Case 3

A 60-year-old man with a history of combined heart and liver transplantation in 2014 for dilated cardiomyopathy, complicated by the presence of donor-specific antibodies, presented with worsening ascites and dyspnea requiring multiple paracenteses. He was under evaluation by the liver transplant team for potential retransplantation in the context of ongoing alcohol use. TTE showed severe TR with a small filamentous mobile echodensity on the posterior leaflet, suggestive of flail. After multidisciplinary discussion, and given the high surgical risk, the patient underwent T-TEER with 1 TriClip XTW device targeting the septal-anterior leaflets. TR was reduced to moderate. A second clip was not implanted because of interaction with the first device and surrounding chordae. The patient remained clinically stable with no cardiac complications over 2 years of follow-up. After T-TEER, TR remained moderate for 2 years. Subsequent symptom recurrence prompted TTE, which demonstrated advanced RV dysfunction and progression to severe TR, followed by death within months.

Case 4

A 47-year-old man with a history of 3 OHTs (most recent in 2010) and kidney transplantation, now on hemodialysis because of graft failure, presented with exertional dyspnea and hepatic congestion. TTE showed severe TR with a flail leaflet, poor coaptation, and marked biatrial enlargement. Given his high surgical risk and multiple comorbidities, he underwent T-TEER with deployment of 2 clips at the anteroseptal commissure. Postprocedure, TR severity was reduced to mild, with a significant decrease in right atrial pressure on right heart catheterization. The patient remained stable with sustained symptomatic improvement at 2-year follow-up, with a sustained mild TR grade (Video 1).

Discussion

Our case series adds valuable insight to the literature supporting the feasibility, safety, and clinical utility of T-TEER in patients with a history of OHT. These patients, often excluded from major device trials, represent a particularly high-risk and understudied population. Despite their heterogeneous clinical backgrounds—including multiple prior heart and kidney transplants, hepatic dysfunction, and dependence on chronic dialysis—all 4 patients in our cohort underwent technically successful T-TEER procedures. Importantly, each patient demonstrated a marked reduction in TR severity, with associated sustained improvements in dyspnea, hepatic congestion, and overall functional capacity over long-term This clinical benefit underscores the therapeutic value of even modest reductions in TR in transplant recipients. Previous studies have shown that lessening TR severity can favorably alter RV preload, reduce right atrial pressure, and halt or reverse the cycle of right-sided heart failure.5 In a cohort where surgical reintervention is often contraindicated because of comorbidities and prior sternotomies, T-TEER emerges as a valuable tool, not merely for palliation, but for improving hemodynamics, quality of life, and potentially delaying progression toward organ dysfunction or need for retransplantation.

Decision-making in these cases is nuanced and inherently multidisciplinary. It requires the collaboration of transplant cardiologists familiar with the long-term trajectories and immunologic nuances of OHT patients, structural heart disease interventionalists with expertise in complex valve interventions, heart failure specialists attuned to volume management and symptom burden, and imaging cardiologists capable of navigating often distorted post-transplant anatomy. Notably, despite the potential challenges posed by the altered right atrial and tricuspid anatomy, periprocedural imaging was not particularly difficult in our series. TEE remained sufficient to guide the procedure in all cases, facilitated by the expertise of our dedicated interventional echocardiography team. In our experience, the T-TEER procedure provided a safe and effective alternative to repeat surgery, which was deemed prohibitively risky in all 4 cases. This supports the concept of structural interventions serving as a bridge to clinical stability and independence from recurrent hospitalization.

Despite growing enthusiasm for tricuspid transcatheter therapies, data on their use in transplant recipients remain extremely limited. Landmark trials such as TRILUMINATE (TriClip) and CLASP TR (Pascal, Edwards Lifesciences) have expanded our understanding of T-TEER in nontransplant patients with symptomatic TR, yet both excluded OHT recipients.6,7 As such, our findings help bridge a key knowledge gap by offering real-world procedural and clinical insights in this unique patient subset. Dedicated registries and prospective trials including transplant recipients are urgently needed to assess long-term durability, valve function, symptom progression, and survival benefit in this vulnerable population.

In addition, the long-term immunologic and infectious implications of intracardiac device implantation in immunocompromised patients necessitate ongoing evaluation. The risk of device-related endocarditis, leaflet fibrosis, and altered healing responses in patients on chronic immunosuppression remains poorly characterized. There may be unique host-graft-device interactions influencing outcomes that differ significantly from the general population. Conclusions

Our experience suggests that T-TEER represents a safe and effective therapeutic option for selected OHT recipients with severe TR and prohibitive surgical risk. As structural heart interventions evolve, integrating them thoughtfully into the post-transplant care paradigm will be essential. Long-term follow-up, careful patient selection, and inclusion of OHT patients in future prospective studies will be critical to refining indications and optimizing outcomes.
