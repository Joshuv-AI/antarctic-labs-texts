GWAS of Second‐Generation Antihistamine Use: Locus Specificity and Highlighted Signalling Pathways
Authors: M Niemi, S Ruotsalainen, E Saarentaus, I Vähätalo, F FinnGen, I Starskaia, T Harju, M Pelkonen, T Feuth, P Kauppi, J Partanen, R Lahesmaa, V Hytönen, A Palotie, H Kankaanranta, T Laitinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Four of 67 loci were specific to antihistamine use; most overlapped inflammatory disease susceptibility.Genetic variation enriched in IL‐2 cytokine, JAK–STAT pathway genes shared by allergy and autoimmunity.

To the Editor,

Histamine is a central mediator of inflammatory responses released predominantly from mast cells and basophils following IgE‐dependent and independent activation [1]. Second‐generation H1‐antagonists (sgAHs) are first‐line therapy for allergic rhino‐conjunctivitis and urticaria and are widely used in asthma, atopic dermatitis and chronic rhinosinusitis [2]. Although sgAHs differ structurally, they share selective antagonism of the H1 receptor and a favourable safety profile compared with first‐generation antihistamines. Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) of medication use may provide insights into disease mechanisms and pharmacologically responsive biological pathways beyond conventional diagnostic boundaries. We therefore performed a GWAS of long‐term, non‐seasonal sgAH use to identify genetic determinants associated with sustained treatment benefit and to distinguish loci reflecting medication use from those associated with underlying inflammatory diseases.

We analysed 392,000 participants from FinnGen Data Freeze 9 [3]. Cases were defined as individuals with > 10 systemic sgAH purchases, also outside pollen season, thereby enriching for long‐term and year‐round use (N = 39,804). Controls had no recorded sgAH purchases (N = 209,106), while individuals with 1–9 purchases were excluded. Medication use was identified from the nationwide drug purchase register (data available1995‐2021). Disease phenotypes were derived from linked nationwide healthcare registries. Genome‐wide association analyses of approximately 17 million variants were conducted using REGENIE v2.2.4 adjusted for age, sex, genotyping batch, ancestry principal components and relatedness. Fine‐mapping, HLA imputation and pathway analyses used SuSiE, HIBAG and MAGMA, respectively.

Cases had a mean age of 58 years, mean follow‐up of 20.4 years and 28.5 sgAH purchases; cetirizine was the most frequently used drug. Cases were more often female than controls (73.5% vs. 46.7%) and had as expected substantially higher prevalence of inflammatory mucosal and skin diseases, including asthma, allergic or vasomotor rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, conjunctivitis, atopic dermatitis and urticaria (65.4% vs. 18.3% among controls, p < 2.2 × 10−16). Disease‐specific corticosteroids were used by almost everybody among cases (95.5% vs. 65.3% among controls, p < 2.0 × 10−16), further supporting chronic inflammatory disease burden.

GWAS identified 66 independent loci (100 credible sets) and the HLA region associated with sgAH use at genome‐wide significance (p < 10−8) (Figure 1). Two of the identified lead variants were highly Finnish‐enriched missense variants in IL4RA and TESPA1. Additional coding signals included lead variants in IL6RA and C17orf58 and a premature stop codon in FLG. Structural prediction suggested the TESPA1 R282C/R144C substitution may be damaging, whereas the IL4RA substitution appeared benign. Several additional missense variants in linkage disequilibrium with lead variants (r 2 > 0.6) were identified, including loci previously implicated in eosinophilic and atopic disease biology.

Genome‐wide association analysis of second‐generation antihistamine use highlighting locus specificity and associated signalling pathways.

HLA imputations showed associations to both class I and II genes. Two risk haplotypes corresponded to well‐established autoimmune‐associated haplotypes previously reported in FinnGen and other populations: C07:01–B08:01–DRB103:01–DQA105:01–DQB102:01 and DRB104:01/04:04–DRB401:03–DQA103:01–DQB1*03:02 [4]. Protective haplotypes included the most common Finnish haplotype and two rarer class II haplotypes without any known disease associations.

Gene‐based MAGMA analyses identified a total of 179 genes significantly associated with sgAH use. Pathway analysis showed enrichment that highlighted cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions, particularly IL‐2 family cytokine signalling through the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT) pathway (p = 5.0 × 10−9) and shared the common γ‐chain (CD132) receptor subunit, a key regulator of lymphocyte development and function [5, 6] (Figure 1). In this pathway, 14 of 20 genes showed at least nominal association (p < 0.05) across 11 independent loci, suggesting convergence on a shared immunoregulatory mechanism. Enrichment of major histocompatibility complex class II genes was also observed, likely reflecting the single strong HLA signal.

Most loci overlapped with susceptibility to inflammatory diseases, with broadly consistent effect sizes across traits. Comparison of sgAH GWAS summary statistics with six overrepresented inflammatory conditions using MetABF suggested that most signals reflected shared disease susceptibility rather than sgAH responsiveness. However, four loci consistently demonstrated sgAH specificity, mapping near DELEC1, POU2AF1, KLF5 and LINC00334. According to the GWAS Catalog, three of the loci appear largely independent of known disease associations. This may partly reflect incomplete diagnostic data but may also indicate biological mechanisms not captured by conventional disease classifications. The exception was rs13300483 near DELEC1, an eQTL for TNFSF8, previously associated with asthma outside FinnGen. Our GWAS also replicated eight of 14 previously reported sgAH loci [5, 7]. However, in those studies treatment indications were unavailable and most loci were established susceptibility loci for atopic or eosinophilic disease.

Our findings suggest that prolonged sgAH use is genetically linked to broad inflammatory and immunoregulatory pathways rather than to individual diagnostic entities. The enrichment of variants within IL‐2 family cytokine signalling pathways, together with the presence of autoimmune‐associated HLA haplotypes, supports the existence of shared biological mechanisms across allergic and immune‐mediated diseases [8]. These observations imply that genetic determinants of sgAH use may act through pathways that transcend conventional disease classifications and are therefore not fully captured by the diagnostic labels used to define treatment indications. Nevertheless, at least three loci appeared specific to sgAH use and were not associated with known disease susceptibility loci, raising the possibility that sustained benefit from antihistamines may reflect genetically mediated treatment responsiveness or patterns of medication utilisation rather than disease risk alone.

Limitations of the study include the lack of information on over‐the‐counter antihistamine use, incomplete diagnostic coding in early primary care and inability to reliably distinguish allergic from non‐allergic disease subtypes. Nevertheless, nationwide drug purchase and health registry data enabled long‐term characterization of treatment patterns. Individuals benefiting from sustained sgAH therapy may represent a biologically distinct subgroup relevant for future therapeutic targeting of shared cytokine‐mediated pathways [9].
