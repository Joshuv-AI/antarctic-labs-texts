One Neglected Complication in the BPA Process? Severe Vagal Response During the Angioplasty Procedure—A Case Report
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yue Yan, Wanmu Xie, Zhenguo Zhai, Qiang Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBalloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) is now a widely accepted treatment for inoperable chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), but it still faces the problem of high complications. Herein, we report a rare case of severe vagal response during the BPA of a total occlusion lesion in a patient with CTEPH. The patient experienced acute chest pain and dyspnea, accompanied by a significant decrease in heart rate and blood pressure. After administering atropine, the patient's symptoms rapidly subsided. This case highlights the potential for vascular and pleural injury with chest pain, which can lead to severe vagal response during the BPA procedures. To minimize this risk, avoiding guide wire‐induced injury to the pleura situated distal to the target vessel is crucial.

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is the most serious long‐term adverse outcome of pulmonary embolism, presenting significant diagnosis and treatment challenges for healthcare providers. The multidisciplinary management of CTEPH integrates surgical, interventional, and medical treatments [1]. Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) remains the first‐line treatment strategy for operable CTEPH, while balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) has been proposed as an alternative therapeutic treatment for inoperable CTEPH, as outlined in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines [2, 3]. As awareness of CTEPH grows among physicians and the global adoption of BPA accelerates, an increasing number of eligible patients are expected to undergo this procedure. However, the emergence and recognition of new or severe complications will also rise. Timely identification and effective management of complications are crucial for preventing adverse outcomes and ensuring patient safety.

Case Description

A 46‐year‐old woman presented with a 2‐year history of dyspnea. Clinical evaluation, including echocardiography, computed tomographic (CT) pulmonary angiography, and V/Q scan suggested the diagnosis of CTEPH. Right heart catheterization confirmed the diagnosis and the patient was classified as technically inoperable following the multidisciplinary team decision. BPA was performed in the right lung using a routinely refined technique. The patient demonstrated significant improvement and relief of symptoms following the first session of BPA. One month later, the second session of BPA was performed in the left lung as planned. At the beginning of the operation, the patient's blood pressure was 132/98 mmHg and the heart rate was 66 bpm. Angiography showed a total occlusion lesion in the LA5 branch (Figure 1A,B). The guide wire and balloon catheter were successfully navigated through the lesion into the distal vessel, with guide wire inadvertently extended deep into the pleura (Figure 1B,C). However, during the balloon angioplasty of the branch LA5, the patient suddenly complained of left chest pain accompanied by acute dyspnea, prompting her to sit up on the operating bed. The electrocardiogram monitor showed a reduced heart rate of 37 bpm, and a blood pressure of 71/40 mmHg. A vagal response was suspected and 0.25 mg atropine was administered intravenously, resulting in a prompt recovery. Her blood pressure stabilized at 96/60 mmHg and the heart rate returned to 60–70 bpm. She remained stable after her safe return to the ward, but continued to complain of intermittent left chest pain. Chest CT revealed minor pleural effusion and partial atelectasis in the left thoracic cavity (Figure 1D). After 3 days of medical treatment, she was discharged with symptom relief. After discharge, the patient was followed up for 1 month and reported no obvious symptoms.

Discussion

BPA is the recommended treatment for CTEPH patients who are ineligible for PEA [4]. However, high rates of complications from BPA have also been published in the past two decades [5, 6, 7, 8]. The most common BPA‐related complications are vascular or lung injury, including wire‐associated injury, and less commonly pulmonary artery perforation and dissection [7, 8]. Therefore, a key consideration for BPA treatment is ensuring successful revascularization while minimizing complications, particularly, those that are severe and potentially life‐threatening.

The specific symptom of the patient was a sudden, acute pain in the left chest accompanied by obvious dyspnea during the procedure on the pulmonary artery. The critical concern was the decreased heart rate and dropped blood pressure. Timely intravenous atropine was effective in relieving her symptoms. All the information indicated the diagnosis of vagal response. To the best of our knowledge, there is no report of severe vagal response as a complication during BPA procedures. In managing a vagal response, adrenaline may be the most effective treatment. However, atropine is as effective as adrenaline in this situation. The subsequent emergence of pleural effusion also demonstrated vascular and lung injury. In addition, this patient did not experience hemoptysis, suggesting that vascular damage was likely localized to the distal end, causing the pleura damage. Using a balloon for occlusion is the usual remedy for vascular injury. However, the patient did not have hemoptysis, and since symptoms were quickly alleviated after administering atropine, balloon occlusion was not used in this case.

The complications of BPA continue to occur. Jain et al. performed a systematic analysis of the published data reporting on BPA complications and find that the rate of complications has decreased over the years [7]. The study included 1675 patients from 2013 to 2022 and provided evidence that BPA procedure has become safer. Currently, the application of BPA is confined to specific centers and experienced clinicians, yet it is anticipated that this technique will be more broadly adopted in the next decade due to its simpler implementation compared to PEA. Despite the potential for BPA to find a more prominent role within the multimodal therapy for CTEPH, its increasing usage may lead clinicians to encounter more unusual complications, some of which may be severe. The leading cause of complications is distal vascular injury and pleural injury caused by the tip of the wire. This case highlights that chest pain resultant from pleural damage can trigger a severe vagal response. There are multiple strategies for increasing the safety of BPA including cautious revascularization and expectant remodeling of totally occluded vessels, and also very important earlier recognition and management of serious adverse events. It must be emphasized that BPA should still be performed at expert centers to limit rates of complications, especially nowadays more centers are learning how to do BPA. The operators need to be cognizant of the wire throughout BPA to not let it get too distal, which can lead to vascular and pleural injury.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of severe vagal response during BPA treatment in a CTEPH patient. This case aims to improve clinicians' understanding of the complications of BPA. Gentle manipulation of the guide wire should be emphasized to avoid this potentially severe complication.
