Efficacy of precisely tailored phage cocktails targeting carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii reveals evolutionary trade-offs: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Ziqiang Liu, Xin Tan, Min Xiong, Shitong Lu, Yongqing Yang, Heng Zhu, Jieqiong Zhang, Xiangying Luo, Caiyun Zhou, Shujiang Wei, Ning Zhou, Xueyan Liu, Changqing Bai, Yongjun Pan, Yingfei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe rapid emergence of phage-resistant bacterial mutants and the challenge of developing tailored phage cocktails have significantly hindered the broad application of phage therapy. This is particularly critical for infections caused by highly prevalent strains such as capsule locus 2 (KL2)-type carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) in China.MethodsWe employed an iterative phage adaptive selection (iPAS) strategy to develop an optimised phage cocktail specifically targeting KL2-type CRAB strains. To facilitate efficient clinical application, we also established a rapid identification method for KL2-type isolates. The efficacy and safety of this rationally designed cocktail were subsequently evaluated in a clinical setting through its application in two compassionate use cases of CRAB infection as a proof-of-concept study. Whole genomic sequencing was conducted on the isolated phage-resistant mutants to reveal the related mutations.FindingsKL2-type A. baumannii was the predominant lineage, accounting for 17.7% (159/896) of isolates reported across China over the past five years, and 33.3% (46/138) of clinical CRAB isolates obtained by our laboratory from five hospitals and one institute in Guangdong Province. The optimised phage cocktail effectively targeted 89.1% (41/46) of these KL2-type isolates. The phage-resistant A. baumannii mutants exhibited beneficial trade-offs, including increased antibiotic sensitivity, reduced virulence, susceptibility to immune clearance, and impaired biofilm formation. Genomic analysis revealed that these trade-offs were driven by concentrated and consistent mutations in genes involved in lipo-oligosaccharide and capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis. Crucially, the application of this cocktail in two clinical cases of CRAB infection demonstrated both clinical efficacy in resolving infections and a favourable safety profile.InterpretationThis research underscores the potential of rational tailored phage cocktails developed through strategies like iPAS, to address the growing threat of CRAB infections. The successful clinical application highlights the translational impact of this study. Furthermore, the study provides valuable insights into the co-evolutionary dynamics between bacteria and phages, paving the way for broader and more effective clinical applications of phage therapy.FundingThis work was supported by National Key R&D Programme of China; Shenzhen Medical Research Funds; Shenzhen Nanshan District Health Technology Major Project; Shenzhen Science and Technology Innovation Commission and Shenzhen Science and Technology Programme.Keywords: Phage therapy, KL-2 type carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, Trade-offs, Tailored phage cocktail

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Rational phage cocktail design counters phage resistance evolution by combining phages targeting multiple distinct bacterial surface receptors. An advanced application specifically selects for receptors critical to virulence. This ensures that bacteria evolving resistance through receptor modification or loss incur a clinically favourable trade-off, such as attenuated virulence or restored antibiotic sensitivity. Despite its promise as a framework for developing potent therapies, this approach has yet to yield clinically validated treatments for high-priority multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. This gap is particularly evident for the notoriously difficult-to-treat carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB).

Added value of this study

This proof-of-concept study provides the clinical validation of a tailored phage cocktail against CRAB, bridging the critical gap between theoretical framework and clinical practice. The tailored cocktail, developed by systematically screening phages against sequentially resistant bacterial mutants to ensure diverse receptor targeting, demonstrated high efficacy against 89.1% of tested KL2-type CRAB isolates, which are highly prevalent in China. Resistant mutants exhibited the predicted fitness trade-offs. Crucially, when administered to two patients with severe CRAB infections, the cocktail proved rapid, safe and clinically effective, achieving successful infection resolution. This establishes a validated framework for clinical phage cocktail development.

Implications of all the available evidence

By clinical development and validation of a tailored phage cocktail against the highly prevalent clinical strains, this study provides a reproducible framework for developing standardised, potent therapeutics essential for large-scale randomised controlled trials, overcoming a major barrier to phage therapy translation. Our methodology establishes a transferable framework for creating predictable phage cocktails against other MDR pathogens, marking a paradigm shift from empirical screening to rational phage therapy development.

Introduction

Given the escalating prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections and the limited availability of new antibiotics, there has been a renewed interest in utilising bacteriophages (phages) as an alternative therapeutic and prophylactic approach.1, 2, 3 This is underscored by prominent cases, including the first modern use of phage therapy in 2016 to treat a multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii infection.4 Clinical interventions involving phages have surged, yielding encouraging outcomes in combating infections caused by a diverse array of bacterial pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli.5, 6, 7, 8

During phage therapy, the concurrent evolution of phages and their bacterial hosts can lead to the emergence of phage resistance,1, 2, 3,9 thereby reducing the phage therapy efficacy in eliminating infections. An effective strategy to prevent phage resistance is the use of a phage cocktail.1, 2, 3,9 While conventional phage cocktails have been used, often developed through empirical selection or by primarily aiming to broaden the host range,10,11 there is currently a push for more rationally designed approaches. In 2024, researchers have proposed a principle for creating potent phage cocktails by targeting different receptors on the bacterial surface with a combination of phages.12,13 The diversity of bacterial receptors significantly impacts the phage host range,13,14 and theoretically, cocktails that target distinct receptors would offer broad-spectrum coverage, potentially limiting cross-resistance. A more targeted application of this principle focuses on key bacterial receptors, like efflux pumps, capsules, or lipopolysaccharides, that are crucial for virulence and antibiotic resistance.5,15, 16, 17 This approach creates a powerful evolutionary trade-off where surviving bacteria become less virulent or more susceptible to antibiotics, thereby potentially improving clinical outcomes.5,15, 16, 17 However, the lengthy identification process for these receptors creates a major bottleneck, limiting the feasibility of this approach in time-sensitive clinical situations. An efficient strategy that circumvents this limitation involves using phage-resistant bacterial mutants to isolate novel phages with differing infection mechanisms, thereby enabling the rapid construction of more robust cocktails.18,19

The need for such a strategy is particularly urgent for the Gram-negative bacterium A. baumannii, which represents a formidable challenge in healthcare environments, especially in cases of ventilator-associated pneumonia and catheter-associated infections.20,21 This challenge is further intensified by the emergence of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), responsible for 19% of infections in ICU patients, with a mortality rate of 67%.22,23 The complex nature of this pathogen is characterised by its multidrug resistance, persistence across diverse environmental conditions, resilience to disinfectants, ability to form biofilm, and motility, all of which obstruct efficacious clinical treatments.24,25 These attributes not only impede effective interventions but also elevate the risk of reinfection and widespread outbreaks.

While the initial identification of phage receptors poses a major bottleneck for many pathogens, this hurdle has been largely surmounted for A. baumannii. Extensive research has firmly established that the A. baumannii capsule functions as the primary receptor for Acinetobacter phage adsorption.26,27 Targeting its capsule, a key virulence determinant, presents a therapeutic trade-off: phage-induced pressure can select for capsular modifications that often reduce pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance.26,27 A. baumannii strains can be categorised into distinct capsule types based on the polymorphism of their capsule locus (KL), with each type exhibiting a unique capsular antigen structure.28,29 As these antigens are typically phage receptors,26,27 understanding regional KL type prevalence is pivotal for developing effective phage cocktails. A recent large-scale global genomic surveillance study showed that the predominant A. baumannii strains in specific regions and periods display a relatively stable distribution of KL types.30 This discovery underscores the potential of phage cocktails targeting predominant KL types as a powerful therapeutic strategy against CRAB infections.

In this study, we harnessed the aforementioned rapid isolation strategy to develop a comprehensive therapeutic framework against CRAB KL2 strains, the most prevalent type in China. Our approach was designed to achieve two synergistic goals: first, to create a robust phage cocktail capable of suppressing emergent resistance regardless of the underlying mechanism, and second, to exploit the likely evolutionary trade-offs associated with modifications to the primary capsular receptor. This framework consists of two core steps: (1) a tailored phage cocktail, and (2) a companion rapid diagnostic method for identifying susceptible KL2 strains in clinical practice. We first demonstrated the cocktail's efficacy in the treatment of patient #1. Subsequently, we validated the entire therapeutic approach—the optimised cocktail and the rapid diagnostic tool—in patient #2, thereby showing the efficacy and clinical potential of our strategy.

Methods

Ethics

The case series study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern University of Science and Technology Hospital according to CARE guidelines and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles (Ethical review board approval no: 202301).31 From September 2022 to June 2024, two unconscious patients in the ICU were included after their guardians provided written informed consent.

Samples and A. baumannii isolates collection

The clinical isolates from two patients undergoing phage therapy were isolated from their sputum samples, and identified by Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).32 Clinical A. baumannii isolates were maintained on Luria–Bertani (LB) broth and stored in 25% glycerol at −80 °C. For longitudinal monitoring, sputum samples were collected from patient #1 at baseline (day −1), during active therapy at intervals spanning days 1–3, 5–7, 10–11, 18–19, 21–27, 29–42, 45–46, 49, 53, and 56, and finally on day 61 post-treatment. Sputum samples were collected from patient #2 at pre-treatment (day −1), daily from days 1–21 during treatment, and follow-up on days 50 and 89 after therapy completion.

Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii isolates collection

An additional 128 clinical CRAB isolates were kindly provided by Dr. Yongjun Pan from Southern University of Science and Technology Hospital, Dr. Changqing Bai from Shenzhen University General Hospital, Dr. Yang Zhou from Shenzhen Third People's Hospital, Dr. Jinzhu Mao from The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, Dr. Xueyan Liu from Shenzhen People's Hospital, and Prof. Yinyue Deng from Sun Yat-Sen University. ATCC17978 was obtained from our laboratory collection. The strain typing, sample origins, and collection timepoints for all isolates are detailed in Table S1.

Phage screening and isolation

Phages in this clinical treatment were screened, isolated, propagated, purified and characterised at Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology. A. baumannii clinical isolate NAB01B was used to screen lytic phages with the double layer agar spot test method as previously described,33 with some modifications. Briefly, 200 μL of an overnight bacterial culture was mixed with 10 mL soft LB agar (0.7% agar) and poured into 90 mm round dishes with solid LB agar (1.5% agar). After solidification of the top agar, 10 μL of the tested phages at a concentration of 1010 plaque forming unit (PFU)/mL was dropped, left to dry and then incubated at 37 °C overnight. The susceptibilities of the tested phages were visually evaluated by their plaques. Phages forming confluent clear lysis zone were selected for the next characterisation. Meanwhile, the A. baumannii clinical isolate NAB01B was also used to isolate novel lytic phages from sewage for potential application with routine isolation techniques, as previously described.34 Briefly, 200 μL of overnight bacterial culture was mixed with 2 mL of sewage sample and supplemented with 3 × concentrated LB medium. The mixture was incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 12 h, and the supernatant was harvested by centrifugation and filtration. A spot test was conducted by applying 10 μL of supernatant onto a lawn of host bacteria on an LB agar plate. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, it was observed for plaque formation. If plaques were present, we picked a single well-isolated plaque using a sterile loop and streaked it onto a fresh bacterial lawn. This plaque purification process was repeated for three consecutive rounds to obtain a clonal phage population.

Phage propagation and purification

Phage candidates for phage therapy were sequentially added to logarithmic clinical A. baumannii isolates at 37 °C with 220 rpm shaking overnight for propagation. The lysate was centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was filtrated through a 0.22-μm filter for sterilisation. The caesium chloride density gradient was adopted to purify phage lysate as previously described35 and then dialysed three times with a dialysis membrane (MWCO 20 kDa) in a thousandfold volume of adjusted SM Buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4) for 8 h to remove residual CsCl. After further sterilisation by filtrating, 10 μL of drops of 10-time serially diluted purified phages from 10−1 to 10−10 were deposited on their corresponding host clinical A. baumannii isolates lawn, then left to dry and incubated at 37 °C overnight to detect titres. The endotoxin level of the purified phages was determined by the End-point Chromogenic Endotoxin Test Kit (EC80545S, Bioendo) according to the manufacturer's directions. The final endotoxin levels for each phage cocktail are listed in Table S2 and were found to be below the FDA-specified limit of 5 EU/kg/h.

Transmission electron microscopy

A volume of 20 μL of purified phage suspension was placed onto a carbon-coated copper grid, which had been glow-discharged freshly36 for 1 min, to let the phage particles settle, and stained with 2% of uranyl acetate for 1 min. Then the copper grids were observed under Transmission Electron Microscopy (120 kV, 64 k × ).

Phage DNA extraction, sequencing, assembly and analysis

40 mL of phage lysate with a titre greater than 1010 PFU/mL was pretreated in the presence of 10% polyethylene glycol 8000 (PEG 8000) and 1 M NaCl at 4 °C overnight, centrifuged at 10,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and subsequently suspended in SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4, 50 mM Tris-HCl). Then the concentrated phage particles were incubated with 8 U DNase I and 20 U RNase A at 37 °C for 30 min to remove bacterial nucleic acids. The genomic phage DNA was extracted with MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer's directions and then sequenced by the Illumina HiSeq system at the Beijing Genomics Institute-Shenzhen. The high-quality filtered reads were assembled by Spades v3.15.5.37 The complete genome sequences were annotated by the online website: Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology (RAST)38 and complemented by Bakta Web,39 and the genomes of the phages were annotated by the software pharokka v1.6.0.40 Antibiotic-resistant genes and virulence factors were identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD)41 and the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB).42 The genome maps of the phages were depicted with the Proksee.43

Comparative analysis of phage genomes

A comparative genomic analysis of the phages isolated in this study and previously characterised therapeutic or well-established lytic phages was conducted using Clinker website.44 The annotated genbank files of these phages were uploaded to the Clinker website and generated the comparative genome map.

In vitro selection of phage-resistant mutants of clinical isolate NAB01B

Clinical isolate NAB01B was incubated at 37 °C with 1010 PFU AB_CK1 with shaking at 220 rpm for 24 h to select phage-resistant mutants. 10 μL mixed culture was streaked on a solid medium and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Ten A. baumannii clones, named NAB01B-R1 to NAB01B-R10, were chosen. To randomly select resistant clones, the plate was divided by drawing two horizontal lines and five vertical lines to creat ten intersection points. The colonies closest to these ten intersections were selected. All mutants were passaged for three generations in a phage-free environment to confirm the genetic mutations were heritable. Phage adsorption assays were subsequently performed to further verify their resistance to the phages. Briefly, the phage and the bacteria were mixed at MOI (multiplicity of infection) of 0.01 and incubated in a shaker at 37 °C for 5 min. Then the free phage of the filtered mixture was titrated. Meanwhile phage mixed with LB broth served as control. The adsorption rate was calculated by subtracting the phage titre of the bacteria-phage mixture supernatant from the control group's phage titre, dividing the result by the control group's phage titre, and multiplying by 100%.

Phage sensitivity of the A. baumannii isolates

The plaque assay was adopted to assess the sensitivities of the candidate phages on A. baumannii isolates, with some modification.45 In brief, 5 μL of phage suspension was dropped onto the bacterial lawn and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. To further evaluate phage susceptibility, serially diluted phage stocks were spotted onto lawns of clinical isolates or in vitro-selected mutants and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. For phages that formed distinct plaques, we calculated the standard Efficiency of Plating (EOP) by comparing the plaque-forming units (PFU) on the test strain to the reference strain, NAB01B. For phage AB_SZL7, which produced zones of lysis without discrete plaques, we used a parallel method, Relative Titration Efficiency (RTE), which compares the highest lytic dilution on a test strain to the reference strain. Based on the resulting EOP or RTE ratio, isolates were classified as sensitive (ratio >0.1), intermediate (ratio from 0.0001 to 0.1), or resistant (ratio <0.0001). It should be noted that RTE approximates the phage's lytic capacity under specific conditions and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of bactericidal activity.

Phage-mediated killing of planktonic bacteria

The growth curves of the NAB01B, NAB03B and NAB07B were assessed with the selected phages (at MOI = 0.1), as well as without phage treatment. Three clinical A. baumannii isolates were cultured to the logarithmic stage (OD600 = 0.45) corresponding to 2 ∗108 CFU/mL which was validated by plate counting. Experiments were done in 96-well plates in a final volume of 200 μL of LB, bacterial cells were seeded at a concentration of 107 CFU/well, and the phages and phage cocktails were added at a titre of 106 PFU/well corresponding to the MOI = 0.1. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a microplate reader with shaking at 220 rpm for 25 h, and the OD600 readings were acquired every 10 min. The experiments were performed at least twice, each time in triplicate, yielding similar results. One representative result is shown in the Results.

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation experiments were conducted in a 96-well plate (3599, CORNING), each well was inoculated with 200 μL brain heart infusion (BHI) medium containing 1:200 dilutions of overnight cultured A. baumannii isolates, and control wells with BHI only were included. After 48 h of static incubation at 37 °C, the medium was removed, and the residual biofilm was gently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The biofilms were stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime) for 15 min, washed, and solubilised with 95% ethanol. The optical density at 595 nm was determined by a microplate spectrophotometer (EPOCH2C, Biotek) and the software Gen5 CHS v 3.03.46 The experiments were performed at least twice, each time in triplicate. One representative result is shown in the Results.

Phage-mediated dispersal of established biofilm

Biofilm was established in a 96-well plate, each well was inoculated with 200 μL BHI medium containing 1:200 dilutions of overnight cultured A. baumannii clinical isolates (NAB01B, NAB03B and NAB07B) and wells with BHI only served as the negative control. After 48 h static incubation at 37 °C, the planktonic bacteria were removed and the established biofilms were gently washed twice with PBS before adding fresh BHI containing various titres of phage cocktails (102, 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, PFU/well respectively). After 8 h static incubation at 37 °C, the medium was removed, and the residual biofilm was gently washed, stained and quantified as described above. The experiments were performed in triplicate.

Phage-mediated inhibition of biofilm forming

Various titres of phage cocktails (ranging from 102 to 107 PFU/well) were added to each well containing 200 μL BHI medium with 1:200 dilutions of overnight cultured A. baumannii clinical isolates (NAB01B, NAB03B or NAB07B) before 48 h static incubation at 37 °C. Wells with medium only and medium with 1:200 dilutions of overnight cultured bacteria served as controls. The medium was removed, the biofilm was washed with PBS and stained and determined as above. The experiments were performed in triplicate.

Antibiotics susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 11 antibiotics were assessed using the microbroth-dilution protocol as previously described47 and interpreted using the cut-off values from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.48 A 100 μL aliquot of log-phase bacterial suspension was inoculated into the Gram-negative susceptibility panel, AST-GN13 card, (22095, France) and incubated in the VITEK 2 Compact system (France) for 8 h. The card was automatically read every 15 min, generating strain-specific susceptibility profiles against multiple antibiotics. The antibiotics tested were piperacillin/tazobactam, sulbactam/cefoperazone, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, minocycline, polymyxin B and cotrimoxazole.

Cell culture

For the cell culture study, the human HeLa cell line (validated through STR profiling, RRID: CVCL_0030) was kindly provided by Dr. Minghai Chen, Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (A5669701, Gibco) and 100 μg/mL penicillin-streptomycin (FG101-01, TransGen Biotech). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. We regularly checked mycoplasma contamination using the MycAway™ Plus-Colour One-Step Mycoplasma Detection Kit (40612ES08, Yeasen). This assay employs isothermal amplification of a conserved mycoplasma DNA sequence, which, if present, triggers a visible colour change in the reaction from blue-purple (negative) to sky blue (positive), allowing for direct visual confirmation. Only mycoplasma-negative cells were used for all experiments.

Virulence assay in vitro

The virulence of the A. baumannii clinical isolates from patient #1 (NAB01B: NAB07B) and the phage-resistant mutants (NAB01B-R1, NAB01B-R3, NAB01B-R8 and NAB01B-R9) against Hela cells was assessed via measuring the Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) level.49 Hela cells (1∗104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates separately and cultivated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h to 80% confluence. After rinsing with PBS twice, 200 μL full medium containing various bacteria (2∗104 CFU/well) was added, and cells cultured without any bacteria served as a negative control. After 12 h incubation, the LDH levels of the supernatant were detected with a Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity Assay Kit (AKCO003M, Boxbio) according to the protocol of the manufacturer. The experiments were performed in triplicate.

Galleria mellonella infection experiment

The virulence of the A. baumannii clinical isolates from patient #1 (NAB01B: NAB07B) and the phage-resistant mutants (NAB01B-R1, NAB01B-R3, NAB01B-R8 and NAB01B-R9) was further evaluated using a Galleria mellonella infection model.50 The overnight bacterial culture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in PBS, then diluted to a cell density of 1∗107 CFU/mL. The G. mellonella larvae weighing around 300 mg were randomly divided into 13 groups (n = 20). Each individual was injected with 10 μL of 1∗107 CFU/mL of those bacterial suspension. The standard strain A. baumannii ATCC17978 and PBS were injected at the same dose to serve as positive and negative controls, respectively. The inoculated G. mellonella were then incubated at 37 °C and their status was observed at 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h post-inoculation. The G. mellonella that did not respond to physical stimuli was considered dead.

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy analysis

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy analysis (FESEM) analysis was conducted on the A. baumannii clinical isolates (NAB01B, NAB03B and NAB07B) and the phage-resistant mutants (NAB01B-R1, NAB01B-R3, NAB01B-R8 and NAB01B-R9) according to the method described by Ouyang et al.,51 with some modifications. Overnight bacteria cultures were centrifuged at 300×g for 40 min. The pellets were washed with PBS and then fixed with pre-cooled 2.50% glutaraldehyde. The surface morphology, shape and size of the bacterial cells were then determined under a field-emission scanning electron microscope SIGMA -500 at a voltage of 5.0 kV using SmartSEM.

Bacterial DNA extraction, sequencing, assembly and analysis

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) of all the clinical A. baumannii isolates and in vitro selected phage-resistant mutants was extracted with MiniBESTBacteria Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.3.0 (9763, TaKaRa) according to manufacturer's instruction and then sequenced by DNBSEQ-T7 150 bp paired-end platform at the Beijing Genomics Institute (Shenzhen, China). In addition, the gDNA of NAB01B was sequenced with long-reads using the Nanopore MinION platform and the MinION flow cell (R9.4.1 FLO-MIN106, Oxford Nanopore). For, NAB01B: NAB07B and in vitro selected phage-resistant mutants, the high-quality filtered reads were assembled by the hybrid assembler Unicycler v0.4.8,52 at default settings. The assembled genome sequences were visualised using Circos (0.69–9) software to generate a circular genomic map.53 For the rest of the isolates, the high-quality filtered reads were assembled by Spades v3.15.5 with default parameters.37 The complete genome sequences were annotated with prokka (v.1.14.6).54 ARGs were identified using the CARD Resistance Gene Identifier (v 6.0.2) web service41 allowing for perfect and strict hits under default settings. The capsule (K) locus and the outer core (OC) locus55 of the clinical isolates were determined using Kaptive v.2.0.6 with default parameters and the Kaptive reference database for A. baumannii K loci and A. baumannii OC loci. Pangenome analysis was performed using the annotated bacteria genomes and obtained the core genome scheme of the clinical A. baumannii isolates with Panaroo pipeline.56 Based on those core genome-aligned sequences, the FastTree (v.2.1.11) was used to construct a phylogenetic tree.57 The phylogenetic tree was then visualised and labelled using the iTOL Web service (v 5.6.3).58 All sequencing data generated in this study has been deposited in the NCBI BioProject database accession PRJNA1121085.

Sputum microbiome 16 S rDNA analysis

The V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16 S rRNA gene in the sputum was amplified using primers 16F515 (5′-GTGTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 16R803 (5′-CCGGACTACHVGGTWTCTAAT-3′) using a method described by Gu et al.59 The amplicons were then normalised and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system in a single batch (2 × 150 bp). QIIME 1.0 platform (version 4.4.20) was adopted to produce operational taxonomic units (OTUs) clusters and classifications.60

Sputum A. baumannii wzy gene PCR analysis

The mutated wzy region of A. baumannii isolates in the sputum samples was amplified using primers wzy3_F (5′-GCTGTTGTGTGCGCTGGAAT-3′) and wzy3_R (5′-GCAATCATACAAATTCTTTTTGCTGGAGC-3′) using the same method described above. The amplicons were then normalised and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system in a single batch (2 × 150 bp). QIIME 1.0 platform (version 4.4.20) was adopted to split the libraries with barcodes. The reads of original isolate NAB01B and wzy mutated isolate NAB07B were counted from each sample manually.

Serum resistance assay

Serum resistance of the clinical A. baumannii isolates from patient #1 (NAB01B: NAB07B) and phage-resistant mutants (NAB01B-R1, NAB01B-R3, NAB01B-R8 and NAB01B-R9) and other clinical A. baumannii isolates (NAB17B, TAB09B and ZAB01B) were determined in normal rat serum or heat-inactivated serum (56 °C for 30 min) according to61 with some modification. The isolates were grown to the logarithmic phase then washed with PBS and diluted to 2∗106 CFU/mL. The diluted bacteria were mixed at a 1:1 v/v ratio with the normal rat serum or the heat-inactivated rat serum and co-incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. At the starting time point and 60 min, samples from each tube were extracted to perform serial dilutions and CFU counts. The serum resistance of the isolates was calculated as a viable bacteria decrease after 1 h of incubation.

Gene knockout and plasmid-derived complementation

A gene knockout experiment of the gtrOC3 gene was conducted using the CRISPR-Cas9/RecAb system with modifications based on a previous study.62 All primers for cloning and gene complementation are shown in Table S3. Briefly, overnight cultured NAB01B was diluted into LB broth at 1:100, and incubated with shaking at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.5–0.7. In particular, electrocompetent NAB01B containing pCasAB_Apr plasmid cells need to be induced with 1 mM IPTG when the OD600 reaches 0.1. After being chilled on ice immediately for 15 min, the bacterial culture was harvested by centrifugation at 3000g for 6 min. The bacterial pellet was resuspended with 15 mL of sterile ice-cold ddH2O. The cell was washed again with 15 mL of sterile ice-cold 10% glycerol. Then the pellet was resuspended with 1 mL of sterile ice-cold 10% glycerol and divided into 100 μL per tube for electroporation.

Because the NAB01B strain is intrinsically resistant to most ordinary antibiotics, the spectinomycin selection marker of the pSGAb-spe plasmid was replaced by a sodium tellurite selection marker, resulting in the pSGAb-tpm plasmid. The sodium tellurite selection marker, thiopurine S-methyltransferase (tpm) gene, were chemically synthesised according to the reference sequence from plasmid pVT59.63 The backbone of plasmid pSGAb-tpm was amplified from plasmid pSGAb.62 The construction of plasmid was conducted in E. coli strain Trans T1 in accordance with standard procedures. The pSGAB_tpm_gtrOC3 plasmid was then assembled with the gtrOC3 spacer predicted by CHOPCHOP online64 and the pSGAb-tpm plasmid. The 1000-bp (500 bp upstream plus 500 bp downstream) dsDNA repair template for knockout gene gtrOC3 was amplified from NAB01B.

Plasmid gtrOC3_Ab_Apr and wzy_KL2_Ab_Apr were constructed to complement the phage-resistant strains. Gene gtrOC3 and wzy_KL2 were amplified from strain NAB01B. The replication origin ColE1 and WH1266, J23119 promoter and transcription terminator were cloned from plasmid pSGAb-km.

100 μL of electrocompetent cells was thawed on ice for several minutes. Then 200 ng of plasmid with or without 300 ng repair template were added into the electrocompetent cells. The mixture was transferred into a 2 mm electroporation cuvette (Bio-Rad) and electroporated at 2.5 kV. After being pulsed, the cells were recovered and incubated in 1 mL L B broth without any selection at 37 °C for 2 h before being plated onto LB agar plates supplemented with the 50 μg/mL apramycin or 50 μg/mL sodium tellurite. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. In addition, the sodium tellurite exerts strong selective pressure on E. coli, requiring a 2–3 days cultivation period for screening Trans T1 cells containing plasmid pSGAb-tpm.

Rapid PCR assay for screening KL2 A. baumannii

The A. baumannii isolates of the patients were cultured by the clinical laboratory department, and then colony PCR assays with primers, gtr3_F (5′-TTGCTATAGTCCCAACGTTTATATTCCCATC-3′)/gtr3_R (5′-CCTGTCCCTACATATTCCATATATATTGTAGAGTC-3′) and pgt1-F (5′- TGATTGCTAAAAAAGATCTTGGGAAAGTGG-3′)/pgt1_R (5′-ACAAAGGCAACCCTGCTAGC-3′), were conducted to detect the presence of gtr3 and pgt1 genes. A. baumannii isolates with the gtr3 gene but without the pgt1 gene were identified as A. baumannii with capsule type KL2.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed and graphs were generated using the GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software. We employed the Kruskal–Wallis test for multi-group comparisons due to the small sample size, followed by multiple testing corrections using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to determine cumulative survival percentages, and group differences were assessed by the log-rank test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Iterative phage adaptive selection for highly prevalent KL2-type A. baumannii strains in Chinese clinical settings

In June 2022, a 55-year-old male patient #1, in a vegetative state due to a hemorrhagic stroke, was hospitalised for exacerbated aspiration pneumonia. Sputum culture revealed a complex polymicrobial infection, involving P. aeruginosa, Candida tropicalis, and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (Fig. 1a). Despite the administration of intravenous (IV) antibiotic therapies encompassing various medications including amikacin, rifampicin, meropenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, levofloxacin, imipenem, isepamicin, and fluconazole, the lung infection persisted (Fig. 1a). The clinical indicators of patient #1, including white blood cell count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and body temperature, consistently indicated persistent inflammation (Fig. S1). By July 2022, patient #1 had developed a nosocomial CRAB infection, exhibiting limited susceptibility to last-resort antibiotics such as polymyxin B, minocycline, and tigecycline. Further IV antibiotic treatments with polymyxin B, isepamicin, and imipenem failed to eradicate the lung infection (Fig. 1a). Faced with this persistent MDR infection, clinicians turned to phage therapy specifically designed for CRAB, the primary pathogen resistant to all tested conventional treatments.

On day (D) −10, the A. baumannii isolate NAB01B was cultured and isolated from the sputum from patient #1 for therapeutic phage screening. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and further analysis with Kaptive65 suggested that NAB01B belongs to the KL2 capsule type. Our examination of the clinical CRAB isolates (n = 138) that we collected from five hospitals and one institute in Guangdong province revealed that KL2 is the most prevalent capsule type, accounting for 33.3% of cases (Fig. 1b). Similarly, our analysis of CRAB genomic data (n = 896) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database over the past five years indicated that KL2 is the predominant capsule type among clinical CRAB isolates in China, representing 17.7% of cases (Fig. 1c). Thus, developing a KL2-type specific phage cocktail is crucial for controlling CRAB infections in China.

Two phages, AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL4, selected from our in-house phage bank, were curated for their robust lytic capabilities against the isolate NAB01B (Fig. S2a) and formulated into the phage cocktail AB_CK1. Additionally, AB_CK1 exhibited potent biofilm inhibition (Fig. S2b) and biofilm dispersal activities (Fig. S2c). However, bacterial mutants resistant to the phages emerged within 6 h of in vitro phage-bacterial co-incubation (Fig. S2d), a common phenomenon supported by many studies.1, 2, 3 One efficient strategy to circumvent this resistance is to use phage-resistant bacterial mutants to isolate novel phages. These phages, which possess different infection mechanisms, then enable the rapid development of more robust cocktails.18,19 In this study, we designate this strategy as iterative Phage Adaptive Selection (iPAS). Although the selection pressures may differ between in vitro and in vivo environments, we postulate that the development of phage-resistant pathogenic mutants could follow similar patterns in both settings.27,66, 67, 68 Thus, we employed iPAS strategy to pre-select phages for in vitro phage-resistant isolates, thereby streamlining the response to clinical phage-resistant isolates in vivo (Fig. 1d). In the iPAS strategy, after 24 h of coincubation of the initial phage cocktail AB_CK1 and NAB01B, we randomly selected ten phage-resistant mutants. We then isolated two additional phages, AB_SZL2 and AB_SZL7, which retained lytic activity against these phage-resistant isolates (Fig. 1d). Particularly, AB_SZL2 lysed three out of the ten phage-resistant mutants, while AB_SZL7 lysed all ten, albeit with turbid plaques (Fig. 1e and Fig. S5b), suggesting that these phages target distinct bacterial receptors. Notably, both AB_SZL2 and AB_SZL7 were also capable of infecting the parental NAB01B (Fig. 1e). Further genome analysis reveals that these phages exhibit significant sequence similarity to previously characterised therapeutic or well-established lytic phages27,30,69 (Fig. S3). AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL4, from the genus Obolenskvirus, share 91% DNA sequence identity with the therapeutic phage Highwayman30 (Fig. S3a). AB_SZL2, from the genus Friunavirus, demonstrates 92–94% DNA sequence identity with several previously used therapeutic phages, including Tama, ABW311, Fanak, AbTP3phil1, and ABW13230 (Fig. S3b). AB_SZL7, from the genus Lokivirus, shows 77% DNA sequence identity with phage vB_AbaS_Loki70 (Fig. S3c). Despite the high sequence conservation of core gene sets, critical genetic determinants of host specificity, such as depolymerase-carrying tail proteins, vary between these phages and those reported in previous studies (Fig. S3). Furthermore, bioinformatic analysis identified no potential virulence, antibiotic resistance, or integrase sequences in these phage genomes.

Sequential phage therapy and the emergence of phage-resistant mutants in vivo

After receiving approval from the local hospital ethics committee and informed consent from the family, we initiated a clinical investigator-initiated trial (IIT) involving phage therapy for patient #1. The initial phage cocktail AB_CK1 was meticulously selected for the first round of phage therapy. Endotoxin levels were measured using the Limulus Amoebocyte Lysate assay and were found to be below the FDA-specified limit of 5 EU/kg/h. The detailed composition of the phage cocktail is provided in Table S2. From September 9, 2022 (D1), the customised phage preparation was administered via nebulisation (Jet nebuliser) to patient #1 every 12 h over seven days. During this time, antibiotic treatment with isepamicin continued and fluconazole was added due to the presence of fungus in the sputum. As expected, the emergence of phage-resistant isolates, NAB03B and NAB04B, was detected and collected during the phage treatment on D6 and D7 (Fig. 2a). Despite this resistance, patient #1 exhibited clinical improvements, including a decrease in sputum production and no evident infection in successive chest X-ray examinations.

Unfortunately, a relapse of patient #1's infection occurred one week after the discontinuation of phage therapy by D16, marked by a fever at 38.9 °C (Fig. S4a), and the re-emergence of A. baumannii in the sputum culture (Fig. 2a). Since isolate NAB03B exhibited resistance to the initial phage cocktail AB_CK1 (Fig. S5a), we introduced phage AB_SZL2, which effectively lyses the NAB03B isolate, and combined it with AB_SZL3 to create the AB_CK2 cocktail for the second round of phage therapy (Fig. 2a and c). This combination successfully inhibited the growth of NAB03B in vitro for 24 h without the development of resistance (Fig. S5c). In addition, AB_CK2 demonstrated strong biofilm inhibition (Fig. S5d) and dispersal activities (Fig. S5e). The second round of phage therapy commenced on September 27, 2022 (D19), with the phage cocktail preparation administered via nebulisation every 12 h over 24 days. During this round, another clinical phage-resistant isolate, NAB07B, was isolated on D36 (Fig. 2a and c). However, this isolate remained sensitive to phage AB_SZL7 (Fig. 2c and Fig. S5b). We then combined phages AB_SZL3, AB_SZL2, and AB_SZL7 to create the phage cocktail AB_CK3, which demonstrated potent growth inhibition against the clinical isolate NAB07B, for the third round of phage therapy (Fig. S5f). In addition, AB_CK3 demonstrated strong biofilm inhibition (Fig. S5g) and biofilm dispersal activities (Fig. S5h). The third round began on October 27, 2022 (D49), with AB_CK3 administered via nebulisation every 12 h for seven days. Post-therapy, no positive cultures for A. baumannii were detected (Fig. 2a), and amplicon-based 16 S rRNA gene analysis indicated a steady decline in the relative abundance of Acinetobacter in the sputum (Fig. 2b). The synergistic phage and antibiotic treatment successfully controlled the A. baumannii lung infection (Fig. 2a), with no adverse events associated with phage therapy reported throughout the hospital stay (Fig. S4). Over 28 months following this hospitalisation, patient #1 remained stable in a vegetative state with no recurrence of A. baumannii infections, indicating a complete clearance of the pathogen.

Consistent and concentrated genetic mutations in both in vitro and in vivo phage-resistant mutants

The phage-resistant mutants of A. baumannii, derived from both in vivo and in vitro experiments, were sequenced using short-read (DNBSEQ) and long-read (Nanopore) technologies to examine the mutations associated with phage resistance. All these isolates were found to have a chromosome size of approximately 4.0 Mb and two plasmids (Table S4), with slight deviations in chromosome length and identical plasmids across the isolates. Genomic Multi Locus Sequence Typing (MLST)71 analysis identified all these isolates as sequence type 2 (Pasteur scheme). Further analysis using Kaptive65 identified genetic regions associated with the predicted capsule locus of KL2 (Table S5) and the outer core locus of OCL1 (Table S5). The former is the genetic region that encodes the variable capsular polysaccharides (CPS), while the latter encodes the variable outer portion of the lipo-oligosaccharide (LOS)--a molecule in A. baumannii analogous to the lipopolysaccharide of other Gram-negative bacteria. Consistent with their extensive antibiotic resistance, the NAB01B genome has 32 antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) based on the CARD 2023 database41 (Table S6). Comparison of the phage-resistant mutants to the NAB01B prototype uncovered a limited number of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels (Table S7, Fig. 3a), suggesting a common ancestry rather than independent emergence events. These findings underscore a shared evolutionary trajectory and imply that the observed phage-resistant mutations likely arose from a convergent evolutionary process, highlighting the adaptability of A. baumannii in responding to phage selective pressure.

Interestingly, genomic analysis revealed that while the genetic mutations in these phage-resistant isolates are primarily concentrated in two chromosome regions involved in the biosynthesis of LOS and capsular CPS (Fig. 3b), two isolates (NAB01B-R8 and NAB01B-R9) were devoid of significant genetic alterations in these regions, pointing to alternative resistance mechanisms (Table S7). Specifically, mutations impacting LOS synthesis were identified in the NAB03B, NAB04B, NAB01B_R1, and NAB01B_R2 isolates (Fig. 3b and Fig. S6a), with the gtrOC3 gene within the OC locus encoding a glycosyltransferase enzyme. In clinical strain NAB03B, a G to T substitution at nucleotide position 180 of the gtrOC3 gene resulted in tryptophan to cysteine amino acid change. Strain NAB04B exhibited a frameshift mutation caused by deletion of nucleotide 514, leading to premature termination at positions 554–556 that truncates the protein by approximately 70 amino acids. Additionally, the in vitro-selected resistant strains NAB01B_R1 and NAB01B_R2 shared a similar phenotype, where deletion of nucleotide 536 induced a frameshift mutation causing premature termination at the identical position (554–556) in gtrOC3. CPS synthesis was affected by mutations in a separate group of isolates, including NAB06B, NAB07B, NAB01B_R3, NAB01B_R4, NAB01B_R5, NAB01B_R6, NAB01B_R7, and NAB01B_R10 (Fig. 3b, Table S7), with the affected genes mapping to the K locus. Notably, the isolate NAB01B_R3 exhibited a complete deletion of the K locus (Fig. 3a). Of particular interest, the most recent clinical isolate NAB07B harboured mutations within the gene wzy_KL2 encoding a repeating unit polymerase (Fig. S6b). In clinical strain NAB07B, a T to A substitution at position 723 of the wzy gene created a premature stop codon at the tyrosine residue, resulting in an 86-amino acid truncation of the encoded enzyme repeating unit polymerase. Similarly, the deletion of nucleotide 882 in phage-resistant mutants NAB01B_R4 and NAB01B_R7 and the insertion of nucleotide AT at position 774 induced a frameshift mutation, ultimately resulting in premature termination of the wzy gene expression. To investigate this genetic alteration, we designed specific primers and subsequent amplicon-based sequencing analysis revealed a progressive increase in the relative abundance of NAB07B in the sputum samples throughout phage therapy (Fig. S6c). This observation suggests that the emergence of NAB07B was likely driven by the selective pressure excreted by the therapeutic phage administration, underscoring the remarkable genetic plasticity of these bacteria in adapting to phage-mediated challenges in vivo.

Based on these genomic findings, which implicated both CPS and LOS as potential phage receptors, we performed targeted genetic manipulations to elucidate their specific functional roles. To investigate the role of CPS, we utilised the wzy_KL2 mutant NAB07B, as direct knockout of this gene in the NAB01B background was unsuccessful, likely due to its high AT content. Characterisation of the NAB07B mutant revealed that inactivation of wzy_KL2 led to a significant reduction in adsorption rates for phages AB_SZL3, AB_SZL4 and AB_SZL2 (Fig. S7a), resulting in a phage-resistant phenotype (Fig. 3c). This resistance was fully reversed upon genetic complementation of wzy_KL2 (Fig. 3c), thereby identifying the CPS structure as a primary determinant of susceptibility for both phages. Disruption of the gtrOC3 gene conferred resistance to both phages AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL4 across multiple strains (NAB01BΔgtrOC3, NAB03B, NAB04B, and NAB01B_R1), a phenotype that was nullified by complementation with a functional gtrOC3 allele (Fig. 3d). Subsequently, the contribution of LOS to phages AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL4 infection was assessed. Critically, this gtrOC3 deletion, while preventing productive infection, did not abolish initial phage adsorption to the cell surface (Fig. S7b). Collectively, these data support a model where CPS serves as the principal receptor for phage AB_SZL2. For phages AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL4, a dual-receptor mechanism is evident: CPS acts as the primary receptor for initial attachment, while the LOS structure is an essential secondary factor required for a subsequent, critical stage of infection. Interestingly, no strains with mutations in the K locus were detected after AB_CK1 cocktail treatment in patient #1, possibly due to the stochastic nature of mutation emergence and the limited resolution of single-colony screening from clinical samples.

Evolutionary trade-offs in in vitro and in vivo phage-resistant isolates

Genomic analysis revealed both in vitro and in vivo phage-resistant mutant isolates consistently harboured mutations in genes associated with the biosynthesis of LOS and CPS. Given that outer core LOS and CPS are major virulence factors in A. baumannii,25 we hypothesised that these mutations likely lead to alterations in drug susceptibility, biofilm production, serum resistance, virulence, and capsular structure of the phage-resistant mutants.

To investigate this, we first determined the antibiotic susceptibility profiles of clinical isolates and representative in vitro-selected phage-resistant mutants, selected based on their genetic mutations and phage sensitivity patterns (Table S7 and Fig. S8), against 11 clinically relevant antibiotics (Fig. 4a and Table S8). Interestingly, isolates NAB03B, NAB04B, and all in vitro-selected phage-resistant mutants exhibited a 2- to 4-fold reduction in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of sulbactum/cefoperazone compared to the original isolate, thereby achieving a sensitive status according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) breakpoints.48 Additionally, with the exception of NAB06B and NAB01B-R8, all phage-resistant mutants demonstrated varying degrees of reduced MICs to minocycline. Furthermore, the in vitro-selected phage-resistant mutants exhibited increased susceptibility to other beta-lactams. NAB01B-R3, which completely lost the K locus (Fig. 3a), showed reduced MICs for 4 additional antibiotics (Fig. 4a).

We next assessed biofilm formation and observed that it was significantly impaired in isolates NAB03B, NAB04B, NAB05B, NAB01B-R1, NAB01B-R3, and NAB01B-R9 (Fig. 4b). In rat serum complement-mediated bacterial killing assays, compared to the original isolate NAB01B, isolates NAB02B, NAB03B, NAB04B, NAB05B, NAB01B-R1, and NAB01B-R9 demonstrated resistance to the bactericidal effects of the complement system. In contrast, phage-resistant mutants NAB06B, NAB07B and NAB01B-R3, which harboured mutations in KL, along with NAB01B-R8, were susceptible to complement-mediated killing (Fig. 4c). None of the strains above were susceptible to the heat-inactivated rat serum (Fig. S10a).

To evaluate virulence, we conducted in vitro cytotoxicity assays using HeLa cells. The LDH levels were significantly reduced in all phage-resistant isolates compared to NAB01B (Fig. 4d). Further, in vivo virulence assessment using a Galleria mellonella infection model revealed that while approximately 75% of the G. mellonella died within 72 h post-inoculation with the original isolate NAB01B at a dose of 2∗105 colony forming units (CFU), the survival rate in all phage-resistant mutant groups remained around 80% (Fig. 4e). This results collectively indicate a marked reduction in virulence among both in vitro and in vivo phage-resistant mutants.

Finally, morphological examination under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed distinct differences in capsular structure. While the original isolate NAB01B exhibited an intact capsule structure, isolates NAB06B, NAB07B and NAB01B_R8 showed reduced capsule structure on their cell wall surface and NAB01B_R3 displayed a completely smooth cell wall surface, indicating a complete loss of surface structures (Fig. 4f).

In summary, these findings suggest that the phage-resistant isolates undergo significant trade-offs in drug susceptibility, biofilm production, and virulence. This evolutionary trade-off suggests that the emergence of phage resistance may come at the cost of altered pathogenic traits. These changes in pathogenicity, in turn, may have contributed to a more favourable outcome during phage therapy.

Development of a rapid identification method for KL2-type A. baumannii isolates to facilitate clinical application of the tailored phage cocktail AB_CK2

To further assess the therapeutic potential of the phages AB_SZL2, AB_SZL3 and AB_SZL7 selected with iPAS, we conducted a large-scale sensitivity assay on our local collection of CRAB isolates (n = 138) from five hospitals and one institute in Guangdong province. These isolates represent more than five distinct KL types. The results revealed that the majority (91.1%, 41/45) of CRAB isolates sensitive to the phage cocktail AB_CK2 (comprising phages AB_SZL2 and AB_SZL3) belong to the KL2 capsule type (Fig. 5a). Notably, 89.1% (41/46) of KL2 CRAB isolates were susceptible to AB_CK2 (Fig. 5a). Phylogenetic analysis of these KL2 CRAB isolates in our collection and those (n = 159) from the NCBI database showed that these isolates are widely dispersed across the tree (Fig. 5b), indicating that our KL2 CRAB isolates for the assay are genetically diverse and broadly representative. Further genomic comparison analysis revealed that AB_CK2-resistant isolates (n = 5) from our collection harboured similar genetic mutations in the OC and K loci as previously observed (Fig. 5c), suggesting the presence of analogous evolutionary trade-offs in these isolates as well (Figs. Importantly, most (81.2%, 112/138) of the CRAB isolates, regardless of their KL types, demonstrated sensitivity to phage AB_SZL7 (Fig. 5a). Despite this efficacy, phage AB_SZL7 consistently formed turbid plaques (Fig. 1e). While plaque turbidity is often indicative of a temperate lifestyle,72 evidence suggest that phage AB_SZL7 is lytic. Its genome lacks an integrase or other core genes required for lysogeny and shares high sequence identity with the characterised lytic phage vB_AbaS_Loki70 (Fig. S3c). Therefore, we attribute the plaque turbidity to factors, such as the activation of host defence systems like CRISPR-Cas immunity.73 Nevertheless, the formation of unstable, turbid plaques rendered AB_SZL7 suboptimal for the preparation of standardised, high-titre lysates. Consequently, we employed the AB_CK2 cocktail, consisting of phage AB_SZL2 and AB_SZL3, for targeting KL2-type CRAB infections.

These sensitivity results, along with comparative phage genomics analysis, highlight the unique genetic features of our phage cocktail AB_CK2, especially in their distinct host specificity determinants, setting it apart from other therapeutic phages (Fig. S3).

The broad inhibitory activity of these phages against KL2 CRAB isolates inspired us to develop a rapid identification method for KL2 CRAB isolates, potentially shortening the clinical waiting period for patients and bolstering the efficacy of phage therapy. Our analysis within the Kaptive database28 revealed that the gtr3 and gtr4 genes are specific to KL2 and KL81 types, with the latter additionally harbouring the pgt1 gene (Fig. 5d, Table S9). This discovery suggests that concurrent detection of gtr3 and gtr4, alongside the absence of pgt1, could serve as a diagnostic signature for KL2 isolates. Accordingly, we crafted specific primers targeting the presence of gtr3 and the absence of pgt1 as markers for KL2. This was further validated using PCR assays on the 138 clinical isolates and ATCC17978, and the gtr3 gene was identified in 47 isolates (KL2 type), with the pgt1 gene detected in only one isolate (KL81 type) (Table S10 and Fig. S11), consistent with genomic analysis results. This confirmed that PCR assay is a viable and rapid method for identifying KL2 CRAB isolates, facilitating more efficient clinical applications of these phages. We then established a paradigmatic protocol for clinical use for the phage cocktail AB_CK2 against KL2 CRAB infections (Fig. 5d).

To further validate the clinical potential of AB_CK2, we administered the phage cocktail to the 83-year-old male patient #2 with pneumonia caused by A. baumannii infection under compassionate use. PCR assays indicated that the clinical A. baumannii isolate (NAB09B) belonged to the KL2 capsule type, which was confirmed by genomic sequencing (Table S11). Sensitivity assays confirmed the isolate's susceptibility to AB_CK2 (Table S11). Subsequently, the phage cocktail AB_CK2 was administered via nebulisation twice daily for 21 days, alongside antibiotics tailored to the antibiogram of co-infecting pathogens (Table S12, Supplementary information for patient #2). Microbiological recovery was achieved without the detection of phage-resistant isolates (Fig. 5e), as supported by genome comparison analysis of NAB09B and NAB10B which revealed no mutations. Furthermore, amplicon-based 16 S rRNA gene analysis showed a steady decline in the relative abundance of Acinetobacter in sputum samples (Fig. 5e). Throughout the entire treatment period, close monitoring by medical professionals and hospital staff ensured the safety and assessment of any adverse events or signals (Table S12). This case further underscores the promise of AB_CK2 as a targeted therapeutic option for KL2 CRAB infections.

Discussion

A primary concern in phage therapy is the potential for target bacteria to develop resistance to administered phages, representing a fundamental limitation of this therapeutic approach.1, 2, 3 In this study, we addressed this challenge by employing the iPAS strategy to design phage cocktails that target in vitro phage resistance, thereby ensuring clinical therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, our study demonstrated a strong alignment between the phenotypic and genomic characteristics of in vitro-selected phage-resistant mutants and those isolated from clinical settings, alongside the observation of evolutionary trade-offs in phage-resistant bacterial mutants. These findings provide valuable support for the hypothesis that in vitro phage-resistant mutants can predict clinical resistance, offering a promising avenue for phage therapy optimisation. Furthermore, we found that while numerous types of A. baumannii strains were detected in clinical settings, the KL2 type demonstrated the highest prevalence in local hospitals and across China.

Studies suggest that phage-resistant bacterial mutants could be leveraged as an advantage rather than a drawback in phage therapy.15,26 Our findings align with this paradigm, revealing that both in vitro and in vivo-derived phage-resistant isolates exhibit significant trade-offs. These trade-offs are characterised by increased sensitivity to antimicrobial agents, reduced virulence, and diminished capacity for biofilm formation and serum resistance. Such phenomena may explain the observed microbiological recoveries in these cases, highlighting the potential benefits of phage resistance in therapeutic contexts.

The acquisition of phage-resistant mutants through genetic mutation of bacterial surface structures, crucial for phage adsorption, is a well-documented mechanism.26,27,74 Both LOS and CPSs are pivotal cell-surface factors for A. baumannii.26,27,74 Our results also indicate that both LOS and CPS serve as major cell-surface receptors for phage AB_SZL2 and phage AB_SZL3. The genomic analysis revealed mutations in several genes, particularly those involved in the biosynthesis of LOS and CPS in these phage-resistant isolates. These genetic mutations, disrupting LOS and capsule structures, underlie the observed trade-offs. Moreover, the unique cases of R8 and R9 demonstrate that bacteria can evolve resistance through alternative, non-mutational pathways that, while not altering the DNA sequence, still converge on modifying the key receptor structures (CPS and LOS) and are invariably accompanied by distinct fitness trade-offs. This non-mutational strategy aligns with prior reports of phage resistance driven by either non-genetic epigenetic regulation or phase variation, which dynamically regulate the surface expression of the CPS and LOS structures.75, 76, 77 The deployment of phage cocktail AB_CK2, therefore, not only lessens the bacterial load but also selects for mutants that, while resistant to phages, may exhibit reduced virulence, increased antibiotic susceptibility, and greater susceptibility to immune clearance, suggesting that such changes, particularly in pathogenicity, may have contributed to favourable clinical outcomes.

The diverse CPS structure of A. baumannii, governed by the K locus, encompasses up to 237 distinct KL types.28,29 Compared to other types, the KL2 type of A. baumannii showed higher levels of antibiotic resistance, stronger association with severe clinical outcomes and higher patient mortality, posing a significant public health threat.30,78 Over the past five years, the KL2 type has emerged as the most prevalent capsule type among CRAB isolates in China. A recent global epidemic study also confirms that KL2 is one of the most dominant KL types of A. baumannii globally in recent years.30 In this context, a tailored phage cocktail like AB_CK2, which demonstrates lytic activity against 89.1% of tested KL2 CRAB clinical isolates, represents a highly promising therapeutic strategy. The isolates resistant to AB_CK2 exhibit impaired cell surface structures, suggesting a reduced risk of severe infections. Furthermore, according to the Kaptive database,28 KL2 A. baumannii possesses unique genetic features that can be identified through rapid PCR assays, enabling the precise application of phage therapy in clinical settings. This approach is particularly valuable in critical settings; for example, PCR of sputum samples can offer preliminary phage selection guidance when culture results are delayed. Nevertheless, translating this method into a clinical diagnostic kit requires further investigation. Moreover, certain phages can target other specific KL types.30 We propose that, by the iPAS strategy, tailored phage cocktails can be developed to target prevailing epidemic KL-type strains, thus offering a generalisable solution to combat MDR A. baumannii infections.

While our phage cocktail AB_CK2 can target receptors including CPS and LOS, the specific characteristics of phage AB_SZL3 highlight an opportunity for further optimisation. Although phage AB_SZL3 can recognise LOS, our findings suggest that LOS functions only as a secondary receptor; the phage still requires CPS to initiate a productive infection. This dependency on CPS underscores the critical need to develop or isolate phages that can primarily and independently target LOS or alternative surface structures. Such phages are essential for effectively combating capsule variants or strains that develop resistance to CPS-targeting phages. Therefore, although targeting prevalent capsular types like KL2 is a valuable initial strategy, achieving robust and broad-spectrum phage therapy will ultimately require a multi-receptor approach. A key future direction is to incorporate phages with diverse primary receptor specificities--especially those that target LOS independently--to enhance the efficacy, coverage, and resilience of phage-based treatments against bacterial evolution. Moreover, to ensure therapeutic efficacy, an effective phage cocktail must not only target the correct receptor but also possess the ability to evade host intracellular defences (such as restriction modification and CRISPR-Cas immunity).79,80

While our phage cocktail ABCK2 targeting KL2-type A. baumannii demonstrated therapeutic potential in two patients, this study has several limitations. First, the exclusively Chinese origin of all samples limits its generalisability for global A. baumannii treatment. Second, both patients were male, leaving the tolerability in female patients unexplored. More critically, the small sample size designates this as a proof-of-concept study, necessitating larger-scale randomised controlled trials to further validate its safety and efficacy.

In summary, while phage therapy has shown promise in case studies for MDR infections, its broader clinical application has been met with inconsistent success. Our research utilises the iPAS strategy, presenting the tailored phage cocktail to address phage resistance in the treatment of lung infections caused by CRAB. The optimised phage cocktail was further validated in patient #2 and demonstrated significant potential in effectively treating KL2 A. baumannii infections. In the meantime, the safety of our preparation was also demonstrated by the absence of adverse effects in both patients throughout the treatment course. Our research, which combines precise bacterial profiling with robust, standardised phage formulations and the rapid typing method for CRAB, provides valuable insights for designing future controlled clinical trials. Building on these insights, the logical next step is a prospective cohort study that applies our rapid typing method to screen patients in real-time, thereby guiding the administration of targeted, standardised phage formulations for a rigorous evaluation of their clinical efficacy. Such trials are urgently needed to advance the field of phage therapy and establish its role as a reliable therapeutic option for combating MDR bacterial infections.
