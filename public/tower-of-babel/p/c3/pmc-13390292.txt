Aging under scarcity and insecurity: chronic disease, functional limitation, and basic need material insecurity among older adults in Haiti
Authors: James Lachaud, Ruth Climat, Luckny Zephyr, Bogentson André, Marilisse Neptune Rouzier, Nixon Calixte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults in low-income and fragile states often age with intersecting burdens of chronic disease, functional decline, and material deprivation, yet they remain largely invisible in research and policy. In Haiti, recurrent political crises, violence, and weak health and social protection systems may amplify late-life inequities, but national data are scarce. We examined the distribution and social determinants of health, functioning, and material insecurity among older Haitian adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from adults aged ≥ 60 years (n = 1,408) drawn from a 2024 nationally representative household survey on hypertension, plant use, and nutrition in Haiti. The survey used a two-stage stratified sampling design across all 10 departments, with household listing in selected census tracts and inclusion of all resident adults. We estimated weighted prevalences and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for self-rated health, vision and mobility limitations, hypertension, diabetes, psychosocial stress, food insecurity, water insecurity, and underweight (BMI < 23 kg/m²). Multivariable logistic and multinomial logistic regression models assessed associations with age, sex, marital status, rural residence, household socioeconomic status, and residence in areas classified as unsafe due to armed-group control. Analyses accounted for clustering at the household level.ResultsOlder adults experienced substantial multiple burdens. Poor self-rated health was reported by 29.9% (95% CI 26.3–33.8), vision impairment by 42.3% (95% CI 37.8–46.9), mobility limitation by 27.3% (95% CI 23.3–31.7), and high stress by 31.0% (95% CI 26.8–35.6). Hypertension affected 62.5% (95% CI 57.8–66.9) and diabetes 16.6% (95% CI 13.1–20.7). Food insecurity reached 53.0% (95% CI 48.3–57.6), water insecurity 28.4% (95% CI 24.1–33.2), and underweight status 60.5% (95% CI 55.7–65.2). Older age predicted poorer health, functional limitations, and stress. Women had higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.45–2.32) and tended toward worse functional outcomes. Rural residence was associated with poorer self-rated health (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.46–2.47) and higher food insecurity, while higher socioeconomic status and living with a partner were partially protective. Residence in unsafe areas was linked to higher stress, hypertension, and food insecurity. Supplementary sex-stratified models suggested broadly similar patterns across men and women, although some associations differed in magnitude.ConclusionsAging in Haiti is characterized by a high burden of chronic disease, functional impairment, and basic-need deprivation, patterned by sex, place, and household resources. These findings highlight the urgency of developing integrated chronic care, robust social protection, and food and water security policies that explicitly prioritize older adults in fragile health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02881-4.

Introduction

Aging in poverty presents major public health and social policy challenges, particularly in fragile countries such as Haiti [1, 2]. Older adults face overlapping forms of vulnerability driven by economic deprivation, limited access to healthcare, weak or absent social protection, and exposure to environmental and political instability [3–5]. In such settings, social and material vulnerability, such as poverty, limited access to health services and social protection, undermine the ability to meet even basic needs in later life, producing elevated health risks, restricted access to care, and fragile social support, often with little policy response [6].

These conditions are especially acute in Haiti, where chronic political and economic instability, recurrent natural disasters, and a fragile health system constrain service delivery and population health [7–9]. Older adults of 65 years or older, who account for approximately 4.7% of the population in 2024 [10], frequently lack access to essential services for managing common chronic and age-related conditions [1, 11]. Geriatric care is largely absent from the national health system and out-of-pocket costs for consultations and medications remain prohibitive [4, 8, 12, 13]. Consequently, chronic and age-related health conditions often remain undiagnosed or untreated, leading to functional limitations, disabilities, and premature mortality [14]. These medical vulnerabilities are compounded by pervasive food insecurity, unreliable access to clean water, inadequate housing, and increasing strain on informal family support systems shaped by poverty, migration, and changing household structures [15–20]. Together, these factors undermine the ability of older adults to age with dignity and independence.

Despite this high level of need, aging in Haiti remains largely invisible in research, policy, and programming [3, 21]. National and international health agendas continue to prioritize maternal and child health, infectious diseases, and youth, reflecting Haiti’s young demographic profile and broader global health priorities [2]. Consequently, aging-related needs are deprioritized, and the health system is not structured to address the demands of a growing older population. This marginalization is reinforced by the scarcity of age-disaggregated data and absence of routine national surveillance of older adults. Hence, comprehensive data on the prevalence of these conditions and their sociodemographic correlates among older Haitian adults are lacking, which limits the capacity to evaluate chronic disease burden, identify service gaps, and plan targeted interventions [21].

This study addresses these gaps using novel data from a 2024 nationally representative survey of adults aged ≥ 18 years in Haiti on hypertension, plant use, and diet. This study aimed to (1) estimate the prevalence of chronic diseases and functional limitations among older adults in Haiti, (2) assess the extent of material insecurity experienced by this population, and (3) explore the sociodemographic factors associated with these outcomes. We hypothesized that older Haitian women are more likely to experience poorer health and food and water material insecurity than men; that risk factors differ between rural and urban areas; and that marital status buffers certain vulnerabilities, particularly food insecurity. We examined food insecurity, water insecurity, and BMI alongside chronic disease and functional outcomes because these are not peripheral social conditions in later life. In fragile settings, they represent interrelated dimensions of material deprivation that can shape nutritional status, disease management, psychosocial stress, physical functioning, and the ability to live independently. Considering these domains together is therefore important for understanding how social and material vulnerability shapes health and functioning in old age. This approach also helps identify older adults whose risks are concentrated across multiple forms of vulnerability rather than captured by household socioeconomic status alone.

Methods

This study employed a cross-sectional design using data from a nationally representative hypertension, plant use, and diet survey conducted in Haiti in 2024 [22]. The survey applied a two-stage stratified sampling strategy to achieve broad geographic and demographic representation across all 10 regions of Haiti, locally referred to as geographic departments. Stratification was carried out according to department and area of residence (urban vs. rural). First, enumeration areas (census tracts) were selected randomly with probability-proportional-to-size sampling. Because Haiti lacks recent national census data, a comprehensive listing of all households in the selected census tracts was conducted., which the number of residents, their biological sex and age as well as GPS coordinates of the households were recorded. The list of households was used as a sampling frame to randomly select the households for the survey (second stage). In each selected household, all household member aged ≥ 18, who were present and consented to the survey were interviewed. In cases of absence of adult household members, the enumerators revisited the household three times. However, certain zones in the West and Artibonite departments were excluded because they were classified as “red zones” due to being under the control of armed groups and considered unsafe for fieldwork [22].

The national survey included 7,040 adults aged ≥ 18 years, and survey weights were constructed to restore the national representativeness of the adult population [22]. The present analysis was restricted to a subsample of 1,408 respondents aged ≥ 60 years.

Ethical considerations

The survey protocol and study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the State University of Haiti (#CER-UEH/RC05-11/2023-004). The data were collected from June 01 to July 2024. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, in accordance with the ethical standards for research involving human subjects.

Data collection involved structured interviews and biometric measurements of participants. The questionnaire encompassed domains, such as sociodemographic characteristics, health conditions, food and water insecurity, and psychosocial stress levels. Blood pressure (BP) was measured after participants had been seated at rest for 5 min. BP was then recorded three times at one-minute intervals, and the average of the three readings was computed and used in the analyses. Hypertension or being hypertensive was defined as a mean systolic pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg or a mean diastolic pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg) or based on prior medical diagnosis. Self-reported health data, including perceived general health status, were measured on a scale of 1–5 from very poor to excellent, and dichotomized as poor self-reported health (1 if very poor and poor) or otherwise (0). Vision and mobility impairments were also assessed by asking, ‘On a scale of 1 to 3, do you have any of the following difficulties or physical impairments?’ specifically regarding the ability “to see without glasses” and “to go up and down stairs.” The response options were “1 very little,” “2 a lot,” and “3 not at all.” These variables were dichotomized as poor vision or limited mobility (1 if the answer was a lot) and 0 otherwise. Finally, given the prevalence of type II diabetes in Haiti, the participants were asked whether they had been diagnosed with diabetes or had experienced serious symptoms of diabetes that concerned them. This variable was dichotomized as 1 if diagnosed or diabetes concerns were present and 0 otherwise.

Nutritional status was evaluated using Body Mass Index (BMI) and classified according to geriatric-specific guidelines [23]: underweight (< 23 kg/m²), normal (23–29.9 kg/m²), and overweight or obese (≥ 30 kg/m²). Food insecurity was assessed using the two-item Hunger Vital Sign screening tool, which includes the questions: “Within the past 4 weeks, we worried whether our food would run out before we got money to buy more?” and “Within the past 4 weeks, the food we bought just didn’t last, and we didn’t have money to get more?” [24]. The response options were coded as 0 for “Often true,” 1 for “Sometimes true,” and 3 for “Never true.” The response codes were reversed and summed up to compute the food insecurity score, which ranged from 0 to 6, with a higher score indicating greater food insecurity. Food insecurity was dichotomized as 1 if the score was ≥ 3 and 0 otherwise. Water insecurity was measured using the Four-Item Household Water Insecurity Experiences Scale [25], which assesses frequency over the last four weeks for “worries about water,” “activity interruption or rescheduling,” “access to drinking water,” and “water for handwashing and hygiene.” Responses were scaled from zero (never occurred) to four (always or 20 times or more). The responses were summed to compute the water insecurity score, which was dichotomized as 1 if the score was ≥ 8 and 0 otherwise. Finally, psychosocial distress was evaluated using a four-item version of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4) [26]. A stress score ranging from 0 to 16 was generated by summing the responses, with a higher score indicating a higher stress level. Stress level was dichotomized as 1 if the score was ≥ 9 and 0 otherwise.

First, the prevalence and 95% confidence interval (CIs) for each outcome were estimated. Binary and multinomial logistic regression models were used to examine associations between sociodemographic factors and key outcomes. Logistic regression models were applied to dichotomous outcome variables (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, poor self-rated health status, visual and mobility impairment, stress, food, and water insecurity), with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Multinomial logistic regression models were used to analyze the relationship between BMI and other variables. The Multinomial odds ratios (MOR) for being underweight or obese compared with normal weight were estimated. All models adjusted for age group, sex (woman vs. man), marital status (married/living together vs. other), residence (urban vs. rural), and household socioeconomic status (SES). SES was derived via principal components analysis of housing characteristics; access to utilities/services; and ownership of market goods, land, and livestock, and then grouped into quintiles (1 = lowest to 5 = better-off). Finally, a dummy variable was computed to consider unsafe regions (1), namely the West and Artibonite departments, where multiple neighborhoods are under armed groups control, which forced the internal displacement of part of the population, vs. (0) other regions. Household socioeconomic status, rural residence, marital status, and residence in unsafe areas were included as separate indicators of household resources, geographic context, informal support, and exposure to insecurity.

To account for potential intrahousehold correlations among participants, all regression models were estimated using cluster-robust standard errors at the household level. In supplementary analyses, we assessed heterogeneity in associations using sex-stratified models for health outcomes and for material insecurity and nutritional status outcomes and conducted joint tests comparing coefficients across men and women (see Appendix Tables A1, A2, and A3). We also compared patterns across two age thresholds, ≥ 60 and ≥ 65 years (see supplementary file). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Socioeconomic characteristics

Among the 1,408 individuals aged ≥ 60 years, the mean age was 68.3 years (SD = 0.27). The weighted sample comprised 56.4% women; 55.3% resided in urban areas, and 50.5% were married or cohabiting. Most of the senior population lived in households classified in the lowest socioeconomic quintile (36.5%) or better-off households (48.6%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of health and poverty-related conditions

The findings indicate a significant health burden for older adults in Haiti. Approximately one-third (29.9%; 95% CI: 26.3–33.8) of individuals aged ≥ 60 years assessed their health as poor. Visual impairment was notably prevalent, affecting 42.3% of the sample (95% CI: 37.81–46.92), and 27.3% (95% CI: 23.3–31.7) reported mobility issues. Chronic conditions were also prevalent; 16.6% of participants self-reported having been diagnosed with or having serious symptoms of diabetes (95% CI: 13.13–20.69), and 62.5% (95% CI: 57.84–66.87) had hypertension. Additionally, 31.0% of participants reported experiencing psychosocial stress (95% CI: 26.77–35.58). Although 29.9% rated their health as poor, this estimate should be interpreted alongside the substantially higher prevalences of hypertension, visual impairment, food insecurity, and underweight status. Taken together, these indicators suggest that self-rated health alone may understate cumulative health and material need in this population.

Economic vulnerability was evident in the high level of food insecurity, with over half of the older adult population (53.0%: 95% CI: 48.3–57.57) reporting it. Nutritional status, as measured by the body mass index (BMI), also indicated serious concerns. The majority of respondents (60.5%) were classified as underweight, with a BMI below 23 kg/m² (95% CI: 55.67–65.23). An additional 31.5% fell within the normal BMI range of 23–29.9 kg/m² (95% CI: 27.02–36.44), and only 7.9% were categorized as overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²; 95% CI: 5.98–10.4). Water insecurity was reported by 28.4% of older adults (95% CI: 24.09–33.19), indicating limited access to adequate and reliable water sources (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate associations with health-related indicators

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several statistically significant associations between sociodemographic characteristics and health outcomes among older adults. There was an age-gradient association with poor self-reported health, visual impairment, mobility limitation, and stress, with older age groups generally showing higher odds than adults aged 60 to 64 years. Compared with men, women had higher odds of poorer health outcomes overall, although only significantly association in the pooled models was for hypertension (OR, 1.8; 95% CI: 1.45–2.32, p = 0.001). Associations between women and poor self-reported health and mobility limitation were marginally significant, with OR = 1.3 (95% CI: 0.99–1.59, p = 0.064) and OR = 1.3 (95% CI: 0.98–1.62, p = 0.073), respectively. Being married or cohabiting was associated with lower odds of hypertension (OR, 0.7; 95% CI: 0.57–0.93; p = 0.011).

Older adults living in rural areas were more likely to poor self-reported health status (OR: 1.9, 95% CI: 1.46–2.47, p = 0.001) but had lower odds of vision impairment (OR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.6–0.99, p = 0.046). Residence in unsafe areas was associated with lower odds of poor self-reported health (OR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.41–0.74, p = 0.001) and visual impairment (OR: 0.6, 95% CI: 0.43–0.74, p = 0.001); however, they were more likely to have hypertension (OR: 1.5, 95% CI: 1.13–1.96, p = 0.004) and stress (OR: 1.6, 95% CI: 0.43–0.74, p = 0.001). Compared with the lowest SES quintile, living in better-off households was associated with lower odds of poor self-reported health (OR: 0.7, 95% CI: 0.54–0.99, p = 0.047) and high stress levels (OR: 0.7, 95% CI: 0.48–0.88, p = 0.006).

Supplementary sex-stratified models broadly supported the pooled findings while suggesting that some associations may differ in magnitude by sex (Appendix Table A2). However, joint tests did not show strong evidence of systematic coefficient differences between men and women for most health outcomes; the main exception was suggestive heterogeneity for visual impairment (joint F = 1.62, p = 0.087).

Multivariate associations with food and water insecurity

The pooled models showed that material insecurity and nutritional status were patterned by social and geographic factors (Table 4) Age was not significantly associated with food insecurity, but there was an age gradient in BMI, with older adults increasingly less likely to be in the normal BMI category than underweight. Compared with adults aged 60 to 64 years, those aged 65–69 years old and 80 + years were likely to have normal weight rather than be underweight (Multi logit odds ratios: 0.7, 95% CI: 0.48–0.95, p = 0.022; 0.7, 95% CI: 0.24–0.57, p = 0.001, respectively). Women did not have significantly higher odds of food insecurity, but they were more likely to be normal weight (MOR 1.6, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.14, p < 0.001), more likely to be overweight or obese rather than underweight (RRR 2.4, 95% CI 1.48 to 3.87, p < 0.001), and more likely to report water insecurity (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.65, p = 0.014). Being married or cohabiting was associated with lower odds of food insecurity (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.89, p = 0.005) and higher odds of being in the normal BMI category rather than underweight (MOR 1.3, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.74, p = 0.041).

Place of residence showed strong associations with multiple outcomes. Rural areas was associated with higher odds of food insecurity (OR, 1.8; 95% CI: 1.35–2.39, p = 0.001), lower odds of normal weight (OR, 0.6; 95% CI: 0.49–0.85; p = 0.002), lower odds overweight or obesity (OR, 0.5; 95% CI: 0.32–0.87; p = 0.001), and lower odds of water insecurity (OR, 0.5; 95% CI: 0.34–0.65; p = 0.001). Residence in unsafe regions was associated with higher odds of food insecurity (OR: 1.9, 95% CI: 1.43–2.65, p = 0.001). SES was not consistently associated with food insecurity or BMI, although better-off households had lower odds of overweight or obesity (MOR 0.6, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.97, p = 0.039) and higher odds of water insecurity (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.41 to 2.96, p < 0.001).

Supplementary sex-stratified models for food insecurity, BMI, and water insecurity again suggested some differences in magnitude, particularly for rural residence and food insecurity, but joint tests did not indicate statistically significant overall differences in coefficients between men and women (Appendix Table A3).

Discussion

This study indicates that aging in Haiti is shaped by health, economic, and political conditions, rather than chronological age alone. Guided by a Social Determinants of Health (SDH) framework, the findings show that health status and material security in later life are jointly shaped by age, gender, socioeconomic position, rural residence, household structure, and exposure to insecurity [27]. Chronic conditions, functional limitations, and sensory impairments, including elevated rates of hypertension, mobility limitations, and visual impairment, are highly prevalent. Nearly one-third of respondents self-reported poor health. This pattern aligns with evidence that in low- and middle-income countries, noncommunicable diseases and age-related disabilities are now central drivers of morbidity and loss of independence in older age [1, 2, 11, 13, 28]. In the Haitian context, weak geriatric capacity, limited systems for chronic disease management, high out-of-pocket health expenditures, and persistent instability in service delivery mean that these conditions are often underdiagnosed, poorly controlled, and socially consequential [1, 8, 9, 13, 20].

At first glance, the fact that 29.9% of respondents rated their health as poor may appear lower than expected given Haiti’s well-documented conditions of poverty and pervasive material hardship [1, 8, 9, 20]. However, self-rated health should not be interpreted in isolation. In this sample, hypertension, vision impairment, food insecurity, and underweight status were all substantially more prevalent. This discrepancy suggests that self-rated health may understate morbidity in contexts where chronic deprivation, limited diagnosis, and constrained expectations normalize a high burden of symptoms and functional loss.

The age gradients for health were strong and consistent. Adults aged ≥ 75 years had markedly higher odds of reporting poor health, visual problems, mobility limitations, and high stress than those in their early sixties. This pattern suggests that ageing in Haiti is a cumulative process. This likely reflects long-term exposure to poverty, nutritional insecurity, physically demanding work, repeated health shocks, and limited access to preventive or specialty care across the course [23, 27]. The higher levels of stress reported among the oldest respondents further indicated that psychological strain is an important and often under-recognized dimension of aging in fragile health systems [26]. Such strain may arise from the loss of physical autonomy, bereavement, exposure to insecurity, and concern about future care needs.

Sex-related differences were also observed. Older women had significantly higher odds of hypertension than men and tended to exhibit worse functional outcomes than men. This finding is consistent with the known patterns of elevated cardiometabolic risk among postmenopausal women and reflects the long-term toll of intensive caregiving and informal labor performed under chronic material scarcity [23, 29]. Simultaneously, the results suggest a dual burden of malnutrition. Underweight status was widespread; however, women were more likely than men to fall into higher BMI categories. This suggests that cardiometabolic risk in older Haitian women is emerging alongside persistent nutritional deprivation among men and poorer households [15, 16, 28] Exploratory sex-stratified models were consistent with this interpretation. Although joint tests did not show strong sex differences across most pooled coefficients, the supplementary models suggested that some disadvantages may be more pronounced among women, particularly with respect to linving in rural area, food insecurity and selected functional outcomes. These patterns should be interpreted cautiously, but they support treating gender as an important axis of vulnerability rather than only a control variable.

The household structure appears to function as a protective factor. Being married or partnered was associated with lower odds of hypertension and lower odds of food insecurity. In Haiti, where formal elder care, pension structures, and long-term supportive services are extremely limited or absent [6, 17, 18], these findings indicate that partners frequently provide practical care, assistance with medication access, emotional support, and pooled economic resources. By implication, widowhood, separation, or living alone in older age may sharply increase the likelihood of unmanaged chronic illnesses and material deprivation [9, 19, 30, 31].

Place of residence introduces additional inequalities. Older adults residing in rural areas had substantially higher odds of food insecurity and poorer health but were less likely to be overweight or obese. This profile is consistent with chronic caloric and income deprivation in rural zones, where livelihoods are unstable and services are limited rather than over-nutrition. Prior studies in Haiti have linked food insecurity and psychological distress to land precarity, market volatility, and erosion of agricultural livelihoods [15, 16]. Rural residence in this study was also associated with somewhat lower reported water insecurity. This likely reflects greater physical access to local water sources in these regions. However, this should not be interpreted as a reliable access to safe, clean, and low-burden water. The tool used to measure water insecurity captures perceived sufficiency, but may not capture the contamination risk or the physical labor required to obtain and transport water. These burdens are particularly consequential for older adults with mobility limitations [32, 33].

Older adults living in unsafe areas had distinct risk profiles. Residence in these areas was associated with higher odds of hypertension, elevated stress, and food insecurity, consistent with chronic exposure to violence, displacement, and disrupted services. Simultaneously, respondents in these settings were less likely to report poor health or vision problems. This apparent paradox may reflect limited access to diagnosis, constrained expectations of what constitutes illness, and the normalization of chronic symptoms when healthcare is dangerous, unpredictable, or unaffordable [13, 20, 34]. In such settings, describing one’s health as acceptable may indicate that one is stable enough to function at present rather than being clinically well. Consequently, self-rated health may underestimate the true need [8, 11].

Material insecurity was also prevalent and analytically central to this study. Food insecurity, water insecurity, and BMI were examined alongside chronic and functional outcomes because they represent interrelated pathways through which social and material vulnerability affects health in later life [16, 26, 32]. In older adults, inadequate food access can worsen undernutrition, fatigue, and medication adherence; water insecurity can intensify stress, disrupt hygiene and disease management, and increase the physical burden of obtaining water; and BMI provides a complementary marker of the nutritional consequences of scarcity [16, 26, 27, 32]. Taken together, these indicators capture not only poverty, but also pathways linking material deprivation to chronic disease control, functional decline, and reduced quality of life.

Socioeconomic status provided only partial protection. Older adults from relatively better-off households were less likely to report poor health and high stress but were not consistently protected against food and water insecurities. This suggests that household-level resources are insufficient in a context where health care, medication, and even basic goods remain rationed by price, availability, and physical access [9, 13]. Private resources appear to be unable to compensate for weak public infrastructure in the context of chronic illness and disability in later life [8, 18].

This study has several important implications. First, these findings refine, rather than simply repeat, general calls for better geriatric care in low-income settings. The value added of this analysis is that it identifies which burdens are most prevalent among older adults in Haiti and how they are socially patterned. While hypertension requires immediate priority because of its very high prevalence and downstream cardiovascular consequences, mobility limitation and vision impairment also deserve early intervention because they directly affect autonomy and the ability to secure food, water, care, and social support. The findings further show that these burdens are not evenly distributed: rural residence, lower household resources, lack of partnership, and residence in unsafe areas are important fault lines for policy targeting. Policy targeting should therefore consider multiple indicators of vulnerability rather than household socioeconomic status alone. Older adults may require priority attention when low household resources, rural residence, lack of a partner, residence in unsafe areas, food or water insecurity, underweight status, hypertension, mobility limitation, or visual impairment occur together. These findings support outreach models that combine chronic disease screening, medication support, nutrition assistance, water-access interventions, and functional support, particularly in rural and insecure areas.

Similarly, food and water insecurity should not be treated as background social conditions. In this study, they were common and socially patterned, which suggests that older adults should be explicitly included in nutrition, water, and social protection responses. This adds to the existing Haiti literature, which documents health-system weakness and hardship financing, by providing nationally representative evidence on how prevalent were chronic disease, functioning, and basic-need deprivation specifically among older adults [15, 32, 35].

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow the assessment of trends or changes over time. Self-reported outcomes are subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and underreporting, particularly in stigmatized or undiagnosed conditions. In addition, areas classified as unsafe, including parts of the capital region, could not be fully included in the survey; the burdens reported here may therefore underestimate the severity of conditions among the most exposed older adults Finally, the primary pooled models estimate average association across the sample and may hinder meaningful heterogeneity across social groups. To partially address this issue, we added sex-stratified supplementary models and joint tests of coefficient equality. However, further stratified analyses by rural versus urban residence and by socioeconomic level would be valuable in future work, particularly with larger samples or more targeted outcome models.

Despite these limitations, the present study had important strengths. It draws on novel data from a nationally representative sample of older adults in Haiti, which supports broad population relevance. The use of standardized instruments, including validated tools for food and water insecurity, improves the reliability of key measures. The analysis also considered multiple domains of later-life vulnerability, including chronic disease, functioning, psychosocial stress, food insecurity, water insecurity, and BMI. Finally, the Social Determinants of Health framework was used not simply as general background, but to guide the selection and interpretation of predictors related to household resources, partnership status, place of residence, and exposure to unsafe areas.

Conclusion

Aging in Haiti represents both a clinical and equity challenge. Older adults experience high burdens of chronic disease, functional impairment, psychosocial stress, food insecurity, water insecurity, and undernutrition, but these burdens are unequally distributed across sex, household resources, partnership status, rural residence, and unsafe areas. The findings show that older adults’ vulnerability cannot be understood through household socioeconomic status alone. Health, functioning, food access, water access, household support, and exposure to unsafe environments should be considered together when identifying those most in need of support. Older adults may require priority attention when multiple risks occur together, including low household resources, rural residence, lack of a partner, residence in unsafe areas, food or water insecurity, underweight status, hypertension, mobility limitation, or visual impairment. These results support integrated health and social protection strategies that combine chronic disease management with nutrition support, water-security interventions, and functional assistance. In Haiti, aging policy should move beyond general recognition of older adults’ vulnerability toward targeted programs that reach those with overlapping clinical, functional, and material needs.
