Exploring nursing students’ perspectives on disability through campus accessibility evaluation: a phenomenological study
Authors: Güven Soner, Figen Çavuşoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInclusive nursing education plays a crucial role in preparing future healthcare professionals to recognise and respond to the needs of persons with disabilities. Evaluating real-life environments, such as university campuses, may help students develop a deeper understanding of disability and reflect on their own attitudes and approaches to care. This study explores nursing students’ lived experiences during a campus accessibility evaluation activity to understand how they perceive and interpret disability within this context.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological design was utilized, focusing not on the evaluation of the educational activity’s effectiveness, but on exploring the students’ subjective experiences of assessing a university campus’s accessibility for persons with disabilities. Participants were 19 undergraduate nursing students (15 females, 4 males). Data were collected through three semi-structured focus group discussions. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method. Rigour was ensured through member checking, researcher reflexivity, and the maintenance of an audit trail.FindingsAnalysis revealed three main themes reflecting students’ experiential learning and evolving perspectives. (1) Contribution to the Level of Knowledge: Students reported gaining new insights into disability, uncovering previously overlooked environmental barriers, and recognising the multifaceted nature of accessibility. The experience fostered shifts in their understanding of disability beyond theoretical knowledge. (2) Contribution to the Affective Domain: Students described heightened emotional awareness, increased empathy, and a deeper sensitivity to the daily challenges encountered by persons with disabilities. Many expressed that the activity prompted personal reflection and transformed their perceptions of disability. (3) Contribution to Nursing Care: Students highlighted the importance of advocating for inclusive practices, taking initiative in supporting persons with disabilities, and adopting a more holistic and person-centred approach in future clinical encounters. They emphasised the relevance of accessibility evaluations to their ethical and professional responsibilities.ConclusionParticipating in a campus accessibility evaluation meaningfully guided nursing students’ understanding of disability and was perceived to foster the development of empathy, awareness, and inclusive care perspectives. The findings suggest that integrating experiential activities focused on accessibility into nursing education may strengthen students’ readiness to provide equitable, person-centred care to persons with disabilities. Such approaches have the potential to enhance both professional identity formation and advocacy within nursing practice.

Introduction

Disability is a comprehensive concept that reflects the structural and environmental deficits of social spaces that fail to accommodate the diverse spectrum of human bodies, thereby creating barriers to an individual’s full activity and equal participation in social life [1, 2]. Although the concept of disability often refers to persons with vision, hearing, or orthopaedic impairments, it also includes conditions such as chronic illness, cognitive impairment, and neurodivergence, among others [3]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 15% of the global population, representing at least one billion people, live with some form of disability, and this number has been gradually increasing [2]. Despite representing a significant proportion of the global population, persons with disabilities continue to face inequalities in accessing healthcare services due to insufficient and ineffective policy responses and social support [4].

The World Health Organization identifies insufficient knowledge and competence among healthcare professionals regarding disability as one of the main contributors to these inequalities [2]. A key factor underlying this issue is the limited inclusion of disability-related content in the curricula of health professions and the lack of a disability-inclusive educational framework [3, 5]. Nursing, as a central discipline in healthcare delivery, is also noted to have curricula that are insufficiently inclusive of persons with disabilities [6, 7].

Considering that nurses’ competencies and awareness play a crucial role in addressing the health needs of persons with disabilities, the importance of incorporating disability-related content into nursing education becomes evident. However, disability in nursing education is often addressed from a predominantly medical perspective, in which persons with disabilities are viewed primarily as patients rather than as individuals representing diversity within society [7]. This limited view prevents nursing students from fully understanding the inequalities, lived experiences, and systemic challenges faced by persons with disabilities [7–9]. Such gaps can negatively influence the quality and inclusivity of nursing care provided after graduation [10].

Efforts to enhance perspective-taking and ethical responsiveness are increasingly being integrated into healthcare curricula. Educational strategies designed to strengthen students’ understanding of diversity and inclusion have been shown to foster interpersonal competence and reflective practice [11]. Previous studies examining nursing students’ interactions with persons with disabilities have demonstrated positive developments in their knowledge and attitudes [12–14].

Persons with disabilities have the right to equal access to higher education and university campuses, as recognised by international human-rights standards [15]. Despite existing legislation aimed at promoting equal participation, many students with disabilities continue to face multiple barriers in university settings. Their challenges are not solely a result of individual impairments but are often rooted in environmental and systemic barriers, which become particularly visible in mobility, accessibility, and social participation on campus. Common difficulties include inadequate physical arrangements, limited availability of educational materials in accessible formats, and negative or biased attitudes among peers [16, 17].

Educational implementation on accessibility

In this study, as an integral part of their routine coursework within the Public Health Nursing Internship Course, nursing students participated in a structured, participatory campus accessibility evaluation program designed to enhance their understanding of disability and help them identify barriers that may restrict the participation of persons with disabilities in university life. Before the field-based evaluation, students attended a preparatory educational program delivered by the researchers. The program addressed key topics including disability rights, health and disability, the biopsychosocial model of disability, universal design principles, accessibility assessment, and effective communication with persons with disabilities. The educational content was informed by the World Health Organisation’s biopsychosocial framework of disability [18].

Following the educational component, students applied their newly acquired knowledge by evaluating the accessibility of university facilities. To ensure proper utilization of the assessment tools, students were trained through a 4-hour preparatory seminar combining theoretical principles with a practical mock walkthrough led by the researchers. Working in groups of five to six, students assessed designated faculty buildings using a structured accessibility guide adapted by the researchers from the ‘Let’s Overcome Obstacles Together: Awareness and Accessibility Guide’ [19], which is built upon international and national accessibility frameworks [18, 20, 21]. Prior to student deployment, the guide was successfully piloted by the researchers within the campus facilities to ensure its clarity and field usability. The guide directed students to systematically examine both outdoor and indoor environmental domains, including pathways, access ramps, main building entrances, elevators, classroom layouts, accessible restrooms, and internal tactile navigation or signage systems. Particular attention was given to features supporting accessibility for persons with visual, hearing, and orthopaedic impairments, such as tactile paving, Braille signage, large-print indicators, ramps, and communication supports.

To encourage perspective-taking and a multidimensional understanding of accessibility, each student was assigned to focus on a specific type of disability throughout the assessment process. Students documented environmental barriers, accessibility facilitators, and areas requiring improvement while observing how persons with disabilities might experience the campus environment.

The evaluations revealed a range of environmental and structural barriers that could hinder full participation in academic and social life. These experiences subsequently served as the foundation for the focus group discussions, during which students reflected on their observations, explored the meanings they attributed to disability and accessibility, and discussed how the experience shaped their personal and professional perspectives. Aim

Among various academic programs, nursing education plays a pivotal role in preparing future healthcare professionals to deliver high-quality care to persons with disabilities [8, 9]. This study explores how evaluating a university campus’s accessibility contributes to nursing students’ approaches to disability and examines their perspectives through in-depth interviews. Employing a phenomenological approach, the study investigates how nursing students perceive and interpret disability within the context of an accessibility-based campus evaluation. The following research questions were addressed in this study:

Methods

Research design

Addressing the challenges faced by marginalised groups is a key characteristic of qualitative research [22]. Therefore, a qualitative research design was deemed the most appropriate method for this study to explore nursing students’ experiences of evaluating a university campus from the perspective of persons with disabilities and to uncover the nursing perspective. This study employed a descriptive phenomenological design, a qualitative research method that investigates how phenomena are experienced in individuals’ consciousness [23]. A descriptive phenomenological design was intentionally selected to clearly separate the baseline educational activity from the qualitative inquiry. A descriptive-phenomenological approach was considered appropriate because the scope of this study was explicitly not to evaluate the pedagogical effectiveness or pre-post intervention outcomes of the educational program. Instead, it sought to explore how nursing students experienced, perceived, and made meaning of disability while engaging in the accessibility evaluation process, seeking to understand the core essence of this lived experience through direct engagement with environmental barriers. In this design, researchers analyse the participants’ descriptions of the phenomenon without providing any a priori or speculative interpretation of the data, transforming them directly into meaningful expressions [23, 24]. This research was conducted between July 1, 2020, and July 28, 2020.

Participants

The research population consisted of 38 nursing students (30 females and eight males) enrolled in the fourth (final) year of the Public Health Nursing Internship Course at the Nursing Department of a university located in the northern region of Türkiye during the Fall Semester of the 2019–2020 academic year. A purposive sampling approach based on voluntary consent was utilized, resulting in a final sample of 19 students (15 females, 4 males) who participated across three focus group discussions (consisting of 6 to 7 individuals per group). The mean age of the students was 22.47 ± 0.7 (range 22–24). There were no known or systematic differences between the participants (n = 19) and non-participants (n = 19) regarding their demographic characteristics, age, or academic standing in the course. Non-participation was entirely due to external post-graduation factors, such as immediate professional relocation or heavy clinical shift schedules in their new nursing positions during the COVID-19 pandemic. None of the participants had a disability, so their judgments regarding the campus experiences of persons with disabilities were formed from an external perspective. Nine participants reported having a relative with a disability.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted and recorded by two researchers using Zoom, an online meeting application, six months after the completion of the educational program [25]. Following the program, the students graduated, and several transitioned into professional nursing practice. The interviews were conducted online because the data collection period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which several students had relocated from the city after transitioning into professional nursing roles. During the discussions, these practicing nurses shared concrete examples of how the insights gained from the course were being integrated into their professional lives. Throughout this process, data collection was systematically monitored for data saturation and informational sufficiency, defined as the point where newly acquired data ceases to produce novel codes, categories, or thematic dimensions [26]. During the third focus group discussion, the researchers observed complete conceptual redundancy, as the participants’ reflections on environmental barriers, affective developments, and clinical care consistently replicated the findings from the first two groups. Because continuing the interviews would not have yielded any new analytical insights or expanded the existing coding framework, the data collection process was intentionally terminated after the third focus group interview, ensuring a comprehensive yet non-redundant exploration of the phenomenon [26].

Semi-structured interview form

The data were collected using a semi-structured interview form prepared by the researchers, who reviewed the literature [7, 9, 27]. The form was reviewed by three experts (one in qualitative research, one in the field of disabilities, and one in nursing education) and finalised based on expert opinions. The semi-structured interview form included participants’ socio-demographic characteristics (age, disability status, etc.) and questions such as “What did the course you attended contribute to you regarding disability?” “What was it like to examine campus barriers through the lens of disability accessibility? Can you elaborate on that?” and “What can nurses do to solve the problems faced by persons with disabilities?” (Table 1).

In-depth interview questions

Data analysis

The video recordings of the interviews were first transcribed into text format by the principal researcher in a Microsoft Word document, including relevant behavioural data (laughter, expressions of frustration, etc.) to fully capture the participants’ contextual expressions. The focus group recordings lasted 180 min in total (averaging 60 min per session), resulting in 19 pages of transcribed data. Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method was systematically operationalized to uncover the core structure of the students’ lived experiences [28]. In the first step, both researchers immersed themselves in the database by reading the transcripts multiple times to acquire an overall feeling for the narratives. In the second step, significant statements directly pertaining to how students perceive and interpret campus accessibility barriers were extracted. In the third step, meanings were carefully formulated from these significant statements through creative insight, maintaining a rigorous connection to the participants’ original context. In the fourth step, the formulated meanings were clustered into broader themes and sub-themes common to all transcripts. To ensure interpretive rigor during this phase, both researchers independently coded the transcripts using the demo version of MAXQDA 2020 software [29]. Any analytical disagreements or discrepancies regarding code definitions, thematic boundaries, or sub-theme conceptualizations were systematically resolved through iterative peer-debriefing sessions and comparative analysis until a 100% consensus was reached, supplemented by an external qualitative expert’s evaluation. In the fifth step, an exhaustive, detailed description of the phenomenon was written, integrating all clustered themes. In the sixth step, this exhaustive description was distilled into a statement of the fundamental structure of the experience. In the final step, member-checking was performed by conducting one-on-one validation interviews with three randomly selected participants to verify that our findings accurately reflected their lived experiences, and no further modifications to the thematic framework were requested.

Trustworthiness and rigour

The trustworthiness of this qualitative study was ensured in accordance with established criteria, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [22]. Credibility was enhanced through sustained engagement and collaborative interaction with participants during data collection. At the end of each interview, participants were invited to provide feedback on the interview process, including its length, clarity, and relevance of questions. Based on their suggestions, minor adjustments were made to improve the data collection process, such as refining and reducing the number of interview questions and, where necessary, shortening interview duration to reduce participant burden and improve data quality.

Transferability was supported through participant validation. Preliminary findings were shared with three participants selected from the study group. These participants were engaged in follow-up one-to-one discussions to obtain their feedback on the interpretations. Based on their input, minor revisions were made to ensure that the findings more accurately reflected participants’ perspectives and experiences. In addition, thick descriptions of the research context and participants were provided to facilitate assessment of applicability to other settings.

Dependability was ensured through peer examination. An external researcher with expertise in disability studies was consulted at both the initial and final stages of the research process to review the methodological procedures, providing feedback on the strengths and limitations of the study design and analysis process.

Confirmability was strengthened through systematic coding procedures and triangulation of analytic perspectives. A coding guide was developed based on the framework proposed by Guest et al. [30] and used consistently during the coding of transcribed interviews (Table 2). The researchers independently coded all transcripts, and discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. To further enhance confirmability, an expert academic with experience in qualitative research and disability studies reviewed selected interview transcripts and contributed to refining codes and themes. An audit trail was maintained throughout the research process, documenting coding decisions and theme development.

Research team and reflexivity

Both researchers hold doctoral degrees and are actively engaged in research within the field of public health nursing, with a particular focus on disadvantaged groups, including persons with disabilities. Their prior academic and professional engagement with disability-related issues provided a strong conceptual background for the study.

The researchers recognise that persons with disabilities experience structural and social inequalities across multiple domains [20]. The study was designed to enable nursing students to critically engage with these inequalities and develop a more inclusive understanding of disability-related care, thereby contributing to improvements in nursing education and practice.

In line with qualitative research principles, the researchers acknowledged that their prior knowledge and experiences could influence data collection and interpretation. To address this, reflexive practices were maintained throughout the research process, including continuous discussion of assumptions, biases, and interpretations during data analysis. The researchers deliberately avoided leading questions during interviews and ensured that participants’ narratives were prioritised in the analysis and reporting of findings.

Ethical principles

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study obtained ethical approval from the Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee of a university in northern Türkiye (Approval No: 2020/393). Approval for conducting the study was also obtained from the Department of Nursing. Before the research, the participants were informed about the study’s purpose, content, process, and confidentiality. The participants were assured that their identities would remain confidential and that the research data would not be shared with anyone other than the researchers. The participants were informed before the interviews were recorded, and their written and verbal informed consent was obtained. Students’ voluntary participation in the research was strictly protected. To eliminate any potential bias or perceived academic coercion, the researchers did not participate in the students’ formal course evaluations or grading during the academic semester, ensuring that participation in both the coursework activity and the subsequent research interviews had no bearing on their academic success.

Findings

In this qualitative study, using a descriptive phenomenological design, the interview findings were grouped into three themes and various sub-themes under each theme (Fig. 2). The main themes are as follows: (a) Contribution to the level of knowledge [acquiring new knowledge, change in knowledge], (b) Contribution to the affective domain [developing sensitivity, change in the perception of disability, development of empathy, development of awareness], and (c) Contribution to nursing care [making nursing care inclusive, taking action for persons with disabilities, advocacy, creating social awareness] (Table 2).

Development of empathy

The participants reported increased awareness and perspective-taking regarding disability throughout the course. The findings indicate that nursing students’ attitudes towards disability became more inclusive over time. When I saw the difficulties they face, when I tried to look from their perspective and experience it, I realized that those of us without disabilities live a much easier life. Discussion

Nursing is a profession that inherently encompasses human diversity and promotes social inclusion. Traditionally, however, the nursing profession has approached disability through a restrictive medical model, viewing individuals with disabilities primarily as passive clinical patients rather than as equal partners within human diversity [6, 31, 32]. Furthermore, contemporary nursing textbooks often lack adequate content on disability-related care [33]. The failure to update nursing curricula within a rights-based context shapes undergraduate students’ attitudes in a highly institutionalized manner, potentially reinforcing paternalistic viewpoints [8, 34].

Throughout their educational journey and subsequent professional careers, nursing students inevitably encounter and care for patients navigating various environmental and systemic barriers [14]. For future nurses to deliver equitable, rights-based, and inclusive healthcare, they must move beyond traditional frameworks and fully embrace disability as an intrinsic aspect of human diversity [34]. Using a descriptive phenomenological approach, this study explored how evaluating a university campus from an accessibility perspective shaped nursing students’ subsequent professional outlook. This qualitative design allowed the researchers to capture the depth of the students’ subjective reflections and conceptual growth, demonstrating that field-based accessibility assessments can meaningfully refine students’ awareness of equity and inclusion.

The first theme, Contribution to the Level of Knowledge, encompasses two sub-themes. The findings suggest that participants acquired critical insights into universal design and experienced a qualitative evolution in their perspectives through field-based evaluation. The narratives indicate that the practical exercise helped fill curricular gaps, aligning students’ conceptual knowledge with contemporary rights-based literature. Because the scope and delivery of disability related content in nursing curricula directly shape how students conceptualize accessibility [8], integrating practical evaluations offers a strategic educational tool. In line with this, Uysal et al. highlighted that expanding students’ baseline knowledge of disability supports the development of positive professional attitudes [27]. Conversely, insufficient curricular exposure tends to foster institutional bias or apprehension. As Willis and Thurston noted, nursing students frequently express a strong desire for structured learning opportunities regarding individuals with disabilities [14]. Developing an appropriate attitudinal and cognitive foundation during undergraduate training is therefore essential for providing quality, individualized, and person-centered care in future clinical settings.

The second theme, Contribution to the Affective Domain, encompasses four sub-themes. The findings indicate that participants developed heightened sensitivity, refined their perceptions, cultivated deeper empathy, and gained critical awareness through their field-based experiences. Exploring how students interpreted this engagement revealed that their overall attitudes towards disability meaningfully evolved toward a more inclusive framework. Rather than viewing disability through a deficit-based lens, participants recognized that social and architectural misfits within the environment fundamentally restrict social participation. This aligns with the findings of Erawati and Keliat, who observed that nursing students engaging in reflexive experiences underwent an attitudinal transition, moving away from stereotypical perceptions [12]. Similarly, Finkelstein and Orr demonstrated that direct exposure to the unmet needs of specific populations promotes institutional awareness and fosters positive professional behaviors [13]. Collectively, these insights emphasize the need for higher education institutions and nursing programs to invest in experiential disability awareness to better prepare graduates for equitable civic and professional practice.

The third theme, Contribution to Nursing Care, consists of four sub-themes. In this research, participants expressed an intention to actively implement inclusive nursing care, engage in systemic advocacy, and raise societal awareness following graduation. Training nurses to deliver accessible and high-quality care to all segments of the community is a cornerstone of professional equity [10]. Gaining firsthand knowledge of the barriers embedded within campus infrastructure allowed students to extrapolate these challenges to broader clinical and community environments, thereby recognizing the profound link between supportive environments and patient quality of life. Consistent with these reflections, Çömez and Altan Sarıkaya found that a majority of nursing students express a professional interest in caring for diverse populations when adequately prepared [32]. Willis and Thurston similarly identified an urgent need for nursing curricula to equip students with the practical skills required to collaborate effectively with individuals with disabilities [14]. Furthermore, experiential learning has been shown to increase students’ clinical motivation, mitigate social stigma, and enhance professional commitment to advocacy [12, 13].

In conclusion, these findings underscore the value of field-based, experiential learning in cultivating a comprehensive understanding of accessibility within nursing education. To ensure that future graduates possess the necessary competencies to assess complex patient environments and provide truly person-centered healthcare, disability-sensitive topics and universal design principles must be systematically integrated into core nursing curricula [14]. By fostering a deeper, socially grounded understanding of accessibility, nursing programs can contribute significantly to reducing systemic barriers and advancing healthcare equity.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, nursing students were asked to evaluate the university campus from the perspective of persons with disabilities to better understand accessibility barriers and inclusion issues. This research is expected to make an original contribution to the literature on disability and nursing education. Another strength of this study is its use of a descriptive-phenomenological design, which enabled an in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences. Additionally, multiple methodological strategies were employed to enhance the rigour and trustworthiness of the study.

This study has several limitations that should be noted. Most importantly, none of the participating nursing students had a personal disability; therefore, their reflections and judgments regarding campus accessibility barriers represent external perspective-taking rather than the direct, lived experience of disability. Secondly, the findings are localized to a specific cohort of nursing students within a single university, which may restrict wider applicability. Additionally, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, focus group discussions were conducted via online video conferencing rather than face-to-face. This remote setting might have subtly altered the natural dynamic of group interaction or limited the non-verbal expressions of the participants. Lastly, individual cultural and socio-demographic backgrounds may have shaped baseline attitudes toward disability. Future research should consider these dimensions by incorporating diverse institutional settings and involving individuals with lived experiences of disability.

Conclusion

This study examined the experiences of nursing students during the evaluation process of a university campus from the perspective of persons with disabilities. It investigated how these experiences impacted their professional development. The findings suggest that engaging in accessibility evaluation may enhance students’ understanding of the barriers and inclusion challenges faced by persons with disabilities, while also supporting the development of more informed, reflective, and equitable approaches to care. Participants reported gaining new insights into disability, showing potential attitudinal growth, and perceiving improvements in their competence to provide inclusive nursing care.

To prepare future nurse leaders for equitable and person-centred practice, nursing education should consider integrating structured learning experiences that engage students directly with accessibility and inclusion issues. Embedding disability-focused and rights-oriented content into curricula has the potential to cultivate leadership, advocacy, and ethical sensitivity, which are core attributes essential for advancing inclusive nursing practice and healthcare systems.
