Efficacy of Da Vinci robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery combined with three-dimensional imaging for precisive segmentectomy in stage IA non-small cell lung cancer ≤2 cm: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Guibin Weng, Jiarong Zhang, Yijin Lin, Lin Chen, Weimin Fang, Kunshou Zhu, Yuanmei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing use of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), the detection rate of early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with tumors ≤2 cm has significantly risen. While lobectomy remains the standard treatment, it may compromise postoperative pulmonary function. Segmentectomy offers a function-preserving alternative, yet poses greater technical challenges. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) combined with three-dimensional imaging technology for precise anatomical segmentectomy in patients with stage IA NSCLC ≤2 cm.MethodsThis retrospective study included 184 patients with stage IA NSCLC (T1a–1bN0M0) treated between October 2021 and October 2024. Patients were divided into two groups: Group A (video-assisted thoracoscopic segmentectomy, n=96) and Group B (RATS with three-dimensional imaging, n=88). Perioperative outcomes, postoperative complications, and pulmonary function at 6 months were compared.ResultsAlthough Group B had a longer operative time (P<0.05), it showed significant advantages in the number and stations of lymph nodes dissected, shorter chest tube duration and hospital stay (P<0.05). Although the incidence of prolonged air leak (>5 days) was not statistically significant between the two groups, group B showed a lower incidence. At 6 months postoperatively, Group B had significantly better preserved pulmonary function [forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), peak expiratory flow (PEF); P<0.05].ConclusionsRobot-assisted segmentectomy guided by three-dimensional imaging is safe and feasible for patients with stage IA NSCLC. Compared with conventional video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, this technique offers superior preservation of pulmonary function and a lower incidence of postoperative air leakage, highlighting its potential for broader clinical application.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) + 3D imaging had longer operative time but achieved greater lymph node dissection, shorter chest tube duration, and shorter hospital stay vs. video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) (P<0.05).

• At 6 months postoperative, RATS + 3D imaging showed significantly better preservation of forced vital capacity, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, and peak expiratory flow (P<0.05), with a lower trend in prolonged air leak (5.56% vs. 15.28%).

What is known and what is new?

• Segmentectomy preserves lung function better than lobectomy for stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) ≤2 cm (JCOG0802).

• RATS combined with 3D imaging achieves superior pulmonary function preservation and more thorough lymph node dissection compared to VATS alone.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• RATS guided by 3D imaging should be considered a preferred approach for precise segmentectomy in early-stage NSCLC. Prospective multicenter trials with longer follow-up are needed to confirm long-term oncological outcomes.

Introduction

Primary lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, there were approximately 2.5 million new cases of lung cancer and over 1.8 million related deaths globally, accounting for 12.4% of all new cancer cases and 18.7% of cancer-related deaths, respectively, making it a leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide (1). With the widespread adoption of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening, the detection rate of early-stage lung cancer (tumor diameter ≤2 cm) has markedly increased (2). Although lobectomy combined with systematic lymph node dissection remains the standard treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (3), this approach can result in substantial loss of pulmonary function. Therefore, how to achieve oncologic radicality while maximizing lung function preservation has become a focus of current clinical research. The publication of the JCOG0802 trial has provided new evidence for treatment strategies in early-stage NSCLC. The study demonstrated that, in patients with stage IA NSCLC (tumor diameter ≤2 cm, consolidation-to-tumor ratio >0.5), segmentectomy was associated with a higher 5-year overall survival rate compared to lobectomy and better preservation of lung function (4). The results of CALGB 140503 trial further confirmed that, for peripheral early stage non-small cell lung cancers with a maximum diameter of ≤2 cm, sublobar resection (wedge resection or segmentectomy) is not inferior to lobectomy (5).

As a representative approach of anatomical sublobar resection, segmentectomy is technically more challenging than conventional lobectomy. It requires thorough preoperative assessment of segmental pulmonary vessels and bronchial anatomy, including possible variations, to develop a precise surgical plan (6,7). In recent years, the combined application of robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) and three-dimensional reconstruction techniques has effectively addressed these challenges. The Da Vinci surgical system, with its high-definition three-dimensional visualization, articulated instrument design, and stable operating platform, significantly enhances surgical precision. This is particularly advantageous in patients with complex segmental anatomy or significant vascular anomalies, where RATS can reduce the risk of intraoperative bleeding and accidental injury (8-10). Available evidence suggests that da Vinci robot-assisted segmentectomy is safe and feasible in clinical practice, with perioperative outcomes comparable to those of conventional video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) (11,12). Moreover, the use of three-dimensional reconstruction software such as Mimics and IQQA-3D for preoperative planning allows for accurate visualization of pulmonary vasculature, bronchi, and tumor location. This facilitates recognition of individual anatomical variations, enabling surgeons to optimize the surgical approach and maximize preservation of normal lung tissue while ensuring oncologic radicality (13,14). Nonetheless, limitations of the robotic system include a steep learning curve, lack of tactile feedback, and high cost.

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical value of da Vinci robot-assisted surgery combined with three-dimensional reconstruction technology in performing precise segmentectomy for patients with early-stage NSCLC. By comparing perioperative outcomes between patients undergoing this advanced technique and those treated with conventional surgical approaches, the study seeks to provide evidence-based support for the broader clinical adoption of robot-assisted precise segmentectomy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2441/rc).

Methods

Patients

A total of 184 patients with stage IA (tumor diameter ≤2 cm) NSCLC who underwent minimally invasive segmentectomy at the Department of Thoracic Surgery in our institution between October 2021 and October 2024 were included in this study (Figure 1).

Study flowchart. CTR, consolidation-to-tumor ratio; ECOG, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group; NSCLC, non-small cell lung cancer; RATS, robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery; VATS, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age between 20 and 80 years, regardless of sex; (II) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0–1; (III) imaging findings consistent with peripheral NSCLC [tumor diameter ≤2 cm, consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) >0.5, located in the outer third of the lung parenchyma]; and (IV) clinical stage cT1a–bN0M0 according to the 8th edition of the TNM classification by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC).

The exclusion criteria included: (I) history of other malignancies within the past five years or concurrent diagnosis of other tumors; (II) previous treatment with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy; (III) postoperative pathological confirmation of lymph node metastasis.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional ethics committee of Fujian Cancer Hospital (No. K2023-299-01) and informed consent was obtained from all patients and their families.

Surgical procedures

All patients underwent preoperative high-resolution pulmonary imaging using dual-source low-dose spiral computed tomography. The lesions were radiologically confirmed as ≤2 cm in diameter and met the diagnostic criteria for stage IA NSCLC. Preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction was performed to simulate and visualize the anatomical relationships between the tumor, surrounding pulmonary vasculature, and bronchi. A surgical margin of at least 2 cm or greater than the tumor diameter was ensured to guide precise surgical planning. Preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction was routinely performed for all patients in both groups and used for preoperative surgical planning.

The selection between VATS and RATS was determined by patients who met our criteria for segmentectomy during the study period. After thorough communication with patients regarding the advantages and limitations of both surgical approaches, patients ultimately made their own decisions about which surgical approach to undergo. To minimize operator-dependent variables, all procedures in both cohorts were performed by the same senior thoracic surgeon and his teammates. Postoperative management protocols were standardized between groups, encompassing analgesic regimens, early mobilization strategies, and chest drainage management parameters.

Anesthesia and patient positioning

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with contralateral one-lung ventilation via double-lumen endotracheal intubation. Patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position with both upper limbs flexed and supported by a cushion, and the thorax elevated to form a jackknife position.

Surgical approaches

Group A (conventional video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, VATS combined with three-dimensional imaging for precise segmentectomy): a single 3–5 cm incision was made at the 4th or 5th intercostal space between the anterior and mid-axillary lines. A disposable incision protector was placed and served as both the viewing and working port.

Group B (robotic-assisted thoracic surgery, RATS combined with three-dimensional imaging for precise segmentectomy): a 12 mm incision was made at the 7th or 8th intercostal space along the posterior axillary line for placement of the robotic camera Trocar. Two additional 8 mm incisions were made at the 7th or 8th intercostal space along the scapular line and the 5th or 6th intercostal space along the anterior axillary line for robotic instrument arms. A 3–4 cm auxiliary incision was made at the 7th intercostal space along the mid-axillary line, with insertion of a disposable incision protector.

Data collection

In this study, retrospectively collected variables included patient age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities. Perioperative data encompassed operative time, intraoperative blood loss, the number and stations of resected lymph nodes, duration of chest tube drainage, and length of hospital stay. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo grading system. Pain intensity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) on postoperative day 1 and day 3. Additionally, to evaluate changes in perioperative pulmonary function, patients in both groups underwent pulmonary function tests preoperatively and again at 6 months postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and compared using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To account for potential confounding factors and selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to balance the baseline characteristics between groups. Propensity score matching was performing using a 1:1 nearest neighbor matching algorithm without replacement (greedy matching approach), with the caliper width set at 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 184 eligible patients were included in this study. Based on the surgical approach received, patients were divided into two groups: Group A (conventional video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery segmentectomy, VATS) with 96 patients, and Group B (robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery segmentectomy with three-dimensional imaging, RATS) with 88 patients. All patients completed preoperative assessment and postoperative follow-up, with complete clinical data available for analysis.

We conducted statistical comparisons of key preoperative clinical characteristics between the two groups, including age, sex, smoking history, comorbid hypertension, diabetes mellitus and so on. To reduce the potential influence of confounding factors, we conducted PSM analysis, which resulted in 72 matched pairs. The results showed no statistically significant differences in these baseline variables between the two groups after propensity score matching (Table 1). In addition, we analyzed the temporal trends in surgical approaches among the included patients. The data showed that VATS remained the predominant surgical modality in the early period. However, with the increasing adoption of robotic systems and accumulation of surgeon experience, the number of RATS procedures has shown a steady upward trend over time (Figure 3).

Comparison of perioperative outcomes

Perioperative surgical parameters

With respect to key perioperative metrics, the mean operative time in Group A was 124.50±25.21 min, which was significantly shorter than in Group B (141.04±24.52 min; P<0.001). This difference is likely attributable to the additional docking and undocking steps required by the da Vinci robotic system and the time needed to coordinate the robotic instruments. In addition, we observed that the total postoperative drainage volume was significantly higher in Group B than in Group A (488.88±11.37 vs. 449.36±82.88 mL; P=0.02), which may be related to the more extensive lymph node dissection achieved with the assistance of the Da Vinci robotic system. Regarding lymphadenectomy, Group B outperformed Group A both in the number of lymph nodes resected (10.00 vs. 8.00; P<0.001) and in the number of lymph stations dissected (5.00 vs. 4.00; P<0.001), suggesting that RATS combined with three-dimensional reconstruction facilitates a more systematic mediastinal and hilar lymph node dissection. Postoperative recovery parameters also favored the RATS group: the mean length of hospital stay was shorter in Group B (6.00 vs. 7.50 days; P<0.001), as was the duration of chest-tube drainage (5.00 vs. 6.00 days; P=0.02) (Table 2). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of intraoperative blood loss.

Postoperative complications

The incidence rates of postoperative pneumonia, atelectasis, and cardiac arrhythmia showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P>0.05). Group B showed a trend toward lower incidence of prolonged air leakage (>5 days) compared to Group A (5.56% vs. 15.28%, P=0.056), which demonstrated a tendency for benefit, suggesting that RATS combined with three-dimensional reconstruction technology may facilitate precise intraoperative navigation and meticulous dissection, thereby helping to reduce the risk of prolonged postoperative air leaks. Neither group experienced intraoperative bleeding, major vascular injury, or conversion to open thoracotomy. No mortality occurred within 30 days postoperatively in either group (P>0.05), further confirming the excellent safety profile of both surgical approaches in clinical practice (Table 2).

NRS pain scores

Postoperative pain assessment in this study was conducted using the NRS. On postoperative day 1, Group A scored 3.71±1.05 versus 3.44±1.10 in Group B, a difference that was not statistically significant (P=0.14). On postoperative day 3, the pain scores were 1.11±1.16 in Group A and 1.58±1.21 in Group B, with a statistically significant difference (P<0.05). These results indicate that both surgical approaches provide equally effective pain control (Table 2).

Perioperative pulmonary function changes

Preoperative pulmonary function parameters [including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV), and peak expiratory flow (PEF)] showed no significant differences between the two groups (P>0.05). However, follow-up data at 6 months postoperatively indicated that although pulmonary function indices in Group B (including FVC, FEV1, and PEF) declined compared to preoperative values, the magnitude of reduction was significantly smaller than that observed in Group A. These differences were statistically significant across all measured parameters (P<0.05) (Table 3) (Figure 4). These findings suggest that RATS combined with three-dimensional imaging for precise pulmonary segmentectomy offers notable advantages in preserving postoperative pulmonary function.

Discussion

The detection rate of early stage lung cancer presenting as ground glass nodules has increased substantially, and these patients generally achieve favorable survival outcomes after surgical resecting (15,16). RATS has gained significant prominence in minimally invasive thoracic surgery. Compared to traditional open thoracotomy, VATS has demonstrated marked advantages in reducing postoperative complication rates, accelerating patient rehabilitation, and improving quality of life after surgery, showing distinct benefits across multiple dimensions including surgical technique, tumor control, and patient prognosis (17,18). However, traditional thoracoscopic segmentectomy remains technically complex, presenting numerous challenges even for experienced thoracic surgeons, with primary limitations being operational constraints in two-dimensional visualization and restricted instrument movement freedom. The da Vinci robotic surgical system, with its exceptional operational flexibility, high-precision instrumentation, and three-dimensional high-definition imaging system, has demonstrated unique value in complex and delicate thoracic surgeries, continuously expanding its clinical applications. Since DYLEWSKI’s team first reported the safety and efficacy of 17 cases of robotic segmentectomy in 2011 (19), numerous subsequent studies have further confirmed this technique’s excellent safety profile and favorable perioperative outcomes (9,20). A propensity score-matched analysis based on national database data (n=838) showed that despite RATS requiring an average of 25 minutes longer operative time (247.1 vs. 222.6 min, P<0.0001), its conversion rate to open thoracotomy was significantly lower than VATS (4.8% vs. 8.0%, P=0.007), and the 30-day complication rate was also lower (33.4% vs. 39.2%, P=0.013) (21). In terms of lymph node dissection, RATS enhanced the thoroughness of N1/N2 lymph node removal through precise control and three-dimensional visualization, facilitating more accurate pathological staging and post-operative adjuvant treatment decisions. This finding was reflected in both the National Cancer Database and single-center propensity studies. Although prospective evidence regarding long-term survival remains limited, a SEER-Medicare-based study (median follow-up 35 months) showed no statistical differences between RATS and VATS in 3-year overall survival (73.1% vs. 71.4%, P=0.366) and cancer-specific mortality (16.6% vs. 14.9%, P=0.639) (22). Furthermore, another multicenter randomized controlled trial indicated that for N2-stage patients, RATS demonstrated superior perioperative complications compared to open surgery, with no significant disadvantages in long-term survival (23).

The results of this study demonstrate that Group A patients had significantly higher perioperative indices including drainage tube retention time, and postoperative hospital stay compared to Group B. Conversely, Group A showed markedly lower lymph node dissection extent (in both number and stations) than Group B. These findings suggest that RATS combined with three-dimensional imaging technology enables precise pulmonary segmentectomy while ensuring more thorough lymph node dissection. The RATS system integrated with three-dimensional imaging technology offers multiple technical advantages: First, its high-definition three-dimensional imaging system provides exceptional visual magnification, enhancing anatomical identification clarity; second, the high-freedom mechanical arm design allows for more flexible and precise operations; additionally, the built-in anti-tremor function further improves surgical operation stability (24). Pulmonary function assessment revealed that at 6 months postoperatively, both groups showed significant decreases in FVC, FEV1, MVV, and PEF compared to preoperative values. However, Group A exhibited notably greater reductions than Group B, suggesting that RATS combined with three-dimensional imaging technology may be more beneficial for preserving postoperative pulmonary function. Furthermore, compared with traditional CT-guided invasive localization methods, novel non-invasive localization techniques can effectively avoid perioperative localization-related complications. Implementing precise pulmonary segmentectomy through RATS combined with three-dimensional reconstruction technology can significantly optimize surgical trauma control and achieve superior surgical outcomes (25,26).

The overall incidence of postoperative complications and the NRS pain score on postoperative day 1 were comparable between the two groups with no statistically significant difference. However, on postoperative day 3, group B showed a slightly higher NRS pain score than group A, suggesting that both approaches are safe and provide favorable patient-reported pain outcomes, although subtle differences exist. This may be attributed to the use of multiple ports in RATS, which could cause greater traction or compression of the intercostal nerves, whereas conventional VATS is performed through a single incision, thereby reducing intercostal nerve interference. Our center is currently exploring the feasibility of single-port robotic surgery. However, in terms of persistent air leakage, Group B showed a lower incidence of air leakage lasting more than 5 days compared to Group A. This advantage may be attributed to the high-resolution three-dimensional visualization capability of the da Vinci robotic system, which enables clearer identification of anatomical planes between lung segments. When combined with flexible and stable mechanical arm operation, surgeons can more precisely preserve adjacent lung tissue while isolating segmental bronchi and vessels. Furthermore, the stability and precision of the robotic system’s suturing capability also helps reduce the incidence of prolonged postoperative air leakage, thereby accelerating patient rehabilitation, reducing hospital stay duration, and decreasing medical resource consumption. In addition, we observed a gradual reduction in operative time with the increasing number of RATS procedures performed, indicating the presence of a learning curve as surgeons become more proficient with the robotic surgical system (27,28).

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis with a small sample size and lacks a randomized control group, which may introduce selection bias. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short, and the findings mainly focus on perioperative outcomes and short-term efficacy, making it difficult to assess long-term oncological results. Third, future prospective studies with larger sample sizes, multiple centers, and extended follow-up are needed to further validate these findings. Finally, the patient-driven choice of surgical approach introduces inherent selection bias. Despite propensity score matching, residual confounding from unmeasured variables—including patient preferences, socioeconomic status, health literacy, and surgeon enthusiasm for newer technology—cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the Da Vinci robotic system combined with three-dimensional imaging for precise segmentectomy is a safe and feasible approach for patients with stage IA non-small cell lung cancer. Compared to conventional thoracoscopic surgery, it is associated with less postoperative pulmonary function decline and a lower incidence of prolonged air leaks, demonstrating promising potential for broader clinical application.
