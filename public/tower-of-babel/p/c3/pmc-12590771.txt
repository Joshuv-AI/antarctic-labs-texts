Bacillus velezensis Bac-9, isolated from kefir, possesses antifungal activity and improves resistance of tomato plant against whitefly Bemisia tabaci
Authors: Ernesto Pérez-Rueda, Ana Roblero-Aguilar, Carlos López-González, Denisse Domínguez-García, Ángel Herrera-Gorocica, Esaú Ruiz-Sánchez, Gabriel Lizama-Uc, Silvia Tenorio-Salgado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Bacillus genus comprises spore-forming, Gram-positive bacteria widely recognized for their capacity to produce bioactive compounds with antimicrobial properties. This activity is primarily attributed to the synthesis of diverse molecules, including peptides, non-ribosomal peptides, and polyketides, which exhibit inhibitory effects against various pathogens. Bacillus species are ubiquitous and highly diverse, encompassing strains with significant biotechnological potential.ResultsBac-9 is a bacterial strain belonging to Bacillus genus isolated from kefir in Escárcega, Campeche, México. This strain exhibits strong antifungal activity against Fusarium equiseti, F. solani, Curvularia sp. and the oomycete, Phytophthora capsici. The genome comprises 4,059,427 bp, with a total of 4,195 coding sequences and a GC content of 46.3%. A total of 21 biosynthetic gene clusters were identified: 55% were non-ribosomal peptides (NRPs), 36% were polyketides (PKs) and 9% were NRP + PK hybrids. This bacterium produces compounds with insecticidal properties, such as thietane and acenaphthylene, which were detected by GC-MS. In tomato plants, different Bac-9 extracts activate genes involved in defense against insects and pathogens, including genes associated with the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid and salicylic acid. In addition, the extracts reduce oviposition by the whitefly Bemisia tabaci in Lycopersicon esculentum.ConclusionThis study offers valuable insights into bioactive compounds with potential for the biological control of phytopathogens and whiteflies. Overall, the bacterium emerges as a promising candidate for use in biological control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04419-0.Keywords: Bacillus velezensis, Bac-9, Kefir, Induced systemic resistance, Tomato plant, Bemisia tabaci.

Introduction

Accelerated evolution of resistance to commonly-used antibiotics and fungicides, as well as public concern about the harmful effects of agrochemicals, have increased the need to identify new environmentally-friendly antimicrobial agents [1]. Kefir, meanwhile, is a fermented dairy product traditionally produced by symbiotic culture of bacteria and fungi. Between 40 and 50 different species have been identified in this type of consortium, including lactic bacteria, acetic bacteria and yeasts [2, 3]. Different studies have shown the antimicrobial activity of kefir against many pathogenic organisms [4–6] and its antagonistic effects have been associated with the presence of secondary metabolites, such as ethanol, organic acids, acetaldehydes and antimicrobial peptides [7]. Secondary metabolites are key of microbial interactions, providing ecological advantages through antimicrobial, insecticidal, and plant-growth-promoting activities. In Bacillus species, these compounds are encoded in biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), which represent central point of metabolic innovation with biotechnological potential [8]. Bacillus velezensis is a promising biocontrol agent due to its capacity to produce lipopeptides, polyketides, and ribosomally synthesized peptides, such as surfactin, fengycin, difficidin, macrolactin, bacillibactin, and bacilysin [9, 10]. These metabolites suppress phytopathogenic fungi and bacteria, and enhance plant resistance and growth [11].

Recent studies indicate that inoculation of tomato plants with a mixture of bacterial species belonging to Bacillus and Paenibacillus genera, enhances root growth, modifies potato psyllid host-preference behavior and oviposition, and delays colonization by the phytopathogen bacteria Candidatus Liberibacter solanacearum [12]. In addition, inoculation with native Rhizobium strains improved plant growth, fruit quality, and microbiome composition in tomato crops [13]. Highlighting the importance of isolating and characterizing bacteria with plant growth promoting potential.

In this work, the Bac-9 was isolated from kefir from Escárcega Campeche, México, and demonstrated antifungal activity against three phytopathogenic fungi: Fusarium equiseti, F. solani and Curvularia sp., and an oomycete, Phytophthora capsici. In addition, it showed a repellent effect and an oviposition deterrent effect on B. tabaci. These antagonistic activities are associated with the production of non-ribosomal peptides and polyketides, like those already reported as having antifungal properties; as well as thietane and acenaphthene, compounds with insecticidal attributes. Our findings open opportunities for developing effective plant-growth-promoting bacteria-based products, that could be used it for commercial application; however, further targeted field studies are necessary to validate their efficacy under diverse environmental conditions.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Bacillus velezensis Bac-9 was isolated from kefir obtained in Escárcega, Campeche, Mexico, collected at a cattle farm located in the municipality of Escárcega (latitude 18.617, longitude − 90.717; 18°37′1″ N, 90°43′1″ W), and cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) medium at pH 7.2 incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with continuous shaking at 250 rpm in a IKA KS 4000i rotating incubator [5].

Bacterial extracts

Bac-9 strain was cultured in 500 ml of LB medium at 350 rpm for 24 h at 27 °C. The culture media was centrifuged at 6000 rpm to obtain cell-free supernatant, which was used at a 1:1 (solvent: culture) concentration with various solvents, including dichloromethane, chloroform and ethyl acetate. Controls were LB medium and distilled water.

Bac-9 antifungal activity

Antifungal activity from Bac-9 strain was evaluated against three pathogenic fungi: Curvularia sp., F. equiseti and F. solani; as well one oomycete: Phytophthora capsici, using an in vitro dual-culture assay. To this end, Bac-9 culture was inoculated at one end of the petri dish with potato dextrose agar (PDA), and subsequently the PDA plates were incubated for 15 days with one fungus at a time, checking the results every 5 days. The inhibition zones (in mm) were determined by measuring the distance between the edges of the fungal mycelium and the bacterial strain. Genomic DNA extraction and sequencing

Genomic DNA from Bac-9 strain was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method [14], and the quantity and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and gel-electrophoresis respectively. The DNA obtained was sequenced by the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform [15] at the Instituto de Biotecnología, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México. Read quality and trimming were performed with FastQC software v.0.11.9 [16]. Genome assembly was done by means of the Rapid Annotations using Subsystems Technology (RAST) server [17].

Biosynthetic gene cluster (BGCs) prediction

BGCs are responsible for the production of several secondary metabolites that contribute to competition among different microorganisms [18]. Therefore, for the identification of genes encoding the biosynthetic pathways to produce secondary metabolites in the Bac-9 genome, the antiSMASH program version 7 was used, with the default parameters [19]. Briefly, antiSMASH is a tool that allows the user to identify, annotate and analyze the gene clusters encoding the biosynthetic pathways of secondary metabolites in bacterial and fungal genomes.

Amplification of nonribosomal peptide (NRPS) and polyketide (PKs) biosynthetic gene clusters

To identify genes related to non-ribosomal peptide (NRPS) and polyketide (PK) biosynthesis, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using six primer pairs (Table S1) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 8 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C to 65 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 2:30 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Qualitative detection of genes involved in induced systemic resistance in plants by RT PCR

To evaluate the genes involved in induction of systemic resistance in plants, L. esculentum plants aged 31 days in sterile substrate were used and treated with a bacterial suspension of Bac-9. The suspension was prepared in LB culture medium, adjusting the turbidity at 540 nm until an absorbance of 0.5. The treatment was applied uniformly on both the upper and lower surfaces of the leaves by spraying until the run-off point was reached. Complete coverage of the leaf surface was ensured by applying enough volume so that excess solution began to run off the leaf margins, which was considered the threshold for stopping the application. The control group received the same volume of LB medium under the same experimental conditions to ensure consistency in the treatment. At 2, 24, 120 and 336 h after application, total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent 15,596,026 (Thermo Fisher), following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using RevertAid H Minus M-MuLV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Scientific, #K1632). The cDNA was used as a template in a RT-PCR with specific primers for the ACC (ACC oxidase), ACT (Actine), NPR (Non-expressor of PR genes), PDF (Plant defensin) and PR1 (Pathogenesis-related protein 1) genes, previously reported [14]. The primers were added at a final concentration of 0.5 µM, together with 1 U of the enzyme DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific, EP0702). The PCR reaction was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at the specific temperature for each primer pair for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C. The final extension was carried out for 10 min at 72 °C.

Repellence index (RI) and oviposition deterrence index (ODI)

The Repellence Index (RI) is a quantitative metric used to evaluate the efficacy of a substance in repelling insects or other pests. Similarly, the Oviposition Deterrence Index (ODI) assesses the ability of a substance, such as a plant extract or chemical compound—to inhibit oviposition by insects in treated areas compared to untreated controls. In this study, RI and ODI values for the Bac-9 extracts were determined through bioassays using L. solanum and B. tabaci. The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at ambient conditions (20–35 °C, 70–90% relative humidity and 14 h light:10 h dark). Five plants were used per treatment, each sprayed with the corresponding extract. Following application, the plants were placed in a greenhouse housing a whitefly colony. Results

Bac-9 strain antifungal activity

Antagonism tests were performed to determine the antifungal effect of the Bac-9 strain, using dual cultures on PDA plates against the fungi: F. solani, F. equiseti and Curvularia sp., and against the oomycete P. capsici. These fungi were selected because they attack more than 100 species of agricultural significance in the world [21–23] causing large economic losses. In this case, the antagonistic activity was determined based on the limited growth of fungal mycelia in the inhibition zone for 7 days. The results show that the Bac-9 strain effectively inhibits the mycelial growth of all fungi and the oomycete, and demonstrates radial inhibition ranging from 55 to 83%, with P. capsici being the most sensitive (85%) compared to the control. In all cases, inhibitory activity was evidenced by limited mycelial growth in the zone of inhibition against bacterial growth. (Fig. 1).

Sequencing, assembly and annotation of the Bac-9 strain genome

The Bac-9 strain is Gram-positive, spore-forming, catalase-positive, and can synthesize exopolysaccharides. The genome displays a circular chromosome and comprises 4,059,427 bp, with a total of 4195 coding sequences and a GC content of 46.3%. It contains 91 genes for RNA and 345 subsystems (Table S2).

Average nucleotide identity (ANI) revealed that the Bac-9 strain belongs to the genus Bacillus. The sequence similarity of Bac-9 and the strain type B. amyloliquefaciens strain DM7 (NC_014551.1) was 94.6%; while the similarity between Bac-9 and the strain type B. velezensis CBMB205 (CP011937.1) was 98.2%, showing the highest similarity with B. velezensis 83 (CP034203), with a value of 99.32%.

The B. velezensis Bac-9 genome sequence has been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under the BioProject ID SAMN50192864.

Cluster prediction for secondary metabolite biosynthesis

According to the observed inhibition activity of Bac-9 against phytopathogenic fungi, a genome analysis was performed to identify which compounds with antifungal activity it could synthesize. For this purpose, the antiSMASH version 7 program was used to predict gene clusters associated with secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways [19]. Based on this approach, 22 biosynthetic gene clusters were identified, of which 55% are NRPs, 36% belong to the PK category and 9% are NRP + PK hybrids. Among NRPs, fengycin, surfactin, termoactinoamide A, bacillibactin and bacilysin were identified, with a similarity percentage between 8 and 100% (Table S3); in the case of PKs, difficidin, macrolactin H and plipastatin were observed, with a similarity percentage ranging from 7 to 100% (Table S3); while in the hybrids, bacillaene and mycosubtilin were detected with 79 and 100% similarity, respectively (Table S3).

Detections and expression of NRPs by PCR

The Bacillus genus comprises a group of bacteria known for their ability to produce a wide variety of antimicrobial peptides [24, 25]. These include iturin A, bacillisin and mycosubtilin, which have antifungal properties [26]. For the detection of genes involved in the biosynthesis of NRPs, such as iturin, bacilysin and mycosubtilin, specific primer pairs were used for the genes ituA; 3, ituD; 4, ipa14 for the detection of the operon encoding iturin; the primer pair for the mycC gene that participates in the synthesis of mycosubtilin and finally, the primers bacD, bacAB that are involved in the biosynthesis of bacillisin. Amplicons were obtained with each of the primer pairs used and a single band corresponding to the expected size was detected for each of the genes analyzed (Fig. 2).

Induction of resistance genes in tomato plants

It has been documented that strains of the Bacillus genus, in addition to showing antimicrobial activity, have the ability to induce a systemic response in plants, thanks to the great diversity of bioactive molecules that this genus produces; through the signaling pathways of jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA) and ethylene (ET) [27–29]. To determine whether the Bac-9 strain induces a systemic response in plants, tests were carried out on L. esculentum, sprayed with the Bac-9 strain, and the expression of the ACC oxidase, NRP1, PDF1.2 and PR1 genes, which are expressed in response of plants to a wide range of biotic and abiotic stress factors [30], was analyzed. For this purpose, RT-PCR was used, from which amplification products were obtained for the ACC oxidase, NRP1 and PR1 genes, while no amplification product was obtained with the PR1.2 gen. Repellency of B. tabaci adults in tomato plants

To test whether the Bac-9 strain could repel whitefly adults and deter oviposition, the RI and ODI were calculated. For this purpose, extracts from the LB culture of the Bac-9 strain and whitefly adults were used. According to the RI of the treatments, the extracts showed values of M1 = 0.83, M2 = 1.12 and M3 = 1.36 adults/cm2, while the controls gave M4 = 1.64 and M5 = 5.93 adults/cm2, resulting in a repellency index of < 1. The M2 treatment was more effective compared to the control (Fig. 5A, Fig. S1and Fig. S2). Additionally, to determine whether the Bac-9 extracts influenced whitefly oviposition, the ODI was calculated. According to the calculations obtained, sample M1 presented 1.50 eggs per cm2, which gives a calculated ODI of −0.002. For M2, 169 eggs per cm2 were observed, giving an ODI of −0.005, while for M3 2.97 eggs per cm2 were quantified. Interestingly, with extract M2, 1.69 eggs per cm2 were observed and in M3, 2.97 eggs per cm2, while in controls M4 and M5 3.18 and 6.89 eggs per cm2 were observed, respectively (Fig. 5B, Fig. S3 and S4).

Gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

After determining the repellent effect of the Bac-9 strain extracts, and the decrease in whitefly oviposition arising from their use, the compounds with biological activity beyond the PNR and PK were identified by means of a GC-MS analysis. Three organic solvents were used: dichloromethane, chloroform and ethyl acetate, to determine the solvent with which the greatest number of compounds were extracted. In this regard, a greater number of active compounds were detected in the extract with ethyl acetate, in particular aromatic compounds such as acenaphthene, acenaphthylene, naphthalene and benzaldehyde. These compounds have been described as showing insecticidal activity [31]. Likewise, compounds with fungicidal activity such as flucytosine and biphenyl were identified [32]. Finally, the presence of antimicrobial compounds such as benzoic acid, methanamine and quinoxaline were also observed (Table 1, and supplementary tables S4, S5, S6, S7, and S8) [33].

Discussion

In recent years, the use of beneficial bacteria in agriculture has increased considerably, so the isolation, identification and evaluation of these bacteria is a crucial step in the development of environmentally friendly products. In this study, the B. velezensis Bac-9 strain, isolated from kefir from Escárcega, Mexico, exhibit characteristics of plant-growth-promoting rhizobacteria. Based on 16 S gene sequencing, the Bac-9 was assigned to the Bacillus genus, a finding further validated by whole-genome comparisons against B. velezensis 83, which revealed a nucleotide identity (ANI) of 99.32%, confirming a close phylogenetic relationship. Phenotypically, Bac-9 exhibited strong antagonistic activity towards phytopathogenic fungi, including F. solani, F. equiseti, Curvularia sp, and P. capsici. This antifungal capacity is consistent with well documented mechanisms in Bacillus species, where the inhibitory effect is attributed to the diverse arsenal of bioactive secondary metabolites [34]. In Bac-9 were identified gene clusters encoding secondary metabolites as non-ribosomal peptides (NRPs) and polyketides (PKs), including iturin A, mycosubtilin, fengycin and bacillisin [35, 36]. At the molecular level, these metabolites act through complementary mechanisms, i.e., iturin A and mycosubtilin disrupt fungal membranes by pore formation, causing ion leakage and cell death; fengycin targets sterols and phospholipid biosynthesis, impairing cell-wall integrity and hyphal growth, whereas bacilysin inhibits protein synthesis. In particular, the combination of iturin and fengycin, regulates a broad set of genes than either compound alone. Acting together, these metabolites not only block pathogen growth but also enhance antifungal efficacy through synergistic interactions [37, 38]. In our results, RT-PCR analysis confirmed the transcriptional activity of biosynthetic operons for iturin A, mycosubtilin, and bacillisin under different culture conditions and expression during the exponential phase, suggesting that their production is tightly linked to bacterial growth and nutrient availability. This ability to activate secondary metabolite synthesis under different conditions emphasizes Bac-9 environmental adaptability and reinforces its potential for field application.

Previous studies have shown that secondary metabolites produced by B. velezensis, including iturin A, bacilysin and mycosubtilin, not only inhibit phytopathogens, but also elicit systemic resistance in plants [39, 40]. In this regard, the expression of resistance genes in tomato plants treated with Bac-9 bacterial suspension revealed a differential expression of ACC oxidase, and PDF 1.2 genes compared to the controls, being consistent with previous results [41, 42]. The expression of the ACC oxidase gene in the treated plants indicates activation of ethylene metabolism, a key regulator of plant stress response and defense as it catalyzes the conversion of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) into ethylene [43]. The induction of this gene suggests that treatment with Bac-9 promotes defense responses in plants, probably through increased ethylene production. Additionally, the expression of PDF 1.2 exclusively in treated plants highlights its role in defense against pathogens. This gene encodes a protein involved in the response to injury and is considered a defense marker in plants [44]. Additionally, the expression of PDF 1.2 exclusively in treated plants highlights its role in defense against pathogens. The expression of PDF 1.2, together with ACC oxidase, suggests that treatment with B. velezensis Bac-9 induces a state of alert in plants that allows them to respond effectively against possible pathogen attacks. In contrast, the NPR1 gene was constitutively expressed in both the treated and control groups, as it was also observed in Arabidopsis thaliana resulting in disease resistance [44]. The expression of NPR1 in both groups indicates that in L. esculentum it is also expressed constitutively, regardless of treatment, which would allow the plant to be in an alert state, ready to respond to infections. However, the interaction with Bacillus may not have been sufficient to trigger a full defense state, since the NPR1 gene, associated with more specific defense responses, did not show expression in any of the groups analyzed. This could imply that the infection or stress was not intense enough to activate the more robust defense pathways [45].

On the other hand, some NRPs and PKs not only exhibit antimicrobial activity but also induce systemic resistance in plants. This response produces molecular and physiological changes, activating plant defense mechanism against phytopathogens and insect pests [46, 47]. Consistent whit this evidences our results show that Bac-9 extracts reduce whitefly oviposition and act as repellents in tomato plants, as supported by the observed ODI and IR values.

Conclusion

The information obtained in this study provides valuable clues for the identification of active compounds with potential for the biological control of phytopathogens and pests such as whitefly. The extracts are effective in repelling adult insects and in turn, prevent oviposition, likely due with specific compounds present in the extracts. At the transcriptional level these extracts also induce plant resistance mechanisms, triggering defense response through activation of signaling pathways, involving compounds as salicylic acid, and jasmonic acid. In summary, these findings open opportunities for developing effective plant-growth-promoting bacteria or PGPB-based products, including optimized formulations that maintain compound stability and activity, scalable production processes for commercial application, and targeted field studies to validate efficacy under diverse environmental conditions.
