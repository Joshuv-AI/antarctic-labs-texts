Bacterial Hybrid Light‐Emitting Diodes
Authors: Sara Ferrara, Stephanie Willeit, Juan Pablo Fuenzalida‐Werner, Rubén D. Costa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhoton down‐converting filters with fluorescent proteins (FPs) are a new frontier in the quest for rare‐earth‐free and non‐toxic color filters for white light‐emitting diodes. There are, however, concerns related to the FP purification costs and lack of FP recyclability/reuse. Here, the direct use of bacteria in photon down‐converting filters can be of utmost relevance, eliminating purification and allowing in situ production of new FPs. However, their high background autofluorescence/scattering and low stability in polymer coatings have traditionally hampered the application of Engineering Living Materials (ELMs) for photon manipulation. Indeed, there are no examples of ELMs in lighting systems. This work discloses the first protocol to prepare living spheroplasts with > 90% scattering reduction, high FP expression fairly keeping their photoluminescence figures‐of‐merit, and excellent resilience in polymer films over 1 year under ambient storage. This unlocked the preparation of the first bacteria hybrid light‐emitting diodes integrating ELMs for photon conversion. These devices feature similar stabilities to those using purified FPs, while enabling a cost‐effective strategy and active FP recycling by the simple recultivation of spheroplasts. Overall, this work introduces a successful case toward bacteria‐polymer photon manipulation, in general, and a new living lighting concept, in particular.

Introduction

Engineered living materials (ELMs) is an emerging field working on the frontier between Synthetic Biology and Materials Science, in which the functionality and adaptability of living cells along with the mechanical and optical features of polymeric matrices are at force to design active and responsive biogenic and/or bio‐hybrid materials for sensing, medicine (wound healing, tissue engineering, drug delivery, etc.), optoelectronics and photonics (photovoltaics, lasers, photonic wires, etc.), computing and electronics (logical gates, conductive wires, and films, etc.), conversion (solar fuels, moisture to electricity, etc.), among others.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] This new class of materials endows a game‐changing character in eco‐efficient and low‐cost material production, self‐regeneration, environmental adaptation and responsitivity, sustainability, etc., while modern endeavors toward functionalities and materials design can be either genetically programmed or evolved over time.

Today's biological tunable optoelectronics and photonics integrating living building blocks are slowly advancing due to the critical need for cell engineering tools to mature the production and assembly of proteins and structural materials as well as to enhance cell resilence and cell–cell or cell‐artificial component communications. More specifically, the implementation of living components in photovoltaic devices is not trivial due to the complex electronically functional interfaces between living cells (e.g., cyanobacteria and green algae) and organic–inorganic semiconductor materials for charge transfer, transport, as well as collection processes. However, such systems are expected to achieve solar conversion efficiencies of 6–10% and >15% by using existing and enhanced light‐harvesting systems, respectively.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] Living lasers, in which individual cells with either fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins (FP) act as a viable gain medium to produce bright, directional, and narrowband laser emission stable over long periods,[ 11 , 12 ] are limited by surface passivation as well as heat and irradiation damage of cells. In contrast, there are no examples of living devices using external electrical stimuli or photon conversion schemes to produce and manipulate light, respectively.[ 2 , 13 ] Indeed, electronically generated radiative exitons in cells are unknown, and optical applications of living organisms are hampered due to the high background autofluorescence and scattering.

Herein, the first bacteria hybrid light‐emitting diode (BaHLED) is presented by simultaneously reducing scattering events and enhancing bacteria‐polymer matrix compatibility without jeopardizing device stability compared to reference devices with the respective purified FPs – Figure 1 . In short, we provide a new protocol to eliminate the cell wall in Escherichia coli (E. coli), forming spheroplasts that keep the cellular volume inversely by the environment osmotic pressure and the expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) and monomeric Green Lantern (mGL) – Figure 1. Compared to traditional protocols (mixture of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), lysozyme, and sucrose at an alkaline pH)[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] with a reduction of scattering of ≈40%, we hypothesized that the effective hyperosmotic effect of maltodextrin (MD) proven to disrupt different organisms' cell walls to improve the efficacy of antibiotics[ 19 ] or the cytotoxicity of electrochemical scaffolds[ 20 ] in concert with its low diffusivity through the bacteria membrane would be more beneficial than sucrose, facilitating the action of EDTA and lysozymes. Indeed, a fine optimization led to an almost quantitative reduction of the scattering to >90%, while microscopy, steady‐state, and time‐resolved spectroscopy studies confirmed the lack of cell cytolysis, fairly keeping the photophysical properties of FPs compared to purified proteins. What is more, MD also allows a full compatibility of spheroplasts and poly(vinyl alcohol) films that combine excellent water‐oxygen barriers to keep the spheroplasts alive[ 4 ] and optimal optical and mechanical features for bio‐hybrid light‐emitting diodes (BioHLEDs) – Figure 1.[ 21 ]

Scheme of the BaHLED concept: Spheroplasts formation followed by the encapsulation in PVA, resulting in a BaHLED containing the living phosphor.

BioHLEDs is a new class of lighting devices that aim at replacing the unsustainable rare earth (Ce:YAG) or toxic (Cd‐based quantum dots) inorganic color filters used in commercial white LEDs by biogenic color filters with FPs stabilized in polymers, following the “Green Photonics Concept” stated in the EU and US roadmaps for lighting and eco‐efficient functional materials.[ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] To date, HLEDs based on traditional organic phosphors have reached interesting device stabilities (e.g., perylene diimide‐polymer with <700 h at 40 mA[ 27 ] and Iridium(III) complex‐polymer with < 1,000 h at 10 mA,[ 28 ] while the emerging BioHLEDs with EGFP derivatives have reached > 3000 h at 30 mA[ 29 ] and <100 h at 200 mA[ 30 ] and mCherry and smURFP devices have shown >2500 h at 200 mA.[ 21 , 31 ] Thus, further advances will make FP‐based HLEDs truly competitive. However, the high costs related to the FP purification and lack of FP recycling are always concerns. Here, living color filters based on a spheroplast‐PVA mixture represent a fresh alternative – Figure 1, since i) scattering is significantly reduced, ii) the FP expression and photoluminescence features are fairly preserved over 1 year under ambient storage, iii) BaHLEDs feature similar stabilities to those of BioHLEDs with their respective FPs and the same polymer matrices (e.g., mGL devices with ca. 330 h at 200 mA), and iv) the functionality of the spheroplasts is preserved, enabling an easy recycling protocol of the living color down‐converting filter by dissolving them in culture medium.

Overall, this work provides two major milestones in the ELM field. On one hand, a new protocol to form spheroplasts with i) a minimized bacterial scattering, ii) improved viability compared to the prior‐art, and iii) a high concentration of expressed FPs keeping their photoluminescence. This is highly relevant, since spheroplasts have been only used to study macromolecular synthesis[ 16 ] and intracellular enzyme localization,[ 14 ] but there is no fundamental analysis about optimizing their formation and optical/biological functionality features in the literature. On the other hand, the first simple and stable light‐emitting diodes integrating active living materials for photon conversion exhibited similar stabilities to those using purified FPs, while enabling a cost‐effective strategy and active FP recycling by simple the recultivation of spheroplasts, respectively. These are assets that will make this new living lighting concept of high interest in the near future.

Results and Discussion

Enhanced Protocol for Spheroplasts Production

To study the spheroplast formation, the established protocol containing 800 mm sucrose was first evaluated concerning the scattering reduction on E. coli K12 transformed with pET21 plasmid to heterologous express the archetypal EGFP. In line with the literature, this protocol led to 42.7 ± 0.94% scattering reduction compared to non‐treated bacteria − Figure 2a.[ 14 , 15 , 17 , 32 ] Since MD is used to reinforce anti‐biofilm treatments due to its effective weakening effect on bacterial cell walls,[ 19 , 20 ] we decided to replace sucrose with MD. Upon increasing the MD concentration from 30 to 120 mm, the scattering reduction increases, reaching 92.2 ± 0.38% compared to non‐treated bacteria. This is also nicely noted by the naked eye in Figure 2b, in which enhanced light transmittance in solutions with spheroplasts (S, bottom) and non‐treated bacteria (B, top) is evident. The production of spheroplasts was further confirmed by fluorescent light microscopy. In contrast to the classical elliptical shape of intact bacteria (2.50 ± 0.51 µm/1.10 ± 0.05 µm), spherical shape bacteria of diameter 1.91 ± 0.25 µm are present, proving the successful formation of spheroplasts − Figure 2c,d.[ 14 , 15 , 17 ] Importantly, no free EGFP signal can be detected surrounding the spheroplasts, confirming the integrity of the cytoplasm membrane of the spheroplasts. This is also noted upon storage conditions for 24 h at 4 °C, since the optical density is constant − i.e., 5.71 ± 0.30 (fresh) versus 6.43 ± 0.40 (after 24 h) as shown in Figure S1a (Supporting Information). Thus, the spheroplast formation is stable as the metabolism slows down upon freezing.[ 16 ]

Microscope pictures of the non‐treated bacteria c) and spheroplasts d) diluted in 62.3 m Tris‐HCl pH 8 with their respective scheme, showing their average size. e) Representation of a gram− bacterial cell wall, cytoplasm membrane, and the points of attack during the spheroplasts forming process. f) Life forming units from non‐treated bacteria (B) and spheroplasts (S) solutions. g) Picture of antibiotic plates plated non‐treated bacteria control (B, top) and plated spheroplasts (S, bottom).

To the best of our knowledge, the formation of spheroplasts has been always carried out using sucrose as superosmotic reactant.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 32 , 33 ] The major drawback of these protocols for photon manipulation is the limited scattering reduction (typically in the range of 40%) that was good enough for applications of bacterial sonolysis in industrial settings[ 15 ] or protein localization inside E. coli cells.[ 14 ] Thus, a further scattering reduction has not been the main target up to date. However, high hyperosmotic forces should lead to smaller spheroplasts, resulting in reduced scattering. In this rationale, MD and sucrose share a similar mechanism to weaken the cell wall as hyperosmotic reagents,[ 19 , 20 , 34 ] facilitating access to EDTA and lysozyme (Figure 2e) and increasing bacterial plasticity that further reduces its size.[ 16 , 33 ] The use of MD generates a higher osmotic pressure than sucrose as the internal and external concentrations are never equal, since MD features a tenfold higher molecular weight (≈3,000 g mol−1 vs ≈350 g mol−1). Thus, MD cannot diffuse through the cell wall of E. coli K12 (Figure 2e) as the outer membrane is permeable to water and hydrophilic molecules smaller than 600 g mol−1.[ 35 ] In other words, the higher molecular weight of MD does not allow outer membrane permeability and further reduces spheroplast scattering by a more effective cell shrinking. It is important to note that previous reports have shown that MD follows the so‐called U‐shape dose‐response, that is, a certain concentration MD reduces the effect on the permeability of the bacterial cell wall due to diffusion limitation responses.[ 19 , 20 ] This effect could not be observed in our experiments, although our concentrations were up to four times higher. Most likely, the difference arises from the different analyzed bacterial types. Thus, the spheroplast protocols must be optimized for each bacteria strain with respect to membrane permeability, diffusivity, and plasticity.

Next, we systematically optimize i) the lysozyme concentration to degrade the cell wall, ii) the incubation time to ensure bacteria‐spheroplast transformation at stationary growth phase with high bacteria density, and iii) the applied numbers of bacteria and/or FP loading to ensure viability for device application. First, lysozyme concentrations (3, 6, 12, and 24 µg mL−1) were examined to identify optimized conditions to remove the cell wall, keeping the optimized MD concentration constant at 120 mm − Figure S1b (Supporting Information). The scattering reduction slightly enhanced up to an average value of 96.5% ± 0.45% in the above concentration range. Thus, the control of the osmotic pressure is critical for forming the scatter‐reduced spheroplasts, allowing a low lysozyme concentration of 6 µg mL−1. Second, a standard incubation time of 20 min at room temperature (RT; 20 ± 5 °C) is typically applied.[ 14 , 17 , 32 ] Since we are using bacteria on the stationary growth phase and a high cell density is required for device application − vide infra, a longer incubation time was applied, resulting in a slight impact on the scattering reduction going from 96.1 ± 0.22% after 20 min to 94.5 ± 0.11% after 1 h at RT − Figure S1c (Supporting Information). Thus, short incubation times can be applied, since the use of MD to transform bacteria to spheroplasts keeps them metabolically active at RT as the traditional sucrose protocol.[ 16 ]

Finally, the fabrication of hybrid light‐emitting diodes requires a controlled high amount of photon down‐converting bacteria that can be governed by the density of spheroplasts and/or the expression rate of FPs in each spheroplast, fairly keeping the photoluminescence features compared to purified FPs in solution – vide infra. As expected, centrifugation in saturated MD solutions is ineffective in concentrating spheroplasts.[ 36 ] Thus, we first focus on the initial bacteria cell number going to a saturation regime from 9.18 × 1010 to 1.83 × 1011 bacteria mL−1, resulting in a similar scattering reduction of 96.2 ± 0.22% and 94.3± 1.04%, respectively − Figure S1d (Supporting Information). This holds even at longer incubation times of 1 h − i.e., 94.3 ± 1.04% after 20 min vs 94.0 ± 1.36% after 1 h incubation − Figure S1e (Supporting Information), highlighting that our spheroplast protocol is also effective at initial cell density saturation conditions. At this point, we decided to check the full viability of our living spheroplast with respect to their capacity i) to recover the cell wall upon reducing the hyperosmotic conditions and ii) to keep the production of FPs; critical aspects to allow recycling of FPs – vide infra.

Ideally, spheroplasts should be able to recover their cell wall upon eliminating the hyperosmotic conditions,[ 17 , 32 ] but only <1% survival rate has been reported.[ 16 ] Our protocol ensures a >5% survival rate to the deployed number of bacteria – i.e., the total number of viable bacteria of 1.83 × 1010 ± 4.78 for untreated bacteria vs. 4.68 ×109 ± 1.58 for spheroplasts − Figure 2f, most likely as a consequence of the inability of MD to diffuse into the spheroplasts. Though this highlights the advantage of our new protocol, the overall low viability of the spheroplasts is most probably related to the osmotic stress during the platting process given by the change from a hyperosmotic environment toward 1x PBS and a reduction of the cell wall recovery ability by part of the spheroplasts population.[ 17 ] All‐in‐all, the spheroplast‐bacteria transformation for recycling protocols is not ideal at this stage. However, the recultivated spheroplast can produce more EGFP (45.0 µgmL−1) than the one from the cell control (30.1 µg mL−1) as displayed in Figure 2g and Figure S1f (Supporting Information). Probably, the reduced metabolic activity of spheroplasts[ 16 ] compared to the non‐treated bacteria let them retain the plasmid more efficiently, resulting in a higher amount of FP expression. This allows us to study other FP variants with higher protein expression as the above‐mentioned mGL. This is another green‐emitting (514 nm) protein representative with a monomeric character, higher photoluminescence quantum yield (ϕ; 78% vs 68%), almost double molecular brightness, and features high chemical and thermodynamic stability that translates to a record production yield (250 mg L−1 vs 30 mg L−1).[ 29 , 37 , 38 , 39 ] Indeed, spheroplasts with mGL featured a 3.5‐fold increase in protein loading (4.59 ± 0.29 mg mL−1 vs 1.32 ± 0.18 mg mL−1) and enhanced photoluminescence figures with respect to EGFP spheroplasts in both, solutions and films – vide infra. The only drawback is the lower scattering reduction up to 84.7 ± 0.65%, which is related to the higher osmotic pressure inside the mGL‐spheroplasts due to the high mGL concentration. This restricts reaching the same level of shrinking as observed with EGFP spheroplasts.

Living Photon Converting Films

Self‐standing photon converting films with spheroplasts were prepared by adding a solution of them with a total amount of EGFP (1.5 mg) and mGL (8 mg) to a saturated PVA solution in milli‐Q water under gentle stirring conditions followed by a drying process under ambient conditions – i.e., EGFP‐ or mGL‐S‐PVA, see Supporting Information and Figure 3a. As a reference, two films with the same protein load and composition were prepared with the respective purified FP and non‐treated bacteria (B) – i.e. EGFP‐ or mGL‐PVA and EGFP‐ or mGL‐B‐PVA, respectively – Figure 3a.

To the naked eye, all films exhibited bright green emission under UV irradiation − Figure 3a. The EGFP film series featured a similar well‐structured emission band centered at ca. 510 nm (5 nm redshifted for EGFP‐S‐PVA films; Table 1 , Figure 3b; Figure S2a–c, Supporting Information). The excitation spectra of EGFP‐PVA films showed three peaks centered at 488, 400 , and 280 nm ascribed to the emissive ionic chromophore form, the non‐emissive neutral chromophore form, and the emission quenching of the tryptophane residue, respectively – Figure S2c (Supporting Information). In contrast, EGFP‐S‐ and EGFP‐B‐PVA films exhibited the same excitation band of the ionic chromophore at ca. 490 nm along with a new excitation band at ca. 350 nm and a more prominent mid‐energy excitation band at 400 nm that is ascribed to cellular compounds, such as heme‐containing proteins that show a maximum absorbance between 350 and 450 nm − Figure 3b,c and Figure S3a (Supporting Information).[ 40 ] Indeed, the absolute ϕ is reduced from 68% (EGFP‐PVA) down to 45% for both, EGFP‐S‐PVA and EGFP‐B‐PVA films, since the heme‐containing proteins feature strong absorption and very weak emission − Figure S3c,d (Supporting Information). Likewise, excited state lifetime (τ) values are also affected by the presence of cellular components that change the refractive index, showing slightly longer values – Table 1. This is also in line with the solutions of both, spheroplasts (S) and the non‐treated bacteria (B) − Table 1.

In the mGL series, the emission band shape partially loses its vibrational structure with a redshifted wavelength emission maxima for films with spheroplast (ca. 10 nm) and blueshifted for non‐treated bacteria (ca. 10 nm) compared to those with purified mGL – Table 1; Figure 3c and Figure S3b (Supporting Information). This might be related to the formation of soluble protein aggregates as the protein concentration is higher – vide supra. The excitation spectra and photoluminescence features (ϕ and τ) follow a similar trend to that of the above EGFP series with the presence of spectroscopic figures of the cellular components that slightly reduce the ϕ to ca. 65% and increase the τ values in both, solutions and PVA films − Table 1.

Finally, the above films showed good self‐stability under ambient storage conditions with a < 5% (mGL series) and < 15% (EGFP series) loss of ϕ over 1 year, keeping their respective emission/excitation band shapes − Figure 3d and Figure S4 (Supporting Information). Finally, fresh and 1‐year‐old PVA films as well as those used in devices (vide infra) were recultivated, showing active spheroplasts − Figure 3e,f and Figure S4 (Supporting Information). Colony PCR confirmed the presence of our EGFP expression plasmid by showing us the ≈1 kb DNA band corresponding to the fragments encoding EGFP. One‐year‐old EGFP‐S‐PVA and mGL‐S‐PVA were able to grow, as shown in Figure S4b (Supporting Information). There is, however, a better‐growing rate for EGFP‐S‐PVA vs mGL‐S‐PVA that could be ascribed to a higher selection pressure for the bacteria containing the mGL plasmid caused by the higher expression rate – Figure S4b (Supporting Information). It is important to note that 1‐year‐old EGFP/mGL‐B‐PVA films did not show any growing activity as bacteria are absent in the growth media, indicating complete cell cytolysis most likely caused by the lack of MD. Overall, these findings prove that the use of MD preserves the formation of living photon‐converting films that could be recycled after device use – vide infra.

Living Light‐Emitting Diodes

On‐chip LED architectures, in which the above photon converting films are directly deposited onto an unmodified commercial blue‐emitting LED chip (450 nm; Figure 1), were fabricated and characterized under continuous photon flux excitation of 150 mW cm−2 or applied current of 200 mA, monitoring the increase of the coating temperature and the decay of the emission intensity of the conversion band until reaching 50% of either the initial emission intensity during the heating process at the first regime – i.e., brightness loss associated to thermal quenching – or the initial emission intensity at the start the of the second regime in which the temperature is stabilized − i.e., the device lifetime or L50 under device working conditions (150 mW cm−2 at certain temperature as indicated in the text).

In the EGFP series, the first devices featured a partial conversion (≈45%) of the blue LED band to the respective green emission band of the photon‐converting films (1.5 mg of protein and 2 mm thickness; Figure 4a), whose intensity decreases in two regimes as noted in the prior‐art – Figure 4b,c.[ 41 ] As a reference, EGFP‐PVA‐devices featured a first 30% loss due to the temperature increase up to 45 °C attributed to the photo‐induced protein motion (rotation and vibration) and subsequent water‐mediated heat transfer to the polymer composite network – Figure 4b,d.[ 30 ] This is followed by a second decay reaching an L50 of ca. 4.5 h – Figure 4c. In contrast, EGFP‐B‐PVA‐devices feature a high thermal quenching of over 60% with a temperature rise up to ca. 55 °C − Figure 4b,d, since other cell components are freely dispersed in the polymer matrix as cell cytolysis occurred – vide supra. Thus, this device was not further investigated. Finally, EGFP‐S‐PVA‐devices exhibited a first 20% loss as the working temperature reached ca. 40 °C and a slightly enhanced L50 up to 5.5 h − Figure 4b,c,d. Here, the photon‐induced motion of cell components and FPs inside the cytoplasm is expected to be hampered by the cytoplasma membrane, which might reduce heat transfer to the polymer network. Since the conversion efficiency was low, we decided to increase the film thickness up to 5 mm in concert with the highest possible spheroplast loading, reaching a maximum EGFP amount of 2 mg. This led to EGFP‐S‐PVA‐devices with a slightly enhanced conversion of ca. 80% as well as similar working temperatures of ca. 40 °C and enhanced L50 up to ≈10 h after the first thermal quenching − Figure S5a–c (Supporting Information). Importantly, the spectroscopic inspection of all the films after a device used (bleached film; vide supra) shows a similar reduction of the emission band intensity and reduction of the excited state lifetime values – Figure S6 (Supporting Information), suggesting a similar deactivation mechanism as already reported in the literature.[ 29 , 41 ] What is more, these EGFP‐S‐PVA films showed alive spheroplasts – Figure 3e,f, highlighting the potential toward recycling.

In the mGL series, on‐chip devices (8 mg protein and thickness of 5 mm) featured a quantitative blue‐to‐green conversion − x/y CIE color coordinates of 0.36/0.58 − at the applied current range of 5–200 mA − Figure 4e. Similar to what is described for the EGFP series, the photo‐induced temperature rise and first initial loss follow the series mGL‐B‐PVA‐, mGL‐PVA‐, and mGL‐S‐PVA‐devices – Figure 4f,g. mGL‐S‐PVA‐devices feature a slightly enhanced L50 compared to mGL‐devices (ca. 10 h; 330 h in total) – Figure 4g. As a final confirmation, mGL‐S‐PVA‐devices were driven at low power conditions (40 mA or 50 mW cm−2), reaching similar stabilities as the prior‐art of L50 >1500 h − Figure S5c,d (Supporting Information).[ 21 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] Hence, using spheroplast in photon‐converting schemes for lighting does not jeopardize the stability, providing lower costs (no purification) and possible recycling if the survival rate is enhanced − Figure 3e,f and Figure S4 (Supporting Information).

Conclusion

The development of ELM for photon manipulation applied to lighting schemes has been always discouraged by their high autofluorescence and scattering features. This work discloses a successful protocol to prepare the first spheroplast‐polymer photon down‐converting coatings applied to hybrid light‐emitting diodes. Compared to the prior‐art in spheroplasts, the use of MD results in a significantly enhanced scattering reduction (40% vs >90%), high FP expression keeping their photoluminescence features, and high compatibility with PVA polymers, preserving the spheroplast's viability even after 1 year under ambient storage. This resulted in living lighting‐emitting diodes featuring slightly enhanced device stabilities (maximum values of ca. ≈330 h at high driving currents) as the working temperature is slightly reduced compared to reference devices with the purified FPs. What is more, spheroplast‐polymer devices feature stabilities of >1500 h under low driving currents. Importantly, the direct implementation of bacteria‐polymer leads to devices with stabilities of a few minutes due to the significant thermal quenching as a full bacteria cytolysis is present. These findings highlight the substantial progress in harnessing the potential of living organisms to develop advanced photon‐manipulation coatings, in general, and lighting technologies, in particular. On‐going optimization is focused on i) a genetic FP integration with high yields at the absence of antibiotic pressure and ii) the implementation of other living organisms to increase the resistance against high irradiations in concert with low amounts of oxygen and nutrients. In addition, comparative cost analysis and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) studies would be relevant to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the ELMs production toward real market application with respect to current FP‐based and organic phosphors. This will surely expand the application of ELMs in lighting schemes, but could also provide new opportunities in high photon flux conversion purposes like living lasers, in which photo‐induced heat generation remains as a major bottleneck.
