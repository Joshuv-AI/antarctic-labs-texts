The Effect of Foot Reflexology on Sleep Quality in Patients With Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia Under Chemotherapy: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Naser Parizad, Amireh Hassanpour, Rasoul Goli, Hamidreza Khalkhali, Aysan Nozad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsChronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) frequently causes a range of physical and psychological symptoms, including disrupted sleep. Foot reflexology is thought to promote relaxation and improve sleep quality. This study evaluated its effectiveness in patients with CLL undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsA single‐blind randomized controlled trial was conducted with 68 participants (n = 68), who were assigned either to an intervention group or a control group. The intervention group received daily 10‐min foot reflexology sessions for 4 weeks using sweet almond oil, while the control group received standard routine care, including monitoring of vital signs, symptom management, medication administration, psychological support, and general patient education according to oncology ward protocols. Sleep quality was assessed at baseline and after the intervention using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Data were analyzed with SPSS version 25, using chi‐square tests, Fisher's exact test, and t‐tests.ResultsAt baseline, there was no significant difference in PSQI scores between groups (Reflexology: 16.30 ± 1.29 vs. Control: 16.78 ± 1.38; p = 0.143). After the intervention, the reflexology group showed a marked improvement in sleep quality compared to the control group (11.78 ± 1.47 vs. 16.21 ± 1.40; p < 0.001). The effect size was very large and clinically meaningful (Cohen's d = 3.08, 95% CI: 2.55–3.61). Within‐group analysis confirmed significant improvement in the reflexology group (t = 6.161, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 3.50), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (t = −1.559, p = 0.136).ConclusionsFoot reflexology appears to be a safe and effective complementary intervention to improve sleep quality in patients with CLL. However, the study's limitations, including the lack of a sham control, short‐term follow‐up, and a relatively small sample size, should be considered. Healthcare providers may cautiously incorporate reflexology into supportive care, ensuring integration with standard treatments and professional guidance.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a type of hematologic malignancy marked by the abnormal accumulation of lymphocytes in the blood and bone marrow [1]. The prevalence of CLL differs across regions [2], and in Western countries, it represents roughly 25% of all leukemia cases, making it the most common leukemia subtype [3]. The disease occurs more frequently in men than in women, with a male‐to‐female ratio of approximately 1.7:1 [4]. Although the exact cause of CLL is not fully understood, several risk factors have been identified, including advanced age, family history of CLL or other lymphoproliferative disorders, exposure to certain chemicals or radiation, and genetic abnormalities such as mutations in TP53 and ATM [5, 6].

Patients with CLL experience a range of physical symptoms, including fatigue, weakness, and increased susceptibility to infections. They also frequently face psychological and emotional challenges, among which sleep disturbances are particularly common [7, 8, 9]. Poor sleep quality, insomnia, and frequent awakenings can substantially impact patients' overall well‐being and quality of life [9].

Despite the prevalence of sleep disturbances in this population, research on effective interventions for CLL patients remains limited [10]. Recent studies and systematic reviews have explored sleep interventions in hematological cancers, including cognitive‐behavioral therapy, relaxation techniques, mindfulness, and other nonpharmacological approaches, but very few specifically target individuals with CLL [11, 12, 13]. This highlights a gap in supportive care for this patient group.

Foot reflexology is a complementary therapeutic modality based on the theory that specific reflex points on the feet correspond to different organs and systems in the body [9]. By applying targeted pressure to these points, reflexology aims to restore balance, promote relaxation, and enhance overall well‐being [10, 11, 12]. While reflexology has shown promising effects on sleep and anxiety in oncology and palliative care populations, high‐quality evidence specifically evaluating its impact on sleep in CLL patients is scarce.

Given its noninvasive nature, simplicity of administration, low cost, and general acceptability, foot reflexology presents a feasible adjunctive intervention to support sleep quality. Compared with other nonpharmacological options, these advantages make reflexology particularly suitable for patients undergoing chemotherapy. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of foot reflexology on sleep quality in patients with CLL undergoing chemotherapy. We hypothesized that patients receiving daily foot reflexology sessions would demonstrate improved sleep quality compared to those receiving standard routine care. The ultimate goal is to provide evidence‐based recommendations to enhance supportive care and overall well‐being in this patient population.

Research Design

This study employed a single‐blind, parallel, randomized controlled trial design. Only the data analyst was blinded to group allocation.

Participants

The study included 68 patients with CLL admitted to Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 [15]. Based on a prior study by Özdelikara and Tan [16], which reported mean scores of 1.44 ± 0.20 for the intervention group and 2.33 ± 0.65 for the control group, a minimum of 31 participants per group was needed to achieve 80% power at a 95% confidence level. To account for a potential 10% attrition rate, 34 participants were recruited for each group, resulting in a total sample of 68 patients.

Sampling

Participants were randomly assigned to either the reflexology intervention group (n = 34) or the control group (n = 34) using a sealed‐envelope method. A total of 68 cards were prepared: 34 labeled “A” for the control group and 34 labeled “B” for the intervention group. The cards were shuffled, and each participant selected one card, which determined their group allocation. Convenience sampling was used to recruit the study participants.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for participation in the study were as follows: (a) providing consent and expressing willingness to participate, (b) age between 18 and 50, (c) diagnosis of CLL based on the patient's medical records, (d) no history of foot injury or surgery, (e) hemoglobin level above 9 mg/dL, and (f) receiving the same type of chemotherapy without undergoing radiotherapy. The exclusion criteria included: (a) unwillingness to continue participation, (b) missing more than one training session, (c) incomplete questionnaires, (d) patient death, and (e) transfer to another healthcare facility.

Data Collection Instruments

Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

The demographic questionnaire captured participants' age, gender, marital status, education level, place of residence, and occupation.

The PSQI is a widely used instrument designed to assess sleep quality and disturbances over a 1‐month period. Developed by researchers at the University of Pittsburgh, it provides a standardized measure of sleep quality. The PSQI includes 19 self‐rated items and, if applicable, an additional five items completed by a roommate or bed partner. It evaluates multiple dimensions of sleep, including duration, latency, disturbances, use of sleep medication, daytime dysfunction, and overall sleep quality. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Component scores are summed to yield a global score ranging from 0 to 21, where a score of 5 or higher reflects poor sleep quality [17]. The PSQI has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across various populations, both clinical and nonclinical. It has been extensively employed in research studies, clinical trials, and sleep medicine practice to identify sleep problems and monitor treatment outcomes [18]. The study used the Persian (local‐language) version of the PSQI. This version has been previously validated in Iranian populations, demonstrating adequate reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.83) and validity (sensitivity = 0.89, specificity = 0.69) [17].

Intervention

After obtaining permission from the hospital officials at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia, the researcher collected data for the study. Eligible participants were recruited based on the predetermined inclusion criteria. During the initial session, the researcher introduced themselves to the participants, explained the study methodology and objectives, and obtained written informed consent from all participants. Questionnaires were then completed by the intervention and control groups. The study was conducted over a 3‐month period from January to March 2023.

To maintain the distinctiveness between the intervention and control groups, each group was assigned to a different unit, with no interaction between them. Specifically, there were two units for leukemia patients: Unit A and Unit B. Patients in Unit A were designated as the control group, while those in Unit B were designated as the intervention group. The existence of the intervention and control groups was kept unknown to each other, and the ward staff were also informed accordingly.

The study was conducted over a period of 4 weeks, during which all participating leukemia patients remained in the hospital. Eligible participants in the intervention group received a standardized foot reflexology protocol over a 4‐week period. Each session lasted 10 min and was performed nightly on both feet. Before each session, sweet almond oil was applied to the soles. The massage targeted specific reflex points in a fixed sequence, including Head/Brain, Lungs, Liver, Kidneys, and Spine.

All sessions were conducted by the same trained researcher (Therapist Initials: AH) to ensure consistency (File S1). Therapist adherence and intervention fidelity were carefully monitored using a session checklist, which recorded duration, pressure, reflex points covered, and any patient responses or discomfort. The pressure was standardized at a moderate level for all participants to maintain uniform application. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual sleep habits throughout the study.

The control group received routine care only, which included standard nursing care, medication administration, and management of leukemia symptoms, without any additional foot reflexology intervention. Both intervention and control groups were housed in separate units to minimize interaction, and participants were blinded to the existence of the other group to reduce potential bias.

Both groups were instructed to maintain their usual sleep habits and were provided with education on sleep hygiene. Out of 70 consecutive eligible patients, three patients declined to participate, three patients did not meet the inclusion criteria, and two participants were transferred to other medical centers due to disease exacerbation (refer to Figure 1). One month after the completion of the training sessions, the questionnaire was administered again. Finally, the data obtained from the two groups were compared. The present work has received approval from the ethical review board of Urmia university of medical sciences (Ethical No. IR.UMSU.REC.1397.490). All participants provided written informed consent.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and appropriate parametric tests were employed based on the results. Only participants who completed the study according to the protocol were included in the analysis, following a Per‐Protocol (PP) approach. Participants who withdrew, missed more than one session, or were transferred to another facility were excluded from the analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. Independent t‐tests were utilized to compare mean PSQI scores between the intervention and control groups at each time point. Additionally, paired‐samples t‐tests were employed for within‐group comparisons of normally distributed parameters. Effect sizes, calculated using Cohen's d, were reported along with 95% confidence intervals for both between‐group and within‐group comparisons, providing insight into the magnitude of the intervention effect. All analyses were performed by a researcher who was blinded to the data.

There were no significant differences in education level (p = 0.49), marital status (p = 0.69), occupation (p = 0.60), or residence (p = 0.49) between groups. Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of foot reflexology on sleep quality in patients with CLL using a randomized controlled trial design. Our findings demonstrated that reflexology significantly improved sleep quality compared to standard care, suggesting that this nonpharmacological intervention may be beneficial for this patient population.

Sleep disturbances are common among patients with leukemia, often resulting from disease‐related symptoms, treatment side effects, psychological distress, and medication‐induced insomnia. These disruptions can substantially reduce quality of life. While pharmacological interventions are available, they are often limited by side effects and potential interactions, making nonpharmacological approaches, such as reflexology, an attractive complementary therapy.

Previous studies support the potential of reflexology to improve sleep and reduce anxiety and fatigue across diverse patient populations. Recent systematic reviews have highlighted the efficacy of nonpharmacological interventions, including mindfulness‐based stress reduction and cognitive behavioral therapy, in improving sleep quality among cancer patients [11, 12, 13]. Notably, a study by Nissen et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of various nonpharmacological treatments for insomnia in cancer patients and survivors [19]. Moreover, Asltoghiri et al. demonstrated improvements in sleep disorders among menopausal women following reflexology massage [20]. Wang et al. reported enhanced sleep quality and reduced anxiety in adults receiving reflexology interventions [21], and Sajadi et al. observed significant reductions in fatigue and anxiety among patients with multiple sclerosis [22]. Similarly, Rambod et al. found that reflexology improved sleep quality and reduced fatigue in patients with lymphoma [23], while Alinia Najjar et al. showed that reflexology massage alleviated anxiety and enhanced sleep in burn patients admitted to the ICU [24]. Tarrasch et al. also reported improved sleep quality in breast cancer patients receiving foot reflexology during adjuvant radiation therapy [25].

However, the literature also presents some inconsistencies. For instance, Williams found no improvement in sleep following massage with aromatic oils in children with autism [26], and Fazlollah et al. reported no significant effect of reflexology on delirium or sleep quality [27]. These discrepancies may arise from differences in intervention type, duration, sample size, or patient population characteristics.

The control group received standard care only to maintain a clear differentiation from the intervention group. We acknowledge that this may introduce a placebo effect in the intervention group, and future studies could implement a sham reflexology protocol (e.g., oil application and discussion without massage) to control for nonspecific effects while considering ethical implications.

Potential confounding due to attention effects should also be considered. The one‐on‐one interaction, discussions, and social support provided by the reflexology therapist likely contributed to the observed benefits. This interaction may enhance the placebo effect, providing emotional and psychological support that improves sleep quality and overall well‐being. Future studies should account for these attention‐related effects when evaluating the specific impact of reflexology.

Physiological mechanisms underlying the effects of reflexology may include modulation of the autonomic nervous system, reduction of cortisol levels, and improvement in peripheral circulation, all of which could promote relaxation and enhance sleep [28].

We conducted a PP analysis; an Intention‐to‐Treat (ITT) analysis was not performed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future trials should consider including both PP and ITT analyses to strengthen robustness.

Due to the nature of foot reflexology, participants and intervention providers could not be blinded, although the data analyst was blinded to minimize bias. The control group did not receive a sham intervention, which may introduce a placebo effect. Future studies should consider sham or attention controls, larger multi‐center samples, longer follow‐up, and objective physiological measures to strengthen evidence.

In conclusion, foot reflexology significantly improved sleep quality in patients with CLL, and these effects may be mediated by both specific physiological mechanisms and nonspecific factors such as attention, social support, and the therapeutic relationship. Future research should address the current study′s limitations, including sham controls, extended follow‐up, larger sample sizes, and robust statistical analyses, to provide more definitive evidence regarding the efficacy of reflexology for sleep improvement.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that foot reflexology significantly improved sleep quality in patients with CLL. These findings highlight the potential of foot reflexology as a nonpharmacological intervention to enhance sleep in this patient population. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up periods are recommended to confirm these results and investigate the mechanisms underlying the observed effects.
